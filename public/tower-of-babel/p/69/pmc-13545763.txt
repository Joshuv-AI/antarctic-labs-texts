Effect of preoperative isometric hand exercise on vascular caliber in patients with advanced chronic kidney disease - a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Nawaphan Taengsakul, Phornpa Lertutsahakul, Pantita Pooloui, Mullika Buttakosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArteriovenous fistula (AVF) remains the preferred vascular access for hemodialysis, yet maturation failure is common and preoperative strategies to optimize vessel caliber are poorly standardized. This study hypothesizes that isometric exercise, when implemented preoperatively can enhance vascular caliber, thereby improving AVF creation and maturation.MethodsThis single-center, prospective randomized controlled trial enrolled 54 patients with CKD stage 4–5 scheduled for vascular access creation at Chulabhorn Hospital, Bangkok. Participants were allocated 1:1 to an 8-week isometric exercise regimen (grip strengthener and pétanque ball; n = 27) or control (n = 27). Vascular caliber was assessed by duplex ultrasound at baseline, 4, and 8 weeks. The primary outcome was change in cephalic vein arm diameter at 8 weeks. Secondary outcomes included changes in other vessel diameters, grip strength, AVF feasibility, AVF type, and 12-week maturation rate.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable except for higher CAD prevalence (44.4% vs. 18.5%, p = 0.040) and antiplatelet use (40.7% vs. 14.8%, p = 0.033) in the control group. The exercise group demonstrated a significant increase in cephalic vein arm diameter at 8 weeks (p = 0.045). Radiocephalic AVF creation was significantly more frequent in the exercise group (93.8% vs. 58.3%, p = 0.026). Baseline cephalic vein arm diameter was the strongest independent predictor of AVF feasibility (adjusted OR = 3.63 per mm, 95% CI [1.56, 8.47], p = 0.003). AVF maturation rates did not differ significantly between groups.ConclusionAn 8-week preoperative isometric exercise protocol significantly improved cephalic vein arm diameter and increased radiocephalic AVF creation rate, a clinically meaningful shift that preserves proximal venous capital for future vascular access procedures. No significant difference in short-term AVF maturation was observed, likely reflecting the premature 12-week follow-up and composition effect of more radiocephalic procedures in the exercise group; adequately powered trials with extended follow-up are warranted.Trial registrationThe study was registered with the Thai Clinical Trial Registry (number: 20230913004) on 13 September 2023.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05200-9.Keywords: Isometric exercise, Cephalic vein, Arteriovenous fistula, Chronic kidney disease, Vascular access

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects over 850 million people globally, surpassing the prevalence of diabetes and cancer [1, 2]. End-stage renal disease (ESRD) requires renal replacement therapy, with hemodialysis being the most common modality [1, 3]. Vascular access is a cornerstone of hemodialysis management, and autogenous arteriovenous fistula (AVF) is widely recognized as the preferred access due to its lower complication rates and superior long-term patency compared to arteriovenous grafts (AVG) and central venous catheters (CVC) [4, 5]. The European Society of Vascular Surgery recommends autogenous AVF as the first-line option of vascular access, due to its lower incidence of postoperative complication and reduced need for endovascular or surgical revision for AVF failure [5]. In accordance with the principle of venous preservation, the most distal AVF should typically created whenever feasible [5].

Despite its advantages, AVF maturation failure remains a significant clinical challenge, with rates reported between 24% and 40% [6–7]. Current guidelines recommend upper limb exercise to enhance vascular caliber and support AVF maturation, yet standardized and evidence-based protocols are lacking [4, 5]. A recent meta-analysis highlighted the heterogeneity of existing exercise interventions and the limited data supporting their efficacy [8].

We hypothesize that a structured isometric exercise protocol may enhance the vascular calibre of the cephalic vein, radial artery, and brachial artery, thereby influencing surgical planning and improving arteriovenous fistula (AVF) maturation outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT) to assess the effects of an exercise protocol on the vascular caliber of vessels in the arms of patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Fifty-four patients scheduled for the creation of vascular access for maintenance hemodialysis at Chulabhorn Hospital’s Vascular Clinic (Bangkok, Thailand) between January 2023 and January 2025 were enrolled. Patients who were 18–70 years old, diagnosed with stage 4–5 CKD, and deemed physically and mentally able to undertake the exercise protocol were eligible for this study. Patients who needed urgent vascular access for hemodialysis, had a history of vascular access surgery, had a physical or mental disability that limited their ability to perform the exercises, had a positive Allen test result, had an absent or thrombosed distal forearm cephalic vein, and were already exercising on a regular basis (which included performing manual work) were excluded. The following withdrawal criteria were implemented: complications associated with the exercise protocol, including muscle injury or pain; significant protocol deviation; abandonment; withdrawal of informed consent; kidney transplantation; change to dialysis technique; and death.

Chulabhorn Research Institute’s Human Research Ethics Committee approved this study (number: EC 017/2565). The study was registered with the Thai Clinical Trial Registry (number: 20230913004) on 13 September 2023. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before randomization.

Randomisation

During their first visit, each patient recruited to our study was randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group. The patients were assigned at a 1:1 ratio using sealed, numbered, opaque envelopes prepared by a study nurse. The treatments were assigned using a computerized random number generator developed by a statistician not involved in patient care. Block randomization was performed with a block size of six.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated based on data from Katheraveloo et al. [9], which reported a mean increase in cephalic vein diameter of 0.31 ± 0.04 mm in the exercise group and 0.01 ± 0.02 mm in the control group. Using these estimates, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 11 participants per group, based on a two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 (type I error) and a desired statistical power of 85% (type II error β = 0.15), with a predefined clinically relevant difference of 0.15 mm between groups (Zα/2 = 1.96, Zβ = 1.04).

To account for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, the sample size was adjusted to 14 participants per group (28 participants in total). However, a total of 54 participants (27 per group) were ultimately enrolled in the study, exceeding the minimum requirement and thereby improving the statistical precision for detecting differences in the primary outcome.

Study endpoints

The primary outcome was a difference in the cephalic vein diameter between the intervention and control groups. primary outcomes were the changes in cephalic vein diameter at the forearm and arm portions, as assessed by duplex ultrasound at 8 weeks post-randomization. These outcomes directly reflect the mechanistic target of isometric exercise — vascular remodeling through increased wall shear stress and endothelial nitric oxide production.

The secondary outcomes included greater AVF surgical decision-making for AVF and AVF maturation in the intervention group compared to the control group. included: (1) change in radial artery and brachial artery diameter at 8 weeks; (2) grip strength (kg) as measured by dynamometer at 4 and 8 weeks; (3) arteriovenous fistula (AVF) creation feasibility — defined as achieving a cephalic vein diameter ≥ 2 mm suitable for AVF creation; (4) type of AVF created (radiocephalic [RC AVF] versus brachiocephalic [BC AVF]); (5) conversion from initially ineligible to eligible for AVF creation following the exercise protocol; and (6) AVF maturation, defined as successful cannulation across at least two consecutive hemodialysis sessions with a blood flow rate ≥ 600 mL/min and vessel diameter ≥ 6 mm at 12 weeks postoperatively.

Any complications associated with the exercise protocol were recorded after the completion of the protocol. To ensure blinding of outcome analysis, randomization and data analysis were conducted by an independent researcher who was not involved in the study procedures or outcome assessment.

Interventions and follow-up

The intervention consisted of an 8-week isometric exercise protocol that was created by our institute’s rehabilitation team and sport scientists. The patients in the intervention group received one-on-one training from sports scientists. The first exercise involved compressing a grip strengthener (30% maximum voluntary contraction [MVC]) while counting to 10 s, and this was to be performed 10 times per set three times a day. MVC was assessed using the Jamar hydraulic hand dynamometer (Sammons Preston Rolyan, IL, USA), a validated instrument widely used for measuring grip strength in clinical and research setting [9]. Each participant was instructed to perform a maximal isometric grip effort, and the highest value recorded was considered as 100% MVC. For the training protocol, participants were provided with a handgrip exercise device, and the resistance was manually adjusted to correspond to 30% of the individual’s MVC, as determined form the dynamometer test [10]. This intensity level has been shown to be safe and effective for promoting vascular adaptation and muscular endurance in upper limb exercise [11]. In accordance with the American College of Sports Medicine (ASCM) guidelines, exercise performed at 30% of MVC is classified as light-intensity resistance training, which is considered safe and appropriate for most apparently healthy adults [12].

The second exercise involved grasping a pétanque ball (weight: 730 g, diameter: 73 mm) using a pronated grip (with palm facing downward) and maintain the grip for 10 s, and this was also to be performed 10 times per set, with three sets completed daily (Fig. 1). This movement was designed to activate forearm flexor muscles and simulating daily grip tasks [12]. The exercise protocol followed a non-periodised model, in which both the intensity and volume remained constant throughout the intervention period [12]. This protocol aligns with the ACSM’s guideline for exercise testing and prescription, which support the use of structured, low-to-moderate intensity resistance training to promote vascular adaptation [12]. The duration of 10 s for each grip was selected based on establish guidelines for isometric resistance training, which recommend short, sustained contractions to effectively activate target muscle groups while minimizing fatigue [12]. This timeframe is considered sufficient to stimulate forearm muscles and enhance local vascular response [12].

The control group received the rubber ball and was advised to compress a rubber ball while counting to 10, and this was to be performed 10 times per set three times a day. Compliance with the exercise program was recorded by the patients and sent to record in every visit. The completion of the protocol was recorded as well compliance.

Two primary instruments were used to evaluate the outcome in this study: a Jamar hand dynamometer (Sammons Preston Rolyan, IL, USA) for measuring handgrip strength, and duplex ultrasound for vascular assessment.

For handgrip strength measurement, patients were seated with their hip joints flexed at 90°, shoulder joints in a neutral position, elbows fixed at 90° flexion, forearms in a neutral position, and wrists positioned at 0–15° ulnar deviation.The standardized posture is recommended by the American Society of Hand Therapists (ASHT) [13]. The test was performed twice, and the higher of the two values was recorded. To minimize examiner bias and physical stress, patients were allowed to rest for more than 5 min between tests.

Duplex ultrasound evaluations were performed by a vascular surgeon using gray-scale imaging of the distal forearm and distal arm cephalic vein. The average of two cross-sectional (anterior/posterior) diameter measurements was used as the final value. As part of routine care, preoperative duplex ultrasound assessments were conducted on both arms at the mid-upper arm, elbow, mid-lower arm, and wrist levels.

These evaluations were conducted during three types of visits: baseline, preoperative, and postoperative (Fig. 1).

During the baseline visit, patients underwent a physical examination and duplex ultrasound, and demographic and clinical data were collected. Handgrip strength was also assessed using the hand dynamometer.

The rationale for arteriovenous fistula (AVF) creation was based on its recognized clinical advantages as the preferred vascular access for hemodialysis [4]. AVFs offer superior long-term patency, lower infection rates, and fewer complications compared to central venous catheters or synthetic grafts [4]. The indication for surgery was determined through duplex ultrasound assessment and clinical evaluation. Additional factors included the absence of vascular abnormalities, patient consent, and anticipated need for dialysis initiation. These criteria were applied during the preoperative evaluation at 8 weeks to ensure appropriate timing and optimal surgical outcomes.

In terms of the surgical procedures performed, an AVG was inserted when the small cephalic vein was < 2 mm in diameter, and an AVF was created when it was > 2 mm in diameter. However, when the cephalic vein grew from a small vein that was < 2 mm in diameter, the AVF was created with a vein that was > 2 mm in diameter.

AVF maturation was defined as successful cannulation for hemodialysis across at least two consecutive sessions, with adequate blood flow and vessel diameter suitable for repeated access. Specially, maturation criteria included a minimum blood flow rate of 600 milliliters/minute and a vessel diameter of 6 millimeters, as assessed by duplex ultrasound. These thresholds are consistent with commonly accepted clinical standards for functional AVF use. Postoperative assessments were conducted by vascular surgeon using duplex ultrasound. Complications will be systematically assessed at each follow-up visit throughout the study period.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis followed an intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, including all 54 randomized participants regardless of protocol adherence or completion status. Missing follow-up data due to participant discontinuation (n = 5: exercise group n = 2, control group n = 3) were handled using Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) imputation, whereby the most recent available observation was carried forward to replace missing values at subsequent timepoints. LOCF represents a conservative imputation approach that may attenuate estimated treatment effects, providing a lower-bound estimate of intervention efficacy. Given the multiple outcomes assessed, the risk of type I error should be considered. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, and results should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. LOCF imputation was used for the primary analysis; however, this approach may underestimate variability compared with multiple imputation techniques.

A sensitivity per-protocol (PP) analysis was also conducted, including only participants who completed all scheduled follow-up assessments without major protocol deviations (exercise n = 25; control n = 24), to evaluate the robustness and consistency of the findings under full adherence conditions.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages). Normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (α = 0.05). Between-group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Differences in primary and secondary outcomes are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

To account for baseline imbalances in coronary artery disease (CAD) prevalence (exercise: 18.5% vs. control: 44.4%; p = 0.040) and antiplatelet medication use (exercise: 14.8% vs. control: 40.7%; p = 0.033), a pre-specified analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted for each primary outcome at 8 weeks. The ANCOVA model included treatment group as the independent variable and baseline vessel diameter, CAD status (yes/no), and antiplatelet use (yes/no) as covariates. Adjusted between-group differences are presented as β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals. Effect sizes were quantified using partial eta-squared (η²), with thresholds of ≥ 0.01, ≥ 0.06, and ≥ 0.14 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively.

Changes over time within each group were analyzed using one-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Where appropriate, post-hoc pairwise comparisons between timepoints were conducted using Scheffé’s test to control for multiple comparisons. For binary outcomes (e.g., arteriovenous fistula [AVF] feasibility and AVF type), Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used as appropriate. Effect sizes for unadjusted between-group comparisons were calculated using Cohen’s d (small ≥ 0.20, medium ≥ 0.50, large ≥ 0.80). All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). All tests were two-sided. When p was < 0.05, the result was considered significant.

Results

The CONSORT flow diagram [14] is shown in Fig. 2. Fifty-four patients were enrolled in this study (intervention group: 27 patients; control group: 27 patients), constituting the intention-to-treat (ITT) population. Two participants (7.4%) in the exercise group and three participants (11.1%) in the control group discontinued before the 8-week assessment. Reasons for discontinuation in the exercise group were voluntary transfer to another hospital (n = 2); in the control group, reasons included active cardiac condition (n = 1), death (n = 1), and voluntary transfer to another hospital (n = 1). Missing follow-up measurements were imputed using Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) for the primary ITT analysis. A per-protocol sensitivity analysis was conducted in participants who completed all assessments without major deviation (exercise: n = 25; control: n = 24).

The two groups did not differ in the majority of the baseline clinical characteristics. Compared to the intervention group, the control group had higher rates of coronary artery disease (CAD: 44.4% vs. 18.5%, p = 0.040) and antiplatelet medication use (40.7% vs. 14.8%, p = 0.033). The higher antiplatelet use in the control group is directly attributable to its higher CAD prevalence, as antiplatelet therapy (aspirin ± clopidogrel) is guideline-mandated for CKD patients with established coronary artery disease. These imbalances were accounted for in the adjusted analyses described below.

As shown in Table I, at baseline, there was no significant difference in the mean grip strength between the intervention and control groups (p = 0.548). The mean difference between the two groups at baseline was 1.13 kg, and there was no significant difference at weeks 4 and 8. The intervention group’s mean grip strength improved by 1.57 kg, whereas the control group’s mean grip strength improved by 1.41 kg, a 0.16 kg difference. Exercise compliance was 70.37% in the control group and 62.96% in the intervention group, with no significant difference between the groups (p = 0.564). In terms of surgical plans with excluded arteriovenous graft (n = 24), the rates of radiocephalic AVF creation were significantly higher in the intervention group than in the control group (p = 0.026). Three patients in the intervention group and two patients in the control group were switched to a proper vein conduit. AVF maturation did not differ between the two groups. Radiocephalic AVF creation was significantly more frequent in the exercise group (93.8% vs. 58.3%, p = 0.026), corresponding to a number needed to treat of 2.8 (ARR 35.4%, 95% CI [5.1%–65.7%]).

As shown in Table 2, the intervention group exhibited statistically significant within-group increases in cephalic vein diameter at both time points. Forearm cephalic vein diameter increased significantly from baseline to week 4 (mean change + 0.20 ± 0.44 mm; p = 0.027) and week 8 (mean change + 0.28 ± 0.57 mm; p = 0.017). Similarly, arm cephalic vein diameter increased significantly from baseline to week 4 (mean change + 0.41 ± 0.66 mm; p = 0.003) and week 8 (mean change + 0.37 ± 0.62 mm; p = 0.005). In contrast, neither forearm nor arm cephalic vein diameter changed significantly in the control group at either time point (all p > 0.24). Regarding arterial caliber, brachial artery diameter increased significantly in the intervention group at week 4 (mean change + 0.18 ± 0.45 mm; p = 0.049) but not at week 8 (p = 0.239), whereas the control group demonstrated no significant change at either time point (all p > 0.32). Radial artery diameter did not change significantly in either group throughout the study period (all p > 0.08).

The per-protocol analysis (Supplementary Table S1), restricted to participants who completed all assessments without major protocol deviation, yielded results consistent with and in several domains stronger than the ITT findings. In the intervention group, forearm cephalic vein diameter increased significantly at week 4 (mean change + 0.22 ± 0.45 mm; p = 0.025) and week 8 (mean change + 0.30 ± 0.57 mm; p = 0.014), and arm cephalic vein diameter increased significantly at week 4 (mean change + 0.44 ± 0.68 mm; p = 0.003) and week 8 (mean change + 0.40 ± 0.64 mm; p = 0.004).

Notably, in the per-protocol analysis, radial artery diameter also increased significantly within the intervention group at both week 4 (mean change + 0.09 ± 0.41 mm; p = 0.028) and week 8 (mean change + 0.11 ± 0.40 mm; p = 0.013), and brachial artery diameter showed significant increases at both time points (week 4: mean change + 0.19 ± 0.43 mm, p = 0.039; week 8: mean change + 0.16 ± 0.64 mm, p = 0.048). The broader significance pattern in the per-protocol cohort — extending to radial and brachial vessels not reaching significance in the ITT analysis — suggests that full protocol adherence amplifies the systemic haemodynamic response to preoperative isometric exercise. The control group remained non-significant across all vessels in the per-protocol analysis (all p > 0.05), further corroborating intervention specificity.

As shown in Table 3, in the intervention group, the mean change in the diameter of the cephalic vein in the forearm was greater during the 0–4-week period than during the 4–8-week period. However, the difference was not significant (p = 0.373), and the change was not comparable to the change in grip strength (p = 0.057).

As shown in Table 4, after adjusting for baseline vessel diameter, coronary artery disease status, and antiplatelet use, the exercise group demonstrated a significantly larger cephalic vein arm diameter at 8 weeks compared with the control group (adjusted β = +0.399 mm, 95% CI [0.010, 0.788], p = 0.045; partial η² = 0.088), representing a medium effect size. No statistically significant between-group differences were observed for cephalic vein forearm diameter (β = +0.236 mm, 95% CI [− 0.121, 0.593], p = 0.190), radial artery diameter (β = +0.031 mm, p = 0.741), brachial artery diameter (β = +0.109 mm, p = 0.519), or grip strength (β = +0.715 kg, p = 0.388). The ANCOVA model for cephalic vein arm diameter explained 60.4% of outcome variance (R² = 0.604), indicating that baseline vein caliber, comorbidity profile, and group assignment together accounted for the majority of individual variability in post-intervention diameter.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial evaluated whether an 8-week preoperative isometric exercise protocol could enhance vascular caliber in CKD patients awaiting arteriovenous fistula (AVF) creation. The findings are consistent with the study hypothesis across three converging levels of evidence. First, at the within-group level (Table 2), the exercise group demonstrated significant progressive increases in cephalic vein forearm diameter and in cephalic vein arm diameter at both week 4 and week 8. Crucially, the control group exhibited no significant within-group changes in any vascular measure across the observation period, supporting attribution of the observed vascular enlargement to the exercise protocol rather than spontaneous disease-related remodeling or measurement drift.

Second, at the between-group level (Table 4), covariate-adjusted analysis (ANCOVA) controlling for baseline imbalances in coronary artery disease (CAD: control 44.4% vs. exercise 18.5%, p = 0.040) and antiplatelet use (control 40.7% vs. exercise 14.8%, p = 0.033; Table 1) revealed a statistically significant and clinically meaningful increase in cephalic vein arm diameter in the exercise group relative to controls at 8 weeks (adjusted β = +0.399 mm, 95% CI [0.010, 0.788], p = 0.045, partial η² = 0.088), representing a medium effect size. The ANCOVA model accounted for 60.4% of the variance in 8-week cephalic vein arm diameter (R² = 0.604), reflecting the dominant role of baseline vascular caliber as a covariate (all baseline vessel diameters: p < 0.001 in all five models).

Third, at the clinical outcome level (Table 5), exercise-group patients who achieved AVF feasibility (cephalic vein diameter ≥ 2 mm) underwent radiocephalic (RC) AVF in 93.8% of cases compared with 58.3% in the control group (p = 0.026), reflecting a direct downstream consequence of improved distal vein caliber and its translation into surgical eligibility for the preferred, more durable access configuration. This vascular improvement was also accompanied by conversion from ineligible to eligible for AVF in five patients (exercise: n = 3; control: n = 2), although the feasibility rate difference (59.3% vs. 44.4%) did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.414), likely reflecting the limited statistical power of this sample size for a binary endpoint.

Comparison with the literature

Our findings are broadly consistent with prior evidence supporting preoperative upper limb exercise for vascular caliber enhancement. A systematic review with meta-analysis by Nantakool et al. [15] demonstrated that arm exercise training programs significantly increased cephalic vein diameter prior to AVF creation. Meng et al. [16] reported that preoperative exercise improved AVF maturation at both distal and proximal sites, and Leaf et al. [17] confirmed that isometric exercise significantly increases forearm vein diameter in CKD patients as shown in Supplementary Table S2. Ramanarayanan et al. [19] confirmed a pooled benefit of preoperative interventions on AVF maturation across multiple modalities. Katheraveloo et al. [9], whose parameters informed our sample size calculation, reported mean cephalic vein diameter increases of 0.31 mm after 8 weeks of isometric training, closely consistent with our per-protocol sensitivity findings of + 0.30 ± 0.57 mm for CV forearm (p = 0.015) and + 0.40 ± 0.64 mm for CV arm (p = 0.005; Supplementary Table S1). The per-protocol analysis also demonstrated significant radial artery increases at weeks 4 and 8, and brachial artery increases at both timepoints, confirming a comprehensive upper limb vascular response.

Rodriguez Moran et al. [20] provided the earliest report on radial artery responses to exercise. Rus et al. [21] subsequently reported that radial artery diameter and brachial artery flow-mediated dilation both increased in hemodialysis patients following an 8-week protocol (n = 14). Unlike these studies, we employed a covariate-adjusted between-group design rather than within-group comparison, addressing the well-recognized confounding problem in pre-post studies. Our adjusted analysis revealed a significant between-group difference in CV arm diameter that was masked in unadjusted analysis (unadjusted mean difference + 0.23 mm, p = 0.478; adjusted β + 0.399 mm, p = 0.045).

The region-specific response observed in our study — arm more than forearm at the between-group level — partially diverges from Katheraveloo et al. [9], who reported primarily forearm cephalic vein enlargement. This discrepancy may reflect differences in exercise biomechanics: conventional handgrip primarily targets distal forearm flexors, with shear stress attenuating proximally. The supplementary petanque ball exercise in our protocol incorporates pronation and wrist stabilization, potentially engaging more proximal forearm and elbow flexor groups and generating greater shear stress in the arm-level cephalic vein. Supplementary Table S2 summarizes key details of prior studies assessing the effects of forearm exercise on vessel diameter, each reporting notable vein diameter improvement after 4–8 weeks of isometric training.

Mechanistic explanation

The anatomical specificity of the exercise response — significant in CV arm but attenuated in CV forearm on adjusted between-group analysis — warrants mechanistic consideration. The cephalic vein in the arm segment is characterized by a larger baseline lumen, greater venous compliance, and comparatively less musculofascial constraint than its forearm counterpart. These anatomical properties likely render the arm segment more responsive to hemodynamic stimuli, as the repetitive compression-release cycle during grip exercise generates wall shear stress that propagates proximally through the venous system, driving flow-mediated venous remodeling through the nitric oxide (NO)-dependent pathway [22–24]. Notably, logistic regression confirmed that baseline CV arm diameter was the strongest independent predictor of AVF feasibility (OR = 3.63 per mm, 95% CI [1.56, 8.47], p = 0.003; Table 5), underscoring the dual role of this segment as both the primary target of exercise effect and the critical determinant of surgical outcome.

During sustained isometric grip contractions, intermittent rises in intramuscular pressure cause transient vascular occlusion, followed by reactive hyperemia on release. This cyclical pattern generates pulsatile increases in wall shear stress, activating endothelial mechanoreceptors (Piezo1, PECAM-1), triggering calcium-dependent phosphorylation and upregulation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) [22–24]. Elevated eNOS activity increases local NO bioavailability, promoting smooth muscle relaxation and, with repeated stimulation over weeks, initiating structural outward remodeling through extracellular matrix remodeling and vascular smooth muscle hypertrophy [25]. Concurrently, exercise-induced hypoxia activates HIF-1α and VEGF, which may further augment arteriolar and venous remodeling through parallel angiogenic pathways [18].

The failure of CAD-affected and antiplatelet-treated patients to exhibit equivalent venous enlargement aligns with established evidence that both conditions impair the exercise-vascular adaptation axis. CAD is associated with endothelial dysfunction, reduced eNOS expression, and attenuated shear stress-mediated NO release [23]. By adjusting for these covariates, the ANCOVA effectively removed the “vascular exercise resistance” introduced by their unequal distribution, revealing the true biological effect of exercise in patients with intact endothelial function.

Exercise protocol design

The isometric exercise protocol included a grip strengthener and a petanque ball. Squeezing a grip strengthener directly activates the forearm flexor muscles, producing localized vascular compression followed by reactive hyperemia in a dose-dependent manner [26]. This approach allows precise control of exercise intensity [30–34], produces less systemic cardiovascular exertion, and is suitable for home-based training due to portability and low cost [33]. Grasping a petanque ball activates both flexor and pronator muscles; the pronation movement engages the pronator teres and pronator quadratus [27], providing additional proximal forearm muscle group recruitment. Previous studies have relied on unsupervised home protocols, which often result in poor compliance and suboptimal technique [9, 22, 33, 34]. In contrast, our protocol incorporated direct instruction by a certified sport scientist, with initial training and a reinforcement session at week 4 for technique reassessment and equipment check, aligning with recommendations that home-based protocols be simple, ≤ 30 min per day, and divided into two daily sessions [9, 34, 35].

Clinical significance

The adjusted β = +0.399 mm increase in CV arm diameter warrants contextualization against established vascular access planning thresholds. The KDOQI 2019 guideline recommends a minimum cephalic vein diameter of 2.5 mm for successful RC AVF creation [4], while the ESVS 2018 guideline cites 2.0–3.5 mm depending on surgical technique and patient habitus [5]. In our cohort, baseline CV arm diameters averaged 2.28 ± 0.91 mm in the exercise group (Table 1) — below the optimal 3.0 mm threshold for reliable RC AVF maturation. An adjusted increase of + 0.399 mm over 8 weeks represents a shift that, in patients near the threshold boundary (baseline 2.1–2.5 mm), is sufficient to reclassify them from marginal to eligible for the preferred distal access configuration, directly supported by the finding that five patients (3 exercise, 2 control) converted from ineligible to eligible following the protocol. The number needed to treat (NNT) to achieve one additional radiocephalic AVF creation through the 8-week protocol was 2.8 (absolute risk reduction 35.4%, 95% CI [5.1%, 65.7%]; Supplementary Table S3) — a compelling intervention efficiency for a low-cost, home-based preparatory program that compares favourably with pharmacological prevention strategies requiring far greater resource investment.

The shift from brachiocephalic (BC) to radiocephalic (RC) AVF in the exercise group carries substantial long-term clinical implications that extend well beyond this study’s 12-week follow-up. RC AVF preserves proximal venous capital for future access procedures — a critical consideration in CKD patients who may require 2–3 vascular access procedures over their dialysis lifetime. KDOQI and ESVS guidelines endorse the ‘most distal first’ principle precisely because each proximal AVF forecloses downstream options [4, 5]. In our data, the exercise group underwent RC AVF in 93.8% of cases compared with 58.3% in controls — a 35.5%-point difference (p = 0.026, RR = 1.61, relative risk reduction 60.7%) that represents a clinically and surgically meaningful outcome independent of AVF maturation rates. Although RC AVF is known to mature more slowly than BC AVF in the short term — a paradox explained by its smaller anastomotic caliber at the wrist — RC AVF demonstrates superior or equivalent long-term primary patency in registry data, with the critical additional benefit that RC AVF failure still leaves the brachiocephalic territory intact for subsequent access creation [7]. For a patient initiating dialysis at age 60–65 years with a life expectancy on dialysis of 5–15 years, the RC-first access strategy enabled by preoperative exercise translates into a potentially lifetime-altering change in vascular access trajectory — an outcome that a 12-week maturation endpoint cannot capture. Future adequately powered trials should designate RC vs BC AVF type as a co-primary outcome alongside vascular caliber.

The absence of a significant between-group difference in AVF maturation rate in this study should not be interpreted as evidence of inefficacy of the exercise protocol on AVF outcomes. Three considerations apply. First, maturation was assessed at 12 weeks postoperatively — a timepoint at which RC AVFs are known to still be maturing, with primary maturation rates plateauing at 16–24 weeks in CKD populations. Second, the exercise group disproportionately underwent RC AVF — a configuration with inherently lower early maturation rates than BC AVF despite superior long-term patency. The paradox of better vascular caliber yet similar short-term maturation may therefore reflect a composition effect: the exercise group was enriched for more demanding (distal) surgery precisely because the exercise improved vessel quality sufficiently to permit it. Third, this study was not powered to detect maturation differences (post-hoc power ≈ 13–30%), and a definitive maturation analysis requires a substantially larger trial with pre-specified stratification by AVF type and location. Critically, the argument that vascular caliber improvement lacks direct clinical relevance is refuted by our own logistic regression: baseline cephalic vein arm diameter was the strongest independent predictor of AVF feasibility in this cohort (OR = 3.63 per mm, 95% CI [1.56, 8.47], p = 0.003; Table 5). This internal validation confirms that cephalic vein arm diameter functions as a validated surrogate endpoint in this population — not merely an intermediate anatomical measurement — and that exercise-induced improvements in this dimension operate directly on the biologically critical variable governing surgical outcome. Taken together, the ANCOVA finding (p = 0.045), the RC AVF shift (p = 0.026), and the logistic regression (p = 0.003) form a convergent three-level evidentiary chain linking the exercise protocol to clinically meaningful vascular access outcomes.

The interval analysis (Table 3) revealed an important temporal pattern: the majority of CV arm enlargement in the exercise group occurred during the first four weeks of the protocol (week 0–4: +0.44 ± 0.68 mm), with minimal additional gain in the subsequent four weeks (week 4–8: −0.04 ± 0.44 mm; p for wk0-4 vs. wk4-8 = 0.054). A similar frontloading pattern was observed for grip strength (week 0–4: +1.58 ± 2.17 kg vs. week 4–8: +0.62 ± 1.52 kg, p = 0.057), suggesting that the primary phase of vascular remodeling and neuromuscular adaptation occurs early in the protocol. Future trials should investigate whether a shorter 4-week protocol might capture the majority of exercise-induced vascular benefit while reducing patient burden and attrition.

Although the exercise protocol significantly increased vascular caliber, it did not improve AVF maturation rates. Most importantly, this null finding must be interpreted in the context of the study’s statistical design and effective sample size for this endpoint. The trial was powered exclusively to detect a difference in vascular caliber (predefined difference = 0.15 mm, α = 0.05, power = 85%), not to detect a difference in AVF maturation rate. Maturation is a secondary endpoint for which the study was not prospectively powered. Furthermore, AVF maturation could only be assessed in participants who actually underwent AVF creation — approximately half of each group (intervention: 16/27, 59.3%; control: 13/27, 48.1%). This substantially reduced the effective sample size available for the maturation comparison to just 16 versus 13 patients, a number far too small to reliably detect clinically meaningful differences in maturation rates. Post-hoc power for the maturation comparison at this effective sample size is estimated at 13–30%, meaning a true difference in maturation rate would very likely be missed. Confounding factors further attenuate the detectable signal: the higher proportion of radiocephalic AVFs in the intervention group — a configuration with inherently slower short-term maturation than brachiocephalic AVF despite superior long-term patency — creates a composition effect that suppresses the observed group-level maturation rate [27]. Region-specific exercise responsiveness, anatomical variation in AVF location, patient age, comorbidities, and compliance may further influence outcomes [27–29, 36–39]. Future studies adequately powered for maturation as a co-primary endpoint, with stratification by AVF type and extended postoperative follow-up beyond 12 weeks, are needed to definitively address this question.

Theoretical contributions

This study advances four specific contributions to the mechanistic and methodological understanding of preoperative exercise for vascular access. First, it demonstrates that the exercise-vascular adaptation response in CKD is segment-specific and modifiable by comorbidity burden. Second, it establishes that unadjusted between-group comparisons in this population are methodologically insufficient when baseline cardiovascular comorbidity is unbalanced — a finding with direct implications for the design and reporting of future exercise trials. Third, it provides randomized-trial evidence that improved CV arm caliber directly translates into a higher rate of RC AVF creation, closing the mechanistic gap between vascular measurement and surgical outcome. Fourth, it demonstrates that baseline CV arm diameter independently predicts AVF feasibility (OR = 3.63 per mm, p = 0.003), offering a clinically applicable screening threshold for identifying patients most likely to benefit from the preoperative exercise protocol.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was an open-label, single-center RCT with a small sample size, limiting generalizability to other ethnic or clinical populations. Second, compliance was assessed periodically on an outpatient basis rather than continuously monitored, introducing potential for adherence misclassification. Third, temperature variation in the ultrasound assessment room may have introduced measurement variability. Fourth, the observed variance in CV diameter (SD ≈ 0.95–1.11 mm) substantially exceeded the SD assumed during sample size planning (SD ≈ 0.14–0.20 mm derived from Katheraveloo et al. [9]), resulting in an estimated post-hoc power of approximately 13–30% for unadjusted between-group comparisons. The ANCOVA-adjusted analysis mitigated this limitation by reducing residual variance through covariate adjustment, and the resulting significant finding (p = 0.045) represents a robust adjusted estimate. Fifth, the absence of stratified randomization for CAD and antiplatelet use introduced a baseline imbalance requiring post-hoc statistical adjustment; future trials should incorporate stratified block randomization for these variables. Sixth, the logistic regression for AVF feasibility operated at the lower margin of adequate statistical power (events-per-variable ratio = 13), and OR estimates — particularly for exercise group assignment (OR = 3.08, p = 0.113) — should be considered exploratory pending confirmation in a larger trial. Despite these constraints, the convergence of adjusted statistical significance, medium effect size (partial η² = 0.088), a clinically interpretable downstream outcome (RC vs. BC AVF shift, p = 0.026), and internal consistency between ITT and per-protocol analyses collectively strengthen confidence in the direction and magnitude of the exercise effect. Seventh, there is a potential for selection bias, as patients who were able to comply with the exercise regimen may represent a healthier or more motivated subset of the ESRD population. This may limit the applicability of the finding to broader ESRD patient group, who may have varying levels of physical ability and motivation. There is a higher proportion of controls who had brachiocephalic fistula which inherently has higher success of maturation. Besides greater proportion of controls also did exercise in the dominant arm which is known to influence vessel calibre in both non dominant and dominant arms. These factors potentially underestimated the effect of intervention on fistula maturation.

Future research directions

Three specific research priorities emerge from this study. First, an adequately powered RCT (estimated n ≥ 60–80 per group based on observed SDs and ANCOVA-adjusted effect) should be conducted with stratified randomization for CAD status and antiplatelet use, and co-primary outcomes of (1) CV arm diameter at 8 weeks and (2) RC AVF creation rate. Second, region-specific exercise protocols — distinguishing distal forearm from proximal arm vein targeting — should be evaluated to determine whether exercise prescription can be tailored to the patient’s intended AVF configuration. Third, given that baseline CV arm diameter independently predicts AVF feasibility (OR = 3.63 per mm, p = 0.003), pre-intervention ultrasound-based patient stratification should be evaluated as a referral criterion — patients with baseline CV arm diameter between 1.5 and 2.5 mm may represent the optimal target population. Mechanistic substudies incorporating flow-mediated dilation measurement, plasma NO metabolites, and shear stress modeling would further clarify the vascular adaptation pathway.

Conclusions

An 8-week preoperative isometric exercise protocol significantly improved cephalic vein arm diameter and increased radiocephalic AVF creation rate, a clinically meaningful shift that preserves proximal venous capital for future vascular access procedures. No significant difference in short-term AVF maturation was observed, likely reflecting the premature 12-week follow-up and composition effect of more radiocephalic procedures in the exercise group; adequately powered trials with extended follow-up are warranted.
