Mammalian adaptation and zoonotic risk of influenza A viruses in companion animals
Authors: Kyungmoon Lee, Daesub Song, Kwang-Soo Lyoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ImportanceSince the early 2000s, companion animals emerged as unexpected players in influenza A virus ecology. Canine influenza viruses and the increasing detection of highly pathogenic avian influenza viruses in cats have raised concerns about their potential role as intermediate hosts for pandemic emergence. Their unique position at human-animal interface creates unprecedented opportunities for viral evolution and bidirectional transmission between humans and animals.ObservationsThis review examined the transmission pathways and molecular adaptations of influenza A virus in companion animals. Cats primarily acquire infections through alimentary routes, including consumption of raw poultry and unpasteurized milk, as well as environmental exposure through hunting. Dogs transmit influenza viruses via respiratory droplets in high-density settings such as shelters and kennels. Canine influenza viruses demonstrate successful mammalian adaptation through accumulated mutations across multiple viral proteins, particularly in polymerase and hemagglutinin genes, enabling sustained dog-to-dog transmission. Feline isolates consistently exhibit mammalian adaptive mutations across geographically disparate outbreaks. Several molecular changes appear convergently in both species, suggesting shared evolutionary pressures at companion animal-human interface.Conclusions and RelevanceDespite molecular evidence of active viral evolution, companion animals currently pose a limited pandemic risk owing to no sustained zoonotic transmission chains. Critical knowledge gaps remain regarding subclinical infection frequency, natural transmission efficiency, and host genetic factors that influence susceptibility. Surveillance should prioritize high-risk interfaces, including raw pet food supply chains and veterinary facilities, while maintaining the perspective of actual versus theoretical risks. Understanding companion animal influenza virus dynamics is essential for comprehensive pandemic preparedness strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Influenza A viruses (IAVs), members of the Orthomyxoviridae family with segmented negative-sense RNA genomes, pose persistent pandemic threats through their ability to cross species barriers via reassortment and adaptive evolution [12]. These mechanisms have enabled IAVs to establish sustained transmission in diverse mammalian hosts beyond their primary wild aquatic bird reservoirs, including poultry, swine, and more recently, companion animals [345].

This paradigm shifted dramatically in the 21st century, when companion animals emerged as unexpected players in influenza virus dynamics. Until the early 2000s, companion animals were not considered significant hosts in the epidemiology of influenza viruses. This perception changed in 2004 when an equine-origin H3N8 IAV was identified in racing greyhounds in the United States (US), marking the first documented case of sustained IAV transmission in dogs [67]. In 2007, an avian-origin H3N2 virus emerged in South Korean dogs, likely via exposure to infected poultry [8]. The virus adapted to canine hosts and became enzootic in East Asia through sustained dog-to-dog transmission [910]. In 2015, the H3N2 canine influenza virus (CIV) was introduced in the US, where it triggered multiple kennel outbreaks and confirmed its capacity for sustained spread in canine populations outside Asia [11].

Although CIV represents an established but relatively contained threat, highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) virus H5N1 in cats poses immediate concerns because of its exceptional virulence and zoonotic potential [41213]. Although sporadic feline infections have been documented since the early 2000s, the emergence of clade 2.3.4.4b, a globally circulating HPAI H5N1 lineage with unprecedented host range expansion, has fundamentally altered the risk landscape [4141516]. Since 2022, fatal H5N1 infections have been reported in domestic cats in Europe, Asia, and North America [17181920212223242526]. In several cases, the isolates showed mammalian adaptive mutations, raising concerns about increased replication and potential transmissibility to mammals [17181920212223].

The unique position of companion animals at the human-animal interface creates unprecedented opportunities for viral evolution and transmission, with humans generally lacking pre-existing immunity to novel companion animal-adapted strains, as evidenced by low neutralizing antibody titers in population-based surveys [27]. The bidirectional nature of influenza virus transmission at this interface has been clearly demonstrated. Reverse zoonotic transmission occurred during the 2009 H1N1 pandemic when infected owners transmitted the virus to their pets [282930313233343536373839], while direct pet-to-human transmission has been documented in cases such as cat-origin H7N2 transmission to humans [40]. Although documented cases of pet-to-human transmission remain limited, these events illustrate the capacity for cross-species transmission in both directions. More concerning is the potential for companion animals to serve as “mixing vessels” where human and animal influenza viruses could reassort, potentially generating novel strains with pandemic potential.

Despite the growing evidence of IAV evolution in companion animals, critical knowledge gaps remain. We lack a comprehensive understanding of the transmission pathways enabling IAV establishment in companion animals, which mammalian-adaptive mutations have accumulated in these viruses, and the actual zoonotic potential of these accumulated mutations. Therefore, this review examines the primary transmission pathways that enable IAV spillover and establishment in companion animals, identifies the molecular adaptations that facilitate viral replication and potential sustained transmission in these hosts, and evaluates the actual versus theoretical pandemic risks posed by companion animal-adapted IAVs.

We synthesized evidence on the exposure routes, host-adaptive mutations, and zoonotic implications of IAVs, focusing on H5N1 and H3N2, while incorporating insights from other strains. By examining the evolutionary strategies in companion animals, we clarified their role in influenza virus dynamics and evaluated their potential as intermediate hosts for pandemic emergence. This review provides evidence-based guidance for surveillance priorities and intervention strategies at the companion animal-human interface.

METHODS

We conducted a narrative review of studies published between 2004 and early 2025, focusing on IAV infections in companion animals, particularly those involving HPAI H5N1 and avian-origin H3N2. Relevant literature was identified through PubMed, Google Scholar, and Scopus using combinations of terms such as “influenza A,” “H5N1,” “H3N2,” “canine influenza,” “feline influenza,” “mammalian adaptive,” “mutation,” and “zoonosis.” Additional reports describing sporadically detected strains, such as H3N8 and H7N2, were also examined. Studies were included if they addressed IAV infection in cats or dogs, reported relevant molecular, epidemiological, or clinical data, and were published in peer-reviewed journals. This review is organized into three key themes: exposure pathways, molecular adaptations to mammalian hosts, and zoonotic risk. These categories were used to frame the significance of recent outbreaks in companion animals within the broader context of IAV evolution and cross-species transmission.

OBSERVATIONS

Routes of IAV entry and spillover in companion animals

Companion animals, particularly cats and dogs, may acquire IAV infections via multiple exposure routes, including direct contact with environmental contaminants, infected animals, and ingestion of raw animal products with high viral loads (Fig. 1) [45].

Cats are primarily exposed to influenza A viruses through ingestion of raw poultry and unpasteurized milk from infected livestock, as well as by environmental contact with wild birds or contaminated surfaces. Dogs acquire infection mainly via respiratory droplets and fomites in high-density environments, and through direct or indirect contact with infected animals. In cats, alimentary exposure is frequently implicated as the primary transmission route for HPAI H5N1 virus, particularly for recent strains [1819202124]. In Poland and South Korea, several outbreaks have been associated with raw poultry-based diets [18192021]. In Poland, feeding history suggests exposure to fresh chicken meat, with H5N1 RNA identified in a sample collected from a household with infected cats [20]. Similarly, in South Korea, raw duck products and feline tissues tested positive for H5N1, confirming alimentary transmission [18].

The ingestion of unpasteurized milk or colostrum from infected cattle has also emerged as a significant exposure route. In 2024, several fatal feline infection clusters on US dairy farms were epidemiologically linked to this exposure route [2425]. Whole-genome sequencing showed high similarity between bovine and feline isolates, and milk-borne transmission was supported by histopathology and viral RNA detection in feline tissues [2425].

Environmental exposure also poses a risk, especially for outdoor or free-roaming cats that may scavenge bird carcasses or encounter contaminated surfaces [172641]. A serosurvey conducted in France between 2023 and 2025 detected H5 antibodies in 2.6% of outdoor cats, with an estimated seroprevalence of 1.8%. Seropositivity was strongly associated with stray status and hunting behavior, underscoring the role of environmental and alimentary exposure in subclinical feline infections [42].

Dogs are primarily exposed to influenza viruses through direct contact with infected animals, aerosolized respiratory droplets, or contaminated surfaces in high-density environments [43]. High-risk exposure pathways include close physical interactions with infected dogs in shelters, boarding facilities, and live animal markets, and indirect exposure through contaminated fomites and shared water or food sources [543].

Historically, two major subtypes of CIV have circulated in dog populations, H3N8 and H3N2 [51144]. H3N8 CIV, originating from equine influenza virus, was first identified in U.S. dogs in 2004 and subsequently spread across the United States—including Florida, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Colorado—as well as to the United Kingdom and Australia [44]. However, its transmission remained largely limited to geographically restricted outbreaks occurring within high-density shelters and kennels [1144]. As a result, H3N8 CIV failed to persist in the wider dog population and has not been detected since 2016, effectively becoming extinct [1144].

In contrast, the avian-origin H3N2 virus emerged in East Asia between 2005 and 2007, adapted to canine hosts, facilitating sustained dog-to-dog transmission and subsequently becoming endemic to specific regions of Asia [8910454647]. H3N2 CIV was introduced to the US in 2015 via imported dogs or contaminated materials, rapidly proliferating in densely populated canine environments, with phylogenetic evidence linking US strains to East Asian progenitors [48]. Since 2022, H3N2 is the only CIV subtype circulating globally, with repeated introductions from Asia continuing to cause localized outbreaks in North America, followed by regional extinction [1144].

Reverse zoonotic transmission—from humans to companion animals—has also been reported. During the 2009 H1N1 pandemic, numerous domestic cats and dogs acquired the 2009 pandemic H1N1(pdm09) infection from their owners through close contact and respiratory exposure in household settings [282930313233343536373839]. Although these specific spillover events did not result in sustained viral lineages in companion animals, they underscore the bidirectional nature of IAV transmission and the potential for transient spillover and reassortment events.

Adaptation and host range expansion of IAVs in companion animals

Dogs and cats are increasingly recognized as potential intermediate hosts for avian influenza viruses, with sustained transmission in dogs infected with avian-origin H3N2 and sporadic spillover in cats exposed to the H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b viruses [8910111718192021242526]. H3N2 CIV, which has established stable transmission cycles since its emergence, exemplifies how avian influenza virus can achieve endemicity in non-avian hosts through incremental adaptations that optimize replication and receptor binding within canine populations [11]. In contrast, feline H5N1 infections remain limited to sporadic spillover events [1718192021242526]. Despite the lack of sustained transmission, these infections exhibit marked pathogenicity, suggesting robust mammalian adaptation that enables efficient replication and high mortality [17181920212425].

Host-adaptive mutations in H3N2 CIVs

The H3N2 CIV adapts to mammalian hosts through specific molecular changes across its polymerase complex (PB2, PB1, and PA), hemagglutinin (HA), neuraminidase (NA), matrix proteins (M), and non-structural proteins (NS). These mutations enhance replication efficiency, virulence, immune evasion, and antiviral resistance, thereby facilitating stable transmission within canine populations and posing a zoonotic risk.

1. Polymerase activity and replication efficiency

The PB2 subunit drives host adaptation during canine H3N2 endemic transmission (Fig. 2 and Table 1). Within this subunit, L89V and G309D, originally identified in mouse-adapted H5N1 variants, enhance polymerase activity at mammalian temperatures, overcoming thermal barriers without the canonical E627K mutation [4950]. Studies showed that their synergistic effect increases virulence and polymerase efficiency in both canine and murine models [4950]. K251R, widely fixed in both H3N2 and H3N8 CIV lineages, amplifies replication and virulence in mammalians [5152]. The high prevalence of this mutation indicates that it plays a key role in canine host adaptation and endemicity establishment [51]. K389R, located within the cap-binding domain, enhances polymerase activity and viral transcription in mammalian cells by optimizing cap-snatching of host pre-mRNAs [4953]. In H7N9, this mutation improves viral growth in human cells by increasing synthesis of viral mRNA, cRNA, and vRNA [53]. K576E enhances the replication of H3N2 CIVs in mammalian hosts, particularly in mice [54]. G590S, prevalent in both H3N2 and H3N8 CIV lineages, enhances polymerase processivity under mammalian conditions [5155]. G590S fixation suggests strong positive selection in canine-adapted lineages; however, its functional effect appears to be context-dependent, varying with co-occurring polymerase mutations and host-specific factors [5156]. The synergistic interaction between PB2-E627K and PA-S184N mutations exemplifies cooperative adaptations that enhance polymerase activity, immune evasion, and pathogenicity in both mice and dogs [57]. S714I enhances polymerase function and pathogenicity; reversing this mutation (I714S) significantly reduces viral replication by impairing PB2 nuclear import and disrupting ribonucleoprotein assembly [5859].

The PB1 and PA subunits contribute crucial adaptations for viral replication and host adaptation (Fig. 2 and Table 1). PB1-D3V modulates polymerase activity in mammalian cells, and its effect varies according to strain [4960]. In H3N2, D3V enhances polymerase activity and viral replication, potentially optimizing RNA synthesis and viral fitness in mammalian hosts [49]. A reverse mutation (V3D) impairs the polymerase activity in H5N1, suggesting that D3V enhances replication [60]. D154G increases polymerase efficiency and promotes replication in mammalian respiratory tissues [56]. This mutation synergizes with G16S, G146S, and N188D mutations in the HA protein to drive aerosol transmission in ferrets [56]. D622G, in combination with V214I and S159N in HA, K389R in PB2, and N409S in PA, enhances replication in mammalian cells and facilitates transmission in guinea pigs [49]. The PB1-F2 protein variant N66S modulates immune responses by delaying type I interferon (IFN) activation, allowing immune evasion [61].

The PA segment mutations P224S, C241Y, and N409S contribute to mammalian adaptation. P224S enhances replication and pathogenicity, thereby increasing virulence in mice [4962]. When combined with A70V in H1N1, P224S substantially increases murine virulence [62]. C241Y, detected in both H3N2 and H5N1 strains, improves replication in mammalian lung epithelial cells [5163]. Its persistence across distinct lineages suggests a conserved mechanism of adaptation, thereby facilitating efficient host infection [5163]. N409S, which is frequently observed in H3N2 CIV, enhances polymerase activity in mammalian respiratory cells, paralleling H7N9 adaptations [4964].

2. Receptor binding and membrane fusion

HA is the primary determinant of host specificity and transmissibility (Fig. 2 and Table 1). G16S, which became dominant in H3N2 CIV since 2019, enhances HA acid and thermal stability, enabling efficient endosomal fusion and environmental survival [5665]. This ensures efficient viral fusion at lower pH and supports environmental survival, thereby increasing replication efficiency in mammalian cells [56]. This mutation enhances airborne transmission in ferrets, with complete infection rates [56]. G146S enhances binding to human-like α-2,6-linked sialic acid (SA) receptors, facilitating cross-species transmission [515665]. This mutation is associated with enhanced acid stability and elevated replication efficiency in human airway epithelial cells [5156]. Its fixation in CIV indicates strong evolutionary selection pressure for mammalian adaptation [56]. S159N enhances binding to human-like α-2,6-linked SA receptors, promoting replication in mammalian respiratory tissues [4966]. N188D enhances HA acidity and thermal stability, optimizes structural integrity, and facilitates membrane fusion [56]. Ferret models have confirmed that this mutation promotes aerosol transmission [56]. V214I enhances receptor-binding affinity for α-2,6-linked SA, supporting viral persistence and promoting efficient replication in canine cells [4967]. W222L, located within the 220-loop of the receptor binding domain, facilitates enhanced binding to α-2,3-linked SA, which are the predominant glycan receptors in the canine upper respiratory epithelium [68]. Glycan microarray analysis shows that L222 binds strongly to α-2,3-linked glycans in two-folds than its avian precursor [68]. V223I reduces binding to human-type α-2,6-linked SA while enhancing avian-type binding [65]. Their increased thermal stability has facilitated greater environmental durability, allowing variants that first appeared in canine H3N2 in 2018 to become dominant by 2023 [65].

3. Virulence and immune evasion

The M1, M2, and NS1 proteins contribute to additional adaptations for viral fitness and pathogenicity (Fig. 2 and Table 1). M1-V15I increases virulence in mammals and has been observed in both H3N2 and H3N8CIV, indicating convergent evolution [51]. Experimental studies in mouse models have demonstrated that viruses carrying the V15I substitution exhibit higher pathogenicity [69]. M1-N30D enhances pathogenicity in mice primarily through increased pulmonary replication and systemic dissemination [5170]. Combined with M1-T215A, M1-N30D synergistically amplifies virulence [70]. Individually, T215A improves viral assembly and nuclear export of ribonucleoproteins, supporting higher replication competence [5170]. M2-V27I, frequently observed in recent H3N2 CIVs, confers resistance to adamantane-class drugs and novel M2-S31N proton channel inhibitors [6571]. This conserved substitution restricts the binding of antiviral compounds by narrowing the channel pores [71]. Despite its role in reducing drug sensitivity, this mutation does not compromise viral replication fitness, allowing H3N2 CIV to maintain efficient replication in mammalian hosts [6571].

NS1-P42S, which is commonly observed in H3N2 CIV, increases pathogenicity by inhibiting IFN responses through NF-κB and IFN regulatory factor-3 antagonism [4972]. NS1-I106M enhances replication in mice by increasing cleavage and binding to polyadenylation specificity factor 30, thereby suppressing host mRNA processing [4973]. NS1-E227K likely improves IFN evasion; its fixation in H3N2 CIV and human-adapted viruses suggests evolutionary importance [5174].

Recent studies of HPAI H5N1 viruses have revealed critical molecular mutations that enhance their adaptability to mammalian hosts, particularly domestic cats. These adaptive mutations affect viral replication efficiency, tissue tropism, immune evasion, and zoonotic potential (Fig. 2 and Table 2).

1. Polymerase activity and temperature adaptation

Among the most notable PB2 protein mutations are E627K and D701N, which are established markers of mammalian adaptation [7576]. E627K significantly increases H5N1 polymerase activity in mammals by enabling efficient replication at the lower physiological temperatures of the mammalian upper respiratory tract, approximately 33°C, compared with the 41°C optimum in avian hosts [75]. Studies have demonstrated that E627K exhibits up to 20-fold increased polymerase activity in mammalian cells when compared with a wild-type (627E), resulting in accelerated replication particularly in nasal passages and respiratory tissues [7576]. This enhanced replication supports efficient transmission potential in mammalian hosts [7576].

The E627K is frequently observed in mammalian infections of H5N1, H7N9, and other IAVs, correlating with systemic infection and increased pathogenicity in spillover cases involving cats across France, Poland, and South Korea [17192077]. PB2-K526R has been detected in H5N1 isolates from cats in Poland and is responsible for human cases of both H5N1 and H7N9 [50]. Notably, viruses possessing both 526R and 627K mutations demonstrate synergistically enhanced replication efficiency in mammalian cells and increased pathogenicity in mouse models [78]. Field observations support these findings, including a H5N1 case in a French cats where the acquired both the PB2-E627K and NS2-E26G mutations, the latter potentially contributing to temperature adaptation [17].

D701N similarly enhances polymerase function by improving interactions with mammalian importin-α proteins, facilitating nuclear import and replication [7980]. Although E627K favors mammalian acidic nuclear phosphoprotein 32 (ANP32)B, D701N interacts with both ANP32A and ANP32B, thereby expanding the range of mammalian hosts, including swine, dogs, and horses [79]. Additionally, D701N reduces IFN-λ secretion in human alveolar cells, facilitating immune evasion and viral persistence, complemented by increased pathogenicity in animal models [80]. This mutation has been detected in diverse hosts, including recent H5N1 infections in South Korean cats [21].

Another pivotal mutation, PB2-T271A, enhanced viral replication in mammals. This mutation significantly increases polymerase efficiency, resulting in accelerated growth and elevated titers in respiratory tissues [81]. T271A enhances polymerase activity at mammalian temperatures, although not markedly increasing virulence compared with E627K [81]. This mutation has been identified in clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 strains, which cause asymptomatic infections in carnivorous pets, highlighting its capacity for subclinical spread [26]. Moreover, the presence of T271A in the pdm09 strain suggests that it plays a role in promoting efficient replication and asymptomatic transmission, potentially facilitating unnoticed dissemination [81]. Additional PB2 mutations, K389R and G590S, have also been identified in feline-adapted H5N1 viruses. K389R was consistently detected across all feline isolates, whereas G590S was specifically found in South Korean feline cases [1718202122].

Novel mutations F314L and L342Q in the PA subunit were identified in H5N1-infected cats with pronounced neurotropism [23]. While mutations at nearby sites, such as S343A and E347D, have been shown to enhance polymerase activity and virulence, the direct role of F314L and L342Q in neurotropism is unproven [2381]. Additionally, PB1-D3V, D154G, D622G, and PA-N409S mutations were consistently observed across all analyzed feline H5N1 isolates, further highlighting their potential role in mammalian adaptation [1718202122].

2. Receptor binding affinity

HA mutations at positions 133A, 154N, and 156A—observed consistently in feline isolates, including cases in the US—are known to enhance binding affinity toward α-2,6-linked SA receptors, potentially facilitating cross-species transmission [2282]. Furthermore, HA-T143A abolishes N141 glycosylation, exposing the antigenic site A and increasing neutralizing antibody sensitivity [83]. However, this change does not account for feline H5N1 neurotropism, as pseudovirus binding to cat brain and lung tissues was comparable for clade 2.2 and 2.3.4.4b H5 pseudoviruses [23]. Thus, the mechanism underlying the marked neurotropism in feline H5N1 infection likely involves factors beyond HA receptor-binding specificity [23].

3. Virulence and immune evasion

Significant virulence-related mutations also emerged within internal genes. The M1 protein mutations 30D, 43M, and 215A found in feline-adapted isolates from the US States align with the enhanced viral pathogenicity observed previously in mammalian models [227084]. Additionally, mutations in NS1 proteins P42S, 103F, and I106M contribute critically to mammalian host adaptation by effectively antagonizing innate immune responses and enhancing viral replication and pathogenicity, as demonstrated in various animal models [22].

DISCUSSION

Companion animals as bridge hosts in IAV ecology

Companion animals occupy a unique position in IAV ecology, serving as potential bridge hosts between wild agricultural animals and humans. IAV transmission in companion animals demonstrates bidirectional flow at the human-animal interface, with documented spillover events occurring from animals to humans and reverse zoonosis from humans to animals [282930313233343536373839]. This bidirectional transmission, exemplified by the 2009 H1N1 pandemic in companion animals, creates opportunities for viral reassortment between human- and animal-adapted strains. Accumulating evidence suggests these animals merit careful consideration in influenza virus surveillance and control strategies, though current data indicates their pandemic risk remains limited.

Species-specific transmission patterns and prevention strategies

Species-specific transmission routes revealed distinct risk profiles that can inform targeted preventive strategies. Cats show heightened vulnerability to alimentary transmission of HPAI H5N1 strains through the consumption of raw poultry and unpasteurized dairy products [18202124]. The 2024 US dairy farm outbreaks revealed milk-borne transmission as a novel pathway, with high genomic similarity between bovine and feline isolates, suggesting minimal adaptation barriers to cross-species transmission [2425]. Environmental exposure through hunting or scavenging represents another significant pathway, as evidenced by the higher seroprevalence in free-roaming cats [42].

Both milk-borne infection via contaminated bovine dairy products and environmental exposure through hunting or scavenging indicate that H5N1 virus infection in cats predominantly results from external sources rather than sustained cat-to-cat transmission [242542]. Although subclinical infections could theoretically serve as silent reservoirs, no evidence supports sustained intraspecies transmission—individual infections occur without establishing self-sustaining transmission chains. In contrast, CIV transmission in dogs primarily follows respiratory routes in high-density environments such as shelters, kennels, and veterinary clinics [4345464748]. These distinct exposure patterns require tailored interventions for each species. Prevention strategies for cats should address alimentary transmission risks through recommendations against raw poultry or unpasteurized dairy consumption combined with controlled outdoor access to reduce hunting and scavenging. For dogs, emphasis should be placed on reducing transmission in high-density settings through vaccination protocols, isolation of symptomatic animals, and enhanced hygiene practices [8586].

Molecular adaptations in companion animals

The repeated emergence of identical adaptive mutations in companion animal IAV isolates—across both geographically distant populations and diverse viral lineages—demonstrates that mammalian adaptation is governed by a restricted set of evolutionary pathways [171819205156]. These findings indicate that certain mutations represent essential molecular determinants for overcoming key species-specific barriers, such as the temperature sensitivity of avian-adapted polymerase and differences in host factors like ANP32 proteins [757987]. Thus, the apparent evolutionary constraint reflects not only the limited number of viable molecular solutions, but also underscores the critical role of specific adaptive mutations as necessary prerequisites for successful replication and transmission in mammalian hosts.

This adaptive process is exemplified in H3N2 CIV, where key mutations—including PB2-K251R and G590S, PA-C241Y, HA-G146S and N188D, M1-V15I, and NS1-E227K—were rapidly and robustly fixed in most major H3N2 CIV lineages [5156]. Notably, these mutations represent convergent evolutionary pathways previously observed in other mammalian-adapted influenza viruses, such as H3N8 CIV, human seasonal H3N2, and H1N1 strains [5156]. The concurrent fixation of these mutations, which collectively influence critical mammalian adaptation pathways including receptor-binding specificity, acid stability, polymerase activity, and antigenic variation, establishes a synergistic genetic background that could facilitate the emergence and fixation of additional adaptive mutations, further enhancing host adaptation, transmission efficiency, and potentially altering zoonotic risk.

Feline H5N1 isolates exhibit a distinct adaptive pattern predominantly characterized by polymerase complex mutations [17181920212326]. This likely reflects the unique receptor landscape in cats, where both avian-type (α-2,3) and human-type (α-2,6) SA receptors are strongly co-expressed in major tissues such as the lungs and brain, and α-2,6 receptors are predominant in the gastrointestinal tract, with localized expression of α-2,3 receptors also observed [23]. This receptor distribution may lessen the selective pressure for HA adaptation. Despite broad polymerase compatibility with feline ANP32 proteins demonstrated in vitro, the recurrent emergence of polymerase mutations in these isolates remains unexplained, underscoring the need for further investigation [87]. Future studies should clarify the in vivo expression patterns of feline ANP32 splice variants, delineate the molecular interactions that drive host adaptation, and consider the influence of additional host cofactors. Notably, neurological symptoms in H5N1-infected cats remain unexplained by known viral mutations, highlighting the limitations of current genetic markers in accounting for the observed neurotropism and pathogenicity [23]. Previous studies indicate that neurotropism is not conferred by a single segment mutation, but rather results from the synergistic effects of multiple segment mutations [88]. Further research is therefore needed to dissect the multifactorial basis of these clinical outcomes, including the roles of infection route, viral dose, host immune status, and ecological or stochastic factors.

The identification of shared mutations across both canine and feline isolates, including PB2-K389R, G590S, PB1-D3V, D154G, D622G, PA-N409S, M1-30D, 43M, 215A, and NS1-42S, 103F, and 106M, highlights convergent evolutionary trajectories [21224956]. This pattern suggests that feline and canine hosts exert analogous molecular selection pressures, facilitating parallel adaptive trajectories in avian-origin influenza viruses. Such convergence may potentiate viral host range expansion and promote interspecies transmission, particularly in environments with frequent interspecies contact, particularly in shelters, veterinary clinics, and households where cats and dogs commonly share close contact.

Beyond these molecular adaptations, cats in particular demonstrate intermediate host potential through documented bidirectional transmission capabilities, not only transmitting cat-origin H7N2 to humans but also acquiring H3N2 CIV from dogs, illustrating their unique capacity to bridge viral transmission between multiple species [8990].

Pandemic risk assessment

Despite clear molecular evidence demonstrating active viral evolution, the current pandemic risk posed by companion animals remains limited. Most importantly, even the most concerning H5N1 virus has not demonstrated sustained transmission in cats or dogs, preventing the establishment of a true reservoir population [91]. The absence of sustained chains of transmission suggests that while companion animals can acquire and shed adapted viruses, they may lack the population dynamics necessary to maintain endemic circulation.

In contrast, H3N2 CIV has progressively acquired human-adaptive features through stepwise evolution and now circulates endemically in dog populations, raising theoretical concerns about human infection [49515659]. However, molecular adaptation alone does not guarantee efficient cross-species transmission; additional epidemiological bottlenecks and environmental barriers—such as the dynamics of human-dog interactions and prevailing hygiene standards—likely limit the occurrence of human infections. To date, there are no confirmed cases of natural H3N2 CIV infection in humans, and evidence from animal models cannot be directly extrapolated to human-to-human transmissibility. Current evidence suggests that, despite molecular adaptation, H3N2 CIV is not yet equipped to efficiently cross the species barrier to humans.

Pandemic risk assessments must integrate multiple factors beyond molecular markers. Critical research priorities include establishing the frequency and duration of subclinical infections in companion animals, determining transmission efficiency between pets in natural settings, and understanding host genetic factors that affect susceptibility and transmission; this could explain why some animals become infected while others remain resistant. Finally, developing companion animal-specific experimental models, such as canine respiratory organoids, ex vivo cultures, or field-based studies, would enable more accurate validation of mutation effects than the current reliance on ferret and mouse models [92939495]. Advances in computational structural biology and artificial intelligence-based protein modeling could further enhance our ability to predict the functional consequences of observed mutations in authentic host contexts [96].

Surveillance strategies should prioritize systematic monitoring of recognized risk interfaces, including raw pet food supply chains, veterinary facilities, and households with confirmed human influenza cases. The integration of companion animal surveillance with existing human and agricultural monitoring systems could provide an early warning of viral evolution while maintaining an appropriate perspective on actual transmission risks.

In conclusion, companion animals represent important, but currently limited participants in IAV ecology. Their unique position at the human-animal interface and demonstrated capacity for viral adaptation warrant continued attention, although current evidence does not support their classification as a major pandemic threat. Effective risk management requires vigilance while avoiding overinterpretation of molecular findings in the absence of an epidemiological impact. By acknowledging the potential risks and current limitations of our understanding, we can develop proportionate responses that protect both human and animal health without unnecessary alarms.
