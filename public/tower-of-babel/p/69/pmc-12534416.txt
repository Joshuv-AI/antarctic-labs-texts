Exploring colostrum microbiota and its influence on early calf gut microbiota development using full-length 16S rRNA gene metabarcoding
Authors: Leire Urrutia‑Angulo, Medelin Ocejo, Timur Yergaliyev, Beatriz Oporto, Gorka Aduriz, Amélia Camarinha-Silva, Ana Hurtado
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe early gut microbiota of calves is seeded by colostrum and shaped by diet, environment, disease, and antibiotic treatments. This study analyzed the colostrum microbiota of 42 cows and tracked their calves’ gut microbiota during early life (days d1, d16, and d57), assessing the impact of antimicrobial dry cow therapy and infection treatments. The full-length 16S rRNA gene was sequenced using Oxford Nanopore, enabling taxonomic classification down to species level. Microbial richness and diversity were lowest at d1 and increased afterwards. Beta diversity analysis showed that d16 samples had microbial profiles intermediate to those of d1 and d57. The most abundant phyla (Pseudomonadota, Bacillota, and Bacteroidota) were common to all sample categories, while genus-level composition showed greater variability. Colostrum was dominated by Paraclostridium, Romboutsia, and Staphylococcus, while Escherichia/Shigella and Clostridium were more abundant in d1 feces, later replaced by Succinivibrio and Faecalibacterium at d16 and d57. Notably, 56.2% of species in d1 feces were also present in colostrum, and 37.4% of colostrum species persisted in feces at d57, highlighting colostrum´s role in bacterial gut colonization. Interindividual variability in gut microbiota decreased over time as richness and diversity increased. Introduction

Bovine colostrum, the first milk secreted by cows after calving, is generally recognized for its crucial role in providing the newborn calf with essential nutrition and immune protection. Calves are immunologically naïve at birth and rely on the immunoglobulins present in the colostrum for protection against pathogens during the initial weeks of life1. They need to ingest sufficient colostrum as soon as possible before intestinal permeability decreases (12–24 h after birth) for successful passive immunization2. The microbiota present in colostrum also plays a significant role in the development of the immune system and the health of newborn calves, contributing to passive immunity and facilitating gut colonization and establishment of the microbiota3. In addition, the oligosaccharides naturally present in colostrum —such as galactosyl-lactase, sialylated oligosaccharides, and fucosylated oligosaccharides— help shape the early gut microbiota by promoting beneficial bacteria (e.g., Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus) and inhibiting pathogen colonization through pH reduction and competition for epithelial binding sites4. Colostrum, together with the dam´s vaginal canal, skin, saliva, and fecal material, serve as an important maternal source of the microorganisms that contribute to the initial colonization of the calf´s gut microbiota5. The combination of these microbial exposures with other factors, such as farm environment, feeding practices, and antibiotic treatments, further shapes the gut microbiota of calves6. This early microbial exposure stimulates the development of gut-associated lymphoid tissues (GALT) and modulates the calf immune response, promoting tolerance to beneficial microbes while enabling defense against pathogens7.

In calves’ feces, Escherichia/Shigella are the predominant genera during the first hours of life8. Dietary changes, particularly the transition from a milk-based diet to solid food, and environmental factors trigger significant changes in the composition of the intestinal microbiota of calves9,10. The microbial community becomes more complex and diverse, with a notable increase in the abundance of fiber-degrading bacteria as the calf’s diet incorporates more plant-based materials11. These changes are crucial for the efficient digestion of complex carbohydrates and the overall health of the host.

The use of antibiotics in the dairy industry is widespread to treat infections in both calves and adult cows. In addition, antibiotics may still be employed during the drying-off period, a crucial phase at the end of lactation when cows are not milked to allow udder tissue to rest and recover before the next lactation. During this period, antimicrobial treatment can be administered to cows at risk of mastitis as part of the farm´s mastitis control program. Antibiotics used in dry cow therapy (DCT) can alter the microbial diversity in colostrum and milk12. This alteration in the microbiota can extend to the intestinal microbiota of calves, as antibiotic residues in colostrum and milk can select for antibiotic-resistant bacteria13. In calves, antibiotics often reduce microbial diversity and can lead to an overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria —including Campylobacter, Pseudomonas, and Chlamydophila spp.—and a decrease in beneficial taxa like Faecalibacterium, which may cause or exacerbate conditions like diarrhea14. In addition, overuse of antimicrobials increases the risk of spreading antimicrobial resistance genes15.

Despite the importance of gastrointestinal tract colonization in calves, relatively few studies have monitored the microbiota from the time of initial colostrum ingestion until 14–60 days of age6,16,17. The aim of this study was to characterize the microbiome of colostrum collected from the dams and the feces of their calves during their first two months of life to investigate any potential effects of the colostrum microbiota on the development of the gut microbiota of calves in early postnatal life. Furthermore, we investigated the effect of antimicrobial DCT on both colostrum and calves´ fecal microbiota, as well as the impact of antibiotic treatments administered to calves on their fecal microbiota.

Results

Conventional bacteriological methods were used to isolate the main bacterial species present in colostrum and fecal samples from calves with diarrhea

The most frequently isolated microorganisms from colostrum samples were Bacillus spp. (38.1%), non-aureus staphylococci (38.1%), and Corynebacterium spp. (11.9%), either alone or in combination with other microorganisms (Supplementary Table S1). Conventional culture methods failed to isolate any microorganism in 33.3% of the colostrum samples, either due to absence of growth or contamination (growth of more than two different microorganisms). In two of the 14 colostrum samples analyzed, mesophilic aerobic bacteria exceeded 100,000 cfu/mL. The highest count (3,000,000 cfu/mL) corresponded to a sample where Pseudomonas aeruginosa was isolated, and another sample (140,000 cfu/mL) was considered contaminated. Visible signs of diarrhea were observed in four fecal samples of calves on d1 (9.5%), with Escherichia coli being isolated in all of them and three testing also positive for Rotavirus; all were negative for Coronavirus, enterotoxigenic E. coli (F5), and Cryptosporidium. On d16, 37.2% (n = 16) of the samples showed abnormal stool consistency, and they all tested positive to E. coli, identifying enterotoxigenic E. coli (F5) in two of them, with some samples being also positive to Cryptosporidium, Rotavirus, and Coronavirus. In the last sampling (d57), six samples (14.0%) showed signs of diarrhea; E. coli was isolated in all of them, alongside Bacillus spp. in three of them, two also in combination with Coronavirus (Supplementary Table S1).

Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) sequencing captured sample biodiversity and allowed identification at species level

The rarefaction curves reached a plateau at approximately 10,000 reads (Supplementary Fig. S1), indicating that most of the biodiversity within the samples was captured and that the sequencing depth was therefore adequate. After excluding four colostrum samples below the 10,000 reads threshold and applying taxa filtering, a total of 8,511,829 reads remained with a median of 53,317 reads per sample (IQR = 47,081–58,118) and an average QScore of 20.1. A total of 544 species were identified, corresponding to 297 genera, 112 families, 58 orders, 31 classes, and 16 phyla.

Bacterial population diversity varied between sample categories, but no effect associated with the antimicrobial treatment was observed

In general, species richness and evenness (Bias-corrected Chao1 and Shannon indices) varied not only between sample types but also along time points within fecal samples (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1a). The only exceptions were comparisons between colostrum and d57 fecal samples which were not significantly different in terms of alpha diversity. The lowest richness and evenness were observed in d1 fecal samples followed by d16, with colostrum and d57 showing higher diversity. Antimicrobial treatments (DCT of dams and treatments of calves) did not produce any effect on the bacterial community richness or evenness in any of the sample categories.

Regarding the microbial composition, significant differences in beta diversity were observed between all sample categories when compared pairwise (PERMANOVA, R2 = 0.415, F = 38.391, p = 0.001) and a clear clustering of groups was observed in the PCoA plot (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Table S2). There was also significant variability in community dispersion within each sample category (betadisper test, p = 0.03), and colostrum samples were the ones that showed the greatest variability with an average dispersion of 0.51. The bacterial community composition in d16 fecal samples showed an intermediate profile between d1 and d57 fecal samples, with some samples still resembling d1, while others had already shifted towards a composition pattern more similar to that found on d57. Antimicrobial treatments (DCT of dams and treatments of calves) were not associated with significant differences in the microbial composition within each sample category (PERMANOVA: colostrum R2 = 0.021, F = 0.761, p = 0.827; d1 R2 = 0.016, F = 0.686, p = 0.586; d16 R2 = 0.012, F = 0.483, p = 0.960; d57 R2 = 0.023, F = 0.960, p = 0.510). No significant differences in community dispersion were observed either.

Bacterial community composition differed between sample categories and changed with age

The same three phyla were consistently among the most abundant in the different sample categories (Supplementary Fig. S2). In each sample category, only two of them exceeded 10% relative abundance. Pseudomonadota was the most abundant phylum on d1 (62.0%), but its relative abundance decreased with age and was replaced by the phyla Bacillota and Bacteroidota, which together accounted for nearly 81% of the relative abundance by d57. Bacillota was the most abundant phylum in samples of colostrum (68.7%), d16 (74.2%), and d57 (70.2%), and the second most abundant on d1 (35.9%). Notably, Actinomycetota was the third most abundant phylum in colostrum (4.4%), followed closely by Bacteroidota in fourth position (4.1%).

At the genus level, greater diversity in microbial composition was observed among sample categories (Supplementary Table S3, Fig. 2). There was also notable inter-individual variability within each sample category, both in terms of the genera detected and their relative abundances. On d1, Escherichia was the predominant genus, with a mean relative abundance of 42.1%. On d16, Faecalibacterium became the dominant genus (32.6%) and, although its abundance decreased, it was still the most abundant genus on d57 (10.6%). Succinivibrio showed relatively consistent abundance levels on d16 and d57.

Stacked bar-plot illustrating the relative abundance of bacterial genera in each sample by category. The ten most abundant genera are color-coded as indicated in the legend and all other genera are grouped into "Other”.

Nearly half of the 544 species identified (45.0%) were unique to each sample category, representing different proportions of the total species. Specifically, unique species accounted for 40.4% (138/342) of the species in colostrum, 24.2% (43/178) in d1, 7.9% (15/191) in d16, and 19.1% (49/256) in d57 (Supplementary Fig. S3). The d1 samples shared 56.2% (n = 100/178) of their species with colostrum, while colostrum shared the highest number of species with d57 samples (37.4%, 128/342). The d16 and d57 samples shared the highest number of species (n = 154), corresponding to 80.6% of all species in d16 and 60.2% in d57. Only 23 species were common to all four sample categories, 12 of which were species from the genera Clostridium, Acetivibrio, Escherichia, and Shigella (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Few species and genera were present with a relative abundance greater than 0.1% in at least 75% of the samples of each sample category (core microbiota)

Ten genera (Supplementary Fig. S4) and 11 species (Fig. 3) were identified as core taxa of colostrum, with Paraclostridium (present in 97% of samples), Romboutsia (92%), Guggenheimella (89%), and Clostridium (89%) being the most prevalent genera (Supplementary Fig. S4). Within fecal samples collected on d1, five genera and four species were identified, with the genera Escherichia (98%), Shigella (95%) and Clostridium (95%) being the most prevalent. On d16, seven genera and four species were identified, with Faecalibacterium and Mediterranibacter (93%), and Lactobacillus and Butyricicoccus (84%) being the most prevalent. On the last fecal sampling day (d57), the number of genera (n = 23) and species (n = 19) that constituted the core increased, and some genera were present in all (Faecalibacterium and Blautia; 100%), or nearly all (Duncaniella, Roseburia, and Phascolarctobacterium; 98%) samples (Fig. 4).

Fecal microbiota changed dynamically over time, differing among individual calves, with no consistent pattern

As fecal samples were periodically collected from the same animals, the development of the gut microbiota of calves could be individually monitored from birth to weaning in each animal. The mean trend of the relative abundance of the 20 most abundant genera in each sample category, along with individual values for each calf is represented in chronological order to illustrate fluctuations over time in Fig. 4. Individual-level fluctuations in microbial composition revealed high variability with no consistent shift pattern. Some genera present in the colostrum were not among the top 20 genera of d1 samples (Paraclostridium, Romboutsia, Sporobacter, and Staphylococcus), while others were present at higher abundance (Streptococcus, Shigella, Escherichia, Clostridium and Butyricicoccus). Likewise, genera that predominated in the fecal samples on d1 such as Butyricicoccus, Clostridium, Escherichia or Shigella were displaced by other genera that became more abundant and persisted in feces as time progressed (d16 and d57), such as Blautia, Faecalibacterium or Succinivibrio. Lactobacillus and Fusobacterium seemed to increase on d16 to disappear again on d57. Only the genera Duncaniella, Millionella, Oscillibacter, Roseburia, and Sporobacter showed an increase in feces from d16 to d57.

Differential abundance analysis showed temporal shifts in genera abundance among sample categories, with no effect of antimicrobial treatments

Global test with ANCOM-BC2 identified which genera were more represented across sample categories, and the extent of these differences (Supplementary Fig. S5). Christensenella was more abundant in colostrum compared to d1 fecal samples (Supplementary Fig. S5a) and Paraclostridium compared to d16 (Supplementary Fig. S5b) and d57 (Supplementary Fig. S5c). Escherichia was more abundant on d1 compared to all other samples categories. Additionally, another five genera were also more abundant on d1 than d57 (Butyricicoccus, Campylobacter, Prevotella, Shigella, and Streptococcus; Supplementary Fig. S5e). Beyond these categorical differences, the analysis of taxonomic trends over time revealed a significant linear increase in the abundance of Phocaeicola (lfc = 2.02 at d16) and Roseburia (lfc = 3.04 at d57) with age in fecal samples.

Globally, no genus showed significant differences in abundance between antimicrobial-treated groups and untreated groups (DCT for cows, antibiotics for calves), nor were any differences detected within sample categories based on these treatment groups.

The microbiota of the different sample categories formed clusters defined by distinct marker genera

Enterotyping analysis was conducted to explore the different microbial community structures within each sample category and identify their respective distinct marker genera. Three enterotypes were identified in colostrum, characterized by the marker genera Clostridium (enterotype 1), Lacicoccus (enterotype 2) and Lentimicrobium (enterotype 3) (Supplementary Fig. S6a). Among the genera with a relative abundance > 0.1%, seven differed significantly between enterotypes (Supplementary Fig. S6b). Atopostipes and Tissierella showed higher abundance in samples from enterotype 2, whereas Christensenella and Lentimicrobium were more abundant in enterotype 3 compared with the other enterotypes.

Fecal samples on d1 were divided into two enterotypes marked by the genera Escherichia and Butyricicoccus, respectively. Four enterotypes were found among the fecal samples on d16, with Faecalibacterium, Limosilactobacillus, Clostridium, and Succinivibrio being the respective marker genera. On d57, two enterotypes were identified, marked respectively by Intestinimonas and Faecalibacterium.

Individual factors had a greater influence on fecal microbiota of the calves than colostrum

One cow gave birth to twin calves, and their fecal microbiota profiles were compared to investigate the effect of the colostrum on the development of their individual gut microbiome. The colostrum produced by the dam was of low microbiological quality, as indicated by high mesophilic aerobes counts (3,000,000 cfu/mL) and the presence of P. aeruginosa detected by both microbiological culture and sequencing (relative abundance of 89.7%). Despite these microbiological findings the dam showed no clinical signs of disease. Both calves were fed with the dam’s colostrum within the first two and a half hours of life. The first twin, Calf38, remained healthy throughout the study and, therefore, did not receive any antibiotic treatment. In contrast, the second twin, Calf39, had diarrhea on d1, with E. coli being isolated from its feces by microbiological culture. At two days of age, Calf39 showed signs of infection and was treated with Marbofloxacin (Forcyl, Vetoquinol Especialidades Veterinarias S.A.). The fecal microbiota of the two calves differed over time from d1 (Supplementary Fig. S7). The most marked differences between the two individuals were seen in the genera Escherichia, Butyricicoccus and Shigella on d1; Faecalibacterium, Lactobacillus, Veillonella, Shigella and Streptococcus on d16; and Millionella and Succinivibrio on d57.

Discussion

This longitudinal study provides an overview of the colostrum microbiota of dams and the shift in the gut microbiota of their calves from birth to weaning, while also evaluating the effect of antimicrobial treatments on bacterial communities. To achieve this, 16S metabarcoding using long-read ONT sequencing was performed, offering a detailed view of bacterial community profiles. Although 16S ONT metabarcoding is increasingly used for bacterial community profiling in different samples and situations18,19, most studies investigating the links between the colostrum microbiota of dams and the intestinal microbiota of their calves have relied on partial 16S rRNA gene sequencing using Illumina platforms3,6,8. While long-read ONT sequencing has traditionally been associated with higher inherent error rates compared to short-read sequencing technologies like Illumina20, recent developments have significantly improved its accuracy21. This study achieved a mean QScore of 20, corresponding to an accuracy of 99%22. A major advantage of ONT is its ability to offer an affordable solution for sequencing the full-length of the 16S rRNA gene, overcoming a key limitation of short-read sequencing, thus allowing a more precise taxonomical assignation23. Nonetheless, species identification may still be challenging due to the reliance on 16S rRNA databases, which mainly contain partial gene sequences. Additionally, ONT sequencing data needs specialized taxonomic assignment tools, many of which are still under development and evaluation24. In this study, we used NanoCLUST, a tool that has demonstrated better performance than other programs in characterizing bacterial communities derived from ONT sequencing data, facilitating precise bacterial identification and more accurate abundance estimation down to the species level25,26. The NCBI 16S rRNA database, a curated and regularly updated resource, was used as a reference for taxonomic assignment27.

As reported here, the majority of studies that have explored the microbiota of bovine colostrum revealed a rich and diverse bacterial community dominated by the phyla Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes), Bacteroidota (formerly Bacteroidetes), Pseudomonadota (formerly Proteobacteria), and Actinomycetota (renamed from Actinobacteria)3,8,28–30. However, at genus level, there is less consensus in the literature, with notable differences observed between the different studies3,6,8,29,30. These discrepancies can be attributed to variations in the methodologies used in both dry and wet lab procedures, as well as to factors such as individual variability, management practices, and environmental factors. The effects of such factors tend to be more evident at lower taxonomical levels like genus or species, hampering comparisons between studies, whereas results at higher levels, like phylum or class, are more consistent. Nonetheless, sequencing technologies offer a significant advantage over conventional culture methods, which can only identify cultivable bacteria, overlooking the vast diversity of non-culturable genera and species, as well as those requiring specific growth conditions. As demonstrated in this study, sequencing identified a wider variety of bacteria than culture-based methods in both colostrum and calf fecal samples, providing a deeper insight into the microbial communities.

As in milk or colostrum8,30–32, studies have consistently identified Bacillota, Bacteroidota, and Pseudomonadota as the predominating phyla in the gut microbiome of calves10,11,16,32,33. These findings are consistent with our results. Moreover, the relative abundances of these phyla changed significantly with age, reflecting the physiological development of the calf, which may be influenced by factors such as weaning and disease conditions33,34. At 24–48 h of life, the phylum Pseudomonadota predominated in the feces, gradually decreasing as the phylum Bacillota increased, becoming dominant by day seven and remaining so even on day 16, as previously reported8. During weaning, Bacillota is reported to lose its dominance, being replaced by the phylum Bacteroidota35. Although this study did not extend to the weaning age of the calves, the last sampling time point (d57) was very close to weaning (60 days of age). At this age, an emerging increase in the phylum Bacteroidota was observed, along with a decrease in Bacillota and Pseudomonadota, indicating the early stages of this transitional phase, likely driven by changes in management and feeding practices.

The early microbial profile of calves’ gut is significantly influenced by multiple factors. Maternal sources of microorganisms include the colostrum, the vaginal canal, fecal material, and the skin and saliva of the dam5,8,36–38. In this study, calves had minimal contact with their dams after calving but they all received the first colostrum from their mothers. Afterward, calves were housed individually in igloos and were only co-housed with other age-mates around weaning (just around the last sampling). Therefore, microbial exposure was most likely the result of colostrum intake and calf management practices (i.e., the farm environment and contact with farm workers). Microbial composition of colostrum was different to that of calves’ feces, and its species richness and evenness was most similar to that of d57 feces. However, more than half of the species that composed the gut microbiota of calves on d1 were also present in the colostrum. This high percentage of shared species between colostrum and gut microbiota on d1 suggests that, although not the sole contributor, the colostrum may be an important source of early intestinal bacterial colonization. Increasing evidence supports the existence of an endogenous entero-mammary pathway, a mechanism by which immune cells —particularly dendritic cells— transport microbes from the maternal gut to the mammary gland39. This may explain how maternal intestinal microbes reach the colostrum and are then transferred to the calf’s gastrointestinal tract, contributing to early microbial seeding and immune priming in the neonate. Maternal gut health and microbiota composition during late gestation are therefore key to the health of calves. Practices that preserve or enrich the colostrum microbiota and promote the timely and hygienic collection of colostrum may further enhance microbial transfer and promote healthier development of the microbiota of neonates.

The gut microbiota in calves exhibited a highly dynamic pattern, characterized by individual variability and temporal shifts in microbial communities, particularly during the postnatal period, with no unique pattern among animals. Enterotyping analysis suggested that enterotype classification at a given time point did not predict the enterotype to which a calf would later evolve. The unique, individualized nature of the initial gut microbiota of each calf and the evolution observed in early life by others16 and our study, suggests that host genetic or epigenetic factors may influence microbiome development40. As animals grow and get exposed to additional sources of microorganisms by for example direct contact with other animals in the herd, their microbiota continues changing and increasing in complexity towards an adult microbiota with less inter-individual variability41,42. Despite the high degree of individual variation, diversity metrics provided consistent ecological trends. Alpha diversity increased over time, reflecting the natural maturation of the gut ecosystem. These shifts in richness and evenness, particularly between early and late timepoints, underscore the progressive microbial colonization and ecological stabilization that typically occurs as calves transition from a milk-based to a more complex diet.

The presence of shared genera in the colostrum and gut of calves suggests that colostrum, together with other maternal sources, is one of the early sources of intestinal microbiota in newborn calves. In this study, Clostridium was a prominent genus in both colostrum and d1 fecal samples; it was part of the core microbiota of both sample categories and was the third most prevalent genus on d1. The order Clostridiales has been identified as one of the most abundant members of the colostrum microbiota32. Moreover, higher proportions of Clostridium have been described in the gastrointestinal tract of calves fed with colostrum compared to those that were not, further supporting the role of colostrum as a possible source of Clostridium37,38. This genus has been identified in both healthy and diarrheic animals, although it has not always been possible to establish a correlation with health or disease38,43,44. However, some species such as C. perfringens or C. difficile have been associated with diarrhea and enteritis, especially in calves 1–10 days after birth43. In our study, C. perfringens was identified at low abundances on d1, d16, and d57 and we did not observe any differences between healthy and diarrheic animals or between animals treated or not treated with antimicrobials. Similarly, no association was found for other Clostridium species detected, possibly because they were non-pathogenic. Furthermore, a reduction in the relative abundance of the Clostridium genus was observed from 13.3% at d1 to 0.8% and 2.4% at d16 and d57, respectively. Acquired passive immunity has been seen to be negatively correlated with Clostridium which could explain why its abundance decreases over time36.

On d1, Escherichia and Shigella were the most abundant genera, formed part of the core microbiome, and were differentially more abundant compared to other samples. After birth, the gut microbiota undergoes a transition characterized by a decline in aerobic and facultative anaerobic taxa, which initially colonize the gut to create an anaerobic environment and facilitate the subsequent establishment of obligate anaerobic species45. In agreement with our results, high abundances of Escherichia/Shigella have been reported during the initial days of life, particularly between 0–24 h postpartum8. The abundance of these genera decreases as the microbial community stabilizes and becomes more dominated by other genera reflecting the shift towards a more mature microbiota9,45. Nevertheless, their persistence at higher levels has been linked to gastrointestinal disturbances, as they become more prevalent in the gut of affected calves44. We have not been able to associate the high levels of Escherichia/Shigella on d1 with diarrhea and none of the animals showed dysbiosis on d16 or d57 caused by these genera.

Lactobacillus spp., which was part of the core microbiome on d16 and differentially more abundant than in other samplings, also plays a key role in the gut microbiota of calves, as it increases during the first weeks of life, indicating a fundamental role in the early establishment of a healthy intestinal environment34,36. Various Lactobacillus species identified in this study, such as L. reuteri (recently renamed as Limosilactobacillus reuteri), L. johnsonii, and L. amylovorus, have been reported to be particularly prevalent in the intestinal tracts of healthy calves46. Notably, L. reuteri and L. johnsonii are known by their probiotic effect, improving gastrointestinal health in pre-weaned calves by promoting a beneficial and competitive gut microbiota that confers colonization resistance against pathogenic bacteria46. Faecalibacterium, the most abundant genus on d16 and d57 and member of the core microbiome, and Butyricicoccus, mainly identified on d1 and d16, play crucial roles in the early microbial development of calves, as they are linked to growth and health outcomes. Higher prevalences of Faecalibacterium spp. and Butyricicoccus spp. have been associated with lower incidence of diarrhea in calves, suggesting a protective role against gastrointestinal disturbances6,10. Additionally, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, one of the core species in fecal samples on d16 and d57, is a well-known commensal bacterium that contributes to gut health through its anti-inflammatory effect by inhibiting the activation of the NF-ĸB pathway and reducing the secretion of IL-847. Both Faecalibacterium and Butyricicoccus produce butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid that favors intestinal epithelial integrity and modulates immune responses. Their combined presence may synergistically enhance gut barrier function and reduce susceptibility to enteric infections, underscoring their potential probiotic value in calf health management6,10. However, we were unable to associate any of these microorganisms with the health status of the animals (healthy or treated with antibiotics). An experimental design with a larger number of samples per category and samplings at episodes of disease rather than at pre-established age periods could help to identify possible associations.

In this study, no changes associated to antibiotic treatments were observed on neither the colostrum nor the calf’s gut microbiota; the low R2 values obtained from PERMANOVA analyses indicated that treatment explained only a limited portion of the variation in microbial composition. The effect of DCT on bacterial communities remains unclear. Whereas a higher microbial diversity in colostrum from cows treated with antibiotics and lower relative abundance of mastitis causing pathogens in early lactation has been described12, other authors have not seen any effect on the microbial diversity or composition between treated and untreated groups48,49, as reported here. Antimicrobials administered to calves to treat diarrheal episodes could further delay or alter the natural progression of microbial diversity and stability, potentially prolonging the period of vulnerability to gastrointestinal disturbances in calves50. However, changes on microbial community composition and structure between treated and untreated calves were not significant at higher taxonomic levels33,51 or had minimal impacts on microbial composition and function over time52. These results suggest that while antimicrobial treatments may cause short-term changes, they generally do not produce lasting effects. In this study, the time elapsed from antimicrobial DCT administration at dry-off to calving (on average 83 days), may have been sufficient to reverse changes in the udder microbial composition. On the other hand, the study design, which included unbalanced treatment group sizes (particularly in calves), likely reduced our ability to detect small or subtle differences, thereby weakening the confidence in our conclusions regarding antimicrobial impact. As a result, the findings in beta diversity analyses should be interpreted carefully. Nevertheless, antimicrobial treatments increase the risk of development and spread of antimicrobial resistance gene (ARG)-carrying bacteria and its impact on the resistome of milk and/or calves’ gut cannot be ruled out12,53.

In conclusion, these results showed that colostrum, as an important early source of gut microbiota in newborn calves, plays a crucial role in establishing the intestinal microbiota during the first days of life — a period characterized by high interindividual variations in gut microbiota composition. These differences diminish as calves grow, with their microbiota becoming more diverse and uniform over time. Both colostrum and feces from animals of different ages show different microbial profiles that could not be associated with the antimicrobial treatments under the conditions of this study. If antimicrobial DCT or antibiotics administered to the calves had any effects on the bacterial composition of either colostrum or the gut microbiota, the time elapsed between treatment and sampling may have been sufficient to reverse potential changes. By integrating microbiome-informed strategies into calf management protocols, in the future, it may be possible to improve both short- and long-term health outcomes in calves while contributing to more sustainable and welfare-conscious farming practices.

Methods

Experimental design and sample collection

The study was conducted on a Holstein–Friesian dairy cattle farm in Navarra (Spain) with 650 lactating animals housed in open lots with a roofed shelter. In the free-stall sheltered area, straw bedding was used, which was aerated daily by turning over the surface (15–25 cm) with a rototiller. The open recreational area had a concrete floor where cow manure occasionally accumulated. Calves were separated from cows soon after birth to avoid suckling. They were initially housed individually in bedded igloos until 56–60 days of age before moving to group pens, organized by age. For mastitis control, selective antimicrobial therapy was applied to cows during the dry period, where only infected animals or animals at risk of infection received antibiotics, while for the rest of the animals only an internal teat sealant was used. Thus, only animals that had suffered an episode of clinical mastitis during the previous lactation or had somatic cell counts (SCC) above 200,000 cells/mL in any of the last five monthly controls were subjected to antimicrobial DCT (VIRBACTAN 150 mg intramammary ointment—150 mg Cefquinome; Virbac).

In this longitudinal study, conducted between September 2022 and May 2023, a total of 42 cows and their respective calves (n = 43, one cow had twin calves) were included. Of these cows, 16 received antimicrobials at dry-off (DCT) and 26 were not treated (NT) (Supplementary Table S4). Each calf, having ingested colostrum from its respective dam, was monitored for two months. Thus, two sample types, colostrum and calf feces, the latter sampled at three time points were collected and analyzed in four categories: colostrum (30 mL) collected by the staff of the farm from the four quarters of the dams within 1.5 h (± 1 h) after calving; and rectal fecal samples collected by the veterinary staff from the calves at 1.5 (± 11.2 h; d1), 16 (± 2 days; d16) and 57 (± 1 day; d57) days of age. Fresh fecal samples were collected from the rectum of each animal using a gloved hand. Calves were fed with their own mother’s colostrum (4 L) by gavage tube within their first two hours of life (± 1.3 h). For subsequent feedings (5 days, twice a day, 2 L/intake), a mixture of colostrum/transition milk (second and third milkings) from different cows was used. The calves were then fed a 50% skimmed milk powder-based milk replacer for calves (Celtilait PRIMVO, France), administered by the farm staff, and starter feed until weaning (60 days of age). All the samples (colostrum and feces) were sent refrigerated (4 °C) to the laboratory within 24 h of sampling.

The vaccination program implemented in the farm consisted of immunization against bovine viral diarrhea virus (Bovela, Boehringer Ingelheim), infectious bovine rhinotracheitis and bovine herpes virus-1 (Hiprabovis IBR Marker Live, Hipra), bovine respiratory syncytial virus (Rispoval, Zoetis) and enterotoxaemia (Miloxan, Boehringer Ingelheim). Rispoval was the only vaccine administered to the calves included in this study (at 15–20 days of life) since other vaccines were applied after 60 days of age. After the first fecal sample collection, all calves were treated with Parofor (140,000 IU of paromomycin activity; Huvepharma NV) to control gastrointestinal infections caused by Cryptosporidium parvum and E. coli. During the sampling period, 11 calves received antibiotic treatment at some point during the two-month follow-up to treat some infection under the supervision of the farm veterinarian (Supplementary Table S5).

Sample collection was carried out by the farm veterinary practitioners strictly following Spanish ethical guidelines and animal welfare regulations (Real Decreto 53/2013). Ethical review and approval were waived for this study, as all examinations and treatments of the animals occurred as professional activities of the farm veterinarian in the framework of his veterinary services and medical care. Written consent was obtained from the farm owners before the start of the study. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and complied with ARRIVE guidelines.

Microbiological culture

Colostrum samples were analyzed using conventional bacterial culture on COS Columbia agar with 5% sheep blood, incubated at 37ºC for 24 h. Additionally, enumeration of mesophilic aerobes was performed on 14 of the colostrum samples. Fecal samples displaying visible signs of diarrhea (liquid consistency and color alteration) underwent a coprological study. This included general microbiological culture in McConkey agar (37ºC, 24–48 h), detection of bovine Rotavirus, bovine Coronavirus, and enterotoxigenic E. coli (F5) using the commercial Rota-Corona-K99 Ag ELISA Test (IDEXX), and Ziehl–Neelsen staining to identify Cryptosporidium spp. Bacterial colonies were examined by Gram stain and identified by biochemical tests with a VITEK2 system (bioMeriéux).

Sample processing and high-throughput sequencing using Oxford Nanopore Technologies

Upon receipt, colostrum samples were processed using two centrifugation steps as a skimming treatment to prepare them for molecular analysis. Firstly, samples were centrifuged at 4,500 g for 30 min at 4 °C, followed by the removal of the cream and the supernatant. Then, the obtained pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of cold, sterile phosphate buffer saline (PBS) and subjected to a second centrifugation at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Again, the cream and the supernatant were discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in DNA/RNA Shield (Zymo Research Corp.) to stabilize the nucleic acids.

DNA from colostrum was extracted using ZymoBIOMICS DNA 96 MagBead Kit (Zymo Research Corp.) following the manufacturer´s protocol, on the KingFisher Flex robot (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Fecal samples were processed according to ZymoBIOMICS DNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research Corp.). Libraries were prepared according to the 1–24 16S Barcoding (SQK-16S024) protocol (Oxford-Nanopore Technologies, ONT). This step involved PCR amplification of the complete 16S rRNA gene from all the bacteria in the samples. Cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturing step at 95 °C for 1 min followed by 35 amplification cycles at 95 °C for 20 s, 55 °C for 30 s and 65 °C for 2 min, with a final extension at 65 °C for 5 min in an Applied Biosystems SimpliAmp thermocycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The DNA concentration was quantified with Qubit®2.0 (Invitrogen). Sequencing was carried out on R9 flow cells (FLO-MIN106D, ONT) using a MinION Mk1C instrument, aiming for approximately 50,000 reads per sample. The device parameters were set to achieve reads with a QScore greater than 8 and a fragment size ranging from 0.9 to 2.2 kb.

Bioinformatic and statistical analysis

Reads were base called on high accuracy mode (HAC), and barcodes trimmed using Guppy (v6.3.9 and v6.5.7), where ONT adapters were removed. Sequence quality was assessed with FastQC (v0.11.9) and the reports were combined using multiqc (v1.8 + galaxy1). Taxonomic assignment was performed with NanoCLUST25 using the NCBI 16S rRNA as the reference database. NanoCLUST clusters highly similar reads to reduce redundancy and sequencing noise and uses a centroid-based clustering approach that helps to minimize the inclusion of chimeric sequences.

Downstream analyses and graphical outputs were generated in R (v4.3.2) using various packages. Samples with fewer than 10,000 reads and bacterial taxa with fewer than 10 read counts across all the samples or those that were present in only one sample were removed using dplyr (v1.1.4)54. Rarefaction curves were constructed using the ranacapa package (v0.1.0)55 to assess the sequencing effort.

Microbial diversity was estimated with alpha and beta diversity metrics. For alpha diversity, species richness, and evenness estimations were calculated using bias-corrected Chao1 and Shannon indices, calculated with Phyloseq (v1.46.0)56. These metrics were compared across the different sample categories and treatment conditions (DCT in dams and antimicrobial treatment in calves) using a non-parametric Kruskal Wallis test followed by a post-hoc pairwise comparison with the Wilcoxon rank sum test to identify significant differences. Beta diversity analysis was assessed using the Bray Curtis distance matrix and visualized in a PCoA plot (phyloseq). Multivariate homogeneity of group dispersions was assessed using the betadisper function followed by a permutational test with the vegan R-package (v2.6–4)57 to evaluate variance within sample categories. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was then performed to evaluate differences in community composition, with p-values adjusted using the Holm method. PERMANOVA has been shown to be robust to certain degrees of heterogeneity in dispersion, particularly in balanced designs where group sizes are equal and differences between group centroids are pronounced58.

Microbial composition was illustrated using a stacked barplot, using the 10 most abundant genera within each sample category. To descriptively illustrate shifts in microbiota composition over time, ggplot2 (v3.4.4)59 was used to visualize the relative abundance of the 20 most abundant genera in the colostrum and corresponding fecal samples of each calf in the different time points. Additionally, enterotyping analysis was carried out to study the different clusters within each sample category, excluding those clusters that were composed of only one or two samples. The analysis of compositions of microbiomes with Bias Correction 2 (ANCOM-BC2) was performed to look for differentially abundant taxa among the different sample categories (R-package ANCOMBC v2.4.0)60,61. To account for the longitudinal nature of the dataset and repeated sampling from the same calves, we included Calf_ID as a random effect in the model using the rand_formula = "(1|Calf_ID)" argument. First, a global comparison was conducted across all sample categories using colostrum samples as the reference. Subsequently, a separate analysis was performed focusing exclusively on fecal samples, using d1 samples as the reference category. To evaluate monotonic patterns of change over time, a trend analysis was additionally performed by enabling the trend = TRUE argument in the model and specifying a linear contrast matrix in the trend_control parameter. This allowed the identification of taxa with a consistent monotonic abundance trend across fecal samples. Comparisons were also made between treatment groups (DCT in dams and antibiotic treatment in calves) to identify the most significant changes. The analysis was performed at the genus level using a log fold change (lfc) threshold of ± 2.0, for comparisons between sample categories and between treatment groups (DCT in dams and antibiotic treatment in calves) to identify the most significant changes. To detect differentially abundant genera between the enterotypes of the colostrum samples, the lfc threshold was lowered to ± 1.0 to detect less marked differences among genera with relative abundances > 0.1%. A p-value threshold of 0.05, adjusted for the false discovery rate using Holm’s correction, was applied.

The core genera and species within each sample category were identified and visualized in R using the microbiome (v1.24.0)62 and microbiomeutilities (v1.00.17)63 packages, respectively. Core features were defined as those present in at least 75% of the samples with a relative abundance greater than 0.1%. This combined criterion was chosen to capture both consistent presence and meaningful abundance in complex microbial communities. A Venn diagram (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/) was used to visualize the shared species among the different sample categories.

For compositional visualization and exploratory analysis, count data were normalized using Total Sum Scaling (TSS) to compute relative abundances. To descriptively illustrate general shifts in dominant genera between colostrum and fecal samples over time, mean trend plots of the 20 most abundant bacterial genera were generated by calculating the average relative abundance for each genus across all samples within each treatment group and time point, after TSS normalization. For differential abundance analysis, raw (untransformed) counts were used as input for the ANCOM-BC2 analysis, following the pipeline’s recommendation, as it incorporates its own internal normalization procedure.
