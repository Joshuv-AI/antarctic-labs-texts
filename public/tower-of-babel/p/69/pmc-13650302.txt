Motherhood’s immune legacy reshapes the breast for lasting cancer defence
Authors: P. Savas, B. Virassamy, M. Harris, S. Loi
Published: 2026

Resolving the ‘parity paradox’

The postpartum phase is a unique interval in which dramatic alterations occur in breast physiology and structure. When lactation ceases or if it does not occur after pregnancy, the mammary tissue undergoes a process of involution to return to a prepregnancy state. This physiological transition reshapes mammary biology and has effects on subsequent breast cancer risk.1,2 Breast cancers that do develop postpartum are also associated with poorer outcomes adjusted for stage, especially for estrogen receptor–negative cancers.3 This biologically vulnerable postpartum phase is distinct from gestational breast cancer, which is not associated with worse outcomes compared to age-matched nulliparous patients.4

There are complex relationships between reproductive history and postpartum breast cancer, modulated by the age at which pregnancy first occurs and the duration of breast feeding. These factors also display a subtype specific pattern. The largest and most comprehensive review of multiple epidemiological studies by Jung et al.5 found that the risk of breast cancer is elevated in the postpartum phase in all subtypes of breast cancer. For estrogen receptor–positive/luminal cancers and HER2-positive cancers, this risk decreases over time, eventually dropping below the risk of a nulliparous population at ∼10 years from birth. For triple-negative breast cancers (TNBC), this pattern also occurs, but the risk remains elevated above the nulliparous population for 25–30 years and notably never crosses over to reduced risk. Multiple births are associated with greater reductions in risk. Prior studies have shown that longer durations of breastfeeding are protective for breast cancer not accounting for subtype,6 but Jung et al.5 found that breastfeeding is protective only for TNBC, particularly with a duration of >6 months. Although data from diverse ethnicities and geographies are limited, the impact of reproductive history on breast cancer risk appears broadly applicable across populations.7

The biological basis for the paradoxical relationships between parity and breast cancer risk has been investigated on multiple fronts, including the epithelial and myoepithelial compartments,8, 9, 10 the immune microenvironment,11, 12, 13, 14, 15 and stromal architecture.16 Considering the now well-established importance of adaptive immunity in breast cancer, we sought to investigate in greater detail how parity and lactation modulate T-cell function in mammary tissue.17 We demonstrated that in normal breast tissue, pregnancy induces tissue-intrinsic memory CD8+ T cells with anti-breast cancer properties, particularly over the long-term. Parity and lactation positively modulated T cells in the mammary gland immune microenvironment, establishing durable anticancer surveillance that translated to reduced TNBC risk.

To uncover how parity and lactation influences the immune infiltrate in cancer-unaffected normal breast tissue, we analyzed a previously published comprehensive single-cell RNA-sequencing atlas derived from cancer-unaffected breast tissue.18 We observed that women at average risk of breast cancer with at least one full-term pregnancy before tissue donation had an increased frequency of CD8+ T cells and CD8+ T cells with a resident memory-like (TRM) phenotype. These findings were validated in normal breast from women at high risk of breast cancer who had undergone prophylactic risk-reducing mastectomies, with an analogous increase in CD8+ T cell and TRM-like cell frequency in parous compared with nulliparous women. In multiple murine models of pregnancy, we found that parous mice that underwent a complete cycle of lactation and involution showed increased frequency and number of CD8+ T cells and TRM-like cells in mammary tissue, relative to age-matched virgin controls. This increase in local breast CD8+ T cells occurred after involution and was associated with reduced TNBC tumor growth rates after syngeneic breast cancer challenge in the mammary glands, relative to virgin (nulliparous) control mice. Moreover, tumors from parous mice were enriched for CD8+ tumor infiltrating lymphocytes relative to virgin counterparts.

To examine whether T cells were essential for protection after involution, we challenged virgin and parous RAG2-/-IL2rg-/- mice (which lack T cells, B cells and natural killer cells) with breast cancer and observed loss of parity-induced protection in the immunodeficient model. Adoptive transfer of T cells into RAG2-/-IL2rg-/-mice before mating restored protection relative to virgin controls. Depletion of T cells with antibodies targeting CD8α and CD8β confirmed a definitive role for conventional CD8αβ + T cells in parity-induced breast cancer protection. Furthermore, depletion of CD4+ T cells further reduced TNBC tumor growth and enhanced antitumor immunosurveillance. Similarly, pharmacological blockade of T cell egress from lymph nodes abrogated parity-induced protection in mice, demonstrating a crucial requirement for circulating (systemic) T cells in the parous immune microenvironment. Analysis of estrogen receptor–negative/triple-negative primary breast cancers in diverse patient cohorts from Australia and Malaysia19 determined that a prior history of parity, and more specifically longer durations of breastfeeding, was associated with higher levels of lymphocyte infiltration in subsequent tumors and improved overall survival when compared with nulliparous women with breast cancer.

Proposed working model of parity and lactation-induced natural immunity

The sustained modulation of breast cancer risk following parity and lactation suggest these profound physiological stimuli promote a new immune homeostasis in mammary tissue. Following the initial phase of intensive breast tissue remodeling that occurs during pregnancy, high levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, such as TGF-β, TNF, and CXCL16, may cue precursor memory T cells to traffic to breast tissue and differentiate. Secretion of prolactin by the pituitary gland peaks at birth and may play a particular role in attracting T cells to mammary tissue by inducing secretion of chemokines, such as CCL2 and CXCL9, from epithelial cells.20,21 In parallel to T-cell recruitment, local tissue cues are necessary to prime T cells and maintain long-lived populations. This may result from self-antigen flux from mammary epithelial cells and lactation-associated changes in the microbiome. Conventional type 1 dendritic cells (DC), crucial for initiating robust CD8+ T-cell responses and antigen cross-presentation, are likely central to this process by capturing antigens and migrating to tumor-draining lymph nodes to prime conventional T cells.17 T-cell priming may be particularly prominent when lactation ceases and involution occurs.15,22 The end result at the completion of involution is an activated tissue-specific noncirculating CD8+ T-cell population programmed to reside in the native breast tissue and exist as tissue-resident memory (TRM)-like cells that can persist for decades. A subset of these T cells also seeds the systemic circulation as central memory T cells.17,23 These cells display modules of functional breast tissue residency with the capacity to engage in immunosurveillance of the mammary glands and produce rapid, local recall responses to eliminate preneoplastic epithelial cells and mount robust and persistent responses when invasive cancer does develop.

The durability of this tissue level immunity deserves particular attention. The cycle of mammary gland development followed by involution is a unique physiological stimulus that occurs over a relatively long period. This alone may be sufficient to orchestrate long-lived immune homeostasis of the kind we have observed. In this context, the enhanced protection seen with longer durations of breastfeeding may reflect a more prolonged stimulus for recruiting and priming T cells. Recent work also has highlighted the impressive longevity and youthful exuberance of T cells when supplied with the right stimuli.24 Permanent alterations in tissue architecture associated with parity such as reductions in breast density,25,26 or stromal disruption,16 also may promote persistence of local T cells.

Clinical implications

Our findings and the broader literature emphasize that reproductive history is an important determinant of breast cancer risk and outcomes. It appears likely that parity explains a significant proportion of the variation in tumor infiltrating lymphocytes seen across patients, which otherwise has remained obscure. Variation in other immune biomarkers, such as PD-L1 expression, may also be related to reproductive history, which in turn raises the possibility that parity, even many years remote to a breast cancer diagnosis, could influence the response to immunotherapy in TNBC. Clinical trials and prospective observational/translational studies would benefit from collecting and recording data about parity and lactation to enable such questions to be addressed.

Our findings also provide proof of principle that enhanced immune surveillance can prevent TNBC and that a transient stimulus can provide long-term protection. Unlike luminal cancers, TNBC lacks any effective preventative strategies. Lactation, despite its undeniable merits, is not a viable population-level preventative therapy. Women may choose not to breastfeed, or lack the opportunity due to medical, financial, or cultural factors. Furthermore, an increasing fraction of cancers are diagnosed before any planned childbearing. Further elucidation of the mechanisms behind the favorable mammary immune CD8+ T-cell homeostasis and the regulation of CD4 regulatory T-cell axes induced by parity and lactation could lead to strategies to promote and amplify these beneficial effects. We envision this may be possible through adoptive cellular therapy, DC-mediated immunization, hormonal manipulation, or even microbiome-based therapies. A greater focus on tissue-specific immunity may also have wider use in preventing other cancers.

Future research directions and perspectives

Several key research avenues require further investigation. A long-standing question has been the nature of the antigens found in normal breast tissue and breast cancers. Neoantigens arising from somatic mutations are infrequent in breast cancers,27 suggesting there may be breast tissue self-antigens with greater overlap between normal and malignant tissue.28 A challenge is the likely dynamic nature of self, semi-allogenic antigen assembly during mammary development and involution. Defining the immunopeptidome of breast cancer cells of origin and how this evolves with neoplastic transformation will be necessary to develop antigen-targeting approaches with preventative potential.

The local tissue cytokine and chemokine cues that attract and promote long-lived T cells and encourage tissue residency in the mammary tissue are another key question. Recently, it has been proposed that tissue-residency programs limit the ability of T cells to contribute to tumor control, which relies more on precursor exhausted T cells residing in lymph nodes.29 The function of tissue–lymph node immune circuits remains to be elucidated in the setting of parity. The extent to which beneficial tissue cues can be enhanced following pregnancy, or simulated without pregnancy, has relevance to preventative and immunotherapeutic avenues.

TNBC exhibits heterogeneity, with four generally recognized subtypes with differing putative cells of origin: basal-like immune suppressed/activated (luminal progenitor cells), mesenchymal (mammary stem cells or dedifferentiated luminal cells), and luminal androgen receptor (mature luminal cells).30, 31, 32, 33, 34 It is likely that parity has a differential impact on these TNBC subtypes related to the physiological functions of the cell of origin during mammary development and lactation. Further exploration of these relationships through model systems and large well annotated clinical cohorts will provide a more comprehensive view of mammary biology and carcinogenesis.

Taken as a whole, epidemiological, preclinical, and translational data indicate that the ‘paradox of parity’ arises from the profound effects of pregnancy and lactation on mammary biology and that these effects persist over multiple decades. A paradox signifies that better explanatory frameworks are required, and in that regard the parity paradox is a valuable window into mammary biology.
