Electrical Contacts to MoS2 with Covellite (CuS): The Contact Type and Schottky Barrier Modulation
Authors: Meng-Jia Huai, Yu-Meng Gao, Yue-Jiao Zhang, Nie-Wei Wang, Jiang-Long Wang, Xing-Qiang Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrical contacts between conventional metals and two-dimensional (2D) semiconductors often exhibit Fermi-level pinning (FLP), which limits the tunability of the Schottky barrier height (SBH). The use of covellite (CuS), a semimetallic binary compound electrode, shows the potential for mitigating FLP and advancing contact engineering. While experimental studies have reported n-type contact for the interface of CuS and MoS2, theoretical predictions suggest p-type contact for CuS interfaced with pristine MoS2. The current study demonstrates that the above discrepancy can be attributed to dopants such as hydrogencommonly present during material growthwhich induces n-type doping in MoS2. Furthermore, surface doping of CuS with iodine is shown to reduce the SBH by modifying the surface work function. Additionally, we introduce a more general computational approach to address asymmetric surface configurations in the SBH simulation, which arise from surface doping. By investigating CuS/MoS2 junctions with doped MoS2 and doped CuS surfaces, this study elucidates the origin of experimentally observed n-type contacts, proposes a strategy for FLP suppression in 2D electronic devices through semimetallic compound electrodes with tunable work functions, and hence provides guidance for the design of low-power nanoelectronic devices.

Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) have garnered significant interest for their applications in electronic and optoelectronic devices. − However, when conventional metal electrodes are brought into contact with TMDs, Fermi-level pinning (FLP) often occurs, which makes the Schottky barrier height (SBH) difficult to adjust and limits carrier injection efficiency. ,, FLP in conventional metal–MoS2 contacts typically results from the formation of the interface states. In contrast, semimetal or 2D metal electrodes in contact with MoS2 can effectively suppress FLP owing to their distinctive electronic properties. ,, Elemental semimetals such as bismuth (Bi) and antimony (Sb), which possess relatively low work functions, are known to form n-type contacts with MoS2. , Similarly, the binary compound covellite (CuS), which also exhibits semimetallic behavior, shows great promise for contact engineering with MoS2. Different surfaces of CuS exhibit varying work functions that can be further modulated through surface doping.

For the contact between the most stable CuS surface and pristine MoS2, theoretical calculations predict a p-type contact. In contrast, experimental studies have reported n-type behavior in CuS contacts with MoS2. , This discrepancy may arise from intrinsic defects in MoS2 or extrinsic doping by impurity atoms. However, work by Singh et al. demonstrates that intrinsic point defects in MoS2, such as sulfur vacancies, often assumed to cause n-type doping, actually act as electron compensation centers and do not lead to net n-type behavior. Instead, their findings indicate that hydrogen, which is ubiquitous in growth environments, is responsible for n-type doping in MoS2.

In the current work, to elucidate the origin of experimentally observed n-type contacts as mentioned above, we study the adsorption structure and electronic structure of MoS2 with hydrogen in metal–semiconductor junctions (MSJs) with CuS. Moreover, surface doping of CuS with iodine is shown to change the work function of the CuS surface and results in the reduction of SBH, and the SBH is tuned for several CuS/MoS2 MSJs with doped MoS2 and doped CuS surfaces. Surface doping introduces asymmetry in the surface slab models, and we introduce a more general computational approach to address this issue for the SBH calculation.

Methods

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed by using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP). , The projector-augmented wave (PAW) potentials were adopted to describe the core electrons. , The valence electron orbitals were expanded in a plane-wave basis with an energy cutoff of 400 eV. The generalized gradient approximation for exchange–correlation potential of the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof form (GGA-PBE) was used with the semiempirical DFT-D3 method used for including the van der Waals (vdW) interactions. The convergence criteria for energy and force were set to 10–6 eV and 0.03 eV/Å–1, respectively. The Monkhorst–Pack scheme k-point sampling of the Brillouin zone was used with a grid mesh of 3 × 3 × 1 for the supercell structures of CuS(0001)–(3 × 3)/MoS2–( 13×13 )R13.9° supercell. The vacuum separation in the vertical direction was larger than 15 Å to ensure that the interaction between periodic images in the surface slab model was negligible. Dipole corrections were used in the calculations. , In addition, the VASPKIT and VESTA software packages were used for electronic structure analysis.

Results and Discussion

CuS/MoS2 MSJ

Covellite (CuS) is derived from both natural deposits and artificial synthesis. Bulk CuS exhibits two structural phases: a higher-temperature hexagonal phase and a lower-temperature orthorhombic phase, with a transition temperature of ∼55 K, as confirmed by both experimental crystal structure refinement and theoretical calculations. ,, Considering that electronic devices typically operate at temperatures above 55 K, , the thermodynamically stable hexagonal structure was selected to investigate its interfacial contact with MoS2. The CuS crystallizes in a hexagonal structure with the space group P63/mmc. ,

Along the [0001] direction, CuS exhibits three distinct surface terminations: (1) the flat Cu–S surface, labeled as F­(Cu–S) in Figure a; (2) the puckered surface with exposed Cu atoms, labeled as P­(Cu); and (3) the puckered surface with exposed S atoms, labeled as P­(S). Among them, the F­(Cu–S) surface is the most stable one, with the surface energy of the second most stable P­(Cu) termination 3.6 times larger. In the following, we focus on the most stable F­(Cu–S) surface for its contact with MoS2.

Geometric and band structure of the CuS/MoS2 MSJ. (a) Side view of the junction. (b) Band structure of the MSJ projected onto the MoS2 layer. The size of circles indicates the magnitude of the projection.

The optimized lattice constant of monolayer MoS2 is 3.162 Å, which agrees well with that reported in the literature. The band structure of monolayer MoS2 indicates a band gap of ∼1.75 eV. The CuS/MoS2 junction was constructed using a CuS(0001)–(3 × 3)/MoS2–( 13×13 )R13.9° supercell, as shown in Figure a.

Figure b presents the projected band structure of the CuS/MoS2 junction onto the MoS2 layer. It shows that the Fermi level is positioned closer to the valence band maximum of MoS2 than the conduction band minimum, indicating the formation of a p-type Schottky contact or bipolar contact. This finding is consistent with another theoretical prediction of a p-type interface between the F­(Cu–S) surface and pristine MoS2. However, the above theoretical picture contrasts with experimental reports, which have consistently reported significant n-type behavior for CuS/MoS2 contacts. ,

In the following, we demonstrate that the above discrepancy between theory and experiment can be explained by the presence of dopants such as hydrogen, commonly introduced during material growth, which is known to induce n-type doping in MoS2. , It is worth noting that intrinsic point defects, such as sulfur vacancies, often assumed to cause n-type doping, actually act as electron compensation centers and do not lead to net n-type behavior. Therefore, this study focuses on the influence of hydrogen doping in MoS2 on the contact type of the CuS/MoS2 MSJs.

MSJs with HInt and HAds-MoS2

Hydrogen atoms are frequently incorporated into or adsorbed on TMDs, as hydrogen is present in nearly all TMD growth environments. , The incorporation of hydrogen atoms modifies the physicochemical properties of TMDs in multiple ways, including spin-polarized electronic structures, optical characteristics, alterations in conductivity type, and enhanced catalytic activity, thereby broadening their potential applications in electronics, sensors, catalysts, and other fields. − For instance, hydrogen has been shown to induce n-type conductivity in monolayer MoS2. , In the present first-principles calculations, hydrogen is introduced to simulate its incorporation in monolayer MoS2. Further discussion on the hydrogen concentration adopted in our calculations, its experimental relevance, and the sensitivity of the SBH to hydrogen coverage is provided in Note S1 of the Supporting Information. Various hydrogen adsorption configurations in/on monolayer MoS2 have been reported, such as interstitial-site hydrogen within MoS2 (HInt) or adatom hydrogen on the surface of MoS2 (HAds), either vertically or in a tilted geometry above a sulfur atom. − Our adsorption energy calculations confirm that hydrogen incorporation is energetically favorable in MoS2, with adsorption energies of −0.70, −0.44, and −0.19 eV for HTilt-MoS2, HInt-MoS2, and HVert-MoS2, respectively. For MoS2 samples in the experiment, intrinsic point defects, particularly sulfur vacancies, are commonly present and can influence the electronic properties of the material. The possible interplay between hydrogen and intrinsic defects, such as sulfur vacancies, is discussed in Note S3 of the Supporting Information. Recent experimental studies have further shown that postannealing treatments, such as H2S annealing, can effectively heal sulfur vacancies and suppress vacancy-related defect states. For the HAds configurations with freestanding MoS2, the tilted adsorption (HTilt) is more stable than the vertical adsorption (HVert) for hydrogen, refer to Figure c,e for the HTilt and HVert structures. However, in the CuS/MoS2 MSJ, the HVert structure becomes favored [Figure g], possibly due to the doping effect of CuS to MoS2 in the junction.

Geometric and band structures of HInt and HAds-MoS2 (a–d) and the vertical adsorption of hydrogen for HAds-MoS2 in the CuS/HAds-MoS2 MSJ (e–g).

Figures a and c show the optimized structures of hydrogen with freestanding MoS2 (HInt-MoS2 and HAds-MoS2 with tilted hydrogen adsorption, respectively): they both introduce spin-polarized impurity bands close to the conduction band, as depicted in Figures b and d, respectively. To elucidate the orbital origin of these hydrogen-induced states, orbital-projected band structure analyses were performed (see Figures S3 in the Supporting Information). The results indicate that the Mo d orbital in the conduction band edge of pristine MoS2 is moved down to the Fermi level due to hydrogen doping (the hydrogen atom donates one electron to the system), which is predominantly contributed by the three Mo atoms nearest to the hydrogen atom. For the HAds-MoS2 in contact with CuS to form an MSJ, the tilted hydrogen adsorption in Figure c changes to vertical adsorption after structural optimization in the CuS/HAds-MoS2 MSJ [Figure e,g], and the spin-polarized impurity band in Figure d moves up to very close to the conduction band in Figure f. The hydrogen position of HInt-MoS2 in MSJ is the same to that of the freestanding HInt-MoS2. Therefore, the band structures of Figure b,f are related to the following SBH discussion in MSJs. Owing to the negligible spin polarization observed in Figure b,f, we employ the nonspin-polarized calculation results for the following analysis of SBH.

Schottky Barriers

Figures a and b show the projected band structures of HAds-MoS2 and HInt-MoS2, respectively, in MSJs with CuS, which give n-type SBHs of 0.36 and 0.31 eV, respectively. It can be seen that this is a very obvious n-type SBH. As mentioned above in Figure b, p-type contact is obtained for CuS contact to pristine MoS2 and here changes to n-type contact when the MoS2 is electron-doped by hydrogen [as indicated in Figure b,f].

(b) Similar to (a) but for the CuS/HInt-MoS2 MSJ. (c) Summary of SBHs for MSJs with pristine MoS2, with hydrogen-doped MoS2 (HAds and HInt), and further the CuS surface doped with iodine (CuS–I); three sets of results are presented, including that from the DFT-projected band structure, from the Schottky–Mott limit without correction, and with correction by the generalized ΔV (see Subsection below for detail).

In addition to the modification of the MoS2 side of the MSJ with hydrogen, the CuS electrode side can also be doped. For example, an experimental study has demonstrated iodine (I) doping to the CuS surface. Our calculations show that the work functions of the F(Cu–S) surface and the F(Cu–S) surface with an iodine are 5.53 and 5.38 eV, respectively. Namely, the work function becomes smaller with I doping. The iodine concentration is one iodine atom per CuS(0001)–(3 × 3) surface cell, with a surface sulfur atom replaced by an iodine atom. When such an I-doped CuS surface (CuS–I) forms MSJs with hydrogen-doped MoS2 (HAds and HInt), our computational results show a significant reduction in the n-type SBH. Specifically, the SBHs in MSJs of CuS–I/HAds and CuS–I/HInt are 0.2 and 0.16 eV, respectively [compared with 0.36 and 0.31 eV, respectively, in Figure a,b]. Details about the MSJs with CuS–I can be found in Figures S1 and S2 of the Supporting Information.

Figure c lists the SBH of five MSJs, namely, CuS/MoS2, CuS/HAds-MoS2, CuS/HInt-MoS2, CuS–I/HAds-MoS2, and CuS–I/HInt-MoS2, and three sets of results are presented, as obtained from DFT calculations, from the Schottky–Mott limit without correction and with the generalized ΔV correction (as described in Subsection below). A clear discrepancy is observed between the DFT results and the Schottky–Mott limit; however, this discrepancy is largely eliminated after ΔV correction, which yields results in good agreement with the DFT calculations.

In the following, we focus on the SBH values from DFT and that with the ΔV correction. Figure c shows that the n-type SBH value of the CuS/MoS2 junction without hydrogen is significantly larger than those with hydrogen (including CuS/HAds-MoS2 and CuS/HInt-MoS2 junctions), which is due to the hydrogen-induced n-type doping (as mentioned above). The implications of these reduced Schottky barrier heights, resulting from hydrogen incorporation in MoS2 and iodine doping of the CuS electrode, for contact resistance, together with a qualitative comparison to reported CuS/MoS2 device performance, are discussed in Note S2 of the Supporting Information. From a microscopic perspective, we show that hydrogen doping in MoS2 affects the charge density redistribution (Δρ) at the CuS/MoS2 interface, as given in Figure . The change in Δρ with hydrogen doping affects the magnitude of the interface dipole, and hence, the ΔV corrections are larger for the four MSJs with hydrogen than the one MSJ without hydrogen, which reduce the n-type SBH [refer to Figure c].

Figures a and b show the plane-averaged charge density difference, Δρ­(z), for the CuS/MoS2 and CuS/HInt-MoS2 junctions, respectively, which is calculated as the valence charge density of the MSJ minus those of the two subsystems of the MSJ. We define the Δρ for the CuS/MoS2 (CuS/HInt-MoS2) junction as Δρ0 (Δρ1) in Figure a,b. The difference, Δρ1 – Δρ0 in Figure c, gives the additional change from MoS2 to HInt-MoS2 in MSJ. Figure c demonstrates that there is an additional electron gain (electron loss) at the CuS (HInt-MoS2) side in the CuS/HInt-MoS2 MSJ compared with the CuS/MoS2 MSJ. The corresponding additional interface dipole (electron potential energy change) is sketched at the bottom (top) of Figure c, which means that the energy levels in the CuS side are moved up further in the CuS/HInt-MoS2 MSJ relative to the CuS/MoS2 MSJ, and hence, the n-type SBH is decreased from MoS2 to HInt-MoS2 in the MSJ [refer to Figure c]. This mechanism accounts for the considerable difference in SBH from the CuS/MoS2 to CuS/HInt-MoS2 junction. Other MSJs with hydrogen in MoS2 give a similar conclusion.

Generalized ΔV Correction in MSJs with Asymmetric Surface Models

For MSJs with 2D semiconductors (2DS), the Schottky–Mott limit is usually not met due to charge redistribution at the metal–2D semiconductor interface, which induces an interface dipole and causes a shift of the electron energy levels from their original positions. The interface dipole is described by an interfacial potential step, ΔV, as obtained from a slab calculation of the MSJ. , This approach applies for symmetric surface models of the two subsystems (the metal and the 2D semiconductor) in an MSJ. Here, we show that if one of the two subsystems cannot be described by a symmetric surface model, a more general approach is needed.

The n- and p-type SBHs are usually given by ΦSBn=WM−EA−ΔV;⁣ΦSBp=EI−WM+ΔV 1 where EA, EI, W M, and ΔV are the electron affinity and ionization energy of the semiconductor, the work function of metal, and the interface dipole, respectively. However, for the systems studied here, such as hydrogen-adsorbed MoS2 or iodine-doped CuS, structural asymmetry is introduced. To calculate the interface dipole for MSJs with asymmetric surface models in the metal or 2DS subsystems, two approaches may be used: (1) constructing symmetric surface models to force structural symmetry or (2) directly calculating the potential difference between the two surface sides of the subsystem caused by surface asymmetry. Method (2) is a more general approach, especially when the 2DS has no symmetry due to surface adsorption or doping. Constructing symmetric surface models as in method (1) is sometimes unrealistic, for example, for the HAds-MoS2 in MSJs. In contrast, method (2) works without assuming symmetry and applies to diverse doping/adsorption models, providing a more general basis for analyzing band alignment in MSJs. Thus, method (2) is used in the current work.

The asymmetry between the two surfaces in the subsystems of an MSJ gives rise to a potential difference between them, and we define this as ΔV Asym. Considering the ΔV Asym, the n- or p-type SBH is modified as ΦSBn=WM−EA−(ΔV−ΔVAsym)ΦSBp=EI−WM+(ΔV−ΔVAsym) 2 Both hydrogen adsorption on MoS2 and iodine doping on the CuS surface introduce ΔV Asym and are denoted as ΔV Hads and ΔV CuS–I, respectively. ΔV Hads and ΔV CuS–I are 0.30 and 0.15 eV, respectively. Accounting for these, the calculated ΦSB values align closely with those from DFT [refer to Figure c and Table ].

Conclusions

This study employs density functional theory (DFT) calculations to reveal that hydrogen adsorption on MoS2 induces n-type SBH. This effect is the key factor responsible for the experimentally observed n-type contact in the CuS/MoS2 junctions. This mechanism can extend beyond the specific system studied here: for example, the surface work function of another semimetal of CuSe is similarly tunable by doping, and hydrogen also induces n-type doping in another TMD of WS2. Surface adsorption/doping introduces asymmetry in the slab models, and we introduce a more general computational approach to address this issue. These findings not only deepen the understanding of electronic structures at 2DS/semimetal interfaces but also provide theoretical guidance for designing low-stress SBH devices. The results offer actionable insights into interface engineering for low-power 2D electronic devices.
