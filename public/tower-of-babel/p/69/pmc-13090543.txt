Ovarian torsion after saline infusion sonohysterography in a woman with an adnexal cyst: A case report
Authors: Emilse Almanza, Stephanie Hallisey, Louisa Drake, Prachi Godiwala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Saline infusion sonohysterography is a commonly performed and generally well-tolerated diagnostic procedure in gynecologic practice to assess the uterine cavity. Serious complications are rare, and no reports could be found of ovarian torsion following saline infusion sonohysterography.This report describes the case of a 29-year-old nulligravid woman who developed acute left lower quadrant pain and nausea 1–2 h after undergoing routine saline infusion sonohysterography for fertility evaluation. Imaging suggested left ovarian torsion in the setting of a 7 cm complex ovarian cyst. Laparoscopy confirmed a necrotic ovary torsed three times around the infundibulopelvic ligament. A left salpingo-oophorectomy was performed. Final pathology revealed a mature cystic teratoma.Clinicians should counsel patients found to have large adnexal cysts at the time of the procedure about the potential risk of torsion, which may be precipitated and worsened by saline infusion sonohysterography, and provide clear post-procedure precautions.

Introduction

A sonohysterogram, also called a saline infusion sonogram (SIS), is widely used in gynecologic practice and considered a standard diagnostic tool for the evaluation of abnormal uterine bleeding, infertility, and intrauterine pathology [1]. In the procedure, sterile fluid is instilled transcervically into the uterine cavity to enhance visualization of the endometrial cavity using transvaginal ultrasonography. The procedure is typically performed in the office setting.

Most adverse effects of SIS procedures are mild and infrequent, including pelvic pain or cramping, vasovagal symptoms and light vaginal bleeding. More serious complications include infections such as pelvic inflammatory disease and endometritis, with reported rates less than 1% [2], [3]. Very rare cases of severe infection such as peritonitis or pelvic abscess have been described [2], [4]. Overall, sonohysterography is considered a safe and well-tolerated diagnostic procedure with a low rate of significant complications.

The authors were unable to find previous reports of ovarian torsion as a complication of SIS, and so this case is likely to represent the first reported of acute ovarian torsion occurring shortly after a routine sonohysterogram.

Case Presentation

A 29-year-old woman (G0), with a BMI of 49.16 kg/m2 and no pertinent past medical history, underwent a routine sonohysterogram for procreative management with plans for therapeutic donor insemination. Her surgical history was notable only for a cholecystectomy. Prior to the sonohysterogram, the patient was asymptomatic.

During the procedure, a sonohysterography catheter was introduced into the uterus transcervically and the balloon was inflated in the lower uterine segment in standard fashion. Under direct vaginal ultrasound guidance, less than 20 mL of sterile normal saline was instilled into the uterine cavity. The examination demonstrated a normal symmetric uterine cavity without abnormalities. Inspection of the adnexa demonstrated bilateral ovarian cysts. The right ovary measured 3.3 × 2.3 × 4.3 cm with an antral follicle count (AFC) of 20. A simple-appearing right ovarian cyst measured 1.4 cm. The left ovary measured 7.8 × 6.7 × 8.6 cm with an AFC of 16. A left complex ovarian cyst measured 6.96 cm (Fig. 1). It was unclear how long the patient had had the large cyst, as she not undergone prior pelvic imaging. The sonohysterogram was well tolerated by the patient and completed in less than 15 min. Approximately 1–2 h after the procedure, while in a sitting position at rest, the patient developed severe left lower quadrant pelvic pain that was described as constant with intermittent periods of worsening stabbing pain. Associated symptoms included nausea, lightheadedness, clamminess and difficulty urinating. Given the timing and severity of her symptoms, particularly in light of her ultrasound findings, the patient was instructed to present to the emergency department for evaluation.

The patient presented to an outside facility due to her proximity to the outside hospital at the time of symptom onset. On presentation to the emergency department, the patient continued to have intermittent episodes of severe pain in her lower left quadrant. On physical examination, she was alert, oriented and visibly distressed. Her vital signs were within normal limits. Her abdomen was tender to palpation at the left lower quadrant without rigidity or guarding. Labs were significant for a mild leukocytosis of 12,900 cells/μL and lactic acid of 3.0 mmol/L. A pelvic ultrasound confirmed the previous findings of a 7.4 × 7.3 × 7.8 cm lesion within the left ovary that was partially cystic with a hyperdense component and described as most consistent with a dermoid cyst. Due to the large cyst, minimal ovarian tissue was visualized on ultrasound, but the tissue that was visualized appeared edematous. Given these findings, she was admitted for suspected left ovarian torsion with a plan for surgical management.

The patient was taken to the operating room approximately 14 h after the sonohysterogram and 12 h after symptom onset of pain and nausea. Upon laparoscopic entry into the abdomen, the left ovary was visualized and appeared necrotic and hemorrhagic. The ovary was noted to be torsed around the infundibulopelvic ligament (IP) three times. The accompanying left fallopian tube also appeared necrotic and edematous. The right fallopian tube and ovary appeared normal. The left ovary was detorsed around the IP until normal anatomy was restored. The ovary was observed for 5 min without a return of color and the tissue was deemed non-viable. The performing providers made the decision to proceed with a left salpingo-oophorectomy. The procedure was uncomplicated and the patient was taken to the post-anesthesia care unit in stable condition. The patient's postoperative course was uncomplicated and she remained asymptomatic. Pathology was consistent with a mature cystic teratoma.

Discussion

To the authors' knowledge, no case reports or case series have described ovarian torsion following sonohysterography. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the American College of Radiology (ACR), in their technology assessments and guidelines on sonohysterography, do not list ovarian torsion as a recognized complication, nor do they describe any unique procedural or patient-related risk factors for torsion following sonohysterography [1], [5].

Though the patient in the present case did have a 7 cm left ovarian cyst identified at the time of SIS, which placed her at baseline risk for ovarian torsion, no prior evidence was found to suggest that this risk could be precipitated or exacerbated by performing a SIS. The literature on adnexal torsion broadly identifies risk factors such as ovarian masses or cysts (especially >5 cm), reproductive age, prior history of torsion, and increased ovarian mobility due to congenital or acquired ligamentous laxity [6], [7]. However, procedures such as SIS are not currently recognized as potential risk factors. While the ACOG and ACR guidelines highlight the importance of not performing sonohysterography in patients with unexplained pelvic tenderness or suspected pelvic infection, they do not provide specific caution regarding ovarian torsion or recommendations about performing sonohysterogram procedures in patients with large ovarian cysts.

Theoretical mechanisms related to SIS that may contribute to an increased risk of ovarian torsion during or after the procedure involve the displacement of the ovaries from their anatomic position and lubrication of the surface of the ovary due to fluid passing through the fallopian tubes into the pelvic cavity during the procedure.

There are several ways in which the ovaries may have become displaced in this case. First, application of gentle abdominal pressure during a SIS to improve visualization of the adnexa, if needed, may increase intra-abdominal pressure leading to increased mobility of the adnexa.

Second, manual manipulation of the SIS catheter or vaginal speculum during the procedure may temporarily displace the uterus or ovaries from their anatomic position. In addition, if the bilateral fallopian tubes are patent and greater than 20 mL of normal saline is required to adequately assess the uterine cavity, the volume of saline may lead to a disruption in normal adnexal anatomy.

While manipulation of pelvic contents with a known dermoid cyst may certainly predispose the adnexa to torsion, the unique mechanics of SIS may have contributed to a more severe clinical outcome in this case. Specifically, the infusion of saline into the pelvis may have contributed to the triple torsion of the ovary around the infundibulopelvic ligament, resulting in significant vascular compromise. The degree of torsion ultimately necessitated oophorectomy rather than cystectomy alone.

Conclusion

This case represents a rare (possibly unique) complication after sonohysterography. Although risk factors for ovarian torsion after a sonohysterogram are presumed to be similar to those in the general population, practitioners should consider discussing ovarian torsion as a potential complication of SIS with all patients during the consent process. This conversation is particularly relevant for those with known risk factors prior to the procedure and for those in whom a risk factor, such as a large ovarian cyst, is identified during the procedure, as outcomes have the potential to be more severe, as in this case.
