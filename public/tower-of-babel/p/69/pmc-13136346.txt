Uncovering the structural influence of urban park landscapes on psychological restoration via graph learning
Authors: Yujie Zhang, Yaping Li, Yanchuan Yin, Ying Jin, Guodong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOver half of global population now resides in urban areas, where mental health challenges such as anxiety and depression are increasingly prevalent. Urban park landscapes are recognized as critical settings for promoting psychological restoration, yet a systematic understanding of what makes these spaces restorative remains limited. In this paper, we propose a novel graph learning-based framework that models and interprets the perceptual structure of urban park landscapes in relation to their restorative potential. The framework comprises two key components: a predictive module that quantitatively estimates perceived restorativeness based on scene-level visual and spatial features, and an interpretive module that identifies the importance of individual landscape elements and their spatial configurations. To support this approach, we introduce a novel dataset of urban park landscape images annotated with semantic, structural, and perceptual labels, facilitated by a large language model–assisted annotation pipeline. Experimental results demonstrate our framework’s predictive accuracy and its ability to provide interpretable visual explanations grounded in environmental psychology. Our findings offer actionable guidance for urban planners and landscape designers aiming to create psychologically supportive public environments.Keywords: Urban park landscape design, Psychological restoration, Graph learning, Structural analysisSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

In recent years, mental health issues—including anxiety, depression, and cognitive fatigue—have risen worldwide1, underscoring an urgent need for accessible and effective strategies to support psychological restoration2–4. Urban parks are important to maintain livable and sustainable cities. Urban park landscape, as essential elements of city fabric, is widely valued for their capacity to enhance mental well-being by providing restorative environments that alleviate stress and replenish cognition5–8. We all have had such an experience: when encountering a landscape with a tranquil lake, dappled sunlight through tree canopies, and open green space, we may experience a sense of calm and attentional relief, helping to ease cognitive fatigue and emotional tension. Building on the attention restoration theory (ART)9 and the stress reduction theory (SRT)10, psychological and landscape-architecture research has demonstrated the restorative effects of natural environments and urban park landscape on human health, particularly in enhancing attention and reducing stress. Consistent with these theoretical perspectives, cities are investing heavily on urban parks. According to the Trust for Public Land, the 2025 ParkScore Index shows record-high investment of major American cities in their urban parks, as well as growing usage11. Urban parks are increasingly recognized by city dwellers as vital spaces for daily respite, recreation, and psychological well-being12,13.

Psychological studies highlight attributes such as naturalness, enclosure, and vegetation as key drivers of positive emotional responses2,14–20. Instruments such as the Restorative Outcome Scale (ROS)21 have been widely used to capture self-reported restorative experiences across diverse settings and populations, demonstrating robust psychometric properties22–24. Recent work has further explored perceived sensory dimensions, the presence of other people, exposure duration and supportive built elements, showing that vegetation types and density, visual enclosure, spatial arrangement, serenity and species richness are all associated with stress recovery and improved well-being25–33. Eye-tracking studies indicate the role of urban landscape images in alleviating emotional stress34,35, and several recent investigations suggest that the visual proportion of landscape elements such as sky, trees, waters are positively related to restorative potential35–38. In landscape designs, research has largely focused on broader factors such as accessibility, greenery presence, and openness5,39–41. Despite this rich body of work, most studies rely on self-reported surveys and controlled experiments using pre-selected visual stimuli within urban parks. Although these approaches are predominantly qualitative or semi-quantitative, they linked survey-based reports to perceived environmental quality or health outcomes at aggregated community or city scales. Therefore, they are inefficient in scalable data acquisition and provide limited insight into how concrete combinations and spatial configurations of landscape elements jointly shape restorative outcomes at the human-eye level.

In recent years, the restorative potential of landscapes is increasingly understood to be governed not merely by its constituent elements, but by their spatial structure—the specific arrangement and relational geometry that shapes visual perception and cognitive processing. Qualitative work at the human-eye level confirms that structural features (such as the contiguity of green space, vertical stratification, and the connective integration of natural and built elements) are significant predictors of restorative outcomes42–44. These observed features align with the mechanisms proposed by ART and SRT: spatial structures that offer coherent spatial organization (e.g., contiguous green patches with clear depth cues) foster soft fascination and alleviate psychological stress, while configurations that suggest prospect and refuge (e.g., a sheltered bench with an open view) can reduce cognitive load. Consequently, a systematic understanding of landscape spatial structure is not merely supplementary but fundamental to advancing the science of psychological restoration. It represents the critical pathway to move beyond descriptive, element-centric accounts toward predictive models and generalizable design principles. However, this understanding remains qualitatively constrained. The lack of computational models capable of representing and quantifying the multivariate relational syntax of landscapes impedes progress. As discussed later in Sect. "Basic analytics of graph annotations", considering landscape element types and even their pairwise relationships alone is insufficient to explain the observed variation in restorative quality, highlighting the need for a framework that can capture higher-order structural configurations.

Parallel advances in computer vision and machine learning have enabled more scalable, human-centric assessments of urban environments from imagery, offering opportunities for quantitative analysis and automated reasoning regarding the restorative benefits of urban park landscapes45–47. Deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs), Transformers and related models have been used to analyze street-level or aerial images, quantify visual exposure to greenery through metrics such as the Green View Index (GVI) and relate these measures to health or affective outcomes48–51. Vision Transformer-based models have demonstrated correlations between visual elements, human activities, and positive psychological perceptions52,53, while panoramic street-view analysis connect neighborhood-level visual cues with urban experiences such as wealth, boredom, and depression54. Machine learning models have also been applied to identify specific landscape characteristics that moderate emotional restoration32,55, and to integrate spatial parameters (for example, enclosure and walking distance) into design-support frameworks for assessing green spaces56. More recently, graph neural networks (GNNs) have been adopted to estimate restorative outcomes from street-view imagery at large scales and to model urban networks in planning contexts41,57,58. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the power of machine learning to capture complex visual patterns in urban landscapes and to link them with human perceptions. However, existing research methodologies are primarily qualitative and based on on-site survey, and focus on broad geographic or community-scale evaluations, lacking the specificity, granularity, and scalability required for practical and future-oriented applications.

The above limitations lead to the central research problem of this study: how to computationally model and explain the restorative potential of urban park landscapes in a way that is both accurate and interpretable for psychological and design practice? Specifically, we investigate three questions: (1) Can a learning framework reliably predict scene-level restorative outcomes from human-eye-level images of urban parks? (2) How can such a framework represent park landscapes as structured compositions of elements and spatial relations, rather than as unstructured feature vectors? (3) Can the learned model provide interpretable explanations that identify which elements and configurations contribute most to psychological restoration, thereby offering actionable guidance for landscape design?

Here we address these questions by introducing a graph learning-based framework that links the perceptual structure of urban park landscapes to psychological restoration. At its core, the framework comprises two complementary modules: Perceiver, a predictive module to predict restorative potential from visual landscape input, and Miner, an interpretive module to reveal the spatial and semantic factors driving these predictions. Perceiver is an effective graph neural network-based model, which accurately predicts psychological restoration scores by processing landscape images represented as structured graphs. Each graph captures landscape elements as nodes and their spatial relations as edges, with perceptual depth information integrated to reflect the viewers’ visual experience. Miner is a second deep learning model that learns to assign soft importance weights to graph components, identifying significantly influential elements and spatial configurations through a graph-masking strategy. Additionally, we develop a new dataset of urban park landscape images with restorative emphasis. Particularly, we leverage large language models (LLMs) to generate scene graph annotations from pre-segmented landscape images via a targeted prompting strategy, significantly reducing manual annotation effort while maintaining semantic and structural integrity. Together, these components yield a first-learn-then-interpret architecture, offering a novel, computational lens for quantitatively exploring how urban park landscapes influence psychological restoration. While this study does not aim to establish definitive theoretical conclusions, it introduces a practical methodology that enables automated prediction, interpretable analysis, and scalable data generation. This framework provide psychologists, landscape designers, and urban planners with a quantitative, real-time tool for refining theoretical models, informing evidence-based spatial design, and advancing urban development strategies that support mental well-being.

Dataset

To date, no existing datasets of urban park images are designed to support graph learning. In this section, we introduce ParkScape-PR (urban park landscape dataset emphasizing psychological restoration), comprising 1,346 high-resolution images of urban park landscapes. To ensure data diversity, the images were sourced from five types of urban park scenes: cultural heritage parks, amusement parks, comprehensive parks, community parks, and ecological parks. ParkScape-PR comprises 236 on-site photographs and 1,100 web-sourced images. On-site images were captured at eye level using a smartphone to approximate a pedestrian’s horizontal field of view; web-sourced images were selected to match this perspective. We removed blurry, distorted, or poorly composed images and considered variations in weather and season. We detected human faces in images by YOLOv559 and obscured them with the Gaussian blur60. Different from previous urban imagery datasets, ParkScape-PR features multi-modal annotations, including instance segmentation, scene graphs, depth information, and survey-based ratings.

Graph annotations essentially encode the structural and spatial features of their corresponding images. To efficiently generate graph annotations for all images, we leveraged ChatGPT, an LLM, to interpret visual scenes based on predefined prompts. An overview of the annotation pipeline is presented in Fig. 3. ChatGPT was used to identify not only the instances within each image but also their spatial relationships. To mitigate the tendency of LLMs to lose contextual continuity, we prompted ChatGPT individually for each image, rather than relying on a single initial prompt. For each image, ChatGPT was provided with two inputs: (1) a list of instance class labels identified for the given image, and (2) all spatial relation types defined in Table 2. This approach guides the ChatGPT to semantically focus on relevant instances and infer spatial relationships within a constrained relational framework. ChatGPT then generated a textual description of the spatial relationships present in each image. These descriptions were manually reviewed for accuracy and then, saved in plain text format. Any descriptive errors or omissions were corrected to ensure fidelity to the actual spatial layout. Based on the textual descriptions, ChatGPT subsequently produced a corresponding graph structure, in which nodes represent instances and edges represent their spatial relationships.

Depth annotation

In urban landscape design, depth not only contributes to visually pleasing compositions but also about fosters a connection between viewers and space. Well-designed landscapes with clear or dynamic depth cues offer a range of aesthetic benefits and promote psychological restoration by encouraging relaxation, engagement, and a sense of well-being20,64. To quantify the scene depth within each image, we extracted the depth information using the Depth Anything V2 model65, which measures the depth variation in the scene and adds an additional annotation layer for ParkScape-PR. In implementation, we simplify the depth maps using a statistical matrix which will be detailed later in Sect. "Depth extraction". Basic analytics of graph annotations

We performed a graph analysis of the graphical structures extracted from all images of ParkScape-PR to explore whether specific structural features are associated with ROS score variations. As shown in Fig. 7, we examine the influence of five graphical attributes on each image’s total ROS score. These attributes are (1) the number of instances (“#instance”), (2) the number of relations (“#relations”), (3) the average degree (“avg. degree”, i.e., the average number of relations per instance), (4) the number of Level-2 instance classes (“#instance classes”), and (5) the number of relation types (“#relation types”). Across all five subfigures in Fig. 7, no consistent or interpretable patterns emerge. The ROS scores exhibit considerable variability across different attribute values, with overlapping error bars and no clear monotonic trends. These results suggest that simple graph analytics alone is insufficient to account for variations in perceived restorative quality, reinforcing the need for more advanced methodologies to uncover deeper and more informative structural patterns.

Methods

We propose a graph learning-based framework to quantitatively assess images and uncover structural relationships between landscape composition and psychological restoration. Figure 8 illustrates this framework, which consists of two complementary modules: Perceiver and Miner. The Perceiver module leverages graph convolutional operations to accurately predict ROS scores from each image in a manner closely aligned with human perceptual judgments. Complementing the Perceiver, the Miner module provides critical interpretability by analyzing and uncovering key features and structural patterns that contribute to predicted ROS scores. Together, these two modules offer a computational framework, systematically combining predictive accuracy with insightful structural and semantic interpretability.

Overall architecture and workflow of the proposed framework.

Design of Perceiver

Graph embedding

As described in Sect. "Dataset", images were annotated with graph structures. During training, a batch of b images is processed, resulting in b corresponding sets of graph embeddings.

Graph convolution

We propose a graph learning layer that scores input images by learning from their graph embeddings. This layer includes three sequential processing units: two edge-aware GCN (EaGCN) units followed by a pooling unit. They form a pipeline integrated with ReLu nonlinear activation and dropouts. The basic design principle behind this layer is to hierarchically encode node-level features into global graph-level representations by incorporating attention mechanisms into graph convolution process.

The structure of the EaGCN block is shown in Fig. 9. EaGCN begins with a GATConv unit, a widely used graph attention mechanism that captures hierarchical relationships through attention-weighted neighborhood aggregation66. This unit aggregates node information by assigning learnable attention weights to neighboring nodes. Subsequently, a fully connected neural network (FCN), consisting of a single layer of neurons, is applied to transform and refine intermediate features. These features are then passed to an Edge-aware Attention unit, which extends the conventional attention mechanism by incorporating both node and edge attributes. This allows EaGCN to differentiate between various types of node interactions. While edge attributes often encode key structural or relational cues, in Perceiver, they primarily represent spatial orientation between nodes. To fully leverage edge information, we explicitly integrate edge features into the attention computation. This design enables the network to adaptively modulate attention weights based on the semantic content of edges, thereby enhancing its ability to capture spatial structure and improving the representational capacity of the learned graph features. Finally, dropout and ReLU activation functions are applied to prevent overfitting.

Structure of the EaGCN block.

In Perceiver’s graph convolution component, we apply EaGCN twice, motivated by the need to effectively capture both local and global structural patterns within the graph. Specifically, the first EaGCN extracts initial features and models node-edge interactions using edge-aware attention, which dynamically adjusts attention weights based on node and edge attributes. The second EaGCN refines these weights by incorporating a broader structural context across the graph. The edge-aware attention mechanism in both stacked EaGCN further enhances the model’s ability to leverage spatial and relational information encoded in edge attributes.

The texture descriptor includes five statistical variables extracted from the depth feature map: contrast, energy, homogeneity, correlation, and Angular Second Moment (ASM). “Contrast” captures local intensity variation, reflecting differences between light and dark regions. “Energy” quantifies the smoothness of depth map, with higher values indicating more uniform patterns. “Homogeneity” measures the consistency of depth distribution, while “correlation” evaluates the relation between depth values in different regions. ASM captures texture roughness, where higher values typically indicate fine-grained or repetitive patterns. these metrics form a compact and informative depth matrix that balances descriptive richness with computational efficiency. Before feature fusion, the depth matrix is flattened into a 20-dimensional vector representing the global depth feature.

Feature fusion and prediction

Effective feature fusion plays a critical role in our scene understanding with multiple modalities. In Perceiver, we need to integrate the structural semantics extracted by the graph convolution layer with the depth information derived from the depth extraction layer. To this end, we introduce a feature fusion layer that ultimately generates the ROS score for the input image. Specifically, we first concatenate the depth matrix and the pooled graph feature to form a unified representation. However, due to the difference in dimensionality of these two features, direct concatenation may lead to over-representation and feature imbalance. To address this, we employ an FCN with seven neurons to both fuse the modalities more effectively and align their dimensionalities. This layer also performs implicit dimensionality reduction, mitigating the overfitting risk while enhancing the quality of the fused representation.

Design of Miner

The Perceiver module presented above is capable of predicting ROS scores from landscape images by learning the structural feature and depth feature. From a statistical learning perspective, Perceiver, hereafter denoted as , learns a conditional probability distribution , where Y is a random variable representing ROS scores and G is the graph annotation derived from the input image. As demonstrated later in Sect. "Evaluation of Perceiver", Perceiver is able to predict ROS scores with high accuracy. However, it alone cannot identify which elements or spatial patterns play a critical role in determining the restorative capacity of a landscape. Therefore, we introduce the Miner module to understand the factors that predominantly influence Perceiver’s ROS score predictions.

Principles underlying Miner

As illustrated in Fig. 8, Miner employs the trained model as a frozen, callable predictor, and takes a graph G along with its corresponding predicted ROS scores as two inputs, where . Fundamentally, Miner is a machine learning model that learns a graph mask to assess which components of G (individual nodes, edges, and subgraphs) are more important in contributing . During inference, Miner assigns soft importance weights for each node and edge of G. Furthermore, based on these weights, a weighted subgraph (referred to as a perceptual subgraph) can be extracted, by a -thresholded strategy, to represent the original full graph G. The weighted nodes, edges, and perceptual subgraph collectively serve as an interpretable explanation of the Perceiver’s prediction, offering insights into which landscape elements and structural patterns contribute most to the predicted ROS score. Our Miner is built on the GNNExplainer model67, a widely-used post-hoc explanation method for any graph neural networks. It aims to identify graphical features that are most influential for a given prediction.

Without practical constraints, one could theoretically enumerate all possible weight assignments over the input graph G, and evaluate each candidate assignment by the trained model to identify the optimal one. However, such exhaustive search is computationally intractable, as the number of possible assignments grows exponentially with the size of G. As a learning-based scalable alternative, Miner introduces a graph mask that acts as a soft, differentiable filter over G. This approach formulates the discrete combinatorial problem of weight assignment into a continuous optimization task over the graph mask parameters, enabling efficient end-to-end learning.

Generation of perceptual subgraph

Figure 11 presents two example images from ParkScape-PR, along with their corresponding graph annotations and graphical explanations generated by Miner. We now turn to deriving a perceptual subgraph from the weighted graph. This process involves two main steps: (1) selecting a subset of important nodes base on their assigned importance weights, and (2) expanding this node set by incorporating relevant edges and nodes.

Examples of Miner’s input and output. For clarity, in the two graph annotations, the relation types for edges and the instance classes for nodes are all omitted, while in the weighted graphs, node and edge weights are partially omitted.

In our study, the selection of nodes for perceptual subgraph construction is motivated by the Theory of Visual Attention68 and the Spotlight Theory of Attention69, both of which suggest that individuals tend to focus on a limited set of salient elements or features within complex visual scenes. To simulate this constrained cognitive focus, we select the top- nodes—the top percentile of nodes ranked by node importance—as core nodes. At this step, the perceptual subgraph includes only these core nodes, without any connecting edges.

In the subsequent expansion step, we first identify core edges, defined as edges connecting two core nodes, and put them into the perceptual subgraph. Next, we examine all non-core edges incident to core nodes, and retain those with importance above a threshold . These retained non-core edges are referred to as extended edges, and the non-core nodes they connect, as extended nodes. The inclusion of extended nodes and edges completes the construction of a perceptual subgraph. We will demonstrate the representational capacity of perceptual subgraphs, as well as the setup of parameters and , in Sect. "Evaluation of Miner".

The above expansion strategy is inspired by both theoretic and empirical findings from visual attention research, showing that visual attention can extend from focal elements to spatially adjacent regions and, under certain conditions, be even distributed across discrete zones70,71. Alignment with human attention. As a result, perceptual subgraphs closely reflect human perceptual responses, thus ensuring psychological validity.

Results and analyses

The preceding evaluation demonstrates that Perceiver achieves high-accuracy ROS score prediction consistent with human ratings, and Miner effectively assigns importance weights and extracts perceptual subgraphs that it preserves representational fidelity with respect to restorative outcomes. In this section, we investigate which structural features of urban park landscape influence ROS scores, by analyzing the importance weights and perceptual subgraphs generated by Miner. The analysis unfolds across three levels: individual instance classes, triplet types, and perceptual subgraphs. This supports a progressively holistic understanding of how urban park landscape composition influences psychological restoration.

Does individual instance classes matter?

To evaluate whether specific landscape instance classes are more influential for restorative outcomes, we analyze their average importance values across four ROS score groups, as illustrated in Fig. 15. Although elements such as tree, lawn, and leisure facility exhibit higher importance in high-scoring groups, they also appear prominently in lower-scoring ones. This overlap indicates that instance classes alone do not reliably differentiate restorative potential. For example, a tree beside a road may have different restorative effects than one near a fountain; a leisure facility near a natural element may be perceived differently than the same facility surrounded by civil infrastructure. Consequently, Fig. 15 highlights a limitation: relying solely on the importance of instance classes is insufficient for interpreting restorative potential. Substantial variance within groups, as indicated by error bars, further underscores the inconsistency of instance-level significance. In sum, meaningful interpretation requires integrating spatial structure and relational context beyond individual instance classes.

Figure 16 demonstrates this variation using two example graphs, highlighting Miner’s progressive refinements of assessment. This dynamic adjustment suggests that edges—each corresponding to a triplet composed of two instances and their spatial relationship—play a critical role in shaping ROS predictions. We first compare the full original graph annotations shown in the middle gray rows of Fig. 19. This can reflect topological features that help elucidate the factors influencing ROS scores. (1) Connectivity: Low-ROS graphs tend to exhibit sparser connectivity among landscape elements, whereas high-ROS graphs demonstrate denser, more cohesive structures characterized by uniformly connected nodes. This structural coherence is indicative of greater spatial integration and is consistent with the principle of coherent scene organization proposed in Attention Restoration Theory (ART). (2) Triplet balance: High-ROS graphs exhibit more balanced and meaningful spatial triplets, which reflect layered, naturalistic compositions that may evoke soft fascination and spatial depth; in contrast, low-ROS graphs more frequently involve redundant relations and the spatial semantics are less likely to invoke restorative responses. (3) Topological complexity: While high-ROS graphs are denser, they are not chaotic. They maintain a moderate level of structural complexity that aligns with theories of optimal stimulation—rich enough to sustain attention, but not overwhelming. Low-ROS graphs may be either overly simplistic (failing to engage the viewer) or separately clustered, which can disrupt perceptual fluency. (4) Topological shape: Low-ROS graphs are more linear or unbalanced, lacking a clear center of perceptual gravity, while high-ROS graphs often form radial or clustered shapes, indicating a strong center or focal region surrounded by supportive elements—a common motif in restorative natural scenes.

The comparative analysis reveals several key structural and semantic differences. (1) Instance diversity: High-ROS subgraphs typically feature a greater variety of natural elements (e.g., trees, lawns, flowers, water), which frequently appear as central nodes with high relative importance. In contrast, low-ROS subgraphs are often dominated by more built or inert elements (e.g., architectural features, sidewalk), displaying less diversity in instance classes. (2) Structural balance and symmetry: High-ROS subgraphs tend to display visually balanced or radially distributed structures, where nodes are evenly distributed and symmetrically connected around a central focal element. This configuration suggests a higher degree of perceptual coherence. Low-ROS subgraphs, by contrast, often present linear or sparse arrangements, often lacking a clear central element, which may lead to fragmented or less engaging visual experiences. (3) Relation richness: In high-ROS subgraphs, core nodes are typically linked to multiple peripheral nodes via semantically meaningful and spatially diverse relations (e.g., “adjacent to”, “above”, “in front of”). These layered relationships contribute to immersive and complex scene compositions. Low-ROS subgraphs, on the other hand, exhibit fewer and more homogeneous relations, indicative of simple and monotonous spatial structures. (4) Edge saliency: High-ROS subgraphs feature strong, consistently colored edges, denoting stable and confident attribution of spatial importance and suggesting well-defined structural hierarchies. In contrast, low-ROS subgraphs contain weaker, more diffusely colored edges, indicating lower structural cohesion and perceptual clarity.

These above observations are consistent with those observed in the full original graphs. Importantly, these findings indicate that restorative urban park landscapes are not defined solely by the presence of individual elements, but by the richness, coherence, and connectivity of their underlying structural configurations—features that are prominently revealed in perceptual subgraphs. The perceptual subgraph thus functions as a distilled yet faithful representation of the restorative core of a landscape, offering both psychological interpretability and structural fidelity.

Discussion

Achievements

In this paper, we present a novel graph learning-based framework, which can systematically explore the structural relationships between urban park landscapes and psychological restoration. This methodological innovation addresses critical limitations in previous approaches that pay less attention to structural interrelationships. In the proposed framework, Perceiver leverages graph convolutional neural networks and depth extraction to accurately predict restorative potential, paving a machine learning way to achieve a human-aligned perception in psychological restoration. On the other hand, Miner effectively interprets Perceiver’s predictions, by explicitly identifying influential nodes and edges, effectively bridging computational models with psychological insights. Moreover, the perceptual subgraphs produced by Miner provide simplified yet representative structural insights, reflecting the theoretical proposition that spatial structures of landscape elements influence human psychological restoration. This interpretability is crucial for actionable urban park landscape design, enabling stakeholders to make evidence-based decisions about spatial arrangements and element combinations. Moreover, it facilitates the design of environments that are better aligned with mental health needs, thereby helping to mitigate urban stress and promote broader community well-being.

In addition to proposing a novel framework for understanding influential elements and spatial structures, our study successfully leverages LLMs to facilitate dataset annotation. Specifically, by designing a targeted prompting strategy, we leverage the reasoning and contextual understanding capabilities of LLM to interpret spatial relationships among pre-segmented landscape elements. Compared to traditional annotation approaches, our LLM-assisted method considerably reduces human labor and time cost, while maintaining semantic richness and structural consistency across the dataset. This not only streamlines large-scale dataset development but also exemplifies the practical synergy between vision-language models and graph-based spatial representation, offering a scalable and generalizable pathway for future research.

Our findings suggest several practical strategies for configuring restorative park environments. First, designers should favor compositions where vegetation frames, rather than blocks, mid- and long-range views, balancing refuge and prospect. Second, integrating water features, continuous canopy edges and gently curved paths appears especially beneficial, whereas excessive hard infrastructure or visual clutter may weaken restorative effects. These scene-level patterns can be translated into concrete guidelines for siting vegetation, water and amenities in future park designs.

Limitations

Our framework is currently limited to the domain of visual perception. Other sensory modalities—such as auditory and olfactory stimuli, which are known to influence restorative experiences in urban environments72–74—have not been yet incorporated. This study is based on isolated landscape images of urban parks, which do not capture the spatiotemporal continuity of real-world environments as experienced by urban dwellers. To more robustly evaluate the framework, future studies should incorporate landscape imagery with spatiotemporal correlations that reflect the scene continuity from the viewer’s perspective. Additionally, we primarily focus on the classes of landscape elements and their spatial relationships, with less attention given to their proportional presence within the viewer’s visual field, a factor shown in prior studies to influence restorative perception36,38,51. Our framework is not in conflict with such findings; rather, it is complementary. Integrating the proportional presence of landscape elements does not require architectural modifications to our framework. Such proportional information can be quantified through semantic segmentation and encoded as an additional node feature for graph annotations.

The developed dataset ParkScape-PR provides a diverse collection of urban park landscape images, and our results on this dataset demonstrate a powerful proof-of-concept for the machine learning framework. However, the current dataset lacks cultural and seasonal diversity and a broad subject pool, which may constrain its ability to capture global variations in urban park design and restorative experiences. Future expansion of the dataset to include parks and participants from different cultural and geographic contexts can address these limitations without requiring any modification to the framework itself. Another limitation of this study is the absence of a formal integration between the observed patterns and established psychological theories in visual attention and restoration. Our findings intuitively align with concepts such as focal attention, spatial coherence, and element saliency; however, we have not yet established a rigorous mapping to these theoretical constructs. Addressing this gap will be a valued avenue for future research, which could strengthen the theoretical grounding of our framework and enhance its interpretability and practical applicability in urban park landscape design.

Theoretical implications

Our framework does more than predict restorative potential; it offers a new language to formalize and extend the theoretical premises of ART and SRT. By distilling landscapes into networks of spatial relations (i.e., the perceptual graphs), we shift the theoretical focus from restorative elements to restorative configurations, suggesting that psychological efficacy is encoded in a scene’s underlying spatial syntax.

First, we provide a structural operationalization of key ART constructs. The higher graph connectivity and cohesion characteristic of high-ROS scene quantitatively define what constitutes “coherent organization”—a property hitherto described qualitatively. Furthermore, the predominance of relations like “above” and “in front of” in influential triplets delineates the layered depth that materially creates the sense of ART’s extent, essential for fascination. Crucially, our model reveals that coherence is not merely visual homogeneity but a specific topological property of the perceptual graph.

Second, our approach refines the prospect-refuge theory from a configurational typology to a relational one. High-importance subgraphs often encapsulate a refuge node (e.g., a tree or pavilion) spatially linked via “in front of” or “adjacent to” relations to a prospect node (e.g., a lawn or water body). This suggests the theory’s restorative effect arises not from the mere co-presence of enclosed and open spaces, but from their specific, legible spatial linkage as captured in the graph structure.

Ultimately, the most significant theoretical implication is the primacy of relations over elements. An element’s restorative contribution is conditional on its relational context—“a tree behind a building” differs fundamentally from “a tree in front of a lake”, a distinction our triplet analysis captures but element-centric approaches miss. Thus, our graph-based formalism moves the field toward a more predictive, mechanistic understanding of restoration, where theories can be expressed and tested as hypotheses about spatial graph topology.

Future research grounded in this study

In the future, our framework can be extended in several directions to deepen the understanding of restorative urban landscapes and enhance its practicality and potential for integration with emerging smart city applications.

Keeping human in the loop. Collecting diverse urban landscape data is critical to understand the the relationship between urban landscapes and their restorative benefits. A promising extension for our framework is to deploy it on smartphones, leveraging the mobile crowd sensing (MCS) paradigm51,75 to enable urban-scale data collection. With MCS, park visitors—or urban citizens more broaderly—act as active participants, using their smartphone to capture landscape settings they are experiencing. Modern smartphones are typically equipped with camera (visual sensor), microphone (acoustic sensor), and GPS unit (location sensor), enabling the collection of rich, multimodal data. In addition, users can annotate their experiences with self-reported emotions or reflections (via text or voice input), providing valuable ground-truth labels for restorative assessment. The MCS-powered data collection keeps human in the loop and thus, could allow us to capture fine-grained dynamics of human-environment interactions across time and space. Our framework is lightweight and efficient, implemented as a compact software module of approximately 1.5 MB. On devices with moderate computational resources, Perceiver generates ROS predictions in ~0.5 seconds per image, while Miner constructs perceptual subgraphs in ~2 seconds. This computational efficiency makes the framework well-suited for smartphone deployment, enabling real-time, user-driven, and scalable collection of restorative landscape data.

Integrating more human senses. Another promising and feasible extension is to incorporate auditory perception into our framework. Auditory stimuli, such as birdsong, flowing water, or the rustling of leaves, enhance restorative experiences in natural environments76. These sounds can be passively detected via the smartphone’s microphone or actively reported by users through text input. Such integration would involve real-time audio data collection and processing via machine learning models capable of sound classification. The resulting sound labels could be appended as attributes to existing instances or introduced as additional instance classes within graph annotations. This enhancement does not require fundamental changes to our framework. Instead, it can be implemented through dataset augmentation or multimodal feature fusion, enabling a seamless extension of Perceiver and Miner to incorporate auditory perception.

Enhancing interpretation through interaction. While our framework achieves high predictive accuracy and generates representative perceptual subgraphs, interpretability remains essential—particularly in urban planning contexts where stakeholders require clear, actionable insights. To this end, our framework could be extended with interactive features that allow users to intuitively explore why specific landscape elements or spatial configurations contribute to psychological restoration. Such interaction would facilitate connections between graph-derived visual patterns and underlying cognitive mechanisms from environmental psychology, supporting both psychologists and urban designers. Users could dynamically adjust threshold parameters in real time to observe how these variations affect perceptual subgraph construction and the resulting restoration outcomes. Additionally, the framework could incorporate predictive simulations to assess the potential restorative benefits of hypothetical or what-if landscape modifications. These capabilities would support informed, data-driven decision making grounded in interpretable and scenario-based feedback.

AI-generated designs of urban park landscapes. One promising direction for future research lies in leveraging the findings of this study to support generative design in urban park landscape planning. By identifying spatial structures associated with higher restorative potential and representing them through graph-based encodings, our framework offers a transferable and interpretable design language. Urban park planners and designers could use these high-ROS graphical representations as templates or constraints within AI-generated content (AIGC) tools for landscape synthesization or simulation77. This extension would enable rapid visual prototyping and feedback, allowing stakeholders to explore, evaluate, and iterate on restorative design concepts more efficiently and with data-driven guidance.

Conclusion

This study introduces a graph-based computational framework for modeling the psychologically restorative potential of urban park landscapes. By combining perceptual encoding and structural reasoning, the proposed framework not only predicts restorative outcomes with high accuracy but also provides interpretable, structural explanations grounded in environmental psychology. The introduction of the ParkScape-PR dataset, along with a scalable LLM-assisted annotation pipeline, advances methodological capabilities for analyzing landscape imagery at the level of human visual experience. Our findings highlight the critical role of spatial structure—beyond the mere presence of natural elements—in shaping restorative perception. Importantly, the graph representation methodology developed in this study offer a transferable, interpretable format that can inform evidence-based and data-driven urban park design. By identifying structural patterns linked to high restorative value, this study lays the groundwork for generative design approaches that merge environmental cognition with computational creativity. More broadly, this study not only offers a new lens for understanding the relationship between urban park landscape and mental well-being. It also contributes to a growing interdisciplinary convergence between computational modeling, environmental psychology, and human-centered design—an intersection that promises to reshape how we envision, evaluate, and construct restorative urban spaces in increasingly complex cities.
