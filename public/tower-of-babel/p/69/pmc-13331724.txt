Integrative Multi‐Omics Analysis Reveals the Tumor‐Suppressive and Immunoregulatory Roles of SEMA5B in Prostate Cancer
Authors: Zhiguo Fan, Yihaoyun Lou, Xuan Zhou, Guang Liu, Xiaomin Zhang, Yuanjie Tang, Zhen Zhu, Shancheng Ren, Yifan Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSEMA5B plays an important role in the maintenance of neural development and is highly environment dependent in tumorigenesis. The roles of SEMA5B in prostate cancer remain underexplored. This study investigates SEMA5B's functions in prostate cancer, revealing its role as a tumor suppressor transcriptionally regulated by androgen receptor and uncovering novel biomarkers and potential immunotherapeutic mechanisms. We analyzed SEMA5B's expression in cancer and its spatial association with cells in the tumor microenvironment using single‐cell transcriptome sequencing datasets and spatial transcriptome sequencing samples. Using bulk RNA‐seq data, we analyzed the immune infiltration of SEMA5B in prostate cancer. The association of SEMA5B with androgen receptor signaling and with metabolic pathways was evaluated by transcriptome and enrichment analysis. The phenotypes, cell cycle, apoptosis, and mitochondrial metabolic function of prostate cancer cell lines were further evaluated. SEMA5B regulation by androgen receptor was examined by gene knockdown, androgen/enzalutamide treatment, and epigenomics. Finally, the effect of SEMA5B on prostate cancer in vivo was detected by tumor xenograft model. Pan‐cancer single‐cell analysis revealed that SEMA5B's expression showed significant tumor type specificity and spatially correlated with immune cell infiltration. SEMA5B is associated with abundant lymphocyte and immune‐inflammatory microenvironment in prostate cancer. SEMA5B overexpression inhibited tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Moreover, high SEMA5B is associated with oxidative phosphorylation and low glycolytic profile. SEMA5B expression is positively correlated with androgen receptor. Androgen receptor inhibition and androgen stimulation affected the expression of SEMA5B. ChIP confirmed the binding of androgen receptor to the SEMA5B promoter. Finally, SEMA5B was verified to induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis and reduce tumor growth. SEMA5B inhibits tumor malignant phenotype and is associated with immune‐inflammatory tumor microenvironment. SEMA5B can regulate mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and induce cell cycle arrest and cell apoptosis, inhibiting the growth of prostate cancer.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most common malignant tumors in men with unique gene mutation characteristics [1]. Androgen receptor (AR) is closely linked in the pathophysiology of PCa, and AR and its transcriptional program are key therapeutic targets in prostate cancer [2]. For patients with locally advanced or metastatic prostate cancer, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) remains the first line treatment [3, 4, 5]. However, despite the initial promising therapeutic effects of ADT treatment or AR inhibitors such as enzalutamide, many patients eventually develop castration‐resistant PCa (CRPC) [6, 7].

Semaphorins (SEMAs) are a class of cell‐surface soluble proteins containing an extracellular domain of about 500 amino acids, which were initially identified as neural guidance factors [8]. Recent studies have found that these proteins may play important roles in physiological and pathophysiological processes [9, 10, 11]. Notably, although SEMAs have been recognized as cancer‐related potential signaling molecules and therapeutic targets, current research is still mainly focused on Class 3 and Class 4 members [12, 13, 14]. SEMA5B, belonging to the SEMAs, undergoes proteolytic processing from its transmembrane form and is distinguished by thrombospondin repeats [15]. Typically, SEMA5B along with its receptors, PlexinA1 and PlexinA3, is involved in neuronal axon development and guidance [16, 17]. Recent research has revealed that SEMA5B participates in the regulation of proliferation in renal cancer cell lines and influences the immune microenvironment [18, 19, 20]. In addition to eliciting inflammation, SEMA5B seems to foster angiogenesis, suggesting a potential role in regulating tumor‐immune interactions [21, 22].

In the study of SEMA5B in prostate cancer, we found that SEMA5B is related to tumor immune regulation and functions as a tumor suppressor. In addition, SEMA5B affects the mitochondrial metabolic capacity of tumor cells and also the cellular pericellular regulation. Our findings provide novel understanding of how the SEMAs family functions.

Methods

Public Data Acquisition and Analysis

Gene expression profiles and clinical data were obtained from public databases. Pan‐cancer single‐cell transcriptome data were obtained from the Sparkle database (https://grswsci.top/), comprising 45 datasets across 29 cancer types. Five prostate cancer spatial transcriptomic samples were obtained from 10× Genomics [23, 24] and the European Genome‐phenome Archive [25]. The Pancancer Multiomics and Spatialomics functions of Sparkle database were used to analyze the data. RNA sequencing and clinical information of TCGA pan‐cancer cohort were acquired by TIMER3 to analyze the relationship between SEMA5B and immune infiltration. GEPIA2 software was utilized to integrate TCGA and GTEx data to evaluate the expression level of SEMA5B in PCa tissues and normal tissues and its correlation with AR. The immunogenomics data set and data related to immunotherapy involving SEMAs family members and their ligands were derived from TISIDB.

Cell Culture

Human prostate cancer cell lines LNCaP, C4‐2, DU145, and PC3 were obtained from the Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, and maintained in RPMI‐1640 medium (Gibco, 11875093, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (BI, 04‐001‐1A, HAEMEK, Israel). Enzalutamide (MDV3100) was acquired from Sellerk (Sellerk, S1250, Houston, USA). Dihydrotestosterone (DHT) was sourced from Merck (Merck, D‐073, Darmstadt, Germany); DMSO was purchased from Sigma, with 0.01% DMSO used as a NC control (Sigma‐Aldrich, 20‐139, St. Louis, USA).

Cell Transfections

The transfection reagent Lipo3000 was acquired from Invitrogen (Invitrogen, L3000008, Carlsbad, USA), and the transfection procedure was conducted in accordance with the provided protocol. A GFP‐labeled SEMA5B plasmid was acquired from Hanheng Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The efficiency of overexpression and functional effects were validated 24 h after plasmid transfection. The CRISPR‐Cas9 assay, aimed at knocking out SEMA5B, was conducted using a CRISPR‐Cas9 toolkit (Santa Cruz, sc‐418922, CA, USA) [26].

Cell Proliferation Assays

To evaluate cellular proliferation, transfected LNCaP, C4‐2, PC3, and DU145 were plated in 96‐well culture plates (Corning, 3599, Corning, USA) at 1.5 × 103 cells/well. Following transfection, cell viability was determined using a CCK‐8 assay kit (Vazyme, A311‐01/02, Nanjing, China). After incubating with a 10% CCK‐8‐RPMI‐1640 mixture for 2 h at 37°C, density measurements were analyzed at a 450 nm wavelength with a Synergy4 microplate spectrophotometer.

Migration and Invasion Assay

Twelve millimeters transwell chambers (Corning, 3402, Corning, USA) were employed to evaluate cell migration and invasion capacities. Transfected prostate cancer cell lines were plated at 1.5 × 103 cells/compartment. Twenty‐four hours later, cells were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, P0099, Shanghai, China) and stained with crystal violet for migration assessment. Invasion assays employed Matrigel‐coated inserts (Corning, 3494, Corning, USA) with fixation/staining at 48 h. Migrated and invaded cells were quantified through ImageJ analysis.

RNA Isolation and qRT‐PCR

RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018CN, Carlsbad, USA), and transcribed into cDNA with the HiScript III RT SuperMix kit (Vazyme, R323‐01, Nanjing, China). qRT‐PCR was performed on a StepOne Plus system (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). GAPDH served as the internal control, and relative mRNA levels were determined by the 2‐ΔΔCt method with StepOne software v2.1. The primer sequences were referred to Table 1.

RNA Sequencing

RNA sequencing analysis was performed with STAR alignment and Salmon quantification. Differential gene expression was analyzed employing DESeq2.

Flow Cytometry

Cells were harvested and processed for apoptosis detection employing the Annexin V‐FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Vazyme, A211, Nanjing, China). Then the trypsinization and PBS washing were processed. Cells were resuspended in binding buffer and in the dark at room temperature, incubated for 10 min with Annexin V–FITC and PI. Stained samples were then analyzed by flow cytometry within 1 h. For cell cycle analysis, cells were processed using the BD Cycletest Plus Kit (BD Biosciences, 340,242, San Jose, USA). The data were acquired employing the BD FACSCanto II system (BD Biosciences, San Jose, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo.

Mouse Tumorigenicity Assay

A group of eight male BALB/c nude mice were acquired from Jiangsu GemPharmatech LLC (https://cn.gempharmatech.com/). Six to Seven weeks male BALB/c nude mice were randomly assigned to SEMA5B‐OE and NC groups (n = 4/group) while the body weight of the mice ranged from 17 to 21 g. DU145 prostate cancer cells, pre‐treated for 24 h with either SEMA5B overexpression construct or empty vector, were subcutaneously injected once into nude mice, and tumor growth was monitored twice weekly for 3 weeks. Tumor bearing mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation at study endpoint. The tumor volume was calculated by V = π/6 × Length × Width2. The methods were approved by the Naval Medical University Animal Care Committee.

ChIP Assay

ChIP assays were performed 72 h post‐transfection using an AR‐specific antibody (Abcam, EPR1535, Cambridge, UK). Immunoprecipitated DNA was analyzed by PCR to identify AR‐binding sites near transcription start sites. Three biological replicates were performed to determine consensus AR chromatin interactions under each experimental condition. The cell line used in AR regulation experiments was treated with DHT and MDV3100 at concentrations of 1–10 nM/μM for 48 h.

Immune Estimate

Computational methods (CIBERSORT, xCell, TIMER, MCP‐counter, EPIC, quanTIseq, ESTIMATE) were employed to characterize tumor microenvironment composition. For the SEMAs family and its potential ligands, TIDE analysis was performed in multiple immunotherapy cohorts [27, 28].

Lymphocyte Proliferation Assay

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were obtained by Percoll gradient centrifugation. T lymphocytes were enriched by negative selection (Invitrogen, 11344D, Carlsbad, USA), after which the purified cells were stimulated with tumor cell‐conditioned medium for 48 h, followed by CFSE labeling and flow cytometric analysis of proliferation.

Whole Transcriptome Correlation Analysis

This study was based on the mRNA expression data of the TCGA‐PRAD cohort [29]. With SEMA5B as the target gene, the Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated between SEMA5B and whole transcriptome gene expression levels. Correlation tests were two‐sided with correction for multiple testing using the Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure. According to the direction of the correlation coefficient and statistical significance, genes with significant positive and negative correlation with SEMA5B were screened.

KEGG‐GSEA Enrichment Analysis

The included genes were ranked according to their correlation coefficient with SEMA5B and subjected to GSEA enrichment analysis based on KEGG pathways. Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler, which can be used for pathway annotation, gene set enrichment analysis and visualization of multi‐omics data [30]. FDR < 0.05 was used as the significant enrichment threshold. A positive normalized enrichment score (NES) indicated that pathways were mainly enriched in SEMA5B positive related genes, and a negative NES indicated that pathways were mainly enriched in SEMA5B negative related genes.

Machine Learning Diagnostic Models

Based on the results of the whole transcriptome correlation analysis, the candidate genes with the strongest correlation with SEMA5B were selected, and combined with SEMA5B to construct a diagnostic modeling gene set. Taking prostate cancer tumor tissues and normal prostate tissues as binary classification outcomes, supervised machine learning algorithms were used to establish tumor‐normal classification models. Model performance was comprehensively evaluated by cross‐validation, residual distribution, ROC curve, and AUC value, and the optimal diagnostic model was determined accordingly [31, 32]. In the optimal model, the SEMA5B‐related core genes with high contribution to classification were further screened by variable importance analysis to form the SEMA5B‐related diagnostic gene module. Subsequently, the expression differences of core model genes in tumor and normal tissues were compared, and the diagnostic ability of individual genes to distinguish tumor from normal samples was evaluated separately. The ROC curve was used to evaluate the classification performance, and the AUC and its confidence interval were used to quantify the diagnostic efficacy.

Immune Infiltration Analysis

To evaluate the relationship between SEMA5B related gene modules and the immune microenvironment of prostate cancer, the expression levels of SEMA5B and its core model genes in PRAD samples were extracted, and the samples were matched with the immune cell infiltration score provided by the TIMER2.0 platform [33]. The relationship between gene expression levels and immune cell infiltration scores was assessed using Spearman's rank correlation analysis with correction for multiple testing using the Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure.

Prediction of Immunotherapy Response

Gene expression data and treatment response information in the published immunotherapy cohorts were collected, and treatment response status was defined as response group and non‐response group. First, using SEMA5B single gene expression as a continuous predictor variable, ROC curves were constructed and AUC was calculated in each immunotherapy cohort to evaluate its ability to predict immunotherapy response. Subsequently, SEMA5B was included in the immunotherapy prediction analysis together with core model genes. Aucs were calculated for each gene in each immunotherapy cohort, and the predictive performance of different genes in different cohorts was presented in the form of heat maps. AUC ≥ 0.7 was defined as having good prediction efficiency [34, 35, 36].

Survival Analysis

In the immunotherapy cohort with survival follow up information, the signature score was calculated based on SEMA5B and its core model genes. According to the signature score, the patients were divided into high score group and low score group, and the Kaplan–Meier method was used for survival analysis. The differences between groups were evaluated by log‐rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression model was further used to calculate hazard ratios and their confidence intervals to evaluate the relationship between SEMA5B related gene modules and the prognosis of patients in the immunotherapy cohort.

Statistical Analysis

Unpaired samples used unpaired t‐test and One‐Way ANOVA was used to compare multiple groups of data, and Dunnett's multiple comparisons test was used among groups. All computations were conducted with R software (v4.2) and GraphPad Prism 8 (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

Pan‐Cancer Single‐Cell and Spatial Transcriptomic Characters of SEMA5B

To disclose the SEMA5B expression landscape in malignant tumors, we integrated 45 single‐cell transcriptome datasets covering 29 cancer types (Figure 1A). Pan‐cancer analysis demonstrated that SEMA5B's expression showed significant tumor type specificity. SEMA5B is specifically expressed in malignant cells of tumor types such as glioma and medulloblastoma. SEMA5B shows distribution characteristics of fibroblasts in solid tumors such as prostate cancer and ovarian cancer, but its expression level is low in hematologic malignancies, suggesting that SEMA5B may play a more important role in the biological process of solid tumors. To further validate the expression pattern of SEMA5B in prostate cancer, we included five prostate cancer spatial transcriptome samples to analyze the enrichment of this gene in different cell types (Figure 1B). The results showed that the expression of SEMA5B was lower in the tumor region than in other regions, and was relatively enriched in the regions with high abundance of prostate epithelial cells, DC cells, and endothelial cells. Correlation analysis showed that SEMA5B was associated with the infiltration of immune cells such as CD8 T cells. It suggests that it may be involved in immune activation and has a positive effect on tumor prognosis (Figure 1C–G).

Analysis of SEMA5B Expression and Its Tumor‐Suppressive and Immunoregulatory Potential in PCa

In the TCGA‐PRAD dataset, the expression of SEMA5B in tumor tissues was significantly lower than that in normal prostate samples (Figure 2A), suggesting a potentially tumor‐suppressive expression pattern. Pan‐cancer deconvolution analysis based on multiple TIMER3 algorithms revealed that, in PCa, higher SEMA5B expression was closely associated with increased infiltration of multiple lymphocyte subsets, including T cells and B cells (Figure 2B). Moreover, SEMA5B expression showed a positive association with the ESTIMATE immune score but an inverse relationship with tumor purity (Figure 2C), suggesting that SEMA5B is preferentially upregulated in immune‐inflamed, lymphocyte‐enriched tumor microenvironments.

Further analysis of immunotherapy‐related cohorts in TISIDB showed that, among SEMA family members and their receptors/ligands, SEMA5B ranked highest in terms of its association with immune checkpoint blockade efficacy and immune cell signature profiles (Figure 2D), underscoring its close link with potential benefit from anti‐tumor immunotherapy. In vitro, qRT‐PCR demonstrated that SEMA5B was generally expressed at low levels with evident heterogeneity across prostate cancer cell lines, with the highest expression in LNCaP cells, followed by C4‐2, PC3, and DU145 (Figure 2E). On this basis, ectopic overexpression of SEMA5B markedly increased its mRNA levels in all four cell lines (Figure 2F). CFSE‐based proliferation assays further confirmed that conditioned media from SEMA5B‐overexpressing C42 and DU145 cells significantly promoted T‐lymphocyte proliferation and cell division (Figure 2G). Taken together, these findings indicate that SEMA5B is downregulated in PCa but is closely associated with an immune‐inflamed tumor microenvironment and T‐cell expansion, suggesting that SEMA5B possesses both tumor‐suppressive and immunoregulatory potential and may play an important role in the development of prostate cancer and responses to immunotherapy.

Functional Validation of the Tumor‐Suppressive Role of SEMA5B in Prostate Cancer

Following the observation that SEMA5B is downregulated in PCa and associated with an immune‐inflamed microenvironment, we next evaluated its tumor‐suppressive potential in prostate cancer cells. Ectopic overexpression of SEMA5B in LNCaP, C4‐2, PC‐3, and DU145 cells reduced cell growth, as indicated by significantly decreased OD450 values in CCK‐8 assays compared with negative controls over time (Figure 3A). In Transwell‐based functional assays, SEMA5B overexpression also led to a marked reduction in cell migration and invasion across all tested cell lines (Figure 3B,C). Complementary loss‐of‐function experiments were then performed in LNCaP cells, which exhibit relatively high endogenous SEMA5B expression, and DU145 cells, which express SEMA5B at lower levels. CRISPR‐mediated knockout of SEMA5B efficiently decreased its mRNA expression (Figure 3D) and was associated with increased proliferative activity, as shown by higher OD450 readings in CCK‐8 assays (Figure 3E), as well as enhanced migratory capacity in Transwell assays (Figure 3F). In summary, these experiments indicate that SEMA5B functions as a tumor suppressor in prostate cancer.

AR Regulates SEMA5B Transcription in Prostate Cancer

Although the central role of AR signaling in prostate cancer progression is well established, the potential association between SEMA5B and the AR pathway remains poorly studied. Having identified SEMA5B as a tumor suppressor in PCa, we further investigated whether its expression was regulated by AR. Analysis of the TCGA‐PRAD cohort showed a significant positive correlation of transcription between SEMA5B and AR, with a stronger correlation in normal prostate tissue and a weaker but still statistically significant correlation in prostate cancer (Figure 4A–C). These results suggest that SEMA5B behaves as an AR responsive gene in normal prostate, and that the transcriptional regulation of SEMA5B by AR is partially uncoupled during malignant transformation. Given that AR normally functions as a transcription factor, we used siRNA to knock down AR in LNCaP and C4‐2 cell lines to observe its regulatory effect on SEMA5B. The efficiency of AR knockdown was verified by qRT‐PCR (Figure 4D) and showed a significant decrease in the mRNA level of SEMA5B in both cell lines (Figure 4E). Pharmacologic modulation of AR signaling revealed that DHT treatment upregulated SEMA5B expression in a dose‐dependent manner, whereas the AR antagonist MDV3100 (enzalutamide) downregulated its expression (Figure 4F). Prediction analysis identified three potential AR binding sites within the SEMA5B promoter region (Figure 4G). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis further showed that AR was significantly enriched in the −1335 and −1289 bp regions upstream of the SEMA5B transcription start site, with less binding at the proximal site (Figure 4G), suggesting that AR directly bound to the SEMA5B distal promoter and regulated its transcriptional activation.

SEMA5B Reprograms Mitochondrial Metabolism in Prostate Cancer

We compared the gene expression profiles of LNCaP in SEMA5B overexpression group and control group by RNA‐seq to explore the transcriptional changes mediated by SEMA5B. The principal component analysis showed a clear separation between the two groups of samples, indicating that SEMA5B overexpression triggered extensive transcriptome reprogramming. Differential expression analysis identified a large number of significantly altered genes, with SEMA5B among the most significantly up‐regulated transcripts. Hierarchical clustering further revealed that the SEMA5B overexpression group showed a different expression pattern from the control group (Figure 5A). To unravel these changes at the functional level, we performed enrichment analysis using Gene Ontology (GO) and WikiPathways. GO enrichment showed that genes altered in the SEMA5B‐OE group were predominantly associated with nuclear speck, chromosomal region, and ATP hydrolysis activity (Figure 5B), while WikiPathways analysis indicated significant enrichment of terms related to cytoplasmic ribosomal proteins, the mitochondrial OXPHOS system, and oxidative phosphorylation (Figure 5C). We next applied GSEA to identify biologically relevant signaling cascades. GO‐based GSEA revealed significant negative enrichment of pathways involved in aerobic respiration, mitochondrial respiratory chain, and oxidative phosphorylation (Figure 5D). Consistently, WikiPathways‐based GSEA confirmed reduced activity of mitochondrial energy‐metabolic pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation and complex I assembly (Figure 5E), suggesting that SEMA5B overexpression suppresses mitochondrial oxidative metabolism.

SEMA5B Impairs Mitochondrial Metabolism to Induce Cell‐Cycle Arrest, Apoptosis, and Tumor Suppression

Building on the transcriptomic evidence of metabolic reprogramming, we next examined the cellular consequences of SEMA5B overexpression. Mechanistically, SEMA5B overexpression impaired mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, as reflected by the negative enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation‐related signatures, and this metabolic inhibition was accompanied by a prominent redistribution of the cell‐cycle profile. Flow‐cytometric analysis showed that SEMA5B‐overexpressing C4‐2 and DU145 cells exhibited a reduced proportion of cycling cells and an accumulation in non‐proliferative phases, indicative of cell‐cycle arrest (Figure 6A). In parallel, Annexin V/7‐AAD staining revealed a significant increase in both early and late apoptotic populations upon SEMA5B overexpression (Figure 6B), demonstrating that SEMA5B not only slows cell‐cycle progression but also actively drives programmed cell death in prostate cancer cells. Consistent with the in vitro results, the xenograft model validated the tumor suppressor function of SEMA5B in vivo. Nude mice transplanted with the SEMA5B overexpressing DU145 cell line developed tumors that were significantly smaller than those in the control group (Figure 6C), indicating that SEMA5B inhibits tumor growth under physiological conditions.

SEMA5B Whole Transcriptome Correlation and Enrichment Analysis

In 498 PRAD samples, 20,138 genes were analyzed by genome‐wide correlation analysis with SEMA5B as the target gene. The results showed that 6872 genes were positively correlated with SEMA5B and 3407 genes were negatively correlated with SEMA5B (p < 0.05). The representative positive correlation genes included FSCN1, ESAM, PLXND1, FLT4, and CD34, and their correlation coefficients were 0.6248, 0.6190, 0.6129, 0.6113, and 0.6040, respectively (Figure 7B). These results suggest that SEMA5B high expression is closely related to cytoskeleton remodeling, endothelial adhesion, vascular/lymphatic endothelial characteristics, and semaphorin‐plexin related cell communication. Representative negatively correlated genes included STEAP4, CSGALNACT1, ALG6, DSC2, and DPAGT1, suggesting that the low expression state of SEMA5B may be accompanied by enhanced epithelial/tumor cell‐intrinsic programs, glycosylation modification, and protein processing.

A total of 70 significantly enriched KEGG pathways (FDR < 0.05) were found after KEGG‐GSEA analysis based on the correlation coefficient ranking of all genes. Representative pathways positively correlated with SEMA5B included ECM‐receptor interaction, cell adhesion molecules, focal adhesion, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, cytokine‐cytokine receptor interaction, JAK–STAT signaling pathway, T cell receptor signaling pathway, B cell receptor signaling pathway, natural killer cell‐mediated cytotoxicity, and leukocyte transendothelial migration. The above results showed that SEMA5B high expression samples had more obvious characteristics of extracellular matrix remodeling, cell adhesion, endothelial‐matrix interaction, immune cell recruitment, and immune inflammatory signaling (Figure 7A).

Instead, the enriched pathways negatively correlated with SEMA5B mainly included aminoacyl‐tRNA biosynthesis, N‐glycan biosynthesis, proteasome, ribosome, spliceosome, RNA degradation, ubiquitin‐mediated proteolysis, pyruvate metabolism, citrate cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. These pathways mainly reflect tumor cell intrinsic protein synthesis, glycosylation, protein homeostasis, RNA processing, and mitochondrial energy metabolism programs. Collectively, the transcriptomic profile of SEMA5B supports its dual role in bridging the immune‐vascular‐matrix microenvironment and metabolic inhibition programs.

Machine Learning Diagnostic Model of SEMA5B Related Genes and Immune Infiltration Analysis

Based on the SEMA5B coexpression network in Figure 7, this study further included the top 30 genes with the strongest correlation with SEMA5B and combined with SEMA5B itself to construct a diagnostic modeling gene set consisting of 31 candidate genes. A total of 494 PRAD samples and 52 normal prostate samples were included. Tumor and normal tissue were used as dichotomous outcomes. After model construction by various machine learning algorithms, the results of residual distribution and reverse cumulative distribution showed that the Random Forest model had the best overall classification performance (Figure 8A,B).

Based on the variable importance ranking of the best model, 10 key SEMA5B related model genes were finally selected, including FSCN1, FLT4, CLEC14A, SEPTIN4, CD34, HIGD1B, SPARC, COL5A3, THY1, and ACVRL1. These genes were mainly related to vascular endothelium, lymphatic endothelium, matrix remodeling, extracellular matrix organization, and cytoskeleton regulation, consistent with the results of the SEMA5B‐related transcriptional network in Figure 7 pointing to the immune‐vascular‐matrix microenvironment.

Further comparison of the differentially expressed 10 model genes between PRAD tissues and normal prostate tissues revealed that FLT4, CLEC14A, SEPTIN4, CD34, SPARC, and ACVRL1 were significantly down‐regulated in tumor tissues (Figure 8C). Single‐gene ROC analysis showed that these SEMA5B related model genes had tumor‐normal tissue discrimination ability, among which ACVRL1 showed the relatively highest AUC, suggesting that this gene module could be used as a SEMA5B related auxiliary diagnostic feature (Figure 8D).

Immune infiltration correlation analysis showed that SEMA5B and its related model genes were widely correlated with a variety of immune cell infiltration scores, especially involving immune cell populations such as dendritic cells, B cells, T cells, and NK cells (Figure 8E). These results suggest that SEMA5B related model genes reflect an immune‐vascular‐stromal microenvironment state, rather than merely the intrinsic expression characteristics of tumor cells.

Immunotherapy Response Prediction and Survival Analysis of SEMA5B and Its Related Gene Modules

The ability of SEMA5B single‐gene expression levels to predict treatment response was evaluated in 30 published immunotherapy cohorts. The results showed that the AUC of five datasets was ≥ 0.7, which achieved good prediction performance. The AUC of 22 datasets ranged from 0.5 to 0.7, indicating moderate prediction performance. The AUC of three datasets was less than 0.5. The mean AUC across the cohorts was 0.624, suggesting that the SEMA5B single gene had moderate and stable predictive power across multiple immunotherapy cohorts (Figure 9A).

The response prediction ability of SEMA5B‐related 11‐gene modules was further evaluated in 31 immunotherapy cohorts. The included genes included SEMA5B, FSCN1, FLT4, CLEC14A, SEPTIN4, CD34, HIGD1B, SPARC, COL5A3, THY1, and ACVRL1. A total of 329 gene‐cohort combination analyses were completed, of which 87 analyses reached AUC ≥ 0.7, with an average AUC of 0.643. After being aggregated by genes, ACVRL1 reached AUC ≥ 0.7 in 12 datasets, showing outstanding predictive ability, suggesting that vascular endothelial and microenvironment components in SEMA5B related modules may have important value in the prediction of immunotherapy response (Figure 9B).

In the immunotherapy cohort with survival follow up information, the ssGSEA signature score was constructed based on 11 SEMA5B related genes, and survival stratification analysis was performed. In representative cohorts, patients in the high score group showed a trend toward better overall survival, including the KIRC anti‐PD1 cohort (log‐rank p = 0.023, HR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.23–0.95, p = 0.037), SKCM anti‐PD1 cohort (log‐rank p = 0.016, HR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.07–0.81, p = 0.022), and SKCM anti‐CTLA4 Post cohort (log‐rank p = 0.030, HR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.01–2.18, p = 0.166) (Figure 9C). These results suggest that SEMA5B‐related gene modules can not only predict the response to immunotherapy but also have the potential for survival stratification after immunotherapy.

Discussion

SEMA5B was first identified as a transmembrane signaling protein in the nervous system, which is involved in the regulation of axon guidance, synaptic connection, and neural circuit formation [37, 38, 39, 40]. In addition to its classical function in neurodevelopment, SEMA5B has also been found to be related to cancer biology and metabolic regulation in recent years. In renal cell carcinoma, SEMA5B expression is increased and plays as a tumor promoter while it is associated with a better prognosis in burkitt lymphoma [20, 41]. In vivo studies by Kundu et al. showed that SEMA5B promoted tumor growth and reshaped the tumor microenvironment [18]. Furthermore, experimental models of maternal high‐fat diet exposure have shown upregulation of SEMA5B expression in the offspring, which is associated with increased breast tumor susceptibility, suggesting that SEMA5B may be associated with systemic metabolic disorders and cancer risk [42]. In summary, these findings suggest that SEMA5B plays an environmentally dependent role in regulating development and tumorigenesis, and its role in prostate cancer needs to be elucidated urgently.

The environment dependent role of SEMA5B may be related to differences in the receptor repertoire present on tumor cells and cells within the tumor microenvironment. Semaphorin signaling is primarily mediated through Plexin receptors, and the biological outcome of this signaling can vary according to the specific Plexin receptor profile and downstream signaling partners expressed in a given cellular context [12, 43]. For example, Type B Plexins have been implicated in prostate cancer biology, whereas Plexin‐B3 has been reported as a functional receptor for SEMA5A and can mediate distinct cellular responses in different cancer contexts [44, 45]. Therefore, the tumor‐suppressive association of SEMA5B observed in prostate cancer may depend on the receptor landscape of PCa cells and their surrounding stromal or immune compartments. Differences in Plexin receptor expression between prostate cancer and other tumor types may partly explain the context‐dependent effects of SEMA family signaling.

In this study, we evaluated the immune infiltration profile of SEMA5B and found that SEMA5B may play both tumor suppressive and immune regulatory roles in prostate cancer, suggesting a potential role of SEMA5B in prostate cancer progression and response to immunotherapy. Consistent with this, in vitro results showed that SEMA5B inhibited the malignant behavior of prostate cancer cells, while SEMA5B knockdown had the opposite effects. These findings suggest that SEMA5B functions as a classical tumor suppressor in prostate cancer. Together with its strong association with a lymphocyte‐rich, immune‐inflamed microenvironment and immune activation‐related signatures in PCa, these findings support the view that SEMA5B may act as a key mediator linking intrinsic tumor suppression with extrinsic antitumor immunity.

The positive association between SEMA5B expression, lymphocyte infiltration, and T‐cell proliferation raises the possibility that SEMA5B may contribute to the conversion of immunologically “cold” prostate tumors into “hot” tumors. Mechanistically, this effect may involve chemokine‐mediated recruitment of T cells. Previous studies have shown that the CXCL9/CXCL10/CXCL11–CXCR3 axis plays a central role in cytotoxic T‐cell trafficking, immune activation, and antitumor responses, while CCL5 can cooperate with CXCL9 to support tumor‐infiltrating T‐cell recruitment [46, 47]. Therefore, SEMA5B‐associated immune activation may be partly mediated through these T‐cell‐attracting chemokine pathways. However, whether SEMA5B directly regulates these chemokines or indirectly modulates them through stromal or myeloid cells remains to be determined. Further studies, including chemokine profiling, spatial immune mapping, and functional perturbation experiments, are needed to validate the role of SEMA5B in promoting a “hot” tumor immune phenotype.

Currently, therapeutic strategies for PCa rely predominantly on androgen‐deprivation therapy and pharmacologic inhibition of AR signaling [48, 49]. Our data identify SEMA5B as a downstream transcriptional target of AR with clear tumor‐suppressive properties. Results suggest that SEMA5B is an AR‐responsive gene in the normal prostate and that this regulatory relationship becomes partially uncoupled during malignant transformation. The combination of pharmacological experiments and epigenomics places SEMA5B within the AR‐driven transcriptional network in prostate epithelial cells and suggests that alterations in AR signaling may have important consequences for SEMA5B‐mediated tumor suppression and immune modulation.

Our study reveals for the first time that SEMA5B is closely related to the regulation of mitochondrial metabolism in PCa. Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation is not only involved in ATP production but also contributes to redox regulation, biosynthetic activity, tumor cell survival, metastatic adaptation, and therapeutic resistance in several cancer contexts [50, 51, 52]. The high expression of SEMA5B is associated with oxidative phosphorylation, tricarboxylic acid cycle activity, and mitochondrial respiration. In contrast, low SEMA5B expression is associated with a shift to glycolysis and other tumor‐promoting anabolic programs in the tumor microenvironment. Combined with functional experiments, it is suggested that SEMA5B may maintain a metabolic state with stronger oxidation and lower glycolysis by limiting metabolic reprogramming, thus exerting its role in tumor suppression and immune regulation to a certain extent [53]. Therefore, the downregulation of mitochondrial oxidative metabolism observed in our data may indicate a less energetically active and less aggressive tumor phenotype, which is in line with the proposed tumor‐suppressive role of SEMA5B. Nevertheless, further functional metabolic assays, such as oxygen consumption rate analysis, are required to directly validate the regulatory relationship between SEMA5B and mitochondrial metabolism.

This metabolic inhibition was accompanied by significant changes in cell cycle distribution. SEMA5B overexpression resulted in a decrease in the proportion of S and G2 phase cells in tumor cells, suggesting cell cycle arrest. At the same time, SEMA5B also significantly increased the population of early and late apoptotic cells, indicating that SEMA5B not only delays cell cycle progression, but also induces programmed cell death in prostate cancer cells.

Given the strong tropism of prostate cancer for bone, the potential relevance of SEMA5B‐mediated metabolic regulation to the bone metastatic niche warrants further consideration. The bone microenvironment is metabolically distinct and exposes disseminated tumor cells to unique selective pressures, including altered nutrient availability, relative hypoxia, oxidative stress, lipid‐derived substrates from marrow adipocytes, and reciprocal interactions with osteoblasts and osteoclasts [54]. Previous studies have shown that prostate cancer cells undergo metabolic adaptation in the skeletal microenvironment, and bone marrow adipocytes can further shape tumor metabolism through lipid transfer and metabolic crosstalk [55, 56]. In this context, the suppression of mitochondrial oxidative metabolism associated with SEMA5B may affect the metabolic fitness, survival, dormancy, or reactivation of prostate cancer cells within the bone niche. Although our current study does not directly evaluate bone metastasis, these findings provide a rationale for future studies investigating whether SEMA5B modulates prostate cancer adaptation to the bone microenvironment.

Despite these strengths, our study has several limitations. Most mechanistic data were generated in a limited set of prostate cancer cell lines and xenograft models, which may not fully capture the heterogeneity of human tumors. In addition, although we identify SEMA5B as a direct AR target and link it to mitochondrial metabolism, the broader signaling networks around the AR‐SEMA5B axis remain incompletely defined. Future studies with larger patient cohorts, additional PCa models, and prospective validation will be needed to further define the clinical and therapeutic relevance of the AR‐SEMA5B axis.

Conclusion

Our study identifies SEMA5B as an AR‐induced, context‐dependent tumor suppressor in prostate cancer that links intrinsic growth control with extrinsic antitumor immunity. We show that SEMA5B restrains malignant phenotypes, is associated with an immune‐inflamed tumor microenvironment, and has survival stratification value after immunotherapy. SEMA5B may serve as a novel prostate cancer biomarker and holds potential as a target for androgen‐related therapies.
