A unified vision-language model for precision oncology and biomarker prediction in neuroblastoma
Authors: Jin Zhu, Ruizhen Hu, Sen Yang, Qing Sun, Zhenzhen Zhao, Juan Cao, Peiying Pan, Kun Wang, Liyan Cui, Hongping Tang, Qianqian Fang, Sijin Jiang, Linli Lei, Wenjian Zhang, Jiajun Xie, Shuo Kang, Dongyuan Xiao, Ming Xiao, Xuan Zhai, Yuntao Jia, Junyang Chen, Wei Yuan, Xiao Han, Le Lu, Junhan Zhao, Xiyue Wang, Yi Li, Jinxi Xiang, Biyue Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neuroblastoma is a leading cause of childhood cancer mortality, presenting persistent management challenges due to its biological heterogeneity and the limited accessibility of molecular profiling in routine practice. Here we present NEVA (NEuroblastoma Vision–language AI), a multimodal foundation model designed to address these barriers. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on frozen encoders and multiple instance learning, NEVA implements a pathologist-inspired hierarchical workflow with end-to-end optimization. Developed and evaluated in a large multi-institutional cohort of 1,238 patients across multiple centers, NEVA outperforms ten representative foundation models, including TITAN, UNI, and Virchow, across the majority of the 11 clinical tasks evaluated. The model demonstrates diagnostic capability, achieving Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curves of 0.916 for subtype classification, 0.823 for Shimada classification, and 0.806 for risk group stratification. Furthermore, NEVA predicts key molecular alterations from routinely available pathology data, reaching an AUROC of 0.924 for NMYC amplification and 0.830 for 1p36 deletion, while enabling prognostic stratification for progression-free and overall survival across multiple test cohorts. By integrating interpretable attention maps that localize histologically relevant regions, NEVA establishes a scalable framework for neuroblastoma risk stratification and clinical decision support.

Introduction

Neuroblastoma, the most common extracranial solid tumor in children, accounts for approximately 15% of all pediatric cancer-related deaths1,2. Although numerous efforts have been invested in therapeutic interventions3, neuroblastoma remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in children, especially high-risk neuroblastoma that represents 40–50% of cases with poor survival4. With marked biological heterogeneity5, the therapeutic decision-making of neuroblastoma relies on comprehensive information derived from age, stage, histopathological analysis, and molecular diagnosis3,5,6, including molecular markers and chromosomal abnormalities that correlate with disease stages, relapse rates, and prognosis7. However, the widespread utilization of precision care for neuroblastoma is constrained by the global disparity of pediatric medical resources, especially in limited-resource settings8,9. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop novel diagnostic tools for the automated management and care of neuroblastoma.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated growing utility in oncology, with applications spanning diagnostic classification10–15, treatment stratification16–19, prognostication20–23, and biomarker discovery24–26. However, the application of AI in neuroblastoma remains relatively underexplored. Existing approaches are predominantly unimodal, focusing on either imaging27,28 or genomic29 data, and are designed for narrowly defined tasks such as NMYC amplification detection, tumor subtype classification, or survival prediction30,31. These models are typically trained on small, single-institution datasets and rely heavily on manual annotations or molecular labels, limiting their scalability and generalizability. Moreover, most prior systems are architecturally siloed, and task-specific-a model built for histopathology classification cannot be easily repurposed for survival analysis, while genomic models often fail to generalize due to platform variability and missing data. Few models account for the interdependence of critical biological features, such as chromosomal aberrations, somatic mutations, and histologic parameters, that collectively influence disease behavior and prognosis assessment32,33. These methodological gaps constrain clinical utility, reinforcing narrow workflows rather than offering integrated decision-support tools that reflect the real-world complexity of neuroblastoma management.

The emergence of foundation models, large-scale AI systems pre-trained on diverse data, has opened new avenues for overcoming these limitations34–38. Vision-language models (VLMs), in particular, offer an approach to bridge whole-slide images (WSIs) and clinical notes34–36. By jointly embedding these modalities, VLMs can capture complex relationships with minimal supervision, supporting multi-task learning across institutions and clinical scenarios35. However, general-purpose VLMs face a key limitation in neuroblastoma: the disease is severely underrepresented in their pretraining data, leading to domain shift and reduced performance. Simply applying off-the-shelf models is therefore insufficient, and neuroblastoma-specific applications of foundation models remain relatively underexplored. Dedicated strategies are required to unlock their full potential for neuroblastoma diagnosis, biomarker prediction, and prognosis.

To address this gap, we introduce NEVA (NEuroblastoma Vision–language AI), a unified vision-language foundation model for precision neuroblastoma care developed through a large-scale, multi-center study. The framework leverages histology images and pathology reports to support 11 clinically relevant tasks spanning diagnosis, biomarker prediction, and prognosis. By implementing a pathologist-inspired hierarchical workflow, NEVA achieves improved computational efficiency while demonstrating generalization across multiple centers for clinical decision-making. This work presents a scalable AI system for neuroblastoma care and demonstrates its potential for task-flexible foundation models in rare diseases.

Reflecting the clinical workflow, NEVA implements a two-stage, pathologist-inspired pipeline (Fig. 1c). In the screening phase (Stage 1), the model mimics low-power scanning by identifying diagnostically relevant regions, filtering tiles via a hybrid scoring system that combines attention-based deep learning (FEATHER39) with histological quality metrics (e.g., nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, tissue quantity). In the diagnostic phase (Stage 2), these selected regions are reprocessed alongside clinical reports using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) of the MUSK35 encoder, fusing visual and textual features for final prediction.

Notably, NEVA improves computational efficiency. Benchmarked on an NVIDIA RTX 4090, processing a WSI with approximately 5000 patches, the proposed method reduced the average inference time to ~15.6 s, compared with ~75.6 s for the conventional approach (frozen encoder processes all patches with MIL). This acceleration demonstrates the practical advantage of NEVA’s two-stage strategy.

Finally, in comprehensive benchmark evaluations, NEVA consistently outperformed representative vision-language (CONCH_v1.536, TITAN40, PRISM41) and vision-only (CHIEF10, GigaPath42, UNI43, Virchow44, CtransPath45, FEATHER39, H-optimus46) foundation models, supporting the integration of histopathology images with clinical text for decision support (Fig. 1d).

NEVA supports accurate diagnosis

We evaluated NEVA on four diagnostic tasks: Histologic Subtype, MKI, Shimada classification, and Risk Group. These tasks exhibit substantial interobserver variability and typically require specialized pediatric pathology expertise. Histologic subtyping distinguishes neuroblastoma, ganglioneuroblastoma, and ganglioneuroma, representing a malignant-to-benign biological spectrum47. The MKI quantifies tumor proliferative activity through mitotic and karyorrhectic counts, serving as a well-established prognostic indicator7. Shimada classification (International Neuroblastoma Pathology Classification) categorizes tumors into Favorable or Unfavorable Histology groups, providing critical prognostic guidance48. Risk stratification followed the International Neuroblastoma Risk Group (INRG) system49, where accurate classification is essential to avoid overtreatment in low-risk patients while ensuring appropriate therapy intensity in higher-risk cases49. Evaluations were performed on an internal test set, two temporally separated prospective test sets, and three external multi-center cohorts from PUFH, SCH, and GCI.

As shown in Fig. 2a–d and Supplementary Fig. 2a–d and Tables 2–7, NEVA achieved diagnostic performance across all test cohorts, with macro-average AUROCs of 0.823 for Shimada classification, 0.916 for subtype classification, 0.791 for MKI classification, and 0.806 for Risk Group classification. The model consistently matched or exceeded benchmark models, performing comparably to TITAN, CONCH_v1.5, Virchow, FEATHER, and H-optimus on Shimada classification while surpassing PRISM, CHIEF, UNI, CtransPath, and GigaPath. On subtype classification, NEVA performed similarly to top-performing CHIEF (0.917) but exceeded all other models. For MKI classification, it outperformed TITAN, PRISM, FEATHER, UNI, GigaPath, Virchow, and H-optimus, with performance comparable to CHIEF, CtransPath, and CONCH_v1.5. In the Risk Group classification, NEVA surpassed all benchmarked models. To evaluate the model’s capacity for comprehensive risk assessment, we analyzed its performance in predicting the integrated INRG pretreatment categories49. Overall, the model achieved an accuracy of 88.2% (75/85) across the four risk groups (Very Low, Low, Intermediate, and High). Within the clinically heterogeneous L2 subgroup (n = 20), it attained 90.0% (18/20) accuracy in distinguishing between Low-risk and Intermediate-risk patients (Supplementary Table 8). These findings suggest that histopathology-based AI may augment multi-factorial risk stratification in neuroblastoma, although the limited sample size in certain subgroups highlights the need for validation in larger cohorts.

Furthermore, to enhance the transparency and clinical trustworthiness of NEVA, we performed a detailed interpretability analysis. Board-certified pathologists, blinded to model predictions, evaluated high-attention (top 1%) and low-attention (bottom 1%) patches from independent datasets. As shown in Fig. 2e and Supplementary Figs. 3–6, NEVA demonstrates alignment with pathological principles. Specifically, high-attention regions consistently corresponded to diagnostic features across all tasks: neuroblastoma showed diffusely distributed small round tumor cells; ganglioneuroma displayed abundant schwannian stroma with mature ganglion cells; high-MKI cases exhibited necrotic tissue with karyorrhexis and mitotic figures; high-risk tumors contained coagulative necrosis; and unfavorable Shimada subtypes demonstrated fibrous hyperplasia with hyaline degeneration. In contrast, low-attention patches primarily contained non-specific stromal components such as fibrous tissue, adipose tissue, hemorrhage, or myxoid degeneration, confirming that NEVA learns pathologically meaningful features.

NEVA predicts key genetic biomarkers

Accurate identification of molecular biomarkers is critical for risk stratification and treatment planning in neuroblastoma50,51. However, access to molecular testing can be limited by cost, infrastructure, or sample constraints. We evaluated whether NEVA could predict key biomarkers directly from histopathology slides and reports. Prediction tasks included established prognostic markers NMYC amplification, 1p36 deletion, and 11q23 deletion52, as well as ALK and CMYC expression levels, which are biomarkers of growing mechanistic or therapeutic research interest53–57. As shown in Fig. 3a–e and Supplementary Fig. 7a–d and Tables 9–11, NEVA achieved the highest macro-average AUROC on all five biomarker prediction tasks: 0.924 for NMYC, 0.830 for 1p36, 0.776 for 11q23, 0.764 for ALK, and 0.703 for CMYC. The model consistently outperformed all benchmark models. Notably, NEVA demonstrated relative improvements over key competitors, achieving performance gains of 50.7% over PRISM on ALK prediction, 36.9% over TITAN on NMYC prediction, and 19.6% over TITAN on 11q23 prediction.

We further validated NEVA’s interpretability across all biomarker prediction tasks using the same attention-based protocol applied in diagnosis. As shown in Fig. 3f and Supplementary Figs. 8–14, high-attention regions consistently highlighted morphologically distinctive and diagnostically relevant areas. In NMYC-amplified cases, these included densely packed, pleomorphic tumor cells with frequent karyorrhexis, mitotic figures, and occasional “bull’s-eye cells.” In 1p36-deleted cases, high-attention areas showed coagulative necrosis, vascular hyperplasia with “onion-skin” perivascular structures, and prominent nucleoli. For 11q23-deleted cases, tumor giant cells and Homer-Wright rosettes were selectively attended to. In ALK- and CMYC-overexpressed cases, high-attention regions respectively emphasized neuropil-infiltrating tumor cells with low mitotic activity and schwannian stroma-rich zones. Across all tasks, low-attention patches predominantly corresponded to non-specific stromal components such as fibroadipose tissue, hemorrhage, or treatment-related changes, reinforcing that NEVA’s attention aligns with pathologically meaningful features.

These data highlight NEVA’s capability as a versatile tool for biomarker identification directly from routine histopathology data.

NEVA enables prognosis stratification

Prognostic stratification remains a core challenge in neuroblastoma58. Emerging evidence suggests that computational approaches can decode subtle histopathological features not fully leveraged by current stratification systems to facilitate outcome prediction35,59,60. We therefore evaluated whether NEVA could augment prognostic accuracy by estimating PFS and OS directly from WSIs and pathology reports across internal and external PUFH cohorts. Risk stratification into high- versus low-risk groups was performed using hazard ratios and log-rank tests.

As shown in Fig. 4a and Supplementary Figs. 15–19 and Tables 12–14, NEVA demonstrated prognostic capability across multi-center cohorts, achieving the highest average C-index on OS at 0.717 and competitive performance on PFS at 0.645, as detailed in Supplementary Tables 12–14. The model consistently matched or exceeded benchmark models, surpassing all competing VLMs (TITAN, PRISM, CONCH_v1.5) and seven vision-only foundation models (including CHIEF, FEATHER, UNI, Virchow, H-optimus, CtransPath) in OS prediction. For PFS prediction, NEVA performed comparably to models such as CONCH_v1.5 (0.659) and GigaPath (0.655). We next evaluated the prognostic value of model-predicted and experimentally determined biomarkers based on their ability to stratify patients into high- and low-risk groups. Consistent with established clinical evidence, the true molecular-assay-detected status of NMYC amplification significantly stratified patient survival (Supplementary Fig. 20), and this prognostic value was successfully recapitulated by our model across most test cohorts (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, ALK overexpression emerged as a potential prognostic indicator, and its model-predicted status also enabled significant risk stratification on the internal test set (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 20).

We also observed distinct patterns in different contexts (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21). For 11q23 deletion, both the true and predicted status showed non-significant prognostic trends across most analyses. For CMYC, the model-predicted status achieved significant risk stratification in the internal but not the external test set, whereas its true biomarker status did not show a strong prognostic association. Although true 1p36 deletion was prognostic, its model-predicted counterpart exhibited only a non-significant trend.

These data demonstrate the model’s capacity to identify key prognostic biomarkers, while also revealing context-dependent performance. This variability may arise from biological heterogeneity, the complexity of learning specific alterations, and limited sample size.

We conducted the interpretability analysis for prognosis prediction using the same attention-based approach as in diagnosis. As shown in Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 15, high-attention regions in poor-outcome groups (OS status 1) consistently highlighted morphologically aggressive features, including neuropil-infiltrating tumor cells exhibiting two distinct patterns—small round cells with chromatin-rich nuclei and scant cytoplasm, alongside larger cells with visible nucleoli and mitotic activity. In contrast, low-attention patches in these cases predominantly corresponded to normal adrenal tissue. For favorable-outcome cases (OS status 0), high-attention regions emphasized stromal changes such as hyaline degeneration, while low-attention areas showed coagulative necrosis or hemosiderin-laden stroma. Similarly, in PFS prediction, high-attention patches in progressive disease (PFS status 1) localized to necrotic tumor tissue with calcification, whereas low-attention regions contained differentiated ganglion cells. In non-progressive cases (PFS status 0), high-attention areas were associated with stromal components, and low-attention regions with coagulative necrosis. These patterns confirm that NEVA’s prognostic attention is driven by histopathologically meaningful features related to tumor progression and treatment response.

Multimodal integration improves neuroblastoma prediction across clinical tasks

To evaluate the added clinical utility of integrating histology images and pathology reports, we systematically compared the performance of our multimodal NEVA model against unimodal counterparts using either WSIs or pathology text alone. Comparative analyses were conducted across the internal test set, prospective validation cohorts, and multi-center external datasets for 11 clinical tasks.

As shown in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Tables 15–20, the multimodal NEVA achieved improved performance in 8 of 11 tasks, with marked gains in biomarker prediction. The most notable gains in average performance were observed for ALK prediction (0.764 vs. 0.571 [image] and 0.648 [report]), NMYC amplification (0.924 vs. 0.807 and 0.765), and 1p36 deletion (0.830 vs. 0.663 and 0.718). On the PUFH external set, only the full model significantly stratified both PFS (P = 0.044) and OS (P = 0.042), whereas both unimodal variants failed (image-only: PFS P = 0.398, OS P = 0.513; report-only: PFS P = 0.076, OS P = 0.416). Discussion

This work presents a multimodal AI approach for neuroblastoma diagnosis and prognosis by integrating routinely available WSIs and paired pathology reports. By embedding a vision-language foundation model into a clinically aligned, hierarchical workflow, NEVA captures complex morphological and clinical representations without relying on task-specific engineering. This enables interpretation across a range of clinically relevant endpoints while reducing dependence on expert annotations and expensive molecular assays36,43.

In contrast to prior neuroblastoma studies that employed task-specific, supervised learning approaches27,61, NEVA adopts a unified multimodal, multi-task framework capable of addressing a broad array of diagnostic, prognostic, and biomarker prediction tasks. This design supports a more holistic view of disease biology, aligning with the multi-factorial nature of neuroblastoma, where risk stratification depends on histologic, molecular, and clinical parameters49,62. Our findings contribute to the growing evidence that foundation models may offer advantages over domain-specific models in some oncology tasks, offering improved accuracy, scalability, and adaptability across varied clinical contexts10,38.

NEVA achieves robust performance across 11 tasks. The results demonstrate that foundation models can extract clinically meaningful visual and textual features without the need for extensive manual annotations. Moreover, model interpretations from both images and reports align closely with established pathological knowledge, supporting their relevance to pathological interpretation63,64. Compared to 10 state-of-the-art foundation models, NEVA consistently delivers improved performance in most tasks, underscoring the synergistic value of combining pathology images and reports within a unified architecture.

NEVA addresses key diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in neuroblastoma, where delays and inaccuracies can critically affect outcomes, especially for high-risk patients. Current limitations-including interobserver variability, restricted access to molecular testing, and the slow integration of histologic and other clinical data-often lead to under- or overtreatment48,51. Even in advanced centers, aligning histological features with biomarkers like NMYC remains time-consuming. NEVA may help address these barriers by enabling rapid, standardized analysis of histopathological features, molecular correlates, and prognostic indicators directly from routine H&E-stained slides and pathology reports. This streamlines risk stratification and accelerates clinical decision-making, facilitating timely access to targeted therapies for high-risk cases while reducing unnecessary toxicity in low-risk patients. By bridging the gap between histopathology and precision oncology, NEVA may improve the efficiency and accessibility of neuroblastoma risk assessment.

In addition to established prognostic biomarkers, we expanded our biomarker panel to include ALK and CMYC protein expression based on their growing mechanistic or therapeutic research interest53–57. Notably, the prognostic significance of ALK overexpression was supported by both true detection and model prediction, as both enabled significant risk stratification, suggesting its potential as a prognostic indicator. Interestingly, although true CMYC overexpression showed only a non-significant prognostic trend, the model identified a specific morphological pattern within the predicted CMYC group that carried significant prognostic value. These exploratory analyses suggest the potential of our AI model to uncover associations between histopathological features and prognosis, warranting further investigation.

Nonetheless, this study has limitations. First, although our current cohort includes 1238 patients from multiple centers, it may not fully reflect the genetic and demographic diversity of global neuroblastoma populations, which could limit generalizability. Second, long-term prognostic endpoints were not included in the prospective test set due to limited follow-up, constraining validation of survival predictions in a real-world setting. Third, model performance for certain tasks (e.g., PFS) indicates room for improvement, possibly due to weak or heterogeneous morphological cues. In addition, our survival analyses revealed important discrepancies between the prognostic performance of true and model-predicted biomarkers of 1p36, 11q23, and CMYC. In addition, while FISH was the clinical standard during our study period, its use for genomic biomarkers may not capture the full complexity revealed by advanced techniques (e.g., SNP arrays). These findings highlight key challenges in model development, including biological heterogeneity, the complexity of learning specific molecular alterations, and statistical constraints imposed by limited sample sizes. Despite these limitations, AI-based prediction may still provide clinically meaningful information, particularly in settings where access to comprehensive biomarker testing is limited, and represents an important direction for future methodological improvement.

Future work should prioritize larger, more diverse cohorts—including underrepresented populations and rare subtypes—and explore integration with complementary modalities such as multi-omics to further improve prediction fidelity24,65.

In summary, NEVA represents an accessible approach for AI-assisted neuroblastoma care by integrating multimodal inputs readily available in clinical workflows. It combines the strengths of foundation models with real-world applicability, offering an approach for diagnostic support and risk stratification. With extensive validations, NEVA has the potential to reduce disparities in access to advanced diagnostics and improve personalized treatment strategies for children with neuroblastoma worldwide.

Methods

Ethics statement

This multi-center study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (No. 2023-436). This study utilized digital images of anonymized archival tissue samples. All data collection and analysis procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and other relevant ethical regulations. The study followed an institutional ethics committee-approved observational protocol governing data analysis. Informed consent was waived by the institutional review boards because the study involved analysis of de-identified clinical data and digital pathology images obtained during routine clinical care, posed minimal risk to participants, and did not involve prospective recruitment, intervention, or alteration of patient management. Although temporally separated cohorts were used for prospective validation, these data were collected as part of standard clinical practice without additional intervention or prospective recruitment.

Data collection

We assembled a multi-institutional dataset comprising 1238 NB patients diagnosed between March 2010 and August 2025, with matched 1419 pathology reports and 3593 WSIs of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded H&E-stained tissues. The data originated from four sources: the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (CHCMU: 1146 patients, 1325 reports, 3453 WSIs), Peking University First Hospital (PUFH: 46 patients, 48 reports, 93 WSIs), Shenzhen Children’s Hospital (SCH: 27 patients, 27 reports, 27 WSIs), and a combined set from Guiyang Maternal and Child Healthcare Hospital, Guiyang Children’s Hospital, the Children’s Brain Tumor Network, and Inner Mongolia Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital (GCI: 19 patients, 19 reports, 20 WSIs). Within this dataset, we defined a retrospective development cohort comprising all patients diagnosed between March 2010 and October 2023.

To rigorously assess the model’s performance in a real-world, forward-looking setting, we reserved patients diagnosed after November 2023 as a temporally separated validation series. This temporally separated prospective validation series (257 patients with 260 reports and 553 WSIs) consisted of two sequential cohorts from CHCMU: Prospective Validation Cohort 1 (November 2023–September 2024, 78 patients, 81 reports, 217 WSIs) and Prospective Validation Cohort 2 (October 2024–August 2025, 179 patients, 179 reports, 336 WSIs).

All model development (training, hyperparameter tuning) was completed and locked using only data collected prior to November 2023. The model was then applied without any modification to cases that presented sequentially after this cut-off (November 2023–August 2025), simulating its application to future patients. This sequential design tests the model’s immediate and longer-term temporal generalizability.

This study employed a standardized whole-slide digital scanning protocol across multiple centers. Specifically, CHCMU, GMCH, and IMMCH utilized SQS-1200P scanners at 40× magnification with a resolution of 0.209 μm per pixel; PUFH employed KF-PRO-120 scanners at 40× magnification with 0.125 μm per pixel resolution; and SCH used SQ-600P scanners at 20× magnification with 0.215 μm per pixel resolution. For each patient, at least one H&E-stained tissue section was acquired to ensure morphological representation, with all image quality verified by a team of certified pathologists and clinicians including J.Z., Q.S., P.P., K.W., L.C., and J.C. Clinical data were extracted from institutional electronic medical records using pathological identification numbers, encompassing the following aspects: tumor characteristics including histological subtypes, favorable/unfavorable Shimada classification, and low/intermediate/high MKI status; risk stratification according to low/intermediate/high criteria documented at the time of pathological slide collection; and survival outcomes including PFS and OS obtained from each center’s records.

For the risk group task, our total cohort comprised 651 patients with clinically annotated risk groups, including 170 patients in the combined very low-risk and low-risk category, 141 intermediate-risk patients, and 340 high-risk cases. For model development, the cohort was partitioned into distinct datasets for training and testing purposes. Regarding the INRG risk group distribution, we analyzed the subset of patients for whom all requisite information for definitive INRG classification was available. After excluding cases with missing data, 85 patients from the internal test set were included in this analysis. Within this subset, the distribution of the integrated INRG risk groups was as follows: Very Low-risk, 12; Low-risk, 3; Intermediate-risk, 19; High-risk, 51. This curated test set enabled us to conduct an analysis in which the model directly predicts these final, integrated INRG pretreatment categories.

This study is a multi-institutional analysis based on de-identified clinical data and digital pathology images, without prospective recruitment for research purposes. All tissue sections were obtained as part of routine clinical care at the participating institutions, and no additional tissue processing or staining procedures were performed for this study. WSIs were generated from these routine sections, and all analyses were conducted on the resulting digital images. The cohort comprised pediatric patients aged 0–18 years. Patient sex and age were obtained from clinical records. Informed consent was waived by institutional review boards, and no participant compensation was provided. Sex was not included in model development, as the study focused on pathology-based prediction tasks; no sex-disaggregated analyses were performed. Tissue samples remain at the respective institutions and are not available for redistribution due to institutional and ethical restrictions.

Molecular profiling

The biomarker data used in this study were generated as part of routine clinical care, ensuring their relevance to real-world diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making. The biomarker assessment protocols for the training dataset are described below. Immunohistochemical staining for ALK and CMYC expression was performed using fully automated platforms (Leica BOND-MAX) with standardized commercial antibodies (ALK antibody clone 5A4, catalog no. MAB-0281; CMYC antibody clone EP121, catalog no. RMA-0803) from Maxim Biotechnologies, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Appropriate positive and negative controls were included in each staining batch to ensure specificity and reproducibility. Staining results were interpreted according to established pathological criteria: positive staining was defined as moderate-to-strong intensity in >10% of tumor cells, with ALK positivity requiring cytoplasmic and/or nuclear staining, and CMYC overexpression defined by clear nuclear localization.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed using commercially available probes from Anbiping for MYCN amplification (F.01013-01), 1p36 deletion (SRD probe, F.01025-01), and 11q deletion (KMT2A probe, F.01308-01). All FISH assays were conducted within accredited molecular pathology laboratories as part of standard clinical workflows, with results routinely reported to treating clinicians. For MYCN amplification assessment, ≥100 tumor cells were enumerated; amplification was defined by MYCN signal clustering or double minutes, with an MYCN:LAF signal ratio >1.5 considered amplified and ratios of 1.0–1.5 requiring clinical correlation. For 1p36 and 11q23 deletion analysis, a “1 red, 2 green” signal pattern was interpreted as a heterozygous deletion.

All clinical data underwent validation by a joint pathologists and clinicians team comprising J.Z., Q.S., W.W., P.P., K.W., L.C., and J.C.

Model design and training strategy

To address the challenges of domain shift and data scarcity inherent in applying general-purpose pathology foundation models to rare pediatric cancers, we developed the NEVA framework. Unlike conventional MIL approaches that utilize frozen encoders on all available tissue patches (typically >10,000), which often result in high computational costs and diluted signal-to-noise ratios, NEVA employs a resource-efficient, “coarse-to-fine” paradigm. This design mimics the clinical workflow of a pathologist, who first scans a WSI at low magnification (e.g., 2 MPP) to identify regions of interest (ROIs) before examining diagnostic features at high resolution (e.g., 0.5 MPP).

The framework operates in two distinct stages: (1) High-Attention Patch Selection, which filters the WSI to retain only the most diagnostically relevant tiles using a lightweight screening mechanism; and (2) End-to-End Multimodal Adaptation, where a high-capacity VLM is fine-tuned on the selected regions using LoRA to predict clinical outcomes.

Stage 1: High-Attention Patch Selection

The primary objective of the first stage is to reduce the search space from tens of thousands of patches to a tractable subset of highly informative regions (N ≈ 200), thereby enabling the use of computationally intensive foundation models in the subsequent stage. This selection process integrates data-driven attention mechanisms with heuristic morphological quality assessment.

Stage 2: End-to-End Multimodal Adaptation

In the second stage, the selected high-resolution patches and the patient’s corresponding clinical report serve as inputs to a multimodal architecture underpinned by the MUSK35, a vision-language foundation model based on the BEiT-3 Multiway Transformer architecture. We chose MUSK over other models (e.g., BERT, CLIP) because it is explicitly pathology-native. While general-purpose models capture broad text concepts, they often lack the granularity required to interpret complex pathology descriptions. In terms of pretraining data, MUSK was trained on a large-scale corpus of approximately 50 million images and 1 billion pathology-related text tokens, comprising de-identified pathology reports and domain-specific literature. To adapt this large-scale model to the neuroblastoma domain without the prohibitive cost of full parameter fine-tuning, we employ parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques.

Whole-slide inference for interpretability

Although the model is trained using a curated subset of the most informative patches (e.g., N = 200) per slide to ensure training efficiency, the architecture remains mathematically invariant to the input bag size. The attention-based MIL aggregator computes importance weights for each patch instance independently, relying solely on the local feature representation learned by the encoder rather than the global sequence length or order. Consequently, once the encoder and attention module are trained to distinguish and weight diagnostic features within the top-K subset, they generalize to bags of arbitrary size without modification. By feeding all extracted tiles from a slide (N ≫ 200) into the trained network, we can visualize whole slide attention heatmaps for interpretability. Instead, for quantitative prediction (e.g., probability and risk scores), we don’t require whole-slide inference.

Model evaluation

For classification tasks-including diagnostic categories (Risk Group, Subtype, MKI, Shimada) and biomarker predictions (ALK, CMYC, NMYC, 1p36, 11q23)-model performance was evaluated using 1000 bootstrap samples to calculate the area under the AUROC along with 95% CIs. Survival tasks, including PFS and OS, were assessed using bootstrapped concordance indices (C-index) with corresponding 95% CIs. Among classification tasks, Subtype and MKI were treated as three-class problems; a one-vs-rest strategy was employed to compute AUROC and CIs for each class, which were visualized using bar plots with error bars. Binary classification tasks (Shimada, ALK, NMYC, CMYC, 1p36, 11q23) were evaluated using ROC curves.

For regression tasks, we generated Kaplan–Meier survival curves and applied log-rank tests to evaluate statistical differences between predicted high- and low-risk groups. By default, the median of the risk scores was used as the cut-off for stratification. An exception was made for biomarkers (predicted) prognosis predictions (PFS and OS in Fig. 4), where we employed the MaxStat67 to determine an optimal cut-off, due to the pronounced class imbalance associated with NMYC status in the patient cohort. Additionally, multivariable Cox regression analysis for PFS was conducted on the internal test set, incorporating NEVA predictions alongside CMYC, MKI, Shimada, and patient age. As one patient may be associated with multiple WSIs linked to the same pathology report, we adopted a unified inference strategy across all tasks. For each WSI-report pair, the model first computed its corresponding logits. The logits across all WSIs belonging to the same patient were then averaged, and a softmax function was subsequently applied to obtain the final patient-level prediction.

Model interpretability

To validate the morphological relevance of the model’s attention mechanism and ensure interpretation was not biased by example selection, we implemented a cohort-level analysis. Attention scores were computed for all patches across the entire test dataset. For each WSI, we selected the top 1% (highest-attention) and bottom 1% (lowest-attention) patches. To evaluate morphological consistency across the cohort, these selected patches were clustered using their underlying feature embeddings. Representative patches from each resulting cluster were randomly sampled and presented to board-certified pathologists for blinded qualitative assessment (concealing slide identity and prediction labels). Pathologists evaluated whether clusters exhibited coherent histopathological features and if high-attention clusters consistently aligned with task-relevant biology compared to low-attention regions. To visualize these spatial attention distributions on individual slides, we generated heatmaps using the trained weights of the AbMIL model. Patch-level attention scores were extracted and overlaid onto the original WSIs to highlight regions most influential to the model’s inference. For the Shimada, Subtype, PFS, and OS tasks, heatmaps were constructed by normalizing attention scores to a 0–1 intensity scale. For the ALK, 1p36, and 11q23 tasks, a risk-weighted approach was employed: patch attention percentiles were weighted by the corresponding patch-level prediction probabilities to yield risk scores, ensuring that the resulting heatmaps reflected task-specific risk distributions.

Training details

All models were optimized using the Adam optimizer (initial learning rate ηmax = 1 × 10−4, weight decay = 1 × 10−2) without AMSGrad. We employed a cosine annealing learning rate schedule that decayed the learning rate from ηmax to a minimum value of ηmin = 1 × 10−6 over the full course of 100 epochs (Tmax = 100), without warm-up or restarts. Training employed a batch size of 24. To handle variable WSI dimensions, feature bags were padded with zero vectors to match the maximum bag size in the training set. Loss functions were task-specific: cross-entropy for classification and Cox partial likelihood for regression. Early stopping was triggered after a patience of 10 epochs if the primary validation metric (AUROC for classification; C-index for regression) failed to improve. Experiments utilized PyTorch Lightning with 16-bit mixed-precision training on servers hosted at Tsinghua University. All clinical data were processed and retained locally, and international collaborators contributed exclusively to model development and technical support without access to the underlying clinical data.
