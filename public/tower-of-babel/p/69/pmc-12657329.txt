Disrupted gut microbiota, suppressed inflammation, and impaired bone health in turkey poults challenged with Histomonas meleagridis and co-infected with Eimeria and E. coli
Authors: Hamid Reza Rafieian-Naeini, Catherine Fudge, Seshidhar Reddy Gudidoddi, Hanyi Shi, Venkata Sesha Reddy Choppa, Hemanth Reddy Katha, Doyun Goo, Chongxiao Chen, Woo Kyun Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHistomonas meleagridis (HM) induces severe inflammatory and necrotic lesions in the ceca and liver of turkeys. It is shown that some pathogens may interact with the progress of HM. Shifts in microbial communities can impair intestinal barrier function, allowing bacterial translocation to systemic organs. This study investigated the effects of H.meleagridis challenge and coinfections with Eimeria (EM) and E.coli on turkeys' gut microbiota and bone health. Three hundred and sixty day-old male turkey poults were placed in six treatments: NC, negative control; HM, challenged with 105 HM/bird at d 18; EM, inoculated with 8,000 oocysts of E. adenoeides and E. meleagrimitis at d 22; E. coli, challenged with 109E. coli/bird at d 18; HM+EM, challenged with 105 HM/bird at d 18, and 8,000 oocysts of E. meleagrimitis and E. adenoeides at d 22 and HM+E. coli, challenged with 105 HM and 109E. coli /bird at d 18. The trial lasted for 28 days. H. meleagridis challenge decreased body weight, feed intake, weight gain, bone density, total tissue and lean weight, increased feed conversion ratio, mortality, cecal wall thickness, and ileal crypt depth (P < 0.05). Eimeria Co-infection reduced duodenal and jejunal villus height, jejunal VH/CD, and upregulated tight junction gene expression (P < 0.05). HM and co-infection interactions decreased ileal villus height, increased crypt depth in HM+EM, increased cecal muscle thickness in HM+E. coli, reduced bone total volume, cortical and trabecular BMD, and number of closed pores in HM+E. coli. Microbial community shifts included a higher Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio in HM, with reduced Ruminococcaceae abundance in HM (∼2 %) compared to NC (∼10 %) and increased Leuconostocaceae in HM. Weighted and unweighted UniFrac analyses revealed significant group separations in HM and HM+E. coli. In conclusion, HM markedly impaired survival and growth regardless of co-infection with Eimeria or E. coli. Co-infections had slight additional effects, mainly altering intestinal histology, pathogen colonization, and microbiota.Keywords: Histomoniasis, Eimeria, E. coli, Bone health, Gut microbiota

Introduction

Histomonas meleagridis, an anaerobic protozoan, is responsible for causing Histomoniasis (widely recognized as blackhead disease) in gallinaceous birds (Ahmad et al., 2022; Fudge et al., 2025). For many years, the disease had effectively been managed with chemotherapeutic agents such as Nitarsone, dimetridazole, metronidazole, and ornidazole, used for prevention and treatment (Hu and McDougald, 2004). However, changes in drug regulations in the EU and the USA have led to no available treatments for this disease and the resurgence of Histomoniasis (McDougald, 2005; Regmi et al., 2016). Turkeys are highly susceptible, with the disease capable of causing mortality rates of up to 100 %. In contrast, chickens experience a milder form of the disease with lower mortality (Liebhart et al., 2017). The necessity of live bacteria for the propagation of H. meleagridis in vitro and establishing infection in its host in vivo has long puzzled researchers about the etiology of Histomoniasis. The nematode Heterakis gallinarum plays a critical role in the complex life cycle of H. meleagridis, functioning as its intermediate host and primary vector (Hess, 2017).

Escherichia coli (E. coli), prevalent in the intestinal tracts of poultry and often found in contaminated poultry products, has been linked to numerous foodborne illnesses (Davis et al., 2018). In poultry, avian pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC) is associated with a range of conditions, including colibacillosis, egg peritonitis, swollen head syndrome, cellulitis, omphalitis, and colisepticaemia (Seidavi et al., 2010; Joseph et al., 2024). Colisepticaemia, the most serious form of these diseases, is a systemic infection marked by the presence of pathogenic E. coli in the bloodstream and its colonization of key organs such as the heart, liver, and spleen (La Ragione and Woodward, 2002; Barnes, 2003).

Eimeria, a genus of obligate intracellular protozoa, causes intestinal coccidiosis in various vertebrate hosts, including poultry (Sharma et al., 2024). These intestinal parasites penetrate and damage the host’s epithelial cells, disrupting gut homeostasis and impairing performance (Shivaramaiah et al., 2014). Seven Eimeria species have been recognized that contaminate domestic turkeys (Meleagris gallopavo). Among these species, E. meleagrimitis, E. gallopavonis, E. adenoeides and E. dispersa are recognized as causing clinical disease in commercial turkeys (Lund and Farr, 1965; Cervantes, 2020). The presence of multiple Eimeria species complicates predicting the impact of any single species on a flock. At present, the only licensed live coccidiosis vaccine for turkeys encompasses two species: E. adenoeides and E. meleagrimitis (Imai, 2018). No significant cross-protection exists among the various Eimeria species that infect turkeys (Trujillo-Peralta et al., 2023).

Developing a system using a monoxenic clonal culture of H. meleagridis allowed the targeted exchange of the cultivated bacterial strain. In vitro experiments confirmed that E. coli was more effective than other bacterial species for H. meleagridis growth (Ganas et al., 2012). These findings underscore the importance of microflora interactions with parasites during an infection and its clinical consequences. Bilic and Hess (2020) indicated that E. coli benefits H. meleagridis by providing nutrients and helping regulate environmental conditions such as pH and oxygen levels (elimination), which are essential for the parasite's growth. The relationship is mutual because E. coli can utilize glucose in culture media and lacks endogenous amylolytic enzymes for starch degradation; H. meleagridis, which can hydrolyze starch molecules, may contribute to increased levels of extracellular glucose (Mazumdar et al., 2019; Bilic and Hess, 2020). In addition to the interaction between H. meleagridis and bacteria, co-infection with E. tenella has also been shown to increase both the incidence and severity of liver lesions in broilers (McDougald and Hu, 2001). Fudge et al. (2024) also indicated that feed withdrawal, coccidiosis, low-density diets, or a combination of stressors can increase disease severity and susceptibility in turkey poults. However, limited research over the past three decades has explored co-infection effects with H. meleagridis, Eimeria spp., and E.coli in turkeys, particularly concerning gut microbiota and host-pathogen interactions.

Intense genetic selection for meat production has made turkeys more susceptible to bone abnormalities. Additionally, protozoan diseases such as Eimeria infections have been closely linked to disrupted bone homeostasis (Tompkins et al. 2023c). Some studies have reported incidences of bone impairment associated with the disease. For example, Sharma et al. (2023b) and Choi (2025) demonstrated that the inflammatory response caused by Eimeria infection and necrotic enteritis can impair bone growth in chickens by modulating the RANKL–RANK signaling pathway. However, there is currently no evidence linking H. meleagridis or other enteric infections to bone health in turkeys. Hence, this study was designed to explore the effects of H. meleagridis infection, as well as co-infections with Eimeria spp. and E.coli , on gastrointestinal leakage, immune response, bone health, gut microbiota, and performance traits in turkey poults.

Materials and methods

All animal experimental methods used in this work were conducted in accordance with approval from the University of Georgia IACUC (A2021 12-012).

Birds, diets, management, and growth performance

Three hundred and sixty, day-old male turkey poults (Nicholas Select, Aviagen) were randomly allocated to six treatment groups, with five replicate cages each, and reared for 28 days. A 2 by 3 factorial arrangement was employed in this study, with two levels of H. meleagridis challenge (challenged [+] or non-challenged [–]) and three co-infection conditions (No challenge, Eimeria spp., or E. coli). Treatments included unchallenged negative control (NC), challenged with H. Meleagridis (HM), challenged with E. meleagrimitis and E. adenoeides (EM), challenged with E. coli (E. coli), challenged with H. Meleagridis and Eimeria (HM+EM), and challenged with H. Meleagridis and E. coli (HM+E. coli). Birds were challenged with Histomonas meleagridis on day 18 via cloacal gavage of 1 mL inoculum containing 100,000 cells per poult. Eimeria was administered on day 22 with 8,000 oocysts per bird, and was given on day 18 at a dose of 10⁹ CFU per bird. The treatments and challenge times are detailed in the supplementary materials.

Temperature and lighting were controlled following the breeder's guidelines (Aviagen Turkey, 2021). Diets were formulated to meet or exceed the nutrient requirements outlined in the male turkey poult’s feeding guide (Aviagen Turkeys, 2024), as provided in Table 1. On day 18, 24, and 28, Body weight (BW) and feed intake were measured (FI) to calculate body weight gain (BWG), and feed conversion ratio (FCR). Mortality was noted and expressed as a percentage for each period.

Lesion score in the ceca and liver

All birds that died during the trial were necropsied on the day of death, and the rest of the surviving birds were opened at d 28 for gross pathology of the ceca and liver. In total, 360 livers and ceca of the turkeys were examined. The observed lesions were consistent with descriptions in the literature, characterized by moderately to severely thickened walls with caseous cores (Hauck et al., 2018). The serosa frequently appeared pale and mottled in the ceca, with occasional fibrin deposits on the surface. The livers exhibited pale white foci of varying sizes, occasionally shrunken and saucer-shaped (lesion scoring visual guide provided in supplementary materials).

Preparation of Histomonas meleagridis, Escherichia coli and Eimeria spp. Inoculum

Frozen H. meleagridis (Buford isolate, 1 mL) was thawed and introduced into 9 mL of modified Dwyer’s medium with 0.8 % (wt/vol) rice powder (Bob’s Red Mill, Milwaukie, OR, US) and 5 % horse serum (Cytiva HyClone, Waltham, MA, US). The cultures passed multiple times (every two days) and were cultured anaerobically at 42°C until they reached the target concentration of 100,000 cells/mL (If needed, the stock was diluted using media without rice powder). An Improved Neubauer hemocytometer was used to determine cell counts (Thermo Fisher, Raleigh, NC). The inoculum was maintained at 42°C in a portable incubator (AZZOTA, Delaware, USA) throughout the inoculation procedure. On day 18, birds were transcloacally inoculated with 1 mL of the inoculum using a blunt-tipped pipette, delivering 100,000 H. meleagridis cells at a pH of 7. After inoculation, each bird was gently inverted and held by its legs for 2 min to ensure effective delivery of the inoculum before being returned to its cage (Fudge, 2022). This protocol was carefully followed to maximize successful inoculation in each bird.

A loopful of selectively enriched broth from the previously incubated cloacal swab and fecal material samples, along with 0.1 ml of the intestinal fluid sample, were spread on the solid surface of the eosin methylene blue (EMB; HiMedia , Kelton, PA ) agar. All plates were incubated for 24 h at 37°C in three replicates of EMB plates agar to successfully isolate typical colonies. On EMB agar, a single colony of passaged E. coli was cultivated. As strong acid producers, E. coli colonies typically appear dark with a nucleated center and display a green metallic sheen due to eosin Y precipitation in acidic environments (Divya et al., 2016). After streaking, the sample was grown aerobically at 37°C in tryptic soy broth (TSB; Sigma-Aldrich Co, St. Louis, MO). After 24 h of incubation, cultures were washed by centrifugation at 7000 × g for 10 min, followed by suspension in buffered-phosphate-saline (PBS). Bacterial density measured at OD600 using UV-Vis spectrometer (Genesys 10S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Peptone water (0.1 %; Fisher Scientific, Fair Lawn, NJ) was added to adjust the concentration to achieve a final inoculum concentration of 109 CFU/mL.

The Eimeria challenge inoculum was prepared from the Immucox®T turkey coccidiosis vaccine (Ceva, Libourne, France), containing sporulated oocysts of E. meleagrimitis and E. adenoeides. Following centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min, oocysts were enumerated microscopically using a hemocytometer (Carl Zeiss, Promenade, Germany) and diluted with distilled water until the target concentrations were of 8000 oocysts (4,000 E. meleagrimitis and 4,000 E. adenoeides) obtained (Rafieian-Naeini et al., 2025).

Intestinal permeability and morphology

To measure intestinal permeability and leakage, fluorescein isothiocyanate–dextran (FITC-d; 2.2 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was orally administered to one bird per pen on day 27. Two hours post-administration, birds were euthanized, and blood was quickly collected by cardiac puncture (Shi et al., 2024). Following the procedure described by Tompkins et al. (2024), after the blood was coagulated, samples were centrifuged at 1,000 g for 15 min using an Eppendorf centrifuge 5430R (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). A 100 µL aliquot of the serum was then used to determine the FITC-d concentration (Liu et al., 2023). A standard curve was generated using different concentrations of FITC-d, yielding a strong linear relationship (R² = 0.9915).

On day 28, a 2 cm segment of each of the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and liver was collected from one bird per pen, rinsed in PBS, and then preserved in 10 % formalin for 14 days. Following fixation and processing, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 7 µm-thick slices (Rafieian-Naeini et al., 2021; Irani et al., 2023). Hematoxylin and eosin staining was applied to the tissue sections, which were then observed and photographed at 2X and 4X magnifications using a BZ X800 light microscope (Keyence Inc., Itasca, IL). Using the methods described by Liu et al. (2023), Villus height (VH), crypt depth (CD), and the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (VH:CD) were quantified using ImageJ 1.52a software, with three measurements per slide (replicate) for each parameter (Rafieian‐Naeini et al., 2024).

E. coli colonization analysis in ceca and liver

On day 28, one bird from each pen was euthanized via cervical dislocation. Employing aseptic techniques, samples of the ceca and liver (excluding the gall bladder) were collected for analysis (Adhikari et al., 2019). Ceca and liver samples were placed in sterile sample bags (VWR International, Radnor, PA) and stored on ice until transport to the laboratory. Upon arrival, 10 mL BPW (HiMedia, Mumbai, India) added; samples weighed, and homogenized using paddle blender (Masticator Silver Panoramic, Neutec Group Inc., Farmingdale, NY). The cecal content and liver homogenate were then transferred to a 10 mL sterile glass tube and diluted to 104 and 102, respectively. One hundred microliters of the diluted samples were plated on eosin methylene blue (EMB) agar and incubated at 37°C for 20 h. Following incubation, the number of colonies was counted, and the results were reported as colony-forming units (CFU).

Body composition analysis and micro-computed tomography

On day 28, one bird per pen was euthanized by cervical dislocation and analyzed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA; GE Healthcare, Madison, WI, USA) to determine bone mineral density, bone mineral content, fat, and total tissue weight (Chen et al., 2020a). On day 28, a total of 30 tibias (one bird per pen) were collected, and all soft tissues were removed. The bones were wrapped in cheesecloth and securely placed in a 50 mL Falcon tube to prevent any movement during scanning. The scanning procedure followed the method described by Chen and Kim (2020). Specifically, the X-ray source was set to 70 kV and 142 mA, with a 0.5 mm aluminum filter. Scanning was performed using a SkyScan 1275 system (Bruker MicroCT, Billerica, MA) with a pixel size of 25 µm and a 0.4° rotation step, acquiring four images per rotation. Two-dimensional images were reconstructed into three-dimensional images using N-Recon (Bruker MicroCT, Billerica, MA), straightened in Data Viewer (Bruker MicroCT, Billerica, MA), and imported into CTAn (Bruker MicroCT, Billerica, MA) for volume of interest (VOI) selection. Calibration of bone mineral density (BMD) was performed using two calcium hydroxyapatite phantoms (0.25 and 0.75 g/cm³; 8 mm diameter) (Chen and Kim, 2020). The proximal tibia (metaphysis) was analyzed from a total of 200 slides. The following parameters were quantified from the entire volume of interest, as well as the cortical and trabecular bone sections: tissue volume (TV), bone volume (BV), BV:TV ratio (BV/TV), bone mineral density (BMD), bone mineral content (BMC), number of closed pores (Po.N(cl)), and closed pore surface area (Po.S(cl)). Gene expression

Cecal tonsil (Ct) tissues were collected on day 28 from the same bird used for histological analysis and were immediately placed in liquid nitrogen. Tissues were crushed using MiniBeadBeater 16 (BioSpec Products Inc., Bartlesville, OK), and RNA was extracted with QIAzol Lysis Reagent (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop Eight spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA) (Teng et al., 2021). Following dilution to a standardized concentration, RNA was reverse-transcribed with a High-Capacity cDNA Synthesis Kit (Applied Biosystems, Life Technologies, CA). The resulting cDNA was combined with SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) and gene-specific primers, and amplification was carried out in duplicate using a StepOne thermocycler (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). According to Livak and Schmittgen (2001) target gene expression was normalized to β-actin, a housekeeping gene. Primer sequences for cytokines and tight junction proteins are provided in the supplementary materials.

Gut microbiota

On d 28, one bird/pen cecal contents were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, then stored at −80°C for future analysis. DNA was extracted from the cecal contents using QIAamp® DNA stool mini kits (Qiagen GmbH, Hildesheim, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. Sample quality and quantity were first assessed using a NanoDrop Eight spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Extracted DNA samples were submitted to LC Sciences (Houston, TX, USA), where the V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced using paired-end Illumina technology (Wang et al., 2021). Raw reads were processed in QIIME2 (version 2022.2), with sequence denoising and chimera removal performed using the DADA2 plugin, resulting in amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomic classification was done using the SILVA reference database (515–806 classifier) with the classify-sklearn algorithm. Downstream analyses included calculation of alpha diversity metrics (Shannon index, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, Pielou’s evenness, and observed features) and beta diversity metrics (weighted and unweighted UniFrac) using the core-metrics-phylogenetic pipeline in QIIME2. Taxonomic composition was summarized at the phylum and family levels in the main manuscript to highlight broader community shifts. At the same time, genus-level results were generated and provided in supplementary files. Statistical group significance was assessed using ANOSIM (Analysis of Similarities) applied to the beta diversity distance matrices(Bolyen et al., 2019; Rafieian-Naeini et al., 2025).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using JMP Pro 18 (SAS Institute Inc). Data normality assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test. A Two-way ANOVA test was performed. Differences among groups were analyzed with Student’s t-test and Tukey’s post hoc comparisons. Data were presented as the mean and standard error of the mean (SEM), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Lesion score distributions were analyzed using a Chi-square test of independence to assess the frequency of lesion severity across treatment groups. Additionally, a two-way ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the main effects of H.meleagridis challenge and co-infection, as well as their interaction effects, on both cecal and liver lesion scores. Beta-diversity matrices were analyzed using Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM) with 999 permutations, and Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was performed on these dissimilarity indices in RStudio software (R Version 4.2.1, RStudio PBC, Boston, MA). Beta-diversity analyses were conducted to compare each group against the NC group, allowing for the assessment of community composition differences across treatments.

The co-infection main effect did not significantly influence BWG at any time point. Similarly, the HM challenge significantly increased FCR during d 19–24 and d 19–28 (P = 0.009 and P = 0.02, respectively). On d 19–24, FCR was more than double in the HM-challenged poults compared to the non-challenged birds (3.01 vs. 1.45). From day 19–28, the FCR remained 40 % higher in the HM-challenged groups (2.12 vs. 1.51). Co-infection treatments did not significantly affect FCR at any measured time points. Although numerical differences were observed, the interaction effect between HM and co-infection was not statistically significant for BWG or FCR across all time points (P > 0.05).

Discussion

The current experiment aimed to investigate the interaction effects between Histomonas meleagridis coinfection with Eimeria and on intestinal and bone health, as well as the gut microbiota of turkey poults. In the past few decades, the importance of coinfection diseases in the poultry industry has increased due to their significant impact on bird health, productivity, and economic losses. Coinfections often result in interactions between pathogens, leading to more severe disease outcomes than single infections (McDougald and Hu, 2001; Rafieian-Naeini et al., 2025). H. meleagridis achieves full virulence in the cecum of chickens only when combined with particular bacterial species (McDougald, 2005). Such close associations of H. meleagridis and bacteria have been proposed to offer advantages, including protection from environmental stressors, improved nutrient access, or the use of the parasite as a vehicle for transmission. Consistent with this idea, the use of commensal (e.g., DH5α) to propagate H. meleagridis has been demonstrated as an effective method to sustain parasite growth (Mazumdar et al., 2019; Bilic and Hess, 2020).

Losing weight and anorexia in the poults challenged with H. meleagridis were reported in the study; meanwhile, FCR drastically increased in the H. meleagridis challenged birds. Barros et al. (2020) suggested that infected birds may reduce their feed intake, leading to a weakened physical condition and increased susceptibility to disease. Furthermore, the pathological nature of histomoniasis causes severe damage to the ceca and liver (Figs. 6 and 7), disrupting gut function and contributing to systemic illness, which may further reduce the birds’ willingness to eat. Daş et al. (2011), Kyriazakis and Houdijk (2006) know the bird's body weight loss is in response to the partial diversion of nutrients from growth to the development of immunity to support the repair of damaged mucosal structures of the intestine in infected birds with parasites.

In the current study, the survival of the birds significantly dropped on 5dpi in H. meleagridis challenged groups. In coinfection, groups, Eimeria had higher mortality rates at the end of the trial; however, the interaction effect between H. meleagridis and co-infection was not detected.

In addition, the severity of the ceca and liver lesion scores was not elevated in cases of coinfection with Eimeria or E. coli. In the current study, the absence of interaction between HM and other pathogens on mortality, ceca, and liver lesion scores suggests that coinfection may have a limited impact on HM-challenged birds. Eimeria infections are known to compromise gut integrity, disrupt the immune response, and render birds more susceptible to secondary infections (Rafieian-Naeini et al., 2025). In contrast to our findings, McDougald and Hu (2001) reported a positive interaction between H.meleagridis and Eimeria tenella in broilers, with increased lesion scores observed. They suggested that prior inoculation with Eimeria tenella may predispose birds to more severe H. meleagridis infection by facilitating its penetration through the ceca to the bloodstream. Differences in species (turkey vs. broiler) and the timing of Eimeria inoculation, whether Eimeria is introduced before or after H. meleagridis infection, may account for the discrepancies in outcomes. In our study, due to the differing life cycles and clinical signs of the protozoa (Eimeria ≈ 6 days; H. meleagridis >10 days), Eimeria adenoeides and Eimeria meleagrimitis were inoculated four days after H. meleagridis challenge. Birds were sampled on day 28 (corresponding to 10 dpi H. meleagridis and 6 dpi Eimeria) to assess interaction effects. However, it is likely that by the time Eimeria was administered, H. meleagridis had already colonized the ceca, potentially minimizing any additive or synergistic impact of Eimeria on cecal damage. Previous studies have shown that can be ingested by H. meleagridis through phagocytosis (Mazet et al., 2008), serving as a nutrient source and thereby promoting its proliferation (Munsch et al., 2009).

The ability of to enhance H. meleagridis growth and increase host susceptibility has been previously discussed; however, cecal colonization by E.coli was not significantly altered in cases of co-infection with H. meleagridis compared to a single E.coli infection. On the other hand, liver colonization by E.coli was significantly increased in the group co-infected with H. meleagridis and E.coli. Escherichia coli typically remains confined to the gastrointestinal tract under normal conditions (Ribeiro et al., 2023); however, intestinal damage caused by H. meleagridis may allow the bacteria to penetrate the gut barrier and reach internal organs such as the liver. Similarly, Rafieian-Naeini et al. (2025) reported elevated levels of Salmonella Typhimurium in the spleen of birds challenged with Eimeria, emphasizing the role of intestinal barrier disruption in enabling systemic bacterial dissemination. Interestingly, in some cases, coinfection with E. adenoeides and H. meleagridis in turkeys has been reported to reduce Histomoniasis severity (McDougald and Fuller, 2005), suggesting potential competitive or inhibitory interactions under certain conditions. Abdelhamid et al. (2020) reported that severe dysbiosis of the cecal microbiota in turkeys was associated with the extent of lesions following H. meleagridis infection. This dysbiosis was marked by an increased abundance of E. coli in the gut. As necrosis progressed due to H. meleagridis, penetration of the cecal wall and systemic translocation of E. coli were observed.

The intestinal morphological changes observed in this study were primarily localized to the duodenum and cecum of the infected birds. The characteristic lesion distribution associated with E. meleagrimitis and E. adenoeides, specifically, the lesions in the upper part of the intestine, including the jejunum and duodenum, are likely attributable to E. meleagrimitis, while E. adenoeides primarily affect the ceca of the birds (Gadde et al., 2020). Additionally, the increased thickness of the cecal wall was strongly associated with H. meleagridis infection. Notably, in the coinfection group of H. meleagridis and E. coli, the cecal wall thickness was significantly higher compared to other groups. The presence of E. coli in coinfection with H. meleagridis appears to exacerbate the histological alterations in the cecum, a compounded inflammatory response that further disrupts intestinal integrity. In previous studies, Chapman (2008) reported that E. adenoeides predominantly develops in the rectum and ceca, whereas E. meleagrimitis is concentrated in the duodenum and jejunum, extending into the lower intestine during heavy infections. Similarly, Ruff et al., (1981) observed a reduction in villus height in the jejunum and ileum of turkeys infected with E. meleagrimitis. In the ceca, initial histopathological lesions such as increased cellularity and crypt debris were evident as early as 1 dpi, with gross lesions, including hyperemia and thickened mucosa, becoming apparent by 3 dpi (Hauck and Hafez, 2013). Fig. 7 provides a qualitative representation of the histopathological changes in the intestine and liver of turkey poults subjected to H. meleagridis coinfection with Eimeria and E. coli.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) is a noninvasive and reliable technique for assessing body composition in poultry (Schallier et al., 2019). In the current study, significant reduction in body composition parameters (BMD, BMC, Total tissue, Fat, and lean) were observed in turkey poults infected with H. meleagridis. These changes suggest severe malabsorption of nutrients and a metabolic shift from growth-related pathways to those prioritizing defense and immune responses, as described by Kyriazakis and Houdijk (2006). Similarly, Shi et al. (2024) and Sharma et al. (2022) reported that Eimeria challenges significantly reduced all body composition parameters, including bone mineral density (BMD), bone mineral content (BMC), bone area, muscle mass, and total tissue, compared to unchallenged birds.

Walking ability is a critical factor in ensuring animal welfare in meat and poultry production (Mench, 2004). However, lameness, bone fractures, and leg abnormalities remain prevalent issues in turkey production (Swalander, 2012). Oviedo-Rondón et al. (2018) and Lilburn (1994) estimated that 2–6 % of turkey flocks exhibit observable skeletal problems, with an even greater proportion experiencing less noticeable bone-related issues. Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) has emerged as a powerful and nondestructive imaging tool in poultry research, offering detailed insights into the internal microstructures of avian bones, particularly for advanced bone analysis (Chen and Kim, 2020). There is limited data regarding the alterations in turkey bone microstructure following H. meleagridis infection and coinfections, making it essential to gain a comprehensive understanding of the variations in bone traits resulting from these challenges. In the current study, most femur microstructure parameters were significantly affected by H. meleagridis infection, with further deterioration observed in coinfections involving E. coli or Eimeria. Our findings showed that H. meleagridis challenge disproportionately impairs the total and cortical compartments of bone, while trabecular parameters are comparatively preserved, a surprising pattern given that metabolically active trabecular bone is usually the first to reflect systemic inflammatory stress. Notably, in the H. meleagridis challenge groups, the surface of closed pores was substantially reduced, particularly in birds co-infected with E. coli. Sharma et al. (2023a) observed that reduced cortical pore volumes in Eimeria-infected birds suggest diminished osteoblast activity, leading to a reduced deposition of type I collagen, which is essential for bone mineralization and new bone formation. Lopes et al. (2024), documented a notable decrease in femur microstructure parameters following Eimeria challenges in broilers and pullets.

We hypothesized that oxidative stress may be one of the factors contributing to impaired bone formation. Previous studies have shown that oxidative stress can negatively affect bone development in birds, both during embryonic stages and in stem cell differentiation. For example, Tompkins et al. (2023b) found that exposing fertilized Cobb broiler eggs to H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress disrupted the expression of key bone formation genes, such as COL1A2, COL2A1, BMP, RUNX2, and BGLAP and led to reduced embryo length and altered antioxidant enzyme activity. Similarly, Tompkins et al. (2023a) reported that long-term H₂O₂ exposure in chicken mesenchymal stem cells inhibited osteogenic differentiation by reducing the expression of osteogenic markers and increasing their tendency to shift toward adipogenic pathways. In line with these findings, (Tompkins et al., 2023c) demonstrated that the inflammatory immune response and oxidative stress following Eimeria infection in broilers were closely associated with disrupted bone homeostasis, further emphasizing the role of both inflammation and oxidative stress in skeletal development during coccidiosis. Together, these findings support the idea that oxidative stress may play a significant role in bone development during early avian growth. Notably, the present study is the first to report body composition changes in H. meleagridis-challenged birds, providing novel insights into the effects of this pathogen on nutrient utilization and physiological adaptations in turkeys.

One objective of this study was to examine how different challenges alter local gene expression by assessing tight-junction protein genes in the jejunum and cytokine genes in the cecal tonsils. Claudin (CLDN), occludin, and the JAM family are three critical transmembrane proteins that form the core structure of tight junctions, while plaque proteins, including the ZO family, act as adaptors linking claudin, occludin, and JAM proteins to F-actin in epithelial cells (Aijaz et al., 2006). In our study, the mRNA abundance of tight junction proteins revealed an upregulation of CLDN1 in the Eimeria coinfection groups. Similarly, Teng et al. (2020) reported a linear upregulation of tight junction protein gene expression in response to graded Eimeria challenges in broilers. The relatively low impact of H. meleagridis on tight junction proteins in the jejunum is likely due to its minimal effects on this region, as its primary site of pathology is the ceca. IL-10 plays a crucial immunoregulatory role in the intestine, particularly as a differentiation factor for a subset of T cells with suppressor functions. In chickens, Th2 cytokines (IL-3, IL-4, IL-13, GM-CSF) are clustered in the genome and expressed in lymphoid tissues (Hong et al., 2006). In the current study, IL-4 and IL-10 gene expression in the cecal tonsils was significantly upregulated in the Eimeria challenge groups, with no significant changes observed in other groups. This finding is consistent with previous research, where Eimeria maxima was shown to increase the expression of IL-10 in chickens (Rothwell et al., 2004; Hong et al., 2006).

The significant increase in these cytokines indicates that their gene expression is tightly dependent on Eimeria infection and is directly triggered by Eimeria, irrespective of the H. meleagridis challenge. The upregulation of IL-10 and IL-4 during Eimeria infection represents the host's strategy to regulate inflammation and maintain the integrity of the intestinal barrier while combating the parasite. In contrast, the absence of changes in IL-10 and IL-4 during H. meleagridis infection underscores the parasite's ability to manipulate the host's immune response, driving severe inflammation that facilitates its survival but results in significant tissue damage and high mortality. Together, these results reveal fundamental differences in host defense strategies against Eimeria versus H. meleagridis and clarify their distinct pathogenic mechanisms.

The cecum is a complex and dynamic ecosystem with a highly variable microbiome influenced by numerous factors, including infections (Diaz Carrasco et al., 2019). Research has shown that bacterial and parasitic invasions can profoundly impact gut microbiome composition (Abdelhamid et al., 2021; Rafieian-Naeini et al., 2025). In the present study, microbial richness and diversity indices were significantly altered with H. meleagridis. Additionally, infections with Eimeria and coinfections involving H. meleagridis exhibited distinct microbiome diversity patterns compared to other groups. Infections with Eimeria, Salmonella Typhimurium, Clostridium perfringens, Campylobacter jejuni are known to have effect on gut microbiota and richness (Awad et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2017). Additionally, Abdelhamid et al. (2020) suggested that changes in the cecal microbiota of layers are likely associated with the severity of macroscopic and microscopic lesions. They reported significant shifts in the microbial population structure, with more pronounced effects observed in the ceca exhibiting higher lesion scores in H. meleagridis challenged birds.

The current study revealed a high relative abundance of the phylum Firmicutes and a lower abundance of Bacteroidetes in birds infected with H.meleagridis, resulting in an elevated Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio. The gut microbiota of birds is predominantly composed of the phyla Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria (Oakley et al., 2014). Such dominance highlights the critical roles these phyla play in gut health and function. Analysis of experimental data across different infection time points demonstrated that genera such as Enterococcus and Streptococcus (both members of Firmicutes) showed increased abundance during Eimeria tenella infections (Chen et al., 2020b). Notably, specific members of the Firmicutes are known to inhibit the growth of opportunistic pathogens and contribute to the degradation of complex carbohydrates, emphasizing their functional importance in maintaining gut ecosystem stability (Brown et al., 2012). Conversely, studies by Zhou et al. (2017) reported that E. tenella-infected chickens exhibited a marked decrease in Bacteroidetes phyla, particularly those in the family Bacteroidaceae. Such changes highlight the dynamic interplay between gut microbiota and pathogenic infections. Disruptions to gut microbiota homeostasis, including alterations in the F/B ratio, have been associated with various pathological conditions, as the balance of these dominant phyla is critical for proper physiological functioning. The F/B ratio is increasingly recognized as a biomarker for gut health, with implications for disease development and management (Magne et al., 2020).

Results indicated that Ruminococcaceae in H.meleagridis challenge birds showed a significant decrease. reported that co-infection of H. meleagridis with pathogenic E. coli in layers was linked to reduced abundances of Lactobacillus, Faecalibacterium, and certain Ruminococcaceae members, taxa considered highly beneficial for host metabolism. Wu et al. (2014) also reported that all Ruminococcaceae members were significantly reduced in Eimeria-challenged birds. Abdelhamid et al. (2020) found that layers co-infected with H. meleagridis and avian-pathogenic showed sharply reduced populations of Lactobacillus, Faecalibacterium, and several Ruminococcaceae taxa microbes essential for short-chain fatty-acid production and intestinal-barrier integrity. Wu et al. (2014) reported a pronounced contraction of Ruminococcaceae in broilers challenged with Eimeria spp. Together with an overview of core chicken gut microbiota (Rychlik, 2020), these studies underscore a consistent pattern, showing that enteric pathogens preferentially deplete key butyrate-producing communities, potentially compromising nutrient metabolism and mucosal health.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the study demonstrates that Histomonas meleagridis had a major impact on mortality regardless of co-infection. Interaction between H. meleagridis and co-infections showed minimal effects, primarily influencing intestinal histology, pathogen colonization, and specific bacterial populations. Notably, H. meleagridis infection highlights the parasite’s ability to evade or suppress the host’s immune response, leading to significant tissue damage and high mortality without triggering a robust inflammatory reaction. These results reveal fundamental differences in host defense strategies against Eimeria versus H. meleagridis, emphasizing their distinct pathogenic mechanisms.

Additionally, the decrease in beneficial microbial groups, such as Ruminococcaceae, and the altered Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio underscore the essential role of gut microbiota in regulating host health and the response to infections. These findings highlight the importance of targeted management strategies to preserve gut integrity and microbiota stability while preventing pathogen colonization. Future research should investigate interventions such as probiotics, prebiotics, or dietary modifications to improve resistance to infection and support recovery in affected birds.
