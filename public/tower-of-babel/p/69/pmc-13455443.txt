Placental and umbilical cord morphometry in preeclampsia and their association with neonatal outcomes: a preliminary study in Ghana
Authors: Rita Agyei Boakye, Nancy Darkoa Darko, Atta Kusi Appiah, Chrissie Stansie Abaidoo, Juliet Robertson, Frank Enoch Gyamfi, Francis Kofi Sarkodie, Ronald Awoonor-Williams, Emmanuel Ansah Owusu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe placenta and umbilical cord play a crucial role in fetal growth, development, and neonatal survival. Although extensively studied in developed countries, quantitative data on placental and umbilical cord morphology in preeclampsia are scarce in Ghana. This study aimed to assess the gross morphometry of preeclamptic placentae and umbilical cords and their relationship with neonatal indices.MethodA cross-sectional study was conducted on 152 freshly delivered placentae (100 normotensive; 52 preeclamptic) at the Holy Family Hospital, Berekum, Ghana. Maternal data collected included age, parity, blood pressure, and occupation. Standard anthropometric techniques were used for placental and umbilical cord measurements after they were fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin. Statistical analyses included t-test, Chi-square, and Spearman correlation, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsPlacental and umbilical cord indices differed significantly between preeclamptic and normotensive groups, except for cord length. Moderate to strong correlations were observed between placental and cord indices and neonatal outcomes, but not with neonatal length. Placentae were more often oval, and cords were eccentrically inserted in preeclamptic births compared to normotensive births.ConclusionsPreeclampsia is associated with altered placental and umbilical cord morphology and reduced neonatal growth indices. These findings provide baseline data to support multidisciplinary strategies for improved management and pregnancy outcomes in Ghana.Keywords: Preeclampsia, Normotensive, Placenta, Umbilical cord, Neonatal indicis, Morphometric, Ghana

Background

Preeclampsia is an important medical condition that poses serious risks to the health of both mothers and newborns worldwide. Approximately 2–8% of pregnancies globally are affected by this condition, with the prevalence increasing to as high as 10% in developing countries [1]. In Ghana, preeclampsia is one of the top five leading causes of maternal and neonatal deaths, with a prevalence rate ranging from 6.55% to 7.03% [2].

Preeclampsia is characterized by an abrupt increase in blood pressure and proteinuria, often accompanied by edema, occurring after 20 weeks of gestation. If left untreated, it can progress to eclampsia, which is a life-threatening condition characterized by seizures, coma, or even death. The underlying cause of preeclampsia is often linked to defective placentation, which leads to the release of placenta-related factors into the maternal circulation and an abnormal expression of pro-inflammatory, anti-angiogenic, and angiogenic factors [3, 4]. However, the exact etiology of preeclampsia remains unclear.

Knowledge of the gross morphology of the placenta and the umbilical cord is key to understanding the issues that surround fetal development and perinatal outcome. The placenta plays a critical role in fetal development by serving as the interface between the fetus and the maternal circulation. The placenta is typically discoidal in shape and develops from both the uterus and the growing embryo [5]. Abnormalities in placental development can have serious consequences for both maternal and fetal health. Placental thickness can provide valuable diagnostic information and hence serve as an indicator of the well-being of the fetus. Another vital metric for assessing placental development is the placental weight, which reflects both lateral chorionic plate enlargement and disk thickness [6, 7].

Nutrients and oxygen required for fetal growth are transported by the umbilical cord, which links the placenta and the fetus. The cord is typically pearly white, 1-2 cm in diameter, and 50-60 cm in length, although its length and structure can vary. The umbilical cord contains the right and left umbilical arteries, the left umbilical vein, and Wharton’s jelly, a mucous connective tissue that provides flexibility, strength, and resistance to compression [8]. The length of the umbilical cord is associated with fetal movement and available space in the amniotic cavity, while the attachment of the cord to the placenta can be central, eccentric, marginal, or velamentous [9]. Abnormalities in the structure of the umbilical cord, such as knotting, poor insertion, and unusual length, can indicate fetal distress or impaired placental function, often seen in preeclamptic pregnancies. Neonatal health is often assessed by measurements such as birth weight, length, ponderal index, and head and abdominal circumferences. The birth weight to placental weight ratio, or the feto-placental weight ratio, is a commonly used indicator of fetal well-being and placental health. A balanced ratio suggests adequate development, while deviations from this ratio may indicate issues with fetal or placental growth.

Conditions like preeclampsia and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) can alter the structure and function of the placenta. For instance, a thin placenta is commonly associated with intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), while a thick placenta may indicate conditions like hydrops fetalis [9, 10]. In preeclamptic pregnancies, the morphology of the placenta and umbilical cord may undergo significant changes, including altered attachment points, abnormal cord lengths, changes in Wharton’s jelly content, and variations in the number and structure of umbilical cord vessels. These alterations can contribute to adverse neonatal outcomes.

Despite the growing prevalence of preeclampsia in Ghana, there is limited information on the morphological features of the placental and umbilical cord in preeclamptic pregnancies and their impact on neonatal outcomes. This study aims to address this gap by exploring both qualitative and quantitative assessments of the placenta and umbilical cord in preeclamptic and normal pregnancies, specifically within the Ghanaian population. The findings could provide valuable insights into the relationship between preeclampsia and placental health, ultimately contributing to improved maternal and neonatal care.

Materials and methods

Aim, study design and study population

This study aimed to assess the gross morphometry of preeclamptic placentae and umbilical cords and their relationship with neonatal indices. This study was a cross-sectional study carried out on mothers who gave birth at the Holy Family Hospital, Berekum in the Bono region of Ghana. One hundred and fifty-two (152) pregnant women who delivered at the facility were enrolled using a convenient sampling method. One hundred and fifty-two (152), consisting of hundred (100) normal and fifty- two (52) preeclamptic placentae, were collected.

Data collection

Background information on the parity, age and occupation of mothers was obtained from their maternal and child health record book. Their measured blood pressures at each visit were also obtained in order to categorize them as hypertensive or non-hypertensive. Continuous Blood Pressure (BP) measurements started immediately after the diagnosis of preeclampsia and subsequently at each antenatal visit to the health facility until delivery. The mean BP derived from these serial measurements was then used for the analysis.

All the placentae with their attached umbilical cords were collected within 1–6 h after delivery, and blood clots were washed off with water after the umbilical cords were cut, leaving 0.025 m from the fetal site of insertion. All the placentae were tagged with codes and placed in a plastic bucket containing 20 L of 10% neutral buffered formalin. The container was covered with an airtight lid. The specimen was kept at room temperature in the sluice room of the labour ward. The collected placentae were later washed and stored in a solution of 0.5% formaldehyde in saline. More detailed examination and measurement were then performed on the fixed placente and cords. To ensure measurement consistency, inter- and intra-rater reliability were assessed, yielding an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.93 (95% CI).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Women with preeclampsia who delivered at term with reliable gestational age, normal singleton pregnancies, and complete information on socio-demographic characteristics from the maternal and child health record book were included in the study. Placentae were excluded from the study if they were from women with multiple pregnancies, known chronic maternal diseases (e.g. hypertension, sickle cell disease, autoimmune disorders), gestational diabetes mellitus, Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection, malaria, untreated syphilis, substance abuse, or major fetal anomalies (e.g., neural tube defects, congenital heart disease, or chromosomal abnormalities).

Placental measurements

The weight of the collected placentae was determined grossly using a sensitive mechanical kitchen scale (Nope Kitchen Scale). Before weighing, the umbilical cord, membranes, and any clots were removed, as recommended by the Amsterdam Placental Workshop Group Consensus, and the values obtained were recorded in kilograms.

The thickness of the placenta was determined using the toothpick method [5]. Toothpicks were used to pierce the placenta from the chorionic plate to the basal plate at nine points selected along two planes that bisect at right angles, including the point of umbilical cord insertion, to capture variation across the placenta. The lengths were transferred onto a clear ruler (0.3 m / 12 inches) calibrated in metres, and the average thickness was calculated to represent placental thickness (Fig. 1). Maximum and minimum thickness values were not recorded in this study.

A photograph showing the determination of placental thickness using the toothpick method (×0.2)

Placental diameter was determined using a measuring tape (Shanghai, China) on the placenta at four different angles. The points for measuring the diameter of the placenta were indicated, and the mean diameter was determined. This procedure was used for accuracy since most of the placentae, upon gross examination, were discoid or ovoid in shape, making it impossible to take a single reading; however, some were spherical or round in shape (Fig. 2).

A photograph showing the measurement of placental diameter using a measuring tape (×0.2)

The shape of the placenta was determined by gross observation after removing the membranes and any clots to allow for accurate determination of the true placental contour. For this, the fetal surface was wiped dry, and the placenta was placed on a clean flat surface, after which the shape was assessed and recorded as round, oval, or irregular.

Umbilical cord measurements

The umbilical cord length was measured in its entirety using a tape measure (Shanghai, China) from the fetal end to its point of insertion into the placenta. In each birth case, the umbilical cord was immediately clamped at delivery, and in all cases, the height of the umbilical cord stump left on the neonate was measured. Umbilical cord measurements were made with the umbilical cord still attached to the placenta. The length of the stump was added to each umbilical cord length measurement, and the results were recorded in meters (Fig. 3). In cases of maternal or neonatal complications, measurements were performed only after stabilization of the mother and neonate to ensure that clinical care was not affected. No umbilical cord entanglements were observed in any of the deliveries examined in this study.

A photograph showing the measurement of the umbilical cord length using a measuring tape (×0.3)

The umbilical cord diameter was measured by placing a thread across the cord from outer edge to outer edge, ensuring that Wharton’s jelly was included in the measurement. The thread measurement was transferred onto a calibrated meter rule, and the measurements were recorded in metres. The number of vessels was identified, and the respective vessel diameters were measured using the same technique as that used for the cord diameter.

Data on the site of umbilical cord insertion into the placenta were obtained by gross observation and recorded as central, eccentric, or marginal (Fig. 4a and b). Paramarginal insertions (≤ 1 cm from the edge) were included in the marginal category.

Photographs showing (a) central insertion and (b) eccentric insertion of the umbilical cord (× 0.2)

Measurements of neonatal indices

The neonatal indices measured in this study included the birth weight, height, and head circumference. All measurements were taken within 24 hours after delivery. The birth weight of the neonate was measured with a mechanical baby weighing scale (Rongta, Xiamen, China) calibrated in kilograms. The height of the neonate as well as the head circumference were also measured using a flexible nonstretchable tape measure (Shanghai, China).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 26.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). Normality of each parameter was tested. Descriptive statistics included the mean and standard deviations. An independent Student’s t-test was performed to compare mean differences between normotensive and preeclamptic groups. For categorical variables, chi-square tests were applied. Spearman’s correlation coefficients were used to determine correlations between placental indices and neonatal anthropometric measurements. The level of statistical significance was set at P-value < 0.05.

Results

Descriptive statistics of participants

The mean age of the normotensive mothers was 27.94 ± 5.97 years (Range: 17–41 years), whilst that of the preeclamptic mothers was 27.52 ± 6.24 years (Range: 16–42 years). Preeclamptic mothers had a higher mean blood pressure (155/103 mmHg) compared to normotensive mothers (118/75 mmHg). A total of 33% of the normotensive mothers were nulliparous, 32% were primiparous, and 35% were multiparous. In preeclamptic mothers, 36.5% were nulliparous, 32.7% were primiparous, and 30.8% were multiparous.

Maternal occupation was generally similar between the two groups. In the normotensive group, most women were traders (31), followed by farmers (17), teachers (15), and hairdressers (13). Smaller numbers included seamstresses (9), unemployed women (7), nurses (4), bank cashiers (3), and one midwife. A similar pattern was observed among preeclamptic mothers, where traders were also the most common (14), followed by farmers (9), teachers (8), and hairdressers (7). Seamstresses and unemployed women accounted for four each, while only a few participants were medical laboratory technicians (2), secretaries (2), a nurse (1), and an accountant (1). Overall, the distribution of occupations did not differ significantly between the two groups (p > 0.05).

Placental variables

Analysis of placental characteristics showed that normotensive mothers had significantly higher mean placental weight, thickness, and diameter than preeclamptic mothers (p < 0.001). Mean placental weight was 0.607 ± 0.124 kg in normotensive pregnancies and 0.434 ± 0.068 kg in preeclamptic pregnancies. Placental thickness averaged 0.025 ± 0.003 m versus 0.019 ± 0.002 m, and placental diameter was 0.200 ± 0.013 m versus 0.184 ± 0.014 m for normotensive and preeclamptic groups, respectively.

Figure 5 illustrates the distribution of placental shapes among normotensive and preeclamptic births. Most normotensive placentae were round, whereas the majority of placentae from preeclamptic births were oval. The difference in placental shape between the two groups was statistically significant (χ² = 53.827, df = 2, p < 0.01).

Umbilical cord indices

The mean umbilical cord length was 0.499 ± 0.082 m in normotensive births and 0.527 ± 0.102 m in preeclamptic births, showing no statistically significant difference (t = 1.826, p = 0.070). However, the overall umbilical cord diameter was significantly smaller in preeclamptic births (0.014 ± 0.003 m) compared to normotensive births (0.015 ± 0.003 m, t = -2.114, p = 0.036).

For the individual umbilical vessels, the mean umbilical vein diameter was 0.006 ± 0.001 m in normotensive births and 0.005 ± 0.002 m in preeclamptic births, a statistically significant difference (t = -3.289, p < 0.001). For the umbilical arteries, the mean diameter of the first artery was 0.002 ± 0.001 m in normotensive births compared to 0.001 ± 0.001 m in preeclamptic births (t = -4.123, p < 0.001). The second artery showed a similar pattern, with mean diameters of 0.002 ± 0.001 m versus 0.001 ± 0.001 m for normotensive and preeclamptic births, respectively (t = -5.940, p < 0.001).

Umbilical cord characteristics

In this study, umbilical cord length was classified into short (< 0.4 m), normal (0.4–0.7 m), or long (> 0.7 m). Among the normotensive umbilical cords, none were classified as long, while normal cord lengths were observed in both normotensive and preeclamptic groups (Fig. 6).

Percentage distribution of umbilical cord length in normotensive and preeclamptic births

With regard to cord insertion sites, the results, presented as a bar chart in Fig. 7, show statistically significant differences in umbilical cord insertion sites between normotensive and preeclamptic births (χ² = 25.563; df = 2: p < 0.001). No velamentous or furcate insertions were observed in this series.

Discussion

In this study, placental weight correlated positively with neonatal birth weight, height, and head circumference in both groups, with stronger associations in preeclamptic births. Studies in Ghana [5], Ethiopia [11], Nigeria [12], Nepal [13], and India [14] confirm that placental weight correlates strongly with neonatal weight. This supports the idea that preeclampsia adversely affects placental function, reducing nutrient and oxygen transfer efficiency, which in turn influences neonatal growth. Although birth weight may be compromised, head circumference may be relatively preserved due to the “brain sparing effect” [15], explaining the stronger correlations observed. Clinically, understanding these relationships allows healthcare professionals to anticipate potential neonatal complications and guide targeted postnatal monitoring and care for infants born to mothers with preeclampsia. Although these measurements are only available at delivery, they can inform clinical decision-making, family counseling, and multidisciplinary strategies to improve maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Placental weight reflects villous area and fetal metabolism [16]. Mean placental weight in normotensive births (0.607 ± 0.124 kg) was higher than that reported in previous Ghanaian [5] and Nigerian [17] studies but lower than values from China [18]. In line with earlier reports [19, 20], preeclamptic placentae were significantly lighter, likely due to abnormal spiral artery remodeling and chronic hypoxia [21, 22].

Placental thickness was positively associated with neonatal weight and head circumference but not with neonatal height, aligning with earlier findings [23, 24]. Normotensive pregnancies showed significantly greater thickness compared with preeclamptic pregnancies, consistent with reports by Ulla et al. [25] and Wubale and Tolera [26], though Najafian et al. [27] reported the opposite. Overall, reduced placental thickness in preeclampsia may reflect villous arborization impairment and compromised maternal–fetal exchange [19].

In this study, placental diameter was higher in normotensive births (0.200 ± 0.013 m) than reported in Ethiopia [26] and India [28] but lower than in Pakistan [25]. The differences in these findings could be attributed to geographical, genetic, racial, and medical conditions, which vary from the setting of these studies. Preeclamptic placentae were significantly smaller, consistent with other studies [16, 25, 26]. Although no significant associations were found between placental diameter and neonatal outcomes in normotensive births, strong correlations with birth weight and head circumference emerged in preeclamptic births. This may relate to chorionic plate vascular organization, where larger diameters enhance nutrient and oxygen transfer [29].

Placental shape also differed significantly between groups, with more oval placentae observed in preeclampsia compared with rounder shapes in normotensive births, supporting reports by Navbir [30], Tiruneh et al. [11], and Girish et al. [31]. Altered shapes may result from reduced uteroplacental blood flow, apoptosis, and compensatory hyperplasia, ultimately contributing to impaired fetal growth [26].

Umbilical cord length showed significant negative correlations with neonatal weight and head circumference in both groups, and with neonatal body length in normotensive births only. This suggests that longer cords are not necessarily associated with better neonatal outcomes, consistent with previous reports [32]. Although some studies reported no association [33] or positive correlations [34], our findings suggest cord length may serve as a predictor of fetal outcomes. The slightly longer cords observed in preeclampsia may reflect intrauterine constraints or cord entanglement [35]. In contrast, cord diameter correlated positively with neonatal parameters in both groups, except for neonatal length in preeclampsia, corroborating earlier studies [34, 36]. Reduced cord diameter in preeclampsia may result from placental insufficiency and endothelial damage induced by antiangiogenic and inflammatory factors [37, 38].

Umbilical cord diameter correlated positively with neonatal parameters in both groups, but correlations with neonatal length were weak and non-significant in preeclamptic births. This is consistent with earlier findings [34, 36]. The reduced cord diameter observed in preeclampsia may result from placental insufficiency and vascular damage due to antiangiogenic and inflammatory factors [37, 38].

We acknowledge that umbilical cord length and diameter can vary with gestational age, and adjusting for gestational age could provide a more precise interpretation of their associations with neonatal outcomes. In this study, all pregnancies were term (≥ 37 weeks), which minimized gestational age-related variability; however, future studies including preterm births should consider such adjustments.

Variations in umbilical cord morphology, including insertion site, length, and diameter, may reflect underlying placental vascular development and function [39]. Abnormal cord insertions, particularly marginal insertions, have been associated with impaired placental perfusion and adverse neonatal outcomes [40]. Although velamentous and furcate insertions were not observed in this study, the differences in insertion patterns between groups suggest that preeclampsia may be associated with altered placental cord attachment and vascular distribution [39, 40]. These structural alterations may contribute to compromised fetal growth and highlight the importance of cord morphology in understanding the pathophysiology of preeclampsia [39].

Similarly, umbilical vessel diameters were significantly smaller in preeclamptic pregnancies, supporting previous findings [34, 38]. Reduced vascular size may impair blood flow and nutrient delivery, contributing to fetal growth restriction [37–39]. Nevertheless, vessel diameters correlated positively with neonatal weight and head circumference in both groups, consistent with Bimpong et al. [34], who reported increased birth weight with larger cord diameter.

This study has several strengths, including the use of standardized methods for assessing placental and umbilical cord morphometry and the exclusion of participants with known confounding conditions, which enhanced the internal validity of the findings. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, maternal factors such as nutritional status, body mass index, lifestyle, socio-economic status, and access to healthcare were not assessed. Placental thickness was evaluated using average values without recording maximum and minimum measurements, which may not fully capture regional variability. Furthermore, other umbilical cord characteristics, such as coiling index and direction, were not assessed. Future studies should consider including these features, as they may provide further insights into cord morphology and its relationship with neonatal outcomes in preeclampsia. The single-center nature of the study may also limit external validity.

Overall, the findings of this study highlight significant alterations in placental and umbilical cord morphometry in preeclampsia and their associations with neonatal outcomes. These structural changes likely reflect underlying placental insufficiency and vascular dysfunction characteristic of preeclampsia. Understanding these relationships may improve risk assessment, guide postnatal care, and inform future research aimed at optimizing maternal and neonatal outcomes. These preliminary findings may also support the development of multidisciplinary strategies to improve pregnancy outcomes in preeclampsia.

Conclusion

Preeclamptic umbilical cord and placental indices differed significantly from those of normotensive pregnancies, except for umbilical cord length. Moderate to strong correlations were observed between preeclamptic umbilical cord and placental indices and neonatal outcomes, except for neonatal length. Placentae were predominantly oval, with umbilical cords showing eccentric insertion in preeclampsia. These findings provide baseline data and highlight the relevance of placental and cord morphometry in understanding neonatal outcomes in preeclampsia.
