MZT2A drives epithelial-mesenchymal transition in lung adenocarcinoma via the LGALS3BP/ITGB1/TGF-β/smad2 axis
Authors: Yizhuo Li, Yi Yang, Zhe Zhang, Rui Wang, Zongang Liu, Huanxi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe high mortality of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is largely attributed to its metastatic propensity; therefore, elucidating novel mechanisms driving metastasis is crucial for developing diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. This study aimed to elucidate the function and mechanism of MZT2A in the metastasis of LUAD. Analysis of clinical samples and public databases revealed that MZT2A is highly expressed in LUAD tissues and significantly associated with lymph node metastasis, advanced stage, and poor prognosis. In vitro functional assays revealed that MZT2A overexpression significantly enhanced the invasion, migration, and adhesion of LUAD cells and induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Mechanistically, via its MOZART2 domain, MZT2A interacts with the SRCR domain of galectin-3-binding protein (LGALS3BP), promoting the sorting of LGALS3BP into exosomes and its subsequent secretion. Secretory LGALS3BP acts as a ligand, binding to integrin beta-1 (ITGB1) on the cell membrane through its BTB domain, thereby activating the downstream TGF-β/smad2 signaling pathway to drive EMT and metastatic phenotypes. Molecular docking and mutation experiments confirmed these critical domain interactions. In vivo experiments also validated that MZT2A overexpression promoted pulmonary metastasis, while LGALS3BP knockdown reversed this effect. Collectively, this study elucidated a novel “MZT2A-LGALS3BP-ITGB1-TGF-β/smad2” signaling axis that drives LUAD metastasis, providing a potential novel target for prognosis assessment and targeted therapy in LUAD.

Introduction

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prevalent subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which accounts for a significant proportion of the global cancer burden. The incidence of LUAD has gradually increased to 40%–50% in Western countries, such as the United States and Europe, while in Asian countries, over 60% of lung cancers are LUAD, especially among non-smokers and women.1,2,3,4 Although patients with early-stage (stage I) LUAD have high (73%–90%) five-year overall survival (OS) rates, those with advanced-stage (stage IV) LUAD usually have a worse survival rate (<20%).5 Studies have shown that LUAD cells can metastasize to distant organs of the liver, bone and brain through the blood circulation and lymphatic system,6,7,8 which dramatically shortens the survival of patients with LUAD. During the metastasis, LUAD cells usually undergo EMT, which is crucial for cell migration and invasion, leading to distant metastasis.9,10

EMT is a key biological process during cancer progression, particularly for cancer metastasis.11,12,13 Epithelial cells undergoing EMT lose their intercellular connections and gain stronger migration and invasion ability. In addition, the EMT process is closely regulated by cytokines in the tumor microenvironment, such as transforming growth factor β (TGF-β). These cytokines not only induce EMT but also promote tumor cell proliferation and enhance the malignant behavior of LUAD.11,14 Current findings suggest that EGFR mutations or TKI resistance in LUAD can promote EMT,15,16 which significantly undermines treatment efficacy. Therefore, cancer cell EMT is an important predictor of poor prognosis in LUAD and can serve as a therapeutic target for this cancer.

Microtubules are major components of the cytoskeleton and are mainly composed of α-tubulin and β-tubulin dimers.17 They are crucial for the maintenance of cellular shape, material transport, cell division, and signal transduction.18 The recombination and dynamic changes in microtubules can induce EMT and promote the migration and invasion of tumor cells.19,20,21 Therefore, targeting the dynamic changes in microtubules may provide a new strategy for the treatment of LUAD.

MZT2A, also known as FAM128A, mediates cell septation signaling during cell growth by balancing microtubule polymerization and depolymerization at the centrosome.22,23 MZT2A is a member of the γ-tubulin family, closely associated with the malignant progression of NSCLC.24 However, it is still unclear how MZT2A regulates the EMT of LUAD cells. The present study explored how the relative expression levels of MZT2A in LUAD are associated with the prognosis of patients with LUAD. In vitro and in vivo loss and gain of function studies were also conducted to determine the role of MZT2A in regulating tumor cell EMT, and to investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Results

MZT2A overexpression in LUAD tissues was associated with worse OS of patients with LUAD

To determine the pathogenic role of MZT2A in the development of LUAD, 147 LUAD tissue samples were collected and the expression levels of MZT2A were measured in LUAD and surrounding non-tumor lung tissues by immunohistochemistry (Figure 1A). MZT2A was mainly localized in the cytoplasm of lung cells, whereas a small portion was located in the cell nucleus, and its expression was significantly upregulated in the LUAD tissues compared to paired non-tumor lung tissues. Particularly, MZT2A expression was significantly higher in tissues with lymph node metastasis than in tissues without lymph node metastasis (Figure 1A ii and iii). Association of MZT2A expression with clinicopathological data from patients

Meanwhile, bioinformatic analyses of MZT2A mRNA transcripts in LUAD tissues in the TCGA database revealed that upregulation of MZT2A mRNA transcripts in LUAD was associated with worse OS of patients with LUAD (Figures 1B and 1C). COX regression analysis indicated that MZT2A overexpression was positively associated with higher T, N, and M stages of patients with LUAD (Figure 1D), suggesting that MZT2A overexpression may affect the metastasis of LUAD.

In single-cell database analysis, the major cell types were identified based on cross-cohort typical cell type-labeled scores (Figure 1E). We selected cases of Lung_N (normal lung tissue), Lung_T (non-metastatic LUAD), and EBUS (lymph node metastatic LUAD) from the dataset for subsequent analysis, and analyzed the proportion of major cell types in each group (Figure 1F). The expression of MZT2A in the dataset was also analyzed (Figure 1G). Subsequently, we analyzed the expression of MZT2A in the above three groups. The results indicated that the expression of MZT2A in LUAD (LungT, EBUS) was significantly higher than that in normal lung tissue (LungN), especially in the EBUS group (Figure 1H). All the previous results suggest that MZT2A is closely related to the metastasis of LUAD.

MZT2A promoted the EMT of LUAD cells by activating the TGF-β signal pathway

We produced A549 and H1299 cells with MZT2A overexpression or silencing to explore whether MZT2A expression can modulate the EMT of LUAD cells. Cell transfection efficiency was confirmed using western blotting and RT-qPCR (Figures S1A and S1B). Next, we measured their invasion ability in vitro using transwell assay. The results indicated that MZT2A overexpression significantly increased the number of invading LUAD cells, whereas MZT2A silencing significantly decreased the number of invading LUAD cells (Figure 2A). Altered MZT2A expression similarly affected wound healing (Figure S1C). Cell adhesion experiments also demonstrated that MZT2A overexpression increased the adhesion ability of tumor cells with collagen I, fibronectin, and laminin proteins. Conversely, MZT2A silencing undermined the adhesion ability of tumor cells with those proteins (Figure 2B). We conducted time-course adhesion assays to further assess the effect of MZT2A on cellular adhesion. Western blotting displayed that MZT2A overexpression dramatically enhanced the expression of N-cadherin, vimentin, and phosphorylated smad 2 and reduced E-cadherin expression. However, MZT2A overexpression did not significantly affect the expression of smad 2, HES1, HEY1, and β-catenin in LUAD cells (Figures 2D and S1E). MZT2A silencing exhibited opposite effects on gene expression in LUAD cells. Previous studies have shown that the EMT of tumor cells is regulated by the TGF-β, Notch, and Wnt pathways.25,26,27 The lack of significant changes in the expression levels of HES1, HEY1, and β-catenin, together with a considerable increase in phosphorylated smad2 to non-phosphorylated smad2 ratio, indicates that MZT2A may regulate the EMT of LUAD cells through the TGF-β pathway.

Immunofluorescence assay and nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation assay also showed that MZT2A overexpression can promote smad2 phosphorylation and enhance its nuclear translocation (Figures 2E and 2F). After adding the TGF-β pathway inhibitor (LY2109761) to A549 and H1299 cells with MZT2A over-expression, the inhibitor reversed the promoting effect of MZT2A on smad2 phosphorylation (Figure S2A). This indicates that MZT2A can regulate the TGF-β signaling pathway. All these results suggest that altered MZT2A expression modulates the EMT of LUAD cells in vitro by enhancing the TGF-β pathway.

To explore how the TGF-β pathway regulates MZT2A, we first investigated whether it functions by controlling the TGF-β ligand. In A549 and H1299 cells in the control, transfection control, and MZT2A-manipulated groups, we measured the mRNA expression and protein secretion of TGF-β1. Manipulation of MZT2A did not significantly alter TGF-β1 mRNA levels (Figure S2B) or secretory protein levels (Figure S2C), ruling out ligand upregulation as the activation mechanism. Thus, we investigated upstream regulatory nodes, focusing on the control of ligand-receptor engagement.

MZT2A promoted the EMT of LUAD cells by enhancing LGALS3BP secretion and activating the ITGB1/TGF-β pathway

By consulting the literature and the BioGRID database 5.0 (https://thebiogrid.org/576069), we focused our interest on LGALS3BP. We first measured the potential interactions between MZT2A and LGALS3BP in A549 and H1299 cells using co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays to unravel the role of LGALS3BP in MZT2A-mediated EMT. The anti-LGALS3BP antibody effectively identified MZT2A in both cell types (Figure 3A). Next, we found that LGALS3BP silencing did not alter MZT2A expression levels, but effectively attenuated the promoting effect of MZT2A overexpression on EMT-related proteins in both A549 and H1299 cells (Figure S3A). LGALS3BP is a glycoprotein and is usually secreted into the extracellular space as a ligand. By binding to specific molecules, it regulates various biological functions. To investigate the regulatory role of MZT2A in LGALS3BP exocytosis, this study isolated and characterized exosomes from the cell culture supernatant after modulating MZT2A expression. We assessed variations in exosome quantity and LGALS3BP enrichment. Comprehensive exosome characterization was conducted using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and western blotting (Figures 3B, 3C, and S3B). Subsequently, western blotting was employed to quantify the protein levels of LGALS3BP in exosomes (Figure 3D). The findings indicated that MZT2A overexpression significantly elevated LGALS3BP content in exosomes, while the cellular expression of the LGALS3BP protein did not show a significant change. Next, we conducted ELISA to measure LGALS3BP levels in the pleural effusion fluid of patients with or without LUAD and the supernatants of cultured cells. LGALS3BP levels were higher when tumor cells were found in the pleural effusion or metastasis existed in patients with LUAD (Figure 3E). LGALS3BP levels in the supernatants of cultured cells were positively associated with MZT2A expression in both A549 and H1299 cells (Figure 3F; Table S5).

To clarify the effect of LGALS3BP exocytosis on tumor cells, we prepared LGALS3BP-deficient culture medium and co-cultured it with A549 cells and H1299 cells. We found that the lack of LGALS3BP in the culture medium significantly inhibited cellular migration and adhesion abilities (Figures 3G, 3H, and S3C), significantly upregulated the expression of E-cadherin in A549 and H1299 cells, and suppressed vimentin expression and smad2 phosphorylation, but did not change MZT2A expression (Figure 3I). Thus, we proposed that MZT2A promoted the EMT of LUAD cells by enhancing LGALS3BP secretion.

Given that LGALS3BP acts as a ligand of integrin,28 we used a Co-IP assay to measure the potential interactions between LGALS3BP and ITGB1 in A549 and H1299 cells (Figure 3A). In single-cell sequencing experiments, we measured the intracellular molecular communication in LUAD cells (Figure 4A), and separately detected the significant differences in receptor-ligand communication in cells based on our previous grouping (Figure 4B). The results indicated that ITGB1 was actively involved in the communication between epithelial cells and between epithelial cells and fibroblasts. These communication signals were mostly concentrated in pathways linked to cell migration and adhesion, such as the FN1 and LAMB pathways. Although there was no significant difference in ITGB1 activity between epithelial cells in the Lung_T and EBUS groups, both groups exhibited higher activities than the Lung_N group. Bioinformatics analysis using data from the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) suggested the potential interactions between the LGALS3BP and ITGB1 proteins (Figure 4C). All of these findings support the results of our Co-IP experiment. Immunofluorescence indicated that both LGALS3BP and ITGB1 proteins were co-localized predominantly on the cell membranes of MZT2A-overexpressing A549 cells, while this co-localization phenomenon disappeared in H1299 cells with MZT2A silencing (Figure 4D). To determine the role of ITGB1 in MZT2A/LGALS3BP-mediated EMT, A549 and H1299 cells were transfected with LGALS3BP cDNA and ITGB1-specific siRNA. Transwell, wound healing, and cell adhesion assay indicated that ITGB1 downregulation significantly inhibited the promoting effect of LGALS3BP overexpression on cell invasion and adhesion (Figures 4E, 4F, and S4). Western blotting showed the same trend in the expression of EMT-related proteins in A549 and H1299 cells (Figure 4G). Meanwhile, we measured the effect of alterations in MZT2A expression on the expression and activation status of ITGB1 protein. The results indicated that MZT2A does not directly affect the protein expression of ITGB1 (Figure S5A), but it can regulate the activity of ITGB1 through LGALS3BP (detected by the activity conformation-specific antibody 12G10, Figure S5B). Thus, MZT2A-mediated EMT may rely on the increased secretion of LGALS3BP and its interaction with ITGB1 to activate the downstream signaling pathways.

Identification of the interaction sites between LGALS3BP and MZT2A or ITGB1 proteins

To understand how LGALS3BP interacts with MZT2A or ITGB1, their molecular docking was analyzed using GRAMM docking (Figure 5A). The results predicted that LGALS3BP might interact with MZT2A through its SRCR domain and bind to ITGB1 via its BTB domain. Accordingly, three domain mutants of LGALS3BP were designed (Figure 5B). Functionally, induction of BTB-mutated LGALS3BP overexpression failed for immunoprecipitation of ITGB1 protein. Induction of SRCR-mutated LGALS3BP also failed for immunoprecipitation of MZT2A protein in both A549 and H1299 cells (Figure 5C). Furthermore, induction of LGALS3BP overexpression, but not any of the mutants, effectively promoted the EMT of both cell types (Figure 5C). Moreover, ELISA unveiled that LGALS3BP levels were significantly increased in the supernatant of cultured MZT2A-overexpressing A549 and H1299 cells after inducing the overexpression of wild-type LGALS3BP or its SRCR-mutant, but not after inducing the overexpression of BTB-mutated LGALS3BP (Figure 5D). In addition, Co-IP and ELISA assays indicated that LGALS3BP, but not its SRCR-mutant, interacted with MZT2A through its MOZART2 domain and enhanced the LGALS3BP level and EMT of both cell types (Figures 5E and 5F). MZT2A promoted the metastasis of LUAD in nude mice

Finally, we measured the oncogenic role of MZT2A in the development and metastasis of LUAD in a nude mouse model of caudal vein tumor formation. First, we conducted a subcutaneous tumorigenesis experiment in mice. We found that compared to the control group, MZT2A overexpression significantly increased the volume and growth rate of subcutaneous tumors in mice (Figure S6), suggesting that MZT2A promotes the development of LUAD.

Subsequently, we established a mouse tail vein tumor metastasis model to verify the effect of the MZT2A-LGALS3BP signaling axis on the metastasis of LUAD in vivo. After the infusion of pMZT2A, shLGALS3BP, co-trans or control A549 cells for eight weeks, we found that compared to mice with control A549 cells, mice with MZT2A-overexpressing A549 cells developed more and larger metastatic lung tumors (Figures 6A–6C), whereas metastatic lung tumors were hardly observed in shLGALS3BP and co-trans groups. HE staining revealed that the tumor tissue sections had a typical adenoid structure, which is a characteristic feature of adenocarcinoma. Furthermore, the serum level of LGALS3BP in mice with MZT2A-overexpressing tumors was significantly higher than that in mice with control tumors (Table S6; Figure 6D). Moreover, western blotting unveiled that compared to control tumors, MZT2A overexpression significantly reduced the expression levels of E-cadherin, but enhanced vimentin expression and smad2 phosphorylation in the tumor tissues of mice (Figure 6E). However, in the shLGALS3BP and co-trans groups, these proteins showed an opposite trend. Discussion

Our previous study showed that MZT2A can promote the growth of NSCLC.24 The present study extended previous findings and found that MZT2A acts as an oncogenic factor and promotes the EMT and metastasis of LUAD cells in vitro and in vivo. Consistently, MZT2A overexpression in LUAD tissues was associated with worse OS of patients with LUAD in this study. Mechanistically, MZT2A enhanced the expression and secretion of LGALS3BP and MZT2A through its MOZART2 domain and interacted with the SRCR domain of LGALS3BP. Through its BTB domain, LGALS3BP binds to ITGB1 on the cell membrane, thereby enhancing the TGF-β signaling and promoting the EMT, invasion, and metastasis of LUAD cells (Figure 7). These findings provide novel insights into the mechanisms by which MZT2A enhances the EMT and metastasis of LUAD cells and reveal new therapeutic targets for the treatment of LUAD.Figure 7Mechanism diagramA diagram illustrating the potential mechanisms through which MZT2A enhances the metastasis of LUAD.

The dynamic changes in the microtubules of tumor cells can facilitate their EMT, migration, and invasion.29,30 In this study, we found that MZT2A overexpression enhanced the EMT of LUAD cells by enhancing the secretion of LGALS3BP. Microtubules are crucial for endocytosis and exocytosis of tumor cells and can provide a transport track for moving materials across the membrane, ensuring that secretory vesicles can successfully reach the cell membrane.31,32 MZT2A promotes the secretion of LGALS3BP and affects the EMT of LUAD. In this research, we also detected the regulation of LGALS3BP expression by MZT2A, and our results were inconsistent, which we speculate may be due to MZT2A continuously promoting the extracellular secretion of LGALS3BP, thereby affecting its steady-state intracellular levels. However, the specific mechanisms through which MZT2A promotes the exocytosis of LGALS3BP have not been investigated. We will also conduct in-depth studies on this aspect in subsequent studies.

LGALS3BP is a glycoprotein and is secreted by several types of cancer cells. Previous studies have shown that LGALS3BP is crucial for cell adhesion, migration, proliferation, and EMT, and high levels of LGALS3BP can be detected in several types of cancer, particularly in metastatic cancers.20,33,34 The EMT process is regulated by various signal pathways, such as the TGF-β/Smad, Wnt/β-catenin, and Notch signaling pathways.26,27,35 In this study, MZT2A overexpression significantly enhanced smad2 phosphorylation and slightly increased β-catenin levels, but not other molecules in LUAD cells. Therefore, MZT2A-induced EMT in LUAD cells may be predominantly mediated via the TGF-β/Smad signaling. We used data mining and other means to identify the possible role of ITGB1 in the MZT2A-LGALS3BP-EMT axis and verified its involvement and specific targets. Our results indicated that extracellular LGALS3BP bound to ITGB1 on the cell membrane via its BTB domain, thereby activating the downstream Smad signaling and promoting EMT.

In-depth mechanism analysis revealed that MZT2A promoted the exocytosis of LGALS3BP, activated ITGB1, and subsequently regulated the TGF-β1/smad2 pathway.36,37 Notably, this regulatory effect was not achieved by increasing the expression levels of TGF-β1, but by enhancing the activity of ITGB1, making cells more sensitive to TGF-β1 and more effectively activating the smad2 signal. The molecular mechanism may involve two aspects: first, the activated ITGB1 can directly interact with latent TGF-β binding protein (LTBP) or latent associated peptide (LAP). Through the application or response to mechanical force, it induces conformational changes in the inactive TGF-β complex, thereby releasing the biologically active TGF-β ligand. Second, the activated ITGB1 can form a stable signaling complex with TGF-β type II receptor (TβRII) on the plasma membrane. This physical coupling not only promotes the dimerization and activation of TGF-β receptors but also recruits receptors to specifically target membrane microdomains rich in downstream signaling molecules, thereby more efficiently triggering smad2 phosphorylation and subsequent signaling cascades. We will conduct more in-depth research on this mechanism in the future. Specifically, the discovery of the complete signaling axis of “MZT2A-LGALS3BP-ITGB1-TGF-β/Smad” reveals a new mechanism of MZT2A in cancer metastasis, highlighting the key role of ITGB1 activity in the TGF-β signaling pathway and providing new ideas for targeted therapy.

Our previous study indicated that MZT2A overexpression promotes the proliferation of NSCLC cells and the growth of NSCLC tumors by enhancing PI3K/AKT signaling.24 As an important factor regulating tumor growth, tubulin has been regarded as a therapeutic target against cancer. Paclitaxel, vincristine, and other drugs targeting tubulin are being widely administered as first-line anti-tumor drugs, although they lead to some side effects.38,39 MZT2A is a non-core member of the tubulin family, with a highly conserved molecular profile. Small-molecule drugs targeting MZT2A may effectively control LUAD with few side effects. We will also target the main functional domain (MOZART2) of MZT2A in subsequent studies to develop small-molecule targeted drugs and validate their potential application.

In summary, the present study found that MZT2A acted as an oncogenic factor and promoted the EMT of human LUAD, leading to metastasis. MZT2A can serve as a therapeutic target for the treatment of LAUD and improve the prognosis of patients.

Limitations of the study

While this study reveals a novel MZT2A-LGALS3BP-ITGB1-TGF-β/smad2 axis driving LUAD metastasis, limitations include reliance on cell line and xenograft models that may not fully capture the human tumor microenvironment. The EMT process is complex. In fact, the regulatory effect of MZT2A on EMT may not be limited to LGALS3BP-mediated exocytosis. As a GALS3-binding protein, LGALS3BP also transmits signals promoting EMT, not merely through ITGB1. We will conduct more in-depth and extensive research on these processes in subsequent studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

Human lung cancer cell lines A549 and H1299 were used. Both cell lines were obtained from Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, Gaithersburg, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Clark Biosciences, USA) in a humidified incubator at 5% CO2 and 37°C. Cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.

Animal models

Female BALB/c nude mice (age: 6–8 weeks, weight: 18–22 g) were purchased from Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Mice were housed in SPF conditions under a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. The animal study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of China Medical University (approval #: CMU2022011 and CMU20260088) and conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by the Chinese Council on Animal Research.

Human subjects

Between June 2023 and August 2024, we sequentially collected 147 treatment-naïve surgical lung specimens from patients with LUAD in Shengjing Hospital Affiliated with China Medical University. All patients underwent radical surgical excision, and their LUAD was analyzed by pathological examination and classified based on the TNM grading 9th edition. Their tumor and paired non-tumor lung tissues were paraffin-embedded and histologically examined for retrospective analyses. The demographic and clinical data of patients were obtained from medical records, including age, gender, tumor size, lymph node metastasis, and TNM stage. We also collected pleural effusion samples from 37 LUAD or normal cases between September 2025 and December 2025. This study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University (approval No. 2023PS303K and 2025PS1563K) and informed consent was obtained from all subjects. All samples were used for a single retrospective analysis; no separate allocation to experimental groups was performed.

Sex as a biological variable

Animal models

Only female mice were used in this study. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) exhibits no significant sex-based differences in incidence or progression. We further analyzed the potential influence of sex on the regulation of LUAD malignant progression by MZT2A using clinical samples and found no significant association. Therefore, the selection of female mice was primarily for practical animal husbandry reasons: due to the long observation period required for the experimental model, male mice were avoided to prevent fighting-related injuries and mortality that could have led to insufficient sample size. We acknowledge that the lack of male mouse data is a limitation; however, given the known absence of sex-specific effects in LUAD, this choice is unlikely to bias the results. Future studies incorporating both sexes will help validate the generalizability of these findings.

Human subjects

To evaluate whether sex influences the role of MZT2A in LUAD progression, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by sex. The results showed no significant difference in the association between MZT2A expression and clinical outcomes (e.g., tumor stage, lymph node metastasis, survival) between male and female patients. Therefore, sex was not considered a confounding factor in this study.

Method details

Immunohistochemical analysis

The paraffin-embedded tissues were cut into 3-μm sections, dewaxed, rehydrated, and blocked with 5% BSA in staining buffer. Next, individual sections were incubated with MZT2A antibody (1:200, Cat. PA5-62653, ThermoFisher, USA) at 4°C overnight. The sections were washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at 37°C for 1 h, followed by incubation with DAB. The intensity indices of antibody staining in individual sections were scored from 0 to 12 in a blinded manner following a previous study.24 MZT2A expression levels in individual sections were categorized as high or low, based on a cutoff staining index of 3.

Database and bioinformatics analysis

The relative levels of MZT2A mRNA transcripts in LUAD and non-tumor lung tissues in the TCGA database were analyzed using the R package (Version 4.4.1) and the relationship between the levels of MZT2A mRNA transcripts and the OS of patients with LUAD was analyzed. The potential association of MZT2A expression and other clinicopathological measures with the OS of patients with LUAD was determined based on multi-factor COX analysis.We downloaded the single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data of 58 patients with LUAD (GSE131907) from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The LUNG_T, LUNG_N, and EBUS groups were extracted for further analysis. Data with fewer than 200 or more than 8000 expressed genes were removed. The top 2000 highly variable genes were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function in the Seurat package with default parameters. After Z-score normalization, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on highly variable genes. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was employed to reduce the dimensionality of the top 16 important principal components (PCs). Clusters were determined using the FindClusters function (resolution = 0.5). Manual annotation was based on canonical markers, and marker information for each cell type was obtained from the marker genes in the original dataset. Data were visualized using the ggplot2 package. We analyzed the intercellular communication of scRNA-seq using the CellChatV2 package. The communication results of all ligand-receptor interactions for each signaling pathway were calculated using computeCommunoProbPathway. The results were visualized using the “netVisual_circle” function.

Plasmid construction, cell transfection, and establishment of stably transfected cell lines

MZT2A siRNAs, ITGB1 siRNAs, LGALS3BP siRNAs, LGALS3BP shRNA, and negative control siRNA/shRNA were purchased from Riobobio (Guangzhou, China). Plasmids expressing human MZT2A, LGALS3BP, and the MOZART2 domain of human MZT2A (designated as pMOZART2) were constructed in our laboratory. We also constructed 3 LGALS3BP mutants with changes in their BTB and SRCR domains. The first mutant carried the BTB domain deficiency named the p-ΔBTB plasmid, the second mutant had an SRCR domain deficiency named the p-ΔSRCR plasmid, and the third mutant carried a deficiency in both the BTB and SRCR domains named the ΔBTB+ΔSRCR plasmid (GenePharma, China). Detailed information about all siRNA sequences, the negative control, and all sequences of overexpressing and mutant plasmids is provided in Tables S1 and S2. Using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA), A549 and H1299 cells were transfected with individual types of plasmids, shRNA, and siRNAs. The transfection efficacy in individual types of cells was measured by Western blotting, and ideal cells were selected for subsequent experiments. The cells were cultured in a medium supplemented with puromycin 5μg/mL, MCE, USA for three weeks to establish cell lines with stable gene expression.

Transwell and wound healing assay

Transwell invasion and wound healing assays were conducted to determine the effect of altered MZT2A expression on the invasion and metastasis of lung cancer cells. Briefly, different groups of A549 (8x104 cells/well) and H1299 cells (5x104 cells/well) were loaded on the upper chambers of 24-well plates 8μm pore size that were coated with 100 μL of Matrix gel and the bottom chambers were filled with complete medium. The cells were cultured at 37°C for 24 h and the invaded cells on the bottom of the upper chamber membranes were stained with crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime, China) at room temperature, observed and counted under a microscope (Olympus), as described previously.24 Similarly, different groups of A549 and H1299 cells were cultured in 6-well plates. After reaching confluency, the monolayer of cells was cultured with a serum-free medium containing 1% mitomycin (MCE, USA) for 2 h and wounded using a 200 μL sterile pipette tip. After washing, the cells were cultured in a complete medium for 24 h. The wound healing of cells in five randomly selected fields in individual wells was photographed under an inverted microscope. Images were analyzed using Image J (version 1.53p, NIH, USA).

Cell adhesion capability

A549 and H1299 cells were transfected with MZT2A siRNA or cDNA. After 48 h of transfection, the cells were washed and trypsinized. A549 (2x105 cells/well) and H1299 (1x105 cells/well) cells were seeded into the CytoSelectedTM 48-well Cell Adhesion Assay (CBA-070, Cell Biolabs, USA) and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. For adhesion kinetics assay, we also incubated the cells for 15 min, 30 min, 45 min, 60 min, 90 min, and 120 min. Then, non-adherent cells were removed by gentle washing with PBS, which was repeated four times. The remaining cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 5 min. After washing, the precipitates were dissolved by adding 30% acetic acid (SINOPHARM, China), and absorption was read at 590 nm. The percentage of cell adhesion was determined considering control wells to be 100%.

Western blotting

Different groups of A549 and H1299 cells were harvested and lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer (Beyotime) containing PMSF (Beyotime) and phosphatase inhibitor (MCE). After quantifying protein concentrations using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) kit (Beyotime), the cell lysates were separated using SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, Millex, Germany) membranes. After blockade with 5% milk powder at room temperature for 1 h, the membranes were incubated with the primary antibody (Table S3) in 2% BSA in TBS-T at 4°C overnight. The next day, after washing with TBS-T, the membranes were incubated at 37°C with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse/rabbit IgG at a dilution of 1:20000 (Zsbio, Beijing, China) for 1 h. After washing again with TBS-T, the membranes were incubated with the enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Beyotime) to visualize the positive protein bands captured using an enhanced chemiluminescence device (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The relative protein expression levels were quantified using Image Lab software (Bio-Rad, version 3.0, USA ).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

The potential LGALS3BP/MZT2A and ITGB1/LGALS3BP protein complexes in A549 cells and H1299 cells were analyzed by Co-IP. The cells were collected and lysed for western blotting. After quantitation, 2 mg of each protein sample was incubated with Protein A+G Agarose (Beyotime) at 4°C for 150 min. After centrifugation at 1000 rpm at 4°C, the sample was incubated with anti-LGALS3BP antibody or anti-IgG (as a control; Table S3) at 4°C overnight on a shaking platform as previously described24. The next day, Protein A+G agarose beads were added to the mixture to capture the binding antibody complex at 4°C for 6 h. After centrifugation and washing, the obtained samples were analyzed using Western blotting.

ELISA assay

The expression levels of LGALS3BP or TGF-β1 in the supernatants of each group of cultured cells were quantified using ELISA. Anti-LGALS3BP antibody-coated 96-well plates (E-EL-H1456c, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) or anti-TGF-β1 antibody-coated 96-well plates (E-EL-M0051, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) were used for this purpose. When the cells reached 70%-80% confluence, the culture medium was removed and the cells were washed with serum-free medium to remove residual proteins. Thereafter, the medium was replaced with fresh serum-free medium, and the cells were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 48 h. We collected the cell culture medium and centrifuged it at 1000 ×g at 4°C for 20 min. The samples were acidified with 1 M HCl (20 min, room temperature) and neutralized with 1.2 M NaOH before loading into the microplate for TGF-β1 detection. The supernatant was added to the microplate for incubation at 37°C for 90 min. The biotinylated antibody working solution was added and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. After washing, we added the enzyme conjugate working solution and incubated the sample at 37°C for 30 min. Then, we added the substrate solution (TMB) and incubated the samples at 37°C for 15 min. After adding the stop solution, the absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Extraction of exosomes, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) identification, and particle size analysis

After 48 h of culture in an exosome-free medium (Umibio, Shanghai, China), the supernatants of untreated or transfected A549 and H1299 cells were collected. The exosomes in the cell supernatants were extracted using the TransExo® Cell Media Exosome Kit (FE401-01, Trans, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions.Transmission electron microscopy was employed to identify exosome morphology. The exosome precipitate obtained after separation was resuspended in phosphate buffer, and the concentration was adjusted to approximately 0.2 mg/mL. Five microliters of the sample was dropped onto a formvar/carbon-coated copper grid and left at room temperature for 2 min for adsorption. Excess liquid was carefully absorbed from the edge with filter paper, and then negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate solution in the dark for 1 min. After drying at room temperature for 15 min, the prepared copper grid was observed under a Hitachi HT-7700 transmission electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV. Images were captured at a magnification of 100.0 K.Particle size distribution and the concentration of exosomes were measured using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Briefly, the extracted exosomes were precipitated and diluted with sterile phosphate-buffered saline to a concentration within the optimal detection range of the instrument (20-100 particles per frame). The detection was conducted using a NanoSight NS300 system (Malvern Panalytical, UK) equipped with a 520 nm laser and a scientific-grade CMOS camera. All samples were measured three times under temperature-controlled conditions 28-29°C, with the injection pump speed set at 50 AU. Data were collected and analyzed using NTA software (ZetaView, version 8.05.14 SP7), which calculates the average particle size, mode particle size, and concentration based on the Brownian motion of the particles.

Immunofluorescence

After transient transfection, A549 and H1299 cells were cultured on coverslips and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.25% Triton-X100, blocked with 5% BSA, and incubated overnight with anti-LGALS3BP, anti-ITGB1, anti-ITGB1 12G10 or anti-p-smad2 antibody at 4°C (Table S3). The bound antibodies were dark incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody, mounted on DAPI Fluromount-G (SevenBio, China). A fluorescence inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan) was used to observe cells and take images. Z-stack images were acquired at 0.5 μm intervals and processed into maximum intensity projections. All images within the same experiment were taken using identical settings. The co-localization of proteins was analyzed using the Image J software (version 1.53p).

TGF-β inhibitor assay

At 36 h after transfection, the TGF-β inhibitor (10 μM, Cat. LY2109761, MCE, USA) was added to A549 and H1299 cells. After 12 h, total cellular protein was extracted for Western blotting to detect the phosphorylation level of smad2, which was the downstream effector molecule of the TGF-β pathway.

Nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation

After 48 h of transfection, nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins from A549 and H1299 cells were extracted using the nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction kit (Cat. P0027, Beyotime, China), respectively. The expression changes of p-smad2 in different cellular locations were detected using Western blotting.

LGALS3BP depletion assay

The effect of anti-LGALS3BP on MZT2A-mediated EMT was analyzed. A549 cells and H1299 cells stably transfected with LGALS3BP shRNA were routinely cultured. When the cells reached 70%-80% confluence, the culture medium was removed and the cells were washed with serum-free medium to remove residual proteins. Thereafter, the medium was replaced with fresh serum-free medium, and the cells were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 48 h. The culture medium was collected and centrifuged to remove impurities. The concentration of LGALS3BP in the medium of shNC and shLGALS3BP groups was quantified using ELISA (Table S4). Next, the processed medium was reintroduced into the cells, and this filtration protocol was repeated every 12 h for 48 h. Total cellular protein was extracted for Western blotting of EMT-associated proteins.

Molecular docking

The potential protein-protein docking was analyzed by searching interesting protein sequences in the UniProtKB database. The sequences were input into SWIEE-MODEL to identify the optimal protein structure. Subsequently, the protein-protein docking in the output PDB file was analyzed using the GRAMM. The conformation with the lowest binding energy was selected for subsequent analyses.

In vivo nude mouse experiments

Female BALB/c nude mice (4-week-old) from Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) barrier facility with controlled temperature and humidity. MZT2A-overexpressing A549 cells, shLGALS3PB A549 cells, co-transfection A549 cells (with pMZT2A and shLGALSBP), or control A549 cells were injected via tail vein (0.5 × 106 cells, 100 μL, n = 5 mice per group). Eight weeks after inoculation, all animals were anesthetized with 3%-4% enflurane and euthanized via carbon dioxide inhalation. The venous blood samples of animals were obtained. Their lung tissues were dissected and photographed. Lung metastasis was quantified by a comprehensive count of total nodules, including both macroscopically visible surface nodules and internal nodules observed on the cut surfaces after sectioning the entire lung at 0.5 cm intervals. Lung tissues with tumors were subjected to HE staining. The serum levels of LGALS3BP in individual mice were measured by ELISA. To ensure the representativeness of the samples and reduce individual differences, the protein samples were derived from the pooled lysates of multiple (typically n≥3) independent lung metastatic nodules in each group.A549 cells stably transfected with pNC, pMZT2A were injected subcutaneously into the right forelimb armpit (1 × 106 cells, 100 μL), each group with 10 female BALB/c nude mice. The tumor volume was determined once weekly during the 4-week experiment. At the end of the experiment, all animals were anesthetized with 3%-4% enflurane and euthanized using a carbon dioxide chamber. The tumors were harvested, photographed, and weighed. The tumor tissues were subjected to HE and immunohistochemical staining using anti-MZT2A staining.The animal study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of China Medical University (approval #: CMU2022011 and CMU20260088) and conducted following the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by the Chinese Council on Animal Research.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates (n = 3, representing independent cell culture preparations). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, Student's t-test (parametric, two tailed) or Mann-Whitney U test (non-parametric, two-tailed) was applied as appropriate. For multiple-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA was used. The association between MZT2A expression levels and clinicopathological characteristics of LUAD patients (n = 147 surgical specimens) was analyzed using the chi-squared test. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Detailed statistical information for each experiment, including the specific test used, exact n values, and p-values, is reported in the corresponding figure legends and the results section.
