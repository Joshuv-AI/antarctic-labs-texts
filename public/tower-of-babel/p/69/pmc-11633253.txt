Assessing disaster mental health preparedness among primary health care physicians of Karnataka, India: Designing and conducting a knowledge, attitude, and practice survey
Authors: Aishwarya Prakash, Ateev S. Chandna, Tushar K. Panda, Madhavi Puri, Rajani Parthasarathy, Sanjeev K. Manikappa, Jayakumar Christy, Ajay K. Goyal, Dinakaran Damodharan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Disaster mental health is a critical yet neglected aspect in India. Primary care physicians (PCPs) play a crucial role in mitigating mental health challenges following disasters. This study conducted a knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) survey among PCPs in Karnataka to assess their disaster mental health preparedness.Methods:A cross-sectional online survey was conducted from February to May 2023 targeting PCPs across 29 districts of Karnataka in a convenient snowball sampling method. The questionnaire review included KAP questions on disaster mental healthcare. A total of 324 PCPs responded.Results:Knowledge scores were moderate (mean: 7.05), attitudes were positive (mean: 9.15), and practices were poor (mean: 0.32). Significant correlations were found between clinical experience and knowledge/attitudes/practices (rho 0.358, P < 0.001; rho 0.147, P = 0.007; rho 0.232, P = 0.003).Conclusion:There is a large gap between theoretical knowledge and practical preparedness in disaster mental health. Training programs and government initiatives are essential to improve preparedness among PCPs.

INTRODUCTION

The impact that disasters have on mental health cannot be overstated. Studies have consistently reported that up to 90% of survivors experience psychological distress, while a substantial percentage, ranging from 20% to 50%, ultimately develop psychiatric disorders.[1] Moreover, developing countries like India are particularly vulnerable to the mental health consequences of natural and manmade disasters.[2]

Primary care practitioners (PCPs) play an essential role in mitigating the mental health morbidity of disasters by raising awareness, increasing community resilience and providing psychosocial support.[3] In fact, studies have demonstrated that survivors of disasters are more likely to seek mental health services when they have contact with a PCP.[4]

On a concerning note, surveys from the UAE and Jordan show that PCPs often lack the necessary disaster management knowledge, skills, and preparedness.[56] Similarly, in a small survey, Indian medical students demonstrated a poor understanding of disaster mitigation strategies.[7] However, surprisingly comprehensive studies specifically assessing PCPs’ preparedness in disaster mental health in India are scarce.[8] Given this lacunae in the literature and the crucial role of PCPs, our study aimed to evaluate their capabilities and identify felt needs with the aim of informing further policies and guiding training programs.

METHOD

Study setting

The study was an online survey questionnaire, conducted by a tertiary care center from February 2023 to May 2023. All registered PCPs working in Karnataka were eligible for the study.

Data collection

Before initiating the survey, awareness was raised among PCPs through messaging groups. The study employed a cross-sectional survey design with a convenient snowball sampling method. The survey was distributed online via Google Forms to key PCPs working in primary health care centers across 29 districts in the state, who then further distributed it to other PCPs. To prevent multiple participations, responses were limited to a single email address and cross-checked against the district registry. The Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) guidelines were followed for the study.[9]

Study tool

The survey questionnaire was developed by two authors (AP, TKP) based on relevant literature[6101112] and subsequently reviewed by five experts in disaster psychiatry, psychology, psychiatric social work, and mental health administration (DD, JC, SKM, AG, RP) to ensure face and content validity. Initially comprising 40 questions, after review, the questionnaire was refined to 30 questions to enhance relevance and significance to disaster mental health and to eliminate potential redundancies.

The questionnaire covered basic principles of disasters and mental health, including psychological first aid (PFA) and triaging.

The questionnaire had four parts:

The questionnaire took approximately 20–30 minutes to complete.

Statistical analysis

The data from the questionnaire were analyzed using Microsoft Excel and R software, with descriptive statistics and bivariate analysis conducted. Normality testing was done using Shapiro–Wilk’s test. Spearman’s correlation was used. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance was obtained from the IEC, and informed consent was taken from participants. Anonymity and confidentiality were maintained, with only researchers having access to the data. No additional benefits were provided to participants.

RESULTS

Results

The survey was circulated to primary care doctors from all districts, and doctors from all districts responded, totaling 324 participants. Since the sampling was conducted using a convenient snowball method, the response rate could not be calculated.

As reported in Table 1, the majority of respondents held an MBBS degree (75%) and fell within the 30–39 years age group (40%). The male respondents (54%) slightly outnumbered the females. Furthermore, almost half of the participants (45%) reported having less than 10 years of experience as doctors, while two-thirds of them had worked in their current primary healthcare center (PHC) for less than 5 years.

In the knowledge domain, a substantial proportion of practitioners demonstrated good disaster-related knowledge, with high awareness of potential disasters (88%), predictive disaster scenarios (87%), the Disaster Management Act (80%), and the Mental Health Act of 2017 (78%). Most felt confident about diagnosing common mental disorders (85%) and providing brief psychological interventions (62%). However, awareness of psychosocial support was limited, with only about a third of doctors (38%) familiar with the National Guidelines and about half knowing about psychosocial triaging (55%) and PFA techniques (48%). The total mean knowledge score was 7.05 (SD 2.3).

Practitioners’ attitudes showed that 63% had a positive outlook on district disasters, with 62% believing in their ability to mitigate the impact. A significant majority (86%) emphasized the importance of disaster preparedness training. Regarding psychosocial matters, 90% highlighted the importance of addressing survivors’ psychological well-being, but only 43% felt that all RMPs had adequate PFA skills. Additionally, 48% believed that psychosocial preparedness was sufficiently integrated into district plans. Most practitioners advocated for PFA training and regularly updated Emergency Operational Plans for mental health. The mean attitude score was 9.15 (SD 3.9).

Practices were suboptimal, with only 23.3% having received training in Disaster Mental Health and feeling confident in its application. Approximately 41% recognized local disaster hazards and mapped vulnerable groups, while only 19% conducted HRV analysis in their district. DISCUSSION

This study is the first to assess disaster mental health preparedness among PCPs in India. It found that while PCPs had moderate knowledge and good attitudes, their practices were poor, likely due to a lack of training and clarity about operational plans.

Respondents showed a strong general understanding of disasters but lacked knowledge about psychosocial support in disaster contexts. This gap is likely due to insufficient disaster management training during medical education. For instance, a study among family medicine residents in Turkey found that only 9.2% of doctors received any training in disaster health management.[13] This underscores the necessity of early education in disaster management within the medical curriculum, as supported by research on Chinese medical university students, which shows a significant correlation between early education and readiness for disaster management.[14]

Furthermore, our results emphasize the need to provide psychosocial support training to currently practicing primary care physicians. Field reports from three developing countries indicate that such training significantly improves physicians’ abilities in addressing the mental health needs of disaster survivors.[15]

Our study participants generally held positive attitudes toward disaster management and recognized the role of primary care physicians in disaster response, expressing a desire for more training opportunities. This differs from a study of physicians in emergency units in Ethiopia, where poor knowledge translated to negative attitudes toward disaster preparedness.[16] One potential explanation is that our sample consisted of primary care practitioners working in the community, who, despite having less experience, may have showed greater motivation for involvement in disaster management.

Despite moderate knowledge and positive attitudes, these did not translate into good practices, a common issue in disaster mental health where specialized training and individual traits play significant roles.[17] Additionally, the sizeable majority of participants reported not receiving any training and being unclear about disaster operational plans and guidelines, factors which could have significantly impacted their disaster preparedness efforts. This echoes findings from Lagos, where physicians, despite having positive attitudes toward disaster management, had inadequate practices, primarily attributed to a lack of training and clear organizational policies.[18]

The study found that positive knowledge, attitudes, and practices scores were linked with experience rather than educational qualifications, which is line with a prior study that has indicated that experience dealing with disasters was the most significant factor contributing to better skills and practices in disaster management.[19]

Limitations of the study include the use of a nonstandardized questionnaire, focusing solely on PCPs in one Indian state, making the results not generalizable to the entire country. The sampling method also prevented calculation of the response rate and detailed information on respondent characteristics.

CONCLUSION

The role of PCPs in disaster mental health mitigation and management is of paramount importance, especially in India where there is a shortage of healthcare specialists. Our pioneering large-scale survey revealed that while PCPs had good attitudes and knowledge, their practices were hindered by insufficient training and unclear operational plans.

Countries like Indonesia and Bangladesh have successfully included PCPs in disaster health management by implementing training programs and formulating clear, culturally appropriate policies.[20] Similarly, India could leverage its extensive PCP workforce by improving practices through implementation of training workshops from the undergraduate level and establishment of well-defined policies and operational plans for effective disaster mental health services.
