Multi-trajectory modeling of metabolic syndrome indicators and cardiovascular disease risk: a study based on health examination big data
Authors: Mingwang Fu, Wantong Han, Haoran Zhou, Yongjie Zhang, Jinshui Xu, Ya Shen, Biyun Xu, Haijian Guo, Bingwei Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical utility of metabolic syndrome (MetS) in predicting cardiovascular disease (CVD) and diabetes has been questioned due to its binary definition, which results in substantial loss of information from metabolic indicators. This study aims to longitudinally evaluate MetS measurement indicators, identify their multi-trajectory patterns over time, and assess the associated CVD risk.MethodsGroup-based multi-trajectory modeling (GBMTM) was applied to metabolic syndrome indicators to identify multi-trajectory patterns and describe baseline characteristics of subgroups. Subsequently, with CVD as the outcome, trajectory groups were incorporated into interval-censored Cox proportional hazards models to estimate the associated CVD risks across different trajectory patterns.ResultsThis study identified five distinct metabolic patterns through trajectory typing. The “progressive hyperglycemia trajectory” (characterized by high and continuously increasing fasting plasma glucose [FPG] levels) and the “persistent dyslipidemia trajectory” (characterized by persistently high triglycerides [TG], low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C] with continuous decrease) demonstrated higher CVD incidence density. After adjusting for age and sex, their hazard ratios (HR) were 1.469 (95% CI: 1.319–1.635) and 1.355 (95% CI: 1.189–1.544), respectively.ConclusionsThis study identified five distinct longitudinal trajectories of MetS indicators and demonstrated their significant associations with CVD risk. The progressive hyperglycemia trajectory and the persistent dyslipidemia trajectory were associated with higher CVD risk, underscoring the importance of longitudinal monitoring of FPG, TG, and HDL-C for CVD prevention in elderly populations.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-026-02211-3.Keywords: Group-based multi-trajectory modeling, Metabolic syndrome, Metabolism and cardiovascular disease, Interval-censored Cox proportional hazards model

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of risk factors associated with cardiovascular disease (CVD) and diabetes, primarily characterized by metabolic disorders including increased waist circumference, elevated triglycerides (TG), elevated fasting plasma glucose (FPG), elevated blood pressure (both systolic blood pressure [SBP] and diastolic blood pressure [DBP]), and decreased high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) [1]. With accelerating population aging and the global adoption of Western lifestyles, non-communicable diseases typified by MetS have become major threats to global public health [2]. In China, with urbanization and lifestyle transitions, the prevalence of MetS in elderly populations continues to rise, posing enormous challenges to the national healthcare system.

Since the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP ATP III) first formally proposed universal diagnostic criteria for metabolic syndrome in 2001 [3], its definition and clinical utility have remained subjects of extensive controversy [4–6]. Traditional MetS criteria aim to identify high-risk populations with insulin resistance-related conditions and guide clinical interventions to reduce the risk of CVD and diabetes [7, 8]. However, multiple studies have questioned its clinical value as an independent predictor. Although prospective studies have confirmed that combinations of metabolic abnormalities can predict increased CVD risk [9–13], the risk prediction capability associated with this syndrome is not significantly superior to the cumulative effects of its individual components [14]. A prospective study published in The Lancet found that in elderly populations, the association between MetS and vascular risk was weak or even non-existent, questioning the clinical value of applying existing MetS criteria in elderly populations [5].

The core controversy surrounding the traditional definition of MetS lies in its artificially set binary cutoff points and static assessment approach. A joint statement from the American Diabetes Association and the European Association for the Study of Diabetes claimed that there is sufficient evidence to suggest that simply assuming arbitrary dichotomization in MetS definition criteria can achieve optimal CVD prediction capability is unreasonable, as metabolic indicator risk is a gradual process that cannot be simply determined as present or absent based solely on whether certain thresholds are exceeded [15]. This static, one-size-fits-all assessment method cannot accurately reflect the evolutionary process of individual long-term metabolic status, nor can it comprehensively capture the interactions among multiple metabolic indicators and their combined impact on disease risk.

With rapid advances in statistics and computer science, fully taking advantage of the information available in multivariate longitudinal data for tracking the course of the outcome of interest is feasible. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) is a finite mixture model used to identify heterogeneity in the developmental trajectories of single indicators, capable of dividing populations into multiple subgroups with similar change patterns. Group-based multi-trajectory modeling (GBMTM), as an extension of GBTM, can simultaneously analyze multiple longitudinal indicators and identify coordinated change trends among different indicators [16], providing a novel perspective for MetS research.

Based on large-scale longitudinal data from health examinations of elderly individuals in Kunshan City, Jiangsu Province from 2016 to 2023, this study employed the GBMTM method to simultaneously analyze the long-term change trajectories of six core MetS indicators (waist circumference, TG, HDL-C, SBP, DBP, and FPG). By identifying natural evolutionary patterns and potential subtypes of metabolic indicators, combined with interval-censored Cox proportional hazards models, we evaluated the associations between different metabolic trajectory types and CVD risk.

Moreover, research has shown that older adults often have insufficient awareness of CVD risk factors and tend to underestimate their own cardiovascular risk, highlighting a gap between risk perception and the actual burden of metabolic risk factors in this population [17]. This study aims to overcome the limitations of traditional binary MetS definitions, identify the joint evolution of metabolic indicators, and provide more precise and dynamic new approaches for metabolic health assessment and CVD risk stratification in elderly populations. Additionally, it aims to provide scientific evidence for identifying long-term characteristics of metabolic indicator combinations in high-risk populations and developing individualized prevention and intervention strategies.

Methods

Study population and study design

This retrospective study utilized data from the Basic Public Health Service Program in Kunshan City, Jiangsu Province, which has offered free annual health examinations for residents aged 65 years and older. The study period covered from 2016 to 2023. Data collection was primarily conducted by physicians and nurses at community health centers, with multiple quality control measures in place, including expert review and staff training, to ensure accuracy and reliability. The examinations included demographic characteristics, medical and family history, lifestyle factors, physical measurements, laboratory tests, imaging, electrocardiography, and basic health assessments. The study population consisted of residents aged ≥ 65 years in Kunshan City who voluntarily participated in the program.

The study was conducted in two main phases. Phase 1 involved multivariate trajectory modeling of metabolic indicators to identify heterogeneous longitudinal patterns among older adults. Phase 2 used the derived trajectory group information to construct interval-censored Cox proportional hazards models, evaluating the associations between different metabolic trajectory patterns and the risk of CVD.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Drum Tower Hospital (approval number: 2024-953-01), and all data were de-identified before analysis to strictly protect participant privacy.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants aged 65 years or older who had completed at least four health examinations during the study period were included. Individuals with a diagnosis of CVD at their first examination were excluded from the analysis.

Variable definitions

According to the 2009 international harmonized definition of metabolic syndrome [1], six core metabolic indicators were selected for multi-trajectory modeling: waist circumference (cm), TG (mmol/L), HDL-C (mmol/L), SBP (mmHg), DBP (mmHg), and FPG (mmol/L). These indicators were obtained through standardized clinical measurement methods.

This study used CVD as the primary outcome event, defined as: (1) cerebrovascular diseases (ischemic stroke, cerebral hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, transient ischemic attack); (2) cardiac diseases (myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, coronary revascularization, congestive heart failure); (3) vascular diseases (aortic dissection, arterial occlusive disease); (4) self-reported disease history (coronary heart disease, stroke). Outcomes (1)–(3) were identified through electronic health records maintained in the residents’ health archive system, whereas outcome (4) was collected through self-reported information during health examinations. Individuals who first experienced any of the above conditions during the study period were determined to have CVD occurrence.

Covariates

This study collected four main categories of covariates: (1) Demographic characteristics: age, sex, education level, occupation, marital status; (2) Lifestyle factors: self-rated health (SRH), physical exercise frequency, dietary habits (balanced diet/meat-based diet/vegetarian diet/preference for salt/preference for oil/preference for sugar), smoking status, alcohol consumption frequency; (3) Laboratory examination results: hemoglobin (Hb, g/L), white blood cell count (WBC, ×10^9/L), platelet count (PLT, ×10^9/L), electrocardiogram (ECG, normal/abnormal), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L), serum creatinine (Cr, µmol/L); (4) Medication use: use of antihypertensive drugs, use of antidiabetic drugs.

Age and laboratory test results were treated as continuous variables. Other covariates were treated as categorical variables. SRH was categorized as good, fair, or poor based on questionnaire responses. Physical exercise frequency was classified as regular, occasional, or none. Alcohol consumption was categorized as non-drinking, occasional drinking, or frequent drinking, with frequent drinking defined as drinking often or daily. Smoking status was classified as never, former, or current smoking.

Statistical analysis

K-nearest neighbor (KNN) imputation was used for baseline missing data, with K = 10 set to balance accuracy and computational efficiency. For extreme values in laboratory test results, screening was performed based on clinically reasonable ranges, and values obviously exceeding physiological ranges were processed before imputation.

Continuous variables following normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA); non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using Kruskal-Wallis test; categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage) and compared using Pearson chi-square test.

GBMTM was implemented using the “gbmt” package in R software [18]. Longitudinal changes in six metabolic indicators were modeled simultaneously to identify potential subgroups with similar change patterns. During the modeling process, candidate models with 1–5 potential trajectory groups were first constructed, then the optimal model was selected based on four key criteria: (1) average posterior probability > 0.7 for each group; (2) lower Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) values; (3) proportion of individuals in each trajectory group > 5%; (4) clinical interpretability of trajectory groups. Each indicator used quadratic polynomial functions to fit its longitudinal change trajectory to capture non-linear change trends.

Multinomial logistic regression models were constructed using the metabolically healthy stable group as reference, estimating relative risk ratios (RRR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for each baseline factor’s association with other trajectory group membership. Incidence density per 1,000 person-years was used to evaluate CVD incidence risk for each trajectory group, with 95% confidence intervals calculated based on Poisson distribution. Considering the unknown exact timing of events occurring between examination intervals, interval-censored Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate associations between metabolic trajectory groups and CVD risk, implemented using the “icenReg” package in R software [19]. For individuals with events, the interval midpoint (average of the last event-free examination and first event-confirmed examination times) was used to estimate event occurrence time; for individuals without events at study endpoint (right-censored), the last health examination time was used to calculate their contributed person-years.

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3). A 2-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

The flow of participant selection and the derivation of the final analytical sample are illustrated in the study flowchart (Fig. 1). The final analytical sample included 100,191 elderly individuals with a mean age of 69.00 ± 4.88 years. The missing rates for baseline indicators ranged from 0% to 2.8%. Baseline characteristics were compared according to the occurrence of CVD events during the study period (Table 1). Participants who developed CVD were older (70.25 ± 5.08 vs. 68.95 ± 4.87 years, P < 0.001), had higher SBP (141.83 ± 18.94 vs. 138.92 ± 18.62 mmHg, P < 0.001), higher FPG (5.92 ± 1.46 vs. 5.82 ± 1.34 mmol/L, P < 0.001), larger waist circumference (84.02 ± 9.15 vs. 82.99 ± 8.94 cm, P < 0.001), and higher proportions of antihypertensive and antidiabetic drug use.

Metabolic trajectory groups

GBMTM was applied to classify individuals into distinct trajectory groups based on the longitudinal patterns of six metabolic indicators. Through comparison of goodness-of-fit indicators for 1–5 trajectory models, the five-group trajectory model was determined to be the optimal choice. Details of the model fit indices are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Table S1). Based on the longitudinal change characteristics of six metabolic indicators (Fig. 2), we named and described the five trajectory groups as follows:

GBMTM trajectory plots of metabolic syndrome indicators. Abbreviations: SBP, systolic blood pressure; DBP, diastolic blood pressure; FPG, fasting plasma glucose; TG, triglycerides; HDL-C, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol

Group 1 (15.8%, Progressive Hyperglycemia Trajectory): Characterized by progressively increasing FPG (gradually rising from approximately 6.0 mmol/L to 8.0 mmol/L), SBP maintained at relatively high levels (approximately 140 mmHg), and waist circumference maintained at elevated levels (approximately 86–87 cm). The baseline FPG mean value was 7.51 ± 2.04 mmol/L, significantly higher than other groups (P < 0.001), and this group had the highest proportion of antihypertensive and antidiabetic drug use at 43.2% and 21.3%, respectively, reflecting the treatment needs for metabolic abnormalities in this group.

Group 2 (11.4%, Persistent Dyslipidemia Trajectory): Characterized by persistently high TG levels (approximately 3.0 mmol/L) and low HDL-C levels (approximately 1.2 mmol/L) with progressive decline. The baseline TG mean value reached 3.09 ± 1.59 mmol/L, and HDL-C was 1.24 ± 0.36 mmol/L, representing the highest and lowest levels respectively (P < 0.001).

Group 3 (17.8%, Metabolically Healthy Stable Trajectory): Except for slightly elevated SBP, other indicators remained within ideal ranges, with FPG stable at normal levels (approximately 5.5 mmol/L), lower waist circumference (approximately 75 cm), TG close to normal (approximately 1.2 mmol/L), and HDL-C maintained at optimal levels (1.7–1.8 mmol/L). This group had the highest mean age (70.13 ± 5.57 years, P < 0.001), but the most favorable metabolic indicators and lowest medication use rates, with antihypertensive and antidiabetic drug use rates of only 22.0% and 0.7%, respectively.

Group 4 (24.5%, Mild Central Obesity Progression Trajectory): Blood pressure, FPG, and TG levels were similar to the metabolically healthy stable group, but waist circumference showed a mild upward trend, and HDL-C exhibited a pattern of initial decline followed by increase. This group had the highest proportion of women (62.7%, P < 0.001) and the lowest baseline TG (0.98 ± 0.30 mmol/L).

Group 5 (30.4%, Moderate Metabolic Risk Trajectory): Contained the most individuals, with metabolic indicators between the high-risk groups (Groups 1 and 2) and low-risk groups (Groups 3 and 4). Waist circumference and HDL-C status were similar to Groups 1 and 2, blood pressure and FPG were similar to Groups 3 and 4, and TG was at moderate levels (1.71 ± 0.66 mmol/L). Detailed baseline characteristic comparisons among trajectory groups are presented in the Supplementary Materials (Table S2).

Overall, Groups 3 and 4 showed relatively healthy indicator levels, Groups 1 and 2 showed relatively poor indicator performance, and Group 5 was intermediate between the above groups.

Multinomial logistic regression of metabolic trajectory groups

Incorporating baseline characteristics, with the metabolically healthy stable group (Group 3) as reference, multinomial logistic regression analysis revealed predictive factors for trajectory group membership (Table S3). Results showed: Regarding age, as age increased, the risk of entering other unhealthy groups decreased relative to the healthy stable group, possibly due to the “healthy survivor effect,” where populations maintaining health at advanced age may possess special protective factors, while some unhealthy elderly individuals may have already died, emphasizing the importance of long-term maintenance of healthy metabolic indicators.

ECG abnormalities demonstrated consistent relative risk ratios of less than 1 across all groups with minimal between-group variation (RRR ranging from 0.877 to 0.930). Compared to the metabolically stable group (which represents a unique “healthy survivor” subpopulation), ECG status may not serve as a critical discriminating indicator for metabolic trajectory classification.

Regarding sex, males had significantly increased risk for Group 4 (relatively healthy obesity trajectory) (relative risk ratio [RRR] = 3.013), indicating that males are prone to developing central obesity, while males had significantly decreased risk for Group 2 (persistent dyslipidemia trajectory) (RRR = 0.490), suggesting that females are more likely to develop dyslipidemia. Higher education significantly increased risk across all groups, possibly reflecting the occupational characteristics (sedentary occupational activity) and stress factors of highly educated populations.

Regarding occupation, physical labor showed protective effects across all groups, demonstrating the positive impact of physical activity on metabolic health. Regarding medication use, antihypertensive drug use was significantly associated with most metabolic abnormality trajectories, with users showing significantly increased risk of entering Group 1 (RRR = 1.805, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.715-1.900), Group 2 (RRR = 1.948, 95% CI: 1.845–2.056), and Group 5 (RRR = 1.543, 95% CI: 1.475–1.613), which also explains why Groups 1 and 2 showed deterioration in other indicators while blood pressure indicators continued to improve. Antidiabetic drug use was mainly associated with glucose abnormalities, with users showing significantly increased risk of entering Group 1 (RRR = 24.846, 95% CI: 20.804–29.673) and also significant increase for Group 2 (RRR = 9.826, 95% CI: 8.174–11.811).

Regarding diet, meat-based diet showed consistently strong risk increases, and sugar preference significantly increased risk for Groups 2, 4, and 5, emphasizing the important impact of dietary structure on metabolic health. Exercise and alcohol consumption showed unexpected results in some groups, possibly because individuals with health problems proactively adopted healthy lifestyles such as exercise and alcohol cessation following health examination center recommendations [20]. In summary, we recommend adopting lifestyle habits that increase physical activity and reduce meat and sugar intake, with males particularly focusing on central obesity and females particularly focusing on TG abnormality interventions.

Metabolic trajectories and cardiovascular disease risk

The cumulative incidence curves for CVD across the five metabolic trajectory groups are displayed below (Fig. 3). The stepwise ascending curves reflect the characteristics of interval-censored data. After approximately 7 years of follow-up, Group 1 (progressive hyperglycemia trajectory) showed the highest cumulative incidence (approximately 6.7%), while Group 3 (metabolically healthy stable trajectory) showed the lowest (approximately 3.0%), indicating an association between metabolic trajectory classification and cardiovascular risk. The group curves began separating from the second year of follow-up, with maximum differences reached after the sixth year, suggesting the long-term predictive value of metabolic trajectories.

The CVD incidence density and hazard ratio (HR) analysis results for the five metabolic trajectory groups are presented below (Table 2). CVD risk across groups was generally consistent with the trajectory performance of the respective group indicators. The “progressive hyperglycemia trajectory” (Group 1) showed the highest incidence density (8.06/1000 person-years), representing a 37% increase compared to the “metabolically healthy stable trajectory” (Group 3, 5.87/1000 person-years), followed by the “persistent dyslipidemia trajectory” (Group 2) with an incidence density of 7.26/1000 person-years. HR analysis showed that compared to Group 3, after adjusting for age and sex in Model 2, the “progressive hyperglycemia trajectory” (Group 1) had significantly elevated CVD risk (HR = 1.469, 95% CI: 1.319–1.635). The “persistent dyslipidemia trajectory” (Group 2) similarly showed significant risk increase (HR = 1.355, 95% CI: 1.189–1.544). In other models, all groups showed varying degrees of risk increase compared to Group 3 (except Group 4 in Model 1, which was not statistically significant).

Discussion

This study utilized group-based multi-trajectory modeling (GBMTM) to analyze the longitudinal changes of six core MetS indicators, identifying five distinct metabolic evolutionary patterns based on elderly population health examination big data, and explored the associations between these trajectory patterns and CVD risk through interval-censored Cox proportional hazards models. This multivariate trajectory-based classification method transcends the traditional binary definition of MetS, providing a new perspective for understanding the dynamic evolution of metabolic abnormalities and disease risk assessment.

Through multinomial logistic regression analysis, we identified multiple important factors influencing metabolic trajectory membership. The age effect demonstrated the “healthy survivor phenomenon,” where the risk of entering unhealthy groups decreased with increasing age, emphasizing the importance of long-term maintenance of metabolic health status for successful aging. Sex differences showed that males were more prone to developing central obesity (Group 4), while females were more susceptible to dyslipidemia (Group 2), suggesting that metabolic abnormality interventions should consider sex differences. The results of medication use variables indicated that users of antihypertensive and antidiabetic drugs were more likely to enter Groups 1 and 2, and combined with trajectory changes, glucose control effectiveness appeared less ideal than blood pressure control. Dietary habit analysis revealed that meat-based dietary patterns were highly associated with all metabolic abnormality trajectories, while sugar preference was associated with dyslipidemia and central obesity. Reducing meat and sugar intake may have important protective effects on metabolic health. Physical labor showed protective effects across all trajectory groups, while higher education was associated with increased risk of metabolic abnormalities. This phenomenon may reflect the negative impact of sedentary lifestyles and work stress among highly educated populations in modern society.

The study found that the joint longitudinal trajectories of metabolic indicators over time were associated with CVD risk. The “progressive hyperglycemia trajectory” (Group 1) and “persistent dyslipidemia trajectory” (Group 2) demonstrated higher CVD risks, with HR values of 1.469 (95% CI: 1.319–1.635) and 1.355 (95% CI: 1.189–1.544), respectively, after adjusting for age and sex. This finding is consistent with recent literature. Tao et al. [21] reviewed that the combined biomarker triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index can substitute for insulin resistance and is significantly associated with CVD occurrence, development, and prognosis. Similarly, Tai et al. [22] evaluated TyG index trajectories in type 2 diabetes patients and found that higher TyG index trajectories were positively correlated with adverse cardiovascular event risk. Our multi-trajectory analysis not only revealed the importance of glucose and triglycerides but also emphasized the clinical significance of coordinated changes among multiple metabolic indicators. Particularly, the combination pattern of progressive glucose elevation with maintained high waist circumference or persistent lipid abnormalities was associated with high cardiovascular risk. This trajectory pattern likely reflects a state of chronic insulin resistance accompanied by visceral adiposity and atherogenic dyslipidemia, which together play a central role in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease. Progressive hyperglycemia is linked to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and low-grade systemic inflammation, all of which accelerate atherosclerotic plaque formation [23]. Meanwhile, sustained central obesity contributes to adipose tissue dysfunction and increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, further exacerbating metabolic and vascular injury. Persistent lipid abnormalities, particularly elevated triglycerides and reduced HDL-C levels, impair reverse cholesterol transport and promote lipid accumulation within the arterial wall [24]. The coexistence and long-term persistence of these metabolic disturbances may therefore exert synergistic effects on cardiovascular risk through shared pathophysiological pathways, including insulin resistance, inflammation, and endothelial damage. Zhou et al. [25] found through multi-trajectory analysis that high TyG index combined with other indicators showed substantial increases in CVD risk. The TG/HDL-C ratio has been recognized as a surrogate marker of atherogenic dyslipidemia and insulin resistance. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies demonstrated that an elevated TG/HDL-C ratio is significantly associated with increased risk of cardiovascular events in the general population, independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors [26]. Future studies may consider incorporating derived indices such as the TG/HDL-C ratio and the TyG index to build more targeted trajectory models.

This finding also underscores the challenge of cardiovascular risk assessment in elderly populations. Mazalin Protulipac et al. reported that older adults frequently lack adequate knowledge of CVD risk factors and significantly underestimate their personal cardiovascular risk [17]. The discrepancy between perceived and actual risk further supports the need for objective, data-driven longitudinal monitoring approaches, such as the multi-trajectory modeling employed in the present study, to identify high-risk metabolic patterns that may otherwise go unrecognized by older individuals themselves.

Additionally, we observed a negative correlation between waist circumference and HDL-C across different groups, with groups having lower waist circumference showing higher HDL-C levels and lower CVD risk. This is consistent with Toth et al. [27] who proposed that HDL-C and waist circumference show negative correlation and their changes are related to CVD progression. We also noted that blood pressure indicators did not differ as significantly between trajectory groups as expected. Except for Group 4, the other groups showed similar blood pressure values, and Groups 1 and 2 even showed blood pressure decline over time, possibly related to widespread antihypertensive drug use. Mancia et al. [28] also reported that quantifying overall cardiovascular risk through blood pressure variability requires consideration alongside other metabolic factors.

Traditional MetS definitions employ binary combinations of multiple metabolic abnormality indicators, which has the following main limitations: first, dichotomizing continuous variables leads to substantial information loss; second, it ignores the dynamic changes of metabolic indicators over time; finally, it fails to adequately reflect interactions between different indicators, i.e., the differential importance of various metabolic indicators cannot be determined. Using the GBMTM method not only preserves the continuous characteristics of each indicator but also captures their coordinated changes over time. This study applied multi-trajectory modeling methods to simultaneously analyze the longitudinal changes of six metabolic indicators. This approach allows identification of natural coordinated change patterns of metabolic indicators, dividing populations into potential subgroups rather than predetermined artificial combinations; preserves continuous variable information, avoiding information loss from dichotomization; captures dynamic changes of indicators over time rather than static cross-sections; and explores associations between different metabolic trajectory patterns and CVD risk.

Regarding big data processing, this study simultaneously attempted both multivariate latent class mixed models (MLCMM) and GBMTM methods. MLCMM considers random effects of individual observations [29, 30], theoretically offering more flexible modeling, but the assumption that all variables have the same random effects distribution does not conform to reality [31], and computational efficiency is low on large sample datasets. In contrast, GBMTM significantly improved computational efficiency while maintaining model adequacy, providing a feasible solution for trajectory analysis of large-scale health big data. This methodological comparison may inform the design of future similar studies, requiring careful consideration of the balance between model complexity and computational efficiency.

Study limitations include: First, although the study included large sample elderly population health examination data, participants were primarily from one region, potentially introducing selection bias and limiting external generalizability of results. Additionally, all participants in this study were aged 65 years or older, and the relative importance of certain metabolic risk factors may differ in this age group compared with younger populations. Therefore, the findings may not be directly extrapolable to younger populations. Second, due to the relatively short project initiation time, we could not construct a true CVD prediction model but rather explored the characteristics of different metabolic indicator multi-trajectory groupings and their potential associations with CVD. Future research should extend follow-up duration to construct more robust prediction models. Finally, although health examination data contains rich information, the exact timing of disease onset could not be determined. While interval-censored Cox models partially address the uncertainty in onset timing, long examination intervals (usually one year) may lead to imprecise event time estimation. If such information were available, traditional Cox models might provide more accurate estimates of risk.

Additionally, although data on antihypertensive and antidiabetic medication use were available and adjusted for in the analyses, information on lipid-lowering drug use (e.g., statins) was not collected in the health examination program. Given that lipid-lowering therapy can directly influence TG and HDL-C trajectories, the absence of this information may have introduced residual confounding. Furthermore, data on nutraceutical supplementation were not available. Evidence from systematic reviews has shown that certain nutraceuticals — such as flaxseed oil, which can reduce oxidative stress [32]; whey protein, which may improve glycemic control and lipid profiles [33]; and resistant starch, which has been associated with improved fasting glucose and cholesterol levels [34] — can influence metabolic syndrome components. The lack of such data represents an additional source of unmeasured confounding in the present study.

The findings of this study have implications for clinical practice and public health strategies. Longitudinal trajectory analysis of metabolic indicators provides a new tool for identifying high-risk populations, particularly for those with gradually rising glucose or persistent lipid metabolism disorders who should receive early intervention even before reaching traditional diagnostic criteria. At the public health level, multi-indicator, dynamic assessment strategies should be incorporated into health examination and chronic disease management systems for elderly populations to earlier identify adverse changes in metabolic health trajectories and implement timely interventions.

Conclusion

This study identified five distinct longitudinal evolutionary patterns of MetS indicators through multi-trajectory analysis and demonstrated that these trajectory patterns are significantly associated with CVD risk. In particular, the progressive hyperglycemia trajectory and the persistent dyslipidemia trajectory were associated with higher CVD risk, highlighting the importance of longitudinal trajectories of FPG, TG, and HDL-C. These findings underscore the value of longitudinal assessment methods for monitoring metabolic risk and may contribute to more precise cardiovascular disease prevention strategies in elderly populations.
