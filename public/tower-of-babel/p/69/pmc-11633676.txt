Exploration of epileptic networks in temporal lobe encephaloceles with stereotactic EEG: Electroclinical characteristics and surgical outcomes
Authors: Andrew J. Zillgitt, Eric R. Mong, Angelique M. Manasseh, Hannah C. Guider, Nour Baki, Michael D. Staudt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTemporal lobe encephaloceles (TLEN) have been implicated as a cause of temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE), the treatment which is primarily surgical; however, there is no clear consensus on the optimal surgical approach, because it is unclear whether TLE related to TLEN can be addressed by a restricted encephalocele resection or if a more extensive resection is required. The aim of the current article is to report the clinical and electrophysiological profile of patients with TLE secondary to TLEN who underwent stereotactic electroencephalography (SEEG) implantation to identify the epileptogenic network.MethodsA retrospective review was performed of patients with TLE related to TLEN who underwent SEEG implantation. Medical charts were reviewed for demographic data, the results of noninvasive and invasive investigations, and operative details. Surgical outcomes were based on Engel classification with at least 6 months follow‐up.ResultsNine patients were identified. The mean age at epilepsy onset was 28 years (range, 15–41 years), and 7/9 patients were female. Scalp EEG revealed interictal epileptiform activity most often maximum in the frontotemporal and/or temporal regions. A discrete TLEN was often not identified on initial imaging, but was identified during re‐review or at the time of surgery. Seizure onset zones during SEEG were localized to the mesial temporal structures, the temporal pole, or both. One patient became seizure‐free following SEEG and another refused further surgery. Of the 7 patients who underwent epilepsy surgery, 5/7 underwent an anterior temporal lobectomy—surgical outcomes were favorable, with 5/7 achieving Engel I outcomes.SignificanceInvasive SEEG monitoring demonstrated ictal onsets may not be restricted to the TLEN, and often the temporal pole and mesial structures are involved at seizure onset. Ictal propagation patterns vary significantly, which may be related to the underlying pathology and explain the variability in semiology. These findings may inform surgical treatment options.Plain language summaryTemporal lobe encephaloceles can cause intractable epilepsy, although their presence may be missed on routine imaging. The management of encephaloceles is primarily surgical; however, the optimal surgical approach can be unclear. Invasive monitoring with SEEG may help characterize the epileptogenic network and result in more optimal surgical outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Temporal lobe encephaloceles (TLEN) are distinct pathological entities that have been implicated as a potential cause of temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE). 1 , 2 , 3 TLEN, which are contained defects or herniations of non‐functional brain parenchyma into congenital or acquired defects in the base of the middle cranial fossa, historically have had an underestimated prevalence in the population of epilepsy patients. 1 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 They are often overlooked on imaging studies and are discovered either after reviewing subtle imaging findings or discovered at the time of surgery. 1 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 The proposed epileptogenicity of TLENs has been attributed to irritation, traction, or scarring of the local brain parenchyma. 14 TLEN are often identified concurrently with other developmental abnormalities including focal cortical dysplasia (FCD), hippocampal sclerosis, and nodular heterotopia, 8 , 9 , 13 and are typically surrounded by gliotic tissue. 15 Idiopathic intracranial hypertension has also been associated with TLEN, although the pathophysiological mechanism has not been fully elucidated. 16 , 17 , 18

The treatment for medically refractory TLE in patients with TLEN is primarily surgical; however, there is no clear consensus on the optimal surgical approach. 9 , 19 , 20 It is unclear whether TLEN is part of a single epileptogenic focus that can be removed by restricted resection of the encephalocele and surrounding gliotic brain, or if it is part of a more extensive epileptogenic network requiring a more extensive resection. 8 , 9 , 21 The osseous defects are sometimes repaired to prevent recurrence. 9 , 22

Several studies have demonstrated good seizure (i.e. Engel class I or II) and neuropsychological outcomes with restricted lesionectomy resection of the surrounding gliotic brain, effectively treating the pathology like lesional epilepsy. 1 , 9 , 11 , 21 Several studies have demonstrated non‐inferior seizure outcomes and superior complication profiles compared to anterior temporal lobectomy (ATL). 20 , 22 Advocates of a more extensive resection argue the association of TLEN with malformations of cortical development that are often missed on imaging, suggesting that focused lesionectomy would not always be curative. 9 Some advocate for an extensive presurgical evaluation including stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG) implantation to identify the full epileptogenic network to allow for a more tailored resection. 23

Although several studies have supported the epileptogenicity of TLEN, there is limited literature describing the role and characterization of intracranial EEG monitoring. In particular, the extent of the epileptogenic zone (EZ) in relation to the epileptic lesion is not entirely understood in TLE related to TLEN. The aim of this study was to comprehensively characterize the clinical, electrophysiological and surgical profile of patients with TLE secondary to TLEN.

METHODS

This study is a retrospective review of the patient database from the Corewell Health William Beaumont University Hospital Adult Comprehensive Epilepsy Center from January 2019 to December 2023. Patients with TLE secondary to TLEN were identified. The electronic medical record was reviewed to document epidemiological, diagnostic, procedural details and outcomes. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB 2023–166). This case series has been reported in line with the PROCESS Guideline. 24

Patient demographics included age, age of epilepsy onset, body mass index (BMI), and history of headaches. Clinical characteristics included anti‐seizure medication (ASM) use, seizure semiology, seizure frequency, and surgical management, if relevant. Seizure outcome was reported based on the Engel classification.

All patients underwent MRI (1.5 or 3 T) of the brain as per the institutional epilepsy protocol. CT sinus scans to evaluate bony defects were not routinely obtained, but have now become part of the institutional protocol when clinical characteristics are suggestive of TLEN. Imaging was reviewed and reported by a neuroradiologist. Images underwent a secondary review in a comprehensive epilepsy patient management conference by neurologists and neurosurgeons. Fludeoxyglucose‐18 positron emission tomography (FDG‐PET) scans were obtained in all patients. Further advanced imaging including subtraction ictal SPECT co‐registered to MRI (SISCOM) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) were obtained in some patients.

All patients were admitted to the epilepsy monitoring unit (EMU) for video and scalp EEG monitoring. The EEG during the EMU admissions were reviewed by an epileptologist using Nihon Kohden. Scalp EEG was completed with a 32‐channel digital scalp EEG recording with time‐locked video and single‐channel electrocardiogram. Electrodes were placed according to the 10–20 International System, with added anterior temporal (F9, F10) and electrooculogram (EOG) electrodes. Automated spike and seizure detection were also performed. The sampling rate was 250 Hz, and scalp EEG data was typically reviewed with a bandpass of 0.16–70 Hz. With SEEG, Intracranial depth electrodes were placed in the operating room using robotic stereotactic assistance. EEG data was then collected at a sampling rate of 1000–2000 Hz and typically reviewed with a bandpass ranging from 0.08 to 600 Hz. Time‐locked video was also recorded and reviewed during SEEG monitoring. Visual inspection of interictal and ictal waveforms as well as ictal semiology was completed and then re‐reviewed during a comprehensive epilepsy patient management conference with other neurologists, epileptologists, and neurosurgeons.

All patients were presented at a comprehensive epilepsy patient management conference, and selection of SEEG targets and trajectories was chosen by expert consensus. The SEEG and ictal semiology were reviewed by an epileptologist trained in surgical epilepsy (author, AJZ) and surgical approaches were dictated by the results of SEEG investigations and in discussion with patients.

In this series, 7/9 patients were female with an average age at epilepsy onset of 28 years (range, 15–41 years old). At initial presentation, the BMI was >30 kg/m2 in 6/9 patients (range 21.47–38.49). In addition, 8/9 patients reported a history of headache. For all patients, the described semiology was most consistent with temporal lobe seizures (see Table 2). For example, patient 3's seizures began with “a wave in the room, and everything slows down”, followed by a “flutter” sensation in her abdomen. She will then lose awareness, become unresponsive, and exhibit oral and manual automatisms. The average number of ASM trials prior to epilepsy surgery was 3, with a range of 0–6 ASM trials. At the time of first presentation, the patients were averaging 4–5 seizures per month with a median seizure frequency of 2 seizures per month.

Imaging characteristics

A discrete TLEN was not identified on initial brain MRI in 8/9 cases. On imaging re‐review, a TLEN was identified in nearly all cases. In patients 5 and 6, where a definitive TLEN was not confirmed on neuroimaging re‐review, a TLEN was identified during surgery. A CT scan of the sinuses was only obtained in a minority of patients; however, in patients with typical clinical features of TLE and TLEN, CT scans of the sinuses are now routinely obtained to assess for bony skull defects (Figure 1).

Concordant hypometabolism in the temporal lobe on FDG‐PET was identified in most cases (7/9). Discordant FDG‐PET findings were present in patient 8, and bilateral temporal hypometabolism was present in patient 4. Three patients underwent SISCOM, and a total of 8 patients completed MEG. SISCOM was discordant in 1 patient, while MEG was concordant with scalp EEG in 5/8 cases. In 3 cases, no interictal epileptiform activity was identified on MEG.

Invasive monitoring and surgical outcomes

All patients underwent SEEG implantation, with coverage of the temporal lobe, insula, orbitofrontal region, cingulate cortex, and other areas in the frontal lobe (Table 4). Irritative zones (IZ) were most consistently localized in the temporal lobe, including the temporal pole and mesial temporal structures. IZs were also frequently present in the insula, orbitofrontal region, and cingulate cortex. Bilateral IZs were present in two patients (4 and 7). Seizure onset zones (SOZ) were isolated to the mesial temporal structures in 4 cases, the temporal pole in 1 patient, and both temporal pole and mesial temporal structures in 4/9 patients. In patient 2, their habitual seizures emanated from the left mesial temporal structures, but subclinical and atypical seizures arose from the left insula and left superior temporal gyrus, respectively.

One patient became seizure‐free following SEEG implantation (patient 5) and another refused epilepsy surgery (patient 3)—this patient has continued to experience frequent seizures with 2–3 focal impaired awareness seizures per month. Of the 7 patients who underwent epilepsy surgery, 5/7 underwent an ATL. One patient elected for laser interstitial thermal therapy, while another underwent a focal resection of the temporal pole, sparing the mesial temporal structures (patient 7). In these latter two cases, more selective surgeries were pursued in the patient's dominant hemisphere. Surgical outcomes were favorable in almost all patients with most patients (5/7) achieving Engel I outcomes (Table 4).

Pathology was obtained in all patients who underwent open surgical resection (6/9). The most common histopathologic finding was FCD Type I (4/6). Gliosis was present in 2 patients. Definitive hippocampal sclerosis was not identified in any of the patients who underwent an ATL. No patients sustained an intraoperative complication or were identified to have a long‐term complication in follow‐up.

DISCUSSION

This case series illustrates, and reinforces, several common clinical characteristics that may be present in patients with TLE related to TLEN. Specifically, similar to previous case series and case reports, the patients in this series often had an epilepsy onset after the second or third decade of life (average age of onset 28 years old), BMI >30 kg/m2, and a semiology primarily consistent with temporal lobe seizures. 12 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 Although not reported in other case series, a history of headache was also present in most patients in this case series (8/9 patients). Headaches are frequent in people with epilepsy 29 ; however, the constellation of clinical features including, age of onset in the second or third decade, elevated BMI, headaches, and temporal lobe seizure semiology with a “negative brain MRI,” should raise the suspicion for TLEN. Some of these clinical features may also be present in people with idiopathic intracranial hypertension. However, this was not formally diagnosed in the patients in this study.

Identifying encephaloceles

TLE is the most common type of drug‐resistant focal epilepsy. Hippocampal sclerosis is the most common pathologic substrate contributing to drug‐resistant TLE; however, case series have estimated TLEN are present in 2%–13% of drug resistant TLE cases. 23 , 25 , 30 Interestingly, in a case series from Penfield published in 1953, 100/157 patients who underwent temporal lobe surgery exhibited temporal lobe herniation. 26 The high number of patients with temporal lobe herniation in this case series is not entirely clear. The authors noted, TLE onset “…may be delayed until the second or third decade of life…” but the most common cause of herniation was “…found to be in the mechanic of birth compression.” 26 Presumably, in patients with temporal lobe herniation due to mechanic compression at birth, the onset of epilepsy would occur earlier in life. However, the observation of the delayed onset of TLE into the second or third decade of life has been a common clinical characteristic identified in this cases series as well as several other published case reports and case series. 1 , 2 , 14 , 15 , 25 , 30 , 31

An elevated BMI has been reported in patients with TLEN in this case series as well as others. 28 However, definitive conclusions between the role of BMI and temporal lobe herniation cannot be made with the small sample size in this study.

Despite this relatively common pathology, initial brain MRI scans often “miss” subtle TLEN. 25 For example, in one study only 1.7% of TLEN were identified on MRI, while occult TLEN were present on the initial brain MRI in 5% of cases. 25 , 30 With subsequent brain MRI, or imaging re‐review, up to 13% of cases reviewed revealed TLEN. 25 It has been suggested that some cases may be missed as the adjacent brain is frequently isointense with gray matter, and furthermore the low magnetic signal of bone makes skull base abnormalities challenging to identify on MRI. 12 In the series presented here, TLEN was not initially identified in 8/9 patients.

Although a high‐resolution brain MRI, preferably on 3 T or 7 T magnets, are paramount in epilepsy evaluations, in cases where MRI findings remain ambiguous, CT scans of the sinuses can identify bony defects and TLEN. 32 The utility of CT scans in the evaluation of TLE related to TLEN has been well‐established since CT scanners became more widely available in the 1980s. 5 , 32 , 33 In fact, when the clinical features of TLE related to TLEN are present, our institutional protocol has changed to reorder a brain MRI with additional sequences, e.g., T2 sampling perfection with application‐optimized contrasts by using flip angle evolution (SPACE) and obtain a CT scan of the sinuses to assess for bony skull defects. This approach has allowed for improved identification of TLEN (Figure 1).

The role of invasive monitoring and epilepsy surgery

In patients with drug resistant TLE related to TLEN, epilepsy surgery often results in favorable outcomes, e.g., Engel class I or II. 5 , 20 , 21 , 23 , 27 , 32 , 34 For example, in one case series of 13 patients with TLE related to TLEN, all 13 were free of disabling seizures after epilepsy surgery. 28 In another case series of TLE cases related to TLEN published in 2023, 27/34 patients (79%) achieved an Engel I/II outcome after epilepsy surgery. 20

Similar surgical outcomes have been published in patients who have undergone intracranial EEG monitoring. For example, in one case series of 9 patients with TLE related to TLEN, 6 underwent intracranial EEG monitoring and subsequent resective surgery. 21 Of the 6 patients who underwent resective surgery, all had Engel class I/II outcomes. 21 A case report of patient who underwent SEEG exploration of TLE related to TLEN reported an Engel class I outcome at 12‐month follow‐up. 23

However, the optimal type of surgery is not entirely clear. In most of the case series reported in the literature, lesionectomy, ATL, or a selective temporal lobectomy were completed, often based on individual characteristics, e.g., dominant vs. non‐dominant temporal lobe involvement. 20 , 21 , 27 , 28 Moreover, the extent of the EZ in relation to the epileptic lesion is not entirely understood in TLE related to TLEN.

Frequently, the EZ often extends beyond the lesion. 35 , 36 This appears particularly true in patients with TLE related to TLEN. In a recent study evaluating TLEN in TLE with scalp EEG source localization, the EEG source localization was near the encephalocele but not within the encephalocele specifically. 8 EEG source localization was not completed in the current series, but magnetic source imaging (MSI) was obtained in 8/9 patients. Three patients had normal simultaneous EEG‐MEG, but in patients were MSI was completed, source localization often extended beyond the region of the TE.

Non‐invasive diagnostic studies, such as FDG‐PET or scalp EEG source localization may overestimate the extent of the EZ; however, like non‐invasive diagnostic studies in TLE related to TLEN, similar results have also been illustrated in patients who underwent intracranial EEG monitoring. 12 , 20 , 21 , 23 For example, one case series presented 9 patients who underwent chronic or intraoperative electrocorticography. Two patients underwent intracranial monitoring that explored the mesial temporal structures. Ictal onsets were simultaneous at the TLEN and hippocampus in one patient, whereas seizures emanated from the TLEN region and rapidly propagated to the hippocampal depth electrodes in another. 21 Both underwent an ATL with amygdalohippocampectomy and both achieved Engel Ia outcomes; however, few studies have consistently explored TLEN with invasive monitoring, and even fewer with SEEG.

SEEG offers several advantages over subdural grids and strips when exploring seizures that may involve deep cortical structures, such as the hippocampus. 37 In 3 studies, a combined 7 patients underwent SEEG implantation. 20 , 21 , 23 In 5 patients, the SOZ was in the temporal pole with late, or no, propagation to the hippocampus. 21 , 23 In a larger case series of 34 patients, 2 underwent SEEG implantation—one patient's seizures arose from the hippocampus without involvement of the electrodes nearest the TLEN. The other patient's seizures emanated from the electrode adjacent to the TLEN. The latter patient underwent a limited resection and achieved an Engel class Ib outcome, while the former underwent an ATL with amygdalohippocampectomy and had an Engel Ia outcome. 20

In the current series, 9 patients with TLE related to TLEN underwent SEEG implantation. The results from these 9 patients were similar to previously published series. Specifically, SEEG identified SOZ isolated to the mesial temporal structures in 4/9 cases and nearly simultaneously ictal onsets in the temporal pole and mesial temporal structures in 4/9 patients. Only 1 patient had a SOZ isolated to the temporal pole. Interestingly, 1 patient with habitual seizures arising from the mesial temporal lobe also had subclinical and atypical seizures that emanated from the left insula and left superior temporal gyrus, respectively (patient 2). These findings, in conjunction with previous published studies, support the EZ in people with TLE related to TLEN extends beyond the encephalocele and may involve adjacent structures, e.g., amygdala and hippocampus, but more distal brain regions, including the insula and lateral temporal neocortex. Delineating the epileptogenic zone

Exploring the EZ with SEEG in TLE related to TLEN allows for more complete understanding of the EZ. The temporal pole has widespread functional and anatomical connectivity that involve long association fibers connecting the temporal pole with the basal occipital network, orbitofrontal region, and lateral temporal neocortex through the inferior longitudinal fasciculus, uncinate fasciculus, and superior longitudinal fasciculus, respectively. 38 Short association fibers provide connectivity between the temporal pole and hippocampus as well as the cingulate cortex. 38 This connectivity may explain why SOZ and EZ are not only restricted to the temporal pole and region of the TLEN but could also explain the variability seen in the seizure semiology. 2

For example, with patient 7, ictal onsets were localized to the temporal pole but very rapidly propagated to the frontal operculum, insula, and mesial temporal structures (Figure 3). This patient's semiology varied from “more typical” temporal lobe seizures—specifically, their seizures began without warning and quickly progressed to sialorrhea, impaired awareness, bimanual automatisms, and back‐and‐forth rocking of his body. Conversely, with patient 3, seizures begin with “wave in the room, and everything slows down” that is followed by a “flutter” sensation in her abdomen. She will then lose awareness, become unresponsive, and exhibit oral and manual automatisms. In patient 7, SEEG revealed rapid propagation from the temporal pole to the frontal operculum and insula, while in patient 3, SEEG ictal onsets began within the left hippocampus and propagated through mesial temporal structures and then the temporal neocortex. In these 2 cases, SEEG investigations illustrated the variable connectivity in the temporal and allowed for complete delineation of the symptomatogenic zone and EZ (Figure 2).

Choosing an approach and prognostic factors

Identifying involvement of the temporal pole in drug‐resistant TLE cases may inform surgical outcome prognosis. 39 Involvement of the temporal pole at onset of seizures may be a positive predictor for surgical outcomes. In this series, patients with temporal pole involvement at ictal onset often had more favorable surgical outcomes. For example, patients 4, 7, and 8 all had temporal pole involvement at ictal onset, and all achieved Engel I outcome.

There remains doubt regarding the surgical approach and extent of resection in TLE associated with TLEN. 20 In the current series, most patients underwent a standard ATL with amygdalohippocampectomy. Although the overall success of any type of surgery in patients with TLE related to TLEN is high, both non‐invasive and several invasive studies have illustrated the EZ in TLE related to TLEN extends beyond the lesion. As such, exploration with invasive monitoring, and specifically SEEG, may provide a more refined approach to resection. 20 , 21 , 23

For example, in patients 4, 7, and 8 where the temporal pole was involved at the seizure onset, resective surgery approaches varied. Patients 4 and 8 underwent a right ATL, while patient 7's resection was limited to the left temporal pole, sparing the mesial temporal structures. This patient (7) has since self‐discontinued his ASMs and has remained seizure‐free without adverse neuropsychiatric sequelae. In these cases, SEEG allowed for delineation of the epileptic network and informed the surgical approach, resulting in optimal (Engel I) outcomes.

In addition, histopathology in TLE related to TLEN varies significantly. 1 , 2 , 3 , 9 , 12 , 20 , 21 , 23 , 27 , 31 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 In a recent case series, 24/34 patients with TLE related to TLEN, gliosis was the identified pathologic substrate, while in other case series pathology varied from FCD to hippocampal sclerosis to benign neuroepithelial tumors. 20 , 23 , 28 Similar to other series, gliosis and FCD were the most common pathological findings in the current series. Overt hippocampal sclerosis was not identified, and in previous hippocampal sclerosis was not a common histopathology finding. Overall, these pathologic findings, such as FCD, may indicate a more widespread EZ. Taken together, the widespread functional and anatomical connectivity of the TP and diffuse pathology, provide further support in exploring the EZ in TLE related to TLEN with intracranial EEG and SEEG.

Limitations

This study has several limitations, primarily due to the retrospective nature of data collection and the small size of the patient cohort. Although the patient population and treatments were heterogeneous, this highlights the individualized nature of the workup and potential utility of SEEG for informing a surgical treatment strategy. Although there is a potential relationship between idiopathic intracranial hypertension and encephaloceles, this study has limited data on the potential diagnostic criteria for this pathology. Furthermore, beyond a clinical history of headaches, formal classification as per the International Classification of Headache Disorders was not defined.

CONCLUSIONS

This case series highlights several common clinical characteristics that are present in people with drug resistant TLE related to TLEN. Specifically, the onset of epilepsy is after the second or third decade of life, BMI >30 kg/m2, and a semiology most consistent with temporal lobe seizures. In most cases, the TLEN was identified on the initial brain MRI, and imaging re‐review is imperative in patients with these clinical features because subtle TLEN are often recognized. In many cases, the identification of the TLEN can be augmented by obtaining a CT scan of the sinuses.

In addition to reaffirming common clinical characteristics in TLE related to TLEN, this case series also explored the EZ with SEEG. Invasive EEG monitoring with SEEG demonstrated several important points—first, SEEG is an advantageous diagnostic modality when investigating an EZ that may involve deep regions of the brain. Second, ictal onsets are not simply restricted to the TLEN, and often the temporal pole and mesial temporal structures are involved at seizure onset. Finally, ictal propagation patterns vary significantly, which may be related to the underlying pathology, e.g., gliosis v FCD, and explain the variability in the seizure semiology. These findings may inform surgical treatment options and produce more optimal surgical outcomes.
