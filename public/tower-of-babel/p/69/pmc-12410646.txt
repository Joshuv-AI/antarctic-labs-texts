Association of C-reactive protein with cause, duration and severity of lower respiratory infections in primary care: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Mark Ebell, Dan J Merenstein, Bruce Barrett, Michelle Buhr, Cassie Hulme, Caroline Hamer, Sarah Walters, Alea Sabry, Shari Barlow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe association of C-reactive protein (CRP) with cough duration and severity has not been evaluated in a contemporary US population of patients with acute lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI).MethodsWe identified outpatients with less than 2 weeks of cough plus at least one lower respiratory or systemic symptom. The duration of illness, demographics, signs, symptoms, CRP and PCR for 46 pathogens were assessed. Duration and severity of cough were tracked using a paper diary plus responses to text messages. We classified CRP as low (<5 mg/L), moderate (5–19 mg/L) and high (≥20 mg/L).ResultsA total of 575 patients had valid CRP data and 503 had both valid CRP and PCR data. Patients with a CRP ≥20 mg/L were more likely (p<0.001) to have any bacterial detection (34.4%) or a mixed detection (37.7%) than viral infection (19.7%) or no detection (7.2%). Patients with elevated CRP were also more likely to have a likely bacterial pathogen detected (61.2% vs 37.8%, p<0.001) and were more likely to have a clinician label their illness as moderate or severe (53.7% vs 26.0%, p<0.001). Patients with a CRP ≥20 mg/L had a longer duration of illness than those with a lower CRP (18.5 vs 16.1 days, p=0.026) as well as a greater overall severity of cough (29.9 vs 23.0 points, p=0.001). In multivariable analysis, CRP ≥20 mg/L was the strongest independent predictor of a likely bacterial pathogen (adjusted OR 3.21, 95% CI 1.61 to 6.40).DiscussionCRP ≥20 mg/L has a strong, independent association with the presence of bacterial pathogen in patients with acute LRTI and predicts longer duration and severity of illness. Further research is warranted to understand the impact of CRP on antibiotic prescribing and patient outcomes.

Introduction

C-reactive protein (CRP) is an inflammatory biomarker that in previous studies was found to be an independent predictor of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)13 and acute bacterial rhinosinusitis in primary care patients.4 Adding it to the clinical examination has been shown to improve discrimination and diagnostic accuracy for CAP.3

Antibiotic stewardship is an important goal for primary care physicians due to overuse of antibiotics for largely viral respiratory tract infections. Several randomised trials in Europe have shown that using CRP can reduce antibiotic prescribing without resulting in patient harm.57 Furthermore, a Cochrane systematic review concluded that the use of CRP significantly reduced the likelihood of receiving an antibiotic prescription from 51.6% to 39.7% (number needed to treat=8).8

In surveys, US physicians have indicated a desire to use CRP at the point of care.9 10 However, its use is not allowed in basic physician office laboratories by the US Food and Drug Administration. In part, that is because to date its use has not been evaluated in a contemporary US primary care population. The goal of the current study was to examine the association of CRP with the type of pathogen, the duration and severity of illness and the association with specific symptoms and clinical assessments in outpatients with acute lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI). We hypothesised that higher levels of CRP are associated with a greater duration of cough, severity of cough and need for unscheduled follow-up visits.

Methods

Overview

The Enhancing Antibiotic Stewardship in Primary Care (EAST-PC) study was a prospective cohort study in primary and urgent care settings of adults with acute LRTI. The goal was to describe the epidemiology of acute LRTI in primary and urgent care patients in the USA, including the association between clinical variables and the duration, severity and microbiological cause of these infections. Main results have been reported elsewhere.11 12

Participants

Adults aged 18–75 years were identified at primary or urgent care clinics in Washington, DC and northern Virginia, Madison, Wisconsin, and Athens, Georgia. All participants reported a cough for no more than 14 days plus at least one of the following systemic or lower respiratory symptoms: measured fever or feverishness, shortness of breath, sputum, muscle aches, chest discomfort or congestion, and chills or sweats. Patients who had taken an antibiotic or antiviral in the previous month, those with serious immunodeficiency, those currently on cancer chemotherapy or those taking systemic steroids or immunosuppressive drugs were excluded. Patients with chronic lung disease other than mild to moderate asthma were also excluded. The study was originally designed to recruit 1400 patients based on estimates of the precision needed for a split sample validation of clinical risk scores. However, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, we recruited 718 symptomatic adults and added an arm (reported separately) of 500 asymptomatic adults, all of whom underwent PCR testing.

Patient and public involvement

This study was an investigator-initiated project and did not involve patient participation in the design, analysis or reporting.

Data collection

Data collection for EAST-PC took place from June 2019 until April 2023. The COVID-19 pandemic slowed and then halted data collection due to a lack of protective equipment and changes in outpatient clinic procedures. Data collection was resumed in October 2020 and returned to normal operations in January 2022.

Data collection at the index visit

At baseline, after obtaining written informed consent, we recorded patient demographics, comorbidities, the severity of respiratory symptoms, medications and any tests or referrals that were ordered. Radiographs were ordered at the discretion of the treating physician in 95 patients.12 Pneumonia was diagnosed clinically or radiographically in 27 of 575 patients with a valid CRP result (4.7%). Participants had a nasal and a pharyngeal swab, which were combined and stored in a viral transport medium at −80 C. The samples were analysed by the Pneumonia Response and Surveillance Laboratory at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

CRP was measured using the point of care QuikRead go CRP device (Orion Diagnostica, now Aidian). The blood sample was obtained via fingerstick using a 20 µL capillary tube and analysed in about 4 min. The measurement range was 5–150 mg/L, with measurements out of range reported as <5 or >150 mg/L. If the sample was insufficient or there was a device error, an ‘invalid’ result was reported. CRP results were for research purposes and were not shared with the treating clinicians and thus did not influence treatment decisions. Categorical analysis of CRP data was chosen since it is more useful and clinically interpretable in the context of patient care decisions. Definitions of low (<5 mg/L), moderate (5–19 mg/L) and high (≥20 mg/L) levels of CRP were established a priori based on previous research.13

Determining the duration and severity of cough

Patients were asked about the duration of their cough prior to the index visit. The severity of the cough was recorded at baseline, on days 3, 7, 14, 21 and 28 via text message and in daily paper diaries. The diary value was used if the text message and paper diary values were different. If needed, we used interpolation to estimate severity and duration. This is described in detail in a previous publication.11

The severity of acute cough at baseline was measured using the Bronchitis Severity Score,14 which assigns 0–3 points each for severity of cough, sputum, chest pain with coughing, dyspnoea and impact on activities (range 0–15 points). The overall severity of cough was estimated using diary data, assigning 1 point for mild cough, 2 points for moderately severe cough, 3 points for severe cough and 4 points for very severe cough. The severity was summed across the entire duration of the cough illness (‘area under the symptom curve’). For example, 1 day of severe cough (1×3 points), 4 days of moderate cough (2×4 points) and 3 days of mild cough (1×3 points) would have an overall severity of 3+8+3 = 14 points.

Analysis

All analyses were performed using Stata V.18.5 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). Categorical variables with three groups (<5 mg/L, 5–19 mg/L and ≥20 mg/L) or two groups (<20 mg/L versus ≥20 mg/L) were compared using a χ2 test. Continuous variables were compared using a t-test if two groups or ANOVA if three groups. Multivariable analysis of complete cases used stepwise backwards logistic regression with p<0.1 for retention in the model.

Results

Participant characteristics

The characteristics of the participants are summarised in table 1. The mean age was 38.6 years with a range of 18–74 years; 65.2% self-identified as female. Of 718 total participants in the study, 618 had a valid PCR result, 575 had a valid CRP result, and 503 had both valid CRP and valid PCR results for pathogen detection. With regard to race, 24% were non-white, and the sample was skewed towards higher educational attainment and income. Demographics for each subgroup (all participants, valid CRP, valid PCR and both valid CRP and PCR) are shown in online supplemental appendix table 2. Demographics were similar across these subgroups.

Participants with a CRP ≥20 mg/L were significantly more likely than those with a lower CRP to have a clinician label their illness as moderate or severe (53.7% vs 26.0%, p<0.001). Participants with a CRP ≥20 mg/L were also numerically more likely to be prescribed an antibiotic at the index visit, but this difference was not statistically significant. Patients with a CRP ≥20 mg/L were significantly more likely to be clinically or radiographically diagnosed with pneumonia (9.0% vs 4.1%, p<0.001). There was no association between CRP and the likelihood of having an unscheduled follow-up visit in the 28 days following diagnosis. Participants with CRP ≥20 mg/L had a longer duration of illness than those with a lower CRP (18.5 vs 16.1 days, p=0.026) as well as a greater overall severity of cough (29.9 vs 23.0 points, p=0.001).

Association with individual symptoms

Table 3 summarises the association between individual signs and symptoms and the mean CRP. The mean CRP was significantly higher in patients with moderate to severe fever (13.9% vs 7.9%), chills or sweats (13.6% vs 8.0%), wheeze (12.2% vs 8.6%), myalgia (11.8% vs 8.2%), shortness of breath (11.8% vs 9.0%), fatigue (11.0% vs 6.7%) and cough (10.3% vs 7.5%). It was also higher in those reporting being more unwell (10.7% vs 6.4%) and having more interference with usual activities (11.4% vs 6.1%). Rhonchi (16.6% vs 9.1%), but not crackles, and a pulse oximetry <97% (14.0% vs 9.0%) were both also associated with a higher mean CRP.

Multivariable analysis

We performed a multivariable analysis with detection of a likely bacterial pathogen (Bordetella pertussis, Chlamydophila pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, group A streptococcus, or Streptococcus pneumoniae) as the dependent variable and adjusting for 19 symptoms, vital signs, oxygen saturation, physician assessment and lung exam findings. The results are shown in table 5. The adjusted OR for CRP <5 mg/L was 1.0 (referent), for CRP 5–19 mg/L was 0.617 (95% CI 0.41 to 0.93) and for CRP ≥20 was 3.21 (95% CI 1.61 to 6.40). CRP ≥20 mg/L was the single strongest independent predictor of a likely bacterial pathogen. The full model is shown in online supplemental appendix table 1; the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for the multivariable model was 0.72.

Discussion

In US primary and urgent care patients with an acute lower respiratory infection, an elevated CRP (≥20 mg/L) was associated with greater illness severity at baseline (8.3 vs 7.0 points on a 15-point scale) as well as greater overall severity of cough over the duration of the illness (18.5 vs 16.1 days). While these differences are modest, a difference of 2.4 days in the duration of illness would seem to be clinically significant.

An elevated CRP was also significantly more likely in bacterial (20.2%) and mixed infections (15.6%) than in viral infections (6.9%), and patients with a CRP ≥20 mg/L were more than twice as likely to have been diagnosed with pneumonia. Patients with an elevated CRP were twice as likely to be judged as moderately or severely ill by their physician (53.7% vs 26.0%, p<0.001).

Detection of one of seven likely bacterial pathogens was much more common in patients with CRP ≥20 mg/L (61.2% vs 37.8%, p<0.001). In the multivariable analysis, CRP ≥20 mg/L was the single strongest independent predictor of likely bacterial pathogen. While CRP was not more likely to be elevated in patients with clinically diagnosed pneumonia, this may have been due to the relatively small number of cases of pneumonia in our sample (26 out of 575 participants). We would argue that these results, coming from multiple geographically and ethnically diverse sites in the USA, are generalisable to contemporary primary care practice.

Our findings are consistent with those of other researchers, especially in European primary care. They found that elevated CRP was an independent predictor of radiographically confirmed community-acquired pneumonia2 3 and bacterial sinus infection.15 Researchers also concluded based on a systematic review that the use of CRP in primary care could safely reduce inappropriate prescribing of antibiotics.8 However, these studies have largely been in European populations, and FDA regulators may wish to have US data such as our own on which to base decisions regarding the use of point-of-care CRP.

Previous studies have developed simple risk scores that incorporate CRP to determine the likelihood of community-acquired pneumonia2 and acute bacterial rhinosinusitis.15 These risk scores require further research to validate them in contemporary primary care populations and to assess their impact on antibiotic prescribing and patient outcomes.

Limitations

Our study had some limitations. We did not have complete follow-up data on all participants with a valid CRP test due to the failure of some participants to adhere to recording the duration and severity of their symptoms. Also, since we were not allowed by the US Food and Drug Administration to share the point of care CRP results with clinicians, we were unable to assess the impact of CRP on antibiotic prescribing. Our population excluded patients with exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease at the recommendation of the human subjects committee. Finally, only 13% of patients received a chest radiograph at the discretion of their treating physician, with most patients diagnosed with pneumonia clinically.

Strengths

This was a contemporary, representative sample from three different US states and the District of Columbia, with extensive follow-up for up to 1 month and extensive microbiologic data. To our knowledge, it is the first such study in the primary or urgent care setting in the USA. Our prevalence of pneumonia was very similar to that of the large European GRACE study that did obtain radiographs on all patients with acute LRTI.2 In a pragmatic sense, this also mirrors real world primary care practice.

Conclusions

We identified CRP ≥20 mg/L as the strongest independent predictor of a likely bacterial infection in outpatients with LRTI. Further studies should assess the impact of CRP on antibiotic prescribing, patient outcomes and cost. Since CRP can safely reduce inappropriate antibiotic prescribing,8 and we have confirmed the association of CRP with more severe illness in a US population, we also suggest that the US Food and Drug Administration consider revising their guidance to be more in line with European agencies and allow point-of-care devices to be used in primary care settings as a ‘CLIA waived’ device since they are simple devices that produce reliable results.
