Combining Languages in Bilingual Input: Using Experimental Evidence to Formulate Bilingual Exposure Strategies
Authors: Margarita Kaushanskaya
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Purpose:Traditional approaches to studying bilingual language development through bilingual–monolingual comparisons are deeply flawed. They are also insufficient as the evidence base for informing advice to bilingual parents regarding the optimal bilingual exposure strategy and for supporting the formulation of bilingual intervention approaches. The purpose of this review article is to provide an overview of empirical studies that have queried the question of how different types of dual-language input shape learning and language outcomes in bilingual children.Method:We rely on tightly controlled experimental studies of word learning in Spanish–English bilingual children, where we contrast children's learning in dual-language conditions (defined as distributed exposure and code-switched input) to a single-language condition in a within-subjects design.Results:Word-learning studies querying the role of distributed exposure indicated that distribution of exposures across Spanish and English reduced children's performance in comparison to English-only exposure. However, this effect was rooted in the abrupt switch from Spanish to English rather than distributed exposure itself. In contrast, an experiment designed to test the role of code-switched context on children's word learning revealed that code-switched context where switches resembled naturalistic code-switching behaviors enhanced learning in Spanish–English bilingual children. Notably, across different studies, children with weaker language skills (developmental language disorder) were no more affected by dual-language input than children with typical language skills.Conclusions:Together, experimental studies of word learning indicate that bilingual children can effectively learn from dual-language input but that different ways of combining languages in the input to bilingual children can have distinct consequences for learning. Ultimately, word-learning experiments, beyond answering critical questions regarding bilingual learning, can serve as an effective bridge between laboratory-based work and intervention studies whose goal it is to discover the optimal way of combining languages in the input to bilingual children with communication impairments.Presentation Video: https://doi.org/10.23641/asha.23929515

Historically, parents of bilingual children with neurodevelopmental disabilities, including developmental language disorder (DLD), have been advised to limit the child's exposure in the home to a single language (Genesee et al., 2004; Kohnert et al., 2005). These recommendations followed the intuition that input in two languages must be especially challenging for children who have difficulties learning language. In contrast, at present, maintenance of the home language and integration of the two languages in treatment are encouraged as best clinical practices (Kohnert, 2008, 2010; Kohnert & Derr, 2004; Peña & Bedore, 2009; Restrepo, 2005). However, in truth, there is little empirical work that tests how children with different levels of language ability accommodate to different ways of combining the two languages in the input. Further, the evidence for the effectiveness of bilingual intervention is not at all straightforward (Ebert et al., 2014; Gutiérrez-Clellen et al., 2012; Restrepo et al., 2013), and intervention studies are characterized by extensive variability in how the two languages are combined in treatment (e.g., Harvey et al., 2021).

The question that drives the work reviewed in this article is whether there is an optimal bilingual exposure strategy that would enhance language outcomes, especially in children who are at risk for language and communication difficulties. We argue that studies that test how dual-language input shapes bilingual children's language outcomes and studies that test how different ways of combining languages in the input affect learning in bilingual children are critical to our understanding of how bilingual input may be optimized, both in everyday life and in structuring of bilingual intervention. Our purpose here is to review the available literature that has tested these two questions and to link the findings with the bilingual intervention work focusing on children with language impairments.

How Does Dual-Language Input Shape Bilingual Children's Language Outcomes?

Dual-language input is a general, all-purpose term that describes input in two languages. Yet, the specific aspects of dual-language input (i.e., how precisely the two languages are combined within a communicative exchange) may matter for how children accommodate to dual-language input and learn from it. For instance, one type of dual-language input involves code-switching (or alternation between languages within or across sentences), and it is a common practice among many bilinguals (Bail et al., 2015; Byers-Heinlein, 2013; Place & Hoff, 2011). Dual-language input can also refer to input that does not involve code-switching within a single communicative exchange and, instead, involves distinct contexts experienced by a child where each language is spoken. Such context-specific dual-language circumstances are common for children exposed to one language in the home (e.g., Spanish) and another language in school (e.g., English). Both types of dual-language input involve distributed exposure to the two languages (i.e., the child receives some information in one language, and some information in the other) and may have consequences for how bilingual children learn.

Distributed Exposure and Bilingual Children's Language Skills

Distributed exposure is one of the main causal factors that is thought to contribute to differences between monolingual children's performance on language tasks (most notably, vocabulary) and their bilingual peers' performance on the same single-language tasks (e.g., Bialystok & Feng, 2011; Bialystok et al., 2010; Fernández et al., 1992; Gross et al., 2014; Hemsley et al., 2010; Lesaux et al., 2010; Leseman, 2000; Mancilla-Martinez & Lesaux, 2011; Marchman et al., 2010; Teoh et al., 2012; Thordardottir et al., 2009; Uccelli & Páez, 2007; Uchikoshi, 2006; Umbel et al., 1992; Vagh et al., 2009; etc.). This deficit-focused idea of bilingual–monolingual discrepancies in language-specific skills persists despite the fact that it is well known that bilingual children's language skills must be assessed across both languages (Bedore & Peña, 2008; De Houwer, 2009; Gross et al., 2014). The distributed exposure hypothesis posits that bilingual children receive exposure to two languages within the same waking hours that monolingual children receive exposure to one language (Oller et al., 2007; Pearson, 1998). Because words are learned through exposure, the necessary consequence of such distributed exposure is a smaller number of exposures to words in each language. This in turn leads to weaker links between semantic and lexical information within each language (termed the weaker links hypothesis; Gollan & Acenas, 2004; Gollan et al., 2002, 2011) and less robust language-specific lexical networks in bilinguals versus monolinguals. Ultimately, smaller language-specific vocabularies in bilinguals are seen as a mathematical inevitability.

In our work, we have attempted to reframe this deficit-based account of bilingual language development and asked: Is this really so? We suggest that the evidence for the role of distributed exposure in bilingual children's vocabulary outcomes stems from bilingual–monolingual group comparisons, an approach that is highly susceptible to confounds and biases. The usefulness and, indeed, the very legitimacy of bilingual–monolingual comparisons for understanding bilingual language development are deeply questionable, and yet, the role of distributed exposure is an important one to consider given its centrality to theories of bilingual language development. The crucial gap in the existing literature is that the effect of distributed exposure on bilingual children's language skills in general, and on their ability to learn new words specifically, has not been tested experimentally. Another crucial gap is that the vast majority of available research on bilingual language development does not include children who represent different levels of language ability and rarely focuses on children with communication disorders (e.g., Kohnert & Medina, 2009). In Libersky et al. (2023), we tested the role of distributed exposure empirically and offered a viable alternative hypothesis regarding the effects of distributed exposure: that distributed exposure does not compromise bilingual word learning and, under some circumstances, can even enhance it.

Prior studies testing distributed exposure. The possibility that exposing bilingual children to words in both of their languages enhances learning has found some empirical support and yet rarely has this evidence been integrated into the work on distributed exposure effects. Bilingual vocabulary development literature indicates that for young bilingual children, learning of translation equivalents (dog/perro) is no more difficult than learning of singletons (Pearson & Fernández, 1994; Pearson et al., 1995), possibly because mutual exclusivity constraints function somewhat differently in bilingual than in monolingual development (Byers-Heinlein & Werker, 2009; Houston-Price et al., 2010; Lewis et al., 2020). In fact, bilinguals may learn translation equivalents more easily than singletons (Bilson et al., 2015; Lanvers, 1999; Pearson & Fernández, 1994) because when a bilingual comes across a translation equivalent for a known word, part of the word-learning task—learning of semantics—is already accomplished. These findings from developmental studies have been supported by a few small-scale empirical studies that have attempted to contrast single-language and dual-language learning conditions experimentally (Kiernan & Swisher, 1990; Perozzi & Sanchez, 1992). In both sets of studies, the authors found better outcomes for learning of second-language (L2) English targets (words or morphemes) in participants who initially received instruction in their native language (L1). For example, Kiernan and Swisher (1990) examined novel word learning in Spanish- and Navajo-speaking children learning English as a second language, testing the hypothesis that initial exposure to the novel words in their L1 would improve English learning. They found that bilingual children learned novel words in their L2 more quickly when they had first learned these novel words in their L1. Perozzi and Sanchez (1992) found a similar benefit for children with language delay learning prepositions and pronouns in their L2.

Libersky et al. (2023). Our work built on this prior literature and investigated how distributed exposure impacted bilingual children's word learning experimentally, by teaching bilingual children novel words in dual-language (distributed) and single-language learning contexts in a within-subjects experimental design. This approach allowed for an empirically sound comparison between single-language and dual-language contexts within the same bilingual participants, avoiding the pitfalls of bilingual–monolingual comparisons. We taught Spanish–English bilingual children two sets of novel words in two conditions. One set of novel words was taught exclusively in English (single-language condition; their L2). Another set of novel words was first taught in Spanish, followed by English (dual-language condition). Teaching occurred in six blocks across both conditions. In the single-language condition, children were exposed to the novel English labels associated with the novel objects in all six blocks. In contrast, in the dual-language condition, children were exposed to the novel Spanish labels for the novel objects in the first three blocks and to the novel English labels for the same novel objects in the final three blocks. The Spanish-like and the English-like novel words were marked by distinct syllable structure and phonetic realization, to accurately reflect natural parameters of the two languages. All stimuli, in both English and Spanish, were recorded by a female bilingual speaker of English and Spanish, who acquired both languages from birth.

We hypothesized that if reduction in language-specific exposure to the novel words in the distributed, dual-language condition has a negative effect on learning (in line with the weaker links hypothesis), we would see more robust learning of English-like novel words in the single-language condition. If, on the other hand, exposure to Spanish-like novel words can support bilingual children's learning of English-like novel words for the same novel objects (in line with Kiernan & Swisher, 1990), we would observe more robust learning of English–like novel words in the dual-language condition. We tested 26 Spanish–English bilingual children (Mage = 4.72 years, SD = 0.52 years), who represented a broad range of language ability, as indexed by Bilingual English–Spanish Assessment (BESA; Peña et al., 2014) Language Index scores. We found that children learned novel words above chance in both conditions (single-language and dual-language), and furthermore, in the dual-language condition, children learned Spanish-like and English-like labels for the novel objects above chance. However, children learned English-like novel words significantly better in the single-language versus the dual-language condition, seemingly confirming the deleterious role of distributed exposure for language-specific learning.

However, further analyses revealed a much more complicated story. When we contrasted children's learning performance on the first three blocks of the English-only condition to their performance on the English blocks in the dual-language condition, we effectively equated the number of exposures to English-like novel words across the two conditions. If distributed exposure were at the core of bilingual children's performance in the dual-language condition, we should no longer have observed an effect of condition in our analyses. And yet, once again, performance was significantly higher in the single-language condition, suggesting that it was not in fact distribution of exposure that had caused a dip in bilingual children's performance in the dual-language condition (vs. the single-language condition). Furthermore, when we zeroed in on children's word-learning performance in the dual-language condition, we found that higher levels of learning for the Spanish novel words in the first three learning blocks were associated with higher levels of learning for the English novel words in the last three learning blocks. This result supports the findings from earlier studies (Kiernan & Swisher, 1990; Perozzi & Sanchez, 1992), indicating that learning novel words in the children's native language bolsters learning in the second language. How then to reconcile these seemingly irreconcilable data?

We believe that the pattern of results in the dual-language condition was driven not by distribution of exposures across Spanish and English, but by a sudden switch in the language of learning from Spanish (Block 3) to English (Block 4). Follow-up analyses that focused on this transition indicated that when the language switched from Spanish to English, children's word-learning performance plummeted down to chance levels. Learning rates then recovered to above-chance levels in the last two English blocks, but this recovery was not sufficient to bring overall learning rates for the English novel words in the dual-language condition to the level of the single-language condition, where no switching had occurred. We replicated this result with a different group of Spanish–English bilingual children (Libersky et al., 2023; Experiment 2), where we followed the same experimental design but added a speaker cue to the dual-language condition, as a possible way to reduce the effects of distributed exposure.

One belief frequently held by parents is that the optimal bilingual exposure strategy is to segregate input across speakers, a mode of bilingual exposure known as one-person/one-language. In this approach, each caregiver (assuming a two-caregiver household) strives to speak in only one language to the child, and ideally, this language is the native language for each caregiver (e.g., one caregiver addresses them in English and the other addresses them in Spanish). However, there is only a handful of studies that had queried the utility of this exposure strategy empirically (De Houwer, 2007; Potter et al., 2019), and to our knowledge, only one study had found some support for it in the area of statistical learning (Weiss et al., 2009). In our study (Libersky et al., 2023; Experiment 2), we tested a different group of Spanish–English bilingual children (N = 25, Mage = 5.07 years, SD = 0.56 years) using the same experimental paradigm as in Experiment 1 but tagged language by speaker. A native male English speaker produced all English novel words, and a native female Spanish speaker produced all Spanish novel words. Otherwise, the design remained the same, testing the possibility that the addition of the speaker cue would reduce the effects of distributed exposure.

Replicating the results of Experiment 1, analyses of Experiment 2 data once again revealed above-chance levels of learning in both conditions. Addition of the speaker cue made little difference, however. Just like in Experiment 1, in this experiment, children learned English-like novel words better in the single-language condition than the dual-language condition and showed a reduction to chance levels in the dual-language condition when the language switched from Spanish (in the first three blocks) to English (in Block 4). To our dismay, in this experiment, there was no evidence of transfer in the dual-language condition, and children's performance on the Spanish-like novel words did not predict their learning of the English-like novel words. Together, these results argue strongly against the usefulness of the one-person/one-language exposure method as a possible strategy to enhance bilingual language outcomes. In fact, it appears that cross-linguistic transfer of newly learned information is more likely to occur when bilingual children receive input in both languages from the same speaker (as in Experiment 1) than from two different speakers (as in Experiment 2). These findings further call for a serious and purposeful reevaluation of distributed exposure as a driver of bilingual language performance and showcase the importance of considering how exactly input switches from one language to another. This is because, at least in these studies, it was the switch between languages (rather than distribution of exposure) that drove bilingual children's learning performance in the dual-language condition.

Summary of distributed exposure studies. Together, prior studies (Kiernan & Swisher, 1990; Perozzi & Sanchez, 1992) and our own work (Libersky et al., 2023) are only partially consistent with the distributed-exposure account of bilingual language development. Rather, they indicate that under some circumstances, learning words in one language can support word-learning in the children's other language and that costs associated with distribution of exposures across languages may be grounded in switches between languages rather than in reductions in language-specific exposure.

Code-Switched Input and Bilingual Children's Language Skills

A robust psycholinguistic literature indicates that code-switching carries processing costs, both for the speaker (e.g., Bobb & Wodniecka, 2013; Fricke et al., 2016; Gross & Kaushanskaya, 2015, 2018; Meuter & Allport, 1999) and for the listener (e.g., Altarriba et al., 1996; Bultena et al., 2015a, 2015b; Byers-Heinlein et al., 2017; Gross et al., 2019, 2021; Proverbio et al., 2004). Experimental studies of language comprehension indicate that a language switch poses processing and integration costs (Altarriba et al., 1996; Bultena et al., 2015a, 2015b; Proverbio et al., 2004), and induces an increased cognitive load (Byers-Heinlein et al., 2017). In fact, neuroimaging literature has revealed that code-switches encountered during comprehension tasks elicit responses that suggest an increased effort in memory updating processes (e.g., Moreno et al., 2002; van Der Meij et al., 2011).

Notably, costs associated with code-switching have been mostly noted in laboratory-based experimental tasks, such as cued language switching tasks (for production), and auditory moving window tasks (for comprehension). Yet, when experimental conditions more closely resemble everyday communication (e.g., when switches are not cued and when units of switching are not single words but conversational units), the costs of switching languages diminish, often to the point where switching is observed to carry little cost (e.g., Adamou & Shen, 2019; Blanco-Elorrieta & Pylkkänen, 2017). Whatever the psycholinguistic reality of code-switching, it is rather common in the speech of bilingual parents, although the degree of code-switched language input in a bilingual child's environment varies widely both within and across bilingual communities (e.g., Bail et al., 2015; Byers-Heinlein, 2013; Nicoladis & Secco, 2000; Tare & Gelman, 2011). The question then is: Does exposure to code-switched input contribute to individual differences in bilingual children's language abilities, akin to other qualitative aspects of language input? We might expect an inverse association between code-switched input and children's language outcomes, if we posit that every switch encountered by the child carries processing costs and that these costs accrue over time. In contrast, with the view toward recent, more ecologically valid studies indicating that naturalistic instances of code-switched input are not costly, it is possible that fluctuations in the amount of code-switched input in bilingual children's environments carry little predictive power in shaping their language outcomes.

Prior studies testing the role of code-switched input in children's language skills. Notably, only a handful of studies have examined the relationship between exposure to code-switched input and bilingual children's language skills, and the findings paint a rather confusing picture. For instance, two related studies (Place & Hoff, 2011, 2016) found that exposure to mixed-language input (which was defined as the proportion of time during which the child was exposed to two languages within the same 30-min block) was not associated with language outcomes in 25- to 30-month-old Spanish–English bilingual children. The language outcome measures in these studies targeted productive vocabulary, grammar, and auditory comprehension.

In contrast to Place and Hoff (2011, 2016), a few studies revealed significant associations between bilingual children's exposure to code-switched input and language outcomes, albeit with conflicting outcomes. For example, Byers-Heinlein (2013) examined the relationship between a parental report measure of intrasentential code-mixing (using words from two languages within the same sentence) and 18- to 24-month-old bilingual children's performance on English receptive and productive vocabulary measures. The findings suggested that increased frequency of parental intrasentential language mixing was associated with reduced vocabulary in the children, over and above the effects of English exposure, gender, age, and linguistic balance. Similarly, Lipsky (2013) found that the amount of code-switching by the teacher during storybook reading (quantified as the number of Spanish words used by the teacher during an English reading session) was negatively related to children's English receptive vocabulary outcomes. The children in the Lipsky (2013) study were 36- to 59-month-old Head Start students. Together, studies by Byers-Heinlein (2013) and Lipsky (2013) point toward a possible negative impact of code-switched input on bilingual children's language skills. In contrast, Bail et al. (2015) found the opposite pattern, showing that intrasentential (within-sentence) code-switching was positively related to 18- to 24-month-old bilingual children's vocabulary size. Unlike Byers-Heinlein (2013), Bail et al. (2015) derived an objective measure of code-switching, which they obtained from a parent–child interaction sample. How then to reconcile the rather conflicting findings across these studies?

Kaushanskaya and Crespo (2019). Beyond considering the obvious culprits (the distinct approaches to measuring code-switched input and the different indexes of language ability used in these studies), there is a theoretically grounded possibility related to individual differences in children's ability to process code-switched input. In our work (Kaushanskaya & Crespo, 2019), we tested the hypothesis that individual differences in bilingual children's verbal working memory (WM) would moderate the association between code-switched input and children's language outcomes. Verbal WM is a capacity-constrained system with well-documented individual differences in the population (Pickering & Gathercole, 2001), and empirical evidence supports a strong link between comprehension performance and verbal WM measures in bilingual children (e.g., Buac et al., 2016; Cockroft, 2016; Engel de Abreu et al., 2011; Kormos & Sáfár, 2008). Daneman and Carpenter (1980) argued that weaknesses in WM would lead to deficits in comprehension, particularly for the more demanding comprehension tasks that require integration of words, phrases, and sentences into a coherent whole. We reasoned that processing of code-switched input may constitute just such a task.

In Kaushanskaya and Crespo (2019), we hypothesized that children with lower levels of verbal WM capacity would find code-switched input challenging, and thus, we predicted a negative association between increased amounts of code-switched input and language skills for children with lower levels of verbal WM. We predicted that children with higher levels of WM capacity would find it easier to process code-switched input and, thus, that code-switched input would not affect their language skills. We tested these hypotheses in a large sample of school-age Spanish–English bilingual children (N = 174, ages 5–11 years). Similar to prior studies, our measure of code-switched input relied on parent report. However, in contrast to prior studies, we queried not only the parent's use of code-switching but also their perceptions of how often other individuals in the child's life code-switched. Parents were provided with a list of individuals that the child could interact with, including the child's mother, father, siblings, grandparents, other relatives, friends during play, classmates at school, adults at school, and strangers. The parents were then asked, “If this individual/these individuals speak both Spanish and English, how often do they code-switch around your child?” The parents were provided with a frequency scale, where 0 stood for never, 5 stood for half the time, and 10 stood for always. Ratings were averaged across all persons to create a measure of average exposure to code-switching that the child experienced in their environment.

We tested children's language skills in both English and Spanish. Children's English language skills were indexed by their performance on the Clinical Evaluation of Language Fundamentals–Fourth Edition (Semel et al., 2003), and children's Spanish language skills were indexed by their performance on the Clinical Evaluation of Language Fundamentals–Fourth Edition, Spanish (Wiig et al., 2006). Verbal WM was assessed via the Numbers Reversed subtest from the Woodcock-Johnson III Tests of Cognitive Abilities (Woodcock et al., 2001).

We found that for children with lower levels of verbal WM, increased exposure to code-switching was associated with reduced language scores, in both English and Spanish. In contrast, for children with higher levels of verbal WM, increased exposure to code-switching was associated with improved language scores, in both languages. These findings suggest that children with different levels of verbal WM respond differently to code-switched input, although the correlational, cross-sectional nature of these data precludes a causal interpretation. One surprising finding from the Kaushanskaya and Crespo (2019) study was a positive association observed between exposure to code-switched input and language scores for children with higher levels of verbal WM capacity. There is a number of reasons why children's language skills might benefit from code-switched input. For instance, code-switched input may highlight translation equivalents (Bail et al., 2015), thus facilitating vocabulary acquisition across both languages. It may also draw children's attention to the pragmatic situations within which language input unfolds (Yow & Markman, 2016), thus enhancing the child's ability to acquire linguistic information from a communicative exchange. These possibilities are entirely speculative, but they indicate that in principle, it is possible for code-switched input to be associated with positive language outcomes in some bilingual children.

Summary of studies linking exposure to code-switching and language skills. In summary, our findings indicate that code-switched input can positively shape children's language outcomes, especially in children who have sufficient cognitive capacity to process variable input. Our results provide a possible reconciliation for the discrepant findings in the literature linking exposure to code-switched input and children's language outcomes and speak to the broader psycholinguistic literature on language switching. It is possible that at least some of the discrepancies across studies may be the result of fluctuations in participants' verbal WM skills. Beyond a theoretical contribution that such an observation makes, it is also immediately practically relevant because limitations in verbal WM are well documented for children with neurodevelopmental disabilities, including DLD (e.g., Archibald & Gathercole, 2006; Montgomery, 2000; Weismer et al., 1999).

A significant limitation of all studies that have attempted to link exposure to code-switched input and children's language skills is that these studies did not include children with communication impairments. If the findings in Kaushanskaya and Crespo (2019) replicate and are confirmed by well-controlled experimental work, they may indicate that code-switched input may pose a risk for children with DLD and for children with other neurodevelopmental disabilities that may have constrained processing capacity. However, as they currently stand, the findings of the Kaushanskaya and Crespo (2019) study cannot speak to whether code-switched input might impact language acquisition differently in children with weaknesses in language and communication versus children with typical language skills. Active recruitment of children with DLD and other neurodevelopmental disabilities and their inclusion in future studies of code-switched input are necessary in order to understand how such input might shape language development in children who are at risk for language and communication disorders. It is also imperative to go beyond correlational studies (which have their uses) to well-controlled experimental studies that purposefully test the role of code-switched input (vs. non–code-switched input) in bilingual children's learning performance.

Experimental Studies of Code-Switched Input and Word Learning

Laboratory studies of code-switching suggest that comprehending information that involves a code-switch can be more cognitively taxing for children than engaging in single-language comprehension (e.g., Byers-Heinlein et al., 2017; Gross et al., 2019; Morini & Newman, 2019; Potter et al., 2019). If these costs flow downstream, presence of code-switches in input may negatively affect bilingual children's learning from such input.

Prior studies of word learning in code-switched contexts. Only a few studies have examined the role of code-switched input in bilinguals' word learning, and the findings have been highly inconsistent. For instance, Byers-Heinlein et al. (2022) found that French–English bilingual children learned French and English novel words better when they were embedded in single-language sentences (e.g., “Look! Do you see the dog on the teelo?”) than in code-switched sentences (e.g., “Look! Do you see the chien on the teelo?”). A similar pattern of results was reported for English-speaking adults learning Spanish as a second language (Blair & Morini, 2022). Adult participants were taught the names of novel creatures in English-only, Spanish-only, and code-switched conditions. Results revealed that hearing novel words in English-only sentences led to significantly higher accuracy at test compared to the other conditions. Delayed retention measures indicated that this single-language advantage persisted long term. Together, these studies are consistent with the hypothesis that code-switched context, at least when switches are intrasentential, is less conducive to word learning than single-language input.

However, studies that have embedded word learning in naturalistic activities have yielded a different set of results. For instance, Read et al. (2021) showed that preschool-age Spanish–English bilingual children retained more novel words in their second language from a code-switched book than a book formatted in only one language. Similarly, Brouillard et al. (2022) found highly comparable word-learning performance from single-language and dual-language books in their sample of French–English bilingual children. These findings are consistent with a growing literature indicating that naturalistic code-switching behaviors that conform to community norms may carry little processing cost (e.g., Adamou & Shen, 2019; Blanco-Elorrieta & Pylkkänen, 2017).

Kaushanskaya et al. (2022). In this study, we tested whether code-switched input would influence word-learning performance in Spanish–English bilingual children. Our initial hypothesis was grounded in the literature indicating processing costs associated with code-switched input. We hypothesized that if processing costs associated with code-switched input have negative downstream consequences for learning, then bilingual children should be less successful learning novel words in code-switched contexts than in single-language contexts. However, we also considered an alternative hypothesis that views code-switched input through the theoretical lens of variability effects. One long-standing finding in the study of learning is that variability can enhance learning under some circumstances (Estes & Burke, 1953; Munsinger & Kessen, 1966). For instance, exposure to multiple exemplars during category learning facilitates children's ability to extend the newly learned words to untaught exemplars and to retain the novel word-referent mappings over time (e.g., Twomey et al., 2014). Exemplar variability also accelerates children's vocabulary development outside the lab (Perry et al., 2010) and can enhance word learning (e.g., Aguilar et al., 2018) and morphosyntactic treatment outcomes (Plante et al., 2014) in children with clinically significant weaknesses in language. One explanation for why variability may be helpful to word learning is that learners (especially young learners; Vlach & Sandhofer, 2011) encode all cues to the novel word, including those that are noncontrastive. Variability in the noncontrastive cues can promote learning of the contrastive (target) information. In the context of bilingual word learning, code-switched input may promote word learning because variability in the linguistic context would enable children to zoom into the word itself.

To test these competing hypotheses, we tested 45 Spanish–English bilingual children (Mage = 5.05 years, SD = 0.57 years) on an experimental word-learning task, where we manipulated linguistic context (single-language vs. code-switched) in a within-subjects design. Critically, in both conditions, children learned English-like novel words and were exposed to each novel word the exact same number of times. However, in the code-switched condition, English-like novel words were embedded in sentences where English and Spanish switched in an ecologically valid manner, with patterns of switching corresponding to naturalistic inter- and intrasentential switches produced by Spanish–English bilinguals living in the United States (Bail et al., 2015; Pfaff, 1979; Timm, 1975). Unique to our study, the word-learning paradigm was modeled after the common intervention parameters in speech and language pathology, with extensive iterative exposure to the novel words, and a learning-to-criterion protocol. The learning-to-criterion protocol was implemented to go beyond the typical studies of word learning and to examine whether code-switched input, if it does influence word-learning performance, would do so persistently over time, until children reached mastery.

We analyzed word-learning data in two waves. The first wave of analyses focused on children's performance on the novel word recognition measure after the first teaching block (similar to how previous laboratory-based studies of word learning indexed performance). In this analysis, we found that children learned novel words above chance in both the single-language and the code-switched conditions and did so equally well. This finding runs counter to prior experimental studies of word-learning that have observed better outcomes in single-language conditions (Blair & Morini, 2022; Byers-Heinlein et al., 2022) but are perfectly consistent with studies indicating that code-switches carry few processing costs when these switches closely align with code-switches that are common in the bilingual community (Adamou & Shen, 2019; Blanco-Elorrieta & Pylkkänen, 2017). It is highly likely that dosage matters as well, in that in our study, children received multiple exposures to the novel words, even within the very first teaching block, and these exposures were embedded in semantically informative sentences.

The second wave of analyses focused on the learning-to-criteria data, with number of teaching cycles to criterion acting as the dependent variable. In this analysis, we found that children reached criterion sooner in the code-switched condition than in the single-language condition. This finding indicates that repeated exposures to code-switching during a learning task can facilitate novel word learning. We predicted that should code-switched input function similarly to variable context for the novel words, it may help learners to isolate the novel words and to retain them. The presence of Spanish in the input may have enhanced children's awareness of the novel word form and facilitated its retention over time. In other words, hearing novel words in language frames that are different from the language of the target novel word (i.e., in code-switched contexts) may have highlighted the invariant structure of the novel words. It will be the charge of the future studies to determine whether specific types of code-switches (for instance, the combination of the Spanish determiner with the novel English-like novel word) are particularly conducive to word learning, or whether this effect does not depend on the particular type of code-switching.

Notably, the children in the Kaushanskaya et al. (2022) study represented a wide range of language ability, with BESA scores ranging from clinically low (Standard Scores below 85, i.e., DLD) to high. Equally notably, while children with higher BESA scores tended to be better word learners, language ability did not moderate the effect of condition, indicating that the effects of code-switched input were similar for children with higher and lower levels of language ability. This finding is reassuring, in light of the fact that word learning is a well-established deficit in children with DLD (Alt & Plante, 2006; Alt et al., 2004; McGregor, 1997; McGregor et al., 2013; Rice et al., 1992). Children with clinical and even subclinical weaknesses in language are also characterized by processing deficits (Bishop & McArthur, 2005; Leonard et al., 2007; McArthur & Bishop, 2005) and by difficulties with switching between activities and mental sets (Iluz-Cohen & Armon-Lotem, 2013; Marton, 2008). Therefore, one might anticipate that input minimizing language switching would be particularly advantageous for children with weak language skills. Yet, this was not what we observed in our study. Of course, replication will be crucial, and it will be important for future studies to test the question of how code-switched input influences bilingual children's word-learning performance in larger samples of children with DLD, and along longer timescales. As they stand, our findings indicate that code-switched input carries no cost to word learning (as long as switching is predictable and exposure to novel words is iterative) and may even enhance it, in children at all levels of language ability.

Our findings stemming from our word-learning work also stand in contrast to the findings stemming from correlational studies. Whereas in Kaushanskaya and Crespo (2019) we observed that increased exposure to code-switched input was associated with reduced language scores in children with lower levels of verbal WM, in Kaushanskaya et al. (2022) we found that code-switched context did not interfere with word learning in children at all levels of language ability (including children with clinically low language skills, who are likely to have difficulties with verbal WM). Because correlational and experimental studies have been conducted in different groups of children, characterized by distinct ages, it is possible that this discrepancy is simply an outcome of sampling, with different bilingual samples characterized by different dynamics between code-switched input and language outcomes. It will be the charge for future studies to combine experimental and observational methods to build a comprehensive framework for studying the effects of dual-language input, including code-switching input, in bilingual populations. A combination of methods would form a stronger foundation for being able to translate experimental and empirical findings into clinically motivated intervention studies.

Summary of word-learning studies examining effect of code-switched exposure. Although the work in this area is limited, it indicates that single instances of code-switching can reduce learning (vs. learning in a single language; Blair & Morini, 2022; Byers-Heinlein et al., 2022), consistent with laboratory-based processing studies of code-switching (Byers-Heinlein et al., 2017; Gross et al., 2019; Morini & Newman, 2019; Potter et al., 2019). Yet, when learning is embedded in a naturalistic context, involving multiple, information-rich exposures to the novel words (as in Kaushanskaya et al., 2022), code-switched contexts carry little learning costs and can even enhance learning. Once again, it is imperative that future studies examine word learning in code-switched contexts in children with communication disorders, since bottlenecks in language ability, memory skills, and attention may moderate the effects of code-switching on learning.

It is also critical that we attempt to test effects of code-switched input on learning and language outcomes in children across different linguistic and cultural communities, as there are indications that code-switching shapes learning differently in children from distinct groups (Byers-Heinlein et al., 2022). Factors related to socioeconomic status, community and cultural norms, patterns of language dominance and dominance shifts, as well as maturation-related and cognitive factors can all, in theory, modulate and/or moderate effects of code-switched input on language and learning outcomes. The direction of such effects is nontrivial to predict, in part because there is very little precedent for these types of manipulations in the literature and in part because there are theoretical possibilities that yield divergent outcomes. For instance, it might be hypothesized that in communities where code-switching is common, there would be minimal (or perhaps positive) effect of code-switched context on bilingual children's word learning, since such structures would be familiar to the children and comply with their linguistic expectations. At the same time, it might be hypothesized with an equal degree of probability that in communities where code-switching is rare, code-switched context might enhance word learning because it would draw children's attention and highlight the relationship between linguistic context and the novel word. Availability of diametrically opposite but similarly viable hypothesis charts an exciting line of future studies, with both theoretical and practical/clinical implications.

Discussion

Currently, advice to parents raising bilingual children with or without language disorders regarding the optimal language exposure is based on intuition rather than on solid empirical evidence. Furthermore, the evidence for the benefits of bilingual treatment in bilingual children with language impairment is not clear-cut (Ebert et al., 2014; Restrepo et al., 2013; Thordardottir et al., 2015). The particular way of combining two languages in the input (e.g., by switching languages within sentences vs. across sentences vs. blocking language exposure) and the particular treatment targets (e.g., lexicon–semantics vs. morphosyntax) may be key to successful integration of the two languages in treatment. Studies attempting to link specific parameters of dual-language input to bilingual children's language outcomes and word-learning performance can serve as a useful foundation for structuring bilingual treatments in the most optimal way.

From Bilingual Word-Learning Studies to Bilingual Intervention

The effects of distributed exposure on children's language outcomes have been at the core of the studies that interrogate the process by which bilingual children acquire language. Yet, there has been a surprisingly sparse number of studies that has attempted to experimentally test the effect of distributed exposure on bilingual children's uptake of information. Existing work on distributed exposure effects has rarely considered the fact that distributed input, by its very nature, is likely to involve code-switching. Our word-learning studies that have explicitly targeted distributed exposure as the possible mechanism of bilingual word learning (Libersky et al., 2023) strongly suggest that it is the sudden switching between languages (rather than distributed exposure itself) that negatively impacts children's word learning. This finding aligns with lab-based studies of code-switching indicating that there are persistent processing costs associated with perceiving some types of code-switches (Byers-Heinlein et al., 2017; Gross et al., 2019; Morini & Newman, 2019; Potter et al., 2019). Further, switches in both the speaker and the language appear to interfere with learning to the greater degree than switches in language only (Libersky et al., 2023; Experiment 2) and prevent children from being able to rely on their newly encoded knowledge in the L1 to support L2 learning.

In contrast, in an experiment that explicitly tested the role of code-switched input in bilingual children's word learning (Kaushanskaya & Crespo, 2019), we failed to find code-switching costs and, in fact, observed a benefit of code-switched context versus single-language context for children's word learning. This experimental finding is consistent with correlational evidence indicating that for children with good processing skills (as indexed by higher levels of verbal WM), increased exposure to code-switched input is associated with improved language outcomes, in both languages (Kaushanskaya & Crespo, 2019). How can we then reconcile these two sets of studies indicating that switching languages can both enhance learning and interfere with it?

Clinical implications. We believe that the exact way in which languages are combined shapes the effects of dual-language (and code-switched, specifically) input on bilingual children's word learning and, possibly, on their long-term language outcomes. An abrupt language switch may carry costs (as in Libersky et al., 2023), but these costs appear to be short-lived, and over a learning period characterized by repeated, semantically informative exposure, switches between languages, especially when these are predictable and consistent with community norms (as in Kaushanskaya et al., 2022), can enhance learning. Such an insight would be impossible to glean from persistently prevalent yet deeply flawed group-comparison studies between bilinguals and monolinguals—a deficit-based approach that has dominated this field of inquiry for the past 30 years. Experimental, well-controlled studies of bilingual children's word learning, which attempt to replicate naturalistic word-learning scenarios, can take us further and provide a necessary bridge into clinical work. They can also serve as critical evidence for advice doled out to bilingual parents and clinicians regarding the optimal bilingual exposure and intervention strategy, should such a strategy exist.

The bilingual intervention literature is not extensive, and the studies that do exist differ widely not only in basic methodological detail (ages and specific diagnoses of the target population, specific languages and contexts of treatment, treatment types, dosage and longevity of treatment, etc.) but also in how exactly the bilingual treatments are implemented. In a recent systematic review of bilingual intervention studies specifically with children with DLD, Harvey et al. (2021) gathered evidence from nine treatment studies and ultimately reached a conclusion that across these studies, bilingual treatment was no less effective than treatment that focused exclusively on the second language. However, variability in the structure of bilingual intervention across studies is striking. For example, Gutiérrez-Clellen et al. (2012) and Simon-Cereijido et al. (2013) implemented a bilingual intervention with Spanish–English bilingual children where the language of treatment (English or Spanish) varied across sections, such that children were treated in only one language per session, alternating weekly. In contrast, Thordardottir et al. (2015) tested a bilingual intervention where the speech-language pathologist addressed children in their L2, while the child's parent participated in treatment using their L1, during the same session. Notably, whereas the Gutiérrez-Clellen et al. (2012) and Simon-Cereijido et al. (2013) studies suggest better outcomes in both the L1 and the L2 for the bilingual treatment versus L2-only treatment, Thordardottir et al. (2015) found little difference between the two treatment conditions for the L2 and little gains in the L1, even for the bilingual treatment group. Because the available number of studies is rather small, it is difficult to conclude that a particular strategy for implementing bilingual intervention is more or less conducive to positive outcomes. Nevertheless, the contrasts in the outcomes for the intervention studies are notable for their general consistency with word-learning studies probing for effects of dual-language input on bilingual children's learning.

Our word-learning studies indicate that abrupt switches between languages across different communication partners may interfere with learning, at least in the short term. It is possible that this is precisely what modulated the effects of bilingual treatment in Thordardottir et al. (2015). At the same time, rigid blocking of bilingual intervention by language (English-only vs. Spanish-only in alternating sessions) while shown to be effective in the Gutiérrez-Clellen et al. (2012) and Simon-Cereijido et al. (2013) studies may not be necessary to engender successful treatment outcomes. In fact, switching between languages within session in a predictable manner consistent with community norms may be a better and a more effective treatment strategy, as long as children receive sufficient exposure to the structure being targeted in treatment.

Conclusions and Future Directions

We believe that word-learning experiments, beyond answering critical theoretical questions regarding bilingual learning, can serve as an effective bridge between laboratory-based studies and intervention studies whose goal it is to discover the optimal way of combining languages in the input to the bilingual child. Before word-learning studies can serve this function, however, it is critical to conduct work that probes for generalization of dual-language exposure effects, both across different linguistic targets (e.g., nouns vs. verbs) and from targeted structures to related but untargeted structures (e.g., from familiar to novel exemplars). Future work should also carefully interrogate the longevity of dual-language input effects on word-learning outcomes across different time scales and probe for processing-based and memory-based mechanisms that likely contribute to the presence and persistence of these effects. Finally, it is crucial to conduct this work with children from different language and cultural groups with different levels of language ability, taking into account the patterns of language use by the child as well as the community partners with whom the child communicates. Ultimately, testing dual-language input effects through the strength-based lens of bilingualism and implementing studies that manipulate language input in ecologically valid ways within the bilinguals rather than through comparisons with monolinguals can move us toward outlining an empirically grounded set of recommended exposure strategies—one that can be readily shared with parents and clinicians.
