Emulsion-structured pectin-based edible films with whey protein and short-chain dietary fibers for geranylgeraniol encapsulation and strawberry preservation
Authors: Adela Cristina Martinez Urango, Maria Angela A. Meireles, Eric Keven Silva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study reports the design and characterization of emulsion-structured pectin-based edible films incorporating whey protein concentrate (WPC), short-chain prebiotic fibers (fructooligosaccharides, FOS; galactooligosaccharides, GOS; and xylooligosaccharides, XOS), and annatto seed oil as a source of geranylgeraniol. The formulation strategy combined citrus pectin with WPC and fibers to enhance the structural and functional properties of films while improving oil stabilization. The film-forming emulsions were processed by thermosonication to promote biopolymer dispersion and stable droplet incorporation. All films exhibited desirable technological features, including high encapsulation efficiency of geranylgeraniol (≥70 %). They also showed strong UV–vis light barrier properties and improved thermal stability in fiber-containing formulations. FTIR and fluorescence analyses indicated protein-carbohydrate interactions, although covalent Maillard-type conjugation was not confirmed through SDS–PAGE analysis. Dietary fiber addition influenced film microstructure, water vapor permeability, and crystallinity without compromising overall mechanical integrity, except for GOS films, which showed lower tensile strength. When applied as coatings, emulsions containing annatto seed oil effectively delayed strawberry spoilage during refrigerated storage, demonstrating their potential for extending shelf life. These findings highlight the relevance of emulsion-structured edible films as active packaging systems for delivering lipophilic bioactives and improving postharvest preservation of fresh produce.

Introduction

The growing interest in edible films as sustainable alternatives to synthetic packaging has driven research toward multifunctional systems capable of protecting food while delivering health-promoting compounds. Edible films based on biopolymers, such as polysaccharides and proteins, provide biodegradability, safety, and flexibility for incorporating bioactive ingredients, making them promising candidates for active food packaging applications (Bocker & Silva, 2024; Wang et al., 2020). Beyond acting as physical barriers, these films can serve as carriers for bioactive compounds, particularly hydrophobic molecules, such as essential oils or lipophilic extracts, that offer antioxidant and antimicrobial benefits but are often limited by low chemical stability and solubility (Mendes et al., 2020; Nahas, Andrade, et al., 2025; Norcino et al., 2020).

Annatto (Bixa orellana L.) seed oil, which is rich in geranylgeraniol and tocotrienols, has been associated with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial properties (Basting et al., 2021; Vardanega et al., 2019). Its incorporation into edible films may extend food shelf life and deliver functional compounds to consumers. However, encapsulating annatto oil in polymer matrices remains challenging due to its hydrophobic nature and the challenge of maintaining stability during film formation. Emulsion-based film systems offer a viable strategy to overcome this limitation, especially when combined with structuring agents that enhance matrix integrity and compatibility with oil droplets (Chen et al., 2025; Nahas, Furtado, Lopes, & Silva, 2025; Yang et al., 2024).

Recent studies indicate that dietary fibers and proteins can improve the functional and structural properties of edible films (Azevedo et al., 2018; Scarcella et al., 2024). Short-chain prebiotic fibers, such as fructooligosaccharides (FOS), galactooligosaccharides (GOS), and xylooligosaccharides (XOS) have attracted attention not only for their health benefits but also for their ability to interact with proteins, improving water barrier properties, thermal stability, and film flexibility (Correa et al., 2024; Fernandes et al., 2020; Silva et al., 2022). These fibers can interact with proteins through chemical reactions, such as the Maillard reaction, to modify the structure and functionality of edible films (Urango, Meireles, & Silva, 2024). Although the Maillard reaction between proteins and carbohydrates has been investigated as a mechanism to strengthen film networks, its occurrence in complex matrices under mild processing remains uncertain. In this scenario, thermosonication emerges as an innovative processing tool that improves solubility, reducing aggregation, and enhancing interactions among biopolymers, while simultaneously aiding oil emulsification for stable film formation (Strieder et al., 2022; Urango, Strieder, et al., 2022).

In this context, this study aimed to develop citrus pectin-based edible films enriched with whey protein concentrate (WPC), short-chain prebiotic fibers (FOS, GOS, and XOS), and annatto seed oil as a bioactive ingredient. Specifically, the research sought to: (i) investigate whether homogenizing the components via thermosonication could induce Maillard-type conjugation between WPC and prebiotic fibers, (ii) evaluate the influence of these fibers on the formation and stability of annatto seed oil emulsions, (iii) assess the effect of fiber incorporation on the structural, thermal, and functional properties of the films, including the encapsulation efficiency of geranylgeraniol, and (iv) examine the potential application of the film-forming emulsion as a coating to extend the shelf life of strawberries. This integrated approach addresses both the technological and functional aspects of edible film design, providing insights into strategies for developing active packaging systems that enhance food preservation and delivering bioactive compounds.

Material and methods

Ingredients and chemicals

The ingredients used for producing the edible films included citrus pectin (CP Kelco, Limeira, Brazil), whey protein concentrate (Alibra®, São Paulo, Brazil), Orafti® FOS from chicory (FOS, DP = 2–4) (Sweetmix, Sorocaba, Brazil), QHT-GOS-90S, a high-purity (∼90 %) galactooligosaccharide (GOS, DP = 2–7) (Quantum Hi-Tech, China), PreticX™ XOS from non-genetically modified corn (XOS, DP = 2–6) (AIDP Inc., City of Industry, CA, USA). Glycerol, sodium bromide, and calcium chloride were obtained from Sinergia-Científica (Campinas, Brazil). Annatto (Bixa orellana L.) seed oil was extracted using supercritical CO2 as solvent according to the methodology described by Urango, Arruda, et al. (2024).

Production of pectin-based films incorporated with annatto seed oil emulsion

The film-forming emulsion was formulated using citrus pectin (2 g/100 g), prebiotic short-chain dietary fibers (FOS, GOS, or XOS) (1 g/100 g), whey protein concentrate (1 g/100 g), annatto seed oil (0.3 g/100 g), glycerol (3 g/100 g), and deionized water (92.7 g/100 g). These proportions were established following preliminary trials in which the concentrations of annatto seed oil and whey protein concentrate were investigated to optimize film-forming ability, mechanical integrity, and oil encapsulation efficiency (preliminary data not shown). To ensure optimal hydration, each solid component (citrus pectin, dietary fiber, and whey protein concentrate) was first dispersed in deionized water in proportions matching their final g/100 g formulation and allowed to hydrate at room temperature for 24 h. At the end, all hydrated suspensions were combined and topped up with deionized water to reach the intended total mass, thus preserving the exact component ratios in the final film-forming solution. The dissolution of citrus pectin in deionized water was carried out using a magnetic stirrer set to 600 rpm for 10 min at 70 °C. After complete solubilization, the prebiotic dietary fiber solution was gradually incorporated, followed by 15 min of continuous stirring to ensure homogeneous dispersion. Next, the whey protein concentrate solution was added to the mixture, and glycerol was subsequently introduced. The stirring process continued for a total of 60 min to guarantee full homogenization and uniformity of all ingredients. For comparison, a control film-forming solution was prepared following the same protocol but without the addition of prebiotic dietary fibers or whey protein concentrate.

The film-forming solution underwent thermosonication at 70 °C with an acoustic power of 20 ± 1 W for 15 min. Following this treatment, annatto seed oil was added to facilitate emulsification, performed for an additional 5 min. Thermosonication parameters were established based on previous studies conducted by our group on whey-based beverages enriched with prebiotic fibers (FOS and XOS) and encapsulated fennel extract. These conditions were optimized to promote structural modifications and emulsion stability while avoiding excessive heating during sonication. The processing time (15 min) was limited to prevent the cumulative temperature increase caused by simultaneous water bath heating and ultrasonic energy dissipation (Urango, Neves, Meireles, & Silva, 2022). The resulting film-forming emulsion was then cooled in an ice bath. Thermosonication was conducted using a 13 mm ultrasonic probe operating at 19 kHz (Unique, Indaiatuba, SP, Brazil) in combination with a hot water recirculating bath (Marconi, Piracicaba, Brazil). The method used to determine acoustic power followed the protocol described by Strieder et al. (2020). In addition to promoting oil emulsification, the thermosonication process was designed to induce the in-situ formation of conjugates via the Maillard reaction between whey protein concentrate and dietary fibers.

To assess the impact of thermosonication on the film-forming solution, aliquots were collected after 15 min of treatment. The thermosonicated film-forming solution (TSFFS) containing whey protein concentrate (WPC) and prebiotic fibers (FOS, GOS, or XOS) was designated as FOS-TSFFS, GOS-TSFFS, and XOS-TSFFS, respectively. The TSFFS prepared without the addition of prebiotic fiber or WPC was labeled as Control-TSFFS. Following the emulsification of annatto seed oil, the resulting film-forming emulsions (FFE) were similarly categorized: emulsions containing WPC and prebiotic fibers were coded as FOS-FFE, GOS-FFE, and XOS-FFE, while the emulsion without prebiotic fiber or WPC was labeled as Control-FFE. For clarity, the control samples were formulated by omitting both WPC and prebiotic fibers and compensating their mass with water. Thus: Control-TSFFS: citrus pectin (2 g/100 g), glycerol (3 g/100 g), deionized water (95 g/100 g); Control-FFE: same as Control-TSFFS, with the addition of annatto seed oil (0.3 g/100 g) during emulsification. In all cases, the total mass was kept constant by adjusting the water content to replace the omitted components.

To prepare the films, 30 mL of each film-forming emulsion were poured into acrylic Petri dishes (140 mm × 15 mm) and dried at 40 °C for 24 h in a forced-air oven. The dried films were stored under controlled conditions of 58 % relative humidity at room temperature, using a desiccator containing saturated sodium bromide solution, until further analysis.

Analysis of Maillard conjugates formation

Degree of glycation

The o-Phthaldialdehyde (OPA) assay was conducted on the TSFFS rather than the film-forming emulsion to avoid interference from annatto seed oil during the analysis. The degree of glycation (DG) of the conjugates present in the TSFFS was investigated using two protocols. The first involved the OPA assay in the presence of dithiothreitol (DTT), and the second used β-mercaptoethanol, following the methods described by Church et al. (1983) and Nielsen et al. (2001), with slight modifications. All samples contained approximately 2 mg/mL of WPC. Samples were diluted with distilled water and centrifuged at 7000 rpm for 10 min to remove insoluble components. After centrifugation, aliquots were collected and subjected to analysis according to the following procedures:

i) OPA assay in the presence of DTT: A solution of 0.1 M sodium tetraborate (80 mL) was prepared and incubated at 50 °C to ensure complete dissolution. After cooling, 88 mg of DTT and 100 mg of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) were added. Subsequently, 80 mg of OPA, pre-dissolved in 2 mL of ethanol, was incorporated into the solution. The final volume was adjusted to 100 mL with distilled water. This solution was freshly prepared prior to use. To perform the assay, 10 μL of the sample solution was mixed with 200 μL of the prepared OPA reagent and incubated at 25 °C for 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 340 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax® Mini, Molecular Devices, USA). WPC subjected to the same process as the TSFFS served as a control and was designated as treated WPC. Distilled water was used as a blank. A calibration curve was generated using serine standards ranging from 0 to 10 mM. The DG was calculated using Eq. 1:(1)DG=C0−CtC0×100

C0 and Ct (mol/L) are the concentrations of free amino groups in treated WPC solution and in the FOS-TSFFS, GOS-TSFFS, and XOS-TSFFS conjugates solution, respectively.

ii) OPA assay in the presence of β-mercaptoethanol: This method follows a similar procedure to the assay performed with DTT, with key modifications. To the 0.1 M sodium tetraborate solution, 200 μL of β-mercaptoethanol and 5 mL of 20 % (w/w) SDS were added. After the preparation of the OPA reagent, 10 μL of the sample solution was mixed with 200 μL of the reagent. Prior to measuring the absorbance, the sample mixture was heated to 35 °C for 2 min. Absorbance was then recorded at 340 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax® Mini, Molecular Devices, USA).

Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis analysis (SDS–PAGE) analysis

SDS–PAGE was performed to evaluate the conjugation between WPC and other components, such as citrus pectin and prebiotic fibers (FOS, GOS, or XOS), in the thermosonicated film-forming solutions. The technique was used to determine potential structural modifications or the formation of high molecular weight conjugates induced by thermosonication. To assess the specific impact of thermosonication, several controls were included: untreated WPC (native WPC), WPC with glycerol, WPC with pectin and glycerol, and thermosonicated WPC (treated WPC). These controls allowed for a comprehensive comparison of the effects of thermosonication and the presence of individual components. Samples were prepared by mixing each film-forming solution or control with SDS sample buffer and heating at 95 °C for 5 min to ensure denaturation. Electrophoresis was carried out using a 12 % polyacrylamide gel, with a molecular weight marker run in parallel to estimate the size of the protein bands. Gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 and destained to visualize protein migration and conjugate formation.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

The films and controls obtained were analyzed using a Cary 630 FTIR spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, USA) to confirm the conjugation of WPC with citrus pectin and short-chain dietary fibers (FOS, GOS, or XOS). FTIR spectra were recorded to identify characteristic functional groups and assess potential structural changes associated with the formation of Maillard conjugates.

Intrinsic fluorescence analysis

The intrinsic fluorescence of the film-forming solutions was analyzed using a Horiba Fluorolog 3 spectrofluorometer. This instrument features a spectral resolution of approximately 0.3 nm (double 1200 g/mm gratings), a minimum spectral increment of 0.07 nm (double 1200 g/mm gratings), and a 450 W xenon lamp as the excitation source. Temperature control during measurements was achieved using a Peltier heater/cooler unit, with a temperature range of −10 °C to 110 °C. Samples were excited at 295 nm to specifically monitor tryptophan fluorescence, minimizing interference from tyrosine or phenylalanine residues. Emission spectra were recorded between 300 and 400 nm, with a spectral increment of 0.07 nm. Control samples, including native WPC, treated WPC, and WPC with pectin and glycerol, were analyzed alongside TSFFS samples. The effect of thermosonication, as well as the presence of prebiotic fibers (FOS, GOS, or XOS) and citrus pectin, was evaluated by analyzing changes in fluorescence intensity and emission peak wavelength shifts. Fluorescence data were used to assess the structural environment of tryptophan residues and the conformational changes induced by thermosonication. Additionally, the interactions between WPC, prebiotic fibers, and citrus pectin were interpreted based on fluorescence quenching or enhancement, indicating conjugation or structural rearrangements via the Maillard reaction.

Characterization of the film-forming annatto seed oil emulsion

Droplet size distribution and average diameter

The droplet size distribution and average diameter of the film-forming emulsion were determined using laser diffraction with a Mastersizer 2000 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, UK) at 25 °C. Samples were analyzed using the wet dispersion method, with water as the dispersant. The mean droplet diameter was expressed as the surface area mean diameter (D3,2), calculated according to Eq. 2. Microstructure

The microstructure of the film-forming emulsion was examined using an optical microscope (Jeneval 30-G0020, Carl Zeiss, Göttingen, Germany). Samples were placed on microscope slides and covered with glass coverslips to prepare for imaging. Observations were conducted using a 50× objective lens to capture detailed microstructural features of the emulsions.

Kinetic stability

The kinetic stability of the samples was evaluated following the methodology described by Urango, Neves, Meireles, and Silva (2022). Measurements were performed at the initial time (0 h, immediately after emulsion preparation) and at hourly intervals over a 24-h period at 40 °C. To monitor destabilization processes occurring within the measurement cell, the Turbiscan Stability Index (TSI) was calculated. Additionally, backscattering profiles (BS) of the samples were plotted to assess changes in light scattering, providing insights into sedimentation, creaming, or flocculation phenomena within the emulsions.

Characterization of the edible films incorporated with annatto seed oil emulsion

Thickness

The thickness of the films was determined using a digital caliper (Matrix Tools for eXistence, China). Measurements were taken at five randomly selected positions on each film to ensure representative and accurate thickness determination.

Morphological characterization

The morphological characteristics of the films were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Leo 440i, Leo Electron Microscopy/Oxford, Cambridge, England) equipped with an Energy Dispersive X-ray Detector. Micrographs of the surface and cross-section of each film were obtained at a magnification of 1000×. To prepare the samples, films were fractured using liquid nitrogen to preserve structural integrity, then mounted on sample holders with double-sided carbon adhesive tape. The samples were coated with a thin layer of gold via sputter coating to enhance conductivity. SEM imaging was performed with an accelerating voltage of 10 kV.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy

The confocal microstructure of the samples was examined using a LSM780-NLO Upright Confocal Microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany). Samples were prepared by placing them directly onto microscope slides for imaging. A laser with a wavelength of 488 nm was employed to specifically excite δ-tocotrienol molecules, enabling the visualization of their distribution within the samples. Images were captured at high resolution to provide detailed insights into the microstructural organization and the localization of fluorescent components.

Color parameters

The total color difference (ΔE) and the L*, a*, b* color parameters of the edible films were measured using a CR-400 colorimeter (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan). Light transmission

Following the sample preparation described in section 2.4.5, UV–Vis scans of the films were performed using a DU®-730 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Beckman Coulter, USA). The spectral range analyzed was 200–800 nm. The light transmittance (%T) of the films was recorded and plotted as a function of wavelength to evaluate their optical properties.

Water vapor permeability analysis

The water vapor permeability (WVP) of the films was evaluated using a modified gravimetric method. Approximately 15 g of calcium chloride were placed into screw-cap septum vials. Each film was used to replace the silicone septum, effectively sealing the vials. The prepared vials were then stored in a controlled environment at 100 % relative humidity and 25 °C. The mass of each vial was recorded at 2-h intervals for a total of 10 h, with a final measurement taken after 24 h of storage. The mass change over time was plotted, and the slope of the linear portion of the curve was used to calculate the WVP, as described by Eq. (5).(5)WVP=ΔM×HA×t×ΔP

Where, ΔM is the weight change from the straight line (g), H the thickness of the film (mm), t is the time (hours), A is the mouth area of the flask (m2), ΔP is the saturation vapor pressure at test temperature (3.169 kPa at 25 °C).

X-ray diffraction analysis

The crystallinity of the films was evaluated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. Measurements were performed with a D2 PHASER diffractometer (Bruker, Azerbaijan, Baku) over a 2θ range of 5° to 40°. The diffractometer operated at an accelerating voltage of 30 kV and a current of 10 mA, with a scanning rate of 0.13°/min. All analyses were conducted at room temperature to ensure consistency in the evaluation of the crystalline structure.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The thermal properties of the films were evaluated using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA/DSC1, Mettler Toledo, Schwerzenbach, Switzerland). Approximately 10 mg of each film sample was placed into aluminum crucibles for analysis. Thermograms were recorded over a temperature range of 25 to 600 °C, providing data on the thermal stability and decomposition behavior of the films.

Mechanical properties

The tensile strength (TS), elongation at break (EAB), and modulus of elasticity of the films were evaluated using a TAXTplus texture analyzer (TA Instruments, New Castle, USA). Film samples were cut into strips measuring 20 mm × 115 mm and tested under standardized conditions. Each film strip was secured between the grips of the analyzer, with an initial grip separation of 60 mm. The test was performed at a constant crosshead speed of 1.0 mm/s. A total of ten measurements were conducted for each sample to ensure statistical reliability.

Encapsulation efficiency of geranylgeraniol by gas chromatography (GC)

Film samples (∼1 g) were cut into small pieces and placed in individual reagent bottles. Methanol (30 mL) was added to each bottle, which were then sealed and subjected to ultrasonic treatment in a bath for 30 min. Following this, the bottles were incubated in an oven at 50 °C for 24 h. This extraction process was repeated twice to ensure complete recovery of the oil incorporated into the film matrix. After extraction, the liquid phase containing the geranylgeraniol was separated from the residual solid material and stored at −18 °C until further analysis. The geranylgeraniol content was quantified by gas chromatography (GC) following the methodology described by Urango, Arruda, et al. (2024). Application of film-forming solution or emulsion as coating for strawberries

For assessing the preservation effect, strawberries were divided into three groups: (i) uncoated strawberries (control group); (ii) strawberries coated with film-forming solution without annatto seed oil (containing citrus pectin, glycerol, and water only); and (iii) strawberries coated with film-forming emulsion containing annatto seed oil, whey protein, and prebiotic dietary fibers (FOS, GOS or XOS). This setup allowed evaluation of the individual roles of the film matrix components, particularly the potential preservation effect attributed primarily to annatto seed oil.

Fresh strawberries were cleaned and sanitized to ensure the removal of solid particles and potential contaminants from their surfaces. Strawberries of similar size and weight (17 ± 2 g) without visible defects were selected to minimize variability among treatments. After this preparation, the fruits were randomly selected and immersed in the film-forming solution or emulsion for 2 min. Excess coating solution was allowed to drain, and the coated strawberries were left to dry at room temperature for 3 h. Once dried, the strawberries were individually placed in Petri dishes and stored in a B.O·D incubator (SSBOD model, Piracicaba, Brazil) at 10 °C and 76 % relative humidity, maintained using a saturated sodium chloride solution. Storage continued for 11 days. To accelerate the shelf-life study, additional strawberries were stored at room temperature (25 °C). Uncoated strawberries served as controls. The visual appearance of both coated and uncoated strawberries was evaluated on days 0, 2, 4, and 14 of storage to assess the effectiveness of the coating in preserving fruit quality.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Minitab 18® software. The significance level was set at 5 % (p-value <0.05). Comparisons between means were conducted using the Tukey method with a 95 % confidence interval to identify significant differences among groups. In addition to statistical analyses, descriptive evaluations were conducted for qualitative responses obtained from FTIR, XRD, SEM, and other characterization techniques. These analyses provided a detailed discussion of the results, highlighting key structural, morphological, and compositional features observed in the films. Graphical outputs were interpreted to identify trends, relationships, and variations relevant to the film formulations and their properties.

Results and discussion

Investigating the formation of Maillard conjugates

This section investigated the potential glycation between whey proteins and dietary fibers, including citrus pectin and short-chain prebiotic fibers such as FOS, GOS, and XOS, via the Maillard reaction induced in situ during the thermosonication process. A multidisciplinary analytical approach was employed to characterize this phenomenon: the degree of glycation was evaluated using the OPA assay with DTT and β-mercaptoethanol, while structural modifications were analyzed through SDS–PAGE, FTIR spectroscopy, and intrinsic fluorescence analysis.

Confirming glycation between proteins and dietary fibers represents an innovative step toward developing functional ingredients, particularly in the formulation of edible films capable of encapsulating annatto seed oil. Literature suggests that delivering glycated proteins with prebiotic fibers not only enhances the structural properties of edible films but also can provide a promising strategy to improve the synergy between protein sources and prebiotic carbohydrates, potentially benefiting probiotic growth and intestinal health (Urango, Meireles, & Silva, 2024).

Degree of glycation

The OPA assay was initially applied to estimate the degree of glycation in thermosonicated film-forming solutions (TSFFS). However, during the analysis, strong interference from other formulation components was observed. Specifically, samples developed an intense yellow to dark coloration upon contact with the OPA reagent, except for the treated WPC control, which exhibited minimal color change. Absorbance readings did not follow the expected trend for glycation, and negative DG values were observed for the treated WPC sample (see supplementary Fig. S1-A). This behavior indicated that factors other than the availability of free amino groups influenced the color response.

To identify the source of interference, the OPA reagent was individually tested with each ingredient of the film-forming solution. Citrus pectin caused the most pronounced color change, suggesting a strong interaction with the reagent (see supplementary Fig. S1—B). XOS produced a slight yellow color, while the other components showed no significant reaction. These findings confirm that the complexity of the formulation, which includes pectin, glycerol, whey protein concentrate, and prebiotic fibers (FOS, GOS, or XOS), compromised the reliability of the assay.

Given this interference, it was not possible to quantify the degree of glycation using the OPA method. This limitation is acknowledged as an analytical challenge for complex matrices containing multiple reactive polysaccharides and proteins. The discussion of structural changes and potential Maillard interactions was therefore based on complementary techniques, such as FTIR spectroscopy and intrinsic fluorescence analysis, which provided consistent evidence of protein-carbohydrate interactions.

Protein profiling

The electrophoretic profiles of the thermosonicated film-forming solutions and their controls are shown in Fig. 1. Lane M represents the standard molecular weight markers. Native WPC displayed two prominent bands at approximately 14.4 kDa and 18.6 kDa, corresponding to α-lactalbumin (α-La) and β-lactoglobulin (β-Lg), respectively, consistent with previous findings (Zhang et al., 2023). A similar banding pattern was observed for treated WPC and WPC combined with glycerol, indicating that the thermosonication (TS) process and the presence of glycerol did not alter the molecular weight of the WPC proteins. Likewise, all thermosonicated film-forming solutions exhibited the characteristic strong bands of α-La and β-Lg. These results suggest that no significant reduction or disappearance of the WPC protein bands occurred, making it challenging to confirm the conjugation of WPC with citrus pectin or the prebiotic dietary fibers (FOS, GOS, or XOS). Functional groups

FTIR spectroscopy is a well-established technique for identifying non-enzymatic covalent conjugation between proteins and carbohydrates, as it can detect the imine group (C=N) formed during Maillard reactions (Liu et al., 2021). In this study, FTIR analysis was employed to investigate the structural changes in WPC and carbohydrates subjected to thermosonication by examining the spectra of the resulting films.

The spectra of FOS, GOS, and XOS films showed the presence of amide I and II bands, confirming the incorporation of WPC into the films. Notably, the amide I band (1628 cm−1) shifted to lower wavenumbers (1625, 1623, and 1617 cm−1 for FOS, GOS, and XOS films, respectively) with broadening of the band. The amide II band (1526 cm−1) shifted to a higher wavenumber (1541 cm−1 in all three films). Additionally, the intensities of both amide I and II bands were reduced in the FOS, GOS, and XOS films compared to WPC. These spectral changes suggest alterations in the secondary structure of WPC, potentially resulting from the reduction of free amino groups, glycation, or the formation of Maillard reaction products, such as Amadori compounds (C=O) and Schiff base intermediates (C=N) (Liu et al., 2021). Comparable findings have been reported in the literature. Gu et al. (2010) observed shifts and intensity reductions in the amide I and II bands of casein-glucose conjugates, which were attributed to Maillard reaction occurrence.

Similarly, Zhang et al. (2021) documented decreased intensity and band shifts in the spectra of WPC-flaxseed gum conjugates, consistent with glycation. Based on these parallels, the observed FTIR spectra of the thermosonicated films strongly suggest that the Maillard reaction and associated chemical interactions occurred between WPC and carbohydrates. Furthermore, all film samples displayed an intense spectral range between 1200 and 950 cm−1, corresponding to the fingerprint region of carbohydrates (Rana et al., 2023). This region exhibited lower intensity in the WPC spectrum due to the lower carbohydrate content in pure protein samples. In summary, the observed spectral shifts, reduced intensities, and characteristic carbohydrate and protein absorption bands suggest that thermosonication induced structural changes in the film-forming solution, likely involving chemical interactions such as glycation via the Maillard reaction.

Water vapor permeability

As shown in Table 2, the control film and the XOS film exhibited similar WVP values, measured at 8.59 × 10−2 ± 0.01 and 9.95 × 10−2 ± 0.01 g·mm·kPa−1·m−2·h−1, respectively. In contrast, the FOS and GOS films displayed significantly higher WVP values, at 1.19 × 10−1 ± 0.01 and 1.07 × 10−1 ± 0.01 g·mm·kPa−1·m−2·h−1, respectively (p-value = 0.002). These findings align with the microstructural observations (Fig. 8), where the FOS and GOS films exhibited visible cracks, facilitating the passage of water vapor through the material. Additionally, the higher WVP values in these films may also result from a non-uniform distribution of oil droplets within the matrix, as evidenced in the micrograph of the FOS film (Fig. 8) (Scartazzini et al., 2019).

The lower WVP of the XOS film compared to the FOS and GOS films may be partly related to the presence of residual phenolic compounds in the commercial XOS used in this study. In our previous work with the same XOS source (Urango, Neves, Meireles, & Silva, 2022), significantly higher total phenolic content was detected in XOS-containing formulations, regardless of processing conditions. Such phenolic constituents can interact with pectin and protein chains via hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, promoting additional crosslinking within the polymeric network. This structural reinforcement likely reduces the free volume and diffusion pathways available for water molecules, thereby improving the barrier properties and maintaining low WVP values.

Despite the increased WVP in the FOS and GOS films, all films in this study demonstrated superior water vapor barrier properties compared to other film matrices reported in the literature. For instance, pectin and gelatin composite films loaded with tangerine essential oil (0.3 % v/v) exhibited a WVP of 0.233 ± 0.001 g·mm·kPa−1·m−2·h−1 (Bhatia, Al-Harrasi, Ullah, et al., 2024), while pectin and xanthan gum films containing grapefruit essential oil (0.3 % v/v) showed a WVP of 0.617 ± 0.011 g·mm·kPa−1·m−2·h−1 (Bhatia, Al-Harrasi, Shah, et al., 2024). These results highlight the potential of the pectin-based edible films formulated in this study to act as effective water vapor barriers, particularly in applications requiring enhanced protection against moisture transfer.

Thermal stability

The thermal stability of the pectin-based edible films was assessed using TGA and its DTG over a temperature range of 25 to 600 °C (Fig. 12). The thermal decomposition data, including weight loss (Δw), maximum degradation temperature (Tmax), and residual mass at 600 °C (R), are summarized in Fig. 12.Fig. 12TGA profiles of pectin-based edible films composed of pectin, WPC and short-chain dietary fibers.Fig. 12

TGA profiles of pectin-based edible films composed of pectin, WPC and short-chain dietary fibers.

The DTG thermograms revealed two distinct transformation stages corresponding to the primary weight loss phases of the films. The first transformation stage (Δw1), occurring below 100 °C, was attributed to the loss of free and bound water. The Δw1 values were lower in the FOS, GOS, and XOS films compared to the control film, suggesting a reduced water content or stronger binding of water molecules in these formulations.

The second transformation stage (Δw2), representing the primary weight loss phase, accounted for 38 % to 49 % of the total weight loss and was associated with the degradation of molecular chains from WPC, citrus pectin, and prebiotics. The Δw2 values were also reduced in the FOS, GOS, and XOS films, indicating improved thermal stability. During this phase, the maximum degradation temperature (Tmax) was observed, reflecting the decomposition temperature threshold of the materials.

The Tmax increased from 219.57 °C in the control film to 225.27 °C, 224.32 °C, and 221.03 °C for FOS, GOS, and XOS films, respectively. This increase suggests that chemical bonds formed between pectin, WPC, and short-chain dietary fibers during the thermosonication process enhanced the thermal stability of the film matrix. Additionally, the residual mass (R) at 600 °C was higher in the FOS, GOS, and XOS films compared to the control film, further supporting their increased thermal resistance.

These findings indicate that the FOS, GOS, and XOS films exhibit greater thermal resistance, as evidenced by their reduced weight loss (Δw), higher Tmax, and increased residual mass. The enhanced thermal stability is likely due to the bonding between WPC and short-chain dietary fibers via the Maillard reaction during the thermosonication process, which strengthens the film network. Similar studies have reported that Maillard conjugation between amino groups and carbohydrates enhances the thermal resistance of films (Affes et al., 2022; Hamdi et al., 2020).

These findings suggest that the Maillard reaction and interactions between proteins and carbohydrates in the FOS, GOS, and XOS films maintained the overall mechanical properties of the films without compromising flexibility or stiffness.

Similar results have been reported in the literature. Affes et al. (2022) observed no significant effect on the mechanical properties of chitosan-based films cross-linked with chito-oligosaccharides through the Maillard reaction. Likewise, Kchaou et al. (2018) reported similar findings for fish gelatin and glucose-based films. However, it is important to note that the linkage between amine and carbonyl groups during the Maillard reaction can occasionally result in brittle films. For example, chitosan-based films cross-linked with glucose were found to be excessively fragile, making mechanical tests unfeasible (Affes et al., 2022).

These results highlight that while the Maillard reaction can influence the microstructure of edible films, its impact on mechanical properties depends on the specific film formulation and the extent of cross-linking.

Encapsulation efficiency

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) of the pectin-based edible films was evaluated by quantifying the geranylgeraniol content in the annatto seed oil (Fig. 14). The films exhibited EE values ranging from 70.07 ± 12.00 % to 76.72 ± 2.44 %. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences in the encapsulation performance of the FOS, GOS, and XOS films compared to the control film (p-value = 0.656). These findings indicate that the film matrix demonstrated a good encapsulation performance (≥ 70 %) for the annatto seed oil during the drying process, preventing its migration or loss. The results align with observations from the stability of the film-forming emulsions, droplet size distribution, and the uniform visual appearance of the final films. The ability of the matrix to encapsulate and stabilize the oil phase demonstrates the robustness of the film formulation and highlights its potential for use in applications requiring the retention of bioactive oil components.

Preservation of strawberries coated with pectin-based edible film containing annatto seed oil

Fig. 15 shows the visual appearance of strawberries during storage. Visible spoilage occurred on day 4 in the uncoated control group and on day 5 in samples coated with films without annatto seed oil. In contrast, strawberries coated with annatto seed oil films showed delayed deterioration, with visible spoilage only on day 7. This suggests that antimicrobial compounds in annatto seed oil inhibited microbial growth on the fruit surface. These qualitative observations are consistent with previous reports on the antimicrobial potential of annatto derivatives. Indeed, the presence of annatto seed oil effectively delayed deterioration and rotting processes, likely due to its antimicrobial and antioxidant activity, particularly from geranylgeraniol (Fleischer et al., 2003; Kar et al., 2022). It should be noted that this evaluation was based solely on visual inspection, which represents a limitation of the current study. All strawberries experienced noticeable shrinkage by day 14, indicating excessive weight loss. This was likely accelerated by the high air circulation in the incubator, which promoted water evaporation from the fruits.

Conclusion

This study developed citrus pectin-based edible films incorporating whey protein concentrate (WPC), short-chain dietary fibers (FOS, GOS, and XOS), and annatto seed oil, processed by thermosonication. Evidence from FTIR and intrinsic fluorescence suggested protein-carbohydrate interactions and possible Maillard-type modifications. However, SDS–PAGE did not confirm significant covalent conjugation, indicating that these reactions remain inconclusive in this multicomponent system. Functionally, films exhibited desirable properties, including high annatto oil retention (≥70 %), strong UV–Vis light barrier, and enhanced thermal stability in fiber-containing formulations. Coatings with annatto seed oil effectively delayed visible spoilage in strawberries, reinforcing the antimicrobial potential of oil constituents. These findings demonstrate the applicability of these films for food preservation. Further studies are needed to assess their microbiological and chemical safety during storage, which is critical for their application as active packaging.
