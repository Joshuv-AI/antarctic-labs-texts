Isolation, identification and optimization of biomass production of native Spirulina species from South of the Caspian Sea
Authors: Matin Shakoori, Reza Safari, Asieh Makhlough, Tahereh Bagheri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of the present study was to identify the native Spirulina species from Caspian Sea and optimize the growth conditions of this microalgae in order to reduce costs and increase the benefits of its production. In this study, water sampling was carried out from a depth of 5 meters on the coast of south Caspian Sea (Iran) in 2023–2024. After observing the Spirulina genus, its microalgae were isolated and purified using the dilution method, and finally pure single colonies were obtained. Initial identification was carried out using a light microscope and valid identification keys. Molecular identification and 16 SrRNA sequencing were used to confirm and verify it. The results showed that the studied species has the least genetic distance from the microalgae Spirulina laxissima. Then, the effect of three physical factors, temperature (27–29 °C), light (3000–3500 lx) and pH (9–11), on its biomass production rate was investigated using the response surface methodology. The results obtained from the design of this experiment showed that the biomass amount on the seventh day was 0.382 (g/L) with pH=10, light 3250 lx and temperature 29 °C and on the fourteenth day the biomass amount reached 0.839 (g/L) and pH=10, light 3250 lx, temperature 29 °C.•Isolation of microalgae Spirulina laxissima from Caspian Sea.•Molecular identification Spirulina laxissima.•Optimizing of Spirulina laxissima culture with physical changes (temperature, light, pH).Keywords: Spirulina, Purification, Biomass, Response surface methodology

Background

The large-scale cultivation of microalgae and the use of their biomass as a food source date back to the early 1950s. Since then, researchers in various countries have made significant progress in the production, utilization, and large-scale processing of algae [1]. Cyanobacteria, in particular, are widely used as food and nutritional supplements [[2], [3]]. Their coloration ranges from blue-green to red or purple, which is attributed to the pigments C-phycocyanin (blue) and C-phycoerythrin (red) that are embedded within chlorophyll-containing thylakoids [[4], [5]]. >30 species of Spirulina are known, and Spirulina platensis is widely used in various fields, especially in the food and pharmaceutical industries, due to its high nutritional value and medicinal applications [6].Most cyanobacteria produce the pigments phycocyanin and phycobilin, which in high concentrations give them a blue-green color [[7], [8]]. In 1964, Spirulina platensis was analyzed for its nutritional value and nutrient composition [[9], [10]], and studies on this microalgae by botanists, microbiologists, and scientists began [11]. Spirulina has multiple functions such as biological purification and wastewater treatment, replacing protein sources in fish diets, as well as improving growth rate and health status of fish, pigmentation, improving antioxidant capacity and immune system function in fish. Reports showed that Spirulina can be used to replace protein sources in the diets of Nile tilapia [12], ornamental guppy fish [13], rainbow trout [14] and farmed Huso huso [15]. Spirulina platensis can be included as a protein source up to 75 % in the diet without any negative effects on the growth of Nile tilapia [[16], [17]]. Furthermore, the algae in question can effectively replace fishmeal in the diets of catfish and rohu without any detrimental effect on growth [18]. Furthermore, the use of this algae in the diet has increased immune indices (serum bactericidal activities and lysozyme function and total serum IgM levels) and increased the resistance of fish against challenging bacterial pathogens [19]. Also, the use of this algae in the diet has increased pigmentation in ornamental fish such as Japanese ornamental carp [20], Kenyan cichlids [21]. Recent reports have shown that adding spirulina to the diet can enhance the antioxidant ability of Nile tilapia to combat environmental pollutants [22,23]. The effect of this algae in improving antioxidant capacity and transcription of genes related to the production of antioxidant compounds in rainbow trout has also been proven [24]. Since spirulina is a suitable alternative to animal-derived protein, its use in the diet of many fish, including parrot fish [25], farmed rainbow trout [26], rose goldfish [27], catfish [[28], [29]], carp [[30], [31], [32]], zebra cichlid [33] and ornamental guppy fish [[13], [34]] have been successfully used.

According to the studies conducted, the microalgae Spirulina platensis, which is a commercial species, has great value and importance in the aquaculture industry [35]. Providing nutrients for cultivation or import of this valuable species is a high cost [36]. In addition, the imported species may not be compatible with the environmental conditions of the region, so identifying the native species of Iran and preserving and maintaining them in genetic reserve collections in order to exploit their biological capacities is of particular importance and necessity. Also, accurate identification of native phytoplankton in each region can be the basis for further research on the species because the foundation of studying phytoplankton is based on accurate species identification. Considering the high nutritional value of the microalgae Spirulina and its numerous applications in industrial, agricultural, pharmaceutical, cosmetic and health dimensions [37], the need to identify its native species in the Caspian Sea and purify it seems necessary [38]. Several Spirulina species have been morphologically identified over the past years [9]; however, molecular analyses confirming and validating these identifications have not yet been conducted. In the present study, an attempt was made to identify the native Spirulina species from the southern Caspian Sea using morphological and molecular approaches and to introduce it as a potential candidate for further research. This effort aims to strengthen the microalgae production chain, promote applications in the food industry, and reduce production costs in animal, poultry, and aquaculture feed sectors.

Following the identification of the native Spirulina strain, research was carried out to determine the optimal conditions for sustainable and cost-effective growth while maintaining high nutritional value and metabolite content. The main objective of this study was to achieve maximum biomass production under optimized and economically feasible laboratory conditions. Considering that culture media are generally expensive and impose limitations on large-scale production, the experimental design focused on optimizing key physical factors, including light intensity, temperature, and pH. The process response was analyzed using Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to determine the most effective combination of these parameters [39].

Method details

Sampling

Sampling was carried out continuously from a depth of 5 m on the Farahabad-Sari coast in Mazandaran province, located at longitude (18°53′), latitude (57°53′). Sampling was carried out using a plankton net during the summer and midday hours (noon) when light radiation is maximum and phytoplankton migrate to the surface layers of the water for photosynthesis. Water samples were collected in glass bottles and transferred to the Algae Cultivation Laboratory of the Caspian Sea Ecology Research Institute.

In this study, Zarrouk's (1966) and modified Zarrouk culture media [40] were used.

Isolation and cultivation

Isolation and purification were performed by serial dilution methods with repeated liquid cultivation and the use of solid culture medium. In cases where the abundance of cells in the collected samples was low, 10 ml of the samples were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min (SIGMA 2–16 KL) for further accumulation, and eventually a greater number of cells were accumulated at the bottom of the test tube [41]. To prepare the solid culture medium, 1.5 g of agar in one liter of Zarouk culture medium was used [42]. After 14 days, the grown colonies were examined and transferred to a test tube containing liquid culture medium. Sample counting was performed using a mirrored Neobar slide and a Nikon light microscope with a magnification of 100 × . For the cultivation and propagation of Spirulina native to the Caspian Sea, Zarouk culture medium with a different formulation was used [40].

Given the increasing progress of molecular biology methods and the reliability and ease of identification by comparing conserved genes at the genus and species level of microalgae, it is appropriate to use molecular methods. For molecular evaluation and DNA extraction, two milliliters of algal suspension were taken and after homogenization, 400 microliters of STE buffer (pH=8, EDTA 1 mM, Tris-Hcl 10 mM, Nacl 100 mM) were added to it and the remaining extraction was performed by phenol-chloroform method. The quality and quantity of extracted DNA were evaluated using 1 % agarose gel electrophoresis. Polymerase chain reaction was performed to amplify the cytochrome oxidase I gene fragment [43]. The amplification of the 16srRNA gene was performed using a PCR device and specific round-trip primers (Table 2). The PCR device used in this study was a Termo Cycler Bio Rad T100n. Design of experiments for optimizing spirulina microalgae growth

In the present study, the response surface methodology (RSM), which is a set of mathematical and statistical techniques used in optimizing processes where the desired response is affected by a number of variables, has been used. With the help of this method, the number of experiments has been reduced and all the coefficients of the quadratic regression model and the interaction effects of the factors can be estimated.

Quantitative and qualitative study of extracted DNA

Quantitative evaluation of extracted DNA showed that its measurement at optical absorption of 260 to 280 nm was in the range of 1.8 to 2.2, which indicates the appropriate concentration of DNA for molecular studies. Evaluation of the quality of DNA extraction using agarose gel kinetic pattern showed that the DNA extracted by this method was suitable. After performing polymerase chain reaction, the amplified product of the 16 SrRNA gene was run on an agarose gel.The amplified length of the 16 SrRNA gene fragment was 1000 base pairs.

The sequences obtained from the amplified fragments were evaluated using the BLAST program on the NCBI website. The results of the phylogenetic tree analysis showed that the studied microalgae species was Spirulina laxissima with a probability of 99.9 % (Fig. 1).

The present study was conducted after the identification of the microalga Spirulina laxissima with the aim of optimizing the growth of this cyanobacterial species by changing the physical conditions of light, temperature and pH on a laboratory scale to produce maximum biomass and reduce costs. Retrospective studies showed that environmental conditions, concentration and type of chemical compounds used for the culture medium play a significant role in the production of cell mass and by using changes in environmental conditions and the amount of minerals used in the culture medium, more biomass can be produced [8]. In the present study, the amount of biomass on the seventh day was 0.382 (g/L) with pH=10, light 3250 lx and temperature 29 °C and the amount of biomass on the fourteenth day reached 0.839 (g/L).

Experts reported that growth conditions were influenced by two main criteria: environmental factors and habitat characteristics [12]. Colla [45] studied the effect of different temperatures on the growth of Spirulina platensis and stated that at 30 °C the biomass was (0.82–0.92 g/l) and at higher temperatures the amount of biomass decreased and the amount of protein, lipid and phycocyanin increased. They stated that in Spirulina the lower the cell concentration, the faster the growth rate and the decrease in cell concentration increases the penetration of light. Jerley and Prabu [46] reported 280 mg/l of Spirulina dry biomass in a study that used modified Zarrouk culture medium with pH=9 for a 9-day period of Spirulina platensis cultivation. Ismail et al. [47] stated that for the cultivation of Spirulina platensis from a specific Zarrouk culture medium with pH=9, 1320 mg/l of Spirulina dry mass was obtained after a 14-day period. Safari et al. [11] also reported 1120 mg of biomass production of this commercial strain using modified Zarrouk medium after 16 days. Optimization of cyanobacterial growth conditions is complex and depends on various factors such as medium composition, temperature, aeration, salinity, light intensity, and pH, each of which can be either growth-stimulating or growth-inhibiting. For example, aeration stimulates the medium and causes homogeneous distribution of Spirulina filaments in the culture system for adequate light exposure [48]. Also, if aeration is excessive, it causes shear stress and can reduce biomass production due to cell damage and have the opposite effect [49]. Hosseinpour Azad and Fathalipour [50]. identified and genetically studied the microalgae Spirulina laxissima on the southern coast of the Caspian Sea and stated that the higher the number of modified nucleotides, the greater the effect of environmental factors and mutations on the desired sequence during evolution, and that the higher the number of modified nucleotides, the lower the similarity of the desired gene. Paknejadi et al․ [10] studied the growth of Spirulina major and observed the highest growth rate at pH=7.5 and stated that the acidity of the soil or water is the selective factor for changing the microalgae population and that the response of cyanobacteria to pH changes can be different. The results obtained from the present study also showed the highest biomass at pH=10.

The difference in the amount of biomass in different studies could be due to the type of species, continuous changes in the dominance of photosynthesis, respiration, and cultivation conditions. In general, it can be concluded that Spirulina laxissima can be used as a suitable alternative to the commercial species of Spirulina due to its compatibility with the culture medium used and its favorable growth rate. Considering the many applications of the microalgae Spirulina, it is suggested that other responses such as examining the amount of protein, lipid, chlorophyll, and phycocyanin of this species under different culture conditions be investigated. Social, economic, and environmental factors must be considered in natural. resource use planning for sustainable development in order to break free from the curse of natural resources [51].

Method validation

In this study, response surface methodology (RSM) was utilized to verify the reliability of the experimental design and to optimize the cultural parameters influencing biomass accumulation in the native Spirulina strain collected from the southern region of the Caspian Sea. This methodology integrates statistical modeling with mathematical analysis to quantify the combined effects of several independent factors on the response variable. The experimental data were fitted to a second-order regression model, which was used to predict the response and identify potential interaction effects among the tested variables of three physical factors temperature, light, and pH . The performance of the model was statistically validated through analysis of variance (ANOVA), coefficient of determination (R²), and examination of residual plots. The random distribution of residuals and the insignificant lack of fit values verified that the model accurately represents the experimental system [24]. Employing RSM also decreased the total number of experimental runs required, thereby reducing analytical costs and time without compromising predictive precision. Consequently, the validation outcomes confirmed that the developed RSM model is statistically consistent, experimentally sound, and suitable for predicting the optimal culture conditions for enhanced Spirulina biomass production.

Conclusions

In general, it can be concluded that Spirulina laxissima can serve as a suitable alternative to commercial Spirulina species due to its compatibility with the culture medium and its favorable growth rate. Given the numerous applications of Spirulina microalgae, it is recommended that additional responses such as the amounts of protein, lipids, chlorophyll, and phycocyanin in the native Caspian Sea species be investigated under different cultivation conditions.
