Using AI to forecast student dropout risk in technical education using a learning analytics approach
Authors: Olga Ovtšarenko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The integration of artificial intelligence and learning analytics in higher education creates new opportunities for adaptive curriculum design and evidence-based decision-making. This study investigates how predictive analytics can improve e-course development, detect early signs of students at risk of dropping out, and support timely pedagogical interventions. Using data from six CAD course logs delivered via Moodle at TTK University of Applied Sciences (Estonia) during the 2024–2025 academic year (99,104 activity records from 154 students), a machine learning model was developed to identify students at risk of academic failure. The proposed weighted attribute method combines key metrics—engagement, learning difficulty, and time allocation—to standardise behavioural and cognitive factors for outcome prediction. Logistic regression and decision tree classifiers were trained and assessed. The findings show that predictive models can identify at-risk students, allowing early alerts and adaptive support through ongoing monitoring. The framework turns learning analytics into actionable insights for educators, enabling proactive measures. The study concludes that AI-driven analytics, developed within ethical and pedagogically sound frameworks, can enhance transparency and foster equitable learning in higher education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44919-1.

Introduction

The rapid progress in research on learning analytics (LA) and artificial intelligence in education (AI) has resulted in a wide array of tools, frameworks, and empirical data. In this review, these topics focus on key areas central to modern AI research: early warning and predictive systems, adaptive and diagnostic learning models, human-AI collaboration in learning design, and responsible and equitable AI deployment.

Early warning systems and predictive learning analytics

The primary focus of AI research is on identifying at-risk students and making predictions related to academic performance, engagement, or dropout. Systematic reviews consistently show that supervised machine learning dominates this field—for example, Ref1. reports that 88% of studies use supervised predictive models to forecast outcomes. Similarly, Ref2. highlight the central role of machine learning and new generative artificial intelligence methods in recognising engagement patterns and predicting dropout.

Empirical research in this area increasingly stresses the importance of interpretability and actionable insights. Traditional predictive pipelines often act as “black box” systems, making it difficult for educators to translate risk assessments into pedagogically meaningful interventions. This issue drives methodological advances such as the weighted attribute method (WAM)3, presented in this study, which offers transparency by revealing the relative importance of individual activities and interactions within a course. Unlike purely predictive models, WAM provides a structural understanding of e-course components, enabling educators to engage not only at the learner level but also at the level of curriculum and event design.

Adaptive and diagnostic modelling for personalised learning

Beyond prediction, research is increasingly focusing on adaptive systems that customise learning to individual needs. The multi-layered framework by4 illustrates how analytics can be integrated into the design cycle to support personalised learning decision-making. Similarly, Ref5. demonstrate how analytics-driven design can reveal learners’ cognitive pathways, enabling more adaptable course structures.

Recent progress in AI-enabled diagnostic modelling and adaptivity further advances this work. Reference6 show how GPT-4-based analytics tools can classify learner engagement, map learning progress using Bloom’s taxonomy, and assist in generating adaptive feedback. Newer research introduces AI-based multi-agent educational systems where individual agents collaborate to simulate the roles of instructor, analyst, and student support, providing a richer, adaptive ecosystem for learners. These systems indicate a new research frontier, where diagnostic modelling becomes dynamic, continuously updated based on real-time student data, instructor feedback loops, and AI-generated reasoning7.

Nonetheless, despite these advancements, many models remain limited in capturing course-level structural relationships, such as the interactions of various tools, tasks, and learning objects in shaping outcomes. The WAM approach helps bridge this gap by modelling weighted attributes of course components, offering an additional diagnostic perspective that informs instructional design, not just student adaptation.

Human–AI collaboration and instructional analytics

A growing body of research explores how analytics and AI can support, rather than replace, educators. Research by8 on curriculum analytics and9 on instructional strategy optimisation highlight AI’s potential to empower teachers to redesign curricula. Generative AI has further expanded collaboration possibilities: Ref10. show how GenAI can analyse unstructured student artefacts, generate synthetic data to improve models, and provide explanations about student behaviour.

The empirical results of11 further widen this perspective. Their engagement mapping systems exemplify AI-enabled pedagogical decision-making, where the teacher remains central, interpreting analytics and coordinating interventions. These studies demonstrate that AI is most effective when seen as a collaborative partner, complementing human pedagogical decisions rather than replacing them.

This focus aligns with broader calls for human-centred AI. Reference12 emphasise themes of equity, inclusion, cognitive development, and social sustainability in educational informatics, insisting that technological innovations should enhance, not undermine, the human aspects of learning.

Responsible, ethical, and equitable AI implementation

Scaling AI-enabled learning raises important questions about privacy, autonomy, fairness, and cultural responsiveness. Earlier seminal studies13 drew attention to these issues, but contemporary research considers them within the broader context of ethical digital transformation. Reference14 make a significant empirical and conceptual contribution by exploring how institutions can implement AI tools while ensuring fairness, cultural adaptation, and alignment with local educational values. Their work emphasises that responsible AI implementation requires co-design with teachers and students, transparency in algorithmic decision-making processes, and a commitment to reducing, rather than exacerbating, educational inequalities. Similarly, Ref15. warn that even highly effective ML models, such as ensemble learners, can unintentionally produce biased or opaque results without robust ethical safeguards. Research in exploratory learning16 and assessment analytics17 supports the need to create analytics systems that preserve learner agency and support reflective learning rather than observation-based assessment.

These arguments converge on a central conclusion: the future of artificial intelligence (AI) depends not only on technological advances but also on incorporating responsible, human-centred values into the design and implementation of analytics systems18.

Along these lines, the literature reveals a rapidly evolving field in which predictive modelling, adaptive learning, collaborative AI tools, and responsible analytics frameworks are becoming increasingly integrated. However, gaps remain, particularly in the ability to link course-level structural analysis with learner-level predictions and translate data into pedagogically applicable insights. The WAM method proposed in this study directly addresses these shortcomings, offering an interpretable, design-oriented framework that facilitates more effective curriculum development, early identification of ineffective course elements, and more informed instructional decision-making.

This paper analyses empirical results from structured data and student activity logs in e-learning on the Moodle platform. It explores how modern technologies can enhance curriculum development and teaching strategies for student-chosen subjects to reduce student attrition and boost academic performance.

Methods

This study employed a quantitative approach to analyse data on student interactions with Moodle-based CAD courses and to develop a predictive model capable of identifying students at risk of academic failure. The methodological process encompassed data extraction, preprocessing, feature engineering, model training, and performance evaluation.

Data source

The dataset included 12,941 Moodle activity logs from 80 students enrolled in two elective CAD courses (courses 131 and 138, academic year 2024–2025). These courses showed the highest attrition rates within their programme (6/20 and 21/60 students, respectively), making them suitable for developing a predictive early warning model.

Because the dataset included 80 students from two CAD design courses at a single institution, the machine learning results should be viewed as preliminary. Constraints of a small sample increase variance in tree-based models and raise the risk of overfitting, which may partly explain the unusually high performance metrics observed. The behavioural specificity of CAD coursework further limits the generalisability of the findings outside this context.

These weighted attributes allowed the model to reflect qualitative learning design principles, such as distinguishing between highly complex resources, low-engagement tasks, or tasks requiring significant planned learning time. This structure directly links analytics to instructional decision-making (e.g., content redesign, adjusting instructional pacing, or implementing targeted interventions).

Missing numerical values were imputed using column medians to reduce outlier bias, while missing categories (e.g., activity types) were imputed using the mode. Categorical activity features were converted to one-hot vectors, enabling machine learning models to capture qualitative interaction patterns.

A total of 12,941 Moodle activity logs from 80 students were extracted, cleaned, and anonymised prior to analysis. A structured data quality audit examined missing data (in columns with total e-course data), anomalous logs, and differences between cohorts. Missing values primarily occurred in resources with low levels of student engagement. Diagnostics revealed that the missing data followed a “Missing at Random” (MAR) pattern or involved the omission of specific resources linked to pedagogically relevant behaviour. Numerical features were imputed using the median, and categorical features were imputed using the mode. Checks were conducted to remove corrupted, duplicate, or implausible log entries. Potential outliers in log counts and time spent on tasks were investigated using time sequence rules. Entries related to rapidly recurring system events or excessively long periods of downtime were removed, maintaining plausible engagement. Since the dataset included both full-time and part-time students, their engagement patterns were compared. Although part-time students showed more irregular time activity, the differences between cohorts did not significantly affect feature distribution or model performance. Consequently, cohort type was retained as a feature rather than used to build separate models, ensuring a balanced split between the training and test sets. The study examined 49 different learning resources from two CAD design courses. Each resource was characterised using weighted parameters derived from the weighted attribute method (WAM), including cognitive level (L_resource, i), difficulty weight (D_resource, i), logarithm-based engagement weights (T_resource, i and W_resource, i), and time metrics. The normalised success rate (success_norm) was calculated using equation (Appendix, 5), incorporating the difficulty, scheduled time, and engagement weights. To support supervised modelling, a binary target variable (success_binary) was created by median-splitting the normalised success values, where 1 indicates higher tool effectiveness.

The initial analysis investigated relationships between resource-level attributes, activity categories, and performance metrics. For each activity type, summary data were compiled to evaluate how difficulty, time allocation, and engagement patterns varied across course components. The descriptive results then formed the foundation for identifying potential predictors for machine learning models.

Cross-validation procedure

The model was estimated using stratified k-fold cross-validation instead of a single split into training and test sets. Stratification maintained the class distribution (e.g., at-risk and not-at-risk students) across folds, reducing selection bias. This approach allows all observations to contribute to both training and validation, increasing robustness to small sample sizes and providing a more reliable estimate of generalisation ability.

The study utilised anonymised data from the TTK UAS Moodle log in line with the institution’s policy, which allows the use of such data for research purposes with proper encryption and anonymisation as secondary, anonymized educational rather than direct human experimentation20,21, including anonymisation, secure storage, and data minimisation. Results

Analysis of the 49 learning resources revealed significant variation in difficulty levels, planned time allocation, and learner interaction patterns. Aggregated activity summaries indicated that complex, multi-step tasks such as homework assignments, modelling exercises, and test-related activities showed the highest mean success_norm values. These resources were generally characterised by higher difficulty weights, elevated cognitive levels, and longer planned engagement times. Conversely, introductory materials, registration items, and simple overviews demonstrated notably lower effectiveness scores, aligning with their reduced cognitive and temporal demands.

Correlation analysis confirmed strong relationships among the weighted WAM parameters and success_norm, reflecting the internal coherence of the weighting framework. Time-based and difficulty-related attributes showed the strongest associations with effectiveness, while log-based features had more moderate correlations.

Two supervised models—Logistic Regression and Random Forest—were trained to predict the binary success measure (success_binary). Performance was evaluated using five-fold stratified cross-validation. The Random Forest model achieved the highest cross-validated performance (accuracy = 0.8756; F1 = 0.8267), while Logistic Regression produced slightly lower but consistent scores. Importantly, none of the cross-validation metrics approached perfect performance, indicating that both models generalised reasonably within the constraints of the small dataset. The modest variation across folds suggests stability in model behaviour despite the limited sample size. Logistic Regression performance remained relatively consistent across partitions, while the Random Forest showed slightly higher variability, reflecting its sensitivity to small-sample fluctuations.

When the models were tested on the initially designated hold-out set, all performed with perfect accuracy and F1 scores (1.0000). These results sharply contrasted with the cross-validation findings and suggested possible overfitting, data leakage, or an unrepresentative test split. This prompted further investigation.

Testing on a separately prepared version of the test set yielded more realistic results. Logistic Regression achieved 0.800 accuracy and 0.750 F1, whereas the Random Forest achieved 0.700 accuracy and 0.571 F1. These outcomes aligned more closely with the cross-validated expectations, indicating that the initial perfect scores might have been affected by preprocessing inconsistencies or unintended data overlap between training and test sets.

As a result, cross-validation metrics are considered the main indicators of model performance. The study explicitly states the importance of future validation with a completely independent cohort from another academic year or course iteration.

Feature importance analyses provided consistent insights across models. Logistic Regression coefficients (standardised) highlighted level_scaled, difficulty_weight, and time-related features (time, time_weight_norm) as the strongest positive predictors of resource effectiveness. Random Forest feature importances reflected a similar hierarchy, with time-based and difficulty features dominating the model’s decision structure, followed by cognitive level and log-based attributes. These convergent patterns suggest that resources requiring deeper cognitive engagement and sustained time investment are most strongly associated with higher effectiveness. Log-derived engagement measures contributed meaningfully but less prominently, indicating that the quality and complexity of tasks—not merely the volume of interaction—are critical drivers of success.

Discussion

This study investigated the structure and behavioural patterns within an e-learning environment using the weighted attribute method (WAM) to assess resource-level effectiveness and identify activity features most closely linked to successful learning outcomes. The analysis shows that resource complexity, cognitive level, and planned time allocation are the main factors influencing tool effectiveness, while engagement metrics based on logs play a secondary but important role. These findings support the pedagogical idea that deeper cognitive engagement and sustained participation in tasks foster more effective learning.

Machine learning models, specifically Logistic Regression and Random Forest, provided interpretable and generally consistent insights into the influence of attributes derived from the WAM method. Cross-validation results confirmed that both models captured key structural patterns in the dataset, with the Random Forest showing the highest average performance and Logistic Regression offering stable, well-regularised predictions. The agreement in feature importance across methods increases confidence in the conceptual validity of weighted attributes and suggests that the WAM framework may be a useful tool for analysing e-learning design components.

However, the notable discrepancy between the cross-validation results and the initially observed ideal performance on the validation test set highlights the difficulties involved in modelling small, homogeneous datasets. The ideal accuracy and F1 scores seen in several models suggest potential overfitting or artefacts of the methodology, prompting further examination of the data preprocessing and the representativeness of the test set. More realistic performance on the processed test set implies that the initial ideal results did not truly indicate generalisability and should not be taken as evidence of complete model accuracy. Therefore, this study prioritises cross-validation metrics as the main performance indicators, and the modelling is exploratory rather than definitive. From a learning standpoint, the findings stress the importance of designing courses that challenge students cognitively and involve time-consuming activities, such as complex modelling tasks, multi-step assignments, and practical assessments. These resources consistently showed association with higher weighted effectiveness and emerged as the strongest predictors in machine learning models. Lower-level or administrative resources remain necessary but should serve as supporting tools rather than central elements of learning. Collectively, the results demonstrate how data analytics can support the development of more effective, learner-centred e-learning environments.

Conclusion

This study applied the weighted attribute method (WAM) to analyse resource characteristics in two CAD-based e-learning courses and to identify the factors most closely associated with the effectiveness of the learning tools. By integrating complexity, cognitive level, engagement metrics, and temporal attributes into a single analytical framework, the study demonstrates that complex, cognitively challenging, and time-consuming activities make the most significant contribution to learner success. These results offer practical recommendations for designing e-learning environments, emphasising the importance of aligning learning resources with higher-order learning goals and providing adequate temporal support.

The machine learning models employed in this study further confirmed the central role of these weighted attributes, with cross-validation results demonstrating robust predictive patterns despite the small data set. Although the initial perfect performance on the validation test set underscored the risks of overfitting on limited samples, subsequent analysis revealed that cross-validation offers a more reliable assessment of model behaviour. Therefore, the study adopts an exploratory modelling approach, and its results are considered preliminary but informative.

The study contributes to the expanding body of work in learning analytics by demonstrating how structured weighting methods and machine learning outcomes can be combined to evaluate and enhance digital learning design. The limitations—specifically, the small dataset collected from a single institution and the absence of external validation—highlight the need for broader research involving multiple student groups. Future work should extend this approach to different disciplinary contexts, incorporate longitudinal data, and test the models on independent student populations to develop a more generalisable and empirically robust framework. Through such advancements, analytics-driven methods like WAM have the potential to support more adaptive, effective, and evidence-based e-learning systems.
