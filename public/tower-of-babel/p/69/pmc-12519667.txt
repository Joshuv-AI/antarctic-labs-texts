Brain network predictors of changes in symptoms and serum BDNF following antidepressant treatment with escitalopram and Yueju Pill in major depressive disorder: a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot study
Authors: Yuxuan Zhang, Yiwei Ren, Gang Chen, Haosen Wang, Jinlin Miao, Bo Cui, Zhilu Zou, Jin Feng, Chunkou Hong, Mingzhi Han, Jinhui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYueju Pill, a classic traditional Chinese medicine, shows antidepressant effects rapidly. However, biomarkers that can predict its treatment outcomes in major depressive disorder (MDD) are still lacking. Multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers a promising avenue to identify such biomarkers.AimsThis pilot study aimed to explore whether therapeutic responses to Yueju Pill could be predicted by MRI-derived brain networks and to identify drug-specific biomarkers in comparison to escitalopram, a mainstream antidepressant.MethodsWe collected multimodal MRI data and blood samples from 28 outpatients with MDD from the Fourth People’s Hospital of Taizhou, who were randomly divided into two groups to receive either Yueju Pill (23 g/time/day) or escitalopram (10 mg, two times a day) for 4 days. Morphological and functional brain networks were constructed and used to predict individual changes in symptoms quantified by the 24-item Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD-24) scores and serum brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels.ResultsAfter the treatment, both groups exhibited significant reductions in the HAMD-24 scores, while only the Yueju Pill group showed significant increases in the BDNF levels. Gyrification Index-based morphological networks predicted change rates of the HAMD-24 scores in both groups, but sulcus depth-based and cortical thickness-based morphological networks predicted change rates of the HAMD-24 scores and BDNF levels, respectively, only in the Yueju Pill group. Subnetwork analyses revealed that the visual network independently predicted the changes in both the HAMD-24 scores (sulcus depth-based networks) and BDNF levels (cortical thickness-based networks) following Yueju Pill treatment.ConclusionsMorphological but not functional brain networks can predict symptom improvement and BDNF changes of patients with MDD after Yueju Pill treatment. Sulcus depth-based and cortical thickness-based morphological brain networks, particularly their visual subnetworks, might serve as Yueju Pill-specific biomarkers for predicting the therapeutic responses. These findings have the potential to guide personalised therapy for patients with MDD early in the therapeutic process.Trial registration numberChiCTR1900021114.Keywords: Depression, Controlled Clinical Trials as Topic

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a serious mental health condition projected to be the most prevalent and economically burdensome illness globally by 2030.1 Despite significant progress in the pharmacological treatment of MDD, nearly one-third of patients do not respond to their initial antidepressant.2 A substantial number of these patients require either an augmentation or a switch to an alternative medication. However, currently there is a lack of objective guidelines for tailoring individualised antidepressant drug therapy. Accordingly, pursuing biomarkers for predicting antidepressant treatment outcomes is of great clinical significance for timely and effective therapeutic intervention for patients with MDD.

In China, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has been widely used for the treatment of MDD. An increasing number of studies have demonstrated the efficacy of a variety of multiherbal medicines to treat MDD.3 Among numerous multiherbal medicines, Yueju Pill has been popularly prescribed in China for the alleviation of depression-like symptoms.4 Yueju Pill was formulated 800 years ago by a famous Chinese medicine doctor, Zhu Danxi, who proposed a theory of ‘Six patterns of Stagnation’ to explicate the multifactorial aetiology of depression-related disorders. According to this theory, Yueju Pill is thought to alleviate depression-like symptoms by resolving the six stagnation patterns through a liver-soothing mechanism. Recently, Yueju Pill has been further demonstrated to have rapid-onset antidepressant effects in both clinical settings and preclinical models,5,9 and several key target and effective compounds responsible for the rapid antidepressant action have been identified.6 10 11 Nevertheless, it should be noted that the treatment outcomes of Yueju Pill vary considerably among patients. To date, there have been no studies examining whether the clinical outcomes of Yueju Pill treatment can be predicted before the treatment is initiated. Identifying biomarkers for predicting the therapeutic efficacy of Yueju Pill can help screen patients with MDD who are most likely to benefit from this particular antidepressant drug. Furthermore, considering the diverse array of antidepressant drugs currently available, each with its unique mechanism of action, it is crucial to determine whether the biomarkers are specific to Yueju Pill in comparison to other antidepressant drugs. Escitalopram, renowned for its high selectivity as a serotonin reuptake inhibitor, ranks among the most frequently prescribed antidepressants worldwide. Thus, escitalopram provides a good initial choice to explore predictive biomarkers specific to Yueju Pill.

Multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) technologies allow characterising brain structure and function in vivo. They may offer a promising avenue for identifying potential biomarkers predictive of therapeutic responses of patients with MDD to Yueju Pill. Leveraging these technologies, a growing number of studies have supported that MDD can be viewed as a brain network disorder.12 13 For example, our previous studies found that the topological organisation of both morphological and functional brain networks was disrupted in patients with MDD.14,18 Moreover, network disruptions in MDD can be normalised by antidepressant treatment,17 19 and antidepressant therapeutic responses can be predicted by pretreatment brain network features.20 Based on these previous findings, we hypothesise that multimodal MRI-derived brain networks are able to predict the therapeutic efficacy of Yueju Pill.

In this randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study, we aimed to explore shared and distinct brain network predictors for symptom improvement (ie, changes in the 24-item Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD-24) scores) of patients with MDD following antidepressant treatment with Yueju Pill and escitalopram. Given that Yueju Pill can rapidly improve depression-like symptoms even after a few days of treatment,7 we were particularly concerned with predicting short-term symptom improvement. It should be noted that serum brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels are closely linked with the pathophysiology of depression21 and play a vital role in the antidepressant action of Yueju Pill.6 Thus, we also examined the prediction ability of multimodal brain networks for changes in the BDNF levels in addition to the symptom improvement following the treatment.

Methods

Participants

A total of 46 outpatients with MDD from the Fourth People’s Hospital of Taizhou were enrolled between March 2017 and January 2018, among whom 28 were included in this study. Diagnoses were made based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-IV criteria for MDD. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 18 and 75 years; (2) absence of other psychiatric disorders; (3) no prior use of psychotropic medications; (4) HAMD-24 scores of 20 or above; (5) no antidepressant treatment within 1 week before the MRI scan; and (6) emmetropic vision and right-handedness. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) active suicidal ideation; (2) current pregnancy or lactation; (3) severe systemic diseases or significant organic brain disorders; (4) current alcohol abuse or dependence; (5) impaired comprehension due to cognitive/linguistic deficits or education below secondary level; and (6) MRI contraindications (eg, claustrophobia or the presence of cardiac pacemakers, metal implants or other incompatible devices). The patients were randomly divided into two groups of equal size (n=14) for a 4-day antidepressant treatment. One group received Yueju Pill and a placebo for escitalopram (Yueju Pill group), while the other received escitalopram and an herbal placebo (escitalopram group). HAMD-24 scores and peripheral blood samples for BDNF levels were collected from each patient before and after treatment, while multimodal MRI data were obtained pretreatment. Notably, for all prediction analyses based on functional brain networks, three patients in the Yueju Pill group and one patient in the escitalopram group were excluded after image quality control procedures for the resting-state functional MRI data. Randomisation

Each patient included in this study was first assigned a number based on the order in which the patients arrived at the Fourth People’s Hospital of Taizhou. The numbers and their corresponding patients were then randomly divided into two groups (ie, the Yueju Pill group and the escitalopram group) using a random-number chart. All numbers were packed in opaque envelopes, and both the patients and researchers were blind to the group assignment.

Drugs and treatments

The over-the-counter Yueju Pills were produced by Jiangsu 707 Natural Pharmaceutical (lot number 150801), consisting of ingredients such as Cyperus rotundus L. (Xiang Fu), Ligusticum chuanxiong Hort. (Chuan Xiong), Gardenia jasminoides J.Ellis. (Zhi Zi), Atractylodes lancea (Thunb.) DC. (Cang Zhu) and Massa Fermentata (Shen Qu). Escitalopram was manufactured by Yamagata Pharma (National Medicine Standard H20080599). Patients in the Yueju Pill group were administered a high dose of Yueju Pill (23 g/time/day)—capable of eliciting antidepressant responses7—along with an escitalopram placebo; both of these were consumed once daily post-dinner. Conversely, those in the escitalopram group were given escitalopram (10 mg, two times a day), accompanied by a Yueju placebo. The placebo was designed to be indistinguishable from the actual medications in terms of shape, size, colour, packaging and boxing.

Blood collection and BDNF analysis

Peripheral blood extractions were performed between 08:00 and 10:00 to minimise the potential effects of circadian rhythm variations. All blood samples were collected into coagulation-promoting tubes. Patients were not exposed to drugs known to affect the coagulation system within 10 days before the blood extraction. Following the collection, the blood samples were immediately centrifuged at 1000×g for 20 min. The resulting serum samples were then stored at −80 °C until subsequent analysis. BDNF levels were determined using an anti-BDNF enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Boster Biological Technology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the ELISA, the serum was diluted (1:20) using a sample buffer and tested in duplicate. BDNF standard solution was diluted to concentrations from 0 to 4000 pg/mL to create the standard curve for BDNF levels.

Multimodal MRI data acquisition

All MRI scans were conducted on a 1.5T GE Brivo MR355 scanner equipped with an 8-channel head coil. High-resolution structural images were obtained for each participant using a three-dimensional T1-weighted magnetisation-prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo sequence with the following parameters: 208 sagittal slices; repetition time (TR)=11.2 ms; echo time (TE)=4.6 ms; flip angle (FA)=12°; field of view (FOV)=240 × 240 mm2; matrix=256 × 256; and slice thickness=1.4 mm without interslice gap. During the resting-state functional MRI scanning, participants were instructed to relax, stay still and awake, keep their eyes closed and avoid thinking about anything in particular. The functional images comprised 150 contiguous echo-planar imaging volumes acquired with the following parameters: 30 axial slices; TR=3000 ms; TE=40 ms; FA=90°; FOV=240 × 240 mm2; matrix=64 × 64; and slice thickness=5.0 mm with a 0.7 mm gap between slices.

Structural MRI data preprocessing

All structural images were preprocessed using a standard pipeline in the CAT12 toolbox (http://www.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat/) based on the SPM12 package (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/). The CAT12 offers a fast and reliable approach for the analysis of cerebral surface-based morphometry without the need for extensive reconstruction of the cortical surface. Specifically, individual structural images were first segmented into grey matter, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid. Then, estimation of cortical thickness (CT) and reconstruction of the central surface were performed based on a projection-based thickness method.22 This method could handle the partial volume information, sulcal blurring and sulcal asymmetries. In addition to CT, CAT12 allows estimation of three other morphological indices of fractal dimension (FD), Gyrification Index (GI) and sulcus depth (SD). Briefly, FD was characterised by the slope of the log plot of surface area with respect to the maximum l-value, where the maximum l-value is a measure of the frequency bandwidth used to reconstruct the surface shape; GI was defined as the absolute mean curvature; and SD was quantified as the Euclidean distance between the central surface and its convex hull. All calculations of CT, FD, GI and SD were performed in the subject’s native surface space. Thus, the resultant maps were further resampled into the common fsaverage template and subsequently smoothed using a Gaussian kernel. According to the CAT12 manual, individual CT maps were smoothed using a 12 mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel, while the other maps were smoothed using a 25 mm full width at half maximum Gaussian kernel. The usage of larger smoothing kernel sizes for the FD, GI and SD maps is due to the underlying nature of these folding measures that reflect contributions from both sulci and gyri. Consequently, the smoothing kernel size should exceed the distance between a gyral crown and a sulcal fundus.

Resting-state functional MRI data preprocessing

Resting-state functional MRI data preprocessing was performed using the GRETNA toolbox23 based on the SPM12 package (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/). First, the first five volumes were removed to allow for T1 equilibration effects. Then, a 6-parameter rigid-body transformation was used to correct for head motion. Three patients in the Yueju Pill group and one patient in the escitalopram group were excluded due to excessive head motion in terms of the criteria of displacement>3 mm or mean framewise displacement>0.5 mm. Subsequently, the corrected functional images were subjected to band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz) and nuisance regression (Friston 24-parameter head motion profiles, white matter signals, cerebrospinal fluid signals and global signals) in a single linear model to avoid reintroducing artefacts.24 The white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals were calculated within masks derived from corresponding prior probability maps released in the SPM12 package (threshold=0.9). The global signals were calculated within a prior brain mask released in the SPM12 package. Finally, the functional images were normalised into the standard Montreal Neurological Institute space using deformation fields derived from tissue segmentation of individual structural images and further resampled to a 3 mm isotropic resolution.

Construction of single-subject morphological brain networks

As in our previous studies,25 26 we constructed four single-subject morphological brain networks for each participant: CT-based network (CTN), FD-based network, GI-based network (GIN) and SD-based network (SDN). To define network nodes, we employed a gradient-weighted Markov Random Field (gwMRF) parcellation atlas27 to divide the cerebral cortex into 400 regions of interest (ROIs). This atlas is widely used in previous brain network studies to define network nodes. The ROIs can be categorised into seven subnetworks: visual network (VN), somatomotor network, dorsal attention network, ventral attention network (VAN), limbic network (LN), frontoparietal network and default network. For each morphological feature of each ROI in the gwMRF parcellation atlas, a probability density function was first derived using kernel density estimation (MATLAB function: ksdensity). Construction of functional brain networks

To construct large-scale functional brain networks, we first extracted the mean time series for each ROI in the gwMRF parcellation atlas by averaging the functional signals across all voxels within the ROI. The resultant mean time series were then correlated with each other to generate a 400×400 functional connectivity matrix for each participant.

Network analysis

Threshold selection: For each matrix (morphological similarity and functional connectivity) derived above, we employed a sparsity-based thresholding approach to transform it into a series of binary networks. Sparsity is defined as the ratio of the number of actual edges divided by the maximum possible number of edges in a network. Consequently, the sparsity-based thresholding approach ensures the same number of edges or cost across participants for the resulting binary networks. Given the absence of a universally accepted method to determine a single sparsity, we adopted a consecutive sparsity range from 0.02 to 0.04, with an interval of 0.02. This range was chosen to guarantee that the resulting binary networks were estimable for small-world organisation and had sparse properties.

Network parameter calculation: For each binary network, we computed five nodal centrality metrics: degree (ki), efficiency (ei), betweenness (bi), eigenvector (vi) and pagerank (pri). These metrics measure the roles of individual nodes from different perspectives. For instance, nodal efficiency quantifies the ability of a node to propagate information to other nodes in a network. Details on the calculation and interpretation of the centrality metrics can be found elsewhere.28 Since all nodal centrality metrics were computed as functions of sparsity, we computed the area under the curve (AUC; ie, the integral over the entire sparsity range) for each nodal centrality metric of each ROI. The resultant AUC values of each nodal centrality metric were used for subsequent prediction.

Kernel regression

Within each treatment group, we used kernel regression to predict individual symptom improvement and changes in the BDNF levels, which were defined as the change rates of the HAMD-24 scores (ie, (HAMD-24Post-treatment − HAMD-24Baseline)/HAMD-24Baseline) and the change rates of BDNF levels (ie, (BDNFPost-treatment − BDNFBaseline)/BDNFBaseline), respectively, after the treatment. Kernel regression assumes that individuals with similar cortical organisation will exhibit similar behaviour. We used the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) to assess the performance of the kernel regression. To reduce overfitting, an L2-regularisation term was included in each test fold. The level of this L2-regularisation was controlled by the hyperparameter, λ. To optimise the λ, an internal LOOCV process was executed. Specifically, for each inner training set, a grid search method was applied, spanning a sequence of 100 logarithmically spaced values of λ. These values ranged from 10−5/N to 105/N, where N was the number of subjects in the inner training set. The optimal λ, as determined by the internal LOOCV, was subsequently used to predict the symptom improvement and changes in the BDNF levels in the external test fold. After repeating these procedures for each external test fold, we computed the Pearson correlation and mean absolute error (MAE) between the predicted and actual symptom improvement or changes in the BDNF levels across the entire patient cohort to quantify the prediction accuracy.

To explore the contribution of each brain region to the performance of the kernel regression, we used the Haufe transformation to yield a positive (or negative) weight for each brain region, indicating that a higher strength of a brain region was associated with predicting a greater (or smaller) change in the HAMD-24 score or BDNF level. To investigate possible differential contributions of various subnetworks to the performance of the kernel regression, we first used analysis of variance to examine the differences in the prediction weights among the seven subnetworks. Then, for regions that fell within the top 10% in terms of the absolute prediction weights, we tallied their occurrences within the seven subnetworks. These counts were compared against null distributions, which were derived by randomly selecting 40 brain regions and recording their distribution within the seven subnetworks (10 000 times). For each subnetwork, a p value was computed based on the frequency at which the random selection resulted in a count surpassing the actual observed number. Finally, we reran the kernel regression by using nodal centrality metrics of regions in each of the seven subnetworks to predict individual symptom improvement and changes in the BDNF levels for each patient group.

Statistical analysis

χ2 test was used to compare the sex distribution between the two patient groups. Non-parametric permutation tests were used to examine between-group differences in continuous variables at baseline, including age, HAMD-24 scores and BDNF levels (10 000 permutations). Permutation tests were also used to test the changes in the HAMD-24 scores and BDNF levels before and after the treatment for each patient group (10 000 permutations).

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

In this study, a total of 46 outpatients with MDD were recruited, of whom 36 met the inclusion criteria. The 36 patients were randomised to the escitalopram group (n=18) and the Yueju Pill group (n=18). For each group, four patients withdrew consent for MRI during the follow-up, and therefore, a total of 28 patients were finally included in this study. Table 1 presents the demographic and clinical characteristics of all these participants. Specifically, these patients were composed of 9 males (4 in the escitalopram group and 5 in the Yueju Pill group) and 19 females (10 in the escitalopram group and 9 in the Yueju Pill group), with a mean (standard deviation) age of 44.50 (3.52) and 44.14 (3.25) years for the escitalopram and Yueju Pill groups, respectively. There were no significant differences in sex or age between the two patient groups. There were also no significant differences in the HAMD-24 scores or BDNF levels between the two patient groups, neither at baseline nor after treatment.

The VN (p=0.025) and LN (p=0.039) made significant contributions to the prediction of the change rates of the BDNF levels when using the degree and eigenvector of the CTNs, respectively (figure 5A). Discussion

Main findings

In this study, we investigated the common and specific brain network biomarkers for the changes in the HAMD-24 scores and BDNF levels of patients with MDD following Yueju Pill and escitalopram treatment. We found that baseline GINs predicted the change rates of the HAMD-24 scores in both the treatment groups, whereas baseline SDNs predicted the change rates of the HAMD-24 scores and baseline CTNs predicted the change rates of the BDNF levels only in the Yueju Pill group. Among the predictions, regional prediction weights varied considerably, with the VAN pivotal for the prediction in the escitalopram group, whereas the VN and LN were dominant for the predictions in the Yueju Pill group. These results may hold significant implications for clinicians, enabling them to ascertain suitable therapeutic interventions for individual patients at an early juncture.

Brain network biomarkers shared by Yueju Pill and escitalopram treatment

We found that both the Yueju Pill and escitalopram treatment groups showed significant reductions in the HAMD-24 scores, which could be predicted by the baseline GINs. GI reflects the degree of cortical folding and is frequently reported to be altered in MDD.30 Moreover, GI alterations are found to negatively correlate with the HAMD-24 scores among individuals diagnosed with MDD. According to the tension-based morphogenetic hypothesis, the process of gyrification is deeply implicated in cortical connectivity, and abnormalities in the GI could lead to cerebral dysfunction by changing corticocortical connectivity.31 In this study, we demonstrated that the topological organisation of the GINs might be used as common predictors of early symptom improvement following Yueju Pill and escitalopram treatment in MDD. Presumably, the common predictions may result from the shared neurobiological mechanisms between the two drugs.

It should be highlighted that although the GINs were able to predict individual change rates of the HAMD-24 scores for both the Yueju Pill and escitalopram groups, the distributions of regional prediction weights over the cortical mantle were quite different between the two groups. Particularly, we found that the VAN made a significant contribution to the prediction for the escitalopram group. The VAN is an important mediator of stimulus-driven attention and a task-positive network that is activated during attentional orientation or reorientation.32 A previous meta-analysis of intrinsic functional connectivity revealed hypoconnectivity of the VAN in MDD.12 A recent study further found that the hypoconnectivity can be normalised after treatment with escitalopram.33 These findings, together with our results, collectively highlight that the VAN may be a potential antidepressant target of escitalopram, whose pretreatment network architecture is predictive of the changes following escitalopram treatment.

Brain network biomarkers specific to Yueju Pill treatment

Only for the Yueju Pill group, we found that the baseline SDNs were able to predict the change rates of the HAMD-24 scores of patients. SD is a sensitive measure to detect cortical atrophy in neuropsychiatric diseases. With regard to MDD, prior studies reported widespread SD decreases,34 and the decreases in certain brain regions exhibited negative correlations with the HAMD-24 scores among patients.35 These findings suggest the involvement of SD decreases in the pathophysiology of MDD. Expanding on previous findings, we further demonstrated that SD-derived morphological brain networks may serve as a potential biomarker for predicting symptom improvement in patients with MDD. Moreover, the prediction was specific to Yueju Pill but not to escitalopram treatment. This may be because the SDNs are specifically related to upstream neural processes that are unique to Yueju Pill’s antidepressant action.

Beyond symptom improvement, we found that Yueju Pill treatment significantly increased the BDNF levels in patients with MDD. This finding aligns with a previous study that reported that mice treated with Yueju Pill exhibited rapidly enhanced BDNF expression.36 Accumulating evidence suggests that the BDNF mediates neuroplasticity mechanisms, which are implicated in both depression pathophysiology and antidepressant therapeutic effects.37 Our previous study has demonstrated that a rapid increase in BDNF in the brain underlies the rapid antidepressant responses of Yueju Pill.38 The rapid increase is achieved by upregulating the expression of a specific neuropeptide, PACAP (Pituitary Adenylate Cyclase-Activating Polypeptide), via the synergism of two compounds.39 Accordingly, our results further support that the enhancement of BDNF levels is fundamental for Yueju Pill’s antidepressant action. However, no significant changes were observed in the BDNF levels for the escitalopram group. This is consistent with previous findings that escitalopram did not affect the BDNF levels after 6 months of treatment.40 Escitalopram is found to attenuate depression symptoms quickly via increasing extracellular 5-hydroxytryptamine levels, which promotes 5-hydroxytryptamine neurotransmission.41 Although they mutually interact, the BDNF and 5-hydroxytryptamine seem to work independently to improve depression symptoms, which may account for the differential effects of Yueju Pill and escitalopram on the BDNF.

We found that the change rates of the BDNF levels induced by the Yueju Pill treatment could be predicted by the baseline CTNs. Decreased CT is frequently reported in patients with MDD,42 with a spatial distribution constrained by the connectivity patterns of healthy brains.43 By analysing the same four types of single-subject morphological brain networks as in this study, a recent study found that only the CTNs exhibited significant alterations in adolescents with MDD.15 Moreover, positive correlations were observed between serum BDNF levels and CT in multiple areas that are typically affected by MDD in subjects with a minor depressive episode.44 Based on these findings, we speculate that CT and its derived morphological brain networks are closely related to BDNF signalling in the pathophysiology of MDD.

Interestingly, our subnetwork analyses revealed that the LN and VN were vital for predicting the change rates of the BDNF levels of patients with MDD. Furthermore, the VN was found to be able to independently predict the change rates of both the BDNF levels and HAMD-24 scores following Yueju Pill treatment. The LN consists of a group of interconnected cortical and subcortical regions that are mainly implicated in emotion and memory.45 For example, as a core node of the LN, the amygdala plays a pivotal role in emotional processing. It is found that pathological amygdala hyperactivity contributes to impaired negative emotion regulation in MDD,46 and amygdala-targeted neurofeedback training can effectively enhance emotion regulation capacity.47 The VN is mainly composed of different occipital areas critical for not only facial perception and expression processing but also emotion integration.48 It receives affective feedback from emotion-related regions such as the amygdala, linking emotional valence to visual motion.49,51 In MDD, both LN and VN components have been frequently reported to show alterations,17 52 which may be related to the emotion dysregulation of patients. Based on TCM theory, the liver plays a key role in emotional regulation. Through the liver-soothing mechanism, Yueju Pill normalises emotional regulation by restoring normal Liver Qi dynamics to resolve the six stagnation patterns, thereby ameliorating depressive symptoms. Particularly, the liver is thought to open at the eyes in TCM. This notion is supported by recent studies, which propose the liver-eye axis.53 Accordingly, our findings resonate with the Yueju Pill’s antidepressant principle of ‘soothing the liver’ in TCM and suggest that the liver’s role in emotional regulation may be realised through modulating connectivity patterns of the LN and VN. In addition, our previous study found that the Yueju Pill produced antidepressant effects via rapidly enhancing BDNF levels in the hippocampus,39 a core node of the LN and key disconnection site in MDD.16 The hippocampus was found to work together with the visual cortex to support specific functions.54 Synaptic plasticity in the visual cortex was also reported to affect information processing in the hippocampus.55 Therefore, we speculate that the LN and VN may be involved in the BDNF increases in the hippocampus following Yueju Pill treatment. Future studies are needed for a better understanding of the roles played by the LN and VN in the pathophysiology of MDD and the antidepressant mechanism of Yueju Pill.

Limitations

The present study had several limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small and thus further investigations with multicentre, large sample-sized clinical trials are required to test the reproducibility of our findings. Second, our MRI data were collected on a 1.5T magnetic resonance scanner. Future studies using higher magnetic field strengths for imaging are expected to find more predictors and verify our findings. Third, in this study, we aimed to identify brain network biomarkers for early changes following antidepressant treatment. Thus, the observation interval was set to 4 days. However, such a short observation interval may not be enough for escitalopram to achieve a steady-state concentration. Furthermore, several previous studies have shown that antidepressant effects likely start to separate from placebo effects after at least 7 days of treatment. Future studies can help clarify this issue by adding a placebo control group. Finally, beyond the kernel regression used in this study, there are other sophisticated machine learning techniques, such as the support vector machine. Therefore, future studies are warranted to compare these techniques for identifying more sensitive and reliable biomarkers for early changes following antidepressant treatment, particularly by integrating more advanced imaging protocols (eg, multiband echo planar imaging) and network analytical methods (eg, multiplex network model).

Implications

This study demonstrates that single-subject morphological brain networks can predict the therapeutic responses of patients with MDD to Yueju Pill. Particularly, the SDNs and CTNs might serve as Yueju Pill-specific biomarkers for predicting the therapeutic responses. These findings have clinically significant implications for tailoring personalised antidepressant drug therapy. Specifically, for a patient with MDD, we can first collect his or her structural MRI data and construct corresponding morphological brain networks. The brain networks can then be fed to the predictive models constructed in this study to predict their responses to Yueju Pill treatment. Based on the predicted responses, we can finally determine whether the patient is suitable for Yueju Pill treatment. It should be noted that the predictive models constructed in this study may not be robust enough due to the small sample size and thus should be validated by future multicentre, large-scale studies.
