Epidemiology of Myocarditis in Young and Middle-Aged Population in Asia
Authors: Chenze Li, Zhibing Lu, Dao Wen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyocarditis, characterized by inflammation of the myocardium and subsequent injury of cardiac function, has garnered increasing attention among the young and middle-aged population in Asia. Its epidemiology is influenced by a combination of environmental factors, genetic predisposition, and socioeconomic status. Global disease burden analyses indicate that the prevalence of myocarditis varies by region, with certain areas in Asia reporting higher rates. However, the true burden is likely underestimated due to clinical underdiagnosis and inadequate health surveillance. Regarding prognosis, most acute cases may recover, but persistent subclinical abnormalities often remain. Notably, the most severe form, fulminant myocarditis, is associated with a high in-hospital mortality rate when treated conventionally. Nevertheless, early implementation of a life support–based treatment can significantly improve outcomes. This review focuses on summarizing the epidemiology of myocarditis in the young and middle-aged Asian population, aiming to provide a theoretical foundation for enhancing the management of myocarditis in this demographic in the future.Key Words: Asia, epidemiology, myocarditis, young and middle-aged population

Myocarditis, an inflammatory disease of the myocardium and subsequent injury of cardiac function, is characterized by heterogeneous clinical manifestations ranging from subclinical presentations to life-threatening hemodynamic compromise or sudden cardiac death.1 Its pathogenesis involves the infections of viruses or other pathogens, autoimmune responses, or drug-induced toxicity.2 The long-term implications for the young and middle-aged population are particularly concerning, as myocarditis represents a leading cause of dilated cardiomyopathy and sudden cardiac death in this demographic.3 Although 70% of acute cases may spontaneously recover ventricular function, subclinical abnormalities in diastolic function and regional strain can persist.4 Notably, a significant proportion of patients with myocarditis can develop progressive dilated or inflammatory cardiomyopathy, necessitating advanced therapies including transplantation or ventricular assist devices.5,6

Currently, most existing reviews on myocarditis primarily focus on the Western population. The epidemiology of myocarditis in the young and middle-aged population within Asia remains poorly characterized. In the Asian population, the epidemiological landscape presents distinct characteristics shaped by demographic, genetic, environmental, and health care–related factors. First, Asia’s vast population significantly amplifies the absolute burden of myocarditis. According to data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Collaborative Network,7 the estimated number of incident myocarditis in 2021 was approximately 778,590 cases in Asia, which is substantially higher than in Europe (187,856 cases), Africa (155,453 cases), and the Americas (195,876 cases). Moreover, the number of deaths due to myocarditis in 2021 was also higher in Asia (22,138 cases) compared with Europe (6,166 cases), Africa (1,639 cases), and the Americas (1,768 cases). Second, genetic diversity among Asian ethnic groups influences susceptibility and disease progression. For instance, polymorphisms in the human leukocyte antigen and major histocompatibility complex gene, which modulate immune responses to viral triggers such as coxsackievirus B3 and susceptibility to autoimmune myocarditis, exhibit varied prevalence across different populations.8,9 Furthermore, environmental exposures endemic to Asia, such as high rates of dengue infections, also correlate with the incidence rate of myocarditis.10,11

In this review, we aimed to address critical knowledge gaps by synthesizing existing data on the incidence, clinical profiles, and long-term outcomes of myocarditis in the Asian young and middle-aged population. By integrating these findings, this work seeks to provide a theoretical foundation for improving the management of myocarditis in this population.

Global and Asian Burden of Myocarditis

The global burden of myocarditis exhibits notable regional and age-related disparities, with emerging data highlighting differences between Western and Asian populations, as well as distinct patterns among young and middle-aged adults vs other age groups. A study analyzing the global burden of myocarditis from 1990 to 2021 revealed that the age-standardized incidence rate of myocarditis in Southeast Asia was approximately 17.87 (95% CI: 14.46-21.9) per 100,000 persons, whereas in the high-income Asia Pacific, it was approximately 19.36 (95% CI: 15.94-23.71) per 100,000 persons. Both rates exceeded the global average of 16.16 (95% CI: 13.11-19.76) per 100,000 persons. Furthermore, the disability-adjusted life years rate of myocarditis in Central Asia was approximately 27.41 (95% CI: 21.93-34.56) per 100,000 persons, and in East Asia, it was approximately 25.02 (95% CI: 17.86-30.91) per 100,000 persons, both of which were above the global average of 12.41 (95% CI: 10.37-14.76) per 100,000 persons. The age-standardized death rate of myocarditis in Central Asia was approximately 0.84 (95% CI: 0.68-1.05) per 100,000 persons, and in East Asia, it was approximately 0.91 (95% CI: 0.63-1.18) per 100,000 persons, also exceeding the global average of 0.40 (95% CI: 0.32-0.47) per 100,000 persons. Moreover, this study indicated that the young and middle-aged population carried a predominant burden of disability-adjusted life years, with the exception of children younger than 5 years old.12 For other age-stratified analyses, studies also revealed a pronounced burden among young and middle-aged patients, who accounted for 65% of myocarditis cases in both Western registries and Asian series.13,14 These regional- and age-specific patterns underscore the need for tailored preventive algorithms and therapeutic protocols to address epidemiological heterogeneity in global myocarditis management.

To provide more straightforward data on the burden of myocarditis in Asia, the authors obtained relevant information from the GBD Collaborative Network.15 This review graphically presents the original data available in this open database, which offers detailed information on the incidence and mortality of myocarditis rates across Asia. It should be noted that the data presented does not involve any in-depth statistical analysis. Furthermore, the classification of Asian regions and countries is consistent with the reports from an official United Nations definition.16 The rates presented are crude rates, rather than age-standardized rates, thereby accurately reflecting the true burden of myocarditis, as discussed in the study by Zhao.17 Figure 1 shows that from 1990 to 2021, the crude incidence rate of myocarditis exhibited an upward trend in Asia. The crude incidence rate of myocarditis in 1990 was 14.32 (95% CI: 11.53-17.72) per 100,000 persons, which increased to 16.84 (95% CI: 13.60-20.77) per 100,000 persons by 2021. In terms of the crude mortality rate, a significant increase was observed from 1990 to 2002, followed by no significant change from 2002 to 2021. The crude mortality rate in 1990 was 0.42 (95% CI: 0.33-0.53) per 100,000 persons, rising to 0.48 (95% CI: 0.35-0.57) per 100,000 persons in 2021. Throughout the years, male individuals exhibited higher crude incidence and mortality rates than female individuals. In addition, we present the incidence and mortality rates across different regions in Asia, as illustrated in Figures 2 and 3. The data revealed that Japan had the highest incidence rates in both 1990 (22.67; 95% CI: 18.10-28.04 per 100,000 persons) and 2021 (31.52; 95% CI: 24.74-38.85 per 100,000 persons), whereas Western Asia demonstrated relatively lower incidence rates, ranging from 8.24 to 17.08 per 100,000 persons in 1990 to 8.94 to 21.10 per 100,000 persons in 2021. Regarding mortality, significant changes were noted between 1990 and 2021. Sri Lanka reported the highest mortality rate in 1990 (1.72; 95% CI: 1.02-2.27 per 100,000 persons); however, by 2021, Kazakhstan (2.34; 95% CI: 1.76-3.16 per 100,000 persons) emerged as the country with the highest mortality rate in Asia, despite its relatively low mortality rate in 1990 (0.08; 95% CI: 0.06-0.12 per 100,000 persons). The study from Li et al12 indicated that from 1990 to 2021, Kazakhstan experienced the highest growth rate of 13.64% in the age-standardized mortality rate of myocarditis.

In addition to the data retrieved from the GBD database, there are also published studies from Asian countries investigating the epidemiology of myocarditis following the administration of the BNT162b2 vaccine. A study conducted in South Korea reported a higher incidence of myocarditis/pericarditis among individuals aged 12 to 19 years, with an incidence of 2.25 cases per 100,000 doses of the vaccine occurring within 42 days post-vaccination.31 Similarly, a study in Malaysia evaluated the risk of myocarditis and pericarditis occurring 21 days after BNT162b2 vaccination in individuals aged 12 years and older, revealing an incidence of 1.73 cases of myocarditis/pericarditis per million doses administered, corresponding to an absolute risk of 0.33 cases per 100,000 vaccinated individuals.32

In summary, there are significant disparities in the incidence and mortality rates of myocarditis across Asia, with a particularly substantial burden observed among the young and middle-aged population (Central Illustration). These disparities reflect not only regional epidemiological characteristics but also highlight the varying impacts of health systems and capacity of public health care. This underscores the need for region-specific prevention strategies in the future.

Epidemiology of Young and Middle-Aged Myocarditis in Asia

From 1990 to 2021, the numbers of incident cases and deaths of myocarditis in Asia exhibited an upward trend. In 2021, the young and middle-aged population accounted for 51.64% of the total incident cases and 25.85% of total myocarditis deaths in Asia. In addition, published papers indicated that young and middle-aged people were the predominant affected population, with male individuals comprising the majority.

Challenges in the Report of Epidemiological Data

Although the GBD database provides a valuable source of epidemiological data on myocarditis, it is important to acknowledge that reports on epidemiological data of myocarditis in Asia face challenges rooted in both clinical underdiagnosis and systemic limitations in health care infrastructure, which collectively obscure the true disease burden. For instance, a survey in Singapore indicated that up to 38.5% of childhood myocarditis fatalities were incorrectly coded as respiratory tract infections,33 as myocarditis often presents with nonspecific symptoms, such as fatigue, dyspnea, or vague chest discomfort.2 In resource-constrained settings, where advanced diagnostic tools like cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) or endomyocardial biopsy are scarce, clinicians frequently rely on echocardiography or clinical suspicion alone. Studies in India revealed that up to 30% of pediatric myocarditis cases were often misclassified as rheumatic heart disease or other heart disease due to overlapping clinical features.34 In addition, clinical studies on myocarditis in the Asian population are still relatively scarce compared with those conducted in the Western population. In Western studies, the reported recurrence rate of myocarditis ranged from 2.6% to 18.0%.35, 36, 37, 38 However, there is a notable lack of epidemiological data regarding the recurrence rate of myocarditis in Asia, despite a few case reports documenting instances of myocarditis recurrence and its management. Further efforts and studies are necessary to address these gaps and enhance our understanding of the hidden burden of myocarditis in Asia.

Etiologies in the Asian Population

Viral infections are the predominant cause of myocarditis.39 The most frequently identified viral pathogens are enteroviruses, particularly Coxsackievirus B3, which is associated with both acute myocarditis and the progression to dilated cardiomyopathy due to persistent immune-mediated damage.40,41 However, regional variations in viral pathogens in Asia are influenced by genetic polymorphisms within the population, endemic viral strains, health care infrastructure, and diagnostic practices. An Asian cohort study showed that the genetic polymorphisms in innate immune sensors, such as Toll-like receptors, could modulate host susceptibility to viral persistence and autoimmune transition.42 Furthermore, emerging viruses, such as dengue, exhibit geographically skewed prevalence. Dengue-associated myocarditis shows higher incidence in tropical Southeast Asian countries like Thailand and Indonesia, correlating with endemic mosquito-borne transmission cycles.43

In recent years, other emerging etiological factors, COVID-19–associated myocarditis and post-vaccination myocarditis, also reshape the disease landscape. Studies have indicated that SARS-CoV-2 infection significantly increased the risk of myocarditis,44 with COVID-19–related myocardial inflammation showing high rates of subclinical myocarditis detected via CMR in convalescent patients, as reported in Asian cohorts.45,46 One study reported that acute myocarditis induced by COVID-19 predominantly affected younger adults (median age 37 years), with 25% developing fulminant courses requiring mechanical circulatory support.47 Moreover, the pandemic has exacerbated trends in cardiovascular mortality, driven by indirect effects from health care disruptions and delays in the treatment of acute myocarditis.48,49 Although rare, post-vaccination associated with messenger RNA COVID-19 vaccines, has also been documented in the Asian population, with studies from Japan and South Korea showing a predominant impact on male individuals younger than 30 years.50,51 However, a study showed that the Southeast Asian population exhibited a lower proportion of vaccine-associated myocarditis/pericarditis (0.08%) compared with the European region (32.94%).52

For the noninfectious etiology, autoimmune-mediated myocarditis represents a significant subset, with systemic lupus erythematosus being a prominent contributor in East Asia due to its higher prevalence compared with the Western population.53,54 Its pathogenesis involves the perpetuation of regulated T-cell responses and cytokine cascades.55 This autoimmune propensity may be exacerbated by regional genetic factors, such as the association of HLA-DR2 with the susceptibility to systemic lupus erythematosus, as reported in a cohort from China.56 Moreover, advanced therapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), also have been frequently reported to induce myocarditis in the Asian population.57

Diagnostic Challenges and Clinical Practice in Asia

The diagnostic challenges of myocarditis arise from its nonspecific symptoms, such as chest pain and fatigue, which are further complicated when considering differential diagnoses in the context of COVID-19.58 The overlap in symptoms can lead to misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis, potentially resulting in severe consequences. Countries with robust public health infrastructures, such as China and Japan, have been more proactive in identifying myocarditis cases associated with infections. In contrast, less developed nations may struggle with accurate diagnosis due to limited access to CMR and other specialized diagnostic modalities.59

For the diagnosis of myocarditis, European studies have reported a high positive predictive value and specificity when using CMR,60 establishing it as the cornerstone for noninvasive detection of myocardial inflammation.61 Similarly, diagnostic practices in Asia are increasingly incorporating CMR for noninvasive assessment, aligning with global trends.62,63 Other noninvasive methods also have been explored in Asia. A study from China highlighted that echocardiography-derived global longitudinal strain could serve as a surrogate for monitoring the myocardial fibrosis and edema during the course of myocarditis.64,65 However, for an invasive technique, endomyocardial biopsy remains underutilized in many Asian regions due to procedural risk and limited access.66

The application of laboratory testing is also frequently used in the diagnosis and risk stratification of myocarditis. In the Asian population, conventional biomarkers such as cardiac troponins (cTnI, cTnT) and creatine kinase isoenzyme-MB remain central to the evaluation of myocarditis.67,68 A study from the Asian population showed that elevated cTnI levels were strongly associated with myocarditis severity, with higher cTnI levels observed in patients with myocarditis compared with those with arrhythmias or heart failure (P < 0.001).68 In addition to the biomarkers related to myocardial injury, Japanese research identified novel autoantibody profiles as biomarkers for ICI-associated myocarditis.69,70 Other Asian studies also came up with the utility of inflammatory ratios such as the systemic immune-inflammation index, albumin-to–red cell distribution width ratio, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and neutrophil-to-eosinophil ratio for risk prediction in the ICI-associated myocarditis.68,71 These biomarkers, which integrate hematological and inflammatory parameters, demonstrated superior diagnostic value in Asian cohorts. Specifically, a systemic immune-inflammation index ≥1,020 showed 91% sensitivity and 83% specificity for diagnosing fulminant myocarditis in young adults, outperforming traditional troponins.72 A study conducted in China also showed that parameters like soluble suppression of tumorigenicity-2 (sST2) levels had a sensitivity of 85.7% and specificity of 94.7% in diagnosing fulminant myocarditis.73 In the future, it is hoped that more diagnostic models will be developed to collectively improve the diagnosis of myocarditis.

Prognosis and Treatment Strategies in Asia

Prognosis

Several studies have suggested that mild myocarditis cases in the young and middle-aged population may follow a self-limiting course.74 A population-based study conducted in China further revealed that acute myocarditis severity did not predict long-term outcomes in adults, although early cardiovascular deaths (within 3 months) correlated with initial disease severity.21 This review summarizes relevant published original research investigating the prognosis of myocarditis in Asian populations, as presented in Table 1. The findings showed that, although there were no in-hospital deaths reported in an Israeli study, which may be due to the inclusion of relatively less severe cases, studies in China, Japan, and Korea indicated high in-hospital mortality rates for fulminant myocarditis, the most severe form, ranging from 25.7% to 50.4%, with a heart failure rate of approximately 85%. In terms of long-term outcomes, patients with acute myocarditis faced a risk of up to 8.2% of developing heart failure. In addition, a Japanese study reported an all-cause mortality rate of up to 33.1% in patients with histologically proven fulminant myocarditis during a follow-up period of 600 (36-1,599) days.

In addition, several studies have identified new biomarkers for predicting the prognosis of myocarditis. One study conducted in an Asian population highlighted the prognostic significance of hemoglobin/red blood cell distribution width ratio and red blood cell distribution width/albumin ratio, which correlated with disease severity and survival outcomes in patients with myocarditis.75 Another study performed in pediatric myocarditis demonstrated that N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide and sST2 showed great performance in predicting major adverse cardiac events, with elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (OR: 4.32) and sST2 (OR: 1.02) independently associated with hospitalization outcomes.76 Furthermore, a study found that the acute kidney injury network classification for acute kidney injury and sequential organ failure assessment scores could effectively stratify mortality risk in acute myocarditis, with acute kidney injury network stage 3 and elevated sequential organ failure assessment scores correlating with poor outcomes.77 These tools, validated in the Asian population, underscore the interplay between multi-organ dysfunction and myocarditis-related mortality.

Electrocardiographic patterns also have been shown to predict the course of myocarditis. For instance, early repolarization, which was present in 30% of Japanese patients with acute myocarditis, was associated with lower troponin levels and a benign clinical course. In contrast, non–early repolarization patterns, such as ST-segment elevation, were indicative of severe myocardial injury.78 In addition, a separate analysis from Japan indicated that systolic hypotension (<101 mm Hg) and QRS prolongation (>120 ms) at admission were strong predictors of fulminant courses.79 Together with these indices, Asian prognostic models could be further optimized.

Treatment strategies

There are significant disparities in the management of fulminant myocarditis, the most severe form of myocarditis, between Western populations and those in Asia. In Western populations, fulminant myocarditis was associated with a high mortality, reaching up to 50% to 70%.80 In Asia, the management of fulminant myocarditis frequently incorporates intravenous immunoglobulin, a practice less emphasized in Western guidelines.81 In China, a life support–based comprehensive treatment regimen that emphasized early application of mechanical life support, immunomodulation, and neuraminidase inhibitors for fulminant myocarditis had decreased the mortality rate to 3.7%.82 In Japan, a multicenter retrospective study of patients with fulminant myocarditis revealed complete left ventricular ejection fraction recovery in 86% of survivors at 1-year follow-up after treatment with intravenous immunoglobulin and steroids.27 In addition, lupus myocarditis in Asian cohorts had shown favorable recovery of left ventricular function with high-dose corticosteroids.83 Furthermore, during the Omicron wave, COVID-19–related myocarditis was shown to benefit from the administration of angiotensin II receptor type 1 blockers.84,85

Moreover, Asian countries have increasingly integrated traditional medicine, such as Astragalus membranaceus injections, into conventional supportive care for viral myocarditis, with some trials reporting improved outcomes in heart function and symptom alleviation.86 However, this finding is tempered by methodological limitations, including small sample sizes and high risk of bias, underscoring the need for rigorous multicenter studies. In Table 2, we have listed all registered studies involving the Asian population on clinical trial websites.87 Most of these studies are still ongoing. Several promising drugs for the treatment of myocarditis, such as hydroxychloroquine and tofacitinib, hold potential for future use. The implementation of these clinical studies is expected to optimize therapeutic strategies for this patient demographic in the future.

Public Health Management in Asia

The shortcomings of public health care

The epidemiology of myocarditis exhibits regional variations across Asia, highlighting the necessity for tailored public health interventions to address this health burden. For example, in China, the age-standardized incidence rate decreased by 6.06%, and the age-standardized mortality rate declined by 16.04% from 1990 to 2019, reflecting improvements in health care.88 However, persistent gender disparities existed, with higher age-standardized incidence and mortality rates in male compared with female individuals.88 Moreover, the age distribution of myocarditis-related morbidity and mortality has shifted toward older groups, suggesting improved survival rates among younger individuals but changes in risk factor profiles.88 Besides, delayed recognition of myocarditis is also a significant issue in China, contributing to its prominence as a cause of sudden cardiac death, which accounts for 34.7% of sudden cardiac death cases in individuals younger than 35 years, a rate higher than that observed in Western countries.89 In addition, Japanese pharmacovigilance data revealed that myocarditis was the most common cardiac complication following ICI therapy, with potential cardiac complications arising at a median onset of 33 days post treatment, underscoring the need for vigilant monitoring during the ICI therapies.90 In tropical regions of Asia, infectious etiologies remain prominent, necessitating enhanced health care surveillance of viral pathogens.91

Furthermore, access to advanced therapies varies widely. In high-income Asian countries, left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) and heart transplantation programs have improved outcomes for myocarditis-induced heart failure. For instance, patients in Singapore with myocarditis-related cardiomyopathy achieved functional recovery or successful bridging to transplantation using LVADs.92 However, in resource-limited Asian regions, limited availability of transplantation services and LVADs forces reliance on suboptimal surgical interventions, such as left ventricular volume reduction, which yield disappointing survival rates (65.2% at 18 months).93

The possible solutions

Strengthening the recognition of myocarditis and integrating biomarkers, such as troponin, sST2, and interleukin-17A into diagnostic protocols may improve early detection.94 Furthermore, investigating genetic predispositions, such as interleukin-17A polymorphisms (eg, rs2275913) associated with the severity of myocarditis, could enable personalized risk stratification and targeted therapies.94,95 In addition, promoting the use of CMR in underserved areas through cost-reduction initiatives and training programs is critical. In regions with high infectious disease burdens, vaccination campaigns targeting enteroviruses, dengue, and leptospirosis may help reduce the incidence of myocarditis. Moreover, countries that use immunotherapies or clozapine should adopt stricter monitoring protocols. Similarly, Asian guidelines for managing ICI-related myocarditis should prioritize longitudinal cardiac monitoring, even beyond the first year of treatment.90 Last, training health care providers in myocarditis management and improving referral networks may help reduce disparities in health care quality.

Perspectives and Conclusions

Myocarditis represents a significant public health concern in Asia, particularly among the young and middle-aged population. Clinically, severe manifestations of myocarditis, including fulminant heart failure, cardiogenic shock, or sudden cardiac death, are disproportionately observed in these age groups, contributing to its recognition as a predominant cause of death.96,97 In terms of the long-term sequelae, up to one-third of myocarditis cases progress to dilated cardiomyopathy and chronic heart failure, emphasizing the need for early intervention and longitudinal monitoring.98 In addition, the diagnostic challenges associated with myocarditis are exacerbated by the heterogeneity of presentations and the limited use of endomyocardial biopsy, the gold standard for confirmation, due to its invasiveness and accessibility issues.99 Advanced imaging modalities, particularly CMR, should be advocated to enhance the noninvasive detection of myocardial inflammation and fibrosis,63,100 alongside collaborative efforts to standardize diagnostic criteria. Moreover, exploring the interplay among genetic predisposition, environmental factors, and immune responses in Asian cohorts may also unveil novel therapeutic targets in the future.

Currently, epidemiological data specific to Asian cohorts remain sparse, and the true data on epidemiology of myocarditis in Asia is likely underdiagnosed and underreported. Nevertheless, existing literature highlights critical regional variability and unmet health care needs. This pattern underscores the influence of socioeconomic factors and health care infrastructure on disease burden. Addressing these issues requires a multifaceted approach that includes pathogen control, pharmacovigilance, and infrastructure investment. More importantly, future research must prioritize large-scale population-based studies to delineate the incidence, etiology, and outcomes of myocarditis across Asian subregions. By integrating these strategies, the Asian population may mitigate the burden of myocarditis in young and middle-aged adults, aligning with global efforts to reduce cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.

Conclusions

Myocarditis in the young and middle-aged Asian population shares broad pathophysiological features similar to global patterns, regional nuances in etiology, clinical profiles, and health care challenges require prioritized attention. Bridging these knowledge gaps through targeted research and equitable resource allocation will be pivotal in improving management for this vulnerable population.
