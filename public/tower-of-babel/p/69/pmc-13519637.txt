Platelet miR-144-5p regulates COX-1/2 expression and modulates aspirin response in patients with cardiometabolic risk∗
Authors: Paola G Simeone, Rossella Liani, Vittorio Abbonante, Margherita Alfonsetti, Maurizio Frezza, Paola Lanuti, Antonio Recchiuti, Alessandra Balduini, Francesca Santilli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Platelet miR-144-5p regulates COX-1 expression and contributes to variability in response to low-dose aspirin.•Reduced platelet miR-144-5p identifies patients with faster COX-1 recovery and residual thromboxane generation during aspirin treatment.

Introduction

Platelets are anucleate blood components derived from megakaryocytes, with a circulating life span of 7 to 10 days.1 They play a crucial role in primary hemostasis, and their activation is further enhanced by interactions between specific membrane receptors and agonists, such as thromboxane A2, adenosine diphosphate, and thrombin,1 as well as by pathological and proinflammatory conditions.2 Despite lacking a nucleus and genomic DNA, platelets contain a rich repertoire of microRNAs (miRNAs), mRNAs, long noncoding RNAs, and regulatory proteins within their cytoplasm, which enable de novo gene expression and functional modulation.3 The primary research objective is to identify reliable biomarkers to more accurately characterize patients at high cardiovascular risk undergoing chronic aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid; ASA) therapy who do not fully benefit from the standard once-daily dosing regimen, but who may instead benefit from twice-daily administration.2 A suboptimal response to ASA is observed in approximately a quarter4 of patients with cardiovascular disease.5 The variability in platelet reactivity among patients receiving antiplatelet therapy is a clinically relevant issue, often driven by factors such as increased platelet turnover, enhanced megakaryopoiesis,6 and accelerated recovery of cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) activity within the 24-hour dosing interval. The variability in response to ASA is a multifactorial phenomenon, in which, in addition to genetic differences, epigenetic mechanisms, such as regulation mediated by miRNAs, also play a significant role. In the present study, patients were initially stratified into “good responders” (first tertile) and “poor responders” to ASA, based on the slope of serum thromboxane B2 (sTXB2).2 Patients in the first tertile were used as the control group. We then performed a targeted analysis of platelet miRNA expression profiles, selecting candidates from the literature known to be potentially involved in ASA responsiveness.7 Among these, we focused on miR-144-5p, which is known to regulate the expression of COX-2.8 To further investigate its role, we examined the effects of miR-144-5p overexpression in megakaryocytic and endothelial cell lines, also assessing its potential role in the regulation of COX-1, the primary pharmacological target of ASA.

Methods

Study design and participants

All patients (N = 200) were on chronic treatment with low-dose ASA (enteric-coated Cardioaspirin, 100 mg/day; Bayer, Milan, Italy), administered once daily for ≥1 month before enrollment. They were recruited from the Diabetes and Cardiovascular Prevention Clinic, Department of Internal Medicine, SS. Annunziata Hospital, Chieti, Italy. Blood samples were obtained by antecubital vein puncture 10 and 24 hours after witnessed ASA intake. Urine samples were also collected. Each patient provided written informed consent before blood sampling.

The study was preceded by a 7-day run-in period during which patients were instructed to take ASA at 8 am. Blood samples were collected before the witnessed ASA dose (8 am) and subsequently at 10 and 24 hours after the last administration to characterize the kinetics of COX-1 activity recovery, as reflected by the slope of sTXB2 levels across the 24-hour dosing interval between 2 consecutive witnessed doses. The sTXB2 slope was calculated as (sTXB2 T24 − sTXB2 T10)/14. Patients were stratified into tertiles according to the slope, and comparative analyses were conducted between good (first tertile) and poor (third tertile) ASA responders. The first tertile was the control group. In vivo and in vitro experiments were conducted, including platelet isolation, real-time polymerase chain reaction, transfection of miR-144 mimics and antagomiR into DAMI cells and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), and western blot analysis, to investigate how increasing miR-144 expression (via mimics) or inhibiting it (via antagomiRs) affects platelet COX-1 expression.

This targeted approach was based on previous literature identifying candidate for analyses of miRNAs expression profiles, potentially involved in aspirin responsiveness and platelet function, including miR-144-5p, which has been implicated in the regulation of COX pathways and inflammatory signaling.

Biochemical measurements

Whole blood samples collected for sTXB2 measurement were immediately incubated in glass tubes at 37°C for 1 hour and subsequently centrifuged according to the manufacturer’s instructions of the enzyme immunoassay kit. For urinary analyses, 1 mmol/L of the antioxidant 4-hydroxy-TEMPO (4-hydroxy-2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine 1-oxyl; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was added to urine samples immediately after collection.

sTXB29 and urinary 11-dehydro-TXB210 were measured using a commercial enzyme immunoassay kit (catalog no. 501020; Cayman Chemical) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

sTXB2 was used as a specific index of platelet COX-1 activity, reflecting the maximal thromboxane-generating capacity of platelets during clotting. This approach is widely adopted in pharmacodynamic studies of aspirin to assess the extent and recovery of platelet COX-1 inhibition.

Platelet isolation

Platelet-rich plasma was separated (centrifugation, 100g for 15 minutes) from acid citrate dextrose solution A (ACD-A) anticoagulated blood, mixed with prostaglandin E1 (4μM) and EDTA (10mM), and filtered on Pall Purecell PL (Pall Medical, New York, NY) to remove leukocyte contaminants. Filtered platelet-rich plasma was analyzed by flow cytometry to exclude red blood cell (CD235+) contamination and platelet activation (CD62P+) and by a Beckman Coulter Counter Multisizer to exclude leukocyte contamination. Platelets were then pelleted by centrifugation (1000g for 8 minutes, room temperature) and washed 3 times with Tyrode’s buffer (catalog no. T2145; Sigma-Aldrich).

DAMI cell line

The DAMI cell line is an immortalized human megakaryocytic cell line derived from patient with megakaryoblastic leukemia, widely used as a model of megakaryocyte biology and platelet precursor cell function.

Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Cells were passaged regularly and used within a defined range of passages for all experiments.

Refer to the supplemental Appendix for detailed description of experimental procedures.

Statistical analysis

A minimum of 30 patients per group was estimated to detect 1 standard deviation difference in any investigated parameter between the first and third sTXB2 tertiles, with α = 0.01 and 90% power. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages and compared using the χ2 test. Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges (25th-75th percentile). Comparisons between groups were performed using the unpaired Student t test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Associations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman correlation coefficient. Two-tailed P values < .05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v9.0 (GraphPad Software) and IBM SPSS Statistics v27.0.

The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee (1129/2015, GR-2011-02350450). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Results

Clinical characteristics

From 2015 to 2017, 200 patients on chronic low-dose ASA (100 mg/day) were enrolled at the Diabetes and Cardiovascular Prevention Clinic, SS. Annunziata Hospital, Chieti, Italy. After sTXB2 assessment (ng/mL; first tertile, 1.72 ± 0.89; second tertile, 3.43 ± 0.85; third tertile, 7.90 ± 8.69), patients were stratified in tertiles of sTXB2 slope, an ex vivo marker of COX-1 activity and ASA response. Compared with patients in the first tertile (good responders), those in the third tertile (poor responders) exhibited a more adverse metabolic and inflammatory profile, characterized by higher body weight (P = .021), body mass index (P = .015), waist circumference (P = .002), and platelet counts (P = .012). White blood cell count, an index of systemic inflammation, also tended to be higher in poor responders (P = .050; Table 1).

Platelet miR-144 expression and ASA response

Platelet-derived miR-144-5p levels were significantly reduced in patients in the third tertile of TXB2 slope compared with patients in the first tertile (P = .027; Figure 1A). In contrast, platelet-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) displayed a trend toward relative enrichment in miR-144-5p in the third tertile, although this did not reach statistical significance (P = .097; Figure 1B). Total circulating miR-144-5p remained unchanged (P = .857; Figure 1C), highlighting a compartment-specific regulation. Platelet miR-144-5p levels showed a significant inverse correlation with sTXB2 (ρ = −0.235; P = .007) and with urinary 11-dehydro-TXB2 (ρ = −0.177; P = .046), whereas they showed a trend with the slope of sTXB2 (ρ = −0.118; P = .184; data not shown). In addition, platelet miR-144-5p showed a trend toward a negative correlation with platelet mRNA levels of COX-1 (ρ = −0.286; P = .074) and COX-2 (ρ = −0.325; P = .050), suggesting that this miRNA may be involved in the regulation of COX pathways (data not shown).

Clinical correlates of platelet miR-144-5p levels

To understand the clinical correlates of low platelet miR-144-5p levels, we assessed the relationship between platelet miR-144-5p and clinical and laboratory variables. Platelet miR-144-5p showed robust correlations with vascular and metabolic variables. Higher platelet miR-144-5p levels were directly associated with increased carotid intima-media thickness (ρ = 0.257; P = .014), suggesting an association between platelet miR-144-5p and subclinical vascular injury. Conversely, platelet miR-144-5p levels were reduced in patients with obesity (ρ = −0.208; P = .018), metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD; ρ = −0.292; P = .001), and exhibited a trend toward an association with dyslipidemia (ρ = −0.169; P = .055). Consistent with these findings, negative correlations were also observed with COX-1/2 mRNA levels and sTXB2, further supporting the hypothesis that reduced platelet miR-144-5p is associated with accelerated COX-1 recovery.

ASA affects miR-144-5p expression in megakaryocytic cells

To investigate whether ASA affects miRNA expression, DAMI megakaryocytic cells were exposed to pharmacologically relevant COX-inhibitory ASA concentrations (50μM) in vitro. In these experiments, we simultaneously evaluated 3 miRNAs, including miR-144-5p, miR-21-5p, and miR-155-5p. Among the 3 miRNAs, a significant time-dependent upregulation was observed exclusively for miR-144-5p, whereas no modulation was detected for miR-21-5p or miR-155-5p.

These findings support the hypothesis that ASA can directly induce miR-144-5p expression at the megakaryocytic level (Figure 2A-C).

miR-144 modulates COX-1 expression in DAMI cells

The TargetScan (www.targetscan.org) website predicted a potential target site for miR-144-5p on COX-1 (PTGS1) mRNA, as shown in Figure 3A. Functional experiments further supported a regulatory role of miR-144-5p on COX-1 expression. Transfection of DAMI cells with a miR-144-5p mimic markedly reduced COX-1 protein levels, whereas inhibition of miR-144-5p with a specific inhibitor increased COX-1 protein expression (Figure 3B). Validation in human endothelial cells

The regulatory role of miR-144-5p was also confirmed in HUVECs. Similar to DAMI cells, transfection with a miR-144-5p mimic induced a significant downregulation of both COX-1 and COX-2 expression (Figure 4A-B), suggesting that miR-144-5p may also influence both isoforms of COX expression in endothelial cells. These results extend the functional relevance of miR-144-5p beyond the megakaryocytic lineage, indicating a potential effect on endothelial COX expression in vitro, although the relevance of these findings in vivo remains to be established.

Discussion

In this translational study, we provide converging clinical and mechanistic evidence that platelet-derived miR-144-5p is associated with ASA response and, in particular, with COX-1 recovery. We show that poor responders to ASA exhibit reduced platelet miR-144-5p expression, accompanied by enrichment of this miRNA in platelet-derived EVs, pointing to an active process of export rather than passive depletion. These findings suggest that insufficient platelet miR-144-5p may be associated with incomplete suppression of thromboxane biosynthesis, thereby sustaining residual platelet reactivity despite chronic low-dose ASA therapy.

Although urinary 11-dehydro-TXB2 represents an integrated measure of systemic thromboxane biosynthesis, sTXB2 provides a more specific assessment of platelet COX-1 activity. In the present study, the use of sTXB2 allowed us to directly investigate platelet-specific mechanisms underlying variability in aspirin response, which was central to our research objective.

From a mechanistic perspective, our in vitro experiments provide a plausible explanation for these clinical observations. ASA exposure significantly upregulated miR-144-5p expression in megakaryocytic cells, indicating that this antiplatelet drug can modulate the miRNA machinery at the precursor-cell level. Functional studies further demonstrated that miR-144-5p directly represses COX-1 expression, the canonical pharmacological target of ASA. AntagomiR-mediated inhibition of miR-144-5p restored COX-1 levels, reinforcing the specificity of this regulation. These data support the concept of a dual mechanism of ASA action, which is direct acetylation and irreversible inactivation of COX-1 enzymatic activity and transcriptional and posttranscriptional modulation of COX-1 expression via miR-144-5p induction. In our study, we observed significantly elevated platelet COX-1 mRNA and protein levels in poor responders, pointing to upregulated COX-1 biosynthetic capacity within circulating platelets. In keeping with previous evidence of de novo COX-1 synthesis in platelets,12 this finding supports the concept that accelerated renewal of the enzyme may counteract pharmacological blockade of ASA. Taken together, these observations raise the possibility that ASA-induced upregulation of miR-144-5p may represent an additional mechanism contributing to COX-1 regulation beyond its well-established direct enzymatic inhibition.

Insufficient levels of miR-144-5p may therefore relieve post-transcriptional repression of COX-1, potentially promoting the rapid replacement of acetylated enzyme molecules between ASA doses and sustaining thromboxane generation.

The relevance of miR-144-5p is not restricted to platelets. In endothelial cells, transfection with a miR-144-5p mimic reduced both COX-1 and COX-2 expression, extending its regulatory role to the vascular compartment. Because endothelial COX-2–derived prostanoids play a critical role in vascular inflammation and homeostasis, our findings suggest that miR-144-5p may act as a nodal regulator of both platelet and vascular pathways, linking ASA response to vascular biology. It should be noted that HUVECs represent a simplified in vitro model, and these findings do not directly reflect the effects of low-dose aspirin in the vascular endothelium in vivo. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

From a clinical standpoint, platelet miR-144-5p levels were associated with several cardiometabolic features, including obesity and MASLD, conditions known to influence aspirin responsiveness. Higher levels correlated with subclinical vascular damage, as reflected by carotid intima-media thickness. This suggests that miR-144-5p may be upregulated as an adaptive response to vascular injury or inflammation. Conversely, reduced platelet miR-144-5p levels were associated with obesity, elevated body weight, dyslipidemia, and MASLD, all conditions known to increase platelet turnover and attenuate ASA efficacy.1,2,6,13 In this context, low platelet miR-144-5p levels may contribute to persistent COX-1 expression and heightened thromboxane-dependent platelet activation. These findings should be interpreted as associations and do not imply causality. They may reflect the contribution of broader metabolic and inflammatory pathways.

Obesity has been consistently associated with impaired responsiveness to low-dose aspirin, a phenomenon that has been partly attributed to reduced aspirin bioavailability and altered pharmacokinetics in people with larger body size.13 Several clinical studies have shown that increased body weight or body mass index is associated with lower systemic exposure to aspirin and decreased antiplatelet effects (particularly with enteric-coated formulations),14,15 suggesting that deacetylation, a larger distribution volume, and delayed or incomplete intestinal absorption may all contribute to reduced bioavailability in obese subjects.16 Obesity has also been associated with accelerated COX-1 recovery and a shorter duration of ASA effect.6 Importantly, in our study ASA exposure increased miR-144-5p expression in megakaryocytic cells, and obesity was associated with low platelet miR-144-5p and accelerated COX-1 recovery (poorer ASA response), indicating a possible mechanistic link between the initial metabolic abnormality, miRNA deregulation, and aspirin response. Thus, reduced aspirin bioavailability in obesity may blunt the ability of ASA to induce miR-144-5p, thereby relieving post-transcriptional repression of COX isoforms and promoting increased COX-1/COX-2 expression and faster recovery of thromboxane biosynthesis, a biologically plausible pathway that may help explain residual thromboxane A2 generation and attenuated platelet inhibition in patients with obesity.17,18

These findings integrate with previous literature on platelet miRNAs.3,7 Provost3 and Gutmann and Mayr7 emphasized the importance of platelet miRNA networks in modulating thrombotic pathways. Recent studies have identified altered miRNA profiles in patients treated with ASA, highlighting the contribution of epigenetic regulation to interindividual variability in drug response.7 Specifically, miR-144-5p had previously been implicated in the regulation of COX-2.8 Our data extend this evidence by demonstrating direct repression of COX-1 and showing ASA-induced upregulation of miR-144-5p, thereby unveiling a novel feed-forward mechanism by which ASA may reinforce its pharmacodynamic effect.

These findings have several translational implications: (1) platelet miR-144-5p could serve as a biomarker of ASA response, identifying patients at risk of inadequate platelet inhibition despite standard therapy; (2) assessing platelet miR-144-5p might help stratify patients who could benefit from alternative ASA dosing strategies, such as twice-daily administration or combined antiplatelet regimens; (3) strategies aimed at enhancing platelet miR-144-5p levels, either pharmacologically or via targeted miRNA delivery, could represent novel therapeutic approaches to reinforce COX-1 inhibition in high-risk individuals; (4) given its effect on endothelial COX-1 and COX-2 expression, miR-144-5p might modulate vascular inflammation and remodeling, raising the possibility that its regulation extends beyond platelet biology to systemic cardiovascular protection.

The combination of higher platelet turnover and enhanced COX-1 synthesis could therefore represent a critical determinant of accelerated COX-1 recovery and a suboptimal ASA response, leading to persistent thromboxane production and residual platelet reactivity despite therapy. This mechanistic axis links molecular alterations (miR-144-5p deficiency, increased COX-1 transcription and translation) with clinical phenotypes (poor ASA response, higher thrombotic risk) and provides a biologically coherent framework that integrates our new findings with previous mechanistic evidence.

Beyond the direct pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic alterations, another layer of regulation may involve EVs. The inclusion of platelet-derived EVs in this study was aimed at investigating the distribution and trafficking of miR-144-5p rather than identifying the primary pharmacological target of aspirin. Although aspirin primarily acts on platelet COX-1, the analysis of EVs allowed us to explore the extracellular dimension of miRNA biology. In particular, the concomitant reduction of miR-144-5p within platelets and its relative enrichment in EVs suggest an active process of miRNA export, which cannot be captured by platelet measurements alone. These nanosized particles, including exosomes and microvesicles, serve as important mediators of intercellular communication by transferring proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, notably miRNAs.19 Platelets and megakaryocytes are recognized sources of EVs enriched in specific miRNAs that modulate vascular homeostasis, inflammation, and thrombosis.20 In this context, it is conceivable that platelet-derived miR-144-5p contained within EVs may be preferentially taken up or sequestered by endothelial cells or leukocytes, given the well-established interactions between platelet EVs, endothelial cells, and immune cells. Such sequestration could further reduce intracellular platelet availability of miR-144-5p, thereby amplifying COX-1 derepression within platelets while simultaneously modulating vascular or inflammatory pathways in recipient cells. This possibility warrants dedicated mechanistic studies to clarify whether EV-mediated transfer represents a compensatory vascular signal or a maladaptive mechanism contributing to residual platelet reactivity.

In line with this hypothesis, in our cohort we observed that platelet miR-144-5p was reduced in platelets and increased in platelet-derived EVs among subjects with lower aspirin responsiveness, suggesting that transfer of the miRNA from platelets to platelet-derived EVs may be related to the accelerated recovery of COX-1. Coordinated regulation of miRNA biogenesis, intracellular retention, and vesicular export may explain the complex interaction between the intracellular and EV compartments. Platelet miRNA expression is tightly coupled to megakaryocytic transcriptional programs, and global miRNA abundance can vary among individuals depending on precursor synthesis or metabolic/inflammatory cues.21,22 Similar parallel fluctuations between intracellular and EV-associated miRNAs have been described in other hematopoietic cells,23,24 supporting the concept that vesicular release may reflect total miRNA production dynamics rather than a simple linear depletion model.

The apparent discrepancy between reduced intracellular platelet miR-144-5p levels and its relative enrichment in EVs likely reflects an active and selective export mechanism rather than passive release. Previous studies have demonstrated that platelet miRNAs are selectively packaged into EVs in a stimulus-dependent manner and that extracellular levels do not necessarily mirror intracellular abundance.3,20,23 This supports the concept of regulated miRNA sorting and trafficking.

From a mechanistic perspective, intracellular depletion of miR-144-5p would relieve posttranscriptional repression of COX-1 within platelets, promoting enhanced enzyme expression and thromboxane generation. Conversely, the enrichment of miR-144-5p in EVs suggests a potential role in intercellular communication, as EVs can transfer miRNAs to recipient endothelial or immune cells, where they may modulate COX-1/2 pathways. A similar dissociation between intracellular and EV-associated miRNA pools has been described in other systems, supporting a model of functional compartmentalization rather than simple depletion.24,25

It is also noteworthy that, despite the relative enrichment of miR-144-5p in platelet-derived EVs, total circulating levels remained unchanged. This apparent discrepancy can be explained by the heterogeneous nature of circulating miRNA pools. Indeed, most circulating miRNAs are not vesicle-associated but are instead bound to Argonaute proteins or lipoproteins.26,27 As a result, EV-associated miRNAs represent only a fraction of the total circulating miRNA pool, and changes in EV cargo may be diluted in bulk plasma measurements and therefore may not be detectable.

Moreover, EVs are rapidly taken up by recipient cells, limiting their accumulation in the circulation. Importantly, EV-associated miRNAs function as targeted intercellular signaling molecules capable of modulating gene expression in recipient cells, even in the absence of measurable changes in total circulating levels.25,28

The mechanisms underlying this apparent compartmentalized regulation are likely multifactorial. First, platelet miRNA content is largely inherited from megakaryocytes, and our in vitro findings showing ASA-induced upregulation of miR-144-5p in DAMI cells suggest that upstream regulation at the megakaryocyte level may contribute to miRNA availability in circulating platelets.21,22 Second, platelets retain an active RNA regulatory machinery and are capable of selectively sorting specific miRNAs into EVs through regulated, stimulus-dependent processes involving RNA-binding proteins.20,23 Third, circulating factors such as inflammatory and metabolic signals, as well as platelet activation itself, may dynamically influence both EV release and miRNA cargo composition.21

Obesity is increasingly recognized as a condition that profoundly reshapes the circulating EV landscape. Studies have demonstrated that individuals with obesity release higher numbers of EVs, with altered size distribution and modified molecular cargo, including miRNAs involved in angiogenesis, glucose/lipid metabolism, and inflammatory signaling.25,29

Taken together, the interplay between obesity, aspirin bioavailability, and EV-mediated miRNA trafficking suggests a multilevel mechanism that is decreased systemic exposure to aspirin reduces miR-144 induction, whereas obesity-related alterations in EV release and cargo composition may further impair its intercellular transfer. This dual mechanism could help explain the persistent platelet activation and incomplete suppression of thromboxane observed in patients with obesity despite standard low-dose aspirin therapy and warrants future mechanistic studies to confirm EV-mediated shuttling of miR-144-5p as a contributor to residual platelet reactivity.

Importantly, given the cross-sectional nature of our study, these associations should not be interpreted as evidence of causality but rather as hypothesis-generating observations that require confirmation in longitudinal and mechanistic studies.

This study has limitations. First, it was conducted in a single center, which may limit generalizability to broader populations. Second, our analysis was hypothesis-driven and limited to selected candidate miRNAs, and therefore cannot exclude the involvement of other miRNAs or epigenetic regulators. Third, functional validation was performed in cell lines (DAMI and HUVECs), which, although informative, may not fully replicate in vivo megakaryocyte or endothelial biology. The use of HUVECs as an endothelial model represents a simplification of vascular biology, and the observed effects of miR-144-5p on COX expression may not fully translate to in vivo conditions. Finally, our study design was cross-sectional with respect to clinical correlations; thus, causality between altered miR-144-5p levels and vascular phenotypes cannot be inferred. Because our analysis was hypothesis-driven and focused on selected candidate miRNAs based on previous biological and translational evidence, we cannot exclude the involvement of additional miRNAs not investigated in this study. Unbiased, high-throughput miRNA profiling approaches may provide a more comprehensive characterization of aspirin-induced miRNA modulation and should be explored in future studies.

Future studies should investigate the temporal regulation of miR-144-5p following ASA exposure in vivo, as well as its predictive value for clinical outcomes such as myocardial infarction, stroke, or stent thrombosis. Unbiased miRNA profiling could uncover additional regulators of ASA response, potentially leading to a broader miRNA signature predictive of treatment efficacy. Moreover, in vivo models of genetic or pharmacological modulation of miR-144-5p will be essential to confirm its role as a therapeutic target.

In conclusion, our findings identify miR-144-5p as a potential regulator of COX expression and a candidate biomarker associated with variability in aspirin response. Reduced platelet miR-144-5p levels were associated with enhanced platelet activation and adverse cardiometabolic features, whereas in vitro data support a role for miR-144-5p in modulating COX-1/2 expression.

However, the cellular origin and in vivo functional effects of miR-144-5p, including its potential transfer via EVs to vascular tissues, were not directly assessed and warrant further investigation.

Although our in vitro findings support a potential mechanistic link between miR-144-5p and COX regulation, they do not establish causality in vivo and should be interpreted within a broader biological context in which multiple pathways may contribute to aspirin response.

Overall, these findings provide a translational framework linking miR-144-5p to aspirin responsiveness and vascular biology, while highlighting the need for dedicated mechanistic and in vivo studies.
