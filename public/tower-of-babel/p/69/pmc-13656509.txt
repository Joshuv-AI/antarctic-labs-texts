Genome-wide identification of MYB transcription factors in Rheum officinale Baill. and their responses to UV-B and MeJA treatment
Authors: Hui Lu, Heng Pan, Er-yu Kou, Xiao-hui He, Gao-chang Cui, Feng Yan, Yi-min Li, Xiao-Chen Hu, Gang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRheum officinale Baill., a foundational plant in traditional Chinese medicine, whose roots and rhizomes exhibit significant pharmacological activities primarily attributed to anthraquinones and other bioactive compounds. However, the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms underlying anthraquinone biosynthesis are not well understood, and the key regulatory factors require further investigation. MYB transcription factors play central roles in secondary metabolism and stress signaling, suggesting their potential involvement in anthraquinone regulation, particularly in response to environmental cues such as UV-B radiation and hormonal signals like methyl jasmonate (MeJA).ResultsWe performed a genome-wide identification of MYB genes in R. officinale, identifying 237 RoMYBs, including 150 R2R3-MYBs, 81 R1-MYBs, four 3R-MYBs, and two atypical members. Phylogenetic, structural, and evolutionary analyses revealed conserved subfamily organization and gene family expansion driven primarily by segmental duplication. Notably, UV-B treatment significantly induced the accumulation of several anthraquinones, including rhein and emodin, in a time-dependent manner. Expression profiling identified RoMYBs responsive to both MeJA and UV-B, with RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 showing consistent upregulation that correlated with anthraquinone accumulation. Co-expression analysis further revealed that these two genes strongly correlated with multiple CHS-related structural genes in the anthraquinone biosynthetic pathway. Subcellular localization analysis confirmed nuclear localization of both proteins, and transcriptional activity assays revealed that RoMYB128 functions as a transcriptional activator.ConclusionsThis study provides the first comprehensive genomic overview of the MYB family in R. officinale and highlights candidate regulators that integrate light and hormone signaling to modulate anthraquinone biosynthesis, thereby offering a foundation for future mechanistic studies and metabolic engineering efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09435-4.Keywords: Rheum officinale Baill., Anthraquinone biosynthesis, UV-B, MeJA, MYB

Introduction

Plant secondary metabolites, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, terpenoids, and alkaloids, function not only as protective compounds conferring antioxidative capacity, UV shielding, and stress resistance, but also as key bioactive constituents underlying the pharmacological properties of traditional medicinal plants [1–3]. The biosynthesis and accumulation of these metabolites are governed by multiple environmental and hormonal signals, among which ultraviolet B (UV-B) radiation and jasmonic acid (JA) are recognized as critical upstream elicitors [4, 5].

UV-B radiation (280–315 nm) exerts a particularly significant influence on plant metabolism. Although excessive UV-B exposure can cause photo-oxidative damage and growth inhibition, moderate UV-B acts as a key environmental signal that triggers complex molecular responses, promoting the accumulation of secondary metabolites that contribute to plant stress tolerance and the therapeutic value of medicinal plants [5–7]. Notably, plants inhabiting high-altitude regions are exposed to naturally elevated UV-B radiation, and this distinct ecological setting profoundly affects the biosynthesis of bioactive compounds, ultimately determining the quality of authentic medicinal materials [8, 9]. Growing evidence indicates that UV-B signaling frequently intersects with phytohormone pathways, particularly the jasmonic acid (JA) pathway, to fine-tune metabolic responses. In Arabidopsis, UV-B perception via UVR8 activates JA biosynthesis, modulating anthocyanin accumulation and stress tolerance [10]. In medicinal species such as Salvia miltiorrhiza and Ginkgo biloba, light and JA signaling coordinates with transcription factors to regulate tanshinone and ginkgolide accumulation, respectively [4, 11].

MYB transcription factors are central regulators of secondary metabolism, stress responses, and hormone signaling [12]. Based on conserved MYB repeats, they are classified into 1R-, R2R3-, 3R-, and 4R-MYB subfamilies, with R2R3-MYBs being particularly prominent in controlling phenylpropanoid and flavonoid pathways [13]. MYBs often act as molecular hubs integrating environmental and hormonal signals. For example, SmMYB36 coordinates with SmJAZ-SmMYC2 to modulate JA-mediated tanshinone accumulation [14]; MrMYB95 functions as a UV-B-responsive repressor of flavonol biosynthesis [15]; and GlMYB4/88, FtMYB13–16, and GpMYB81 mediate MeJA-induced flavonoid regulation in various medicinal species [16–18]. These examples highlight MYBs as important TFs in regulation of secondary metabolism in medicinal plants.

Rheum officinale Baill. (Da-huang) is a principal source of rhubarb, possessing purgative, antipyretic, blood-activating, and blood-cooling properties [19]. The plant predominantly grows in high-altitude regions (1,500-4,400 m) of China, where it is exposed to naturally high UV-B radiation [20]. Anthraquinones are its major bioactive compounds, and their accumulation responds to both environmental cues and hormonal signals [21]. With wild resources declining, cultivated R. officinale has become the main medicinal source, making it imperative to understand the molecular mechanisms controlling its quality-related metabolites [22–24]. Our previous work showed that MeJA rapidly enhances anthraquinones content in Rheum palmatum, suggesting that JA signaling may be a key mediator of UV-B-induced metabolic adaptation in Rheum species [25].

To identify MYB genes integrating environmental and hormonal signals, we applied both UV-B and MeJA treatments and prioritized candidates co-responsive to both stimuli. Phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, chromosomal distributions, and expression patterns across tissues and treatments of MYB family members were analyzed. Furthermore, co-expression analyses between candidate RoMYBs and anthraquinone biosynthetic genes were conducted to infer potential regulatory relationships. Basic functional characterization analyses of key candidate genes were conducted using qRT-PCR, subcellular localization assays, and transcriptional activation experiments. Collectively, these results provide a foundation for elucidating MYB-mediated regulatory networks controlling secondary metabolism in R. officinale.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

Seeds of Rheum officinale used in this study were obtained from a cultivated base in Taoshuwan Village (Guanyin Town, Zhenba County, Shaanxi Province, China) in September 2023 and were identified by Prof. Yan Yonggang (Shaanxi University of Traditional Chinese Medicine). Uniform and plump seeds of R. officinale were selected, germinated, and sown in plastic pots (9 cm diameter × 12.5 cm height) filled with 200 g of peat substrate. Five seeds were sown in each pot. The plants were cultivated under controlled environmental conditions with a 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod, a light intensity of 9000 Lx, and a constant temperature of 23 ± 2 °C. Each pot was initially watered with 200 mL, followed by supplemental watering with 50 mL every three days.

After one month of growth, healthy and uniform R. officinale seedlings were selected for stress treatments. The seedlings were divided into three groups: MeJA treatment group, in which plants were sprayed with 200 µmol L⁻¹ MeJA solution; UV-B treatment group, where plants were exposed to UV-B radiation at an intensity of 95–120 µW/cm²; and control group. Both the MeJA and UV-B treatments were conducted under a 16/8 h light/dark photoperiod. Control plants were maintained under the same conditions but received only a standard nutrient solution, following the method of Zhao et al. [26], without additional stress treatment.

For each treatment, leaf samples were collected at specific time points post-induction. For the MeJA group, samples were collected at 2, 6, 12, and 24 h. For the UV-B treatment group, samples were collected at 4, 12, 24, and 48 h. Untreated seedlings collected at 0 h served as the blank control. Each treatment at each time point comprised three independent biological replicates. All samples were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction.

Identification and physicochemical properties analyses of RoMYB gene family members

The MYB gene sequences of A. thaliana were obtained from Plant Transcription Factor Database (PlantTFDB Database; https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/index.php). Using 198 AtMYB sequences as references, Blast searches were conducted to identify MYB genes in R. officinale. Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles corresponding to MYB domains (PF00249, PF08914, PF12776, PF13873, PF13837, PF13921, and PF15963) were acquired from the Pfam protein database (http://pfam-legacy.xfam.org/). Candidate MYB genes were identified by integrating the results from both the blast and Pfam analyses. These candidate genes were submitted to the NCBI Batch Conserved Domain Database (CDD) search tool for validation. SMART (https://smart.embl.de/) and ExPASy PROSITE databases (https://prosite.expasy.org/prosite.html) were further used to examine their conserved domains. Genes lacking conserved MYB structural domains were excluded. The physicochemical properties of the identified RoMYB proteins, including amino acids length, molecular weight, coding sequence length, isoelectric point, instability index, aliphatic index, and grand average of hydropathicity, were predicted using TBtools [27]. Primary and secondary structures features were predicted using ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) and SOPMA (https://npsa-pr-abi.ibcp.fr/cgi-bin/npsaautomat.pl?page=npsasopma), respectively. Subcellular localization of RoMYB proteins was predicted using WoLF PSORT (https://www.genscript.com/wolf-psort.html).

Phylogenetic and protein sequence analyses of the RoMYB gene family

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships among RoMYBs, phylogenetic trees were constructed separately for different MYB subfamilies. 154 R2R3-MYB and 3R-MYB protein sequences from R. officinale were aligned with 131 corresponding R2R3-MYB and 3R-MYB sequences from A. thaliana using Muscle. 81 1R-MYB sequences from R. officinale were aligned with 62 1R-MYB sequences from A. thaliana. For both alignments, phylogenetic trees were constructed in MEGA using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method with the following parameters: p-distance model, partial deletion, and 1000 bootstrap replicates. The resulting phylogenetic trees were subsequently visualized using the online tool ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/). Conserved motifs in all RoMYB proteins were identified using the MEME suite, with the maximum number of motifs set to 10 and other parameters set to default values. Motif patterns were visualized using TBtools. All sequence identifiers used in the phylogenetic analyses are listed in supplementary Table S1.

Chromosome localization and collinearity analysis of RoMYBs

The chromosomal locations of all identified RoMYBs were extracted from the R. officinale GFF3 annotation file using TBtools, which mapped 237 genes on the 11 chromosomes. Using the same software, the genome sequence files were processed to obtain chromosome length and gene density information. Intraspecific collinearity analysis was then performed using the One Step MCScanX tool in TBtools. Furthermore, interspecific collinearity analyses were conducted between R. officinale and four representative species (A. thaliana, Fagopyrum tataricum (L.) Gaertn, Solanum lycopersicum L and Oryza sativa) following a similar workflow to generate comparative synteny plots.

Determination of the components of R. officinale after UV-B treatment

One-month-old seedlings were exposed to UV-B irradiation at an intensity of 130 µW/cm², with leaf samples collected at 0 (control), 4, 12, 24, and 48 h post-treatment, each time point comprising three biological replicates. Authentic standards, including gallic acid, catechin, sennoside B, chrysophanol-8-O-β-D-glucopyranoside, emodin-8-O-β-D-glucoside, aloe-emodin, rhein, emodin, chrysophanol and physcion, were obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Quantitative analysis of ten components was performed using the liquid chromatography determination method and sample preparation method outlined in previous studies [26]. The chromatographic peak areas for each component were measured to calculate the mass fraction of each component in the sample.

Transcriptome analysis of RoMYBs based on RNA-seq data

To examine the tissue-specific expression of the 237 RoMYBs over different years and the transcriptional responses to MeJA and UV-B treatments, RNA-seq data were analyzed and visualized as heatmaps using the heatmap function in TBtools. Subsequently, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) under each treatment were identified based on thresholds of |log₂FoldChange| > 1 and P-value < 0.05. For experimental validation, total RNA was extracted from the corresponding samples using the Novizan RNA extraction kit, with RNA quality assessed by a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer and an Agilent 2100 Bio-analyzer. First strand cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (TaKaRa Biotechnology, Dalian, China), followed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) performed with the One Step SYBR PrimeScript PLUS RT-PCR kit on a QuantStudio system, using β-actin as the internal reference gene. All primer sequences used for qRT-PCR are listed in supplementary Table S2.

Co-expression correlation analysis

Protein sequences of enzymes from the mevalonate (MVA), methylerythritol phosphate (MEP), shikimate, and polyketide pathways were retrieved from the NCBI database and used as BLASTP queries (E-value < 1 × 10⁻¹⁰) against the R. officinale protein database. Candidate genes potentially involved in anthraquinone biosynthesis were identified using BLASTP results combined with conserved domain analysis [28]. Expression matrices for the candidate enzyme and MYB genes were extracted from RNA-seq FPKM data. Spearman correlation coefficients were then calculated for each treatment condition using R software. The results were visualized using ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/).

Subcellular localization

Target gene fragments were amplified from fresh R. officinale cDNA using gene-specific primers (pCAMBIA1305-RoMYBs-F/R). The resulting PCR products and the linearized pCAMBIA1305-GFP plasmid were digested with Spe I and BamH I restriction enzymes (QuickCut, TaKaRa), gel-purified, and ligated using T4 DNA ligase. The confirmed recombinant plasmids-pCAMBIA1305-GFP (empty vector control), pCAMBIA1305-RoMYB128-GFP, and pCAMBIA1305-RoMYB134-GFP-were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 via the calcium chloride transformation method (Shanghai Weidi). Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression was performed by infiltrating the abaxial epidermis of the second to fourth leaves of N. benthamiana. After 36 h of co-cultivation at 25°C, the infiltrated leaf areas were stained with 10 µg/mL 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 20 min, rinsed three times with phosphate buffer (pH 7.2), and observed under an Olympus FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope. All primers used for RoMYBs cloning are listed in supplementary Table S3.

Transcriptional activity analysis

For transcriptional activity analysis, target fragments were amplified from fresh R. officinale cDNA using PGBKT7-RoMYBs-F/R primers. The target genes and the pGBKT7 vector were double-digested with QuickCut restriction endonucleases respectively (EcoR I and BamH I were used for PGBKT7-RoMYB128 and PGBKT7-RoMYB134), and the expression vectors were constructed as described above. The recombinant plasmids pGBKT7-RoMYB128 and pGBKT7-RoMYB134 were transformed into AH109 yeast competent cells, then plated on SD-Trp-His-Ade and SD-Trp-His-Ade-X-α-gal as previously described [25]. The primers used for RoMYBs cloning are shown in supplementary Table S3.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 25) (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan’s multiple range test were used to determine the significant differences (P < 0.05).

Results

Identification of MYB gene family members in R. officinale and analysis of their physicochemical properties

A total of 237 RoMYB genes were identified in R. officinale. Detailed information on RoMYBs is provided in supplementary Table S4, including 1R-MYB (81), 2R-MYB (150), 3R-MYB (4), and atypical MYB (2). The polypeptide lengths of RoMYB protein ranged from 62 amino acids (RoMYB186) to 1844 amino acids (RoMYB36), with an average length of 332 amino acids. The molecular weights of the RoMYB proteins ranged from 7.09 kDa (RoMYB186) to 201.88 kDa (RoMYB36). Theoretical isoelectric points ranged from 4.53 (RoMYB110) to 11.2 (RoMYB68). The GRAVY values of all RoMYB proteins were below zero, indicating their hydrophilic nature. Twelve RoMYB proteins (RoMYB20, RoMYB30, RoMYB39, RoMYB52, RoMYB79, RoMYB89, RoMYB103, RoMYB126, RoMYB127, RoMYB147, RoMYB159, and RoMYB182) exhibited an instability index below 40, suggesting that these proteins are likely stable. Secondary structure analysis revealed that all RoMYB proteins lacked β-turns, and 37 members also lacked extended strands. In the remaining proteins, random coils were the most abundant structural element, followed by α-helices and extended strands. Subcellular localization prediction indicated that 92.41% of the RoMYB proteins were localized to the nucleus, with 2.95% in the cytoplasm, 3.80% in the chloroplast, and 3.80% in the mitochondria.

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of the RoMYB gene family

To explore the homologous relationships of MYB proteins between A. thaliana and R. officinale, phylogenetic trees of R2R3-MYB, 3R-MYB (Fig. 1A), and R1-MYB proteins (supplementary Fig. S1) were constructed using the neighbor-joining method. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that four 3R-MYB proteins from R. officinale (RoMYB21, RoMYB33, RoMYB172, and RoMYB213) clustered with five 3R-MYB proteins from A. thaliana, forming subfamily C32, representing a distinct evolutionary branch within the 3R-MYB subfamily. In subfamily C28 (S22), there were up to 14 RoMYBs, whereas subfamily C30 contained only one RoMYB. Subfamilies C22 (S12), C14 (S7), and C27 (S23) contained only A. thaliana MYB genes, with no R. officinale MYB genes. The 81 1R-MYB genes identified in R. officinale (supplementary Fig. S1) were classified into seven subfamilies: R-R-type, CCA1-like, CPC-like, I-box-binding-like, TBP-like, atypical, and TRF-like. Among these, the TBP-like subfamily was the largest, containing 32 members, whereas the I-box-binding-like subfamily was the smallest, containing three members.

To evaluate the expansion of the MYB family in R. officinale, we compared the number of MYB genes with other plant species for which genome-wide MYB identification has been reported (Fig. 1B). In this study, a total of 237 RoMYB genes were identified, a number higher than that in O. sativa (155) [29], Solanum tuberosum (158) [30], pumpkin (175) [31], Casuarina equisetifolia (182) [32], and A. thaliana (198) [29], but is fewer than in G. max (244) [33] and Pennisetum glaucum (279) [34].

Analysis of gene structures, conserved motifs and conserved domains of RoMYBs

To further validate the phylogenetic classification and evaluate structural conservation within the RoMYB family, gene structures and conserved motif compositions were analyzed. Gene structure analysis revealed that 62 RoMYB genes lacked untranslated regions (UTRs). The R2R3-MYB and 3R-MYB genes contained 1–12 introns and 2–13 exons, with RoMYB98 harboring the highest number of introns (12). Members within the same subgroup generally exhibited similar gene structures (Fig. 2B). Conserved motif analyses were performed using MEME on 154 R2R3-MYB and 3R-MYB proteins (Fig. 2A; Table 1) and on 81 1R-MYB proteins (supplementary Fig. S2; Table 2). A total of ten conserved motifs were identified, ranging from 8 to 50 amino acids in length. Each R2R3-MYB or 3R-MYB protein contained between three and seven motifs. Conserved domain analysis revealed that all MYB proteins contained Motif 1 and Motif 3. Subfamilies containing Motifs 1–6 included C4 (S11), C5 (S10/S24), C9, C11 (S9), C12, C13, and C18. Subfamilies C6 (S1), C8 (S2), C10 (S3), and C16 (S14) contained motifs 1–5. C3 (S4), C15 (S15), C17 (S6), C19 (S5), C24, C25 (S18), and C26 (S21) contained motifs 1, 2, 3, and 5. C1 (S20), C2 (S19), C20, C21 (S13), and C23 (S16) contained motifs 2–4. Additionally, motif 7 was unique to C28 (S22); motif 8 was restricted to C2 (S19) and C24; motif 9 was specific to C32; and motif 10 occurred only in C21 (S13) and C29 (S25). Overall, members within the same subfamily displayed highly similar conserved motif compositions, supporting their close structural and evolutionary relationships.

Chromosome distribution and collinearity analysis of RoMYB genes

To investigate the evolutionary mechanisms underlying RoMYB family expansion, chromosomal distribution and gene duplication events of all 237 RoMYB genes were analyzed. The RoMYB genes were distributed across 11 chromosomes of R. officinale and were sequentially named from RoMYB1 to RoMYB237 based on their chromosomal positions (Fig. S3). The RoMYB genes were unevenly distributed across the 11 chromosomes (Fig. S3). Chromosomes 9 and 11 harbored the largest numbers of RoMYB genes (26 each), followed by chromosomes 3 and 8 (25 each), whereas chromosome 4 contained the fewest genes (12). Chromosomes 1 and 10 each contained 24 RoMYB genes, whereas chromosomes 2, 5, 6, and 7 contained 23, 15, 19, and 18 genes, respectively. A total of seven tandemly duplicated RoMYB gene pairs were identified (supplementary Table S5). Two tandem duplication pairs were identified on chromosome 1 (RoMYB3/RoMYB4 and RoMYB4/RoMYB5) and chromosome 11 (RoMYB216/RoMYB217 and RoMYB232/RoMYB233). One pair was identified on each of chromosomes 2 (RoMYB43/RoMYB44), 3 (RoMYB67/RoMYB68), and 10 (RoMYB208/RoMYB209).

In addition, segmental duplication events were analyzed using MCScanX. A total of 58 segmentally duplicated RoMYB gene pairs were identified (Fig. 3A, supplementary Table S5). Among the 11 chromosomes of R. officinale, chromosome 1 contained the highest number of segmentally duplicated gene pairs (14). Chromosome 3 contained only three segmentally duplicated gene pairs. No RoMYB genes derived from segmental duplication were detected on chromosomes 10 or 11. These findings suggest that both tandem and segmental duplication contributed to the expansion of the RoMYB gene family, with segmental duplication serving as the primary mechanism.

Collinearity analysis of RoMYBs. A Intraspecific collinearity analysis of RoMYBs. a and b: gene density; c: chromosomes. B Interspecific collinearity analysis of MYB genes between R. officinale and A. thaliana, F. tataricum, S. lycopersicum and O. sativa

To explore the evolutionary conservation of RoMYB genes, collinearity analyses were performed between R. officinale and the dicotyledonous species A. thaliana, F. tataricum, and S. lycopersicum (Fig. 3B, supplementary Table S5). A substantial number of collinear gene pairs were identified between R. officinale and these dicot species, including 75 pairs with A. thaliana, 117 pairs with F. tataricum, and 101 pairs with S. lycopersicum. In contrast, only 19 collinear gene pairs were detected between R. officinale and the monocot species O. sativa. These results indicate that R. officinale and F. tataricum, which belong to the same botanical family, exhibit the highest degree of syntenic conservation, suggesting a close evolutionary relationship. Throughout evolution, both species appear to have experienced comparable gene amplification and differentiation patterns, consistent with those reported in F. esculentum.

Expression patterns of RoMYBs in different tissues and developmental stages

The expression patterns of the RoMYB family across different tissues and developmental stages were investigated to identify genes preferentially expressed in anthraquinone-accumulating organs. RoMYB genes with no detectable expression in the transcriptome data were excluded, resulting in expression profiles for 200 RoMYB genes (Fig. 4). Detailed expression data are provided in supplementary Table S6. Based on their expression profiles, the RoMYB genes were classified into eight clusters (C1-C8) (supplementary Table S6). Clusters 3, 4, 5, and 6 were predominantly expressed in roots, comprising 25, 26, 30, and 27 genes, respectively. Clusters 3 and 6 exhibited particularly high expression levels in annual roots and rhizomes. In cluster 3, RoMYB108, RoMYB113, and RoMYB170 showed relatively high expression levels. In cluster 6, RoMYB25, RoMYB84, RoMYB156, RoMYB162, RoMYB173, and RoMYB230 displayed high expression levels. Clusters 1, 2, and 8 were primarily expressed in leaves, containing 26, 29, and 12 genes, respectively.

Expression pattern of RoMYBs under MeJA and UV-B treatments

To identify RoMYB genes potentially integrating environmental and hormonal signals involved in anthraquinone accumulation, their transcriptional responses to both MeJA and UV-B treatments were profiled. To characterize the expression patterns of RoMYB genes in leaves under MeJA treatment, heatmaps were generated based on RNA-seq data. The RoMYB genes were categorized into eight clusters (C1-C8) (supplementary Table S7). Among these, clusters 3, 6, 7, and 8 formed group II, and genes in this group exhibited an upregulation pattern. Clusters 1, 2, 4, and 5 formed group I, and genes in this group exhibited a downregulation pattern (Fig. 5A). Notably, cluster 7 contained the highest number of upregulated genes (32), with RoMYB84 and RoMYB153 showing particularly significant upregulation. Conversely, cluster 5 contained the largest number of downregulated genes (36), with RoMYB163 and RoMYB178 showing marked downregulation. Detailed expression data for RoMYB genes under MeJA treatment are provided in supplementary Table S7. The expression patterns of these genes under MeJA treatment are shown in Fig. 5B. After 2 h of MeJA treatment, RoMYB6, RoMYB54, RoMYB111, RoMYB138, RoMYB154, and RoMYB173 showed significant upregulation. In contrast, RoMYB128, RoMYB151, RoMYB156, and RoMYB203 were downregulated. After 6 h of MeJA treatment, RoMYB84 and RoMYB205 showed significant upregulation. Meanwhile, RoMYB128, RoMYB151, RoMYB156, and RoMYB203 remained significantly downregulated. After 12 h of MeJA treatment, RoMYB128 showed significant upregulation, whereas RoMYB151 and RoMYB203 showed significant downregulation. Overall, the qRT-PCR results under MeJA treatment were consistent with the RNA-seq data.

To investigate the expression responses of RoMYBs in leaves under UV-B treatment, heatmaps were also generated based on RNA-seq data and categorized into eight clusters (C1-C8) (supplementary Table S8). The expression levels of RoMYB138, RoMYB161, and RoMYB205 increased significantly, whereas RoMYB41, RoMYB61, RoMYB95, and RoMYB163 showed significant downregulation. Among these genes, RoMYB138 exhibited the most pronounced upregulation (Fig. 5C). Detailed expression data of RoMYB genes in response to UV-B are provided in supplementary Table S8. After 4 h of UV-B treatment, RoMYB6, RoMYB74, RoMYB151, RoMYB154, RoMYB173, RoMYB203, and RoMYB205 were upregulated and reached peak expression levels. After 12 h of UV-B treatment, RoMYB6, RoMYB12, RoMYB84, RoMYB111, RoMYB128, RoMYB134, RoMYB138, and RoMYB156 were upregulated and reached peak expression levels. Overall, the qRT-PCR results under UV-B treatment were consistent with the RNA-seq data (Fig. 5D). To determine whether the observed transcriptional changes under UV-B treatment were associated with alterations in secondary metabolism, we next quantified the accumulation of anthraquinones in R. officinale after UV-B treatment.

Among the examined genes, RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 maintained relatively high expression levels at 12 h under MeJA treatment, and their expression trends were largely consistent with previously reported anthraquinone accumulation dynamics [26]. After 12 h of UV-B treatment, the transcriptional levels of both genes were significantly upregulated, closely paralleling the changes in anthraquinone content observed in this study. RoMYB134 and RoMYB128 were mainly expressed in roots and leaves, respectively.

Content of anthraquinones in R. officinale after UV-B treatment

To assess the effects of UV-B treatment on the accumulation of anthraquinones in R. officinale, the contents of ten compounds were measured in 30-day-old seedlings exposed to UV-B irradiation for 0, 4, 12, 24, and 48 h. Significant differences were observed in the levels of anthraquinone derivatives following UV-B treatment (Fig. 6). Notably, the concentrations of rhein, emodin, chrysophanol, and physcion ether peaked after 12 h of UV-B treatment. In contrast, the contents of sennoside B, chrysophanol-8-O-β-D-glucopyranoside, aloe-emodin, and physcion reached their maximum levels after 48 h of UV-B treatment. However, the concentrations of gallic acid and emodin-8-O-β-D-glucoside decreased, whereas the level of chrysophanol remained unchanged. These results indicate that UV-B treatment significantly increased the accumulation of multiple anthraquinones in R. officinale, exhibiting distinct temporal dynamics.

Co-expression correlation analysis between RoMYB genes and key enzymes in the anthraquinone biosynthetic pathway

To further evaluate whether the prioritized RoMYB candidates are associated with anthraquinone biosynthesis at the pathway level, co-expression analyses were performed between 15 RoMYB genes and both anthraquinone compounds and structural enzyme genes. Co-expression analysis with anthraquinone compounds revealed that most of the 15 RoMYB genes were positively correlated with multiple anthraquinones. Notably, eight RoMYBs—RoMYB6, RoMYB54, RoMYB84, RoMYB111, RoMYB128, RoMYB134, RoMYB138, and RoMYB151—showed broad correlations with the anthraquinone compounds (Fig. 7). Based on transcriptomic, quantitative and co-expression analyses, RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 were identified as candidate genes for further analyses.

Analysis of co-expression correlations between RoMYB genes and anthraquinone compounds

To assess the relationship between RoMYB128, RoMYB134 and the anthraquinone biosynthetic pathway, 74 structural enzyme genes were identified from the MVA, MEP, shikimate, and polyketide pathways (Table S9). Co-expression analysis under UV-B treatment identified distinct treatment-specific patterns (Fig. S4; Table S10). A large number of strongly correlated gene pairs were generated under UV-B treatment, with RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 correlating with the highest number of CHS protein-related genes, totaling 16. These findings suggest that RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 may play a role in modulating anthraquinone biosynthesis via the UV-B signaling pathway.

Subcellular localization and transcriptional activation of RoMYB128 and RoMYB134

Before investigating RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 potential roles in anthraquinone biosynthesis, we first characterized their fundamental properties as transcription factors through subcellular localization and transcriptional activation assays. Fluorescence microscopy showed that the GFP control signal was distributed throughout the cell, whereas fluorescence signals from the RoMYB128-GFP and RoMYB134-GFP fusion proteins were restricted to the nucleus. These results indicate that RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 are nuclear-localized proteins in plant cells (Fig. 8A). Cells expressing pGBKT7-RoMYB128 grew well on selective SD/-Trp/-His/-Ade medium and on the same medium supplemented with X-α-Gal, exhibiting strong β-galactosidase activity. In contrast, cells expressing pGBKT7-RoMYB134 failed to grow on these selective media. Together, these results indicate that RoMYB128 exhibits transcriptional activation activity, whereas RoMYB134 lacks self-activation activity in the yeast system (Fig. 8B).

Discussion

R. officinale, R. palmatum, and R. tanguticum are the primary botanical sources of rhubarb used in traditional Chinese medicine. Anthraquinone compounds derived from these species exhibit diverse pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antitumor effects [35, 36]. However, genomic and transcriptional regulatory information on diploid rhubarb, which is widely used in modern medicinal applications and cultivation, remains limited. This limitation hampers comprehensive investigations into anthraquinone biosynthesis and its underlying regulatory mechanisms. Transcriptional regulators that fine-tune secondary metabolism are critical starting points for elucidating biosynthetic mechanisms of bioactive compounds in medicinal plants [37].

Numerous studies have demonstrated that MYB transcription factors play fundamental regulatory roles in secondary metabolism, hormone signaling, and abiotic stress responses in plants [38]. In medicinal plants, MYB transcription factors play particularly prominent regulatory roles in the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. For example, TcMYB73 positively regulates paclitaxel biosynthesis in Taxus chinensis [39], AaMYB121 significantly enhances artemisinin accumulation in Artemisia annua [40] and SmMYB52 promotes the accumulation of tanshinone IIB in Salvia miltiorrhiza [41]. Although MYB transcription factors have been extensively studied in model and medicinal plants, their family characteristics and evolutionary patterns in R. officinale remain poorly understood.

In this study, a total of 237 RoMYB genes were identified based on whole-genome data. Compared with other reported plant species, the number of RoMYB genes shows substantial variation: it is higher than that in O. sativa (155) [29], Solanum tuberosum (158) [30], pumpkin (175) [31], Casuarina equisetifolia (182) [32], and A. thaliana (198) [29], but is fewer than in G. max (244) [33] and Pennisetum glaucum (279) [34]. The identified RoMYBs comprise 81 1R-MYBs, 150 R2R3-MYBs, 4 3R-MYBs, and 2 atypical members. The predominance of the R2R3-MYB subfamily is consistent with its central regulatory roles in higher plants. Based on this classification and with reference to the A. thaliana MYB classification system, RoMYBs were further divided into 31 subfamilies (Fig. 1). Certain subfamilies, such as C28 (S22), exhibited notable expansion, comprising 14 members, whereas C22 (S12), C14 (S7), and C27 (S23) were absent in R. officinale. Similar patterns of subfamily absence have also been reported in Salvia miltiorrhiza [42], Pea [43], and Lycium chinense [44], suggesting lineage-specific gene retention events during the evolutionary history of R. officinale. Conserved motif analysis revealed that motif 2 and motif 3 constitute the core domains of RoMYB proteins (Fig. 2). These motifs displayed highly consistent arrangement patterns within the same subfamily, suggesting functional conservation.

Gene duplication is a major driving force underlying plant genome expansion and evolution and is also a primary mechanism promoting gene family expansion [45]. In this study, RoMYB genes were unevenly distributed across chromosomes. A total of seven tandemly duplicated gene pairs and 58 segmentally duplicated RoMYB gene pairs were identified (Fig. S3 and 3 A), indicating that segmental duplication is the primary mechanism driving the expansion of the MYB gene family in R. officinale. Similar duplication-driven expansion has been reported in Artemisia argyi [46], Nicotiana tabacum L [47] and Coptis teeta Wall [48]. Given that R. officinale thrives in high-altitude habitats exposed to elevated UV-B radiation, it is plausible that these expanded subfamilies have been evolutionarily recruited to regulate secondary metabolites, which may function as photoprotective compounds under UV-B stress. To further investigate the evolutionary conservation, cross-species collinearity analysis was performed (Fig. 3B). The results revealed a large number of conserved collinear gene pairs between R. officinale and closely related Polygonaceae species, whereas fewer were shared with distantly related species such as A. thaliana, suggesting that the core framework of the RoMYB gene family was established early during species divergence. Notably, several RoMYBs maintained strict collinearity relationships across A. thaliana, F. tataricum, S. lycopersicum, and O. sativa, indicating strong purifying selection during long-term evolution and suggesting conserved functions in fundamental developmental processes or core metabolic.

Recent studies have performed sequence and evolutionary analyses of MYB transcription factors in four Rheum species, speculating on their potential association with anthraquinone biosynthesis from a phylogenetic perspective [49]. However, the molecular mechanisms by which environmental signals regulate MYB-mediated anthraquinone biosynthesis remain largely unexplored. Both MeJA and UV-B are key elicitors of secondary metabolism. Our study demonstrates that UV-B treatment induces the accumulation of multiple anthraquinones in R. officinale seedlings in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 6), with several components peaking at 12 h post-treatment. This temporal pattern is consistent with previously reported MeJA-induced anthraquinone accumulation [25]. Meanwhile, previous studies have demonstrated that UV-B exposure induces JA biosynthesis via the UVR8–TCP4–LOX2 signaling module, leading to the activation of JA-responsive genes and modulation of the anthocyanin biosynthesis pathway [9]. These findings indicate that UV-B radiation can induce the accumulation of JA and its derivatives, thereby activating defense responses and regulating secondary metabolism–related pathways [3, 50], suggesting potential crosstalk between UV-B and MeJA signaling pathways. To identify potential regulators within this regulatory network, we analyzed RoMYB expression patterns. Tissue-specific expression profiling revealed distinct expression clusters, with certain genes preferentially expressed in roots or rhizomes, which are the primary sites of anthraquinone storage (Fig. 4). Under UV-B and MeJA treatments, transcriptome sequencing and qRT-PCR analyses identified a subset of RoMYB genes exhibiting significant transcriptional responses (Fig. 5). Among these, RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 were notably upregulated by both treatments and displayed tissue-enriched expression patterns correlated with anthraquinone accumulation, highlighting them as priority candidates for further functional validation.

Subcellular localization analysis revealed that both RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 proteins are localized in the nucleus (Fig. 8A), consistent with the canonical characteristics of MYB transcription factors. However, transcriptional activation assays revealed clear functional divergence: RoMYB128 acted as a strong transcriptional activator, whereas RoMYB134 exhibited no intrinsic transactivation activity (Fig. 8B). This functional divergence is consistent with previous observations within the plant MYB transcription factor family. For instance, AtMYB12 directly stimulates the expression of flavonol biosynthesis genes in A. thaliana [51], whereas other MYBs, such as the repressive factors PcMYB114 and PcMYB306, function through protein–protein interactions within regulatory complexes in Polygonum cuspidatum [52, 53]. Analogous to the well-characterized MYB–bHLH–WD40 complexes that control anthocyanin biosynthesis [54], we hypothesize that RoMYB134 may require interactions with co-factors (e.g., bHLH or WD40 proteins) to regulate anthraquinone biosynthesis, whereas RoMYB128 may function as a direct transcriptional activator.

Collectively, these findings suggest that RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 may function as regulatory nodes integrating UV-B and JA signaling to fine-tune anthraquinone biosynthesis in R. officinale. Although these characteristics are essential features of transcriptional regulators, they do not, by themselves, establish a direct role in anthraquinone biosynthesis. However, combined with the co-expression analysis showing that both genes are strongly correlated with multiple CHS-related structural genes, these findings further support their candidacy as potential regulators of the anthraquinone biosynthetic pathway. Future studies combining DNA-binding assays and functional genetic approaches will be required to determine whether RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 directly regulate anthraquinone biosynthetic genes.

Conclusion

This study presents the first genome-wide identification and analysis of the MYB transcription factor family in R. officinale. A total of 237 RoMYB genes were identified in the R. officinale genome. Phylogenetic and structural analyses revealed a high degree of evolutionary conservation as well as distinct subfamily-specific features within the RoMYB family. Gene duplication analysis indicated that segmental duplication was the primary driving force underlying the expansion of the RoMYB family. Notably, UV-B treatment significantly induced the accumulation of multiple anthraquinones, including rhein, emodin, and physcion, with distinct temporal dynamics. Expression profiling revealed distinct tissue-specific expression patterns of RoMYB genes and identified several members that were co-regulated by MeJA and UV-B treatments. Among these, RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 showed strong transcriptional responsiveness, and their expression patterns were correlated with anthraquinone accumulation trends. Functional analyses confirmed that both proteins are localized in the nucleus, with RoMYB128 exhibiting transcriptional activation activity. Collectively, these results suggest that RoMYB128 and RoMYB134 may function as key transcriptional regulators integrating UV-B environmental cues and JA signaling, thereby participating in the regulation of anthraquinone biosynthesis. This study provides a valuable foundation for future investigations into the transcriptional regulatory networks underlying secondary metabolism in R. officinale.
