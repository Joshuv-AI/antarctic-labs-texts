Safety of Gadoterate Meglumine: A Review of 35 Years of Clinical Use and More Than 170 Million Doses
Authors: Imran Shahid, Joëlle Morvan, Elisabeth Darmon-Kern, Frantz Hebert, Eric Lancelot, Philippe Bourrinet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aim:The aim of this review was to evaluate the safety profile of gadoterate meglumine from published literature and pharmacovigilance reports of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) encompassing 35 years of clinical use and more than 170 million administered doses.Materials and Methods:A review of published literature up to December 31, 2024 was performed through a search in Embase and PubMed databases. The analysis focused on current safety concerns associated with gadolinium-based contrast agents, the hypersensitivity reactions (HSRs), nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (NSF), gadolinium accumulation, and symptoms associated with gadolinium exposure.Results:A total of 62 publications reporting safety results for gadoterate meglumine were included. Age ranged from a few weeks to 103 years, including patients with all stages of renal impairment. The incidence of HSRs was reported in 17 studies and ranged from <0.01% to 0.58%. Occurrence of NSF was analyzed in 9 studies; to date, no confirmed unconfounded case of NSF diagnosis has been associated with gadoterate meglumine use. Regarding gadolinium presence in the body, 17 studies did not show any significant signal intensity increase in the deep brain in patients after up to 53 exposures, while 4 showed some signal intensity increase in specific subgroups after repeated exposure, but without any clinical symptoms. Symptoms associated with gadolinium exposure have been described in 8 studies, although no causal relationship with gadolinium deposition was established. Safety data obtained from pharmacovigilance monitoring showed an incidence of adverse reactions in 8 cases per 100,000 patients exposed. The most frequently reported adverse reactions were urticaria, nausea, and vomiting (0.001% each), while the incidence of HSRs was less than 0.00035%. No unconfounded NSF reports were received.Conclusions:Clinical evidence from the published data and pharmacovigilance monitoring demonstrated that gadoterate meglumine is a safe magnetic resonance imaging contrast agent for all age groups in a variety of approved indications throughout the whole body, including in patients with renal impairment.

Gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs) are used to increase the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity in a wide variety of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) applications, including the detection of focal lesions (such as tumors or abscesses) and imaging of blood vessels (magnetic resonance angiography [MRA]). These agents are classified based on their molecular structure as linear or macrocyclic. The macrocyclic GBCAs are more stable and have a lower risk of releasing free gadolinium ions.1

Gadoterate meglumine (or gadoteric acid, Dotarem, Magnescope in Japan) was developed by Guerbet (Roissy CdG, France) in the 1980s. It is composed of gadolinium in complex with DOTA (tetraxetan) and meglumine salts, which makes it the only ionic, macrocyclic and highly stable GBCA. It was first approved in France in March 1989. Gadoterate meglumine is now approved in more than 80 countries worldwide (including Europe and USA) for various indications (MRI of central nervous system [CNS] body and MRA). The recommended dose for gadoterate meglumine is 0.1 mmol/kg (0.2 mL/kg) by intravenous (IV) administration, in MRI of CNS and body in adults as well as in pediatric patients (from neonates to 17 years old), and for MRA indications in adults. Data from the IQVIA-MIDAS database (2008–2022) show that gadoterate meglumine is one of the most frequently used GBCAs worldwide and the first in Europe (49% of GBCA uses).2 The estimated number of gadoterate meglumine administrations is more than 170 million worldwide, based on sales data.

GBCAs in general have shown an excellent safety profile and favorable benefit/risk ratio with a low incidence of mild adverse events, rare cases of severe hypersensitivity reactions (HSRs), and infrequent episodes of neurotoxic reactions. Nausea is reported in approximately 1.5% of patients, hives in 0.2%, and severe reactions in less than 0.001%.3

Immediate HSRs are rare but potentially harmful events may occur leading to death. These events include nonallergic and allergic reactions and manifest by cutaneous and/or mucosal signs such as generalized erythema, extended urticaria, angioedema (grade 1), mild cardiovascular (tachycardia, hypotension) and/or mild respiratory signs (coughing, dyspnea), with or without cutaneous or gastrointestinal signs (severe nausea, vomiting or diarrhea) (grade 2), cardiovascular collapse, possibly associated with bronchoconstriction (grade 3), or cardiac arrest (grade 4).4,5 The reported incidence of immediate HSRs to GBCAs is around 0.1%.5

Nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (NSF) is another rare but life-threatening condition, which has been related to the use of some GBCAs, especially in patients with end-stage chronic kidney disease. Initially described as nephrogenic fibrosing dermopathy, it is a diffuse fibrosis of tissue (skin and systemic tissues) disorder arising in the setting of advanced renal dysfunction. The first case of NSF related to GBCA injection was reported in 2006.6 Data from the International Centre for NSF Registry, the Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS), and a legal data set showed that all single agent cases of NSF were associated with linear GBCAs.7 The prevalence of NSF after exposure to a linear GBCA (gadodiamide) was reported as ranging from 3% to 7% in patients with reduced renal function.8 The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) have subsequently issued a warning regarding GBCA use (in 2006 and 2007, respectively), which led to a significant decrease in the incidence of NSF over the following years.8–10

More recently, gadolinium has been shown to be retained, in a dose-dependent manner, in the brain, independently of the renal function.11,12 An increased signal intensity (SI) in the dentate nucleus (DN) has been observed after repeated application of GBCAs, suggesting the presence of gadolinium in these regions of the brain.13 This phenomenon has been mainly associated with the linear GBCAs and since the initial report by Kanda et al became a safety concern.11 In parallel, clinical studies have reported gadolinium accumulation in various parts of the body, primarily in bones14–16 and skin.17,18 In 2017, the FDA released a safety communication to warn that GBCAs tended to be retained in the body but also concluded that no harmful effects had been observed with regards to retention of GBCAs in the brain.19

Gadolinium deposition disease (GDD) is a recently emerging clinical entity, proposed by Semelka and colleagues as a possible disease process due to gadolinium toxicity, manifesting in most cases by pain (including bone pain) and skin changes in subjects with normal renal function.20 Accordingly, a survey showed that in cases of self-reported suspected GDD, chronic pain is a frequent symptom.21 However, because there is no evidence of a causal relationship between these symptoms and the presence of gadolinium in the body, the members of the American College of Radiology Committee on Drugs and Contrast Media proposed to use the term “symptoms associated with gadolinium exposure” (SAGE) in lieu of GDD.22

The aim of this literature review was to provide an overview of the clinical safety of gadoterate meglumine over 35 years of use worldwide, primarily focusing on published data, assessing the incidence of HSRs, NSF, and gadolinium deposition.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

A systematic literature search for clinical articles in English was performed in Embase and PubMed via Medline, up to December 31, 2024, by an expert librarian with over 35 years of experience. The search terms used included Dotarem OR gadoteric acid OR gadoterate meglumine AND ‘contrast medium’ OR ‘gadolinium’ OR ‘safety’ OR ‘tolerability’ OR (adverse NEAR/1 (event* OR reaction* OR effect*)) OR ‘drug reaction’ OR (gadolinium NEAR/1 (deposition OR retention)) OR ‘signal intensity’ OR ‘dentate nucleus’ OR ‘anaphylaxis’ OR ‘allergy’ OR ‘hypersensitivity’ OR nephropat* OR ‘nephrogenic systemic fibrosis’ OR nephrogen*.

The selection process is depicted in Figure 1. Briefly, after duplicate removal, a first screening was performed based on publications titles and abstracts to exclude nonclinical studies, congress abstracts, letter to editor, narrative reviews, meta-analyses, and out of scope studies. The remaining articles were then assessed for eligibility based on the presence and nature of reported safety results as well as unconfounded data in full text. Additional relevant articles were found through reference checking and previous literature searches.

In addition, all safety data on adverse events reported by health care professionals, health authorities, and in the literature were compiled by the Guerbet pharmacovigilance department.

RESULTS

The literature search identified 62 articles reporting safety data from clinical studies related to IV administration of gadoterate meglumine, which provided data on the general safety of gadoterate meglumine and/or specific results on HSRs (n = 17), NSF (n = 9), or gadolinium deposition (n = 27). The studies were conducted in adult and pediatric population. The patients' age ranged from a few weeks to 103 years.

The clinical development program of gadoterate meglumine consisted of more than 50 studies, from phase I to phase IV, assessing the pharmacokinetics, efficacy and/or safety of gadoterate meglumine in a total of 3491 subjects, including pediatric patients and patients with renal impairment.23–25 Furthermore, 10 postmarketing surveillance (PMS) studies have been conducted to confirm the safety of gadoterate meglumine in routine practice, involving 220,353 patients.23,26–33 Overall, the adverse drug reaction (ADR) rate was 0.25% and serious ADR rate was 0.008% (Table 1).

Few cases were reported during the PMS studies, showing an incidence of <0.01% in Germany28,29,32 and of 0.05% in the large international SECURE study.30 The reported cases were mostly cases of urticaria or pruritus. One severe case of anaphylactic shock was reported, albeit the patient recovered fully after treatment. In the SECURE study, only 1 case of urticaria was considered severe and no HSR occurred in the pediatric group.31 Another patient experienced hypersensitivity (0.003%). In the pooled data of 2 German PMS studies conducted between 2004 and 2013, 3 cases of hypersensitivity were reported.32,34

In addition to the PMS data, a large retrospective investigator initiated study provided safety data on the administration of gadoterate meglumine in 21,770 adult and 698 pediatric patients. HSRs were reported for 2 adult patients (0.01%).39 The incidence of HSRs after administration of gadoterate meglumine was also retrieved from several sizeable studies involving various GBCAs,36,38,41,42 ranging from 0.03%38 to 0.3%.41 In a large retrospective Korean cohort study conducted by Jung et al on 84,367 patients, the overall incidence of immediate HSRs to GBCAs was 0.12% (ranging from 0.013% for gadodiamide to 0.22% for gadobenate dimeglumine) and the incidence for gadoterate meglumine was 0.08%.36 In another large Korean single-center retrospective study41 on 331,070 cases of GBCA exposure, the incidence of HSRs was 0.4% overall and 0.3% for gadoterate meglumine. In this study, the incidence rate of anaphylactic reactions was 0.004%, and none were associated with gadoterate meglumine administration.41 Data from registries37,40 showed an incidence of HSRs of 0.08% to 0.12% with gadoterate meglumine.

Furthermore, a retrospective study including 30,000 patients who received gadoterate meglumine or gadopentetate dimeglumine showed a 0.01% incidence of HSRs with only 3 patients experiencing an acute ADR after gadoterate meglumine administration.49 Three studies focused on HSRs to GBCAs in pediatric patients, including patients younger than 2 years.45–47 Among a total of 14,271 children who received gadoterate meglumine, the incidence of HSRs was 0.05% (Table 2). These cases consisted mostly of wheezing and sneezing.

Two studies evaluated the effectiveness of changing the contrast agent for prevention of HSR recurrence in patients with a history of mild immediate HSRs to a GBCA.42,43 In 1 study reporting on 185 patients with initial ADR to GBCA, gadoterate meglumine was the second most common GBCA to be classified as the initial culprit agent (46/185 cases; 24.9%) and as contrast agent of re-exposure (154/397 cases; 38.8%). The overall recurrence rate was 19.6% (78 of 397 re-exposure cases), with 15 of 78 (19.2%) reported for gadoterate meglumine. Most recurrent HSRs (97.4%) were mild reactions and none were considered severe.43 In a prospective study conducted by Walker et al, switching to gadoterate meglumine in patients with prior HSR to GBCA allowed to reduce the expected rate of subsequent HSRs in patients with and without the use of corticosteroid premedication.44

Other publications did not clearly indicate the incidence of HSRs after gadoterate meglumine administration but gave some indications on the proportion of cases related to gadoterate meglumine. Qu et al studied the incidence of HSRs to GBCAs by analyzing the data from the World Health Organization database VigiBase and the FAERS database from inception to 2023.2 The rate of serious HSRs for gadoterate meglumine was 11.9% (including death [0.22%], life-threatening [3.2%], disability [0.20%], and hospitalization [7.8%]). Bian et al analyzed 1240 reports of anaphylaxis associated with contrast media from the FAERS database, of which 53 (4.3%) were associated with gadoterate meglumine.50 A study focusing on drug-induced anaphylactic reactions in children (age range: 0–17 years), analyzed 159 cases extracted from the German Federal Institute for Drugs and Medical Devices database between 2000 and 2016. Among those, 5 cases were reported as related to gadoterate meglumine.51

We identified 9 studies, including a total of 18,287 patients with chronic kidney disease at various stages, which reported on the incidence of NSF after gadoterate meglumine administration (Table 3). The follow-up duration was at least 3 months and up to several years. Other studies such as Gheuens et al59 and Jawahar et al60 reported the safe administration of gadoterate meglumine in patients with end-stage renal disease, including patients receiving dialysis. None of the patients developed any symptoms or signs suggestive of NSF.

Gadolinium Deposition

We identified 21 articles, including 1252 patients, primarily assessing potential gadolinium retention in the brain after gadoterate meglumine administration (Table 4). Seven studies investigated the possible gadolinium brain deposition after multiple gadoterate meglumine administrations in patients with multiple sclerosis (MS).61–67 Only 1 study reported visible hyperintensity in the DN in 15 out of 57 patients after repeated gadoterate meglumine administrations (range: 2–15).66 The DN-to-pons ratio difference between the last and the first unenhanced longitudinal relaxation time (T1) MRI was statistically significant in the subgroup of patients with visible hyperintensity (n = 15; P ≤ 0.0001) but not in the subgroup with no visible hyperintensity (n = 26; P = 0.4239).66 The other 6 studies including a total of 390 adult patients with MS found that repeated exposure to gadoterate meglumine (from 1 to 26 administrations) was not associated with increased SI in deep brain nuclei.61–65,67

Similarly, in 6 identified studies, including a total of 584 patients, who underwent 3–53 gadoterate meglumine-enhanced brain scans for indications other than MS, no significant SI or relaxivity rate increase was observed in the DN or globus pallidus (GP).68–73,81 Zhang et al showed nonetheless a significant difference in the magnetic susceptibility of GP between the gadoterate meglumine and the control groups (P = 0.001).73 In addition, 2 prospective studies evaluated the effect of multiple gadoterate meglumine-enhanced breast MRI (Table 4).74,75 Bennani-Baiti et al analyzed 25 women who received relatively large cumulative doses of gadoterate meglumine. None of the patients exhibited T1 signal increase in deep brain nuclei, either GP or DN, in unenhanced 3.0-T brain MRI.74 Another study by Krug et al included 49 healthy participants who had received 6–14 doses of gadoterate meglumine, for breast assessment, reported a significant increase in SIs for DN, GP, and pons (P = 0.019–0.037).75

Six studies analyzed potential signal increase in the brain of a total of 237 pediatric patients (age range: 0.1–18 years) who underwent unconfounded administrations of gadoterate meglumine48,76–80 (Table 4). Significant signal increase in the brain was reported in 2 of these studies.48,77 Rossi Espagnet et al showed a significant difference between the first and last administration in both the GP/thalamus (P = 0.004) and the DN/Pons (P = 0.001) ratios.77 Topcuoglu et al also showed that mean T1 SI ratios of DN-to-pons and GP-to-thalamus in the group of 45 patients who received at least 3 (range: 3–15) gadoterate meglumine administrations were significantly different from those in the unexposed group (n = 45, P < 0.001).48 The other 4 studies including 142 pediatric patients who underwent from 3 to 23 gadoterate meglumine-enhanced MRI showed no SI increase in the deep brain.76,78–80

Moreover, Maximova et al assessed the gadolinium liver content in 21 patients (age range: 2–17 years) exposed to gadoterate meglumine and found that 8 patients presented with abnormal liver iron concentration; however, this was not associated with liver dysfunction.82 The patients underwent chelation therapy, which significantly reduced gadolinium liver concentration.

Gadolinium Deposition Disease and Symptoms Associated With Gadolinium Exposure

We identified 8 studies reporting GDD or SAGE in association with the use of gadoterate meglumine.2,20,39,81,83–86 The largest study to explore association of GDD with gadoterate meglumine was reported by Parillo et al where 366 patients were involved. GDD-like symptoms (central torso pain, arm or leg pain, bone pain, headache, skin redness, fatigue, and mental confusion) were reported by 45 (12.4%) patients.83 In addition, Perrotta et al performed a retrospective study in which 10 oncology patients received more than 20 administrations of gadoterate meglumine; however, no report of an unexpected cerebellar syndrome was found even after a mean follow-up period of 91 months.81 Young et al analyzed 7 subjects who received gadoterate meglumine-enhanced MRI in a large cohort study (n = 22,325), and they found that the renal function status and exposure to contrast agent did not impact the rate of chronic pain medications (P = 0.777).39 Other studies, such as Semelka et al87 and Maecker et al84 investigating GDD-like symptoms and Kramer et al86 exploring the incidence of small fiber neuropathy, involved a small number of patients (n = 2) solely receiving gadoterate meglumine, hence no meaningful interpretation could be undertaken.

Finally, Shahid et al analyzed real life safety pharmacovigilance data from FAERS and EudraVigilance to determine the proclivity of SAGE among GBCAs.85 This study showed that SAGE was significantly more prevalent among the linear than the macrocyclic GBCAs, and gadoterate meglumine showed one of the lowest SAGE risk among the selected GBCAs.

Regarding NSF, among the 63 reports received by Guerbet, an administration of gadoterate meglumine was specifically mentioned in less than half of the cases (30 out of 63). In the remaining cases, it was not known, which GBCA was used. Among the 30 reports mentioning the use of gadoterate meglumine, the diagnosis of NSF was confirmed in only 7 cases, all being multiple-agent reports. The diagnosis was doubtful in the remaining 23 cases (including 5 single agent reports), either due to missing information so that the Girardi score58 could not be applied and/or differential diagnoses could not be ruled out.

DISCUSSION

Gadoterate meglumine has been used in clinical practice for over 35 years, with over 170 million doses administered. In a previous publication, the accumulated safety data from 50 clinical studies (phase I–IV), 8 PMS studies and postmarketing pharmacovigilance monitoring of over 8000 adverse events reported to Guerbet were comprehensively discussed.23 In the current review, our aim was to assess the clinical safety of gadoterate meglumine in published literature, primarily focusing on HSRs, NSF, and gadolinium deposition.

Data from the 10 PMS studies included patients of all age groups from neonates to elderly subjects and confirmed the good safety profile of gadoterate meglumine in clinical practice for MRI of the CNS and other body regions and for MRA examinations, with an ADR rate of 0.25% and serious ADR rate of 0.008%.23,26–33

Regarding the HSRs, in the published literature, after a total of 437,369 administrations, the reported incidence of reactions ranged from 0% to 0.58%. Although contrast media are considered to be the main culprits in the advent of an HSR, other factors may be involved in the causality of such reactions. In the SECURE PMS study, 6 out of the 10 patients with HSRs had preexisting allergies or previous reaction to contrast media.30 Braun et al, in the largest PMS study with gadoterate meglumine to-date, reported 13 (<0.01%) patients who experienced serious adverse events, including HSRs.32 A number of these patients, who fully recovered, had preexisting risk factors such as history of allergies, bronchial asthma, history of contrast agent reaction, heart disease, etc.32 In a large cohort study, the absence of premedication, repeated exposures to the culprit GBCA, and a history of HSRs to iodinated contrast media and GBCAs were also identified as the risk factors for HSRs to GBCAs.36 Moreover, Power et al analyzed HSRs in a prospective study including over 30,000 MRI examinations and reported that almost half of the patients with a HSR had an identifiable risk factor.88 Overall, the incidence of HSRs with GBCAs is considered rare; patient's clinical history profiling, premedication, or/and skin testing may be considered to reduce complications in high-risk individuals.89,90

Regarding NSF, among the 9 studies, only 1 NSF report was considered to be associated with gadoterate meglumine.53 However, the diagnosis was not consistent with NSF according to the Girardi score58 and an additional dose of an unspecified GBCA had also been administered to this patient. Therefore, this NSF report with gadoterate meglumine was classified as doubtful on the basis of available information (Guerbet pharmacovigilance database). Single agent (“unconfounded”) cases of NSF have been frequently associated with linear GBCAs. Edwards et al reported 279 individual biopsy-proven, single agent NSF cases with the linear GBCAs, namely, gadodiamide (n = 197), gadopentetate dimeglumine (n = 74), and gadoversetamide (n = 8).7 Attari et al reported 1 case of NSF with the linear agent gadobenate dimeglumine.91 Macrocyclic agents have also been associated with NSF, albeit the incidence of unconfounded cases is extremely low.92,93 On the whole, since the recommendations from European Society of Urogenital Radiology (ESUR),8 FDA,9 and EMA,10 the incidence of NSF has dramatically decreased.7

Regarding gadolinium deposition, among the 21 studies assessing gadolinium retention in the brain, only 4 studies showed signs of visible hyperintensities after gadoterate meglumine injection.48,66,75,77 One study involved MS patients, where about 26% of the patients showed notable SI in DN66; however, majority of the patients (76%), who received up to 15 injections of gadoterate meglumine, did not show visible hyperintensities in DN. It is noteworthy that although MRI is an essential tool for early diagnosis and disease monitoring in MS94; however, this population frequently show increased permeability of the blood–brain barrier as part of the inflammatory process in the brain parenchyma, which theoretically might increase the risk of gadolinium accumulation.62,95 Interestingly, the remaining 6 studies involving MS patients, where patients received up to 26 injections of gadoterate meglumine, showed no sign of hyperintensities.61–65,67 Another study investigating healthy women with an increased risk of breast cancer, reported a significant increase in SIs for DN, GP, and pons; however, they were too small to be visually perceived on the MR images of brain nuclei.75

Among other studies, 2 reported a significant increase in the SI in the brain of pediatric patients.48,77 In both studies, an age-matched control group was included to rule out the possibility of an effect of brain maturation on the SIs. Indeed, T1 and transverse relaxation time (T2) are changing during the process of brain maturation in children through the age of about 14 years.96 In case of 1 study,77 inconsistency in the study design were reported,97 while for the other study,48 the authors did not describe whether the controls were age-matched at first or last gadoterate meglumine injection.

Overall, the safety of gadoterate meglumine was confirmed and there was little evidence of gadolinium brain deposition associated with gadoterate meglumine use even after repeated administrations in adults (up to 52) or in children (up to 23).

Finally, various studies explored the incidence of GDD or SAGE with gadoterate meglumine; however, a causal relationship with the symptoms could not be established as these studies were either surveys, had very small sample size, or involved spontaneously reported events. It is important to highlight that GDD presumes a causal relationship between gadolinium deposition-associated symptoms and GBCAs; however, this phenomenon is not proven through robust scientific research, as acknowledged by the American College of Radiology.22 Therefore, SAGE is a preferred term, which is intended to be used when a causal relationship between GBCA and symptoms is unknown.22,85,98 Recently, the European Society of Magnetic Resonance in Medicine and Biology Gadolinium Research and Educational Committee (ESMRMB-GREC) stated that analysis of data for the last 10 years showed that there is no evidence of clinical symptoms nor harm associated with gadolinium deposition in the brain and body.99

Regarding the data reported to Guerbet pharmacovigilance database, approximately 25,000 adverse events were received, which occurred in over 11,000 patients, yielding a cumulative ADRs reporting rate of 0.017% (8 cases per 100,000 patients). This was not significantly different from a previous publication reporting pharmacovigilance data from over 50 million administrations of gadoterate meglumine.23 The majority of the adverse events were nonserious and belonged to skin and subcutaneous tissue disorders, namely, urticaria, pruritus, erythema, and gastrointestinal disorders, namely, nausea and vomiting. Regarding NSF, there were 5 single agent reports: for 1 case, the final histology diagnosis was “subtle dermal fibrosis” not consistent with the diagnosis of NSF; for another case, the analysis of the biopsy reported pathological signs that were inconsistent with the diagnosis of NSF, and for the other 3 cases, no evidence of clinical pathological score according to Girardi score was made available.58

LIMITATIONS

The main limitation of this study is the fact that safety outcomes were not collected the same way in all included studies and over different time periods, which may have led to underestimation of some symptoms. In addition, some studies analyzing the safety of GBCAs reported limited unconfounded data for gadoterate meglumine.

CONCLUSIONS

More than 170 million administrations of gadoterate meglumine (Dotarem) have been performed for MRI examinations of CNS and other body regions as well as MRA. Gadoterate meglumine has proven diagnostic efficacy and very low incidence of adverse events, including HSRs. To date, no confirmed unconfounded case of NSF has been associated with gadoterate meglumine. Few reports of hyperintensities in the brain have been published, albeit with no confirmed clinical symptoms. Overall, clinical data from literature and safety data from pharmacovigilance monitoring confirmed the favorable benefit/risk profile of gadoterate meglumine in adults and children, including patients with impaired renal function.
