Leveraging port state measures to combat illegal, unreported, and unregulated fishing
Authors: Elizabeth R. Selig, Colette C. C. Wabnitz, Shinnosuke Nakayama, Jaeyoon Park, Richard Barnes, Robert Blasiak, Dawn Borg-Costanzi, Bronwen Golder, Jean-Baptiste Jouffray, Jim Leape, Jessica L. Decker Sparks
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Illegal, unreported, and unregulated (IUU) fishing threatens the sustainability of fisheries and communities dependent on them. The Port State Measures Agreement (PSMA) is a key tool for combatting IUU fishing by foreign fleets, requiring standardized inspections, information sharing, and port denial. Using satellite data, we characterized how PSMA has affected high seas vessel behavior and identify opportunities to strengthen its impact. PSMA adoption has increased travel distances to the nearest ports in States not Party to PSMA and channeled more fishing effort to domestic and PSMA Party ports. However, domestic fishing fleets need greater attention because they constituted 66% of port visits in 2021. Among reflagged vessels, we also found a 30% increase in visits to PSMA ports by vessels shifting to domestic flags after PSMA entered into force, allowing them to avoid PSMA requirements for foreign vessels. Our results highlight the centrality of implementing consistent, effective port State measures across foreign and domestic fleets to address IUU fishing risks.

INTRODUCTION

Illegal, unreported, and unregulated (IUU) fishing undermines the environmental sustainability of fisheries (1, 2), jeopardizing the health, livelihoods, and economies of coastal communities and nations by diverting nutrients and revenues (3–6) away from places where they are most needed. In West Africa alone, IUU fishing results in estimated economic losses of between $2.3 billion and $9.4 billion annually (7, 8). IUU fishing can also trigger and aggravate international conflicts (4, 9), a major barrier to creating durable, effective fisheries management. Eliminating IUU fishing is a clear priority, codified by the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (Target 14.4), embraced by industry actors in multiple voluntary commitments (10, 11), and pursued by diverse civil society actors (12). Concurrently, the availability of satellite tracking data (13, 14) has increased the transparency of fishing activities and enabled the development of IUU fishing risk indicators for the high seas (2, 15, 16). However, progress on addressing IUU fishing has remained elusive for multiple reasons, including harmful fisheries subsidies (17, 18), shifting vessel identities (19), obscure beneficial ownership (20), lack of supply chain transparency (21), and the complexity and high cost of monitoring and enforcing fishing activities across fragmented jurisdictions at sea (22, 23).

In contrast, ports are places where monitoring and enforcement, including the seizure of catches from IUU fishing, are logistically easier and more cost-effective. Jurisdiction within ports is also simpler, so legal and policy frameworks can provide better accountability (24). Consequently, achieving commitments to end IUU fishing may largely depend on transforming ports associated with IUU fishing into hubs of effective governance. A key tool for realizing this goal is the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization’s (FAO) Agreement on Port State Measures to Prevent, Deter and Eliminate Illegal, Unreported, and Unregulated Fishing, or the Port State Measures Agreement (PSMA).

The PSMA entered into force in 2016 (25) and provides a legally binding mechanism under international law that requires Parties to apply certain measures that strengthen the oversight of foreign fishing and support vessels (Article 3; Supplementary Text). These measures include designating ports for them to land their catches (Article 7; Supplementary Text), requiring particular information for port entry (Article 8 and Annex A for criteria; Supplementary Text), authorizing or denying port entry if the foreign vessel is suspected of involvement in IUU fishing (Article 9; Supplementary Text), and conducting standardized vessel inspections for foreign fishing and support vessels, including transshipment vessels that are used to exchange catches and bunker or fuel vessels (Article 13 and Annex B for criteria; Supplementary Text). The PSMA also instructs Parties to exchange information with relevant governance bodies (Articles 6 and 14 to 16; Supplementary Text). Importantly, it is designed to prevent, deter, and eliminate IUU fishing through the monitoring and inspection of foreign vessels at port.

The PSMA’s primary focus is on foreign vessels, but it does stipulate in Article 20 (6) “Each Party shall ensure that measures applied to vessels entitled to fly its flag are at least as effective in preventing, deterring, and eliminating IUU fishing and fishing related activities in support of such fishing as measures applied to vessels referred to in paragraph 1 of Article 3,” a point also emphasized in Article 3 (2) (25). In other words, PSMA specifies that Parties should control IUU fishing by their domestic vessels using measures that are at least as effective as those applied to foreign vessels, but there is no requirement that the approaches must be the same (26). Because PSMA is explicit about actions for foreign vessels, the responsibilities that Parties have for their domestic fleets have received much less attention. This lack of clarity or standards on which port State measures should be applied to domestic vessels can create unintended incentives such as vessels landing catches at domestic ports or changing their identity through the use of their flag to circumvent PSMA requirements (27). The vessel flag, or the State where the vessel is registered, determines who has jurisdiction or responsibility for a vessel’s activities (28). It also affects whether the vessel is treated as foreign or domestic under PSMA. Using a domestic flag (i.e., the flag of the port State) when entering port may allow a vessel to avoid PSMA regulations to which they would otherwise need to adhere.

We used vessels’ Automatic Identification System (AIS) data from 2015 to 2021 to characterize fishing and support (transshipment and bunker) vessel behavior for vessels operating on the high seas. Then, we analyzed whether PSMA entry into force in 2016 has been associated with changes in the ports that vessels visit. Patterns in port usage highlight where vessels may have shifted so that they did not land at ports within Parties to the PSMA, hereafter referred to as “PSMA ports.” We also identified the degree to which these port visits were associated with different national fleets. In addition, we examined changes in the nationality of the vessel flag in the 5 years before (2012–2016) and 5 years after (2017–2021) PSMA entered into force.

The overall effectiveness of PSMA depends on which States have adopted it, how well they have implemented it, and how difficult it is to reach ports not subject to PSMA (Fig. 1) (29). First, we explored whether States becoming Parties to PSMA has made it harder for foreign vessels to reach ports in States not Party to PSMA, hereafter non-PSMA States, where they may face less scrutiny. Then, we analyzed whether the attention to foreign vessels may need to be matched by similar efforts with domestic fleets because of the magnitude of those fleets or the possibilities for vessels to change their flag to become domestic vessels. We assessed the size of domestic fleets and three ways that vessels could use domestic flags to avoid requirements for foreign vessels under PSMA: (i) reflagging, or switching to the flag of the port State where catches are landed; (ii) closed loops, where fishing and transshipment vessels have the same flag as the port State where catches are landed; and (iii) the use of specific arrangements that allow vessels that would otherwise be considered foreign to be treated as domestic when they move between the State with authority to adopt PSMA and their overseas territories (Fig. 2). Our analyses highlight the importance of consistent, effective implementation of PSMA to reduce spatial displacement of IUU fishing risks and the need for more attention to domestic fleets to maximize the potential of PSMA to make a difference in the fight against IUU fishing.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Fishing vessels respond to changes in external factors like fuel prices (13), environmental variability (13), and spatial management measures (30, 31). Between 2016 and 2021, we found that greater adoption of PSMA created additional logistical challenges by requiring longer travel distances for vessels to reach ports in non-PSMA States (Fig. 3). At the same time, we documented a relative increase in landed fishing effort at ports in PSMA Parties over time by both non–PSMA-flagged and PSMA-flagged vessels (Fig. 4), resulting in fleets landing 62% of high seas fishing effort within PSMA Parties in 2021. More landed fishing effort subject to PSMA is likely the result of more States becoming PSMA Parties (fig. S1) or changes in vessel behavior. These results are encouraging for PSMA, if it is enacted as envisioned and foreign vessels are landing in ports where they are subject to PSMA requirements.

However, since 2015, non–PSMA-flagged vessels have also landed more proportional effort in their domestic ports, which fall outside the scope of PSMA controls (Fig. 4). As additional States adopt PSMA, further opportunities for action on IUU fishing risks may need to concentrate on extending equivalent port State measures to domestic vessels that land catches at domestic ports. Domestic fleets that fish on the high seas merit greater focus than they now garner due to their increased landed fishing effort (fig. S2A) and dominance in overall port visits (Fig. 5 and fig. S2B). In addition, the ability of vessels to become domestic through flag use, the proportion of transshipments that are closed loops and the ways in which States navigate relationships with their overseas territories (Fig. 2) indicate a need for greater attention. Given potentially lower AIS usage by domestic fleets, which are often on Vessel Monitoring Systems that are not generally publicly available (32), we may even underestimate the magnitude of these dynamics. Together, our results indicate that effective implementation as well as consistency in port State measures across foreign and domestic fleets will be important for ensuring that IUU fishing risks do not move to places or fleets with weaker governance.

Changes in vessel dynamics after PSMA entry into force

The PSMA’s impacts will depend not only on its adoption by more States but also on its full implementation, including aligned measures for domestic and foreign fleets. The combination of which States have adopted PSMA, the number of foreign vessel visits that they receive, and whether port usage has changed over time can help indicate how PSMA adoption relates to vessel behavior and which aspects of implementation need increased attention. In principle, broader adoption of PSMA (Fig. 1, fig. S1, and table S1) should make it logistically more difficult to bypass PSMA regulations. In practice, gaps in spatial coverage remain (Fig. 1), particularly for non-PSMA States that have substantial foreign vessel visits (Fig. 6). Adoption of PSMA by these States will be particularly important for increasing PSMA’s reach.

Fuel is one of the biggest costs in fishing (33, 34), so longer travel distances should increasingly deter vessels from landing in ports in non-PSMA States, especially as more States adopt PSMA. Between 2016 and 2021, the average distance from a given point in the ocean to the nearest non-PSMA port increased by 98% (Fig. 3A). The degree to which the distances changed varied by region, with the greatest increases in the Northeast Pacific and the Arctic Ocean (Fig. 3B). By contrast, distances to non-PSMA ports in the Southwest and Western Central Atlantic, Western Central Pacific, and the Antarctic south of the Pacific have remained relatively unchanged (Fig. 3C). However, much of the Western Central Pacific has measures aligned with PSMA, which are specified by its regional fisheries management organization (RFMO), the Western and Central Pacific Fisheries Commission (WCPFC) (26, 35). These results indicate progress in making it harder to evade PSMA regulations, although the strength of PSMA implementation remains important and challenging to assess.

The potential for positive impacts from PSMA is also evident when examining patterns in landed fishing effort since PSMA entered into force in 2016 (Fig. 4 and fig. S2). We tested how landed fishing effort changed over time depending on whether a vessel was flagged to a PSMA Party and if it landed at a domestic port, PSMA Party port, or a non-PSMA State port. Then we assessed patterns across fleets (Fig. 4) and for specific fleets (fig. S3). Across vessels flagged to PSMA Parties and non-PSMA States, we found that the proportion of fishing effort landed at foreign PSMA Party ports relative to total landed fishing effort (including domestic ports) increased over time, from 19% in 2016 to 42% in 2021 (Fig. 4). Although many factors may explain this trend, it corresponds to an increase from 41 PSMA Parties in 2016 to 70 Parties in 2021, a 71% increase (fig. S1). Greater adoption likely resulted in longer distances needed to land at ports within non-PSMA States (Fig. 3). At the same time, relative domestic landed fishing effort increased from 31% in 2015 to 46% in 2021 across both PSMA Parties and non-PSMA States (fig. S2A). In particular, the proportion of landed fishing effort by fleets flagged to non-PSMA States brought to their own domestic ports increased from 31% in 2015 to 50% in 2021 (Fig. 4B).

These findings suggest promise for PSMA to reduce opportunities for foreign vessels to land their catches with less scrutiny. Nonetheless, the decrease in landed fishing effort at foreign non-PSMA State ports from 47% in 2016 to 12% in 2021 (Fig. 4) indicates that although additional PSMA adoption is important, the proportion of landed fishing effort remaining to be brought under PSMA is limited. In 2021, only 4% of total landed fishing effort (including domestic ports) by foreign vessels from non-PSMA States was still landed at other non-PSMA State ports (Fig. 4).

Increasingly, greater focus on effectively implementing PSMA may be needed, including designating ports and sharing them through appropriate FAO channels, ensuring that foreign vessels land their catches at only those ports, and conducting inspections to the standards set forth in PSMA. As of April 2025, 23 of 82 PSMA Parties had not shared their designated ports through the Global Information Exchange System (GIES). Because there is no publicly available historical record for designated ports for our analytical time period (2015–2021), we assessed patterns in port visits for 2024 using designated ports as of April 2025. Only 73% of foreign vessel port visits were at designated ports when landing in PSMA Parties, suggesting a need for more adherence to PSMA obligations for foreign vessels to land at designated ports. If foreign vessels are able to land at nondesignated ports within PSMA Parties, then they may be able to benefit from a lack of PSMA requirements similar to landing at ports in non-PSMA States, if capacity is not sufficient to monitor them (36). Of the 27% of port visits by foreign vessels landing at nondesignated ports, the vast majority (82%) were flagged to PSMA Parties.

These PMSA Parties could be proactive in carrying out PSMA flag State responsibilities, such as landing at designated ports in accordance with PSMA (Article 20; Supplementary Text). PSMA Parties can also endeavor to designate ports that are relatively accessible by ensuring an even geographical distribution within a given Party. By directing foreign vessels to land their catches at designated ports, PSMA Parties can guard against creating unintended loopholes that undermine overall PSMA effectiveness. The shifts in landed fishing effort over a relatively short time span also indicate dynamism in vessel movement (37) and a need to be attuned to the ways in which vessels may choose where they land to skirt stricter regulations or PSMA monitoring.

Aligning port State measures for domestic fleets

To understand domestic fleet dynamics more directly, we examined their relative magnitude and how vessels may leverage or change their identity to domestic flags, which would allow them to avoid monitoring mandated by PSMA. The emphasis on foreign vessels within PSMA has been an important first step because vessels fishing on the high seas often land their catches in a different State than their vessel flag State, making them “foreign” vessels under PSMA when they land at port. Foreign fleets in high seas fisheries represented 54% of landed fishing effort in 2021. In addition, monitoring and governance of fishing on the high seas is more challenging, making strong controls when landing catches at port particularly vital (23).

However, fleets that landed their catches in their domestic ports, “domestic fleets,” were also active in high seas fisheries (46% of landed fishing effort in 2021), led by fleets flagged to China (31%), Chinese Taipei (16%), Japan (10%), Indonesia (6%), and the European Union (EU; 5%). In addition, domestic fleets active in high seas fisheries represented 66% of overall port visits by fishing vessels and 33% of port visits by support vessels in 2021 (Fig. 5), a proportion that has been increasing over time (fig. S2). While the strong emphasis on foreign fleets in PSMA adoption and implementation has been needed, comparable attention to the world’s vast domestic fleets, both those that fish on the high seas (Fig. 5) and across all vessels using AIS (fig. S4), is also warranted. China’s adoption of PSMA in April 2025 highlights the benefits of carrying out Article 20 to support equivalent oversight and control of domestic vessels, given the substantial proportion of domestic port visits by Chinese fishing and support vessels (Fig. 5 and fig. S4). Effective port State measures for domestic fleets parallel to those in PSMA could also dovetail with current efforts to establish traceability in supply chains (10, 11). Increased consideration of domestic fleets could involve efforts to place it on the agenda of Meetings of the Parties to the PSMA or through channels like the periodic questionnaires issued to Parties that would allow them to identify it as an issue.

Flag domestication

Applying PSMA-aligned port State measures to domestic fleets could also prevent foreign vessels from opportunistically switching to domestic flags, a practice known as flag “domestication,” which can allow vessels to evade robust port controls (Fig. 2A) (38). Reflagging can be a positive strategy where the port State has strong governance, but is more problematic where monitoring of domestic fleets is weak. Approximately 15 to 25% of vessels in our database changed flags between 2015 and 2021 (4700 of a total of 30,000 vessels; 4500 of 18,000 vessels excluding China). Vessels can change flags for legitimate reasons (e.g., change in ownership or to comply with requirements for access to territorial waters), but certain flags have been associated with higher risks of IUU fishing activity (28, 39, 40), and reflagging can allow vessels to obscure ownership and complicate oversight (19, 39, 40). To determine the number of vessels engaged in flag domestication, we quantified the proportion of vessels that changed their identity, classified if they were flagged to PSMA Parties, and tracked whether they landed at ports in PSMA Parties or not over time.

In the 5-year period after PSMA entered into force in 2016, we identified a 30% increase in domestic port visits in PSMA Parties among vessels that had changed their flag, compared to the 5-year period before. For some countries like New Zealand, Namibia, and the Russian Federation, port visits by vessels reflagged to the port State increased by more than 50% (Fig. 7). At the same time, there was a 12% decrease in domestic flag use for ports in States such as Papua New Guinea, Chinese Taipei, and Marshall Islands, which had not adopted or could not adopt PSMA during the analytical timeframe (Fig. 7) However, regional measures like WCPFC’s Conservation and Management Measure 2017-02 include requirements similar to those outlined in PSMA (26, 35). Reflagging patterns do not necessarily affect the global size of domestic fleets over time, but they are clearly important dynamics for particular States (Fig. 7). Together, these patterns indicate divergent behavior for vessels visiting ports in PSMA Parties versus non-PSMA State ports and the need to bolster consistency in port State measures across fleets. Once vessels have reflagged to the port State, they are no longer subject to PSMA standards unless similar or more rigorous standards are applied to domestic vessels. Applying port State measures to domestic vessels could disincentivize flag switching that may be aimed at circumventing PSMA.

Closed loops

Port State measures for domestic fleets could also provide some measure of control over vessels operating within domestic “closed loops,” in which the flag State of the fishing vessel, transshipment vessel, and port State is the same (Figs. 2B and 8). Foreign fishing vessels that use a transshipment vessel flagged to the port State could also theoretically bypass PSMA requirements on the transferred and landed catch. However, the fishing vessel would still be subject to PSMA, and this pattern appeared relatively infrequently in our data (<2% of port visits). Vessels in domestic closed loops are not subject to PSMA requirements for foreign vessels. Because port State measures are applied for each vessel visit, we calculated how the number of port visits by vessels within closed loops has changed over time since PSMA entered into force.

Of vessels involved in transshipment, closed loops were present in 60% of all transshipment vessel port visits from 2015 to 2021. Russia alone accounted for 84% of all closed loops and was also notable for the number of port visits involved (Fig. 8). The Russian fleet (969 fishing and 151 transshipment vessels in 2021) used Russian ports for 83% of their combined total visits, a consistent increase from 2015 when 77% of visits were to Russian ports. Almost all landings involving Russian flags (i.e., fished by and transshipped to Russian vessels) took place at Russian ports, and almost all landings that took place at Russian ports were made by Russian-flagged vessels. By contrast, 75% of Chinese-flagged transshipment vessels landed at Chinese ports, but they represented only 37% of all transshipment vessel visits at Chinese ports. In other words, many foreign transshipment vessels also land at Chinese ports. Closed loops may be beneficial where governance is strong, but they can facilitate the concealment of IUU fishing activities where governance is weak by providing less opportunity for transparency or due diligence. By implementing Article 20 more fully, PSMA Parties could establish greater oversight of these vessels within their domestic fleets.

PSMA Parties and relationships with their overseas territories

Finally, although the relationships between PSMA Parties and their overseas territories vary (table S2), some PSMA Parties may treat vessels flagged to their overseas territories as if they were domestic vessels (Fig. 2C). Such relationships are not always clearly explained within the instruments deposited with FAO when a State adopts PSMA for themselves or on behalf of their overseas territories. National regulations determine how PSMA Party and territorial fleets are treated when landing at each other’s ports. Under PSMA, vessels flagged to overseas territories could enjoy the same rights as domestic vessels when landing catches at the PSMA Party’s ports, exempting them from foreign vessel inspection requirements. This arrangement may also apply reciprocally when vessels from the PSMA Party land at ports in its overseas territories.

To examine the potential magnitude of this dynamic, we looked at landed fishing effort by vessels flagged to PSMA Parties and their overseas territories over time. In 2021, 1% of landed fishing effort was from vessels flagged to PSMA Parties and their overseas territories landing at ports within that PSMA Party or its respective territories (fig. S5). The largest landed fishing effort was at ports in Denmark and its territories (71%), followed by the United States and its territories (27%) and the United Kingdom and its territories (2%). The overall amount of landed fishing effort has increased since 2016 (fig. S5). While vessels involved in PSMA Party–overseas territory relationships are a relatively small percentage of global fleets, they remain a pathway that could potentially be used to circumvent PSMA regulations. The lack of clarity on these relationships highlights the need for a better understanding of how they operate and underscores the importance of moving toward having consistent port State measures across these networks.

Policy implications

Recent calls for action on IUU fishing have centered on safeguarding the ocean for food security and the livelihoods of millions of fisheries-dependent people (41). The PSMA is an important tool for eliminating the IUU fishing that imperils those benefits. Fulfilling its promise may depend on regional adoption to make it harder for vessels to reach ports in non-PSMA States, Parties cooperating to support robust, consistent implementation, and extending similar port State measures to their domestic fleets. Inconsistencies in how PSMA is carried out can allow IUU fishing vessels to move to the least well-regulated fisheries, ports, or coastal States, exacerbating underlying inequities (42). Creating a minimum standard across all vessels can also deter flag switching that may undermine PSMA. Key steps to more effective implementation include honoring PSMA obligations to designate ports, sharing them through official channels, and directing foreign vessels to use only those ports. Taking these actions will also depend on Parties having access to necessary resources and capacity (36). In addition, it may become increasingly important for PSMA Parties to have domestic laws with measures that enact PSMA provisions to strengthen compliance and enforcement.

Governments are responsible for the implementation of PSMA, but complementary activities by regional bodies and private sector actors can help reinforce these efforts. Seafood companies can create traceability practices to promote transparency and work with government actors to ensure ports that are important to their business have robust port State measures. In addition, RFMOs could act to harmonize their port State measures with PSMA, including for domestic fleets. Fishing has always been dynamic and responsive to changes in weather, trade, and management practices. Actions to reduce IUU fishing will need to be similarly nimble and anticipate the challenges that are already emerging, to truly close the net on IUU fishing.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Our analyses were based on AIS data from Global Fishing Watch (GFW). AIS is a radio trans-receiver system originally designed for ship-to-ship communication using terrestrial and satellite receivers, but its positional data have been increasingly used to monitor human activities at sea, including fishing and IUU fishing risks (15, 16, 32, 43, 44). AIS use is required on vessels over 300 gross tonnes operating on the high seas outside of States’ Exclusive Economic Zones (EEZs) (13). Some regions like the EU mandate AIS use for all vessels over 15 m (32). Because of inconsistent AIS use within EEZs, we considered only fishing activities on the high seas to ensure comparability across States (19) except to illustrate overall AIS patterns (fig. S4). Our use of high seas AIS data does not assume that vessels fishing on the high seas are engaged in IUU fishing.

For our analyses of fishing and support vessels, fishing hours, and port visits, we used data from 2015 to 2021, the most recent years for which data have been monitored and reviewed by GFW for accuracy. For our vessel identity analysis, we looked at trends in the 5 years before PSMA came into force (2012–2016) and 5 years after (2017–2021). Data were extracted from GFW’s AIS data processing pipeline pipe_production_v20201001 in April 2025 (see Data and materials availability).

We used two key metrics to examine patterns in vessel behavior before and after PSMA entered into force: landed fishing effort, as a proxy for potential catch volumes, and port visits, as a general measure of the magnitude of vessel trips that might be subject to PSMA (landing within PSMA Parties) or not (landing within non-PSMA States). Landed fishing effort is defined as fishing hours inferred from AIS data by GFW’s model (13) multiplied by the vessel’s engine power in kilowatts (45). Because landed fishing effort can vary substantially by gear type (e.g., purse seine is substantially greater than longline), port visits provide a complementary, standardized metric when considering fleet patterns.

Under PSMA, Parties shall designate ports and inform FAO of them. FAO originally developed the Designated Ports App for this purpose (46), but that information is now included in the public part of the GIES (47). However, the Designated Ports App only began operating in 2018, and not all Parties have designated ports or reported them in the App or in GIES. In addition, Parties may change which ports they designate under PSMA, and no temporal record of prior designated ports is publicly available. Together, these dynamics made it challenging to identify a meaningful set of designated ports for our time period of interest or to track landings in designated ports back to the PSMA’s entry into force in 2016. Therefore, we used the national scale as our main unit of analysis, treating all ports within a given PSMA Party as PSMA ports. While this approach likely underestimated vessels landing at nondesignated ports within PSMA Parties, it captured how PSMA adoption related to overall patterns of port visits by foreign and domestic fleets fishing on the high seas.

Separately, we collected data on designated ports, including their names and States, from the GIES on 30 April 2025 to create a snapshot of their usage (47). We manually determined the coordinates of 597 ports (96.1%), excluding those in the United States because all ports in the United States are considered designated ports. These coordinates were then matched with GFW anchorages by identifying the nearest anchorages within a 3-km radius. This process successfully linked 536 of the 597 designated ports with coordinates to an anchorage and assigned each port the corresponding anchorage label (i.e., the port name assigned by GFW). These anchorage labels were then paired with GFW’s port visit dataset to analyze vessel visits to designated ports by vessels operating on the high seas before arriving at the port. For the set of designated ports (as of April 2025) that we were able to access, we examined the percentage of Parties that had listed ports within GIES and the degree to which foreign vessels were using this set of designated ports in 2024, the most recent complete year of GFW data. Therefore, we assumed that designated ports in 2025 were the same as those designated throughout 2024.

Landed fishing effort

Ideally, we would measure the actual catch volumes affected by PSMA requirements. However, vessel-specific catch data were not available. Instead, we used vessel tracks from GFW and their associated algorithms for predicting fishing as well as information on engine power to create a proxy for catch volumes. To account for the fact that larger vessels generally catch more fish over the same duration, we used “landed fishing effort,” calculated as fishing hours inferred from AIS data by GFW’s model (13) multiplied by the vessel’s engine power in kilowatts. As with previous research (45), we calculated landed fishing effort in terms of total kilowatt-hours accumulated during a fishing trip, encompassing all likely fishing activities occurring on the high seas between two consecutive port visits. We assumed that all catches made during a trip were landed at the first port visited by the fishing vessel after any fishing activity. We included all transshipment vessels that had an encounter with a fishing vessel that had been fishing on the high seas. In the event of an encounter with a transshipment vessel during a trip, we assumed that all fish caught by the fishing vessel were landed at the next port visited by the transshipment vessel, rather than the fishing vessel’s next landing port. We did not include fishing trips lasting longer than a year with no port visits or encounters with transshipment vessels to prevent a sizeable amount of fishing effort from being erroneously attributed to a port due to missing port visit or encounter data. We made this assumption because it is unlikely for a fishing vessel to operate for more than a year without either a port call or a transshipment event.

Port visits

Our analysis also relied on GFW’s port visit data, which provides likely port visits by vessels using an algorithm based on a vessel’s distance (entering <3 km and exiting >4 km with unequal thresholds to prevent the vessel passing close to a port from being counted as visiting the port) and speed thresholds (entering <0.2 knots and exiting >0.5 knots) from anchorage points or no gaps in AIS for >4 hours while at anchorage. Anchorage points were associated with a port using existing port databases and manual review by GFW (2).

Distance to ports in non-PSMA States

To investigate the extent to which the increasing number of PSMA Parties makes it more difficult for fishing and support vessels to avoid PSMA regulations, we calculated the change in the distance to the closest ports in a non-PSMA State from 2016 to 2021, according to the date on which the PSMA entered into force for the relevant State and any of its overseas territories during that time period (tables S1 and S2). For overseas territories, we used the same date when PSMA entered into force in the State adopting on their behalf, except where there was a specific date for entry into force for the overseas territory (table S1). For example, France and Denmark initially joined PSMA as EU members (entered into force on 5 June 2016) but later approved or acceded to PSMA separately on behalf of their overseas territories (entered into force on 10 August 2016, for territories of France, and on 28 December 2017, for territories of Denmark) (table S1). By contrast, some States like the United Kingdom and The Netherlands have adopted PSMA (entered into force on 5 June 2016), but their overseas territories have not, so these territories (e.g., British Virgin Islands, Turks, and Caicos, as well as Aruba and Curaçao, respectively) were not treated as Parties to PSMA (table S2).

First, we computed the travel distance from each centroid of a 5°-by-5° grid in the oceans to more than 3600 ports listed in the World Port Index (https://msi.nga.mil/Publications/WPI) using a network of the most frequent maritime routes (https://github.com/eurostat/searoute) to ensure that the route did not cross land and to map the closest feasible travel route to the nearest port. We excluded three ports in the United States Minor Outlying Islands (Johnston Atoll, Midway Island, and Wake Island), Kwajalein atoll in the Marshall Islands, and one port in the British Indian Ocean Territory (Diego Garcia), because civilians are restricted from entering them.

From each point in the center of the 5° grid, we found the nearest node in the network (i.e., the entrance to a maritime route) and the nearest node to the end point or port. Then, we calculated the distance from the ocean point to port, using the shortest path to traverse the network. We recorded the distance to the closest port located outside PSMA Parties on a given date. Finally, we determined the mean of all the distances from each point in the ocean to the nearest port outside of a PSMA Party. To account for differences in fishing capacity across grids, means were weighted by the logarithm of total fishing effort per square kilometer, which was calculated as total fishing hours multiplied by engine power for all fishing vessels within each grid in 2021, divided by the corresponding area. We focused on 2021 for computational efficiency and as the most recent year in our time-series analyses. Total fishing hours within each grid were obtained from GFW. We calculated distances using Python package scgraph v1.4.0.

Landed fishing effort and port usage patterns by PSMA Party– and non–PSMA State–flagged vessels

Using AIS data, we tracked the change in the proportion of landed fishing effort for vessels flagged to PSMA Parties and non-PSMA States visiting ports in PSMA Parties, domestic ports, or non-PSMA ports from 2015 to 2021. Because vessels flagged to PSMA Parties would be expected to cooperate with port inspections in PSMA Parties as part of their flag State responsibilities (Article 20; Supplementary Text), we assumed that they may have less incentive to forgo landing in ports of other PSMA Parties. This analysis can also highlight where different fleets may be preferentially landing in their own domestic ports, ports in PSMA Parties, or non-PSMA ports. Because some fleets may have different patterns within the non-PSMA States category, we also analyzed each of the major fleets separately.

Vessel flag patterns and flag domestication

We used GFW’s publicly available fishing vessel list and the dataset developed by Park et al. (19) (see Data and materials availability) to analyze patterns in vessels that have changed their identity (i.e., reflagging to or from the port State). We examined patterns for two periods, 2012–2016 and 2017–2021, as equal time periods to serve as proxies for pre- and post-PSMA entry into force in 2016 and to minimize year-to-year fluctuations. To complement the set of vessels that may not match AIS data due to lack of registry information, we added data on vessel flag changes on the basis of the AIS Maritime Mobile Service Identity (MMSI) flag code, a nine-digit code that identifies a vessel with the first three digits of the MMSI identifier indicating a vessel flag. We then assessed reflagging instances over time by combining them with port visit data and categorizing them into (i) vessels flying the flag of the port State where they are landing and (ii) vessels flying a foreign flag.

Our reflagging data have limitations similar to the data caveats presented by Park et al. (19). The data do not include vessels that change flags unless both identities (before and after the flag change) were broadcast on AIS. In some cases, although vessels that change flags do broadcast an AIS signal, a lack of information in the database means that we (erroneously) considered a vessel that has undergone an identity change (i.e., reflagged) as two separate vessels. Most vessels embarking on international voyages are likely to broadcast AIS signals according to the requirements of the International Maritime Organization’s International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea or other international or national regulations. As a result, missing vessels not broadcasting on AIS in the dataset are unlikely to affect our analysis substantially because we focused on vessels fishing on the high seas. Changes to other EU Member State flags or fishing within different EU States’ EEZs were not counted as reflagging or foreign fishing, respectively, because they are considered by the EU to be domestic (42).

Closed loops

We considered closed loops to be cases where the flags of the fishing and transshipment vessels were the same as the flag of the port State where catches were landed. To identify closed loops, we used GFW’s AIS data on encounters. Encounters occurred when a fishing vessel and a transshipment vessel were within 500 m of each other for >2 hours, traveling <2 knots, while >10 km from an anchorage (14).

PSMA Parties and relationships with their overseas territories

Relationships between the Parties and their overseas territories were defined according to data from Marine Regions (www.marineregions.org/) and combined with AIS to examine the behavior of vessels flagged to either the PSMA Party or their overseas territories (table S2). Both France and the United Kingdom have more than 10 associated territorial flags. We then determined whether the amount of landed fishing effort between fishing vessels flagged to a PSMA Party or their overseas territories landing at ports within the Party or territory changed over time after PSMA entered into force. It is not always clear whether or how PSMA may apply to overseas territories, so we included all Parties and their overseas territories in our analyses. We do not include self-loops, where a vessel flagged to a particular State lands at that port State (e.g., Danish-flagged vessels that land at Danish ports).

AIS coverage

AIS coverage has changed over the period of the main analyses (2015–2021) and the reflagging analysis (2012–2021). AIS messages from vessels flagged to PSMA Parties showed a steady annual increase of 5 to 17% between 2015 and 2021, while those from non-PSMA States, excluding China, demonstrated an increase between 11 to 38% (fig. S6). For China, AIS messages increased by 5 to 16% during the same period, except in 2016, when there was a notable 35% increase compared to the previous year, and a plateau from 2020 to 2021 (fig. S6). The spike in 2016 is likely due to an additional AIS provider that contributed more AIS data to GFW’s dataset. In our analyses, we compared differences between PSMA Parties and non-PSMA States by either the landed fishing effort or the number of port visits using annual proportions or changes in proportion, which are unlikely to be majorly affected by the steady growth of AIS usage.

The limitations of AIS for analyzing global fishing and support fleets are discussed in previous research (19). GFW’s AIS dataset represents a significant portion of the activity of large vessels (more than 300 gross tonnes) that operate on the high seas. Although increasing numbers of fishing vessels are using AIS when operating beyond their flag State’s EEZ, smaller vessels under 24 m long, which constitute a large proportion of the world’s fishing fleets, were likely missed in this analysis as they are not typically using AIS (13). However, they generally operate only within the EEZ of their flag State and are rarely reflagged to another State during their lifespan (19).

AIS data are susceptible to deliberate falsification of vessel location, including incorrect identities, recycled MMSIs, and other types of manipulation (15). Although more recent data may require careful investigation for accuracy, the fishing-related data used in this analysis up to 2021 have been validated through manual reviews and publications on global fleet analyses by GFW and are less likely to be affected by common AIS limitations (19, 48).
