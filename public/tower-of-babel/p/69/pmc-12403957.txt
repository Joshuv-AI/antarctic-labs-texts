Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and the future of male fertility: a path forward for personalized and regenerative therapies
Authors: Seyyed Amir Moradian, Zahra Amirkhani, Mansoureh Movahedin, Seyyede Nazaninzahra Gharib, Yasser Varghaiyan, Behrooz Niknafs, Kobra Hamdi, Hamed Hajipour, Amir Fattahi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlatelet-rich plasma (PRP) and its subtype, plasma rich in growth factors (PRGF), are autologous blood-derived products that have garnered increasing attention as personalized therapeutic tools in the field of male fertility. This systematic review aims to evaluate the current in vitro and in vivo evidence regarding the potential applications of platelet-derived products in various domains of male reproductive health, including in vitro spermatogenesis, sperm preservation, treatment of male infertility, mitigation of testicular toxicity, and management of testicular torsion/detorsion (T/D) injury.StrategyThis review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO. A comprehensive and language-unrestricted search was performed across major databases, including the Cochrane Library, MEDLINE/PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Web of Science. Studies published up to October 2024 were included, with an update in January 2025. Three reviewers independently screened and selected studies, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or consultation with a fourth reviewer.ResultsA total of 43 studies met the inclusion criteria. Available evidence suggests that PRP and PRGF supplementation in culture media may enhance in vitro sperm generation by improving cell viability and proliferation. Their incorporation into sperm incubation and cryopreservation protocols has also been associated with improved motility, viability, and structural integrity. In vivo studies indicate that intratesticular PRP injections may offer therapeutic benefits for male infertility, potentially supporting improvements in sperm parameters, hormonal balance, and testicular tissue restoration. Additionally, PRP has been reported to reduce testicular toxicity and ischemia–reperfusion injury, possibly through antioxidative and tissue-reparative mechanisms.ConclusionWhile preliminary findings highlight the promise of PRP and PRGF as emerging, plasma-based approaches in male reproductive medicine, further research is essential to standardize protocols, evaluate long-term efficacy and safety, and elucidate underlying molecular mechanisms before these therapies can be safely and effectively translated into routine clinical practice.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04602-0.Keywords: Platelet-rich plasma, Plasma rich in growth factors, PRP, PRGF, Male infertility, Spermatogenesis, Sperm cryopreservation, Testicular toxicity, Spermatozoa, Spermatogonial stem cell

Introduction

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and its biologically refined subtype, plasma rich in growth factors (PRGF), are autologous blood-derived products enriched with a high concentration of platelets and associated bioactive molecules [1, 2]. These products are derived through centrifugation of peripheral blood, a process that separates platelets from red blood cells and plasma components. PRP typically contains a heterogeneous mixture of platelets, leukocytes, and plasma proteins suspended in a small volume of plasma [3]. In contrast, PRGF is a leukocyte-depleted formulation that provides a more controlled and standardized release of specific growth factors [4]. A third related product, platelet rich plasma lysate (PRPL), refers to the extract obtained through either mechanical or freeze–thaw lysis of platelets, enabling the direct release of growth factor content from intracellular granules [5]. Platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) is a second-generation platelet concentrate that forms a fibrin matrix enriched with platelets, leukocytes, and growth factors, and promotes sustained tissue regeneration without the need for anticoagulants [6]. While all four share a common origin, they differ in cellular composition, growth factor content, immunogenicity, and clinical effects— differences likely to impact therapeutic efficacy [3, 7].

These platelet-derived products have shown regenerative potential in various fields of medicine, including orthopedics [8], maxillofacial surgery [9], dermatology [10], ophthalmology [11], and increasingly, reproductive medicine [12–17]. Within reproductive science, PRP has been extensively investigated in female fertility—particularly for ovarian rejuvenation and folliculogenesis [16, 18, 19]. However, its role in male reproductive health is far less established.

Male infertility remains a significant clinical concern, particularly in individuals with non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA), hypogonadism, or those undergoing chemotherapy or radiotherapy, which can lead to irreversible germ cell depletion [20]. Although sperm cryopreservation prior to gonadotoxic therapy is a standard fertility-preserving approach [21], freezing and thawing processes often result in cryoinjury, reducing sperm viability, membrane integrity, and motility [22, 23].

Emerging evidence suggests that PRP and its derivatives may exert cytoprotective and regenerative effects on testicular tissues and sperm cells [24–27]. These effects are attributed to their content of key growth factors such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), epidermal growth factor (EGF), and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) [28] —all of which are known to influence angiogenesis, cell survival, stem cell proliferation, and tissue remodeling [29–31].

In vitro studies have applied PRP and PRGF to spermatogonial stem cell (SSC) cultures, demonstrating enhanced viability, proliferation, and early germ cell differentiation [32–34]. Furthermore, in vivo models suggest that intratesticular or peritesticular injections of PRP may promote spermatogenesis, restore testicular architecture, and mitigate damage caused by toxins, ischemia–reperfusion injury, or diabetes-induced degeneration [35–38]. These interventions have also been associated with improved sperm parameters and hormonal profiles in some early clinical studies [27, 36, 39, 40].

Despite these encouraging results, clinical translation remains limited due to heterogeneity in PRP/PRGF/PRPL preparation protocols, inconsistent terminology, and lack of standardized dosage and administration guidelines. Therefore, this systematic review aims to consolidate and critically evaluate the current body of evidence on in vitro and in vivo applications of platelet-derived products in male reproductive medicine. Specifically, it explores their role in vitro spermatogenesis, sperm cryopreservation, male infertility treatment, and testicular regeneration, with the goal of clarifying their potential utility and identifying key areas for future research and clinical innovation.

Methods

Method of search and characteristics of included studies

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [41] (Fig. 1). The protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (ID: CRD42024580852) to ensure methodological transparency.

Flow-chart of the study selection procedure

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted using five major databases: MEDLINE/PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Web of Science. The initial search was performed up to October 2024 and updated in January 2025 to capture the most recent literature. No language or publication type restrictions were applied.

The search strategy employed a combination of MeSH terms and free-text keywords related to male reproductive health and platelet-based therapies. Keywords included: ("Seminal fluid" OR "Semen" OR"Sperm" OR "Spermatozoa" OR "Testis" OR "Testicle" OR "Gonad" OR "Azoospermia" OR "Aspermia" OR "Non‐obstructive azoospermia" OR "Oligospermia" OR "Teratozoospermia" OR "Male infertility" OR"Hypogonadism" OR "Spermatogonial stem cell" OR" Spermatogenesis") AND ("Platelet-rich plasma" OR "PRP" OR "Plasma rich in growth factors" OR "PRGF" OR "Platelet-rich fibrin" OR "Platelet lysate").

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were considered eligible for inclusion if they investigated the effects of PRP, PRGF, PRF, or PRPL on male reproductive outcomes in either human or animal models. Eligible studies included original in vitro, in vivo, or clinical investigations that examined endpoints such as spermatogenesis, sperm quality, hormonal regulation, testicular tissue restoration, or oxidative stress modulation.

Exclusion criteria included review articles, editorials, conference abstracts, and narrative reports lacking original data. Studies were also excluded if the intervention did not directly involve platelet-derived products or if outcomes were unrelated to male fertility or testicular function. Furthermore, articles focusing solely on female reproductive health or using platelet products in non-reproductive contexts were excluded from the analysis.

Study selection

All retrieved citations were imported into EndNote (version 20) for de-duplication. Three reviewers (SAM, AF, HH) independently screened titles and abstracts based on the inclusion criteria. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were reviewed. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, or by involving a fourth reviewer (MM) if necessary. A flow diagram (Fig. 1) illustrates the study selection process.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized and pilot-tested extraction form to ensure consistency and completeness. For each included study, the following information was collected: first author’s name, year of publication, country of origin, type of study (in vitro, in vivo, or clinical), characteristics of the experimental model (e.g., species, sample size), specific platelet-derived product used (PRP, PRGF, PRF, or PRPL), details of preparation protocols, route and frequency of administration, and main outcome measures. These outcomes included changes in sperm parameters (such as motility, count, morphology, and viability), testicular histology, hormonal levels, markers of oxidative stress, and molecular findings related to spermatogenesis or tissue repair. In cases of missing or unclear data, attempts were made to contact the corresponding authors for clarification. Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer to ensure accuracy and reduce bias.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias for each included study was independently assessed using the appropriate tools developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for various study designs [42]. For interventional studies, the JBI checklist for experimental studies was applied (Additional Tables 1 and 2). In the case of individual case reports, the JBI critical appraisal checklist for case reports was employed (Additional Tables 3 and 4). Each study was evaluated based on a series of predefined methodological criteria and rated as “Yes,” “No,” or “"Cannot Determine/Not Applicable/Not Reported”. Based on the percentage of “Yes” responses, the studies were categorized into three groups: low risk of bias (≥ 85%), moderate risk of bias (70–84%), and high risk of bias (< 70%). All assessments were conducted by two reviewers independently, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer. This evaluation allowed for a structured appraisal of the internal validity and methodological quality of the included studies.

Data synthesis

Given the considerable heterogeneity among the included studies in terms of design, species, sample size, type and preparation method of platelet-derived products, administration protocols, and outcome measures, quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) was not feasible. Therefore, a qualitative narrative synthesis was performed. The findings were systematically categorized into five main thematic domains to enhance clarity and interpretability: (1) studies investigating the role of PRP and related products in vitro spermatogenesis models; (2) studies evaluating the effects of PRP, PRGF, and platelet lysate on sperm incubation and cryopreservation outcomes; (3) in vivo studies exploring the therapeutic applications of intratesticular or systemic PRP administration in animal models of male infertility; (4) studies focusing on testicular protection against toxic agents or oxidative stress using platelet-based interventions; and (5) studies addressing the potential use of PRP/PRF in mitigating testicular damage following torsion/detorsion (T/D) injury. Within each domain, results were summarized in terms of biological effects, molecular mechanisms, and reported outcomes, while also highlighting study limitations and inconsistencies where applicable.

Results

Study characteristics

The initial database search yielded 2475 records, of which 538 were identified as duplicates and subsequently removed. Screening of the remaining 1937 titles and abstracts led to the exclusion of 1884 studies based on irrelevance to the review topic. After full-text screening and eligibility assessment based on inclusion criteria, 43 studies were included for final data extraction (Fig. 1). Of these, 4 focused on in vitro spermatogenesis, 17 examined the effects of incubating and/or cryopreserving sperm with platelet-derived products, 6 assessed intratesticular injection of PRP for male infertility treatment, 10 evaluated the impact of PRP on testicular toxicity, and 6 explored the use of PRP or PRF in testicular T/D injury models.

The methodological quality of the included studies varied. Among the 42 controlled intervention studies, 8 were assessed as having moderate risk of bias, while the remaining 34 were considered low risk (see Supplementary Tables 2 and 4).

In vitro spermatogenesis with PRP or PRGF supplemented media

Although limited in number, studies investigating the role of PRP or PRGF in supporting in vitro spermatogenesis report promising findings. All four included studies described positive effects on the maintenance and progression of spermatogenic processes (Table 1). For instance, supplementation of immature mouse testicular cultures with 5% PRGF was associated with enhanced production of flagellated sperm and increased markers of germ cell proliferation and differentiation, such as PLZF, SYCP3, ACRBP, and Ki67, along with reduced Bax expression (34).

Sperm motility and kinematics

Several studies suggest that PRP or PRGF may enhance total and progressive motility in both animal [26, 44–46] and human sperm [24, 25, 47–52]. Optimal concentrations differed; for instance, 10% PRP improved motility in buffalo bull semen and boer buck spermatozoa [26, 44], whereas 1–5% concentrations were more effective in human samples [24, 25, 48, 51]. Ghasemian Nafchi et al. [49] reported improved motility using 1%, 5%, and 10% PRGF in teratozoospermic samples. By contrast, Almadaly et al. [53] reported that a 15% PRP concentration was optimal for improving sperm motility in buffalo semen, indicating that species-specific differences may influence the ideal PRP dose. Nevertheless, not all studies confirmed the beneficial effects of platelet-derived products on sperm motility. Quintana et al. [54] reported no significant improvement in motility in normozoospermic human samples treated with PRGF at concentrations ranging from 5 to 40%. Likewise, Bader et al. [48] observed a decline in motility following the use of 10% PRP in similar samples. In addition, Pinaffi et al. [55] found that PRPL at 5% and 10% concentrations led to sperm agglutination, with no corresponding enhancement in motility.

Sperm viability and structural integrity

A number of studies demonstrate that PRP supplementation can also enhance sperm viability and structural integrity. Salama et al. [26, 44] reported that adding 10% PRP to the cryopreservation medium improved viability, acrosome integrity, plasma membrane integrity, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and DNA integrity in both buffalo and boer buck sperm. Similarly, Almadaly et al. [53] achieved comparable improvements by incorporating 15% PRP into buffalo semen cryopreservation media. In human sperm, Lorian et al. [52] and Nabavinia et al. [50] both found that specific PRP concentrations (1 × 105 platelets/μL) reduced DNA fragmentation and improved overall viability and chromatin integrity.

As in motility studies, determining the most effective concentration is essential. El-Sherbiny et al. [56] reported that 5% PRP was most effective for enhancing viability and structural integrity in bull semen, while Alcay et al. [46] recommended 25 × 106 platelets/mL to optimize MMP, acrosome integrity, and DNA integrity. Meanwhile, Yan et al. [25] showed significant gains in viability and plasma membrane integrity using 5% PRP. Interestingly, Bader et al. [48] found that 2% PRP was optimal for boosting sperm viability and reducing DNA fragmentation in their normozoospermic samples; higher concentrations (10%) proved detrimental.

Sperm morphology

Sperm morphology, another critical measure of sperm quality, has also been examined. Some researchers reported significant improvements after incubation or cryopreservation with PRP. For example, Salama et al. [26] noted a reduction in abnormal forms when 10% PRP was used. Likewise, Angellee et al. [47] documented better morphological outcomes by adding 0.2 mL of PRP to sperm samples, and Mirzaei et al. [24] found improved sperm morphology following 1% PRP treatment. However, not all studies confirm these benefits for improving sperm morphology [49, 52]. For example, Ghasemian Nafchi et al. [49] observed no notable changes in human teratozoospermic semen samples morphology following PRP treatment.

Antioxidant and protective effects

Several investigations have also highlighted the antioxidant and protective properties of PRP or PRGF supplementation. Commonly measured antioxidant enzymes include glutathione (GSH), total antioxidant capacity (TAC), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), while malondialdehyde (MDA) is often used as a marker of oxidative stress. Notably, many studies report that PRP/PRGF incubation or cryopreservation leads to increased levels of these antioxidant enzymes and a reduction in MDA [26, 44, 49, 50, 52, 53, 56].

Fertility outcomes

The positive effects of PRP on sperm quality have been shown to translate into improved fertility outcomes in several studies. Salama et al. [44] reported higher pregnancy and kidding rates in Boer bucks when 10% PRP-supplemented semen was used for artificial insemination. Similarly, Almadaly et al. [53] observed elevated in vivo fertility in both fertile and subfertile buffalo when 15% PRP was included in the semen extender. El-Sherbiny et al. [56] further demonstrated increased fertilization, cleavage, and blastocyst formation rates in buffalo sperm treated with 5% PRP.

However, no statistically significant changes were observed in sperm morphology or ejaculate volume.

In an animal model, Farhan et al. [60] demonstrated that intratesticular PRP injections in rabbits led to significant improvements in germinal layer thickness, Leydig and Sertoli cell counts, sperm concentration, motility, and morphology, compared to controls. Subsequently, in a human clinical study, Farhan et al. [61] evaluated 50 men with NOA and found that 30% (15/50) responded positively within 3–4 months, accompanied by reduced levels of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH).

Similarly, Al-Nasser et al. [62] investigated PRP injections in 91 infertile men with NOA. The authors observed increased numbers of motile and immotile sperm and documented restored spermatogenesis in two patients. However, a considerable amount of data was missing, and no significant changes in FSH levels were reported.

Abdulla et al. [57] assessed the effects of intratesticular PRP in rabbits using both fresh and cryopreserved sperm. Significant improvements were reported in sperm concentration, total and progressive motility, viability, and DNA fragmentation index before and after cryopreservation. Additionally, expression levels of sperm function–related genes, including CATSPER1, SPAG5, and HSPA4, were significantly elevated in the PRP-treated group. In a separate study, Abdulla et al. [63] examined the impact of PRP on hormonal and growth factor levels in rabbits, reporting increased serum testosterone and elevated levels of VEGF and IGF-1, along with enhanced sperm motility, morphology, and viability.

Improvement in testicular structure and function

PRP appears to promote histological restoration of the testes, particularly through enhancement of seminiferous tubule structure and organization—critical components for effective spermatogenesis. Multiple studies have reported regenerative changes, including reestablishment of normal cellular architecture following PRP administration [35–39, 65–68]. Notably, a significant reduction in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels—a marker of cellular injury—was observed in some models [66].

Several reports have also indicated increases in seminiferous tubule diameter and germinal epithelium thickness, suggesting improved spermatogenic capacity [36, 37, 39, 64, 68]. Additionally, PRP treatment has been associated with a reduction in interstitial space expansion, a common feature of chemically induced testicular damage [36, 67]. Increases in the number of spermatogonia, primary spermatocytes, and round spermatids—key germ cells involved in spermatogenesis—have also been reported [36, 64, 67, 68].

At the molecular level, PRP has been shown to upregulate several germ cell-specific genes involved in spermatogenic differentiation and maturation, including Dazl, Ddx4, Pwil1, Scp3, Ccna1, and Stra8 [65, 66]. These molecular changes may underpin the observed improvements in testicular structure.

Functionally, PRP-treated groups in most studies exhibited higher sperm counts and motility compared to untreated toxicant-exposed controls [36, 39, 68]. However, in some cases, sperm parameters remained below those of healthy control animals, indicating that PRP may provide partial rather than full restoration of spermatogenic function [36, 39]. In addition, improvements in sperm morphology have been documented, reflecting enhanced sperm quality [36, 38, 39, 68]. A number of studies have also noted a significant increase in serum testosterone levels following PRP treatment [35, 36, 39, 68].

Reduction of oxidative stress and cellular damage

Oxidative stress is a key mechanism underlying testicular toxicity. PRP treatment has been shown to reduce MDA levels, a major marker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative injury [35, 66]. Concurrently, PRP appears to enhance the activity of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, glutathione reductase (GR), and—depending on the model—GPx and catalase (CAT) [66, 68].

At the cellular level, PRP has demonstrated anti-apoptotic and pro-regenerative properties. This includes the downregulation of Bax (a pro-apoptotic marker), upregulation of Bcl-2 (an anti-apoptotic protein), and increased expression of proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA), a key marker of cell proliferation [65].

Furthermore, PRP has been linked to modulation of autophagic processes, which are essential for clearing damaged organelles and maintaining cellular homeostasis. Specifically, increased levels of LC3—a marker of autophagy—have been observed following PRP treatment, suggesting activation of this reparative pathway [65].

Effects on testicular tissue architecture, hormonal levels, and sperm quality

Multiple studies have reported that PRP and PRF therapies may contribute to the preservation or restoration of normal seminiferous tubule structure following testicular I/R injury in rats. Abdel Ghaffar et al. [69] demonstrated that the combination of PRP and tadalafil (TAD) was particularly effective in minimizing tissue damage. Eisa et al. [27] further found that PRF was more successful than PRP in promoting tissue regeneration and achieving near-normal histological architecture in T/D-injured testes. Similar improvements in seminiferous tubule integrity, germinal epithelium thickness, and the density of spermatogenic cells following PRP injection have been consistently reported across multiple studies [40, 70–72].

Beyond structural repair, several investigations have assessed the molecular responses to PRP and PRF. For instance, Abdel Ghaffar et al. [69] reported that PRP + TAD treatment significantly decreased levels of TNF-α, a key pro-inflammatory cytokine. Eisa et al. [27] found that PRF was superior to PRP in reducing expression of inflammatory genes such as Caspase-3, IL-1β, and TNF-α. These anti-inflammatory effects were further supported by Samy et al. [70], Gazia et al. [72], and Sekerci et al. [40], who reported reduced expression of apoptosis- and inflammation-related markers after PRP injection.

Regarding hormonal modulation, several studies indicated a significant rise in testosterone levels after PRP or PRF therapy. Abdel Ghaffar et al. [69] observed near-normal testosterone levels with combined PRP and TAD treatment. Similar increases in serum testosterone were reported by Eisa et al. [27], Kutluhan et al. [71], Gazia et al. [72], and Sekerci et al. [40]. However, findings on gonadotropin levels were mixed. While Eisa et al. [27] documented increased levels of estradiol, LH, and FSH following both PRP and PRF treatment, Kutluhan et al. [71] reported decreased LH and FSH levels, and Sekerci et al. [40] noted a reduction in LH but an increase in FSH following PRP injection.

The impact of PRP and PRF on sperm quality has also been explored. Eisa et al. [27] demonstrated that PRF led to greater improvements in sperm count, motility, and morphology compared to PRP and untreated T/D groups. Similarly, Samy et al. [70] and Kutluhan et al. [71] observed enhanced spermatogenesis, as reflected by higher Johnsen scores, in PRP-treated animals. In contrast, Abdel Ghaffar et al. [69] reported no significant improvement in sperm parameters following PRP injection, highlighting the variability in therapeutic outcomes.

Effects on antioxidant parameters

Oxidative stress plays a central role in testicular I/R injury, and platelet-derived products have shown promise in restoring redox balance. Abdel Ghaffar et al. [69] demonstrated that PRP combined with TAD restored antioxidant markers such as GSH and SOD to near-control levels. Eisa et al. [27] similarly found that PRF was more effective than PRP in lowering MDA levels and elevating SOD activity. Further support comes from Samy et al. [70], Gazia et al. [72], and Sekerci et al. [40], who reported increased TAC and decreased oxidative stress markers after PRP administration.

Discussion

In vitro sperm production with PRGF supplemented media

In vitro generation of functional sperm remains a long-standing scientific challenge. Although KSR (KnockOut Serum Replacement) has been widely used to supplement culture media, it has demonstrated significant limitations. Despite various modifications to media composition, complete spermatogenesis has not yet been achieved in vitro [73–76].

More recently, supplementing Alpha-MEM basal medium with 5% PRGF has been proposed as a promising alternative. Preliminary findings suggest that this approach may enhance cell viability and proliferation and potentially support the differentiation of mouse SSCs into flagellated sperm within immature testicular tissue culture systems [34]. These results indicate that PRGF could serve as a valuable component in the development of optimized culture media for in vitro spermatogenesis, although further validation is required.

In addition, three studies have reported that PRP enhances SSC viability and promotes differentiation [32, 33, 43]. While some findings indicated upregulation of post-meiotic gene expression (33), others emphasized pre-meiotic gene activation [32]. Moreover, 3D culture systems incorporating PRP demonstrated significantly greater SSC viability and proliferation compared to conventional 2D systems [32, 33]. Notably, one study identified 5% PRP as the optimal concentration yielding the most favorable outcomes [32].

However, a study by Lestari et al. reported no significant difference in SSC marker expression between PRP and FBS-supplemented media, which stands in contrast to the other findings. This study was rated as having a moderate risk of bias [43].

Nonetheless, the collective findings highlight promising directions for future research, particularly toward clinical translation. Notably, autologous PRGF is already approved by both the European Community and the United States Food and Drug Administration as a safe and cost-effective therapeutic option [77].

Use of platelet-rich products to enhance sperm quality and function during incubation and cryopreservation

Incorporating platelet-rich products—such as PRP, PRGF, and PRPL—into sperm incubation and cryopreservation protocols has emerged as a promising approach to improving sperm quality and functional parameters. Numerous studies have reported enhancements in motility, viability, membrane integrity, and resistance to oxidative damage. However, the effects vary depending on product concentration, preparation methods, and species, reflecting the complexity of these interventions. These effects are primarily attributed to the bioactive molecules released by platelets, including FGF, VEGF, IGF-1, NGF, and TGF-β, which modulate various cellular pathways [26, 44, 56, 78, 79].

Improvement in sperm motility

Multiple studies have shown that platelet-rich products significantly enhance sperm motility—including total motility, progressive motility, and velocity parameters such as VCL, VSL, and VAP [24–26, 44–52]. These effects are partly mediated by signaling cascades such as the Akt and ERK pathways activated by FGF [80] (Fig. 2). PRP has also been shown to improve mitochondrial activity and ATP production, contributing to better sperm survival and motility post-thaw [80, 81].

Optimal concentrations vary across studies, ranging from 1 to 15%, depending on the species and sperm condition [24–26, 44, 48, 49, 51, 53]. However, higher concentrations may lead to adverse effects such as sperm agglutination, resulting in decreased motility [54, 55].

Preservation of sperm viability and structural integrity

Platelet-derived products have been shown to improve viability, acrosome and plasma membrane integrity, MMP, and DNA integrity [24, 26, 44, 50, 52, 53]. These protective effects are attributed to the antioxidant capacity of PRP and growth factors such as IGF-1, which help maintain acrosomal protein integrity and reduce cryodamage [82]. Additionally, PRP proteins may buffer cell membranes during freezing and thawing [26, 83], and NGF may reduce necrosis and enhance viability [84, 85]. Effective concentrations range from 1 to 15%, with some studies reporting efficacy at platelet counts up to 25 × 10⁶/mL [24, 25, 46, 48, 53, 56].

Variable effects on sperm morphology

The impact of platelet-rich products on sperm morphology remains inconsistent. While some studies have reported significant improvements [24, 26, 47], others have found no statistically significant effect [49]. These discrepancies highlight the need for more targeted investigations into the mechanisms affecting morphological outcomes.

Antioxidant activity and reduction of oxidative stress

The inclusion of platelet-rich products in incubation or cryopreservation media consistently elevates antioxidant enzyme levels—such as GSH, TAC, GPx, and SOD—and reduces lipid peroxidation marker MDA [26, 44, 49, 50, 52, 53, 56]. This antioxidant activity is largely attributed to the presence of antioxidant molecules and trace elements, including zinc, IGF-1, and VEGF [86, 87]. For example, VEGF has been reported to reduce oxidative damage by activating the Nrf2 pathway [88].

Enhanced fertility outcomes

Sperm quality improvements associated with PRP treatment are closely linked to enhanced fertilization capacity and embryonic development [89–91]. Motility facilitates passage through the female reproductive tract and penetration of the oocyte, while DNA integrity ensures proper embryogenesis. In animal models such as buffalo and Boer bucks, PRP treatment has improved motility, membrane, and acrosome integrity, contributing to better fertility outcomes [44, 53, 56].

Limitations and future directions

Despite encouraging findings, further standardization is needed regarding PRP preparation, platelet concentration, and application protocols. Future research should also explore the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects, assess outcomes in suboptimal or infertile samples, and evaluate long-term reproductive safety and offspring health.

Intratesticular PRP injection as an emerging strategy for male infertility treatment

Intratesticular administration of PRP is gaining attention as a potential therapeutic approach for male infertility. In clinical studies involving infertile men, PRP treatment has been associated with improved semen parameters, including increased sperm concentration, enhanced motility, and reduced DNA fragmentation [58, 59]. Moreover, in select cases of NOA, PRP has shown the capacity to restore spermatogenesis [58, 61, 62].

The regenerative effects of PRP are primarily mediated by its high content of growth factors such as PDGF, TGF-β, IGF-1, FGF, and VEGF. These factors play essential roles in angiogenesis, cellular proliferation, differentiation, and tissue repair [92]. Specifically, VEGF has been linked to improved sperm motility and velocity [93], while IGF-1 is associated with enhanced overall motility [94]. Together, they create a supportive microenvironment conducive to testicular tissue regeneration and the resumption of spermatogenesis.

While some studies show promise in treating NOA, the results are less consistent compared to OAT. For example, Al-Nasser et al. [62] reported limited improvements in sperm presence and no significant change in FSH levels, underscoring the need for further research to clarify the specific effects of PRP across different NOA subtypes and to identify predictive factors for treatment response. It is also important to note that some studies, including Al-Nasser et al. [62], suffered from missing data, which may have affected the reliability of the findings. Moreover, all three studies that investigated PRP treatment in NOA [58, 61, 62] were assessed as having a moderate risk of bias, which may further contribute to the inconsistency in outcomes and limit the overall strength of the evidence.

A further challenge in interpreting the current literature is the absence of standardized PRP preparation protocols. Variability in platelet concentration, activation methods, and injection frequency may contribute to inconsistent clinical outcomes. Standardization of these procedures is critical for reproducibility and comparability across studies. Moreover, long-term safety data are lacking; thus, further research should assess the durability of treatment responses and the potential for adverse effects on testicular tissue.

PRP as a therapeutic modality for testicular toxicity and dysfunction

PRP has been increasingly investigated as a potential therapeutic candidate for the management of testicular toxicity and dysfunction, due to its content of bioactive molecules and its capacity to support tissue regeneration. Its beneficial effects are mediated through modulation of tissue architecture, enhancement of spermatogenesis, anti-inflammatory properties, and antioxidant defense.

Mechanisms of PRP in testicular repair and spermatogenic enhancement

Studies have consistently reported improvements in testicular architecture, including restoration of seminiferous tubule organization and increased epithelial thickness, following PRP injection in various models of testicular damage [35–39, 65–68].

The regenerative capacity of PRP is largely attributed to growth factors such as PDGF, VEGF, IGF-1, and FGF, which promote cellular proliferation, angiogenesis, and differentiation [95]. In particular, FGF and EGF are key regulators of SSC self-renewal and differentiation [96] (Fig. 3).

Supporting this, several studies have demonstrated upregulation of germ cell-specific genes and enhanced sperm parameters following PRP treatment [36, 64–68], further validating its potential to promote spermatogenesis.

Anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects mediated by key molecular pathways

The protective properties of PRP extend beyond tissue regeneration. One major mechanism involves activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, which enhances cell survival and reduces reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation [35, 97]. PRP also suppresses the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) pathway in macrophages, leading to decreased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and mitigation of local inflammation [98, 99]. For example, IL-1β levels are significantly reduced following PRP treatment, reflecting attenuated inflammatory responses [99].

Additionally, PRP boosts antioxidant defenses by lowering lipid peroxidation markers such as MDA and elevating the activity of antioxidant enzymes including SOD and GPx [35, 66, 68]. This antioxidative effect plays a critical role in protecting testicular cells from oxidative injury, especially in chemically-induced or disease-related testicular dysfunction.

Clinical implications and future perspectives

The multifaceted mechanisms of PRP indicate its potential as a therapeutic intervention for various types of testicular injury, including those resulting from chemotherapeutic agents and metabolic disorders such as diabetes [35–39, 64–67]. Nonetheless, substantial inter-study variability exists due to differences in PRP preparation methods, dosing, and timing of administration. It is also worth noting that two of the supporting studies [38, 64] were rated as having a moderate risk of bias, which may influence the reliability of their findings. Establishing standardized protocols is essential for achieving consistent therapeutic outcomes.

Therapeutic potential of platelet-rich products in testicular T/D injury

Testicular T/D injury represents a significant cause of male infertility, underscoring the need for novel therapeutic approaches. Recent studies have explored the potential of PRP and PRF to mitigate the damaging effects of I/R injury, highlighting their emerging clinical relevance.

Improvements in testicular architecture, sperm quality, and hormonal balance

Experimental studies indicate that intratesticular administration of PRP and PRF can alleviate testicular damage, preserve seminiferous tubule structure, and enhance sperm parameters in rodent models of I/R injury [27, 40, 69–72]. These therapeutic effects are largely attributed to the bioactive molecules in these preparations, including TGF-β1, IGF, VEGF, EGF, and PDGF, which collectively promote cellular regeneration, inhibit apoptosis, and suppress inflammatory responses [100] (Fig. 3).

Specifically, EGF has been implicated in stimulating spermatogenesis and reducing germ cell apoptosis [101], while TGF-β1 downregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β [102]. PRP and PRF have also been shown to enhance antioxidant defenses by increasing the activity of enzymes like CAT and SOD, thereby contributing to oxidative stress regulation [27, 70].

The potentially enhanced regenerative efficacy of PRF compared to PRP [27] may be attributed to its sustained release of growth factors over time. Additionally, its ease of preparation, rapid processing, and absence of additives have been reported as advantages that could support its application as an autologous, non-immunogenic, and cost-effective option [103]. Nonetheless, both PRP and PRF have shown encouraging effects in reducing testicular damage and improving sperm quality in preclinical models [27], though further comparative studies are warranted.

In addition to morphological and functional improvements, several studies report that PRP and PRF treatments increase serum testosterone and modulate gonadotropic hormones, including FSH, LH, and estradiol [27, 40, 69, 71, 72], suggesting potential involvement in testicular steroidogenesis and Leydig cell function.

Anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and antioxidant effects

Further supporting their therapeutic role, PRP and PRF have been shown to reduce the expression of pro-apoptotic and inflammatory markers such as caspase-3, IL-1β, and TNF-α in animal models of T/D injury [27, 69, 70]. These findings suggest that platelet-derived products may reduce cellular injury by attenuating inflammation and programmed cell death.

Moreover, PRP and PRF appear to enhance testicular antioxidant capacity by increasing levels of GSH and SOD, while decreasing MDA, a key marker of lipid peroxidation [27, 40, 69, 70, 72]. Such antioxidant activity may play a role in preserving testicular cell integrity and function during oxidative stress.

Limitations and clinical implications

While initial preclinical findings suggest potential benefits, several important limitations must be considered. Most of the available studies were conducted in animal models, and the applicability of their results to human clinical settings remains uncertain due to interspecies physiological differences. Furthermore, standardized protocols for the preparation of PRP and PRF—including optimal dosage, injection frequency, and timing—have not yet been established. The combined use of these treatments with other therapeutic approaches also remains unexplored.

It is also noteworthy that some key studies [69, 72] were assessed as having a moderate risk of bias, which may affect the reliability and generalizability of their conclusions. Therefore, interpreting these findings requires caution.

Nonetheless, the observed regenerative, anti-inflammatory, and hormonal effects suggest that platelet-derived products may have potential as adjunctive therapies following detorsion in cases of testicular torsion. However, robust clinical trials with standardized methodologies and long-term safety evaluations are essential to confirm and define the therapeutic utility of PRP and PRF in this context.

Conclusion

The current body of evidence suggests that platelet-derived products, particularly PRP and PRGF, hold considerable promise as plasma-based therapeutic tools in reproductive medicine. In vitro studies indicate that media supplemented with these products can enhance cell viability, proliferation, and differentiation during sperm generation. Similarly, their use in sperm incubation and cryopreservation has been associated with improved motility, viability, and structural integrity across various animal models. However, the effects are dose-dependent, and optimal concentrations appear to vary by context, underscoring the need for application-specific protocols.

In vivo applications, including intratesticular injections of PRP, have demonstrated potential benefits in male infertility by improving hormonal profiles, testicular regeneration, and spermatogenesis. Additionally, their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and tissue-reparative properties may contribute to mitigating testicular damage caused by toxins or torsion-detorsion injury.

Despite these encouraging findings, the evidence is not yet conclusive. Variability in preparation methods, limited long-term data, and differences between species highlight the need for further well-designed studies. Future research should aim to standardize PRP protocols, explore long-term outcomes, and elucidate molecular mechanisms to better assess its therapeutic potential and ensure safe clinical translation.
