Syncope During Cycling
Authors: Madhumita Kolluri, Aryan Mehta, Paul D Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSyncope during exertion often heralds cardiac disease.Case SummaryWe evaluated 2 cyclists with syncope during bicycling without identifiable cause, then identified 23 additional cyclists (mean age: 68 ± 8 years; 84% male) who had experienced 32 syncopal episodes during cycling to develop a clinical profile. Only 1 cyclist was aged <50. There were no reports of prodrome and 23 episodes of postsyncope confusion. Most episodes occurred on road bicycles with cyclists leaning forward (n = 19). Four cyclists recalled turning their head immediately before the event. No cardiac or neurological etiology was identified in the 23 cyclists.DiscussionWe conclude that cycling may be associated with syncope without an identifiable cause.Take-Home MessageThe etiology of this syndrome is unclear, but the age of the cyclists, the high rate of postevent confusion, and associated head turning in some participants raise the speculative possibility that this is a form of carotid sinus sensitivity.Key words: athletes; cycling, sports cardiology; syncope

Syncope is a self-limited, transient loss of consciousness caused by inadequate cerebral blood flow.1 It has an estimated 2-year mortality of 25%, which is largely attributed to the trauma suffered during syncope-associated falls.1 Syncope during inactivity or immediately after exercise is generally benign and is due to vasovagal or neurocardiogenic syncope, but syncope during exertion is ominous and should raise concerns for structural heart diseases or cardiac arrhythmia.2 We encountered 2 patients, one a board-certified neurologist, with syncope that occurred during bicycling, and who had no detectable cardiac or other abnormalities on testing. We were unaware of similar reports in the medical literature but were aware of similar anecdotal reports in the lay cycling literature.3 Therefore, we queried the members of a large local cycling club to determine if other such cases occurred and to develop a clinical profile of these cases.

Methods

Cases of syncope during bicycling were identified by sending emails about the study to 886 members of a bicycling club with 412 active members. Cyclists who had experienced “fainting” or had “passed out” while cycling were asked to respond. Seven cyclists (3.5%) representing 8 occurrences responded. An additional 26 cyclists who learned of the study by word of mouth, internet searches, or from newspaper reports also responded, yielding 33 cyclists. Of this group, 25 cyclists met inclusion criteria, provided consent as approved by the Hartford Hospital Institutional Review Board, and were included in this study (Figure 1). Participants completed a questionnaire to describe their syncopal event and were interviewed by phone using a standardized questionnaire (Supplemental Appendix). The questionnaire asked if the event occurred when the participant was riding “upright” or “leaning forward.” Medical records were obtained and reviewed if available. Responses were tabulated, and basic statistics were used to quantify the results. If a participant had multiple syncopal events during cycling, each event was analyzed separately.

Results

The 25 study participants consisted of 21 male and 4 female cyclists, with a mean age of 68 ± 8 years (age range: 44-87 years); they experienced a total of 32 syncopal events (Table 1). All but 6 cyclists were aged ≥65 at the time of the event. Twenty-two cyclists (28 events) were riding road bicycles at the time of the event; 1 cyclist was riding a mountain bike (2 events), and 2 were riding gravel bikes (2 events).

Six cyclists had multiple episodes, and 5 (participants E, F, G, H, and R) had also experienced syncope several years before their cycling event. These noncycling episodes occurred both during other exercise (participant G) and at rest (participants E, F, H, and R). Most episodes (n = 19) occurred while the cyclist was leaning forward, whereas 13 episodes occurred in the upright position. Nineteen participants (23 episodes) recalled actively pedaling at the time of syncope. Among the 10 participants who remembered their actions before the event, 5 of the 14 syncopal events occurred immediately after the cyclist turned their head to the side. One cyclist had this experience twice. All 25 participants denied having any “warning signs” or having experienced a presyncopal prodrome.

All syncopal episodes except 1 resulted in injuries, including 9 episodes with rib fractures and 4 episodes with clavicular fractures. There were 2 episodes with subdural hematomas and 5 episodes with concussion. Two episodes were associated with a stroke.

Discussion

A 2021 Danish study estimated the incidence of single-bicycle crashes to be 55 per 1,000 person-years.4 That study included 6,793 active cyclists and found that 18% of single-bicycle crashes were associated with significant injury.4 Most single-bicycle crashes are due to mechanical issues. We are unaware of studies examining the percentage of single-bicycle accidents related to syncope, but we became interested in this issue when 2 patients experienced syncope during cycling without a medical explanation. Of the 32 cycling syncopal episodes in our 25 study participants, 23 episodes were associated with postsyncope confusion, which is typically seen in neurocardiogenic syncope. Two of the participants were found to have had a stroke, but the other participants had no cardiac or other explanations detected on subsequent medical evaluation.

In 23 of the 32 episodes, the cyclist remembered actively pedaling when they passed out. This eliminates the most common benign etiology of exercise-related syncope, neurocardiogenic syncope, which typically occurs immediately or soon after the cessation of exercise and is due to the loss of the “muscle pump” returning blood to the central circulation.5 In contrast to postexertion syncope, syncope during active exercise usually indicates a clinically significant cardiac abnormality—either structural heart disease or arrhythmia—but no etiology was found in most of our study participants. Structural conditions associated with exercise-related syncope include atherosclerotic coronary artery disease, primarily in adults, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, anomalous coronary artery origin, myocarditis, arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, intramural coronary arteries, and aortic stenosis, whereas purely arrhythmogenic causes include long QT, Brugada, and catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia syndromes.5

All but 4 of the syncopal episodes in this report occurred in individuals aged >60 years, a group known to have decreased arterial compliance and increased carotid sinus sensitivity.6 Carotid sinus pressure can produce both bradycardia and peripheral vasodilation, resulting in syncope.7 In 5 of the 14 episodes in which the participant could recall their actions immediately before the crash, they recalled turning their head to the side. We are also aware of a report of a speed-cycling veteran who lost consciousness during a race when he looked backward to assess the competition.8 This cyclist was found to have carotid sinus sensitivity on examination and required a pacemaker. The age of our study participants and the reports of head turning in some of them raise the possibility that at least some syncopal events during cycling were due to carotid sinus pressure. All but 4 of the 32 syncopal episodes in our participants occurred with road bicycles. These bicycles are often ridden with a torso-lowered, head-raised position, which stretches and neck and could increase carotid sinus pressure from the neck muscles when the head is turned, but most of our participants either could not remember or denied turning their head before syncope.

Riding position and decreased arterial compliance may be contributing to these accidents. Since the etiology of these episodes is unclear, preventive strategies are also unclear. We suggest that cyclists who have experienced such an event avoid riding with a torso-lowered, head-raised position, even though many of our participants were not in this position at syncope.

This report has multiple limitations. Our data are based on self-reports and depend on the participant's memory of the event, which introduces responder and reporter bias. We cannot estimate an incidence rate because cyclists who had not experienced an event were not requested to respond, and many of the study participants learned of the study via public media. Participants had to survive the event to be included, so it is possible that those who did not survive had important cardiac conditions that caused the syncope. Initial participants were recruited from a cycling club, which biases the events toward road cyclists. Most of our participants were male, with only 4 females being included in the study.

Conclusions

We identified 25 cyclists with 32 syncopal episodes, primarily while road cycling. The cause of the syncope remains unclear, but the age of the victims, the observation that some events occurred with head turning, and the fact that carotid sinus sensitivity can produce sudden syncope prompts us to question if carotid sinus syncope is a possible cause in at least some of the riders. Nevertheless, until an etiology is clear, cyclists who experience such events require a cardiac and neurological work-up to exclude pathology. Clinicians should be aware that unexplained syncope is possible in cyclists.
