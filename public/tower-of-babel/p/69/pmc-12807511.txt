Youths’ strategies for HIV status disclosure in rural Kenya and Uganda: You can’t just trust everyone”
Authors: Jason Johnson-Peretz, Anjeline Onyango, Cecilia Akatukwasa, Fredrick Atwine, Lawrence Owino, Titus MO Arunga, Marilyn Nyabuti, Janice Litunya, Jane Kabami, Florence Mwangwa, Moses R Kamya, Diane Havlir, Theodore Ruel, James Ayieko, Carol S Camlin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:HIV status disclosure is influenced by social values, roles, and rules people take on and adapt, aligned with developmental transitions and cultural expectations. Understanding motivations for disclosure among adolescents and young adults with HIV (AYAH) is important for promoting HIV care engagement.Methods:A cluster-randomized controlled trial in 28 rural communities in Kenya and Uganda tested an intervention to improve viral suppression and health outcomes among AYAH 15–24 years of age. A longitudinal qualitative study embedded within the trial aimed to identify the intervention’s mechanisms of action via semi-structured interviews with n = 111 AYAH, providers (n = 45), and selected family members. Analysis for this paper focused on motivations and barriers to disclosure among AYAH, with attention to targets/confidants, contexts, and communication strategies.Results:Social roles and expectations influenced HIV status disclosure motivations among AYAH, including filial piety towards parents, spontaneous reciprocity with friends, and conscientiousness towards school supervisors. Women tended to prefer disclosure before marriage. Younger men tended to prefer disclosure after marriage, but older male youth looking for seroconcordance in a partner were willing to disclose before marriage. Medication bottles or pills often provided a contextual opening for disclosure conversations. Disclosure of antiretroviral therapy (ART) use appeared to present more significant hurdles than disclosure of HIV status alone.Interpretation:Encouraging not only HIV status but treatment disclosure may facilitate support for ART adherence. Tailoring disclosure strategies to particular target-confidant types may help youth disclose more easily and validate their social values and chosen ties while supporting successful care engagement.Keywords: Youth, Adolescent, Kenya, Uganda, HIV, Disclosure, Motivations

Background

Young people between 15 and 24 years of age accounted for around 3.4 million people living with HIV globally in 2019 (the last year this age group was disaggregated from the adult population), a number that continues to increase through both the maturation of perinatally infected children and ongoing post-natal HIV acquisition. In 2023, 1.4 million children aged 0–14 were living with HIV, of whom 120.000 were new infections (WHO, 2024). The previous year, despite promising reductions in vertical HIV transmission, perinatal infection rates stood at around 8.5 % in Southern and Eastern Africa (8.9 % in Kenya and 6.8 % in Uganda) (Global HIV, 2023; UNAIDS, 2022b). Kenya saw 5.200 new infections among children and 12.000 among male and female youth in 2022, whilst Uganda recorded 6.000 and 20.000 new cases, respectively (UNAIDS, 2022a, 2022b; WHO, 2022). Until a functional cure becomes available, these children, like adults, will inevitably confront the reality of disclosing their HIV status to others, especially as they grow through adolescence.

Disclosure patterns are important indicators of how youth manage the transition to adulthood (Dahourou et al., 2017; Nöstlinger et al., 2015). Adolescents and young adults with HIV (AYAH) who disclose their HIV status have better health outcomes and HIV care engagement than youth who do not, despite the potential for enacted and internalized stigma (Menon et al., 2007; Midtbø et al., 2012; Montalto et al., 2017; Mwangwa et al., 2023). For youth of school-going age, disclosure patterns while in school are also salient to academic success as well as anti-retroviral therapy (ART) maintenance (Johnson-Peretz et al., 2022; Kimera et al., 2021). Prior research has explored barriers and motivators of disclosure among adults in Africa (Dessalegn et al., 2019; King et al., 2008; Lyimo et al., 2014; Moyer, 2012; Ngige & Ndayala, 2020; Ssali et al., 2010), or have examined adults’ disclosure of a child’s HIV status to the (perinatally infected) child or youth (Arrivé et al., 2018; Doat et al., 2019; Eneh et al., 2019; Mengesha et al., 2023; Midtbø et al., 2012; Okawa et al., 2017; Wadja, 2023), but despite research on barriers to disclosure, few studies have examined young people’s own motivations and strategies for doing so (Hogwood et al., 2013; Mburu et al., 2014; Moraa et al., 2023; Nöstlinger et al., 2015). (For this paper, we define an adolescent as between 15 and 17 years old and a young adult as between 18 and 24 years of age; the term ‘youth’ encompasses both groups.)

Communication of HIV status often involves several distinguishable steps. Chaudoir et al., (2011) categorise the disclosure process based on antecedent, event, and outcome goals. Antecedent goals encompass two separate motivational strategies: approach and avoidance. Approach-oriented goals seek positive rewards like strengthening relationships, while avoidance seeks to foreclose rejection and stigma. The two are not always clearly distinguishable, as researchers have also found that some youth disclose early in relationships precisely to pre-empt rejection (Laborde-Balen et al., 2023). In terms of the disclosure event, context and communication expectations inform goals, while “the effect of confidant type is an additional factor … which warrants much further consideration” (Chaudoir et al., 2011; Ssali et al., 2010). The disclosure target (or ‘confidant type’) is particularly salient for researchers tracking disclosure as a series of events involving different people. Dima et al. (2014) in a study of voluntary and involuntary HIV disclosure in Tanzania advocate that we “conceptualise HIV disclosure as a multi-dimensional phenomenon consisting of several clusters of related disclosure events involving different types of actors” (emphasis ours) (Dima et al., 2014). Thus, some people may ‘test out’ disclosure on friends before disclosing to family (Gillard & Roark, 2013; Hogwood et al., 2013). Importantly, disclosure outcomes may influence future disclosure strategies (Polejack et al., 2020).

Li et al., (2017) builds on this idea to highlight the salience of context on disclosure patterns understood within larger social rules around communication and belonging. Thus, one’s manner of disclosure follows cultural lines “and can come in diverse forms such as stories, parables, or other indirect routes” (King et al., 2008). Using a combination of privacy management theory (e.g. boundary negotiation and privacy rules) and social influence theory, Li argues that disclosure is not simply about the self, but about bringing multiple people into relation through the medium of communication. Other researchers have also noted how HIV disclosure (re-)configures social relationships (Wadja, 2023).

As a process, disclosure changes over the life course, sometimes fading into the background as unnecessary; for adolescents, however, disclosure holds greater importance (Moyer, 2012). When viewed as a process, disclosure is shaped by cultural contexts, social roles, and social rules for communication (Armistead et al., 1999; Cama et al., 2020; Dima et al., 2014; Kalichman et al., 2003). The adolescent life stage entails particular contexts and constraints, in which people not only have unique linguistic patterns with peers but also where they learn what communication styles and content work in their cultural or community contexts and which do not (Bourdieu, 1990; Elias, 1939; Gillard & Roark, 2013; Turner et al., 2017 [1969]; Van Gennep, 2019 [1909]). Indigenous African concepts of Ubuntu (Utu in Kenya, Obuntu in Uganda) recognise that people are human beings through their relationships with other human beings, and these relationships inform patterns of communication and social rules (Letseka, 2012; Mugumbate & Chereni, 2020; Sambala et al., 2020; Wathuta, 2018). This influences how young people in Africa are expected to relate to elders, their peers, and younger children, since in any given cultural group, different targets embody different social norms of relation, and each target group has different information flows, management and communication norms (Mburu et al., 2014; Siu et al., 2012). Thus, how one is expected to relate to teachers, romantic partners, friends, and family may differ depending on cultural orientation. One’s own status within a community based on gender, wealth, or other markers of difference and identity also interact with these ‘constraints’ and cultural ‘givens’ (Bennett, 2015; Tylor, 1871). To support young people in their disclosure processes, we must attend to these culturally-informed communication patterns as they relate to young people’s understanding of their disclosure purpose, target choice, and timing (Gillard & Roark, 2013; Hogwood et al., 2013).

This paper takes the above insights to examine the process whereby young people actively select how and to whom to disclose their status. We seek to answer the question, “What motivates young people to disclose to certain people and to do so in particular ways?” This paper explores that question using data gathered during an HIV-care-engagement intervention involving adolescents and young adults (AYAH) between the ages of 15 and 24 in rural Kenya and Uganda. We also consider family and partner advice to the participant on how or to whom to disclose their status while attending to information about cultural norms as expressed by our participants. Attention to disclosure among peers and school staff builds on our previous work both around adult disclosure and HIV stigma among school-going youth (Johnson-Peretz et al., 2022; Maeri et al., 2016).

Methods

Study context

The SEARCH-Youth study (Strategic Antiretroviral Therapy and HIV testing for Youth in Rural Africa – NCT03848728; KEMRI: SERU 3834; UNCST: HS 2542) was a community cluster-randomized controlled trial in 28 rural communities in western Kenya (primarily Luo, Abasuba, Kisii, and Luhya ethnic regions) and southwestern Uganda. (primarily in the Nyankole ethnic region). We evaluated whether a combination of interventions would improve viral suppression and health outcomes among AYAH aged 15–24 years. Interventions used a life-stage approach to address structural barriers and psycho-social needs. The main (quantitative) study results have been published elsewhere; briefly, of the 1988 enrolled participants, 80 % of youth in the control arm of the trial achieved viral suppression, compared to 88 % of youth in the intervention arm. (Ruel et al., 2023). Our longitudinal qualitative study was embedded within the trial and aimed to identify the intervention’s barriers, facilitators, and mechanisms of action at patient, family, clinic, and community levels.

Participants

The qualitative study comprised three cohorts of participants: 1) youth (n = 111) systematically selected from 8 communities (4 intervention, 4 control) and balanced by age (15–17 vs 18–24 years), sex, and HIV care status; 2) family members (n = 71) identified by youth as most involved in their HIV care; and 3) providers (n = 45) purposively selected from two intervention clinics per region. Providers included nurses, clinical officers, adherence counsellors, research assistants, and peer educators. This paper primarily focuses on data from the youth cohort.

Data collection and analysis

A team of two women and three men, trained qualitative researchers (CA, LO, AO, FA, TOA) and native speakers of the local languages (DhuLuo, English, Swahili, Runyankole), conducted audio-recorded, in-depth, semi-structured interviews with youth at baseline (BL; June to December 2019; n = 83), one year later (F1; October 2020 to February 2021; n = 76, of whom 27 were new, replacing baseline participants lost to follow-up) and with youth who had either a BL or F1 interview at the end of the second year (F2; October 2021 to April 2022; n = 46) (See Table 1.). Baseline interviews were conducted at the commencement of intervention activities; the other two interviews followed either one (F1) or two (F2) years of intervention. To develop the interview guides, we drew upon multiple theoretical models that have informed HIV research among youth, including developmental and life-stage theories (Sawyer et al., 2012); theories of health and social behaviour, including social support, health beliefs around perceived risk (e.g. from not adhering to ART) (Ajzen, 1985; Catania et al., 1990; Fishbein & Ajzen, 1977) and social learning theory pertaining self-efficacy and vicarious efficacy (Bandura, 1989, 2001; Rogers, 2003; Wood, 2000), in addition to theories of stigma (especially as applied to HIV/AIDS) (Goffman, 2009 [1963]; Link & Phelan, 2001; Parker & Aggleton, 2003). Interviews explored social support systems, HIV care and stigma experiences, and HIV status disclosure. The team ensured confidentiality by conducting interviews in private and comfortable locations convenient for the consented participants.

Ethical approvals

The SEARCH-Youth study was approved by the Kenya Medical Research Institute (KEMRI, 3834), the Makerere University School of Medicine Research and Ethics Committee (REC REF 2019-014), the Uganda National Council of Science and Technology (UNCST, HS 2542), and the University of California San Francisco (UCSF) Committee on Human Research (18–25703). All study participants provided written informed consent to be interviewed. For our youth participants, national guidelines in Kenya and Uganda do not require parental consent for youth over the age of 12 and 14 respectively, to participate in sexual and reproductive health research if they have a sexually transmitted disease, including HIV ((KEMRI), 2015).

Results

Youth offered examples from each stage of the disclosure process including several target types. Below, we present antecedent approach motives and target choices before offering event-based examples that underscore culturally embedded decision-making and communication strategies; passive disclosure (e.g. through leaving a medication bottle visible) is included in these strategies. Finally, we note outcomes our participants related to other disclosure experiences before briefly mentioning some disclosure-avoidant motivations. Older youth tended to be more forthcoming, while younger participants were more restrained.

Social expectations and encouragement (antecedent motives)

Participants considered several factors during the antecedent stage, including outside encouragement and cultural expectations around family, marriage, and friendship.

Providers encouraged active disclosure. As one young male said, “The health worker advised me that if I disclose to people, I do not weaken very fast. … I think he told me the right thing” (15 y.o. male, Uganda community A). The rationale promoting ART adherence subtly directed participants to select their disclosure targets accordingly, as another young man stated, “<I disclosed > for them to be able to help me when I am supposed to take my drugs. … They remind me about my scheduled appointment date for the clinic” (16 y.o. male, Kenya community B).

Participants acknowledged that disclosing ART use offered freedom to take medication without having to hide from others. Simply initiating ART can be connected to cultural expectations warranting disclosure, as one participant expressed:

“The reason < I shared with my husband before beginning ART> is that it is good for the two of you to discuss something first and find a solution to it. Once they have been solved, then you go and do it while you are free and happy. You do not just decide to take them and bring them in the house before sharing it out; it cannot be good. Therefore, I had to go, share with him that this is what is going on, and we are supposed to do this.”

19 y.o. female, Kenya community D

This example suggests that disclosure can involve distinguishing between disclosure of HIV status and its separability from disclosure of treatment status. This distinction was more evident in the event phase, but highlighted in this stage by another participant who sought advice after receiving a diagnosis and the resulting counsel to continue ART:

“I feel very free, and one major contributor to my stability is that I disclosed to my sister and my father, who all encouraged me to stick to treatment. Being their junior, I heed their advice, and for that matter, I am very free. < … Before disclosing>, I even thought of committing suicide but after disclosing to them, I felt relieved and strong. < … > I disclosed to my sister and father two days after diagnosis. < … > They have been very key in my life and besides, they are older, and I knew they would show me the right direction.”

24 y.o. female, Kenya community G

Calling upon existing social networks after diagnosis to seek consolation and advice was common, especially among our older participants.

Cultural factors

As the above examples attest, not all motivational factors related to pharmaceutical adherence; our participants also cited cultural responsibilities ranging from the social expectations of friendship, values around filial and matrimonial honour, and academic conscientiousness.

Friendship motivated many of our participants, particularly adolescent men, to disclose, though some adolescents felt a conflict between obligation and fear before disclosing. One participant expressed this conflict clearly: “we are very close with one another and as we usually share, he has some kind of advices that seem to be useful to me – but I still lack the freedom to share with him” (17 y.o. male, Kenya community D). Despite his desire to honour the closeness he has with his friend, the young man was still challenged in opening up.

While many in our younger cohort formed friendships in their local communities or at school, mobile young adults found friends in new locations. In one instance, two housemates became friends and disclosed to one another partly because they were from the same tribe and living in the multi-ethnic capital:

“I just told <my friend> when we were in Nairobi. I shared with her that I had been found HIV-positive but I did not know what to do. … She did not take it badly or even abandon me; instead, she continued advising me that ‘N. if you are found positive, take drugs. Do not forget to take drugs.’ … The reason as to why I chose to disclose to her my status is because she takes me as a sister, and I do the same. We were staying together – there were so many tribes in Nairobi, and she was from our tribe Luo, and also from <the same township > just like me.”

23 y.o. female, Kenya community B

Pre-existing social ties are multifaceted and involve not only affection but also a sense of kinship and reciprocity with one another. In this way, friendship is a disclosure motivation in itself, without necessarily involving ART adherence as a factor.

The concept of filial piety, a feeling of both love and duty towards a parent or elder sibling, is a particular example of this multidimensionality in social ties. Male and female participants expressed this as a motivating factor.

“It was so hard for me to tell mother, although I later did. The health providers had always asked me for her telephone number so as to tell her how I was found HIV positive, but I persistently refused to provide it to them, because I never wanted her to know my status. But later I realised it was wrong telling outsiders and not telling my parent.”

17 y.o. female, Uganda community E

That sense of obligation to parents extends even to others who are viewed as parental figures, as a young man related: “I told the provider to call her in so that she could see my results. I could not keep it secret from her because she is like a mother to me” (22 y.o. female, Kenya community D). These examples show that the sense of devotion to one’s parent figures is a sufficient motivating factor on its own, without the more pragmatic reasons of seeking advice or ensuring care should the participant fall sick.

Participants also revealed culturally salient factors when asked about hypothetical disclosure targets, particularly when distinguishing between disclosure to a spouse versus a boyfriend/girlfriend. This was aptly put by one adolescent male, who said, “You know girlfriend is different from wife. I might tell her and once we have a disagreement, she will go telling other people. And that can tarnish your name.” (16 y.o. male, Kenya community B). In fact, men and women evinced different disclosure patterns. Men expressed a desire to delay disclosure, and women preferred to disclose early; the exception to this pattern was when men agreed to do couple’s testing.

In contrast to the adolescent men, young adult women also mentioned disclosing either early in a relationship or when a decision about marriage would have to be made:

“I disclosed to my husband about my status when he was wooing me. … I just told him point blank about my HIV status that I am HIV positive even though he is interested in me. Social expectations also include being responsible on the job or at school. No participants mentioned disclosing to their employer, but several adolescents mentioned school-motivated disclosure. Surprisingly, the disclosure target was not always a school official; one adolescent male said he’d disclosed to his uncle, explaining, “I wanted him to understand why I missed school on some days … <since> he pays my fee” (16 y.o. male, Kenya community D). Thus, a sense of responsibility and the allowance the sick role gives for otherwise censurable behaviour can motivate disclosure.

Finally, one further hypothetical scenario around disclosure, somewhat new in our field research, indicated a change in the wider community’s expectations around disclosure:

“

I: Is there anyone whom you do not want to know your HIV test results?

P: No. Everyone has to know because otherwise they will be shocked at my funeral. They will wonder why I kept it to myself.

”

22 y.o. female, Kenya community D

This final example, which was not unique, shows an antecedent concern around social expectations about the ‘sick role’: notifying others of potential outcomes. Importantly, this example shows HIV is no longer a sickness to be hidden, even though it may still challenge disclosure.

In the antecedent phase, our participants planned disclosure targets not simply according to who might help them adhere but also in view of affective ties, social obligations, and cultural expectations – including discerning between those who simply acted a social role and those who authentically related to the participant.

Planned and unplanned communication strategies (Disclosure events)

Disclosure events could be planned or spontaneous, and AYAH deployed culturally-informed communication strategies accordingly.

Planned disclosure events evinced two different patterns

The first invoked existing relationship expectations, as when participants let siblings know the outcome of a clinic visit after heeding their advice; only young adult men and women mentioned this sense of obligation. The second, more often seen with romantic relationships, involved participants disclosing to prompt the partner to also disclose. In both cases, antecedent preparation often overlapped with these events.

When counsellors encouraged disclosure, they sometimes helped young adults broker disclosure to romantic partners. Disclosure via partner testing was a regular tactic employed with the cooperation of nurses and providers.

“I talked to the counsellor to help me < disclose to my boyfriend>, and we agreed that she should come back and test me at home so that we get tested as a couple because my partner is also afraid of getting tested and could not even stand the sight of a provider. He came in while the provider was at my house, and because I was going to see my mother, I told the provider to test him first and that she will test me later; he tested positive and was linked into care.”

24 y.o. female, Kenya community D

In addition to planning with the counsellor, this participant also leveraged social expectations around filial piety to get her partner to test first. While oftentimes men seemed reluctant to consent to women’s requests to test together, in at least one instance a woman invoked paternal responsibility for an expected child to get her partner to test: “I stopped taking drugs when I got married because there was no way I could disclose to my partner. … I wanted <the nurse’s > help because I did not want my partner to know it first. … When I got back <from the clinic>, I told him – because I was also pregnant – I told him that the doctor had said that for me to start my ANC clinic, we had to be seen as a couple in the hospital for an HIV test. Therefore, when we came, we were tested together. Then we all turned positive.”

22 female, Kenya community G

This example, however, raises two important points around disclosure: the fear that discovering ARVs will reveal one’s status to a spouse, and the relation of childcare to HIV disclosure. Both elements reappeared in other participants’ accounts. One woman who gave birth was instructed by the provider to give her child HIV prevention syrup until the child was old enough to be brought back for testing, which prompted her to disclose her status to the child’s father. Only women mentioned childcare as a reason to disclose; however, seeing a husband openly giving his own child ART created space for mutual disclosure between one couple, suggesting men may use this tactic as well:

“At first, it was very difficult because I did not know his status yet (laughing), so when we started to stay together, I was still hiding it from him. … I had observed him for around one week because when you are new somewhere you need to humble yourself and be very observant. Every day at 8pm, he would call his child <from a prior marriage > to take drugs. I got curious because I used to take mine at that time too, and I had also seen him take some medicine. I gathered the courage to ask him, ‘Why does this child take these drugs every day?’ but he was not open with me. Then I just told him to be free and open with me because I had seen him as well when he was using his medication. That is when he became open and free. Then we revealed to each other how it all began and what happened.”

23 y.o. female, Kenya community D

These cases show that framing disclosure within the context of childcare is one strategy several women – and potentially at least one man – employed. An antecedent strategy is also evident in this case, namely, humbling oneself and being very observant to gauge both timing and approach for disclosure.

Medication as an eliciting factor shows the intimate but separable relationship between HIV and treatment disclosure. In fact, when one person was open about their medication, it could prompt disclosure to seek advice about side effects:

“When I left Kampala, I started staying in <my sister-in-law’s > home. With time, I realized she was also on HIV medication – remember I had come with my drugs also from Kampala, and so I would feel drowsy after taking my pill. At some point, I thought of asking my sister-in-law if she also experiences side effects … for she would not hide her drugs; she would keep her drugs in the open, and she would take her daily pill while I was looking. … So, with time I composed myself and told her how I had tested HIV positive. … She really got concerned, and immediately started caring for me. I was taking a daily pill at 8:00pm, but she would make sure to get me something to eat first.”

23 y.o. female, Uganda community A

In this case, seeing someone else take medication not only led the participant to disclose her treatment status but also prompted her sister-in-law to show how to adhere well by having something to eat with the medication to avoid side effects.

Framing disclosure in the context of treatment was also a strategy used by men. One school-going adolescent let his teacher know that he could not always make assembly on time because that was his pill time, while a young woman gave an extended example of a disclosure conversation with her husband:

“One day, we stayed up very late, and he told me to wait for him so that we could go together … I just saw him moving towards his coat and picked something from it like a bottle, but I did not ask him what it was. I wondered if he was also on HIV care, but I did not ask him as we went to the beach. When I came back some other day, he told me that ‘there is something I asked you about and although you answered me, I did not give you an answer’. I asked him what it was. Then he said, ‘I told you nowadays we have so many diseases which make people take drugs; did I give you an answer?’ I told him that he did not. He told me that, ‘I did not answer you with a reason. I did not answer you because I am also in the same situation, so do not be afraid to stay with me because I was also in need of someone like you since most of those who are negative are boastful and might lie to you that they are together with you but later run away.’ I just told him it was okay, and so since then, he is just free with me.”

23 y.o. female, Kenya community B

In these examples, treatment disclosure provided an oblique form of HIV status disclosure. These examples further highlight how social contexts inform both the manner of disclosure and the chosen disclosure target. The ART itself, evidence of treatment, can also be a means of both passive and active disclosure, a tactic used by both men and women.

“There’s one night I wanted to take my pills, I asked <my husband> to hold for me the lantern as I was looking for where I keep my drugs. Surprisingly, he did not even ask me what kind of drugs I was taking. With time, I also realised that he was taking bigger-sized pills than mine. That’s how my husband also got to know that I was HIV positive like him and in care. He therefore started to remind me of my return date to the clinic for refill.”

21 y.o. female, Uganda community C

“There is a woman I seduced after my wife left, and the first question she asked me was about my status. I first challenged her by hiding my true status from her, but she immediately challenged me further when she walked into her bedroom – this happened at her house – she walked into the bedroom and brought out her ARVs. She then told me that even if I want to have sex with her, I should be aware of that.”

22 y.o. male, Kenya community B

In these scenarios, no words needed be spoken; a visual display was sufficient to indicate status and allow the other person to deduce the situation.

Spontaneous disclosure

While disclosure can follow prior planning, youth also spontaneously disclose their status. Spontaneous disclosure followed similar patterns to planned disclosure to friends and family, though often entailing a stronger sense of reciprocity.

“I have disclosed to one of my friends who is also HIV positive, though I did not know she was equally infected when I started disclosing. She encouraged me that nothing was going to happen to me and everything would be okay. She is the only one I disclosed to. We share everything with her. … When I told her what had happened she also disclosed her status to me. … I chose to disclose to her because it is good to share issues with people who you are close to and would understand your situation. They can also be able to share their experiences with you and you can also tell them what you have learnt.”

20 y.o. female, Kenya community G

“It’s only one of <my co-workers > who is my close friend that knows about my status. … she was planning to get married to my Uncle. She therefore tested for HIV before they could get engaged; that’s when she was found positive. She came to me seeking advice on what to do. That’s when I also opened up to her.”

21 y.o. female, Uganda community F

In these examples, spontaneous disclosure reveals the dynamics of reciprocity in friendships. A key criterion for discerning the disclosure target was potential seroconcordance; the rules for communication then shift from mere response to a sharing of experiences.

Spontaneous disclosure was also prompted by neighbours enquiring about clinic attendance or loved ones asking the participant what was bothering them, both situations potentially showing a sense of concern for the participant’s well-being. Only young adults mentioned this pattern of disclosing to loved ones, perhaps because most adolescents acquired HIV perinatally – and so their spontaneous disclosure was associated with neighbours who saw them attending clinic alone.

Another form of spontaneous disclosure arose when someone discovered the participant’s medication. In these situations, participants also paired the unplanned disclosure communication with related outcomes that similarly affirmed social roles and relationships and the initiation of HIV care.

“Now back to how my current wife knew about my status; after the ex-wife disappeared again, I one day had some spare time to rest alone at my ex’s house when suddenly my current wife showed up. She started sweeping the house, something she had never done before, and she did not give me time to collect those drugs from where I had been hiding them … the truth be told, she found those drugs ……(silence) ….she shed tears at that time. At that point, I gained the courage to disclose to her, telling her that ‘these are things that happen to any human and they are like accidents; sorry I have never disclosed to you that I am HIV positive and taking ARVs. I urge you not to leave but be there for me.’ For close to a week, she could not talk to me, but she would cook and make sure that I ate well every day. I took her to my parents and brothers to keep peace between us even though none of them knew about my status, and lucky enough, she only said that she was angry because of the ex-wife who showed up for a week and left again. When we came back to our house, we talked as a couple and am happy we are living together to date.”

22 y.o. male, Kenya community B

Disclosure events were marked by both planned and spontaneous contexts. In planned contexts, childcare and family life were salient frames people used to disclose to others, while other contexts were purposefully set up by exposing medications to view – medications whose use is commonly known in the community. In spontaneous contexts, reciprocity and responding to another’s concern seemed paramount.

Re-inscribing social roles (Outcomes)

Disclosure outcomes can encourage or dissuade people from disclosing to others going forward. Like events, outcomes were more often described by older youth. For those still in the home, status disclosure and treatment disclosure were often linked:

“I just told him, ‘Dad, I went to the hospital. I was tested for HIV, and the result came back positive; hence, I was told to take medication.’ Then he said, ‘No problem, my daughter, just take them for you to stay alive.’ … You know that always you have to be free with your parents.”

19 y.o. female, Kenya community D

“I disclosed to <my father> when I ran out of pills and told him that I was tested positive … and I had started HIV treatment. I told him that the providers had also given me a card to enable me to receive HIV treatment at any facility. He offered to take me to the hospital and help me talk to the providers, who did not have any issues when giving me the medication.”

21 y.o. male, Kenya community G

Both examples link antecedent motivations around staying in care with a sense of filial piety. The children disclosed their treatment status, and their fathers expressed understanding and support, affirming preexisting relations of care. Yet some familial responses also come with a hint of avoiding stigma, as one youth’s grandparents, “always advise me to stick to my meds so that my situation does not get worse and I embarrass them.” 23 y.o. female, Uganda community E.

Outside the family, by far the most common outcome was advice to adhere to medication in order to stay alive. Disclosure allowed others the opportunity to also disclose and become exemplars of ART efficacy:

“A friend of mine, we went to school together and grew up together … I hear she was born with the infection, I don’t know how true that is. … I told her, and she told me there is no problem with that; the important thing was for me to start ART. She asked me, ‘Do you see any problem with me?’ … The important thing was for me to start taking my meds in time and avoid negative thoughts. When I went home, I also told my mother …”

2 y.o. female, Uganda community F

Positive outcomes bring people and different disclosure events into relation with one another, as when this participant explicitly associated her initial disclosure to a friend to the later disclosure to her mother. In other instances, participants discovered either ‘guides to living positively’, companions to attend clinic or provide medication reminders.

As in the case above where a spouse cleaned the house and came across her husband’s medication, both wives and husbands sometimes expressed upset at discovering a spouse’s status:

“On the day <my wife> went to the health facility she was referred to, she was given the same drugs again, and she was told she was found HIV positive, and that’s when she told me the truth how she was found positive. I really felt bad, I had a lot of thoughts, we actually got some misunderstandings. I also thought of telling her mother, but when I met with the mother, she advised me to keep calm and mind about our family, she advised me to also go and get tested. Indeed, I accepted to get tested and I was also found HIV positive, and I was started on medication as well.”

19 y.o. male, Uganda community F

In the earlier example of cleaning house, disclosure was tacitly involuntary, but the man framed his status in the context of their spousal relationship. The wife confirmed this ongoing relationship not only by her behaviour in continuing to cook for her husband, but also in maintaining his confidentiality when at her matrimonial home. The ultimate outcome was a frank discussion “as a couple” which seems to have strengthened the relationship. The second case also illustrates the role that in-laws have in brokering peace between the couple, and indeed the outcome of his wife’s disclosure was his own HIV test and enrolment in care.

Although the above examples hint at a degree of acrimony which was overcome, some disclosure events followed from more extreme negative experiences with a partner, particularly among women. One of several women explained, “I tested HIV positive while I was pregnant. When I went back home and disclosed my results to my husband, he chased me away, and since then, I have never gone back. … And so when I went to my Uncle’s home, I told my Uncle’s wife about everything” (23 y.o. female, Uganda community A). Despite having been chased away by their husbands, such negative experiences seem not to have affected participants’ willingness to disclose to other family members, highlighting the differences in expected potential outcomes from different target types.

Positive outcome responses could lend encouragement for disclosure to other target types and reaffirmed relational ties. Negative reactions demonstrated that people differentiated expected social roles and reactions in such a way that negative disclosure experiences with one type of target (e.g. spouse) need not be expected from other target types (biological family, friends).

Brief notes on non-disclosure

While avoidant rationales are not the focus of this paper, to indicate the tenor of avoidant motivations beyond anticipated stigma, one participant explained:

“You see, for a person like Mother, when you look at her age, she is no longer scared of anything in the world and no longer fears being embarrassed among people. … She boldly mentions that she is HIV positive, and she is taking her ARVs. But I cannot compare my age with that of my mother. I still have that fear of being embarrassed if people get to know my HIV status. … I do not want some people to know my HIV status – <except> of course, for a few health workers. You cannot compare my level of wisdom.”

18 y.o female, Uganda community C

The above participant recognizes her difference in communication boundaries, noting embarrassment at disclosing something considered private knowledge compared to her mother’s wisdom in negotiating the distinctions between what warrants openness and what does not. Nevertheless, HIV is still viewed as something relatively private by both older and younger youth, which requires a certain consciousness around disclosure. As one young man said, “You cannot just trust everyone, you tell one person and the next day everyone gets to know”

(16 y.o. male, Uganda community A).

Finally, some adolescents didn’t disclose because they were already encountering not stigma, but encouragement –from people to whom they had never disclosed.

“I: How do you know that the people in the village are aware of your HIV status?

P: Sometimes people say it to my face. … They always advise me to stick to my meds because my life depends on them. But I also keep wondering how a person like that got to know that I am infected with HIV. It keeps me wondering.

I: Are there people that may give negative remarks?

P: No, I have not encountered those ones, most people treat me well.”

15 y.o. male, Uganda community A

As we note below, this is a new finding; in prior studies of this area, stigma was quite pronounced. In part, it may be the result of people being more open with others, as the same adolescent elaborated:

“Almost all people know my status; my mother told our neighbours and all those people in the trading centre. … Her explanation was that if many people get to know, then I would get a chance to live longer – that I would not feel stigmatised.”

15 y.o. male, Uganda community A

Recognition that silence does not overcome stigma and the resulting transparency about one’s status has shifted the boundaries around communication expectations in favour of fostering life and longevity.

Discussion

In this qualitative longitudinal study of HIV disclosure among AYAH in rural Kenya and Uganda, where participants shared their experiences from antecedent through outcome phases, we found that disclosure was not necessarily about disclosure of HIV status as such but was often linked with disclosure of treatment status. When participants simply disclosed HIV status, they did so early, to seek advice on what to do next. When participants disclosed treatment status, which implicitly discloses their HIV diagnosis, they often did so to take their medications freely, to receive support in ART or clinic adherence, or because of felt cultural obligations. This study’s findings therefore extend previous research findings on HIV disclosure to suggest that treatment disclosure may be more efficacious in increasing support for treatment engagement and ART adherence than HIV status disclosure alone.

Adolescents offered more hypothetical examples of disclosure, generally relating to romantic relationships and opportunistic infections, while young adults gave concrete experiences with these situations. Male-female differences were more apparent with respect to romantic relationships than with friendships or parent-child relationships, wherein men would disclose late in a relationship (even after marriage) or when looking for a partner of the same HIV status, and women would disclose early in a relationship, similar to prior findings (Maeri et al., 2016; Okorie et al., 2023). For both men and women, true friends were highly valued for their confidence and counsel, and both men and women evinced a noticeable degree of filial piety in disclosing to their parents. Differences between Kenya and Uganda seemed slight, with Ugandan participants indicating somewhat more examples of negative reactions from spouses than Kenyan participants. However, in both locales, stigma against people with HIV appears less than in previous studies, though it persists (Akatukwasa et al., 2021; Camlin et al., 2020; Maeri et al., 2016).

We confirm the findings of both Li and Dima that cultural values and constraints inform communication with different targets (or, ‘confidants’) (Dima et al., 2014; Li et al., 2017). (See Fig. 2). As reported by Chaudoir and Fisher, we found that participants disclose to deepen an already important relationship or to people of similar serostatus (Chaudoir et al., 2011). These relationships are characterized by social reciprocity and expectations in the context of family and friendship, which almost by definition pre-exist the disclosure event. Disclosure either adds depth or reconfigures roles (and their attendant rules) within these relations. (Bond, 2010; Wadja, 2023). Unsurprisingly, therefore, the way participants disclosed to members of different target groups varied, with certain non-exclusive patterns pertaining to each.

Disclosure Process and Components: Findings from SEARCH-Youth study.

Disclosure targets included family (parents, siblings, aunts and uncles, grandparents, in-laws, co-wives), spouses and sexual partners (particularly fathers of children), friends both seroconcordant and serodiscordant, professional relations (teachers), and the general community (neighbours and clinic-mates). Several participants mentioned they had parents living with HIV who had disclosed to them. Thus, similar to Wadja’s findings from Cˆote d’Ivoire and Siu’s work in Uganda, we found disclosure is often better framed through relationship types than as singular events (Siu et al., 2012; Wadja, 2023). Within these types we found several differences in communication rules and expectations. Partner communication seemed mostly likely to be planned, and the boundaries between partners appeared higher than among friends. This was especially the case before a romantic partner became an official spouse. Once disclosed to, the partnership often formed its own boundary of confidentiality and decision-making around HIV and treatment disclosure. Like other researchers, our sample demonstrated that simply revealing the bottle of medication was itself a common contextual cue for disclosing to partners (Maccarthy et al., 2018).

Disclosure events with friends seemed more often spontaneous, since the social expectations of friends entailed sharing one another’s challenges and seeking advice as expressions of closeness. Spontaneity seemed more common in our group than in other regions like Senegal (Laborde-Balen et al., 2023). Framing disclosure varied by whether the friend was seroconcordant or serodiscordant. In cases of seroconcordance, disclosure was often motivated by a desire for advice on how to adhere to medications, avoid side effects, or arrive at self-acceptance. In cases of serodiscordance, support and advice was also sought, but more often around initiation in care. In this case, disclosure was about HIV status more than treatment status. For those related to the participants by school expectations, disclosure was framed as being conscientious in informing supervisors why participants would be late to expected events, with the expected response that supervisors would validate the priorities of caring for one’s health.

Antecedent planning or prior disclosure experience may facilitate spontaneity, but this was not consistently marked in our dataset; rather, a sense of reciprocity was more evident. In terms of outcomes, previous negative experiences with spouses did not seem to affect our participants’ willingness to disclose to other target types, particularly family members or friends, in contrast to other studies (Polejack et al., 2020). This is perhaps explained by participants distinguishing between disclosure in different social spheres, as noted by prior studies in Zambia (Bond, 2010).

Limitations:

For the individual cases summarised in this paper, disentangling the effect of our youth intervention from the effects our adult interventions had on stigma reduction in the surrounding communities is complex. This is even more the case when we recall that the adolescents also went through developmental changes in the context of an intervention with life-stage approaches to counselling. Thus, while this paper did not intend to make a clear comparison over time, we can make some comparisons to changes from when we first entered these communities. Earlier research identified a high degree of stigma across the spectrum of internalized, anticipated, perceived, and enacted stigma already present in the communities where we worked, especially among adults (Akatukwasa et al., 2021; Maeri et al., 2016). These findings allowed us to articulate intervention pathways for stigma reduction (Camlin et al., 2017, 2020). We also identified that for youth specifically, having a peer who was open about their HIV status and medication use helped them initiate ART (Brown et al., 2020).

We incorporated all these findings into our work in the region, both with youth and adults. The resulting trends based on the presence of our study in these communities already saw a reduction in stigma as treatment (ART) and treatment literacy among both PLHIV and people without HIV increased, similar to findings elsewhere in the region (Whyte, 2015). Increasing openness by people on ART, who additionally brought others into HIV care, may have also fostered these trends (Camlin et al., 2017; Johnson-Peretz et al., 2022). Our initial intervention trials did well, but not as well among youth. Our youth intervention, therefore, also specifically included both life-stage and peer counselling components. This meant that participants had someone openly living with HIV to confide in. Additionally, these peer counsellors, as people who had already gone through the disclosure process, could help guide the youth not only as the youth passed through different life stages, but also as events in their lives intersected with changes in attitudes in the community around them. The network of providers, counsellors, and participants were all potentially attuned to these changes. Disclosure is rarely separate from the dynamic nature of social life, and, in fact, participates intimately in it.

This paper has several additional limitations. We did not detail how youth prioritise one target group over another, as our purpose was to uncover the variety of disclosure motivations among youth. We did not examine the relation of having one’s own status disclosed to self and how this affects disclosure to others. Nor did we look at motivations for youth to disclose other people’s status; that pertains more to a study of involuntary disclosure, stigma, and its social uses in adolescent peer groups. Relatedly, those youth who had poor outcomes may have left the study and were therefore not interviewed. Additionally, although we have evidence of non-disclosure after marriage – some in our cohort seroconverted after marriage – our focus was on active disclosure, not resistance to disclosing (Johnson-Peretz et al., 2024). This highlights that while our findings broadly trace motivational factors for disclosure, they are not universally applicable even in a single region, and those not in treatment obviously may be even more reluctant to disclose their (non-) treatment status in situations where U=U (undetectable = untransmissible) is widely known. Finally, this study is limited to communities in rural Kenya and Uganda and may not be transferable to populations in urban areas, nor to the experiences of young people from other geographic and cultural contexts. The population may also be unique in that they have been exposed to the intervention and community literacy activities through associated studies. However, the dynamics of family, friendship, and romantic partnerships are near universal among humans, and our findings should be broadly applicable in such contexts.

Future directions:

Our findings raise several new questions. Comparing how perinatally infected youth disclose with how perinatally infected youth were disclosed to (how they found out about their own status) may help answer questions around whether youth learn a particular way to disclose from the way they themselves were told about their diagnosis. A second question is whether the dynamic of counsellors suggesting disclosure percolates over time, so that a participant comes back to it later to request support in disclosing to someone, thus facilitating movement from pre-contemplation to contemplation to the action of disclosure, per stages of change theory (Prochaska et al., 2008). A third area concerns the observation that some participants viewed communication around bringing medicines into the house as a necessity. The links between the anthropological categories of home, medicine, fetish, and power – as they apply to ARVs – warrants further exploration from a social science perspective, particularly as injectable prevention and treatment options become available, displacing the centrality of tablets as creators of disclosure contexts.

Recommendations and Conclusions:

HIV status and treatment disclosure are intimately related to pre-existing social values, roles, and rules which people take on and adapt according to specific cultural expectations. Counsellors can manage fear of stigma during the antecedent disclosure phase by highlighting the different social expectations associated with different target types. Providers can encourage AYAH to identify people who would understand their situation and the best way to communicate with them. Finally, since disclosure is not necessarily about disclosure of HIV status as such, but is often linked with disclosure of treatment status, encouraging treatment disclosure rather than HIV status alone may prove more efficacious in increasing treatment support and ART adherence.
