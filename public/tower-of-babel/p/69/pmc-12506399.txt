Unique genetic diversity of Propionibacterium acnes isolated from human facial skins in Thailand revealed by the multi-locus sequence typing scheme
Authors: Khemrutai Sripath, Jaruwan Bangperng, Pramote Chumnanpeun, Teerasak E-kobon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/objective(s)/introductionAcne vulgaris, a prevalent dermatological concern, has shown a consistent increase in reported cases from 2016 to 2019, according to data from the Institute of Dermatology, Thailand. This condition not only causes physical and psychological discomfort but also poses treatment challenges, including the potential development of antibiotic resistance. However, the precise role and distribution of Propionibacterium acnes, an acne-associated bacterium, remain unclear among the Thai population. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of different P. acnes groups in facial samples from Thai individuals.Materials and methodsFacial swabbing was performed to collect bacterial samples from facial skin of Thai individuals. The 16 S rRNA gene was analyzed for species identification. Background

Acne vulgaris, or acne, is a skin disorder that affects the pilosebaceous unit and hair follicles, most commonly appearing on the face, neck, upper chest, shoulders, and back [1–3]. The formation of acne is influenced by four major factors. First, increased sebum production in the sebaceous glands. Second, the accumulation of keratinized cells in the epidermis, which leads to obstruction of hair follicles [2, 4, 5]. Third, the lipid-rich environment within the follicles supports the growth of Propionibacterium acnes, which utilizes lipids as an energy source by hydrolysing sebum triglycerides through triacylglycerol lipase and lysophospholipase enzymes [6, 7]. Forth, the inflammatory response contributes significantly to acne severity [8]. Acne affects up to 85% of adolescents globally [9], and its incidence has risen markedly, with a 48% increase in reported cases in 2019 compared to 1990 levels [10]. Beyond physical symptoms, acne has been linked to increased rates of depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation, and it significantly reduces the social engagement and quality of life among affected individuals [11].

Propionibacterium acnes (P. acnes), recently reclassified as Cutibacterium acnes (C. acnes) based on biochemical and genomic analyses, has been more precisely characterized in terms of its phylogenetic classification and adaptation to human skin [7, 12]. The newly assigned genus Cutibacterium is now used to classify cutaneous species. This bacterial group possesses specific genes, including lipase genes involved in sebum lipid degradation, which are uniquely present in skin-associated species and absent in environmental Propionibacterium species [12]. P. acnes is a Gram-positive, anaerobic, slow-growing, rod-shaped, non-spore-forming bacterium [13]. It can be isolated from acne lesions and plays a role in acne pathogenesis by inducing skin inflammation [14]. In vitro immunological studies have shown that P. acnes exhibits immunostimulatory activity, promoting the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, and activating T-cells [15].

The multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) technique was developed in conjunction with the PubMLST database (http://pubmlst.org/) and released in 2005 [16]. The MLST scheme classifies P. acnes isolates into five phylogenetic groups: IA, IB, IC, II, and III, with group IA further subdivided into IA1 and IA2 [17]. Different P. acnes phylotypes are thought to originate from distinct sources and exhibit varying pathogenic potential. These phylotypic groups have been associated with specific pathological conditions [18–20]. Type I P. acnes has shown a stronger association with acne vulgaris compared to Type II [21, 22]. Within Type I, the IA1 and IC phylotypes are more commonly linked to acne than the IB phylotype [17, 23, 24]. Additionally, IA1 has also been associated with periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) [25]. Phylotypes IB and II have been implicated in various infections involving medical devices, the prostate, urinary tract, bloodstream, soft tissues, and orthopedic implants [17, 26, 27]. In contrast, Type III is not associated with acne but has been linked to ther conditions, such as spinal disc infection [28] and progressive macular hypomelanosis (PMH) [29].

However, limited research has been conducted on the genetic diversity of this bacterium in Asian countries, especially in Thailand. Therefore, examining the genetic diversity of P. acnes in samples from the Thai population may offer valuable insights into the predominant strains compared to those found in other regions. Moreover, these findings could support future medical research and drug development aimed at providing effective and targeted acne treatments for the Thai population, particularly as a model for application in other countries.

Methods

Sample collection, bacterial culture, and preliminary identification of propionibacterium acnes samples

Samples were collected from 110 Thai adolescents aged 15–19 years using the facial swab technique. Participants had various skin types—oily, dry, and combination—and included individuals both with and without acne. Swabbing was performed across the entire face, with a focus on areas around acne lesions. Information regarding medical conditions, allergies, and acne treatment history was also recorded. The samples were cultured on Brain Heart Infusion Agar (BHIA) and incubated under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for seven days. Preliminary identification of P. acnes isolates was based on morphological characteristics, including colony appearance (white to yellowish coloration with circular shape), Gram staining, and microscopic observation of bacterial cell structure (Gram-positive, short rods). Pure bacterial colonies were selected for further molecular analysis to confirm the identity of P. acnes.

Bacterial DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified protocol of the GF-1 Bacterial DNA Extraction Kit (Vivantis, Malaysia). Modification included adjustments to the volumes and incubation times of lysozyme and proteinase K: 40 µL of each enzyme was used, with lysozyme incubation for 1 h and proteinase K incubation for 2 h.

Identification of Propionibacterium acnes from the cultured isolates

Identification of P. acnes was performed using PCR with primers specific to the P. acnes 16S rRNA genes [30]. Two sets of primers were employed: the first set included PAS9 (forward: 5’-CCCTGCTTTTGTGGGGTG-3’) and PAS11 (reverse: 5’-CGACCCCAAAAGAGGGAC-3’), producing a 946 bp fragment. This product was then used as the template in a second (nested) PCR with PAS10 (reverse: 5’- CGCCTGTGACGAAGCGTG-3’) and PAS11, yielding 573 bp fragment. The nested PCR conditions for P. acnes identification included an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 25 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 15 s, annealing at 57 °C for 20 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Subsequently, universal 16 S rRNA primers (forward: 5’-AGAGTTTGATYMTGGC-3’ and reverse: 5’-GYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3’) were employed to amplify a ~ 1,500 bp of the 16 S rRNA gene. The PCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 45 s, annealing at 50 °C for 45 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1.35 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were purified using the TIANgel Mini DNA Purification Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The purified 16 S rRNA gene fragments were then sequenced via Sanger sequencing (Macrogen, Inc., Seoul, South Korea). Resulting sequences were edited and assembled into consensus sequences using BioEdit software version 7.0.5.3 [31]. Low-quality regions were trimmed from the raw sequences, followed by the concatenation of forward and reverse reads to generate consensus sequences. Species identification was performed using nucleotide BLAST [32] with classification confirmed by high BLASTN scores, query coverage, and sequence identity values equal to or exceeding 95%. A total of 65 P. acnes isolates were confirmed and designated JB1-JB65. Phylogenetic analysis of the 16 S rRNA gene was performed using a series of steps. All sequences were then subjected to multiple sequence alignment. The alignment was subsequently trimmed to remove gaps and standardize sequence lengths across all samples. A final aligned sequence length of 1,362 base pairs was obtained and used for phylogenetic tree construction. A maximum likelihood tree was constructed using the Kimura-2-parameter model in MEGA X program version 10.2.1, with bootstrap resampling performed for 1,000 iterations [33]. The tree was rooted using the 16 S rRNA gene sequence of Bacillus cereus strain ATCC 14,579 (NR_074540.1).

Classification of Propionibacterium acnes isolates using multi-locus sequence typing (MLST)

The P. acnes isolates JB1-JB65 were classified using the MLST8 scheme, which includes eight housekeeping gene loci: aroE, atpD, camp2, gmk, guaA, lepA, sodA, and tly [17]. Primer pairs and PCR conditions for these loci are listed in Table 1. All genes were amplified under identical PCR conditions, except for gene-specific annealing temperatures. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, anealing at a gene-specific temperature for 1 min, extension at 72 °C for 2 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The annealing temperatures were as follows: 58 °C for aroE, atpD, lepA, and tly; 56.9 °C for guaA; 55.7 °C for sodA; 56.7 °C for gmK; and 64.7 °C for camp2. PCR products were purified using the TIANgel Mini DNA Purification Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). Purified products were subjected to Sanger sequencing (Macrogen, Seoul, South Korea). Sequence data were trimmed, aligned, and assembled into consensus sequences using BioEdit software version 7.0.5.3 [31]. The sequences of eight housekeeping genes were analyzed using the PubMLST database (http://pubmlst.org/pacnes) [34]. Each allele was assigned an allelic number, and each isolate was designated a sequence type (ST) based on its allelic profile. Clonal complexes (CCs) and phylogroups were predicted according to the most closely matched profiles in the PubMLST database. Phylotypes were determined based on the most probable assignments from partially matched allelic profiles. Concatenated sequences of the eight MLST genes from 65 isolates were used to construct a phylogenetic tree, incorporating an additional 229 P. acnes MLST profiles from the PubMLST database (see Supplementary Table S1). Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using the maximum likelihood method with the Kimura-2-parameter model in MEGA X version 10.2.1, with 1,000 bootstrap replications. The tree was rooted using concatenated sequences of eight genes (aroE, atpD, crp, gmk, guaA, lepA, sodA, and pldA) from Escherichia coli strain 97-3250 (NZ_CP027599) [35].

Minimum spanning tree analysis for a comprehensive Understanding of the P. acnes population structure

The MLST profile and auxiliary data of the 65 P. acnes isolates obtained in this study were combined with data from 276 additional isolates retrieved from the PubMLST database [34] (accessed on 27 October 2021). The MLST profiles included allele numbers for the eight housekeeping genes, while the auxiliary profiles comprised various epidemiological attributes, such as isolate identifiers, countries, continents, subspecies, regions, sources, sequence types (STs), and clonal complexes (CCs). These datasets were analyzed using the PHYLOViZ online tool [36]. In the minimum spanning tree (MST) analysis, tree node colors were assigned based on auxiliary metadata, with emphasis on phylotype and country of origin. The threshold for edge formation in the MST was set to 8, and the N locus variant (NLV) parameter was adjusted to zero. To further explore epidemiological patterns, data from this study and the PubMLST database—covering information on continent, country, source, and MLST profile—were integrated and visualized as bubbe plots. These plots were generated using R, specifically the ggplot2 package [37].

Results

Study subjects and characteristics of the bacterial isolates

This study involved facial swab sampling of 110 participants to isolate P. acnes. Of these, 65 P. acnes isolates were successfully obtained. Participant metadata, including skin type, number of acne lesions, and history of care and treatment, are summarized in Fig. 1. As shown in Fig. 1A, the majority of isolates were obtained from 16-year-old participants, followed by those aged 17 and 15. A large proportion of isolates were from individuals with oily skin. Sixteen isolates were derived from participants with a family history of acne, while 29 of the 65 participants had previously received acne treatment—five of whom reported using clindamycin. Figure 1B presents additional characteristics: thirteen participants had more than ten acne lesions, whereas thirty-five had fewer than ten. Furthermore, thirteen individuals reported frequent acne occurrences, while forty participants experienced acne only occasionally.

Genetic relationship of Propionibacterium acnes isolates in Thailand represented based on 16 s rRNA phylogenetics

The 16 S rRNA gene (~ 1,500 base pairs) was successfully amplified from all 68 P. acnes isolates. BLASTN analysis confirmed genus- and species-level identification for 65 of the 68 isolates as either Propionibacterium acnes or Cutibacterium acnes (Supplementary Table S1). However, the 16 S rRNA gene-based phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2) was unable to resolve distinct P. acnes phylogroups. Specifically, the 65 Thai isolates did not cluster into well-defined phylotypes (IA1, IA2, and IB), as shown in Fig. 2A. Likewise, comparison with 24 additional P. acnes 16 S rRNA gene sequences from the NCBI database (Supplementary Table S1) revealed similar limitations in differentiating phylogroups, as illustrated in Fig. 2B.

Classification of P. acnes isolates and genetic relatedness based on MLST analysis

The multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) method is widely employed in epidemiological studies to investigate the genetic structure of bacterial populations. In this study, the MLST8 scheme developed by McDowell in 2013 was applied to determine the phylogenetic grouping of 65 P. acnes isolates from Thailand. This approach involved partial sequencing of eight housekeeping genes: aroE (762 bp), atpD (1,261 bp), camp2 (1,015 bp), guaA (1,384 bp), gmk (444 bp), lepA (1,218), sodA (488 bp), and tly (909 bp). Sequence types (ST), clonal complex (CC), and phylogroups were assigned based on allelic profiles using data from the PubMLST database (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3). All isolates exhibited allelic variation across the eight loci, indicating the presence of novel allele sequences and suggesting potentially new ST (Fig. 3C). Despite the observed diversity, several isolates—such as JB9, JB12, JB13, JB14, JB16, JB17, JB18, JB33, and JB62—shared identical allelic profiles that did not correspond to any existing entries in the PubMLST database. Specifically, in these nine isolates, one locus (lepA) showed a shift in allele number compared to the predicted ST36: from allele 1 (ST115) to allele 11 in our samples. Similar differences were observed in other isolates, including JB8, JB29, JB30, and JB46 (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Tables S3). These findings suggested that all 65 isolates represent newly identified STs not previously documented in the database. Although some isolates showed partial similarity to known STs (“nearest matched STs”), none had an exact allelic match. Furthermore, novel nucleotide variations were detected in several loci when compared to the nearest STs, further supporting the identification of new allele sequences (Supplementary Table S3).

Analysis of the 65 P. acnes isolates and their nearest-matching clonal complexes (CCs) revealed that the majority (53.8%) belonged to CC2 (phylotype IA2), followed by CC3 (7.7%, IA1), singleton types (7.7%, IA2), CC5 (7.7%, IB), and CC1 (4.6%, IA1). Additionally, 18.5% of isolates were associated with more than one nearest-matched CC (Fig. 3A). Regarding sequence types (STs), ST36 was the most prevalent among the Thai isolates (44.6%), followed by ST22 (7.7%), ST53 (6.2%), and ST115 (6.2%). The remaining 30.8% of isolates were matched to more than one predicted ST (Fig. 3B). Overall, phylotype IA2 was the predominant group in this study (66.2%), followed by IA1 (24.6%) and IB (7.7%) (Fig. 3A and B, and 3C). No isolates belonging to types IC, II, or III were detected in the dataset.

MLST-based genetic relationship of P. acnes isolates

The application of the MLST scheme for phylogenetic reconstruction provided improved resolution in classifying the 65 P. acnes isolates compared to the 16 S rRNA-based phylogenetic trees (Fig. 4A and B). The MLST-based phylogenetic tree, which included the 65 Thai isolates and 229 reference isolates from the PubMLST database, revealed a clearer separation and closer genetic relationships among phylotypes. Notably phylotypes IA1 and IA2 showed greater similarity to each other and to type IB, while being more distantly related to types II and III (Fig. 4B). Isolate JB58 was predicted to match multiple closely related STs, including ST2, ST5, ST12, ST36, ST42, ST57, ST78, ST89, ST98, and ST111, most of which belong to CC2 (type IA2) and CC5 (type IB). Additionally, phylogenetic positioning suggested that JB41 should be classified as phylotype IA2, despite earlier ambiguity based on its allelic profile (Fig. 4A and B). Samples within the same phylotypes clustered closely together, and the Thai isolates formed distinct clades corresponding to IA1, IA2, and IB, clearly separated from international reference strains in the database. The finding indicates a unique genetic structure of P. acnes populations in Thailand. Interstingly, isolate JB37 was identified as phylotype IA2 based on its allelic profile, yet it grouped with IA1 in the MLST phylogenetic tree. This discrepancy between the allele-based and sequence-based classifications suggests the presence of subtle recombination events or allelic convergence that may influence phylogroup assignment.

Population structure of P. acnes isolates based on the MLST allelic profiles across phylotypes and demographic origins

The minimum spanning tree (MST) analysis provided a comprehensive overview of the population structure of P. acnes isolates, based on the MLST allelic profiles across different phylotypes and demographic backgrounds (Fig. 5A and B, and 5C). In the phylotype-based MST (Fig. 5A), phylogroup IA1 was the most prevalent among the P. acnes isolates, followed by IA2. Larger circles in the MST correspond to a higher number of isolates. Notably, Circle 1 represented Group 1—the largest group—with 56 isolates, followed by Groups 10, 6, 5 and 285, which included 39, 19, 12, and 9 isolates, respectively. The MSTs (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S4) illustrated clear phylotype-based clustering. Group 1 and its associated subgroups formed a large and interconnected cluster, representing the IA1 type of P. acnes. Similarly, Group 285 and its related clusters indicated the emergence of the IA2 type. The presence of types IB and II was evident in Groups 10 and 15, respectively, along with their connected groups. These results highlight the extensive genetic diversity and clonal expansion patterns of P. acnes, particularly within the IA1 and IA2 phylotypes, and reveal how these lineages are distributed across isolates from different demographic origins.

Regarding clonal complexes (CCs), CC1 (type IA1), CC4 (type IA1), and CC5 (type IB) emerged as the predominant groups in the current PubMLST database (Figs. 5B and 6). The dominant Group 1 and its associated subgroups premarily belonged to CC1 (type IA1), whereas Group 5 was predominantly composed of CC3 (type IA1). In Group 6, CC4 (type IA1) was the most frequent clonal complex, while Group 10 was largely represented by CC5 (type IB). Additionally, Group 285 conprised CC2 (type IA2) and a singleton isolate (type IA2) (Fig. 5B). According to available global data from the PubMLST databases, CC1 (type IA1), CC3 (type IA1), CC4 (type IA1), and CC5 (type IB) represent the most wildspread P. acnes lineages worldwide (Fig. 6). These major clonal complexes show distribution patterns across various regions and countries. Functionally, CC5 (type IB) was associated with diverse anatominal and clinical sources, including the prostate gland, eye, normal skin, acne lesions, and medical devices. Similarly, CC1 was linked to bone, skin, eye, acne, soft tissue, dental infections, and spinal discs. These findings suggest a high degree of ecological adaptability among P. acnes strains and reflect their involvement in both commensal and pathogenic roles (Fig. 6).

The PubMLST database includes P. acnes isolate data from countries across the globe, with predominant representation from Europe, followed by North America, Oceania, South America, and Asia (Fig. 5C). Within the Asian dataset, only two isolates from China were present in the current database. Thus, the inclusion of the Thai isolates presented in this study represents the first documented P. acnes collection isolate from the Southeast Asian region. The most interconnected group in the minimum spanning tree, Group 1, consisted of isolates from a wide range of countries, including Denmark, Hungary, Italy, the United Kingdom, the United States, and China. Similarly, Groups 5, 6, and 10 also encompassed isolates from multiple countries. However, a noticeable gap was observed in the representation of Asian isolates within these clusters (Fig. 5C). In our analysis, the Thai isolates (JB samples) were primarily assigned to Group 285, which exhibited links with isolates from France, the USA, Spain, Denmark, and Greece. Additionally, JB samples from Groups 91, 316, and 330 were connected to Group 3, which included isolates from France, Greece, USA, and Hungary (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, JB isolates assigned to Group 283, 279, 312, 317, and 325 (type IB) displayed connections with Group 10—a highly interconnected cluster linked to isolates from the UK, Hungary, Spain, Sweden, France, Australia, the USA, Denmark, Germany, and Italy (Fig. 5C). These findings underscore the extensive genetic exchange and global distribution of P. acnes lineages, while also highlighting the underrepresentation of Southeast Asian strains in current databases.

Discussion

This research successfully identified 65 strains of P. acnes from 110 Thai adolescent participants. The highest number of isolates was obtained from individuals with oily skin, whereas participants with dry and normal skin types also yielded P. acnes, though in lower numbers. The predominance of P. acnes in individuals with oily skin aligns with previous studies that have linked excess sebum production to increased P. acnes colonization and acne development [38, 39]. Another factor reported to influence acne prevalence is body mass. Several studies have suggested a correlation between high body mass index (BMI), overweight, or obesity and both the frequency and severity of acne vulgaris [40, 41]. However, in this study, no significant differences were observed in the number of acne lesions or the frequency of P. acnes isolation among individuals with varying BMI classifications. Most isolates were recovered from normal-weight participants, followed by underweight, overweight, and obese individuals. Approximately one-third of the participants had previously undergone acne treatments, primarily with antibiotics—most notably clindamycin. Some participants exhibited multiple acne lesions during the time of sampling. These findings present an opportunity for further investigation into clindamycin-resistant P. acnes isolates. Clindamycin and erythromycin remain among the most widely used atibiotics for acne treatment [42]. However, the emergence of macrolide-resistant P. acnes strains has become a growing global concern, with resistance rates increasing over time and across different regions [43–45]. A previous study in Thailand reported a tenfold increase in resistance to erythromycin and clindamycin among P. acnes isolates [46]. Since then, no subsequent studies have examined antimicrobial resistance in Thai P. acnes populations. Therefore, the isolates obtained from individuals with a history of clindamycin treatment in this study may serve as valuable resources for investigating the mechanisms underlying clindamycin resistance in P. acnes.

Analysis of 16 S rRNA gene sequences from 68 facial swab-derived isolates successfully identified 65 P. acnes isolates. However, three isolates were identified as Staphylococcus aureus, likely due to contamination during the bacterial isolation process. These S. aureus isolates were excluded from subsequent analyses. The limitations of 16 S rRNA gene-based phylogenetic analysis were evident in its inability to differentiate among type I P. acnes isolates. This shortcoming was addressed through the MLST analysis, which provided greater resolution. While 16 S rRNA gene sequencing is widely used for bacterial classification, it often lacks sufficient discriminatory power to distinguish closely related species or subspecies within certain genera, including P. acnes [47]. In this study, partial 16 S rRNA gene sequences—comprising over 1,200 base pairs—were used. Despite the relatively long read length, the analysis still exhibited poor resolution in discriminating P. acnes phylotypes. This limitation may be attributed to the use of single primer pairs and the inherent constraints of Sanger sequencing, which may have prevented the capture of key genetic variations [48]. These findings are consistent with the observations of Yarza et al. (2014), who reported that partial 16 S rRNA sequences are often insufficient for accurately estimating species richness and for robust taxonomic classification at higher levels [49].

The MLST analysis successfully categorized the P. acnes isolates from Thailand into seven sequence types (STs), five clonal complexes (CCs), and three phylogroups. However, no isolates were identified as belonging to phylogroups IC, II, and III. This absence suggests that strain-specific characteristics and anatomical niches may influence the distribution and prevalence of certain phylotypes. Phylotype IC, considered rare, is infrequently found on facial skin and has been more commonly associated with acne-prone individuals [18]. Type II P. acnes strains are generally linked with opportunistic and deep-tissue infections rather than surface-level colonization, distinguishing them from type I strains [50]. Interestingly, a study by Kwon et al. (2013) reported the presence of type II strains in both healthy and acne-associated skin, a finding that contrasts with the results of the present study. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in sampling techniques— specifically, facial swabbing versus acne lesion pressing—which could yield either aerotolerant surface-colonizing or deep-pore anaerobic isolates [23]. Type III P. acnes, on the other hand, has been predominantly identified in body regions such as the lower back, abdomen, oral cavity, and in cases of progressive macular hypomelanosis (PMH) [50]. The localized colonization patterns of type II and III strains may explain their absence in samples collected through facial swabbing. Based on our findings, phylotype IA2 was the most prevalent on facial skin, followed by IA1 and IB. The distinct allelic numbers and profiles of the Thai isolates were matched to their closest STs and CCs in the PubMLST database, indicating divergence from globally reported strains. For example, nine isolates (JB9, JB12, JB13, JB14, JB16, JB17, JB18, JB33, and JB62) closely resembled ST36 and CC2 (Type IA2), with a single locus difference in the lepA gene—where the allele changed from 1 to 11. This variation was caused by base substitutions at specific nucleotide positions: 8 A ->8G, 9 T ->9 A, 443 T ->443 A, and 444 T ->444 A. Other isolates exhibited single allelic variations across various loci, including guaA, gmk, aroE, lepA, sodA, and atpD, indicating the presence of potentially novel sequence types and clonal complexes unique to the Thai P. acnes population.

The results of this study revealed that the majority of P. acnes isolates were associated with CC2 (53.8%), representing phylotype IA2, indicating a high prevalence of this lineage among Thai adolescents. Other identified clonal complexes included CC3 (7.7%), singleton IA2 types (7.7%), CC5 (7.7%), and CC1 (4.8%). These findings diverge from previous reports, particularly regarding the dominant P. acnes lineages detected. For instance, the study by Dagnelie et al. (2018) analyzed P. acnes isolates from two cohorts—healthy individuals and acne patients (average age 25.2 years)—using facial swabbing [18]. The authors reported distinct differences between the two groups. In healthy individuals, the dominant clonal complexes were CC1, CC6, and CC72. Among acne patients, CC1 was predominant, comprising more than 58% of isolates, followed by 16% CC2 (IA2), and small proportions of CC4 (IA1), CC3 (IA1), CC6, and CC72 (type II). In contrast, the current study found only a single isolate of CC1 (IA1), which was surprising given its dominance in both healthy and acne-associated samples in the study of Dagnelie et al. [18]. Notably, CC2 (IA2) emerged as the most prevalent clonal complex in this Thai cohort—a finding that contrasts with prior studies identifying IA1 as the predominant phylotype in both healthy and acne-prone skin samples [51, 52]. Additionally, several clonal complexes reported in earlier research were not detected in the current study, including CC4 (IA1), CC6, CC72, and singleton type II isolates [18, 53]. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in geographical region, host genetics, or environmental factors influencing the skin microbiome. This regional variation is further supported by comparisons with studies by McDowell et al. (2012) and Fitz-Gibbon et al. (2013), both of which found IA1 lineages—particularly CC1, CC3, and ribotypes RT4/RT5—to be dominant in Western populations and closely associated with acne [17, 54]. In contrast, Fitz-Gibbon et al. also identified ribotype RT6, belonging to phylotype IA2, as being associated with healthy skin, which aligns with the predominance of IA2 in the present study. However, all isolates in this Thai cohort exhibited novel sequence types not previously reported in PubMLST, underscoring a potential region-specific diversification of P. acnes populations. The predominance of CC2 (IA2) among Thai facial skin samples suggests a region-specific adaptation or colonization. Further research into the local environmental conditions, host skin properties, and lifestyle factors may help elucidate the reasons behind the high prevalence of this clonal complex.

The current collection of P. acnes isolates in the PubMLST database offers valuable insight into the global distribution and emergence patterns of clonal complexes and phylotypes. The data indicate that certain lineages—particularly CC1 (type IA1), CC3 (type IA1), and CC30 (formerly CC72, type II)—are predominantly associated with acne lesions, normal skin, and facial skin. However, these isolates have thus far been reported primarily in European and North American populations. This study introduces a new perspective by presenting the first Thai P. acnes isolates, representing Southeast Asia—a region previously underrepresented in global databases. Expanding isolate collections to include samples from other Asian countries could help identify regional differences shaped by ethnicity, geography, and lifestyle. The distinct prevalence of P. acnes clonal complexes and phylotypes observed in this study may be influenced by several factors, including participant age, anatomical site of sample collection, nationality, and dietary habits. These variables are consistent with methodological and demographic differences reported in studies by Dagnelie et al. (2018), McDowell et al. (2011), and Bhate et al. (2014) [18, 51, 52]. Such differences may help explain the unique distribution patterns seen in the Thai samples and suggest that specific clonal complexes may have region-specific roles in acne development among Thai individuals.

This study focused on a single cohort of Thai individuals and revealed notable distinctions in P. acnes isolates compared to those reported in the PubMLST database and other international studies. These findings highlight that substantial genetic diversity within P. acnes and emphasize the need for further investigations across additional cohorts to explore inter-population similarities and differences. At present, there is a significant lack of data on P. acnes isolates from Asian countries. Participants in this study were not categorized by age group, as the cohort primarily consisted of teenagers—the age group with the highest expected prevalence of P. acnes. Consequently, this dataset does not support a direct assessment of age-related differences in P. acnes colonization or acne severity. Nonetheless, previous studies have consistently shown that P. acnes abundance and acne incidence decrease with age, with variations observed across different age groups and between sexes [55–57]. These trends suggest that age influence not only acne development but also broader skin characteristics, such as elasticity and wrinkle formation [56]. Expanding isolate collections across different age groups and regions could uncover genetic variations and lineage-specific traits. The observed variability among P. acnes clonal complexes—and their differing pathogenic potentials—remains an important area for future investigation. Additional studies are needed to identify the genetic and environmental factors contributing to the pathogenicity of specific lineages, particularly in the context of acne vulgaris. Although this study represents the first systematic collection of P. acnes isolates from Thai individuals, it lays the background for future research in microbial epidemiology, drug discovery, and molecular mechanisms underlying acne pathogenesis in the Thai population.

Conclusion

This study is the first to investigate the genetic characteristics of P. acnes in Thai individuals using the multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) method. The analysis revealed notable difference in clonal complexes and phylotypes compared to previous reports and similar studies conducted in other populations. The findings suggest that P. acnes from clonal complex 2 (CC2), corresponding to phylotype IA2, is likely the most prevalent strain on the facial skin of Thai individuals. In contrast, other phylotypes such as IC, II, and III, were not identified, possibly due to limitations of the swabbing technique or their preference for different anatomical niches. Furthermore, the absence of previously reported clonal complexes—such as CC4 (type IA1), CC6 and CC72 (singleton, type II)—indicates potential differences in the genetic diversity of P. acnes between countries and sampling sites. These disparities may reflect the current underrepresentation of Asian isolates in global datasets. Overall, the insights gained from this study provide a valuable foundation for research on P. acnes in Thailand. The findings may also support the development of targeted treatment strategies tailored to the specific strains prevalent in the Thai population, potentially improving the effectiveness of acne therapies.
