Dynamic Fracture Monitoring Technique in Deep Coal Seams Based on a Time-Varying Electric Field Method
Authors: Jinshui Zhang, Fan Yang, Qi An, Haiyan Ding, XinLe Cao, Bingsen Yan, Qinghuan Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The efficient development of deep coalbed methane (CBM) is constrained by unclear hydraulic fracture propagation mechanisms and the limited resolution of existing monitoring techniques. Conventional methods such as microseismic monitoring and static resistivity imaging are unable to provide real-time, high-resolution characterization of dynamic fracture growth and fluid migration, thereby impeding accurate evaluation of stimulation effectiveness. To address these limitations, this study introduces a novel dynamic fracture monitoring technique based on a time-varying electric field method. By establishing a coupled electric field–fracturing fluid flow model and deploying a high-density downhole electrode array integrated with real-time data processing, the technique enables millisecond-level signal acquisition and dynamic inversion of fracture geometry. Field validation demonstrates that the method dynamically tracks fracture propagation with a spatial accuracy of 0.5 m and temporal resolution better than 5 min. The monitoring results quantitatively delineate a three-stage fracture behaviorcharacterized by nonuniform initiation, differential extension, and dynamic reorientation among clusterswith the maximum single-cluster fracture length reaching 214.95 m. The interpreted fluid distribution and fracture evolution show strong consistency with independent high-frequency pressure-wave analysis of cluster-level fluid intake, confirming the accuracy of the method. Furthermore, comparison with fracture inversion reveals the presence of stress-extended zones not penetrated by fracturing fluid, highlighting the discrepancy between the actual stimulated volume and the fluid-swept region. This reflects the pronounced heterogeneity and corresponding stress-propagation characteristics inherent to deep coal reservoirs. The method offers a robust technical approach for evaluating stimulation effectiveness and optimizing fracturing design parameters in deep coalbed methane reservoirs, further contributing to the large-scale development and production breakthroughs in deep coal seams.

Introduction

With the global transition toward low-carbon energy structures, coalbed methane (CBM), as a significant unconventional natural gas resource, , plays an important role in ensuring energy security and reducing greenhouse gas emissions. − In China, deep coal seams (with a burial depth exceeding 1500 m) account for over 60% of the total CBM resources. However, their large-scale development faces severe challenges due to complex geological conditions and insufficient alignment between fracturing technologies and geological characteristics. − Deep coal seams are generally characterized by high in situ stress, low permeability, and strong heterogeneity, , making it difficult to predict the propagation behavior of hydraulic fractures. Real-time diagnosis of fracture geometry is crucial for dynamically optimizing operational parameters (such as the injection rate and sand ratio). However, the limited accuracy of existing monitoring methods directly impacts the effectiveness of fracturing treatments. It is estimated that up to 30% of fracturing stages may be ineffective due to the limited precision of monitoring techniques, severely constraining the overall productivity potential of CBM wells. ,

Existing fracturing monitoring methods for deep coal seams, such as microseismic monitoring, exhibit significant limitations in their applications. , Microseismic technology primarily interprets fractures by capturing and analyzing seismic wave signals induced during fracturing. However, their application in deep coal seams faces notable challenges. Because of the relatively high plasticity and softer nature of coal rock, seismic waves undergo significant attenuation during propagation, leading to common event location errors of 15%–20%. This makes it difficult to effectively identify secondary fracture networks, fundamentally constraining the interpretation accuracy. Another method, surface deformation inversion, deduces fracture parameters by observing the ground displacement. However, surface deformation induced by fracturing in deep coal seams is typically extremely subtle in magnitude and is highly susceptible to being masked by environmental noise. The actual spatial resolution is extremely inadequate (typically <5 m), which falls short of the precision required for reliable inversion.

In recent years, electromagnetic methods, particularly those based on static electric fields, have gradually become a research focus in the field of fracturing monitoring because of their sensitivity to the electrical properties of subsurface media. ,− However, several technical bottlenecks remain. On the one hand, traditional resistivity methods infer fracture dimensions by measuring static differences in formation resistivity before and after fracturing. Yet, they fail to capture the dynamic changes in electrical conductivity during the fracturing process and thus cannot support real-time monitoring of fracture propagation. On the other hand, there is the challenge of signal attenuation. The highly resistive nature of deep coal seams makes it difficult for surface-injected currents to penetrate the target layer, resulting in a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of typically below 20 dB. In response, numerous researchers domestically and internationally have explored methods such as time-lapse electromagnetic (time-lapse EM) and cross-well electromagnetic tomography (cross-well EM). The former primarily inverts fracture propagation by periodically measuring resistivity changes, , but its data acquisition intervals are often longer than 10 min, which cannot meet the demands of real-time fracturing control. Cross-well electromagnetic tomography mainly images fractures by analyzing changes in the electromagnetic wave propagation paths between wells. , However, in deep coal seams, large well spacing leads to severe electromagnetic wave attenuation, making high-resolution imaging difficult. Recent studies have shown that fracturing fluid injection significantly alters the electric field distribution around fractures, − and dynamic fracture propagation information can be captured by using high-frequency potential signals exceeding 100 Hz. Nevertheless, the existing methods are largely confined to theoretical simulations. There remains a lack of electrode deployment schemes and anti-interference algorithms specifically designed for the high in situ stress and highly resistive formations characteristic of deep coal seams. As a result, these approaches have not yet matured into industrial applications.

On the basis of this, this paper proposes a dynamic monitoring technique grounded in the time-varying electric field method. Since the conductivity of the fracturing fluid is significantly higher than that of the coal seam, fracture formation induces local electric field distortion, providing the physical basis for dynamic monitoring. First, a dynamic coupling model of the electric field and fracturing fluid seepage is established to reveal the time-varying correlation mechanism between fracture propagation and potential response. Subsequently, a high-density downhole electrode array suitable for deep coal seams is designed based on the wellsite layout. Combined with an adaptive filtering algorithm to suppress formation background noise, we developed a real-time dynamic monitoring technology for horizontal wells. Unlike traditional static monitoring, which can only provide comparative results of the reservoir stimulation before and after fracturing, the proposed time-varying electric field method, through millisecond-level potential signal acquisition, can resolve transient processes of fracture propagation (such as the initiation of branch fractures and deflection of fracture networks). This provides an analytical basis for understanding the actual distribution and extension of the fractures.

Methods

During the hydraulic fracturing process in deep coal seams, the time-varying electric field monitoring technique involves deploying an electrode array on the surface to inject an alternating current of specific frequencies into the formation, while simultaneously measuring the distribution of the resulting potential field in real time (Figure ). This technology leverages the high sensitivity of formation electrical parameters (such as resistivity) to fracture propagation and fracturing fluid migration: when the fracturing fluid enters the coal reservoir and forms a complex fracture network, its conductivity differs significantly from that of the surrounding rock, leading to dynamic changes in the electric field response. By inversion of time-series electric field data, the three-dimensional spatiotemporal evolution of resistivity within the fracturing-affected zone can be reconstructed, thereby enabling the quantitative characterization of fracture orientation, geometric morphology, and fluid distribution patterns.

During on-site monitoring operations, the conductors and casing pipe supply a constant current to the formation, thereby generating a ring-shaped artificial electric field in the strata. This electric field depends solely on the well depth “z” and the radial distance “r” of the measurement ring. During on-site monitoring operations, the target monitoring well serves as electrode A, while a selected nearby production well functions as electrode B, forming a circuit with the formation. Because of constraints imposed by field working conditions and instrument power, electrode B cannot be located too far from the monitored well. However, the electric field generated by electrode B may interfere with the monitoring of the well. Therefore, it is necessary to account for the influence of electrode B, meaning that the potential difference measured at points M and N corresponds to the integral along the dipole length of the electric fields generated by both wells superimposed on the surface.

The potential generated solely by electrode A at the surrounding measuring points is denoted as U AMN, while the potential generated by electrode B at the measuring points around A is denoted as U BMN . After superposition, the combined potential is represented as U QMN , which constitutes the normal field potential.

On the surface, with Well A as the center, the potential difference between points M and N is measured along a circumference with radii vectors R M and R N . Field Application

Well M is a development horizontal well within the deep coalbed methane exploration and development demonstration project in the Linxing area. Structurally located in the Jinxi Fold-Thrust Belt in the northeastern part of the Ordos Basin, this well is a production horizontal well in the Linxing Block. The primary target layer is the No. 9 coal seam of the Benxi Formation. The drilled horizontal section length is 1,200 m, with a coal seam encounter rate of 100% and an average gas logging value of 84%. The well area exhibits stable structure, undeveloped faults, a coal seam thickness of 6∼7 m, and a gas content of 14∼16 m3/t, indicating favorable development potential.

The actual drilled horizontal section length of the coal seam in this well was 1200 m. Due to geographical constraints, the time-varying electric field method was employed in this operation to monitor in real time the fracturing fracture propagation process only in Stages 8 (2541–2663 m) and Stages 9 (2425–2541 m). During the field deployment of monitoring points, the line connecting the top and bottom points (horizontal displacement) of each tested stage served as the baseline. Two survey lines were arranged on each side of the trajectory, with distances from the baseline set at 90 m for survey line 2 and 180 m for survey line 1. The spacing between monitoring points (point intervals) was determined based on the cluster spacing. During the fracturing operation, a high-frequency electromagnetic data acquisition system was deployed onsite to enable real-time monitoring and precise evaluation of fracture propagation geometry. The system automatically acquires electromagnetic field signals associated with fracture distribution every 130 s. Collected data are transmitted in real time via an integrated IoT communication module to a remote data-processing server. Upon receipt, the server completes the full processing workflowincluding noise filtering, signal inversion, and parameter extractionwithin a 40 s window.

The processed results are subsequently pushed into a professional interpretation system (Figure ). On the basis of established algorithms, this system automatically generates interpreted outcomes of the fracture geometry. These outcomes are presented in the form of multidimensional visual graphics, primarily including a plane view reflecting the areal distribution characteristics of the fractures, a profile view characterizing their vertical morphology, and a comprehensive 3D schematic diagram integrating spatial information.

Results and Discussion

(1) Monitoring Results of Stage 8

During the fracturing operation of this stage, remote real-time monitoring revealed that the fracture initiation process in the early stage was relatively slow, with inconsistent initiation times. There was a notable sequential difference in the initiation of fractures from each cluster: the second cluster (well section 2600 m) initiated first followed by the first cluster (well section 2566 m), while the third cluster (well section 2638 m) initiated last. Monitoring indicated that the dominant orientation of the main fractures was concentrated in the NE75° and SW255° directions.

In the initial fracturing phase, fracture propagation exhibited asymmetric characteristics: fractures from the second cluster propagated more rapidly in the SW255° direction, whereas development in the NE75° direction was more pronounced for the third cluster. Fracture development for the first cluster was relatively balanced in both the NE75° and SW255° directions. After reaching a certain extent of propagation, the dynamics of fracture extension shifted: the propagation rate of the second cluster in the NE75° direction increased significantly, while development of the third cluster in the SW255° direction also noticeably intensified. Although some variability was observed due to differences in fracture height, the integrated interpretationcombining fracture length and height extensionaligns with the results from the time-varying electric field monitoring. This result thereby validates the accuracy of the proposed time-varying electric-field monitoring method.

(2)­Monitoring Results of Stage 9

Remote real-time monitoring of the fracturing operation for this stage revealed a clear dynamic pattern characterized by ″nonuniform initiation, differential propagation, and uneven development”. The first cluster served as the primary contributor to this fracturing stage followed by the third cluster, while the stimulation effectiveness of the second cluster was relatively weaker. This demonstrates significant stress interference and propagation asymmetry among the clusters within the stages.

During the fracturing process, each cluster exhibited distinct spatial propagation characteristics: First cluster: During the initial and middle phases of fracturing, development was relatively balanced in both the NE75° and SW255° directions. In the later stage, propagation slightly favored the NE75° direction. Third cluster: In the early stage, propagation was faster in the NE75° direction and relatively slower in the SW255° direction. However, propagation in the SW255° direction accelerated significantly during the later stage. Second cluster: Throughout the entire fracturing period, fracture development for this cluster was notably weaker compared to the other two clusters. The core contribution of this study lies in the development of a time-varying electric field monitoring process, achieved by integrating a coupled electric field-fluid flow model with a high-density downhole electrode array and real-time processing. This approach successfully provides fine-scale characterization of dynamic fluid distribution and swept volume within the fracture network during hydraulic fracturing, thereby offering direct visualization of the spatial extent of the effectively stimulated reservoir volume. These results establish a foundational basis for investigating proppant transport behavior inside fractures. The presented method delivers a direct and reliable technical means for quantitatively evaluating the effective reservoir stimulation scale and optimizing well-cluster spacing design.

However, it is also necessary to consider the limitations inherent in the physical principles of this monitoring method. The time-varying electric field method essentially monitors changes in the electrical properties of the medium caused by fluid invasion, and its signal strength directly depends on the extent of conductive network (fracturing fluid) development within the formation. Consequently, while it exhibits high sensitivity to the “hydraulic fracture network” that is fully filled and connected by fracturing fluid, it has a monitoring blind spot for “stress-affected zones” areas that undergo shear slippage or dilation due to stress disturbance but are not significantly invaded by fracturing fluid. These zones, where microseismic events predominantly occur, may appear indistinct in resistivity imaging due to minimal changes in electrical properties.

This limitation precisely points the way for future technological development. A single monitoring method is insufficient to fully describe the complex fracturing process involving the coupling of ″stress field and seepage field.″ The future core direction lies in promoting the deep integration and synergistic interpretation of the time-varying electric field method with microseismic monitoring. By precisely overlaying and jointly inverting the “fluid-dominant pathways” revealed by the electric field method and the “fracture event clouds” captured by microseismic monitoring in three-dimensional space, a more comprehensive distinction can be made between the “fluid-effective stimulated zones” and the “stress-disturbed zones”. This integration of multiphysics information will enable a deeper evaluation of fracturing effectiveness, moving from “swept volume” to “stimulation quality”, thereby providing more reliable decision-making support for the efficient development of unconventional reservoirs.

Conclusions

By integrating a coupled electric field–fluid flow model with a high-density downhole electrode array and real-time processing, this study enables simultaneous tracking of fracture propagation patterns and fluid migration pathways, thereby overcoming key limitations of conventional microseismic and static resistivity monitoring.
