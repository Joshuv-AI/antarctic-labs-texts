Patient perspective regarding the role of social media, offline reputation, and practitioner characteristics in orthodontist selection: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Prangrath Liengjohor, Saranya Serirukchutarungsee, Neeranart Thirasupa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeterminants of orthodontist selection in the modern era remain inconsistent, especially roles of online versus traditional factors. Background

Orthodontist selection is a multifaceted decision-making process driven by the wide array of contemporary information available to patients [1, 2]. Modern communication tools, particularly social media platforms, now serve as influential information sources, moving the selection process toward visually driven online evaluations [3]. Platforms such as Instagram, Facebook, practice websites, and X (formerly Twitter) allow patients to infer clinical competence by assessing the quality and consistency of before-and-after treatment images [4]. Beyond technical skills, interactive features enable patients to evaluate an orthodontist’s communication style and approachability [5]. Furthermore, perceived expertise is reinforced through online evidence of academic engagement, public reviews, and influencer endorsements [4, 6].

Although social media has become a prominent factor in orthodontist selection [5, 7], the prevalence of misleading online content has fostered growing skepticism. Modern patients are increasingly digitally literate and tend to evaluate online health information critically rather than accepting it at face value [8]. This raises an important question regarding whether patient reliance is shifting back toward traditional factors to validate their choices. Offline reputation, including word-of-mouth recommendations from family and friends or professional referrals from other dentists, is able to influence trust and preference [9]. Regarding orthodontist characteristics, patients value academic background, clinical experience, and consistent treatment outcomes [6]. Interpersonal qualities, such as an orthodontist’s ability to communicate empathetically and create a comfortable environment, are similarly critical to decision-making [10]. Moreover, characteristics like attire, age, and gender can influence patients’ perceptions, although these preferences vary based on cultural and individual differences [11].

Current literature reveals inconsistent findings regarding the actual impact of social media on practitioner selection [12]. While some research suggests that a large follower base and frequent digital engagement enhance perceived credibility [5], another study reported that parents did not prioritize online reviews or social media presence when selecting a practitioner for their children [13]. The influence of practitioner characteristics, such as gender and professional attire, also shows mixed results, indicating that preferences vary across different cultural and demographic groups [14]. Additionally, the importance of less-explored conventional elements, including community involvement or professional awards, has not been well established [15]. Most importantly, existing research tends to evaluate these determinants in isolation. Few studies have integrated digital and traditional factors together within a single comprehensive model, limiting a holistic understanding of how these variables interact to shape modern patient decision-making [7, 12].

To address these gaps, this study evaluated the relative importance of social media, offline reputation, and orthodontist characteristics in orthodontist selection among working-age adults. This specific sample was chosen to ensure that the outcomes reflect genuine decision-making processes, as these participants typically finance their own treatment and make independent healthcare choices. The null hypothesis posited that none of these factors were significantly associated with orthodontist selection, whereas the alternative hypothesis stated that at least one of these factors significantly correlated with patient selection. By integrating these variables, the analysis aimed to quantify their relative contributions and identify which factors most strongly predict decision-making in the current orthodontic landscape.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional analytical study was conducted between October 2024 and January 2025, following approval by the Institutional Review Board (Approval no. SWUEC671053). The study population comprised working-age adults seeking orthodontic treatment at private practices and university clinics. Inclusion criteria were: (1) intention to pursue orthodontic treatment as a first-time patient, without any prior history of orthodontic therapy; (2) age between 25 and 45 years; and (3) provision of informed consent. Patients with a history of previous orthodontic treatment, as well as those who refused participation, voluntarily withdrew, or submitted incomplete questionnaires, were excluded.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined a priori to ensure adequate statistical power for multiple linear regression. Utilizing G*Power software, based on a presumed effect size of 0.05 derived from previous literature evaluating social media influence on orthodontist selection [7], an alpha level of 0.05, a power of 0.95, and 16 predictor variables, a minimum of 262 participants was required. However, to adhere to the more robust multivariate guidelines recommended by Hair et al. [16], which stipulate 10 to 20 observations per predictor variable, a more conservative target of 320 participants was established. To account for potential data attrition, the final sample was increased to 330.

Sampling technique and data collection

Participants were recruited using a consecutive, stratified quota sampling approach. Previous studies have demonstrated that differences exist between private and university-based clinics regarding practice offerings and accessibility [17]. Therefore, to mitigate potential confounding bias and ensure a balanced representation of the broader patient population, a stratification design was employed. The recruitment quota was divided equally, targeting 165 participants from private practices and 165 from university clinics. During the data collection period, all eligible patients visiting the participating sites were invited to participate. Upon providing informed consent, participants independently completed the survey via an online Google Form (Google®, Mountain View, Calif) or a self-administered paper format. To prevent duplicate submissions, the online settings were configured to limit responses to one per account. Additionally, demographic variables were cross-referenced during data auditing to detect and exclude any potential overlapping responses between the two survey modes. Recruitment at each respective site was monitored continuously by the researchers and concluded once the predefined quota was achieved.

Questionnaire design and validation

The questionnaire was developed through a comprehensive review of the literature on practitioner-related factors influencing orthodontist selection [4, 5, 14, 18]. The identified domains and questionnaire items were refined to reflect the objectives of the present study. The development process followed established principles of questionnaire development and validation [19–21]. Content validity was evaluated by a panel of five experts, consisting of dental professionals and a behavioral science specialist, using the Index of Item-Objective Congruence (IOC) to ensure alignment with the study objectives. Items with unsatisfactory IOC values were considered for exclusion before pilot testing. Face validity was assessed through pretesting with 35 individuals who met the inclusion criteria to evaluate the clarity and comprehensibility. Participant feedback was incorporated to improve its readability. Reliability was evaluated in a pilot study involving 35 participants. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, and item discrimination was evaluated using corrected item-total correlation. Only items demonstrating acceptable reliability and discrimination were retained in the final 30-item questionnaire, which was subsequently administered to the study participants.

The structured questionnaire comprised three main parts. Part one collected demographic information and social media usage behaviors (9 items). This included background data, usage frequency of various social media platforms evaluated on a 6-point Likert-type scale from 1 (Never use) to 6 (Always use), and peak activity periods (single-choice response). Part two evaluated practitioner-related factors influencing orthodontist selection based on a comprehensive review of the existing literature (25 items), categorized into three domains: (1) Social media (orthodontist credibility, having online presence, online review, and orthodontist approachability), (2) Offline reputation (referral through another dentist, word-of-mouth recommendation from peers or former patients, awards and recognitions, and community involvement) (3) Orthodontist Characteristics (patient comfort, clarity in treatment explanation, perceived caring, educational background, working experience, appearance and clothing, working age orthodontist, and gender match with the patient). All items were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). For statistical analysis, composite scores for each factor were calculated by averaging the item responses to serve as independent variables. Part three assessed the decision-making process. It consisted of problem awareness, information search, orthodontist comparison, selection criteria, and confidence in the final choice. This part served as the dependent variable (5 items). Responses were rated on the same five-point Likert scale. The total score for this part was calculated by averaging the five items, with higher scores indicating a more engaged and deliberate decision-making process (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

The data analysis protocol stipulated that any questionnaires containing missing data would be excluded from the final dataset. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and social media usage behaviors. To check for survey medium bias between the online and self-administered paper formats, independent-samples Mann-Whitney U tests were conducted. Differences in usage frequency across social media platforms were evaluated using the Friedman test, followed by Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons.

To evaluate the factors influencing orthodontist selection, data normality was first assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Subsequently, multiple linear regression analysis utilizing the forward stepwise method was performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Furthermore, to address the known risk of overfitting associated with stepwise regression, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using a standard multiple regression with enter method. The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Questionnaire validity and reliability

The questionnaire’s content validity was confirmed with an overall average IOC value of 0.83, ranging from 0.60 to 1.00. The overall Cronbach’s alpha was 0.85, demonstrating a high level of internal consistency without redundancy. Additionally, all items showed corrected item–total correlations above 0.30, confirming adequate individual item discrimination. These metrics support the questionnaire’s reliability and appropriateness for use in the study.

Demographic details and social media usage

All 330 distributed questionnaires were fully completed, with none excluded due to missing data. Independent-samples Mann-Whitney U tests confirmed there were no statistically significant differences in responses between the online and self-administered paper formats (P > 0.05). Among the 330 participants, the majority were female (68.8%), single (73.0%), and held a bachelor’s degree (61.2%), income levels sufficient to meet basic needs and allow for some savings (42.7%), with a mean age of 29.9 ± 3.3 years (Table 2).

Factor influencing orthodontist selection

Prior to evaluating the factors, statistical assumptions and overall model diagnostics were assessed. The normality test of the residuals yielded a significant result (P < 0.05). Nonetheless, based on the established literature by Lumley et al. [22], regression analysis remains a reliable method in large sample sizes when other essential assumptions are met. In this study, there was no evidence of multicollinearity among the independent variables (Maximum VIF < 10, Tolerance > 0.1) or autocorrelation (Durbin-Watson = 1.76). Moreover, homoscedasticity was confirmed via a scatterplot that displayed a random and structureless dispersion of points. Thus, the regression analysis proceeded as planned.

The finalized multiple regression model revealed that factors across all three domains were significantly associated with orthodontist selection (F = 19.24, P < 0.001). Among the individual determinants, an orthodontist’s online presence (social media domain) emerged as the most influencial factor (β = 0.200, P < 0.001). This was followed by perceived caring (orthodontist characteristics; β = 0.170, P = 0.004), word-of-mouth recommendations from peers or former patients (offline reputation; β = 0.139, P = 0.007), educational background (orthodontist characteristics; β = 0.136, P = 0.016), and the working age of the orthodontist (orthodontist characteristics; β = 0.130, P = 0.013), in descending order. Ultimately, the comprehensive model explained 21% of the variance in patient decision-making (adjusted R² = 0.21) (Table 4). The sensitivity analysis utilizing the enter method confirmed that these same five independent variables remained statistically significant with no meaningful changes to the coefficients (F = 7.27, P < 0.001), indicating a stable and robust final model.

Discussion

The growing importance of digital engagement was evident, with an orthodontist’s online presence emerging as the strongest factor associated with orthodontist selection. Online presence specifically measured the frequency of educational updates and the orthodontist’s responsiveness to comments and direct messages. These findings align with those of Alali et al. [23] who reported that 54% of participants selected a practitioner based on their online presence, and Hariri et al. [24] who also identified social media presence as an important factor in practitioner selection. These findings suggest that patients value professional knowledge and meaningful two-way communication over entertainment-oriented content, marketing aesthetics, or follower counts. Interactive features, such as comments and live sessions, may enhance transparency and reflect professionalism [25, 26]. Similarly, Tengilimoglu et al. [27] and Parmar et al. [28] found that patients appreciated dentists who actively engaged on social media because these interactions fostered trust and a sense of connection. Furthermore, sharing high-quality educational content is associated with greater perceived professional competence and positively influences practitioner selection [4, 7, 11, 14]. However, the influence of social media is not universal. For example, Gušić et al. [29] reported that 60.3% of participants in Serbia would not select a dentist based on social media presence, likely reflecting concerns regarding the credibility of online information and the promotion of misleading content.

From the practitioner’s perspective, social media has evolved beyond a marketing tool into a valuable platform for professional education, knowledge sharing, and patient engagement [30]. Orthodontists use these platforms to exchange clinical experiences, discuss treatment techniques and digital workflows, and communicate with patients [30–32]. At the same time, social media has become an interactive environment where patients share personal experiences, seek reassurance, discuss treatment concerns, and evaluate healthcare providers, all of which may shape treatment preferences and practitioner selection [33]. In addition, social media has become one of the most widely adopted strategies for practice promotion and new patient acquisition [9]. Given the increasing influence of digital engagement on patient decision-making, orthodontists should maintain a professional and ethical online presence. Although an active online presence may help patients assess an orthodontist’s trustworthiness, competence, and approachability before initiating treatment, greater digital visibility also carries potential risks, including the dissemination of inaccurate information and influencer-driven misinformation [34, 35]. Therefore, orthodontists should maintain a professional and ethical social media presence by providing transparent, evidence-based information to support informed patient decision-making and counter online misinformation [33, 36].

Beyond digital visibility, the established human connection remains significantly important. The findings suggest that perceived caring plays a central role in the selection of orthodontist. Given that orthodontic treatment involves prolonged engagement and repeated interactions over an extended period, the quality of the orthodontist–patient relationship becomes particularly salient [37]. This result is consistent with previous studies reporting that interpersonal behaviors, such as friendliness, empathy, and emotional support, are strongly associated with patient satisfaction and treatment continuity [38–41].

Recommendations from social circles was also indispensable to the evaluative process. Personal referrals from peers or former patients provided firsthand insights that helped reduce uncertainty, especially when direct clinical evaluation was not yet possible. Adanan et al. [18] noted that some patients rely heavily on recommendations without additional research. Previous studies consistently found word-of-mouth to be more influential than social media in dentist selection [4, 11, 14]. Peer recommendations are viewed as inherently more trustworthy, offering patients the confidence to validate conflicting information found online. Furthermore, growing awareness of manipulated digital metrics, such as fake reviews, may lead patients to prioritize the perceived honesty of personal referrals over digital presence [42].

Educational background contributed significantly to patients’ evaluations as a part of professional qualifications. A strong academic record was generally perceived as a reflection of clinical competence and up-to-date knowledge, consistent with prior research [4, 7, 11]. Patients appeared to associate specialty training with treatment reliability and credibility [15, 43]. These findings suggest that academic education provides reassurance of technical capability, particularly for the long-term nature of orthodontic treatment. Similarly, the working age of orthodontists, particularly those between 30 and 55 years, was positively associated with patient perceptions. Consistent with Yuo et al. [14], this age group was viewed as balancing clinical experience with contemporary knowledge [6]. Patients likely perceived these orthodontists as experienced enough to manage complex cases confidently, yet young enough to remain current with technological advances [43].

While peer recommendations positively influenced decisions, referrals from other dentists showed no significant association. This finding contrasts with Fatah et al. [38] and Chambers et al. [44], who reported high patient reliance on dentist referrals for specialized care. The divergence may reflect growing skepticism toward dentist-directed guidance, possibly due to concerns about bias or financial incentives. Similarly, influencer reviews and formal recognitions were non-significant predictors. Although previous study found influencers effective for attracting young adults [18], our results suggest patients may view such content as commercial or inauthentic. Likewise, awards and recognitions may have seemed promotional and disconnected from personal experiences. Instead, patients appeared to value transparent, relatable stories shared through peer networks, building trust on personal connections rather than professional accolades.

In line with previous studies reporting mixed findings on attire and gender, our results showed no significant effect for either factor. While some literature has suggested that professional appearance conveys competence, hygiene, and respect [45, 46], and that gender matching improves communication and reduces anxiety, these associations were not observed in this study. Patients likely favored humanistic qualities, such as empathy and verifiable competence, over external characteristics. Additionally, cultural norms and demographic differences may also contribute to variation in these perceptions.

Taken together, these findings indicate a contemporary pattern of patient expectations. Practical implications may include establishing credible social media content, particularly on Instagram, Facebook, and TikTok during peak night hours, to enhance visibility and deliver education. However, the data highlight that digital visibility cannot replace the human element of care. Prioritizing empathetic communication and ensuring positive clinical experience remain fundamental to stimulating organic word-of-mouth referrals. Practitioners can further build credibility by actively sharing their academic qualifications and clinical expertise, which directly addresses the patient preference for experienced and working-age professionals.

The strengths of this study include its integrated evaluation of multiple selection factors and the use of a rigorously validated and reliable questionnaire. Furthermore, focusing on working-age adults minimized confounding biases related to vastly differing levels of digital literacy across generations, ensuring that the outcomes reflect genuine decision-making processes.

Despite these strengths, some limitations must be acknowledged. Methodologically, the cross-sectional design only captures associations at a single time point, and the reliance on self-reported data introduces potential response bias. Moreover, although the online questionnaire was restricted to one response per account, the possibility of a single individual utilizing multiple accounts cannot be entirely eliminated. Clinically, recruiting participants after they had already visited a practice introduces a risk of recall bias, as their immediate experiences may have influenced their retrospective answers. Consequently, these results may not fully reflect the selection behaviors of the broader general population. Furthermore, because this research focused primarily on practitioner-related factors, the regression model explained 21% of the variance. This suggests that a substantial proportion of patient choice was driven by unmeasured practical factors, such as treatment cost, geographic location, or insurance acceptance, meaning these findings represented only one part of a broader decision-making ecosystem. Lastly, while targeting a specific age group enhanced internal validity, it limits the generalizability of the results. Future research employing longitudinal or mixed-method designs across broader demographics, including adolescents and parents, is recommended to better understand how patient preferences evolve over time.

Conclusions

A combination of social media, offline reputation, and orthodontist characteristics is associated with practitioner selection in the digital age. Among these, an online presence demonstrates the strongest association. Utilizing platforms such as Instagram, Facebook, and TikTok during peak night hours can potentially enhance visibility. Beyond digital platforms, focusing on caring communication and positive clinical experiences possibly help reinforce valuable word-of-mouth referrals. Providing visible evidence of academic credentials and continuing education can further signal clinical competence. Orthodontists might also highlight the balance of up-to-date knowledge and practical experience frequently expected from working-age practitioners. Attention to these areas may strengthen patient relationships and enhance practice success in a digitally connected world.
