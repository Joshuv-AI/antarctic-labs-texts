Effects of Saccharomyces cerevisiae fermented citric waste inclusion in the diet on animal performance
Authors: Chanadol Supapong, Benjamad Khonkhaeng, Bunthum Sangkaew, Wuttichai Seephueak, Chatchai Sriperm, Anusorn Cherdthong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of yeast-fermented citric waste (YFCW) as a protein source in the concentrate diet of Brahman crossbred cattle. Citric waste, a by-product from the fruit juice industry, was fermented using Saccharomyces cerevisiae (1.0 × 108 CFU/g) to improve its nutritional value and digestibility. The experiment lasted for 84 days and was conducted using a 4 × 4 Latin square design, consisting of four periods of 21 days each. Four Brahman crossbred cattle were assigned to receive concentrate diets in which soybean meal was replaced with YFCW at 0, 33, 66, and 100% levels. The findings showed that while rice straw intake was unaffected (P > 0.05), increasing levels of YFCW linearly reduced concentrate intake, nutrient intake (including crude protein, NDF, and ADF), and nutrient digestibility (P < 0.05). Temperature and pH levels during rumen fermentation were within ideal ranges. However, cattle fed higher levels of YFCW (66–100%) showed a decrease in the population of protozoa and the concentration of total volatile fatty acids, particularly acetic and butyric acids. With the exception of platelet counts and specific red blood cell indices, the majority of hematological and biochemical parameters did not differ significantly between treatments. In conclusion, YFCW can be used as a protein source in beef cattle diets without adverse effects on animal health. However, replacement levels above 66% may negatively affect feed intake and nutrient digestibility. Appropriate inclusion levels should be considered to ensure sustainable ruminant production.Keywords: Citric acid waste, Beef cattle, Zero waste

Introduction

The livestock industry is currently facing a serious problem with the growing cost of animal feed ingredients. Two primary causes of this price increase are unstable international market prices, unmanageable logistics and management expenses, and government initiatives to stabilize domestic feed crop prices, which frequently skew market dynamics. Feed ingredients have become much more expensive in the post-COVID-19 era. For instance, in February 2022, wheat, a key ingredient in animal feed, rose from 0.28 USD/kg in 2021 to 0.34 USD/kg. During the same period, the price of imported soybean meal rose from USD 0.52/kg to USD 0.63/kg (Department of Livestock Development, 2022). Geopolitical tensions, especially the conflict between Russia and Ukraine, which impacts major producers of animal feed crops such as corn and soybean, contributed to these increases. The cost of other essential feed ingredients, such as barley, palm oil, and cassava, has also increased significantly. In addition, rising energy costs, including coal, natural gas, and electricity used in feed processing and production, have further contributed to the overall increase in feed prices. These factors have put smallholder farmers, who normally spend 60–70% of their livestock production costs on feed, under a lot of financial strain in addition to raising concerns about possible feed shortages (McDermott et al., 2010). Long-term droughts have also made matters worse by decreasing the amount and quality of feed resources that are readily available locally, increasing reliance on pricey imported feeds.

In terms of livestock production, southern Thailand ranks third, following the northern and northeastern regions, which are the first and second largest producers, respectively. There are 531,899 livestock farmers in the area, and 281,026 of them raise ruminants, which include sheep, goats, buffaloes, dairy cattle, and beef cattle, for a total of >1.6 million animals (Department of Livestock Development, 2022). With 217,455 farmers, beef cattle farming is the most common among these, followed by goat (54,537) and sheep (5574) farming. With 41,852 farmers raising 222,794 cattle, Nakhon Si Thammarat province has the most beef cattle farmers in the South (Department of Livestock Development, 2022). The region's high feed costs, which are mostly caused by the cost of transportation for importing feed materials, pose serious challenges despite its prominence. For example, Rice straw costs 0.10 USD/kg, which is nearly twice as high as prices in other areas. In comparison, napier grass costs around 0.06 USD/kg. This disparity highlights the increasing cost burden of roughage sources for livestock production. The efficiency of farm management has also been hampered by labor shortages and the reduction of forage cultivation areas.

Parallel to this, the production of citric acid is a major global industry, with an estimated 1.7 million tons produced annually as of 2008 and a projected 3% annual growth rate. Cassava and cassava pulp are important raw materials used for citric acid production through microbial fermentation by Aspergillus niger (Adeoye et al., 2015). About 69% of the residual waste produced by the fermentation process is acidic and contains a high moisture content. If this waste is improperly disposed by landfilling or incineration soil, water, and air quality may suffer. Nonetheless, studies have shown that citric acid residues, typically semi-solid by-products with high moisture content (70–85%), crude fiber (10–25%), crude protein (5–12%), and minerals (3–8%). However, the composition may vary depending on processing conditions (Sriroth et al., 2000; Suriyapha et al., 2024).

Agro-industrial residues have the potential to serve as alternative feed resources; however, their nutritional limitations often restrict their direct use in animal diets. Improving their nutritional value through biotechnological approaches may enhance their competitiveness as feed ingredients and promote more efficient feed utilization. One promising strategy is biological treatment using Saccharomyces cerevisiae, a well-established and safe probiotic yeast, which has been shown to improve digestibility and enhance nutrient availability for rumen microbial fermentation. Citric acid production generates substantial amounts of waste residues, which may contain residual nutrients but are underutilized in ruminant feeding systems. Fermentation of these residues using yeast could enhance their nutritional quality and reduce potential anti-nutritional effects, making them a viable alternative to conventional protein sources such as soybean meal.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of replacing soybean meal with yeast-fermented citric waste (YFCW) in the diets of Brahman cattle on feed intake, nutrient digestibility, rumen fermentation, and blood biochemical parameters.

Materials and methods

Animals, feed and treatments

In a 4 × 4 Latin square design, four Brahman beef cattle weighing 175 ± 20 kg at birth and ranging in age from 1 to 1.5 years were divided into four dietary treatments. The local Thai citric acid manufacturer CHOK PHALID SUP Co., Ltd. provided the fermented citric acid residue used in this investigation. Fermented citric acid residue supplemented with Saccharomyces cerevisiae (1.0 × 108 CFU/g) was used to replace soybean meal at four different levels: 0% (Treatment 1, control), 33% (Treatment 2), 66% (Treatment 3), and 100% replacement (Treatment 4). Roughage was given freely, and the concentrate diet (Table 1), which was designed to satisfy the nutritional needs of beef cattle, was fed at a rate of 0.5% of body weight. To prevent exposure to moisture and sunlight, the fermented product was made and kept at room temperature in 200-liter plastic containers. Over the course of 21 days, the animals were fed a concentrate diet that contained the treated material. The fermented product was obtained in batches from the same production source to ensure consistency, and all material used in the experiment was handled under similar storage conditions. Random sampling was conducted to monitor pH and moisture content as indicators of fermentation stability, and both parameters remained within a consistent range throughout the experimental period.

The concentrate was given to the cattle before the roughage twice a day, at 8:00 a.m. and 5:00 pm. There was always clean drinking water available. To guarantee accurate monitoring, daily feed intake for both concentrate and roughage was documented during the trial. Prior to the experiment, every animal received a vaccination and underwent routine preventative medical care in accordance with guidelines authorized by Rajamangala University of Technology Srivijaya's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).

Data collection and chemical analysis

Feed and refusal samples were collected during the final 7 days of each experimental period. Total feces and urine outputs were measured and sampled using the total collection method over the same 7-day period, during which the animals were housed in metabolism cages to evaluate nutrient digestibility and nitrogen metabolism. Approximately 5% of the total fresh weight of feces was sampled daily and divided into two subsamples: one portion was used immediately for dry matter (DM) determination, while the other was refrigerated and pooled by animal at the end of the collection period for chemical analysis. Urine was collected in 10-L containers containing 10% sulfuric acid (H2SO4) to maintain the pH below 3.0 and prevent microbial degradation of nitrogen. Pooled urine samples were collected at the end of each period for nitrogen analysis and nitrogen balance calculation.

All feed, refusal, and fecal samples were oven-dried at 60 °C and ground through a 1-mm screen using a Cyclotec mill (Tecator, Höganäs, Sweden) prior to analysis. The samples were analyzed for dry matter (DM), nitrogen (N), ether extract (EE), and ash according to standard AOAC methods (AOAC, 2012). Acid detergent fiber (ADF) and Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) was determined according to the method of Van Soest et al. (1991), with the inclusion of alpha-amylase but without sodium sulfite, and is expressed inclusive of residual ash. Fecal and feed composition data were used to calculate nutrient digestibility. Urine samples were analyzed for nitrogen content using the Kjeldahl method (AOAC, 2012), and nitrogen intake was subsequently estimated.

Rumen fluid sampling and blood samples analysis

Rumen fluid samples were collected from each animal at 0 and 4 h post-feeding on the final day of each experimental period. Approximately 50 mL of rumen fluid was collected via a stomach tube connected to a vacuum pump. The pH was measured immediately using a digital pH meter (HANNA Instruments, HI 98,153 Microcomputer pH Meter). An additional 40 mL of rumen fluid was collected, to which 1 mL of 1 M H2SO4 was added per 10 mL of sample to halt microbial activity. The treated samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min, and the resulting supernatant (20–35 mL) was stored at −20 °C for subsequent analysis.

Ammonia-nitrogen (NH3—N) concentrations were determined using the distillation method described by Bremner (1960), and total nitrogen was measured using the 828 Series Combustion Method (LECO Corporation, USA). Total volatile fatty acids (TVFA), including major components acetic acid (C2), propionic acid (C3), and butyric acid (C4) were analyzed using gas chromatography (Nexis GC-2030, Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan). The protozoal population in the rumen was estimated via direct microscopic counting, following the method modified by Galyean (1989).

Blood samples (approximately 10 mL) were collected from the jugular vein at 0 and 4 h post-feeding on day 90 of the experiment. Blood chemistry was analyzed using a clinical chemistry analyzer (PKL PPC 125, Salerno, Italy), and routine hematological parameters were assessed using an automated hematology analyzer (Mindray BC-5000, Wanda Center, China).

Results and discussion

Chemical contents in the diets

Table 1 present the composition and chemical properties of concentrate diets in which yeast-fermented citric waste (YFCW) was used to replace soybean meal protein at levels of 0%, 33%, 66%, and 100%. The dry matter (DM) content across all diets remained relatively constant, ranging from 88.9% to 89.3%, indicating that the inclusion of YFCW did not significantly affect the moisture content of the feed. The crude protein (CP) content was 15.93% in the control diet (0% YFCW) and slightly decreased to 15.02% in the 100% replacement diet. This reduction aligns with the lower protein content of YFCW compared to soybean meal, but the CP level remained adequate to meet the nutritional requirements of beef cattle (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2016).

Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) contents slightly increased with higher levels of YFCW inclusion. Notably, ADF decreased from 14.81% to 14.10%, which may positively influence fiber digestibility in beef cattle. Gross energy (GE) tended to decrease from 4.04 Mcal/kg in the control diet to 3.97 Mcal/kg in the 100% replacement diet, possibly due to the lower energy value of YFCW compared to soybean meal.

These findings indicate that replacing soybean meal with YFCW led to minor changes in the chemical composition of the diets. CP levels slightly decreased but remained within the acceptable range for beef cattle. The slight increase in fiber content and the decrease in ADF may improve fiber digestibility (Van Soest et al., 1991). The observed reduction in gross energy is consistent with the report by Promkot and Wanapat (2003), which noted that agro-industrial by-products often have lower energy values than conventional protein sources like soybean meal.

Feed intake and digestibility

Table 2 displays the effects on feed intake and nutrient digestibility in Brahman cattle of substituting 0%, 33%, 66%, and 100% yeast-fermented citric waste for protein sources in concentrate diets. Both the daily intake of rice straw in kilograms and grams per metabolic body weight (BW0.75) did not differ significantly between treatments (P > 0.05). However, there was a significant (P < 0.01) decrease in concentrate intake, both in kilograms per day and grams per metabolic body weight, with a linear trend (P < 0.01). Nutrient intake, including dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), neutral detergent fiber (NDF), and acid detergent fiber (ADF), and total feed intake showed similar trends (P < 0.05). As YFCW replacement levels increased, there were notable decreases in nutrient digestibility, especially in DM, CP, NDF, and ADF digestibility (P < 0.05). The reduction in concentrate intake and nutrient digestibility observed with increasing levels of YFCW may be associated with changes in feed composition, including higher fiber content and the presence of residual fermentation products. However, as non-fermented citric acid residue was not evaluated in this study, it is not possible to determine whether these effects were due to the fermentation process or the inherent characteristics of the original material. The dramatic drop in concentrate intake could be explained by the YFCW's decreased palatability, which could be brought on by fermentation-derived ingredients like odor or built-up organic acids that impair cattle's appetite (Promkot & Wanapat, 2003; Wanapat et al., 2011).

The fact that the cattle continued to consume rice straw indicates that they made an effort to make up for it by consuming the same amount of energy overall. However, the notable reduction in the digestibility of important nutrients, particularly CP and fiber fractions (NDF and ADF), could be a result of poor rumen microbial degradation, perhaps brought on by modifications to the chemical structure of citric acid during fermentation (Beev et al., 2007; Sarnklong et al., 2010). Furthermore, protein binding with lignin or decreased intestinal availability of undegraded dietary protein (UDP) are possible outcomes of yeast fermentation (Harmon & Swanson, 2020).

Therefore, high YFCW levels might not be appropriate for use as the main source of protein in concentrate diets for beef cattle. Reduced palatability brought on by changed odor or accumulated fermentation acids may be the cause of the notable decreases in concentrate intake and nutrient supply with increasing replacement levels (Wanapat et al., 2024). The results of Forbes (2007), who found that variations in the flavor and odor of fermented feed ingredients affect cattle feed intake, are in line with this. Hanif and Sohail (2023) discovered that fermented agro-industrial residues frequently display complex structures or protein-lignin binding, which limits microbial digestion in the rumen. This is also consistent with the decreased digestibility of DM, CP, NDF, and ADF.

Rumen characteristics

The effects of substituting different amounts of yeast-fermented citric waste for soybean meal in the diets of beef cattle are shown in Table 3. The findings indicate that there was no significant difference in the ruminal pH between treatments at 0 and 4 h after feeding (P > 0.05), with values ranging from 6.52 to 6.74. These numbers are within the ideal range for rumen microorganism activity, particularly for bacteria that break down fiber. This is in line with research by Wanapat et al. (2013), which found that rumen pH should be between 6.2 and 7.0 for effective microbial digestion.

Similarly, the mean rumen temperature ranged from 39.1 to 39.5 °C before and after feeding, with no discernible differences (P > 0.05). According to Chanjula and Ngampongsai (2009), this range is thought to be appropriate for the survival and operation of rumen microbes. Increasing the amount of yeast-fermented citric waste had no negative effects on rumen temperature.

Protozoal populations showed a declining trend as yeast-fermented citric waste levels increased. The group that received 100% replacement had the lowest count (9.75 × 10⁴ cells/mL), while the control group had the highest count (13.75 × 104 cells/mL). However, the differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). As specific compounds in YFCW, such as organic acids or antimicrobial substances, were not measured in this study, the underlying mechanisms affecting protozoal populations cannot be clearly determined.

Overall, the findings imply that soybean meal in beef cattle diets can be completely replaced with yeast-fermented citric waste without negatively affecting rumen fermentation parameters. Additionally, it might aid in regulating protozoal populations, which are involved in the dynamics of the microbial ecology of the rumen.

Volatile fatty acid (VFA) concentration in the rumen

Table 4 shows the concentrations of total volatile fatty acids and the proportions of individual VFAs in Brahman cattle fed diets containing different levels of yeast-fermented citric waste replacing soybean meal. At 4 h post-feeding, the group that received 100% yeast-fermented citric waste exhibited the lowest total VFA concentration (99.74 mmol/L), whereas the control group (0%) had the highest value (116.57 mmol/L), with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05). A significant linear decreasing trend (P < 0.05) was observed with increasing levels of replacement, which may be attributed to changes in dietary composition that affect rumen microbial fermentation.

For acetic acid, the 100% replacement group also had the lowest concentration at 4 h post-feeding (67.82 mol/100 mol), while the control group showed a significantly higher value (78.77 mol/100 mol) (P < 0.05). This observed reduction in acetic acid production may be associated with changes in dietary fiber composition and fermentation characteristics resulting from YFCW inclusion. Diets with higher levels of YFCW may alter rumen microbial activity, particularly cellulolytic bacteria responsible for acetate production. (Suriyapha et al., 2021). Propionic acid levels tended to increase in the group receiving 66% replacement (21.15 mol/100 mol) compared to the 100% group (18.88 mol/100 mol), although the differences were not statistically significant (P > 0.05). Nevertheless, this result could have nutritional significance, as propionic acid serves as a precursor for gluconeogenesis in the liver of ruminants, contributing to fat deposition and growth performance (Wang et al., 2024). The proportion of butyric acid tended to decline in the groups receiving 66% and 100% replacement, with the 100% group showing the lowest value (10.12 mol/100 mol). This was significantly lower than the control group (12.19 mol/100 mol) (P < 0.05), and a significant linear decreasing trend was observed (P < 0.01).

The acetate-to-propionate ratio (A:P ratio) did not differ significantly among the treatment groups (P > 0.05), either before or after feeding. The ratio ranged from 3.55 to 3.85, which is considered normal and indicative of balanced rumen fermentation conditions (Lin et al., 2020).

However, changes in individual volatile fatty acids were observed. The reduction in butyric acid and the relative increase in propionic acid may be associated with shifts in rumen fermentation pathways. Propionate production is typically favored under conditions where readily fermentable substrates are available, whereas acetate and butyrate are more closely associated with fiber fermentation.

The inclusion of YFCW may have altered the chemical composition and fermentability of the diet, potentially influencing microbial activity and fermentation patterns in the rumen. In particular, changes in carbohydrate fractions and the presence of residual fermentation products may have contributed to a shift toward propionate production.

Concentrations of blood metabolite

Table 5 presents the effects of supplementing yeast-fermented citric waste in the concentrate diet on blood biochemical parameters in Brahman cattle. The results showed that replacing soybean meal with yeast-fermented citric waste at levels of 0, 33, 66, and 100% had no significant effect (P > 0.05) on average values of hemoglobin (Hb), hematocrit (Hct), red blood cell count (RBC), and white blood cell count (WBC), both before feeding (0 h) and 4 h after feeding. These findings are consistent with Ndlovu et al. (2007), who noted that blood parameters in cattle are generally stable and not easily affected by dietary supplements unless those supplements induce stress or nutritional imbalances.

However, significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed in certain red blood cell indices, including mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and red cell distribution width (RDW), particularly at 0 h post-feeding. The group receiving 66% replacement had the lowest MCV, while the control and 100% groups exhibited higher MCH and RDW values. These changes may reflect alterations in red blood cell quality, possibly related to the absorption of iron, vitamin B12, or folic acid from the fermented feed, all of which are essential for hemoglobin synthesis and red blood cell formation (Obeagu et al., 2024). The platelet count increased significantly (P < 0.05) in the 33% and 66% groups at both 0 and 4 h, whereas the control group showed the lowest values at 0 h, and the 100% group had even lower values at 4 h. This may indicate a potential role of immunostimulatory or bioactive compounds from yeast fermentation in stimulating platelet production in the bone marrow (Venturella et al., 2021).

For blood biochemical parameters, including fasting blood glucose (FBS) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), no significant differences were found among treatment groups (P > 0.05). This suggests that supplementation with yeast-fermented citric waste did not disrupt the energy balance or protein metabolism in the animals. These findings align with those of Vitor Ananias Shah et al. (2025), who reported that using fermentation by-products as energy and protein sources in cattle did not negatively impact animal metabolism.

Conclusions and recommendations

This study demonstrates that replacing soybean meal with yeast-fermented citric waste (YFCW) in the diets of Brahman cattle resulted in a significant reduction in concentrate intake, nutrient intake, and nutrient digestibility with increasing inclusion levels, particularly at 66% and 100% replacement. Despite these reductions, rumen fermentation characteristics, including pH and volatile fatty acid profiles, as well as blood biochemical parameters, were not significantly affected, indicating that YFCW does not adversely impact rumen function or animal health under the conditions of this study. The use of YFCW offers potential advantages as an alternative feed ingredient, including the utilization of agro-industrial by-products and the potential to reduce reliance on conventional protein sources. However, the main limitations observed in this study were reduced feed intake and nutrient digestibility at higher inclusion levels, which may negatively affect animal performance.
