Blue LED Light Attenuates the Bacterial Bioburden in Chronic Wounds Transiently but Dose‐Dependently
Authors: Frederik Plum, Aqella Rasul, Ewa Anna Burian, Claus Henrik Nielsen, Khaled S. A. Ghathian, Magnus S. Ågren, Katrine E. Karmisholt, Klaus Kirketerp‐Møller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhototherapy may improve chronic wound healing through different mechanisms. We have investigated whether escalating doses of blue LED light treatment (λ = 405 nm) impact the chronic wound microbiota. Twenty‐two patients with chronic wounds were sequentially assigned to a low dose (20 J/cm2, n = 7), medium dose (60 J/cm2, n = 8), or high dose (100 J/cm2, n = 7) of blue LED light. Bacterial counts, cytokine levels in wound fluid obtained by surface swab and wound size were determined at baseline. Four days after the baseline visit, wounds were irradiated six times with blue LED light at 2–3‐day intervals over 2 weeks. Bacteria were semi‐quantified just before and directly after each irradiation. Blue LED light reduced bacterial counts dose‐dependently (p < 0.0001) and the mean ± SD log10 reduction of colony‐forming units/swab from the six treatments was significantly larger for 60 J/cm2 (1.55 ± 0.33) and 100 J/cm2 (1.82 ± 0.22) compared with the 20 J/cm2 fluence (0.65 ± 0.095). The reductions of the 60 J/cm2 and 100 J/cm2 fluences did not differ (p = 0.180). Bacterial counts at follow‐up (p = 0.668), 4 days after the last irradiation and the levels of selected cytokines (IL‐1α, IL‐1β, IL‐6, GM‐CSF, TNF‐α and VEGF) did not change significantly from baseline. The linear healing rates from baseline to follow‐up did not differ (p = 0.815) among the three groups. In conclusion, blue LED light treatment resulted in an immediate but transient reduction of bacterial quantities in chronic wounds, with no apparent advantage to increasing the dose from 60 J/cm2. There was no significant effect on wound healing of blue light treatment.

Introduction

The prevalence of chronic wounds in Denmark is 1%, yet the treatment cost adds up to 2%–3% of the total healthcare budget [1]. Chronic wounds create ideal environments for bacterial growth and there is an urgent need for effective antibacterial treatments [2, 3, 4]. Phototherapy is gaining acceptance in wound management, as it may promote wound healing [5]. The biological response to this treatment is biphasic and follows the Arndt–Schulz law, meaning that small doses may stimulate, moderate doses may inhibit and large doses may be lethal [6].

Phototherapy has also been recommended for decontamination and antisepsis. The bacterial inactivation mechanism relies on photon‐absorbing chromophores such as porphyrins, as well as endogenous pigmented molecules like carotenoids, polyenes and catalase [7]. Chromophore photochemical reactions increase reactive oxygen species (ROS), which may damage vital bacterial structures [8]. The susceptibility varies among bacteria and is wavelength‐ and dose‐dependent [9].

Blue light (λ = 400–500 nm) as an antimicrobial therapy for chronic wounds is a subject of ongoing scientific discussion. Halstead et al. found that blue light exerted maximal antibacterial effects at 405 nm in vitro [10, 11]. In vivo, the effects of blue light at a dose of 56 J/cm2 were investigated in murine burn wounds inoculated with Pseudomonas aeruginosa . Blue light at this dose reduced bacterial growth and, strikingly, prevented the occurrence of lethal infections [12]. A blue LED light device was investigated in partial‐thickness skin wounds inoculated with methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus in pigs, where daily treatments (250 J/cm2) reduced the bioburden by 0–1.3 log10 CFU/g 30 min after each irradiation [13]. To the best of our knowledge, there are a limited number of studies on the effect of blue light therapy on the bacteriology of chronic wounds [14, 15, 16].

The question is whether host cells can tolerate blue light therapy while inactivating bacteria. The effects of blue light on human cells are also mediated through chromophores (tryptophan, cytochrome C complex, flavoproteins and opsins) generating ROS and releasing nitrogen oxide, ATP, cAMP and calcium. Opländer et al. determined the blue light action spectrum (410–484 nm) in human dermal fibroblasts and found that the shorter the wavelength, the more toxicity [17]. In endothelial cells and keratinocytes, blue light at 412–426 nm was cytotoxic from 33 J/cm2 [18].

Several processes in wound healing are mediated by cytokines. There is limited information on the effect of blue light on cytokine expression, although it can activate intracellular transcription factors [19, 20, 21]. In one in vitro study, blue light (λ = 470 nm) increased TNF‐α expression in keratinocytes [22].

The skin surrounding the wounds is inevitably illuminated during treatment and concerns have been raised that blue light reduces antioxidant levels in the skin [23]. Murine skin irradiated with blue light at 56 J/cm2 showed swollen basal keratinocytes and an edematous upper dermis in the acute phase, but these changes were reversible and absent 48 h after the irradiation [24]. A narrative review argues that phototherapy with blue light is safe when skin is irradiated with fluences up to 124 J/cm2 [25].

Our hypothesis is that blue light inactivates bacteria in chronic wounds and modulates cytokine levels, thereby promoting wound healing [26, 27, 28, 29]. Due to the uncertainty about what may be the optimal and safe dose, we compared the effects at three fluences of blue light (λ = 405 nm): 20, 60 and 100 J/cm2, referred to as a low, medium and high. As open wounds lack protective stratum corneum, 100 J/cm2 was chosen as the highest dose to maximise the inactivation of bacteria while avoiding adverse effects on the skin. The present report includes the effects on bacterial counts and strains, cytokine levels and wound healing.

Materials and Methods

The study was approved by the Scientific Ethics Committees for the Capital Region of Denmark (H‐2210527) and the Danish Medicines Agency (CIV DK‐22‐08‐040449). The protocol (NCT05739058) was released by ClinicalTrials.gov on February 2, 2023. The Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice and ISO standard DS/EN 14155 for medical devices were all complied with. This report complies with the CONSORT statement.

Participants

Patients were recruited from the outpatient clinic of the Copenhagen Wound Healing Center, Department of Dermato‐Venereology, University of Copenhagen, Copenhagen, Denmark and through Pleje.net (Danish Telemedicine). Eligible patients included men, women and non‐binary individuals over the age of 18 years, fulfilling all inclusion criteria. Patients must present with a chronic wound defined as ≥ 6 weeks old, with an area of ≥ 1 cm2 and ≤ 25 cm2. Furthermore, patients had to understand Danish, be able to comply with the protocol, be fully informed about the study and have given written informed consent. Patients were excluded if they had symptoms of clinical infection, locally or systemically, within 7 days, known or suspected cancer in the wound or previous photodermatitis, photosensitivity, porphyria, or hypersensitivity to porphyrins. Additionally, patients with congenital or acquired immunodeficiency, those who had new or adjusted treatment with immunomodulatory drugs within 4 weeks, those receiving haemodialysis and those diagnosed with dementia, as well as pregnant or breastfeeding women, were also excluded. Participation in wound healing studies in the last 30 days and a final judgement by the investigator that the patient was not suited for study participation, could also exclude the patient.

Intervention and Wound Temperature Measurements

The intervention device delivers continuous LED‐based blue light at a peak wavelength of 405 nm, with a full width at half maximum of 400–415 nm, emitted at 1000 Hz. The field irradiated by the device was kept constant at 10 cm2 at a distance of 6–7 cm. Each treatment session lasted 15 min. The device incorporated a ventilating air system to cool the wound and periwound surroundings, ensuring temperatures below 42°C. This was controlled every fifth min, during which the wound tissue temperature was measured with a non‐contact infrared thermometer (NC200, Microlife AG, Widnau, Switzerland). The LED device emitting blue light on a participant with a wound on an amputated lower leg.

Wound Management

Wounds were treated with neutral dressings and, when required, compression bandages. The wounds were cleansed with tap water and debrided when judged necessary by the investigators. Patients were allowed to change dressings themselves if needed. The use of antibiotics was not permitted.

Study Design

Assignment of Patients to Irradiation With Low, Medium and High Dose Blue Light

Patients were given an ID in the order they were enrolled; that is, the first patient was given ID 1, the second patient ID 2 and so on and the last patient being given ID 22. First, the patients with IDs 1–7 were assigned to the low‐dose (20 J/cm2) group, then the patients with IDs 8–15 received the medium dose (60 J/cm2) and finally, patients with IDs 16–22 were assigned to the high dose (100 J/cm2) (Figure 2A).

Study Flow

Each patient visited our clinic eight times; at their baseline visit, clinical data were collected, swabs from the wound surface were obtained for bacterial and cytokine analyses and wounds were photographed for wound area determination. Blue LED light was administered six times (V1–V6) over the next 2 weeks on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays. Bacterial swabs were taken just before and directly after each of the six blue LED light treatments. At two intermediate visits (V3 and V6) and at the follow‐up visit, swabs from the wound surface were taken for bacterial and cytokine analyses and wounds were photographed (Figure 2B).

Assessments

Swab From Wound Surface for Bacterial and Cytokine Analyses

ESwab (Copan, Brescia, Italy) was used to obtain material from the wound surface by the Essen Rotary method [30]. The swab for cytokine analyses was taken first [31]. The wound was then cleansed with a non‐woven swab moistened with tap water before the sampling for bacteria analysis (the wound was not cleansed after blue light irradiation at V1–V6).

Semi‐Quantification and Identification of Bacteria

Swabs were inserted into 1 mL of Amies transport medium and kept at 5°C in air‐tight tubes until processing, which was performed within 48 h [32, 33]. The swab was shaken for 5 s and 10 μL was transferred to each of three different culture plates: a 5% horse blood agar plate, a blue agar plate with a modified Conradi‐Drigalski substrate selective for Gram‐negative bacteria and finally, an anaerobe agar plate. The 5% horse blood agar plate was incubated at 35°C in 5% CO2/95% ambient air for 48 h, the blue agar plate at 35°C in ambient air for 24 h and the anaerobe agar plate at 37°C in an anaerobic incubator for 48 h. Bacterial growth was estimated semi‐quantitatively as 0 (no growth), 1 × 102 (log10 = 2), 1 × 103 (log10 = 3), 1 × 104 (log10 = 4), or ≥ 1 × 105 (log10 = 5) colony‐forming units (CFU)/swab for each bacterium. Bacteria were identified by Microflex matrix‐assisted laser desorption ionisation time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry with FlexAnalysis software (Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA). Correct identification by the FlexAnalysis software required scores of ≥ 2.0 for species level and ≥ 1.7 for genus level [34]. Bacterial bioburden was estimated by adding the individual bacterial CFU together for each wound. This method was benchmarked against the total counts, that is, disregarding the type of bacterium, obtained from 30 of the blood agar plates representative of the three dose groups using the semi‐quantitative scale. The results of the two methods correlated strongly (r = 0.95, p < 0.0001), as shown in Figure S2.

Cytokines

The swab was immediately inserted into a 1.5‐mL Eppendorf tube containing 300 μL of Tris‐buffered saline (pH 7.4) kept on ice [31]. The sample was vortexed twice and centrifuged (10 min, 8000 g at 4°C) and the cell‐free samples were kept in a research biobank at −82°C until analysis. A multiplex kit composed of the analytes IL‐1α, IL‐1β, IL‐6, GM‐CSF, TNF‐α and VEGF was used (Luminex Performance Assay, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). Analysis was performed in a 2× dilution and a 100× dilution, both analysed in duplicate simultaneously and the dilution containing the fewest out‐of‐range values for each cytokine was chosen. The cytokine concentration was divided by the total protein concentration, determined by a modified Lowry assay (DC Protein Assay, Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA). Bovine serum albumin was used as the standard and was serially diluted (0.196–1.49 mg/mL). Absorbances were measured by a microtiter plate spectrophotometer at 650 nm.

Wound Healing

Standardised digital pictures were taken by a Power Shot G11 camera (Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan) at a distance of 20–30 cm, with a mm‐ruler placed adjacent to the wound edge. The ImageJ (version 1.47) software was used to measure wound area (A) and perimeter (P) by two experienced investigators and the mean of the two measurements was used for the analysis [35]. The linear and continuous wound healing rates were determined. The linear wound healing rate per day was calculated using Gilman's equation D = ΔA/P¯ (D = Linear healing, ΔA = Change in wound area and P¯ = mean perimeter of the wound at baseline and follow‐up) and expressed in mm/day [36]. The continuous wound healing rate in mm/day was determined from the slope of the linear regression lines created from the four time points (baseline, V3, V6 and follow‐up) for each wound with respect to A/P and time [37].

Statistical Analyses

The sample size estimation was based on the safety outcome using conventional calculations of dichotomous outcomes in a single population (Supporting Information S1). The primary aim evaluating the clinical safety of the device did not raise any safety concerns and a detailed report is published elsewhere. Study data were collected and managed using RedCap electronic data capture tools hosted at Bispebjerg Hospital, Capital Region of Denmark, Denmark [38, 39]. The CSV files were imported into Excel (Microsoft 365) for descriptive statistics, SPSS Statistics (Version 29.0, IBM, Armonk, NY) for statistical analyses and Prism (Version 10.12, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA) was used for the creation of graphs. A one‐way ANOVA was used to compare peak wound temperature, log‐transformed bacterial counts and wound healing. A Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare cytokine concentrations. A two‐way ANOVA was used to compare baseline and follow‐up log‐transformed bacterial counts. The level of statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Patients and Blue LED Light Timeline

Recruitment began on 1 March 2023 and ended with the last patient's (ID 22) follow‐up visit on 1 May 2024.

Patients (n = 310) were contacted and informed about the study and 30 of these patients were invited for a baseline visit. A total of 22 patients were included in the study (Figure 2A). Patients were Caucasian (n = 21) and African (n = 1). Venous leg ulcers were the most common wound type (n = 10), followed by pressure ulcers (n = 4) and diabetic ulcers (n = 3). Wounds were primarily localised on the foot and the lower leg (n = 20). Age and BMI were similar across the three groups. The low‐dose group had wounds lasting the longest, with a mean of 174 weeks, followed by the medium group with 135 weeks and the high group with 57 weeks. At baseline, the wound sizes ranged from 0.9 to 11.5 cm2 (Table 1).

Effect of Blue LED Light Irradiation on Wound Bed Temperature, Bacteriology, Cytokines and Wound Healing

Wound Temperature

Wound bed temperatures remained below the predefined safety limit of 42.0°C over the 15‐min irradiation period, except for a single measurement of 42.8°C in the 100 J/cm2 group. This led to a temporary interruption of treatment, which was resumed after the temperature fell below 42.0°C. The course of mean wound temperatures over the 15‐min treatment period is shown in Figure 3. Mean peak temperatures in the low, medium and high dose groups were 35.9°C ± 0.45°C (mean ± SD), 36.0°C ± 0.45°C and 37.3°C ± 0.60°C. The peak temperature was significantly (p < 0.0001) higher in the high dose group compared with the 20 and 60 J/cm2 groups, which did not differ (p = 1.000) (Table S1).

Wound bed temperatures at 0, 5, 10, 15 min of blue light treatment in the low (n = 7), medium (n = 8), and high (n = 6) dose groups. Mean ± SEM.

Bacterial Counts

At baseline, before blue light treatment was started, the mean ± SD log10 CFU/swab was 4.23 ± 0.92 for the low group, 4.03 ± 0.71 for the medium group and 4.54 ± 0.41 for the high‐dose group.

Blue LED light irradiation reduced the bacterial counts at the three doses and the results for the 21 patients are shown in Figure S1. To estimate the overall effect and compare the effects of the three doses, the mean absolute change (Δ) in log10 CFU/swab of the six treatment sessions was analysed by one‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni's correction for multiple hypotheses (Table 2). These analyses showed a significant (p < 0.0001) dose‐dependent effect on log10 CFU/swab with the blue LED light therapy. The low dose of blue LED light reduced the bacterial counts by 0.65 ± 0.095 log10 CFU/swab, the reduction in the medium group was 1.55 ± 0.33 log10 CFU/swab and in the high dose group, it was 1.82 ± 0.22 log10 CFU/swab. The mean reduction of log10 CFU/swab did not differ between the medium and high groups (p = 0.180).

At follow‐up, the bacterial counts in the low group were 4.31 ± 0.37 log10 CFU/swab, 4.47 ± 0.36 log10 CFU/swab for the medium group and 4.34 ± 0.41 log10 CFU/swab for the high group. Bacterial Composition

At baseline (n = 21), 24 different bacterial strains were cultured: 20 aerobic strains and 4 anaerobes (Figure 4). S. aureus , occurring in 6 wounds (28.6%), was the most frequently isolated strain, followed by Enterococcus faecalis (19.0%) and P. aeruginosa (19.0%). At follow‐up (n = 21), after the blue light treatment session period had ended, 20 different bacterial strains were cultured and 2 of these were anaerobes. The most frequent were S. aureus (57.1%) and S. epidermidis (33.3%), followed by E. faecalis (19.0%) and P. aeruginosa (19.0%).

Cytokines

Cytokine levels were normalised to the total protein content of the swab extracts and are presented in Figure 5 and tabulated in Table S2. The changes in cytokine levels from baseline (before blue light treatment) to follow‐up were analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis test (Table S3). There were no statistically significant differences between the groups for any of the six examined cytokines; p‐values are reported in the legend of Figure 5.

Wound Healing Rates

Linear healing rates calculated from baseline to follow‐up were 0.0053 ± 0.13 mm/day in the low group, 0.033 ± 0.095 mm/day in the medium group and 0.00093 ± 0.074 mm/day in the high group. Subgroup analysis of venous leg ulcers treated with 60 and 100 J/cm2 had a linear healing rate of 0.012 ± 0.036 mm/day. The chronic wounds with the highest and lowest linear healing rates from each dose group are presented in Figure 6.

Chronic wounds with the highest and lowest linear healing rates from baseline to follow‐up in the intervention groups receiving 20, 60, or 100 J/cm2 of blue LED light. (A) ID6 arterio‐venous wound; (B) ID8 immunologic wound; (C) ID18 venous leg ulcer; (D) ID2 venous leg ulcer; (E) ID15 diabetic wound; (F) ID21 venous leg ulcer.

The continuous wound healing rate, extracted from the slope of the linear regression lines created from all data points from baseline to follow‐up, was 0.010 ± 0.059 mm/day for the low group, 0.029 ± 0.060 mm/day for the medium group and −0.014 ± 0.055 mm/day for the high group (Table S5). Neither the linear (p = 0.815) nor the continuous (p = 0.415) wound healing rates differed significantly among the three groups.

Discussion

Blue light phototherapy is a treatment with the potential to inactivate bacteria colonising chronic wounds. The antibacterial [12, 13, 40, 41, 42] and anti‐biofilm [10, 11, 43, 44, 45] activities of blue light are well‐documented in vitro, but there is no consensus on the optimal dose for the treatment of chronic wounds. Our study is the first to examine the dose–response behaviour of blue light in chronic wounds. The most striking finding was that there was no apparent additional benefit to increasing the fluence from 60 to 100 J/cm2 in reducing bacterial counts in chronic wounds of different etiologies. The antibacterial effect was photochemical and not photothermal.

Bacteria in chronic wounds are present as dispersed cells (planktonic) or as sessile cells, shielded in biofilms [46]. The proportions between the two growth modes vary. A meta‐analysis of nine studies reported that biofilms were present in 78.2% of 185 chronic wounds [47]. More in‐depth mechanistic studies are needed to distinguish between the two growth modes and would require biopsies for subsequent analyses using confocal laser scanning microscopy or scanning electron microscopy [48, 49], in addition to culture. Biopsies were not taken in the present study because that would severely jeopardise patient recruitment.

Instead, we chose a non‐invasive, semi‐quantitative bacteriological method of surface swabs, which correlated strongly with quantitative swab counts (Figure S2). It is worth mentioning that the wounds were swabbed using the Essen Rotary technique, which recovered more bacteria than conventional methods in one study [30]. Furthermore, van Hecke et al. concluded that quantitative swab counts can be used to estimate bacterial counts in biopsies [50].

The magnitude of bacterial reduction may be compared with that found in vitro. Blue light doses of 110–130 J/cm2 were required to reduce Gram‐positive and Gram‐negative vegetative bacteria by 1.0 log10 CFU/mL, compared with our 1.6–1.8 log10 CFU/mL = CFU/swab using 60 and 100 J/cm2 [9]. Our culture results represent a mixture of planktonic and biofilm‐derived bacteria, although planktonic cells will dominate due to the slower growth of biofilm bacteria. We can only speculate on the effectiveness of blue LED light against planktonic as opposed to biofilm bacteria. Possibly, the transient antibacterial effect reflects insufficient penetration of blue light into the biofilm matrix and the eradication of biofilm bacteria [45, 51]. Regardless, more frequent treatment with blue LED light could reduce bacterial counts further and have a more long‐lasting effect on the microbiota in chronic wounds.

Unexpectedly, the prevalence of S. aureus doubled after blue light treatment. A post hoc test (McNemar test) showed that this change was not statistically significant (p = 0.106). Two independent studies also found that S. aureus and P. aeruginosa were equally susceptible to blue light treatment [42, 52]. Nevertheless, it would be extremely informative to test the susceptibility of the strains isolated from chronic wounds to blue light in future studies.

We studied the changes in concentrations of six different cytokines before and after blue LED light treatment. These specific cytokines were selected based on their possible association with the wound healing of chronic venous leg ulcers from a recent systematic review [53]. There were no significant changes in any of the three fluence groups for the six cytokines. The cytokine measurements were carried out on wound fluid obtained by a surface swab; admittedly, although the technique has been validated, it is not known whether it reflects the wound tissue environment [31]. We compared our results for the venous leg ulcer subgroup with those of Beidler et al. [54], who measured cytokine concentrations in biopsies taken from venous leg ulcers using a similar analytical technique to ours (Table S4). Generally, the concentrations of the cytokines in wound fluid correlated well with those in tissue, with the exception of IL‐1β, which appeared to be overestimated in wound fluid compared to tissue (Figure S4). This may be explained by the fact that IL‐1β is only active in its processed, secreted form. Drinkwater et al. [55] found a significant but weak correlation between VEGF levels in venous leg ulcer tissues and wound fluids. Our measured VEGF levels in wound fluid were similar to theirs [55]. For future studies, TGF‐β1 could be measured [53], as blue light may activate latent TGF‐β1 with regenerative properties and, in addition, may upregulate host tissue antimicrobial peptides [56, 57, 58].

The effect of blue LED light on wound healing was expressed by the linear healing rate, which is independent of the initial size of the wound. The linear healing rate of 0.033 mm/day in the 60 J/cm2 group is within the expected range reported by other investigators [36, 59]. Ricci et al. reported that venous leg ulcers responded with faster healing after blue light therapy compared to other chronic wound types [15]. Venous leg ulcers accounted for half of our chronic wound patients, but the healing rate of this subgroup of patients did not show an obvious difference compared with the other etiologies. However, larger group sizes would be necessary to substantiate this finding.

Our dose‐escalation, non‐randomised study provides valuable preliminary data on blue light dosing; however, the major limitation is the absence of a control group. The inclusion of a control group would have clarified whether the lack of a sustained antibacterial effect was due to treatment failure or natural wound ecology dynamics. Ideally, the control group would receive sham irradiation by incorporating an opaque shield while maintaining the active cooling system.

In conclusion, treatment with blue LED light at 405 nm reduced the bacterial bioburden in chronic wounds transiently but dose‐dependently, with a fluence of 60 J/cm2 being sufficient. The potential of blue LED light phototherapy to reduce bacterial bioburden and promote wound healing would be worthwhile to pursue in larger‐scale randomised controlled trials using more frequent dosing of 60 J/cm2 and with an extended follow‐up period. Our present study may serve as a basis for future sample size calculations. For example, at least 13 patients in the control group and 13 patients in the medium dose group would be required to detect a log101.5 CFU/swab reduction with 95% power.
