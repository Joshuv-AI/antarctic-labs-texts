Antarctica’s ice cliff conundrum
Authors: Alexander A. Robel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Tall ice cliffs at a rapidly retreating glacier in West Antarctica may not be as vulnerable to runaway ice loss as previously thought.

THE PUZZLE OF HIGH SEA LEVELS

At the edges of Earth’s vast ice sheets, icebergs are born through the fracturing (or “calving”) of ice cliffs that rise high above the ocean surface. In 2012, Bassis and Walker (1) developed a theory that ice cliffs taller than a kilometer should calve rapidly under their own weight through shear failure, explaining why all ice cliffs on Earth are shorter than this height. They predicted that if tall ice cliffs become exposed in Antarctica or Greenland by the rapid disintegration of floating ice shelves, they could drive glacier retreat into the thicker ice sheet interior, causing even faster ice cliff failure, and further retreat. This positive feedback loop would later be called the marine ice cliff instability (hereafter MICI).

A few years later, Pollard et al. (2) hypothesized that the calving from such tall ice cliffs could explain why, during warm periods millions of years ago, sea levels could have been more than 17 m higher than today. Pollard and collaborators used two recent examples of rapid ice loss from two glaciers with tall ice cliffs (Greenland’s Sermeq Kujalleq and Antarctica’s Crane Glacier) to build a simple representation of ice cliff failure into a model of the Antarctic ice sheet, which then could simulate high sea level during these warm periods. In a later study (3), this same model predicted that under a future scenario of the highest human emissions of greenhouse gasses, MICI would be initiated in the next century in West Antarctica, causing global mean sea level rise that was more than twice almost all other prior projections for future sea level rise. This study (and an updated study in 2021 by the same authors) widely influenced many and became the basis for the “worst-case scenario” in future sea level projections from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (4) and NOAA (5), and coastal planning guidance issued by the cities of Miami, Boston and the state of California.

REVISITING THE FATE OF THWAITES

Starting in 2018, the US National Science Foundation and the UK Natural Environment Research Council mounted a joint $50 million (USD) research effort, the International Thwaites Glacier Collaboration (ITGC), to measure and model the changes occurring at Thwaites Glacier, a rapidly changing, Florida-sized glacier in West Antarctica. A first step in this direction was taken by Crawford et al. (6) in an ITGC-funded study that used a high-fidelity, but computationally expensive, model to explicitly resolve ice flow and fracture down to a scale of meters. They found that the maximum cliff height was higher than predicted under the much simpler assumptions made by Bassis and Walker (1), but that beyond this height, ice cliff failure rates rapidly increased to levels well beyond those previously considered possible.

In this issue of Science Advances, Morlighem et al. (7) report on the culmination of ITGC-funded work to investigate whether Thwaites Glacier is susceptible to rapid ice loss via MICI as predicted by DeConto and Pollard (3). They added a simple but updated representation of ice cliff failure rates from Crawford et al. (6) to three different ice sheet models. Initial simulations begin from the current state of Thwaites Glacier, instantaneously remove all floating ice, and simulate the next century of evolution of Thwaites Glacier. Despite using model parameters that should produce vigorous cliff failure, the ice cliff retreats very little over the 100 years of simulation in all three models. The exposed ice cliff is only tall enough to fail at a few places, thus preventing substantial retreat of the ice cliff. In further simulations, Thwaites Glacier is forced to retreat at current rates (in kilometers per year) for the next 50 years before the ice shelf is removed, exposing a much taller ice cliff that fails at many locations. This is more akin to the simulations of DeConto and Pollard (3) where ice shelf collapse at Thwaites late in the 21st century initiates MICI. However, again in all three models, the ice cliff retreats very little. They show that calving at the ice cliff would need to be at least 25 times higher than suggested by the high-fidelity model of Crawford et al. (6) for MICI to occur at Thwaites.

In general, the rate of ice cliff retreat is set through a balance between calving, which removes ice from the cliff, and ice flow which both brings ice to the cliff from upstream and can cause thinning of the cliff through acceleration (Fig. 1). Besides Crawford et al. (6), two other recent studies using different high-fidelity models of ice fracture and calving (8, 9) show that the rate of ice cliff failure is set by a more complex set of factors than previously considered. Morlighem and collaborators (7) put all these puzzle pieces together, using a more realistic representation of ice cliff failure, and resolving changes in ice flow down to a much finer scale than in prior studies. These advances proved to be the critical difference for their main finding that if the ice shelf of Thwaites collapses within the next 50 years, MICI and the associated runaway rapid ice loss are unlikely to occur in this vulnerable region of West Antarctica.

WHERE TO GO FROM HERE

In the past several decades, glaciologists have grappled with the challenge of using ice sheet models to explain why global sea levels were much higher during periods when global temperatures were only slightly higher than today. The MICI provided one potential hypothesis, but as models of ice cliff failure improve, they appear to indicate that the rates of retreat observed at Sermeq Kujalleq and Crane are less likely to occur at large and rapidly changing glaciers such as Thwaites.

The intense focus on MICI has driven incredible innovations in very localized modeling of ice fracture and calving (6, 8, 9), which may still partly explain some periods of rapid ice loss. Alternately, the intrusion of warm seawater or ice sheet surface melt under ice sheets may substantially increase the response of marine ice sheets to climate change through increased melting and slipperiness (10, 11). Fundamentally, our capacity to explain and predict sea level rise in the past and future is inextricably connected to the remaining gaps in understanding how ice breaks, flows, and melts. However, the flurry of exciting new advances around these challenging problems provides hope that we are getting closer to reliable projections for these critical reservoirs of ice.
