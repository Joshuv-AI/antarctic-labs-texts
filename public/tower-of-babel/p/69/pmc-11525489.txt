Structural, Optical, and Magnetic Properties of Brownmillerite KBiFe2O5 and KBiFe1.95Ti0.05O5+ via Reactive Templated Method
Authors: Peshal Karki, Rajasekhar Bhimireddi, Lokeswararao Dhavala, Anupama A V, Apparao M. Rao, Raja Karreddula, Anees A. Ansari
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Brownmillerite KBiFe2O5 (KBFO) and KbiFe1.95Ti0.05O5+ (KBFTO) ceramics were synthesized using the prereacted nanopowders of KFeO2 (KFO) and BiFeO3 (BFO), and KFO and BiFe0.95Ti0.05O3+ (BFTO), respectively, via the reactive templated method. The powder X-ray diffraction patterns confirmed the monoclinic phase of the KBFO and KBFTO samples. The incorporation of Ti4+ at Fe3+ site prevented the formation of a secondary phase (Bi2Fe4O9) in the KBFTO sample. Our experimentally determined lattice parameters for the orthorhombic P2/c KBFO and KBFTO are consistent with our density functional theory (DFT) computed lattice parameters (within a 5% error). Scanning electron microscopy images revealed an average grain size of ∼1.38 and 0.85 μm for KBFO and KBFTO, respectively. The calculated optical band gaps of the KBFO and KBFTO using the Kubelka–Munk function from their UV data were found to be 1.69 and 1.71 eV, respectively. The measured surface area of KBFTO (1.339 m2 g–1) obtained from BET analysis was double that of KBFO (0.698 m2 g–1). KBFTO’s room-temperature magnetization was 0.986 emu g–1, which was twice that of the KBFO, while its room-temperature conductivity measured using impedance spectroscopy was 5.69 × 10–3 S m–1 which was 10 times that of KBFO. Notably, our DFT calculations showed that the small Ti dopant concentration enhanced the partial density of states (PDOS) above the Fermi level. It shifted the Fermi level toward higher energy, and the oxygen PDOS for KBFTO was significantly enhanced relative to KBFO, which ultimately helped improve the conductivity.

Introduction

The research on multifunctional materials like multiferroics is growing extensively because of their application not only in making advanced devices like sensors, memory devices, spintronics etc. but also in addressing energy problems of today’s world by taking advantage of their photovoltaic properties to make solar cells.1,2 The photovoltaic property is because of their effective carrier separation caused by ferroelectric polarization and above-band gap photoinduced voltages.3−5 The photoinduced voltage in conventional ferroelectrics is based on the polarization-induced internal electric field. It is a bulk effect, and its application is limited because of the short life span of photoinduced charge carriers and the large band gap (3–6) eV.2 Therefore, a more ordered material, such as a multiferroic material in which the magnetic order parameter or intrinsic elastic strain may lower the band gap, has more scope in photovoltaic applications, e.g., the solar spectrum. However, it is always incredibly challenging to design a new multiferroic material with improved performance because of its multiple properties (such as magnetic, electrical, and optical) that come into play simultaneously.

Zhang et al. synthesized a novel low band gap (Eg ∼ 1.6 eV)6 KBiFe2O5 multiferroic (KBFO) and proposed it as a promising material for optoelectronics and solar energy devices. With four molecules per unit cell and a brownmillerite-derived structure, its orthorhombic symmetry is part of the P21cn space group. The structure consists of tetrahedral Fe3+ containing [Fe2O3] and [(K, Bi)O2] blocks in an alternate fashion, which are connected by sharing corner O via tetrahedral FeO4 and octahedral BiO6. The KBFO structure differs from other brownmillerite compounds because it lacks iron octahedra, which helps lower the oxygen coordination, leading to a low band gap.6 Still, its low conductivity hinders the charge carriers’ transport.7 Many efforts in developing novel and viable techniques to synthesize KBFO or doping different rare earth ions into the KBFO structure are in vogue to improve the performance.

Jalaja and Dutta8 reported a 2.5 enhancement in remanent polarization for KBFO- PVA composite films (Pr = 3.7 μC cm–2) compared to pure KBFO thin films (Pr = 1.45 μC cm–2). Zhang et al.(9) observed that the polarized high-pressure phase exhibits enhanced ferroelectric, electric transport, and photoelectric properties. Zhang et al.(10) explored the impact of calcination temperature on KBFO synthesized through a solid-state reaction route and suggested that the ideal calcination temperature window is between 1023 and 1073 K. Zhai et al.(11) prepared the KBFO thin films on the quartz substrate via chemical deposition method and suggested that the 750 °C was the suitable annealing temperature to achieve the improved physical properties of KBFO thin films. Rai and Molli12 investigated the effects of the chelating agent on the physical properties of KBFO nanomaterials prepared via a sol–gel technique. The KBFO synthesized using tartaric acid as a chelating agent exhibited better physical properties than the KBFO nanomaterial synthesized using other chelating agents, such as citric acid, maleic acid, and sucrose. The same research team also observed the high magnetization values for the 15 mol % La13 and 15 mol % Y14-doped KBFO nanomaterials compared to pure KBFO nanomaterials. Melo et al.(7) prepared the nanofibers of KBFO by electrospinning, which exhibited a paramagnetism of 0.23 emu g–1 at 10 kOe, along with a 1.72 eV optical band gap. Unlike orthorhombic KBFO, monoclinic KBFO is stable at high temperatures, which is a crucial characteristic for many applications.15

In this work, we prepared the monoclinic phases of KBFO and KBFTO via the reactive templated method for the first time and investigated their structural, optical, and magnetic properties. The main objective of this work is to improve important properties, such as magnetization and conductivity, of KBFO, which is a novel multiferroic material. As mentioned above, previous studies used hydrothermal, sol–gel, or solid-state synthesis methods to prepare KBFO and reported its physical properties. It is well-known that the physical properties of the materials are greatly influenced by the methodology adopted for the preparation of materials.16−18 For instance, Sr2Bi4Ti5O18 prepared by the reactive templated method exhibited superior physical properties than those prepared by the simple solid-state reaction method.16 Hence, in this investigation, we adopted the reactive templated technique to prepare KBFO and KBFTO nanomaterials and report their attained physical properties. Moreover, no reports are available on incorporating Ti4+ at the Fe3+ site in KBFO using any of the published methods (sol–gel, hydrothermal, solid state, coprecipitation, or the reactive templated method). Therefore, this study will be useful for the scientific community in developing KBFO further. With this aim, we synthesized and investigated the structural and physical properties of KBFO and KBFTO samples without the presence of the Bi2Fe4O9 secondary phase. It is clearly seen that doping KBFO with Ti results in a higher magnetization than dopants such as Y14 and Co.19 The Eg values for KBFO and KBFTO were found to be 1.69 and 1.71 eV, respectively, and are lower than that obtained for Ru (>2.25 V),29 Y (≥1.75 V),14 and 5% La (1.75 V)13 dopants. A plot of conductivity showed that KBFTO exhibits at least 10 times higher conductivity than KBFO. We also verified this experimental finding using the first-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations.

Experimental Procedure

The KBFO and KBFTO ceramics were synthesized using the prereacted nanopowders of KFeO2, BiFeO3, and BFTO via the reactive templated technique. The starting materials of the analytical grade (>99% purity) were used in our synthesis, viz., Bi(NO3)3·5H2O, Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, CH3CO2K, C12H28O4Ti, NH4OH, and C6H8O7. The key steps involved in preparing KFO, BFO, BFTO, KBFO, and KBFTO are summarized in Figure 1. To prepare BFTO nanopowders, C12H28O4Ti (0.05 mol) was first mixed with C5H8O2 (0.05 mol) for 10 min, and then C6H8O7 (0.05 mol) was added with continuous stirring using a magnetic stirrer. Appropriate amounts of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O (1.0 mol), Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (1.95 mol), and C6H8O7 (2.95 mol) were dissolved in water [3 wt % of HNO3 was used for Bi(NO3)3·5H2O] and also added separately to the above solution and stirred for 1 h at room temperature. Next, Bi(NO3)3·5H2O (1.0 mol) solution (3 wt % HNO3 in 10 mL H2O) was added to the above solution with continuous stirring. Finally, 10 mL of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O (1.95 mol) and 10 mL of chelating agent [C6H8O7 (2.95 mol)] solutions were also added to the above solution with continuous stirring, and the pH of the solution was adjusted to ∼7 using the 34% NH4OH solution at constant stirring. The temperature of the solution was raised to 403 K to evaporate the water, and as a result, the gel was formed after 8 h. Next, the temperature was further raised to 473 K, and the gel was turned into a powder at this stage. The obtained BFTO powder was ground with a mortar and pestle to homogenize into a fine powder, which was heat-treated at 723 K for 3 h to remove the organic residues. Similarly, the KFO and BFO nanopowders were also prepared by using the citrate-assisted sol–gel method with appropriate amounts of starting materials. The nanopowders (KFO and BFO for KBFO and KFO and BFTO for KBFTO) were weighed separately in stoichiometric proportions and mixed with acetone for 20 min. These powders were pressed into pellets (∼1–2 mm in thickness with a diameter of 10 mm) by applying 2.5 tons of pressure for 3 min and then heat-treated at various temperatures for 2 h. These pellets were crushed into powder samples and characterized in detail.

Flowchart for the preparation of BFO, KFO, and BFTO nanopowders and KBFO and KBFTO compounds.

The sample’s crystallinity and phase purity were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (PANalytical, X’Pert3 Powder Cu-Kα radiation). The XRD measurements were performed using the sweeping rate, voltage, and current of 2°/min, 45 kV, and 40 mA, respectively. The Bragg peaks in the resultant XRD patterns were identified by referring to the ICSD database (FIZ Karlsruhe, Germany). Both samples’ (KBFO and KBFTO) XRD patterns were refined using the Rietveld method20 employing the program FullProf21 to extract the crystal structural parameters, such as the unit cell parameters, atomic site occupancies, etc., from raw diffraction data. The crystallite size was calculated for the constituent phases using the Scherrer and Williamson–Hall methods,22 whereas the lattice strain was estimated using the Williamson–Hall method. A scanning electron microscope (JEOL JSM IT300LV) was used to analyze the microstructure of the samples. BET analysis was carried out to find the surface area of the samples. Diffuse reflectance spectra were recorded using an UV–vis NIR spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer Lambda 750 UV/vis/NIR spectrophotometer) for the samples in the wavelength range 200–800 nm. The magnetic properties were measured by using a vibrating sample magnetometer (Model EZ9, MicroSense, USA). The electrical conductivity of each pellet was recorded for a wide range of frequencies at room temperature by using an impedance analyzer (WAYNE KERR, 6500B).

Computational Details

We employed a first-principle DFT calculation using Quantum ESPRESSO23,24 to compute the magnetic moment and partial density of states (PDOS) of KBFO and KBFTO monoclinic crystals. In our computation, a Fe atom was replaced by a Ti atom in a 2 × 2 × 2 KBFO supercell to obtain the KBFTO structure. We used projector-augmented wave-type pseudopotential for K, Fe, T, and O atoms and ultrasoft pseudopotential for Bi atom with generalized gradient approximation applying Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof as exchange–correlation functional. The energy cutoff for the wave function and charge are 85 and 850 Ry, respectively. Both structures were variable cell relaxed until the convergence threshold for self-consistency was less than 3.6 × 10–9 Ry with all component forces less than 10–4 Ry/au.

The crystallite sizes for the intended phases of KBFO and KBFTO samples by the Debye–Scherrer method [d = K*λ/(β*cos θ), where d, λ, β, θ, and K are average crystallite size, X-ray wavelength, reflection width or full width at half-maximum, Bragg’s angle, and shape factor, respectively] are 68.2 and 57.4 nm, respectively. This shows that the incorporation of higher valent (+4) Ti in place of (+3) Fe induces lattice strain within the intended phase of the sample. However, large differences in crystallite sizes are not seen because the monoclinic phase accommodates the substituent cation by slight distortion in its crystal structure (note that the cell parameters and interaxial angle β have changed slightly from the KBFO sample to the KBFTO sample). The crystallite sizes of intended phases within KBFO and KBFTO samples also showed a similar trend when calculated using the Williamson–Hall method (β*cos θ = K*λ/d + 4*ε*sin θ, where d, λ, β, θ, K, and ε are average crystallite size, X-ray wavelength, reflection width or full width at half-maximum, Bragg’s angle, shape factor, and lattice strain in the crystal, respectively), as shown in Figure 3c,d. The crystallite size (d) was calculated from the y-intercept, and the lattice strain (ε) was estimated from the slope. These two intended phases showed a significant decrease in lattice strain: 0.49 × 10–3 for the KBFTO phase compared to 0.54 × 10–3 for the KBFO phase, which indirectly explains the incorporation of Ti4+ in the host Fe3+ site of the monoclinic KBFO structure.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were collected on the surface of the pellets to gain insights into the surface morphology of the KBFO and KBFTO samples (Figure 4a,b). The SEM images showed that the KBFO and KBFTO grain sizes differed. Grains of the KBFO sintered at 973 K/2 h showed regular morphologies with sizes between 0.27 and 2.1 μm (Figure 4c). However, the grains of KBFTO sintered at 973 K/2 h were agglomerated, and a few of the well-defined grains ranged from 0.20 to1.4 μm in size (Figure 4d), which is smaller than that of the KBFO. ImageJ software calculated the average grain sizes of KBFO and KBFTO well-defined particles/grains as 1.38 and 0.85 μm, respectively. The elements present in each sample were also confirmed by the EDX spectrum (Figure 4e,f). With Archimedes’ principle, the densities of the sintered KBFO and KBFTO pellets were measured using xylene (room temperature density ∼0.86 g cm–3) as the standard liquid medium. The relative densities of KBFO and KBFTO sintered (973 K/2 h) pellets were found to be 88 and 90%, respectively. Furthermore, the BET analysis of KBFO and KBFTO revealed their specific surface areas as 0.698 and 1.339 m2 g–1, respectively.

Figure 4g shows the recorded Raman spectra of KBFO and KBFTO ceramics at room temperature. According to group theory,9,15 the predicted Raman active mode for the monoclinic phase of KBFO is 27. However, the observed KBFO and KBFTO modes were 19, which is more than the previously reported modes (14 modes) by Chandrakanta et al.(19) All the observed Raman modes are assigned according to the literature reported monoclinic phase of KBFO ceramic10 with an average grain size of 2.1 μm. Thus, the observed Raman modes at 47, 56, 76, and 83 cm–1 are responsible for the Bi–O–Fe and Fe–O–Fe lattice vibrations. The mode at 260 (KBFO)/259 (KBFTO) cm–1 is due to the Fe–O–Fe/Ti bond stretching, and modes between 190 and 567 cm–1 are mainly due to the FeO4 molecule. The translational movement of the whole tetrahedral molecule is assigned to the mode at 190 cm–1, and modes at 260, 309, 336, and 409 cm–1 are due to bending of the FeO4 tetrahedral molecule. The modes at 382 and 645 cm–1 are due to the symmetric stretching modes of Bi–O–Bi and Fe–O, respectively. However, an asymmetric stretching mode of the Fe–O molecule is assigned at 567 cm–1. A shift of lower frequency (3–5 cm–1) modes was observed when compared to that of literature-reported9,10 modes of KBFO ceramic due to grain size differences (∼0.72 μm).

Magnetic Properties

To estimate the magnetization values for the sintered ceramic at 973 K/2 h of KBFO and KBFTO samples, the magnetization vs applied magnetic field (M-H) curves were measured using a maximum applied field 18 kOe at room temperature (Figure 5a). Both samples showed the S-type hysteresis, indicating a weak ferromagnetic long-range ordering. The presence of Ti4+ in place of Fe3+ improved the magnetic performance of the sample. The magnetization showed no saturation even at 18 kOe, which may be due to the particles’ canted spin, which hinders the ordering of the spins in the direction of the applied field.10 Coercivity is very low for both samples, whereas the remanent magnetization is higher for KBFTO. The observed magnetic behavior in the KBFO ceramic is due to the presence of Fe3+ ions in tetrahedral sites but not the presence of K+ and Bi3+ because these are nonmagnetic. Magnetism in these ceramics is due to the exchange interaction of local spin-polarized electrons and conduction electrons. In KBFO, this interaction leads to the spin-polarization of Fe3+ conduction electrons, which in turn interact with the local spin-polarized electrons of Fe ions. This long-range exchange interaction finally orders all moments along a particular direction, producing net magnetization.13,14,25,26 The KBFO exhibited weak ferromagnetism due to spin canting of G-type antiferromagnetic ordering,6 concluding that the improved magnetism in KBFTO is due to the modified canting by Ti4+ ions. The obtained magnetization (Ms) and coercive field (Hc) values for KBFO and KBFTO were 0.443 and 0.986 emu g–1 at 0.066 and 0.070 kOe, respectively, at 18 kOe (Figure 5a). A comparison of the PDOS of KBFO and KBFTO (Figure 6) shows that introducing Ti into KBFO greatly influences the PDOS of O, making the O electrons dominant near the valence band. As a result, the exchange interaction is enhanced due to the interaction between the Fe and O local spin-polarized electrons. This is attributed to being the primary cause of the large magnetization of KBFTO in an external magnetic field. However, in the absence of a magnetic field, the ground-state DFT calculation showed no improvement in intrinsic magnetization, as listed in Table 3, because of the nonmagnetic nature of Ti.

Optical Properties

The room-temperature optical reflectance spectrum of the KBFO and KBFTO ceramics powders was recorded in the 200–800 nm range using an UV–vis–NIR spectrophotometer. The direct optical band gaps (Eg) of the KBFO and KBFTO were estimated using the Tauc plots, as shown in Figure 5b inset. The band gaps were obtained from the tangents of the (hν × FKM)2 vs hν plots, where h is the plank constant, ν is the frequency, and FKM is the Kubelka–Munk function. The Eg values were found to be 1.69 and 1.71 eV for KBFO and KBFTO, respectively, and are lower than that obtained for Ru (>2.25 V),29 Y (≥1.75 V),14 and 5% La (1.75 V)13 dopants. The direct band gap of KBFTO is obtained from the energy difference of the 2p orbitals of the polarized O, which occupy most of the top of the valence band, whereas the 3d orbitals of the polarized Fe constitute the bottom of the conduction band as seen in the PDOS (Figure 6). Here, the slight increase in the band gap in KBFTO over KBFO may be due to the Burstein–Moss effect.14,30

The impedance analysis was also carried out to determine the conductivity of the KBFO and KBFTO ceramics (Figure 5c). The conductivity of both samples increases with an increase in frequency, and the observed conductivity of KBFTO is 10 times higher than that of KBFO, which is attributed to Ti4+ in place of the Fe3+ ion. The conductivities at 1 and 10 kHz for the KBFO are 6.11 × 10–4 and 6.11 × 10–4 S m–1, while for KBFTO, they are 5.29 × 10–3 and 5.69 × 10–3 S m–1, respectively. Our DFT calculations (Figure 6) show that the Ti states are mostly above the Fermi level (inset in Figure 6b), explaining this increase in conductivity because of the interaction of Ti states with other atomic states. This interaction not only increased the DOS near the Fermi level but also raised the Fermi energy from 8.07 eV in KBFO to 8.23 eV in KBFTO.

Potential Applications

It has been proved that the KBFO has good photocatalytic performance because of its robust magnetic and optical properties.6,9,27,28,31 Our study demonstrated that the novel KBFTO has a similar optical band gap, better magnetization, and higher-surface area than KBFO. This shows that KBFTO can outperform as a visible range photocatalyst for processes such as organic breakdown. Another potential application where KBFTO can be widely used is in photovoltaic applications. Because of its suitable band gap and higher conductivity than its parent KBFO and other species doped KBFO, as explained in the “Optical Properties” section, KBFTO can be a commercial candidate for solar energy harvesting devices. Other suitable areas include magnetic sensors and memory devices.

Conclusions

KBFO and KBFTO ceramics were successfully fabricated using the reactive templated method at 973 K/2 h. The presence of a secondary phase (Bi2Fe4O9) was detected through XRD analysis in KBFO; however, it was absent in the KBFTO sample due to the incorporation of Ti4+ at the Fe3+ site in the KBFO sample. The grains of the KBFO compounds sintered at 973 K/2 h show regular morphologies with sizes between 0.27 and 2.1 μm. However, the KBFTO sintered at the same temperature showed agglomerated grains with sizes in the 0.20–0.1.4 μm range. The decrease in average grain size of KBFTO relative to KBFO is due to the incorporation of Ti4+ at the Fe3+ site of KBFO, which further led to an increase in the surface area from 0.698 to 1.339 m2 g–1 and an increase in relative density by ∼2%. The maximum magnetization value obtained for the KBFTO ceramic was 0.986 emu g–1, which was double that of KBFO (0.443 emu g–1). The KBFTO also showed conductivity of 5.69 × 10–3 S m–1, which was 10 times higher than that of KBFO. The conductivity increase is attributed to Ti states above the Fermi level interacting with other atoms’ states, as revealed by our DFT calculations. With a smaller grain size, longer duration stability, high-surface area, comparatively close optical band gap to the parent compound, and higher magnetization and conductivity values, KBFTO is superior to KBFO and other types of doped KBFO for many applications such as magnetic sensors, memory devices, photocatalysts for organic breakdown processes, and most importantly in photovoltaic cell.
