Effect of a Multidimensional Digital Health Intervention on Quality of Life in Breast Cancer Survivors: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Maria-Angeles Fuentes-Expósito, Santiago Frid, Montserrat Muñoz-Mateu, Immaculada Grau-Corral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSEWe evaluated whether offering access to a multicomponent mHealth app improves quality of life (QoL) and psychosocial outcomes among breast cancer survivors under pragmatic, nonprescriptive conditions.METHODSIn this single-center, randomized, controlled trial at Hospital Clínic de Barcelona, women age ≥18 years, disease-free after breast cancer treatment, were recruited (December 2020-December 2021) and randomly assigned 1:1 to usual follow-up plus app access or usual follow-up alone. The app provided CTCAE v4.03-aligned symptom tracking with self-care guidance, educational content, an events calendar, and gamified smartphone-based step counting; no protocolized clinician monitoring or feedback was provided. Outcomes were assessed at baseline and 3, 6, 9, and 12 months using European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer-Quality of Life Questionnaire (QLQ)-C30/BR23, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), and Three-Item Loneliness Scale (TILS). The primary end point was the difference in QLQ-C30 Global Health Status/QoL at 3 months. Analyses followed intention-to-treat using mixed models for repeated measures adjusted for baseline values.RESULTSOf 124 women assessed, 121 were randomized (intervention n = 60; control n = 61). Patient-reported outcome measures were available for 106 of 121 (87.6%) at 3 months and 95 of 121 (78.5%) at 12 months. At 3 months, there was no significant difference in Global Health Status/QoL (adjusted mean difference [Intervention-Control], -2.24 [95% CI, −9.29 to 4.81]; P = .53); estimates at later time points were similarly imprecise. No significant between-group difference were observed for QLQ-BR23 domains, HADS anxiety/depression, or TILS. Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested possible heterogeneity in TILS by hormonal-treatment category; this was descriptive and hypothesis-generating only. App engagement was the highest in months 0-3 (48/60 [80.0%] with any use) and declined thereafter; 12 of 60 (20.0%) never used the app.CONCLUSIONIn a pragmatic, nonprescriptive survivorship trial, offering access to a multicomponent mHealth app without closed-loop clinical integration did not show a statistically significant between-group differences in QoL or psychosocial outcomes; confidence intervals were compatible with meaningful harm and did not exclude small benefit depending on the threshold used to define clinical relevance.

INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women, and the number of survivors living with long-term physical and psychosocial sequelae continues to increase. In Spain, approximately 34,750 new cases are diagnosed annually, contributing to a substantial survivorship burden.1 As health systems adopt value-based care models, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are increasingly used to monitor quality of life (QoL), symptom burden, and psychosocial well-being and to guide follow-up care.2

Despite routine follow-up, many survivors experience persistent symptoms and psychosocial concerns—such as fatigue, pain, or anxiety—that may remain under-recognized between visits, supporting the potential role of PROM-enabled digital monitoring in survivorship care.

Mobile health (mHealth) tools may support survivorship through symptom tracking, education, and self-management support. However, most breast cancer digital interventions have been evaluated during active treatment, with fewer randomized controlled trials in survivorship during endocrine/targeted therapy or routine surveillance. Sustaining engagement remains a key challenge,3-5 and real-world benefits remain heterogeneous.6-11

Within the European ASCAPE project, our center deployed a multicomponent mHealth app for breast cancer survivors, enabling secure storage and linkage of patient-generated data within the institutional electronic health record infrastructure.12,13 Qualitative research in the same ecosystem identified ongoing informational and self-management needs extending into survivorship.14 During the trial, app-reported data did not trigger protocolized clinical actions.

We conducted a randomized controlled trial to evaluate whether offering access to a multicomponent mHealth app in addition to usual follow-up was associated with differences in global health status/QoL at 3 months (primary end point) and over 12 months. Secondary objectives were to assess breast cancer–specific QoL, anxiety, depression, and loneliness; describe real-world engagement; and explore prespecified effect heterogeneity by socioeconomic context and hormonal treatment.

METHODS

Study Design and Setting

This was a prospective, longitudinal, randomized controlled trial conducted at Hospital Clínic de Barcelona (HCB), a tertiary academic hospital, within the European ASCAPE project. Participants were recruited between December 2020 and December 2021 and followed for 12 months, with assessments at baseline and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. The study was initially designed as a multicenter trial but was conducted exclusively at HCB because of COVID-19–related organizational constraints.

Figure 1 summarizes the Barcelona pilot data flow, including secure storage of patient-generated data from the study app in ontology-based repository (ONTO-CR) and linkage with electronic health record data and PROMs. Artificial intelligence components from ASCAPE were not active during the trial.

The evaluated app remained functionally stable throughout the study period, although an Android step-counter correction during follow-up makes step-count analyses exploratory.

Technical support was available on request for installation or functionality issues. No behavioral coaching or clinical feedback was provided, and push notifications were generic and nonpersonalized.

Usual follow-up at HCB consisted of routine oncology visits, access to standard educational materials, and referral pathways for symptom management. No additional digital monitoring or protocolized symptom review was provided to either group.

Participants

Eligible participants were women age ≥18 years with a prior diagnosis of early-stage breast cancer, no evidence of active disease at enrollment, ownership of a smartphone, and follow-up care at HCB. Eligibility required ≥12 months since completion of chemotherapy (or ≥12 months since surgery for those not receiving chemotherapy). Ongoing endocrine and/or targeted therapy in the survivorship setting was permitted.

Exclusion criteria included evidence of active cancer, ongoing cytotoxic chemotherapy or radiotherapy, severe comorbid conditions interfering with participation, neurodegenerative disorders impairing study participation, or inability to provide written informed consent.

Recruitment and Random Assignment

Eligible participants were consecutively identified from a cohort of breast cancer survivors attending routine follow-up visits at HCB. After written informed consent and baseline assessment, participants were randomly assigned 1:1 to usual follow-up plus app access or usual follow-up alone.

Random assignment was generated before study initiation by an independent investigator using a computer-generated simple random assignment sequence. Allocation concealment was ensured through restricted access to the random assignment list. No blocking or stratification was applied.

Blinding

Owing to the nature of the intervention, participants were not blinded. Outcomes were assessed using identical self-administered questionnaires in both groups, and app-generated data did not influence outcome assessment or clinical decision making.

Intervention

Participants allocated to the intervention arm had the study app installed on their personal smartphones during a routine visit and received brief standardized guidance. No protocolized clinical monitoring, behavioral coaching, or feedback was provided during follow-up.

The app comprised four components: (1) a CTCAE v4.03-aligned symptom tracker with self-care guidance15; (2) curated educational content; (3) a patient-oriented events calendar; and (4) a smartphone-based physical activity module with gamification features. App-generated data were pseudo-anonymized and securely stored in ONTO-CR and did not trigger protocolized clinical actions.

Outcomes

PROMs were collected at baseline and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months using validated Spanish versions of the European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire C30 (EORTC QLQ-C30),16 EORTC QLQ-BR23,17 Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS),18 and Three-Item Loneliness Scale (TILS).19 In the intervention arm, app-generated symptom logs and daily step counts were collected for descriptive and exploratory purposes only.

According to EORTC scoring manuals, higher scores indicate better global health status and functioning, whereas higher symptom scores indicate greater symptom burden.

Sample Size

This trial was pragmatic and feasibility-driven. Sample size was determined by feasibility, with a planned target of 150 participants (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT05401643); COVID-19–related constraints reduced the final randomly assigned sample to 121 participants. Accordingly, the primary estimate should be interpreted mainly through its confidence interval in relation to published minimal clinically important difference thresholds.20-22

Data Collection and Management

Baseline assessments were conducted at month 0. Follow-up assessments were scheduled at months 3, 6, and 9 and were conducted remotely using electronic questionnaires, with reminders sent as needed. The final assessment at month 12 included an in-person or remote visit, questionnaire completion, and a participant satisfaction survey. Reasons for noncompletion of PROMs were not systematically collected.

In the intervention group, app-generated data—including daily step counts and patient-reported symptom logs aligned with CTCAE v4.03—were continuously collected and time-stamped. All study data were pseudo-anonymized using unique study identifiers and stored in ONTO-CR. Data quality checks were performed before database lock. Access to identifiable information was restricted to authorized study personnel and was not used for statistical analysis.

Technical support was available throughout the study period for installation or functionality issues. No behavioral coaching, clinical feedback, or therapeutic guidance was provided.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle. Continuous questionnaire outcomes were analyzed using mixed models for repeated measures (MMRM), including group, visit, group-by-visit interaction, and baseline outcome value. Although EORTC scales are bounded, they were treated as approximately continuous, consistent with common practice in PROM-based randomized trials.

Secondary and subgroup analyses were exploratory and not adjusted for multiplicity.

MMRM used all available observations under a missing-at-random assumption, without imputing questionnaire scores in the primary analysis.23-25 The within-participant covariance structure was selected according to model convergence using the prespecified sequence: unstructured, Toeplitz, AR,1 and compound symmetry.

Primary models were adjusted for baseline outcome only. Prespecified subgroup analyses evaluated effect modification by hormonal treatment, body mass index, education level, and district-level socioeconomic tertiles.

Missing Data and Sensitivity Analyses

Reasons for loss to follow-up were documented, whereas reasons for missing PROMs among retained participants were not systematically collected. As sensitivity analysis for the primary end point, missing values were handled using multiple imputation under a missing-at-random (MAR) assumption (m = 50). Global Health Status/QoL was derived from QLQ-C30 items 29-30 and transformed to a 0-100 scale. The imputation model included treatment group and available QoL values across visits. The primary end point was reanalyzed using ANCOVA at 3 months, with results pooled using Rubin rules. For missing-not-at-random (MNAR) sensitivity, a pattern-mixture delta adjustment was applied to imputed postbaseline QoL values in the intervention arm only (0, -2.5, -5, -7.5, and -10 points; Data Supplement, Tables S5 and S6).

Subgroup Analyses

Prespecified subgroup interaction analyses were exploratory and intended to generate hypotheses. No adjustment for multiplicity was applied and any subgroup-specific estimates should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating only.

Ethics and Reporting

The study was approved by the HCB Ethics Committee (reference HCB/2020/0971). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. The trial was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05401643).26

RESULTS

Participant Flow

Of 124 women assessed for eligibility, 121 were randomly assigned to the intervention (n = 60) or control group (n = 61; Fig 2). A total of 22 participants were lost to follow-up over the 12-month period (16 in the intervention group and six in the control group). In the intervention arm, the primary reason for discontinuation was a lack of technologic knowledge or confidence (n = 10), followed by hesitancy toward the research protocol (n = 4) and personal motives (n = 2). In the control arm, dropouts (n = 6) were attributed to a lack of time, loss of interest, or inability to contact for follow-up. This technologic barrier may limit generalizability, particularly to older survivors.

Baseline Characteristics

Baseline characteristics were generally similar between groups, with a mean age of approximately 59 years and comparable tobacco and alcohol use patterns (Table 1). Among participants with available hormonal treatment data, aromatase inhibitor use was more frequent in the intervention group than in the control group (78.0% v 64.4%). Given simple random assignment and the modest sample size, these differences were interpreted as chance imbalances. The 80.6% value reported in the Data Supplement (Table S3) refers to app users within the intervention arm, whereas Table 1 reports the full intervention arm.

App Adherence and Use

During months 0-3, 48/60 participants (80.0%) recorded at least 1 day of app activity, accounting for 669 user-days (41.1% of total activity). App use declined after the initial 3 months (months 3-6: 411 user-days; 25.3%) and further during months 6-9 (219 user-days; 13.5%), with a modest rebound during months 9-12 (327 user-days; 20.1%). Twelve participants (20.0%) did not use the app at any time during follow-up (Table 2).

Outcomes Over Time

In the prespecified primary intention-to-treat analysis, there was no statistically significant between-group difference in EORTC QLQ-C30 Global Health Status/QoL at 3 months (adjusted mean difference [Intervention-Control], -2.24 [95% CI, -9.29 to 4.81]; P = .53). No statistically significant between-group differences were observed at subsequent assessments at 6, 9, or 12 months (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses for the primary end point were consistent with the main analysis. Under multiple imputation assuming MAR, the conclusion was unchanged (MD, 1.59 [95% CI, −5.79 to 8.99]; P = .67). In MNAR pattern-mixture analyses applying increasingly negative delta adjustments to imputed intervention-arm values, conclusions remained qualitatively unchanged (Data Supplement, Tables S5 and S6).

Across the 12-month follow-up, no consistent between-group differences were observed for breast cancer–specific QoL (EORTC QLQ-BR23) or for anxiety and depression scores (HADS) in the overall randomized population.

Secondary Patient-Reported Outcomes

Adjusted mixed models showed no statistically significant between-group differences in key EORTC QLQ-BR23 domains (body image, breast symptoms, arm symptoms, and systemic therapy side effects) at 3, 6, 9, or 12 months. Point estimates were small and imprecise, and all 95% confidence intervals crossed zero (Data Supplement, Table S1). Similarly, no statistically significant between-group differences were observed for HADS anxiety or depression, and loneliness (TILS) did not differ between groups over follow-up (Data Supplement, Table S2).

Subgroups and Exploratory Analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses were exploratory and descriptive. An interaction involving TILS and hormonal-treatment category was observed; this result should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating only.

Safety/Harms

No app-related adverse events were identified on the basis of participant self-report; no formal adverse event adjudication or active surveillance was performed.

DISCUSSION

In this pragmatic randomized controlled trial, we did not observe a statistically significant improvement in global QoL, and adjusted between-group differences over 12 months were small and imprecise.

Confidence intervals around the primary estimate remained compatible with clinically meaningful harm and, at most, modest benefit. Engagement was concentrated during the initial 3 months, consistent with real-world digital health use.

Patient-generated data were integrated into the electronic health record via ONTO-CR. However, the intervention did not include a closed-loop care model: app-reported symptoms and other digital signals did not trigger protocolized clinical responses. This may have limited the impact of monitoring and self-management features beyond early follow-up. In addition, more patient-centered adaptation—including personalization and codesign approaches—may help improve the relevance, acceptability, and sustained use of survivorship mHealth interventions beyond a primarily data capture–oriented model. The trial therefore estimates the effect of offering the app rather than sustained use.

Secondary outcomes also showed no statistically significant between-group differences. Subgroup analyses were exploratory and underpowered; they should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating only. The observed interaction involving TILS and hormonal treatment category should not be interpreted as confirmatory evidence.

Although analyses followed the intention-to-treat principle, app use was heterogeneous over time. Sensitivity analyses under both MAR and MNAR assumptions did not materially alter the primary result. These analyses should be interpreted as robustness checks rather than as evidence that missingness was ignorable.

Strengths include the randomized controlled design, quarterly patient-reported outcome assessments over 12 months, use of validated instruments, CTCAE-aligned symptom terminology, integration of patient-generated data into the electronic health record, and log-based adherence metrics.

Limitations should be acknowledged. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, and recruitment was limited to a single tertiary center due to COVID-19–related constraints, reducing the final sample to 121 participants. The study was therefore likely underpowered to detect small-to-moderate effects. Chance imbalances in baseline treatments, including hormonal therapy and trastuzumab use, may also have contributed to residual confounding despite random assignment.

PROM availability decreased over time, which reduced the precision of estimates at later follow-up time points. Engagement also declined after the first 3 months, limiting effective exposure. Requiring smartphone use may have introduced selection bias, particularly among older survivors. In addition, smartphone-based step counting was not fully functional on all Android devices early in the study, so step-count data should be interpreted cautiously and used only for exploratory analyses.

Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as an absence of evidence for population-level benefit under the tested implementation model, rather than evidence of no effect.

Maximizing the impact of survivorship mHealth interventions may require a combination of sustained engagement strategies—such as personalization, adaptive prompts, and incentives—as well as deeper integration with clinical workflows so that digital signals can inform timely clinical responses. Future research should also examine whether intervention effects differ across clinically or contextually relevant subgroups; however, any such heterogeneity should be considered hypothesis-generating unless confirmed in adequately powered studies. These efforts should be implemented within robust clinical governance and regulatory frameworks to ensure safety, effectiveness, and scalability.6-8

In conclusion, in this randomized controlled trial, offering access to a multidimensional digital health intervention to breast cancer survivors did not demonstrate a statistically significant between-group difference in global QoL at the prespecified primary time point. Future survivorship programs may benefit from strategies to sustain long-term engagement and from deeper integration with clinical workflows that translate digital signals into timely clinical actions.
