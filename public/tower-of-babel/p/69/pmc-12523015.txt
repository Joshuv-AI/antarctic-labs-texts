Allostatic load and kidney cancer incidence and mortality: a genetic susceptibility and proteomic mediation analysis
Authors: Chenhao Xu, Da Huo, Yanxu Liu, Qiyu Zhu, Junjie Zhao, Jiayu Liang, Xianding Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAllostatic load (AL), reflecting chronic physiological stress across neuroendocrine, immune, and metabolic systems, may contribute to the onset and prognosis of multiple cancers via inflammation and stress-related injury. However, the associations between AL and kidney cancer (KC) remain unclear. And large-scale evidence linking AL to KC risk, prognosis, and molecular mechanisms is lacking.MethodsIn this Prospective UK Biobank cohort study, 334,754 Participants were analyzed for KC incidence, 357 KC Patients for mortality, and 36,120 participants for proteomic mediation. Proteomic mediation was performed using Med4way analysis; protein trajectories were analyzed using locally weighted regression smoothing (LOESS) regression. The expression profiles of key mediator genes were validated by single-cell RNA sequencing. Enrichment analyses were conducted to characterize mediator proteins by biological pathways, tissue specificity, and cell-type distribution. An AL-mediated proteins-based prediction model was developed via LASSO-Cox regression.ResultsDuring mean follow-up of 13.2 years, participants in the highest AL quintile had increased KC incidence (HR 1.70, 95% CI 1.38–2.10). Among KC patients, high AL was associated with increased mortality (HR 3.10, 95% CI 1.51–6.36). Proteins mediating AL-KC associations included HAVCR1, GDF15, TNFRSF11A, FSTL3, and CD83; HAVCR1 showed strongest mediation (72.69%) and distinct pre-diagnostic trajectory. Functional enrichment revealed these proteins were jointly involved in immune regulation, stress response, and epithelial signaling pathways. The predictive model incorporating NOS3, SULT2A1, HAVCR1, FSTL3, and sex achieved robust performance (10-year AUC 0.80) for KC Prediction.ConclusionsAL is associated with increased KC incidence and mortality, mediated through inflammatory and metabolic proteins. These findings underscore chronic physiological stress as a significant factor in KC pathogenesis, providing potential biomarkers for early risk assessment and targeted prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14980-6.

Introduction

The global incidence of kidney cancer (KC) has risen by 2% annually over two decades, with 431,288 new cases and 179,368 deaths reported in 2020 [1–3]. While established risk factors (obesity, hypertension, smoking) account for 47–54% of KC cases [1, 4–6], emerging evidence suggests psychosocial stressors may contribute to the unexplained etiology [7].

Allostatic load (AL), a multisystem dysregulation indicator encompassing neuroendocrine, immune, and metabolic axes [8], demonstrates carcinogenic potential through chronic inflammation and immunosuppression [9, 10]. Epidemiological studies consistently link elevated AL to increased risks of chronic diseases (cardiovascular disorders, diabetes, malignancies) and mortality [11–16]. Notably, KC pathogenesis exhibits unique susceptibility to AL-mediated mechanisms: chronic glucocorticoid excess (a core AL mediator) induces renal tubular apoptosis via glucocorticoid receptor-dependent pathways [17], while sympathetic catecholamine surges activate the HIF-2α/VEGF pro-angiogenic pathway in tumor microenvironments through β-adrenergic receptor-dependent mechanisms, synergistically promoting KC pathogenesis [18, 19]. This biological Plausibility is further supported by cohort data showing elevated AL is associated with a 1.3-fold increased risk of renal function decline [20]. However, large-scale evidence regarding AL’s impact on KC risk, prognosis, and underlying mechanisms remains absent.

The UK Biobank’s proteomic data has been extensively utilized for cancer biomarker discovery [21, 22]. The emerging “proteomic mediation analysis” strategy, which leverages proteomes as mechanistic bridges between exposures and outcomes, enables simultaneous identification of causal pathways and therapeutic targets [23, 24]. Although transformative in cardiometabolic research, this approach remains unexplored in cancer epidemiology, presenting a novel opportunity to dissect AL-related oncogenic mechanisms.

To address these gaps, this study aims to: (1) evaluate whether AL independently increases KC risk and interacts with conventional factors (e.g., obesity, inflammation) and polygenic risk scores (PRS); (2) quantify AL’s association with all-cause mortality in KC patients; (3) elucidate AL-driven molecular mechanisms via large-scale plasma proteomics and single-cell analyses; (4) develop an AL-integrated KC risk prediction model. By providing the first comprehensive evidence linking AL to KC pathogenesis and outcomes, this work will preliminarily clarify neuroendocrine-microenvironment crosstalk in carcinogenesis and establish a translational framework for AL-driven risk stratification and precision prevention.

Methods

Study population

The data for this study were sourced from the UK Biobank (UKB), a large-scale Population-based cohort study that enrolled approximately 500,000 Participants of European ancestry, aged 37 to 73 years, between 2006 and 2010. Recruitment was conducted across 22 assessment centers in England, Wales, and Scotland [25]. At baseline, comprehensive data collection was carried out, including venous blood sampling and a touchscreen questionnaire designed to capture detailed information on demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and various health-related variables [26]. The UK Biobank project received ethical approval from the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee, and all participants provided written informed consent before their involvement [27].

Participants were excluded from the KC incidence and proteomic analysis cohorts if they had missing data for any AL component, a cancer diagnosis prior to baseline, lack of genomic data, end-stage renal disease, renal replacement therapy, a history of kidney-related surgeries, frequent urinary tract infections, or polycystic kidney disease (PKD). In the KC mortality analysis cohort, exclusions included participants with missing data for any AL component, concurrent tumors diagnosed prior to baseline, or those who died within one year after baseline.

Exposure assessment

AL was assessed as an aggregate index comprising 13 biomarkers that reflect the performance of three physiological systems—metabolic, cardiovascular, and inflammatory—and the functions of two major organs, the liver and kidneys. These biomarkers were chosen based on previous cohort studies and measured using biochemical assays conducted on serum and packed red blood cells collected during the baseline evaluation (details are available in supplementary material) [28, 29]. To evaluate metabolic health, 4 biomarkers were included: glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol. Cardiovascular function was represented by 3 indicators: diastolic blood pressure (DBP), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and pulse rate (PR). The inflammatory system was assessed using insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) and C-reactive protein (CRP). Liver function was characterized by three enzymes—alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT)—while kidney function was captured through creatinine levels. Each biomarker was stratified into quartiles, separately defined for subgroups based on age (< 50, 50–64, and > 64 years) and sex. Risk levels were dichotomized, with values in the lowest quartile for HDL cholesterol and IGF-1 and the highest quartile for the remaining biomarkers classified as “at-risk” and assigned a value of 1. A sub-score for each physiological system or organ was calculated by averaging the risk scores of its corresponding biomarkers. Subsequently, a composite AL score was derived by averaging these five sub-scores (one for each system/organ) and scaling the result to a range of 0 to 100 for interpretability. Higher scores indicated greater cumulative physiological risk. Participants with missing data on any biomarker were excluded from the analysis.

In our analysis, multiple approaches were employed to classify AL. First, the AL score on a 100-point scale was dichotomized into two categories: AL-low and AL-high, using the median score as the cutoff. Second, the scores were stratified into quintiles, labeled as AL-Q1 through AL-Q5. Additionally, for specific analyses such as incidence rate evaluation, the AL scores ranging from 6 to 13—representing less than 5% of all study participants—were grouped into discrete categories: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and ≥ 6. For the KC mortality analysis cohort, due to the small number of participants in certain categories (e.g., only seven individuals with a score of 0), adjacent groups were combined to enhance statistical Power. The combined categories included 0–1, 2–3, 4–5, and ≥ 6. This approach ensured more robust group sizes for meaningful analysis while maintaining interpretability of the AL classification system.

To evaluate whether AL exerts effects beyond classical metabolic dysfunction, we created a non-metabolic AL (AL_non-metabolic) score. HbA1c, triglycerides, LDL-C, HDL-C, systolic and diastolic blood Pressure, and Pulse rate were excluded; the remaining 6 biomarkers were re-scored, averaged, and rescaled to 0–100.

In addition to AL, other psychosocial factors were derived from baseline questionnaire data [25, 30]. A total of 25 items were included, and two composite indices—social isolation and loneliness—were calculated based on previous studies [31]. These 27 additional exposures were categorized into four dimensions: emotional and mental health, satisfaction-related, social support and social isolation, and life stress and socioeconomic status. Details regarding variable sources, processing, and classification can be found in the supplementary material.

Outcome assessment

The UK Biobank dataset includes “first occurrence” fields that map clinical codes from primary care visits, inpatient admissions, death records, and self-reported medical conditions to ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes [32]. The outcome of interest in this study was " Malignant neoplasm of kidney, except renal pelvis” identified using ICD-10 code C64. Data were censored at the earliest of three events: the first diagnosis of KC, participant death, or the data cutoff (October 31, 2022, for HES data; August 31, 2022, for SMR; and May 31, 2022, for PEDW) [33].

Covariate assessment

Potential confounding factors considered in this study include age, gender, ethnicity, education, employment, household income, the Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), family history of cancer, alcohol consumption, smoking status, physical activity, sleep patterns, dietary quality, BMI, metabolic syndrome, and the use of relevant medications, as well as individual genetic susceptibility to KC. Baseline information on age, sex, ethnicity, education level, employment status, family history, and medication use were collected through questionnaires or face-to-face interviews [25].

A recent large-scale multi-ancestry GWAS meta-analysis identified 108 independent risk loci for KC, making it the largest GWAS on KC to date. Based on these 108 SNPs, we constructed a PRS [34]. The PRS was calculated using PRSice-235, with p-value thresholds (e.g., p < 5e-8, p < 1e-5) applied to ensure robustness. To reduce redundancy, linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed with parameters set to R² < 0.1 and a window size of 250 kb. Quality control measures included the exclusion of SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01 or genotype missingness > 5%. The Performance of the PRS model was assessed using the C-statistic and AUC. The PRS was subsequently incorporated into a Cox proportional hazards model to account for genetic predisposition to KC. The PRS derived from the UK Biobank data showed a significant association with clinical diagnosis of KC. Each unit increase in the PRS was associated with an HR of 5.65 (95% CI: 2.03–15.72), indicating a strong predictive relationship between the PRS and KC occurrence. To further stratify genetic risk, participants were classified into low and high genetic risk groups based on the median value of the PRS.

We further investigated the interaction effects of inflammation levels and obesity indicators, both of which are closely associated with KC risk and AL. Specifically, we analyzed 2 inflammatory markers—neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHR), and systemic immune-inflammation index (SII)—as well as 2 commonly used obesity indicators: waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and lipid accumulation product (LAP) [36, 37]. These metrics were derived using biochemical test results from baseline blood samples and anthropometric measurements collected during the study. Detailed calculation methods for each indicator are provided in the supplementary material.

OLINK proteomics

The plasma proteome profiling was conducted as part of the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP), using the Olink® Explore 1536 Proteomics Platform to examine 54,306 plasma samples obtained from UK Biobank participants at their assessment visits. This platform applies Proximity Extension Assay (PEA) technology, facilitating the measurement of over 2,000 protein analytes that encompass a wide spectrum of biological pathways, including those related to inflammation, cancer, cardiometabolic health, and neurological functions. Comprehensive details on the implementation of PEA assays, as well as data processing and normalization techniques, are thoroughly documented in the work by Sun et al. (2023) [21]. Protein expression levels were reported in log2 normalized protein expression (NPX) units to standardize comparisons [22]. For this analysis, proteomic data from the baseline assessment of the UK Biobank cohort were employed, offering a foundational overview of the participants’ plasma protein profiles at the time of study entry.

Statistical methods

Comprehensive association between AL, psychosocial indicators and KC

For descriptive statistics, continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages). We constructed the AL score using 13 factors and compared the distribution of individual biomarkers and AL scores between participants with KC events or mortality and the general study population. AL was treated as both a continuous variable and a quintile categorical variable. Statistical differences in continuous variables were assessed using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) test, while differences in categorical variables were evaluated using the χ² test. Missing data for all covariates were reported as Proportions. Some variables, such as family history, Physical activity, and scores related to loneliness and social isolation, had missing values exceeding 5%. In the baseline characteristics, missing data were coded as a separate category.

We applied Cox Proportional hazards models to examine the association between AL and the risk of KC incidence, as well as all-cause mortality in KC Patients. Results are presented as HRs with 95% CIs, with participants in the lowest AL group serving as the reference. Three models were fitted for Cox regression: model I adjusted for gender, age, and ethnicity; model II further adjusted for education, employment status, household income, and TDI to assess the impact of socioeconomic factors; model III additionally included smoking status, alcohol consumption, Physical activity, sleep quality, healthy eating Patterns, BMI, family history of cancer, and PRS to examine other KC susceptibility factors. For the analysis of AL and all-cause mortality risk in KC, three models were also fitted consistently, with adjustments for sleep quality in model 3 reduced due to excessive missing data. The non-linear trend of AL and KC association was also tested using restricted cubic spline regression, and p-values were reported. The proportional hazards assumption for the cox regression model was confirmed using Schoenfeld residuals.

Additionally, separate adjustments and stratified analyses were performed for metabolic syndrome and its components, including blood glucose, blood pressure, lipid levels (including triglycerides and HDL), and waist circumference. We conducted a stratified analysis based on physical activity (IPAQ), alcohol consumption, smoking status, family history of cancer, overall health status, sex, age, TDI, education level, occupational status, sleep quality, AHEI score, and BMI. Continuous variables were categorized into tertiles for stratification. We used least squares regression to examine the associations between 27 psychosocial indicators and AL (continuous variable, 0-100). Additionally, Cox regression (model III) was applied to assess the associations of each indicator with KC incidence and mortality.

In the observational analysis, we also conducted additional sensitivity analyses. First, considering that Participants with self-reported Poor health status may have a higher risk of KC and mortality, we excluded this group from the analysis. Second, to minimize the impact of reverse causality, we reanalyzed the data excluding KC events in the first year and mortality events within the 3 years prior to follow-up. Additionally, we repeated the primary analysis in participants with eGFR > 60. In the mortality cohort, after further adjusting for baseline KC duration, we repeated the main analysis on the association between AL and all-cause mortality due to KC.

Assessment of the interactive effects of obesity, inflammation, PRS, and AL on KC risk

Since KC is a typical metabolic disease with a clear and significant association with metabolic syndrome, we additionally investigated the interaction between AL and other metabolic and inflammatory indicators in relation to KC incidence. AL was treated as a continuous variable and divided into quartiles, which were then combined with the quartiles of the six indices mentioned above. This resulted in 16 categorical variables, with the combination of first quartile values for both indices used as the reference group. The effects and association strengths were visualized using a heatmap, adjusting for all covariates in model III. Furthermore, we explored the association between AL and KC in participants with different levels of genetic susceptibility (low and high).

Four-way decomposition mediation analysis of OLINK proteomics

We performed a four-way decomposition analysis using the Stata med4way package to assess the mediating effect of plasma proteins in the relationship between AL and KC risk [38, 39]. This method has been previously used in some studies [23, 40]. The total effect (TE) is decomposed into the controlled direct effect (CDE), pure indirect effect (PIE), reference interaction effect (Intref), and mediated interaction effect (Intmed). In this study, we differentiated between “no mediation,” “consistent mediation,” and “inconsistent mediation.” Consistent mediation was defined when the absolute value of CDE was smaller than the TE and PIE had the same sign as CDE. In contrast, inconsistent mediation occurred when the absolute value of CDE exceeded TE, and PIE had the opposite sign to CDE.

We first Performed a multivariable Linear regression analysis with Plasma protein data as the outcome and AL as the exposure, adjusted for the covariates in model III. A total of 2,922 plasma proteins were examined, and a Bonferroni correction (significance threshold p < 1.71e-5) was applied. The top 5% of proteins based on absolute effect size were selected as a stringent criterion, consistent with similar studies [41]. These selected proteins were then input into the four-way decomposition model, with AL as the exposure and KC incidence as the outcome, allowing proteins to act as potential mediators or moderators.

As a supplementary analysis, all 2,922 proteins underwent a four-way decomposition without correction, identifying proteins with significant TE and PIE (at the 0.05 level). These mediating proteins were further analyzed for enrichment in KEGG, GO, and Reactome pathways. We further conducted stratified analysis of the significant and consistent mediator proteins based on sex and PRS (classified into low and high groups by median). The entire med4way Process was Performed in Stata 17.

Cell type-specific enrichment analysis and single-cell analysis

We also conducted cell type-specific enrichment analysis (CSEA) for mediating Proteins using WebCSE, which Provides gene set queries based on expression data from 11 single-cell RNA-seq datasets [42]. Genes with the top 5% t-statistic scores in focal cell types were defined as cell type-specific genes. Fisher’s exact test was used to assess whether proteins mediating the relationship between AL and KC risk were overrepresented in cell type-specific genes.

Based on the statistically significant consistent mediatory proteins identified through med4way proteomic mediation analysis, we further investigated the expression characteristics of the corresponding genes in the renal cancer tumor microenvironment using single-cell RNA sequencing data. The single-cell dataset was derived from a study on renal cancer published in Cancer Cell, including more than 270,000 single-cell transcriptomes from 12 patients [43]. This dataset included multi-regional sampling from tumor cores, tumor-normal interfaces, Peritumoral normal tissues, and Peripheral blood. Initially, we retrieved the gene symbols corresponding to the 19 mediatory proteins and matched them against the gene list in the single-cell dataset, selecting target genes that were detected. Subsequently, the expression matrix of these target genes was extracted, and their expression patterns across various cell types (e.g., tumor cells, immune cells, stromal cells) were analyzed based on the cell-type annotations provided in the dataset. All analyses were conducted using Seurat (v4.0) and visualized with the aid of the cellxgene VIP platform [44] aiming to elucidate the potential roles of the mediatory protein-associated genes in the renal cancer tumor microenvironment. Detailed data processing methods for the single-cell analysis are provided in the supplementary materials.

Trajectory modeling and hierarchical clustering of plasma protein dynamics in relation to KC

To examine dynamic plasma protein patterns associated with AL and KC, we applied a trajectory modeling approach. Plasma protein measurements (expressed as NPX values) were first adjusted for relevant covariates used in prior association analyses to isolate effects specifically attributable to AL. Participants without cancer or chronic kidney disease (control group), who had at least 2 years of follow-up, served as the reference group. For each KC case, 50 matched controls were selected based on demographic and clinical characteristics.

We utilized hierarchical clustering to identify distinct trajectories of protein expression leading to KC diagnosis. Briefly, normalized protein expression values (NPX) were adjusted for covariates, standardized into Z-scores, and modeled longitudinally using locally weighted regression smoothing (LOESS) to capture protein fluctuations prior to KC diagnosis [45]. Hierarchical clustering was then performed on these protein trajectories, grouping proteins with similar temporal patterns.

Construction of the LASSO-based proteomic predictive model for KC

To further explore the clinical utility of AL in predicting renal cancer risk, we constructed a multivariable predictive model based on the mediatory proteins previously identified as significantly associated with renal cancer using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. Initially, standardized expression data of mediatory Proteins were input as independent variables into the model. A 10-fold cross-validation (K-fold cross-validation) approach was employed to optimize the penalization parameter (λ) of the LASSO regression. This method progressively shrinks variable coefficients, reducing the coefficients of non-significant variables to zero, thereby selecting only those variables significantly associated with renal cancer risk. The final model was constructed at the λ value corresponding to the minimal partial likelihood deviance.

The predictive performance of the model was evaluated by calculating time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) values at different time points (e.g., 1825, 3650, and 5475 days) using the timeROC R package, and time-dependent ROC curves were plotted to validate the stability and accuracy of the long-term predictions. Subsequently, a nomogram was developed based on the selected significant protein variables (e.g., NOS3, SULT2A1, HAVCR1) along with clinical variables such as patient sex. The nomogram assigned weighted scores to each variable to calculate linear predictors and predict the individual probability of developing renal cancer. The construction and visualization of the nomogram were performed using the rms R package. All analyses were conducted in R software, with the glmnet R package used for LASSO regression and model development.

Results

Overall study design

This study consists of three major components aimed at systematically investigating the association between AL and KC:Observational evaluation. Using the UKB cohort, we first examine how AL influences both the incidence and mortality of KC, including its interaction with inflammation and obesity. In addition, we explore the effects of psychosocial factors closely tied to AL on KC incidence and mortality.Mechanistic exploration. We then conduct large-scale proteomic mediation analyses in proteomics cohorts, coupled with single-cell analysis, to identify the potential mechanistic pathways by which AL may affect KC.Clinical applicability. Finally, we leverage the significant mediator proteins linking AL to KC to develop a predictive model for KC, highlighting potential avenues for clinical implementation.

Baseline characteristics of the cohort population

This study included 334,754 Participants for the analysis of KC incidence, of whom 47.4% were male, with a mean age of 56.27 years. Participants of European descent accounted for 94.2%. For the Prognosis analysis, 357 KC Patients were included, with 63.3% being male and a mean age of 60.98 years. Additionally, Proteomic analyses were conducted among 36,120 cancer-free Participants at baseline to explore the Proteomic mediation between AL and KC risk. In this subgroup, 47.6% were male, and the mean age was 56.50 years. In the mortality analysis, all Patients were diagnosed with KC Prior to baseline, with a median interval of 5.10 years between diagnosis and AL measurement (Fig. 1). Association between AL and risk of KC incidence

In a cohort of 334,754 Participants, 1,060 cases of KC were diagnosed over a mean follow-up Period of 13.2 years. When stratified by the median AL score, individuals in the high AL group exhibited an approximately 70% higher risk of KC (HR = 1.658, 95% CI: 1.464–1.877), which remained significant in fully adjusted models (HR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.23–1.61). When categorized into quintiles, the fully adjusted HRs for KC risk, relative to the first quintile (Q1), were as follows: Q2 = 1.094 (95% CI: 0.873–1.370), Q3 = 1.220 (95% CI: 0.984–1.513), Q4 = 1.365 (95% CI: 1.107–1.684), and Q5 = 1.701 (95% CI: 1.377–2.100), with a significant trend across quintiles (p for trend < 0.001). Notably, significant associations were observed in Q4 and Q5. Examining AL as a discrete variable (range: 0–13), relative to an AL score of 0, the HRs for KC risk were: AL = 1: 1.254 (95% CI: 0.847–1.858), AL = 2: 1.305 (95% CI: 0.893–1.908), AL = 3: 1.294 (95% CI: 0.885–1.892), AL = 4: 1.530 (95% CI: 1.046–2.240), AL = 5: 2.134 (95% CI: 1.458–3.122), and AL ≥ 6: 1.920 (95% CI: 1.317–2.801). Further analyses treated AL as a continuous variable scaled from 0 to 100 and employed cubic spline models to evaluate dose-response relationships stratified by sex and PRS for KC. Results indicated a stronger J-shaped association in females across all PRS levels.

Associations between AL and all-cause mortality in KC patients

Among 357 KC Patients diagnosed Prior to baseline, 119 deaths occurred over a median follow-up of 16.7 years. In fully adjusted models, treating AL as a continuous variable, each 1-unit increase in AL was associated with a 2% higher mortality risk (HR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04). However, dichotomizing AL into high and low groups did not yield statistically significant associations (HR = 1.287, 95% CI: 0.827–2.001). When categorized into quintiles, the HRs for all-cause mortality were: Q2 = 2.207 (95% CI: 1.040–4.685), Q3 = 1.570 (95% CI: 0.719–3.426), Q4 = 1.898 (95% CI: 0.888–4.060), and Q5 = 3.099 (95% CI: 1.511–6.360), with patients in the highest quintile (Q5) exhibiting over threefold increased mortality risk, which was statistically significant (Fig. 2). Psychosocial stressors and their relationship to AL and KC

Beyond examining the association between AL and KC, we conducted supplementary analyses to investigate the relationship between psychosocial factors and KC risk. These factors are believed to be associated with physiological and psychological stress, potentially interacting with AL to influence KC development and progression.

Linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between selected psychosocial indicators (categorized into four dimensions: emotional and mental health, satisfaction-related, social support and social isolation, and life stress and socioeconomic status) and AL, treated as a continuous variable on a 0-100 scale. Significant and consistent associations were observed across most indicators, with exceptions for a few. Among these, health satisfaction (β = 6.30) and stress experienced in the last two years (β = 4.35) demonstrated the strongest associations with AL. Additionally, when examining the impact of psychosocial indicators on the five components of AL (cardiovascular, kidney, liver, metabolic, and inflammation), cardiovascular and kidney metrics clustered into one group, while the other three metrics formed another. Indicators with the greatest overall impact across all components included household income, two-year stress, and health satisfaction (see Supplementary Materials for details).

In fully adjusted models, certain psychosocial factors exhibited statistically significant associations with KC incidence and mortality. For KC incidence, factors such as fed-up feelings (HR = 1.22), suffering from nerves (HR = 1.20), and leisure social activities (HR = 1.17) were significantly associated with increased risk. Regarding mortality among KC patients, significant associations were observed for able to confide in others (HR = 1.72), loneliness (HR = 1.58), and illness, injury, bereavement, or stress in the last two years (HR = 2.05). Interactive effects of obesity, inflammation, PRS, and AL on KC risk

We calculated three commonly used inflammatory markers (MLR, NHR, and SII) and three obesity indicators (BRI, WHtR, and LAP) to assess the status of each Participant. These markers were combined with AL quartiles to form 16 categories, with the Q1-Q1 category serving as the reference group for Cox regression analysis.

For inflammatory markers, MLR, NHR, and SII exhibited a typical cumulative risk intensification pattern, with a significant and consistent increase in KC risk across quartiles. The highest risks were observed in the Q4-Q4 combination: NHR (HR 2.70, p < 0.001), SII (HR 2.76, p < 0.001), and MLR (HR 1.93, p = 0.002). These findings suggest that inflammation may significantly amplify KC risk through its interaction with AL.

For obesity indicators, a similar cumulative risk pattern was observed, though the highest risk categories were not consistently concentrated in the Q4-Q4 combination. This inconsistency may be attributed to sample distribution characteristics (e.g., the distribution of KC cases) or the previously reported “obesity paradox,“ [46] wherein obesity may exhibit protective effects in certain contexts.

To further explore the interplay between AL and genetic susceptibility, participants were stratified into high and low PRS groups. The association between AL and KC risk was stronger and more significant in the high PRS group, with a clear dose-response relationship observed (AL-Q5: HR 2.308, 95% CI: 1.720–3.097). In the low PRS group, while KC risk increased across AL quintiles, the association was not statistically significant (e.g., AL-Q5: HR 1.329, 95% CI: 0.986–1.791). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

In the subgroup analysis, when AL was at Q5, moderate physical activity was associated with the lowest risk of KC incidence, while the lowest activity level corresponded to the highest risk. Current alcohol consumption was linked to a substantially higher risk compared to never or former drinkers. A family history of cancer and hypertension were also identified as risk-enhancing factors, with younger women exhibiting a higher susceptibility. In the sensitivity analysis, the association between AL and KC risk remained robust after excluding participants with poor self-rated health, removing KC cases occurring within the first year and deaths within the first three years of follow-up, and restricting the analysis to individuals with eGFR > 60. In the mortality cohort, after further adjusting for baseline KC duration, the primary analysis of AL and all-cause mortality in KC patients was repeated, yielding consistent results.

Proteomic mediation and trajectory analysis

A total of 2,922 multivariable linear regressions were performed to examine the associations between AL (scaled 0–100) and plasma proteomics. Using the most fully adjusted model III and applying Bonferroni correction (p < 1.71116 × 10⁻⁵), 2,162 proteins were identified with statistically significant associations. Despite strong statistical significance, the effect sizes of AL on individual proteins were consistently small (β range: −0.016 to 0.036), reflecting the characteristic complexity of proteomic studies. These results suggest that AL’s effects on plasma proteins, while small, may be biologically meaningful, as individual protein changes likely capture only a fraction of systemic effects. Furthermore, AL’s influence on Proteins is Likely mediated through complex biological Pathways, and the cumulative impact of these small effects may be amplified within biological networks, particularly when these proteins are involved in critical signaling pathways or biological processes. To focus on the most sensitive biomarkers, we selected the top 10% of proteins demonstrating the most pronounced effect sizes (n = 216) for further analysis using a four-way decomposition model.

Among the 216 Proteins, mediation effect analysis identified 19 proteins exhibiting consistent mediation effects (proportion of indirect effect [PIE], p < 0.05, with |CDE| < |TE| and the same directionality between PIE and TE). No proteins demonstrated inconsistent mediation effects (|CDE| >|TE|, with opposite directions for PIE and TE and p < 0.05 for PIE). The remaining 197 proteins did not exhibit significant mediation effects (PIE, p > 0.05).

Of the consistent mediators, sulfotransferase family 2a member 1 (SULT2A1) showed the strongest association with KC risk (HR = 1.03, p < 0.001), with a mediation Proportion of 31.59%. Additionally, MTUS1 (21%), AKR7L (22.93%), OCLN (28.98%), and FABP1 (32.22%) were identified as the top five proteins contributing most significantly to the impact of AL on KC risk.

Notably, hepatitis a virus cellular receptor 1/kidney injury molecule-1 (HAVCR1/KIM-1), follistatin-like 3 (FSTL3), receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-b (TNFRSF11A/RANK), cluster of differentiation 83 (CD83), and growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) mediated 72.69%, 68.21%, 47.68%, 44.25%, and 41.93% of the association between AL and KC risk, respectively. These proteins emerged as the top five consistent mediators, highlighting their significant roles in the relationship between AL and KC.

In the sensitivity analysis, we additionally stratified participants by sex and by PRS (dichotomized at the median) and conducted the med4way analysis on 19 consistently identified mediator Proteins within each subgroup. Among men, 8 of these 19 proteins remained significant mediators, with TNFRSF11A (73.32%), GDF15 (51.24%), and IL1RN (49.62%) showing the highest mediation Proportions. In women, 3 proteins were significant mediators, led by HAVCR1 (59.23%). For the low-PRS subgroup, 6 Proteins remained significant, whereas in the high-PRS group, 3 retained significance; TREM2 (49.17%) and FABP1 (52.35%) exhibited the highest mediation proportions, respectively. Detailed findings are presented in the Supplementary Materials.

Trajectory modeling of proteomic data further characterized temporal fluctuations in protein expression relative to KC development. After adjusting for covariates and converting protein expression levels into Z scores, LOESS models identified three distinct trajectory clusters with unique functional implications (Fig. 5). Cluster-1 comprised HAVCR1 alone and showed an early sharp surge followed by a decline beginning ~ 6 years before diagnosis (Fig. 5E). This pattern is typical of sub-clinical tubular injury rather than tumour secretion. Crucially, HAVCR1’s proportion-mediated effect (PIE) remained large after CKD exclusion (68.7%) and after additional eGFR adjustment (64.9%), indicating that its mediation is not an artefact of impaired baseline renal function. Cluster 3 consisted of proteins (e.g., TNFRSF11A and FSTL3) gradually increasing prior to KC diagnosis, highlighting their potential roles as markers of chronic inflammation and progressive metabolic dysregulation driving tumor development. To further investigate the Potential mechanisms underlying the association between AL and KC risk, all 2,922 Proteins were incorporated into the four-way decomposition model without Prior filtering. This analysis identified 88 consistent mediators, including the 19 Previously reported Proteins and an additional 69 mediators. Notably, CSF1 (57.06%), PLAUR (48.85%), and CD74 (45.22%) exhibited relatively high mediation proportions. No inconsistent mediators were identified in this analysis, and detailed results are provided in the supplementary materials.

Enrichment analysis of the 88 consistent mediators using KEGG, GO, and Reactome Pathways revealed several significantly enriched biological Processes and pathways. The analysis identified 142 GO biological processes (GO-BP), 7 cellular components (GO-CC), 13 molecular functions (GO-MF), 6 KEGG Pathways, and 10 Reactome pathways. Overall, pathways associated with cytokine signaling, immune regulation, and cellular metabolism consistently ranked among the top findings across multiple methods. KEGG analysis highlighted “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” (P = 5.5 × 10⁻⁹) and “Osteoclast differentiation” (P = 1.1 × 10⁻²), underscoring roles in cytokine signaling and bone resorption. GO analysis revealed enrichment in “Positive regulation of kinase activity” (P = 3.8 × 10⁻⁷) and “Astrocyte activation” (P = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵), indicating the importance of signaling cascades and neuroimmune interactions. Reactome analysis further highlighted “Immune system” (P = 1.5 × 10⁻⁷) and “Cytokine signaling” (P = 3.8 × 10⁻⁷), emphasizing immune regulation as a central mechanism.

The CCLE (Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia) proteomic data revealed several proteins specifically associated with KC mechanisms. In the OSRC2 kidney cancer cell line, the TenPx20 protein set was significantly enriched, including HAVCR1 (KIM-1), FSTL3, and CSF1, which are closely linked to kidney injury markers, immune regulation, and signal transduction. Additional insights were gained from other cancer cell lines. For instance, the gastric cancer cell line NUGC3 showed enrichment of proteins such as CD83, IL4R, and IFNGR1, while the upper aerodigestive tract cancer cell line BICR22 revealed enrichment of GAS6, ICAM1, and FSTL3, all of which are involved in immune regulation. Notably, proteins such as FSTL3, IFNGR1, and GAS6 were consistently identified across multiple cell lines, suggesting their roles as key mediators of AL effects on cancer development.

WebCSEA (web-based cell-type-specific enrichment analysis) revealed significant enrichment of the 88 consistent mediator proteins across various cell types, particularly in kidney-macrophages (-log10P = 2.92), blood-monocytes (-log10P = 2.78), and spleen-macrophages (-log10P = 2.66), suggesting a strong association with immune-regulatory and tissue-specific functions. Other notable enrichments included pancreas-macrophages (-log10P = 2.54), rectum-stromal cells (-log10P = 2.44), and artery-fibroblasts (-log10P = 2.30).

Discussion

This study is the first to systematically explore the impact of AL and its associated social-psychological factors reflecting chronic stress accumulation on the risk and prognosis of KC in a large prospective cohort, and to reveal the potential biological mechanisms through proteomic mediation analysis and single-cell analysis. Furthermore, we constructed a risk model with high interpretability and predictive performance based on key proteins mediating the relationship between AL and KC, providing scientific evidence for the personalized assessment and intervention of patients with high AL.

Our findings indicate that AL significantly influences both the incidence and Prognosis of KC. Compared with Participants in the lowest quintile of AL, each increment in AL category was associated with a 9%−70% increased risk of KC incidence and a 2-to-3.6-fold increase in mortality risk. Among the social-psychological factors related to AL, social isolation induces a chronic stress state by activating the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to immune suppression, elevated inflammation levels, and metabolic disorders, thereby significantly increasing KC risk [47, 48]. Loneliness, as a subjective perception of social isolation, is linked to higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-α) and immune imbalance [49, 50] worsening the tumor microenvironment. Moreover, short-term major stress events, such as bereavement or financial difficulties, activate the HPA axis, leading to glucocorticoid fluctuations that interfere with DNA repair and apoptosis regulation [51] resulting in poorer prognosis in KC patients, including higher recurrence and mortality rates.

Furthermore, the effect of AL on KC incidence was more pronounced in individuals with higher genetic risk scores (PRS), while its effect on prognosis was weaker. Obesity, inflammation, and AL jointly form a cumulative risk intensification pattern, where individuals with higher inflammation, poorer obesity status, and higher AL scores exhibit the highest KC risk. These results suggest that AL not only serves as a comprehensive indicator of chronic stress impacts on health outcomes but may also significantly affect KC risk and prognosis through mechanisms such as the metabolic-inflammatory axis and immune regulation.

These findings are consistent with existing literature. As a comprehensive indicator of chronic stress accumulation, AL not only reveals physiological dysregulation in stress adaptation but is also considered an important predictor of all-cause mortality [11]. Meta-analysis shows that high AL is associated with a 22% increased risk of all-cause mortality compared to low AL populations [52] and a UK cohort study reports that individuals with high AL have a 3.56-fold higher mortality risk [53]. However, existing studies have mostly focused on the general population and lacked specific analysis in kidney cancer patients. This study is the first to demonstrate that high AL significantly increases the all-cause mortality risk in KC patients, validating the applicability of AL as a mortality predictor and highlighting its unique significance in cancer populations.

Our large-scale Proteomic mediation analysis identified 19 key Plasma Proteins mediating the AL-KC association out of 292 potential mediators. Among these, SULT2A1 exhibited the strongest association with KC, and the mediation effects of HAVCR1 and GDF15 accounted for 72.69% and 41.93%, respectively. SULT2A1, a critical sulfotransferase involved in steroid hormone, bile acid, and lipid metabolism regulation, is significantly altered in various metabolic disorders and cancers, and is closely associated with oxidative stress and metabolic syndrome [54–56]. The strong association of SULT2A1 suggests that AL, under chronic stress conditions, may affect KC risk through metabolic reprogramming and redox imbalance, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for kidney cancer.

In terms of metabolic-related proteins, FABP1 and GDF15 also play important roles. FABP1 is believed to be closely associated with lipid metabolism reprogramming in the KC tumor microenvironment [57] while GDF15, as a stress-related secretory protein, is linked to the adaptive survival mechanisms of cancer cells [58]. Both may synergistically promote metabolic dysregulation and tumorigenesis induced by AL. Regarding immune regulation, proteins such as HAVCR1 and TREM2 are significantly enriched in pathways related to the immune microenvironment. Soluble KIM-1 is a validated marker of proximal-tubule damage rather than an immune checkpoint. Its early and transient spike in our trajectory analysis suggests that chronic allostatic stress first provokes low-grade tubular injury, creating a field effect that heightens malignant susceptibility. The mediation proportion persisted after stringent kidney-function adjustments, reinforcing a stress-induced injury mechanism independent of manifest CKD. Membrane-bound HAVCR1 can still contribute to immune escape in advanced tumours, but that later role is unlikely to drive the strong plasma-based mediation seen here [59]. TREM2, on the other hand, participates in the activation of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which are closely involved in the formation of the KC immune microenvironment [60]. These proteins suggest that AL may promote KC risk through immune microenvironment modulation.

Further cellular and tissue enrichment analysis revealed that these proteins are significantly enriched in macrophages and blood monocytes in the kidney and spleen. Macrophages, especially TAMs, play a crucial role in tumor progression and immune evasion [61, 62]. Additionally, these proteins are enriched in vascular-related cells (such as arterial fibroblasts and renal tubular endothelial cells) and dendritic cells, supporting the notion that AL may reshape the tumor microenvironment of KC through oxidative stress and vascular dysfunction. Single-cell analysis further confirmed the high expression of GDF15 in renal epithelial cells, supporting its potential role in kidney injury and tumorigenesis. The enrichment of HAVCR1 and TREM2 in immune cells indicates that AL may influence KC progression through immune evasion mechanisms. In summary, this study provides insights into the potential mechanisms underlying the AL-KC association, where AL regulates KC risk through metabolic, immune, and vascular remodeling.

The KC risk Prediction model constructed using these key Proteins demonstrated high predictive performance, with an AUC of 0.80 in 10 years. Compared to direct screening of all proteins, the model based on key proteins mediating the AL-KC relationship offers stronger interpretability and specificity. Recent studies have shown that plasma KIM-1 (another name for HAVCR1) effectively distinguishes renal cell carcinoma (RCC) from benign kidney lesions, with an AUC of 0.81, and is associated with patient mortality, supporting the potential role of HAVCR1 in KC risk prediction [63].

Although this study Presents several innovative findings, there are some Limitations to consider. First, AL and Proteomic mediators were measured only at baseline. Because these biomarkers vary over time, single-point assessment can lead to exposure misclassification and dilute true associations. To explore this, we censored follow-up at 5 years—thereby shortening the interval during which AL might change—and found near-identical hazard ratios and a mediation Proportion of 70.9%, suggesting Limited bias from baseline-only measurement. We also repeated the four-way decomposition using 1,000 non-parametric bootstraps (bias-corrected 95% CI 65.4–77.9%) and an alternative counterfactual-g-formula (71.0% mediation). These results confirm that the 72.7% estimate is numerically stable. Biologically, this high proportion reflects a composite pathway: early AL-induced tubular injury (reflected by soluble HAVCR1/KIM-1) followed by tumoral HAVCR1 up-regulation during progression. Second, due to the limited clinical information on KC, including tumor stage, type, and treatment protocols, from the UKB, the specific mechanisms by which AL affects KC prognosis were not thoroughly explored. Third, although we adjusted for known risk factors such as diabetes and obesity, AL itself includes components related to these factors, making it challenging to completely exclude their indirect effects. Future research could further validate these findings using causal inference methods. Fourth, as the proportion of non-European populations in the UKB is low, this study primarily analyzes European samples, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should validate the results in multi-ethnic and multi-regional populations. Fifth, although we identified several key mediator proteins (e.g., SULT2A1 and HAVCR1) through proteomics, the temporal dynamics of these proteins and their relationship with KC development remain to be explored. Additionally, the lack of integration with metabolomics and other omics data may limit the comprehensive understanding of the AL-KC relationship. The potential alleviating effects of psychological interventions on high AL patients were also not evaluated in this study, representing an important direction for future research. Besides, our cohort is predominantly White British, which constrains immediate generalizability. KC incidence and risk-factor profiles vary across racial/ethnic groups therefore effect sizes estimated here may not transport unchanged to other populations. We explicitly recognize this limitation and will priorities replication in independent, multi-ethnic cohorts when compatible data become available. Finally, given the current evidence base, AL measurement should be used primarily for risk stratification rather than as an immediately actionable treatment target. Prospective intervention trials with KC end-points are needed to establish whether deliberate AL reduction lowers incidence.
