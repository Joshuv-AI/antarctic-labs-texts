On arrival to the accident and emergency department, she was comatose with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of E1V1M1 and required urgent endotracheal intubation for airway protection. Hemodynamic assessment revealed profound hypotension (66/27 mm Hg) and sinus tachycardia (103 beats/min). The 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated sinus rhythm at 81 beats/min, with 1 mm ST-segment elevation in lead aVR with reciprocal deep ST-segment depression across the precordial leads (V4-V6), a pattern concerning for left main coronary artery (LMCA) thrombotic occlusion or significant triple-vessel disease (Figure 1).Figure 1Electrocardiography Upon Presentation to Accident and Emergency UnitSinus rhythm at a heart rate of 81 beats/min, with deep ST-segment depression in leads V4-V5, I, II, and aVF with 1-mm ST-segment elevation in lead aVR.

Further examination revealed a soft diastolic murmur, and a suspicious intimal flap was identified at the right carotid artery while preparing for central venous catheter insertion in her right neck. The blood pressure was similar on both arms, with a systolic blood pressure of 50 to 60 mm Hg and a diastolic blood pressure of 20 to 30 mm Hg. Radial-radial or radial-femoral delay could not be fully appreciated due to profound hypotension.

Past Medical History

Her medical history was notable for hypertension, which was managed solely through lifestyle modification. Her blood pressure readings at home consistently ranged between 120 and 130/75 and 80 mm Hg. She was a lifelong nonsmoker and nondrinker.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial working diagnosis was acute coronary syndrome, driven by the classic “left main” ECG pattern (ST-segment elevation in aVR with diffuse ST-segment depression) in the setting of profound cardiogenic shock, prompting consideration of emergent coronary angiography with percutaneous coronary intervention under mechanical circulatory support. Risk stratification using the Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events score yielded 188 points, predicting an in-hospital mortality risk of approximately 50% by 6 months of admission.1 However, the presence of a new soft diastolic murmur, a suspected dissecting intimal flap in the carotid artery, and an Aortic Dissection Detection Risk Score2 of 2/3 (abrupt chest pain and new aortic regurgitation murmurs with hypotension) raised concurrent concern for acute aortic syndrome. Key differentials therefore included left main or severe multivessel acute coronary syndrome, acute Stanford type A aortic dissection with severe aortic regurgitation, and, less likely, massive pulmonary embolism or other causes of undifferentiated shock.

Investigations

An urgent point-of-care echocardiography demonstrated a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 60%, severe aortic regurgitation with a prominent aortic root (sinus of Valsalva 3.8 cm), and moderately dilated ascending aorta of 4.5 cm, with a concern for an aortic dissection flap. There was no significant mitral or tricuspid regurgitation, and no pericardial effusion was observed at that time (Video 1).

Urgent computed tomography (CT) aortography with contrast enhancement revealed Stanford type A aortic dissection extending from the aortic root to the renal arteries with aneurysmal dilatation of the ascending aorta (4.9 cm). Preserved blood flow was noted at the left main stem artery, with continuation into the left anterior descending artery and left circumflex artery, with adequate distal runoff. The proximal course of the right coronary artery was also seen on the CT (Figure 2). The dissection involved all major aortic branches: bilateral common carotid arteries showed severe narrowing but preserved true lumens with patent distal runoffs, whereas bilateral subclavian arteries remained patent (Figure 3). The right vertebral artery arose from the false lumen and the left from both lumens. Visceral branches were patent with dual lumen supply and no significant stenosis. Bilateral renal arteries were patent, with the true lumen supplying the right renal artery and the false lumen supplying the left and accessory renal arteries. Management

Shortly after completion of the CT aortography, the cardiothoracic surgery team was urgently consulted. However, given the extensive nature of the dissection and the associated poor prognosis, with reported mortality exceeding 90%, surgical intervention was not pursued.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Despite high-dose inotropic support, she remained in cardiogenic shock. The patient was instead offered comfort care and succumbed 20 hours after admission.

Discussion

This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of acute Stanford type A aortic dissection presenting with an electrocardiographic pattern strongly suggestive of LMCA thrombotic occlusion in the setting of profound cardiogenic shock. The ECG findings of ST-segment elevation in lead aVR with widespread ST-segment depression in the precordial leads (V4-V6) represent a recognized pattern associated with LMCA stenosis or occlusion, proximal left anterior descending artery disease, or severe multivessel coronary disease. This pattern reflects diffuse subendocardial ischemia, with ST-segment elevation in aVR representing a reciprocal change to widespread ST-segment depression in the left-sided leads, and is considered a powerful predictor of LMCA or severe triple-vessel diseases with high sensitivity and specificity.3

In the context of acute aortic dissection, electrocardiographic abnormalities occur in approximately 70% of cases, most commonly as nonspecific ST-segment and T-wave changes, sinus tachycardia, or left ventricular hypertrophy with secondary repolarization abnormalities.4 Evidence of acute myocardial infarction, manifesting as ST-segment elevation or pathological Q waves, occurs in only 4% to 8% of Stanford type A dissections and typically results from extension of the dissection flap into a coronary ostium or extrinsic compression of the coronary artery by an expanding false lumen.4,5 The right coronary artery is more affected than the left, usually presenting with an inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction pattern6, anterior myocardial infarction due to left coronary involvement is rare.

Several reports have described Stanford type A aortic dissection mimicking acute coronary syndrome due to left main or left anterior descending coronary artery involvement. In particular, Singh et al7 and Ivanova et al8 reported fatal cases of LMCA occlusion secondary to acute aortic dissection, with the former presenting with extensive ST-segment elevation in anterolateral leads (I, aVL, and V2-V6) and reciprocal inferior ST-segment depression, and the latter presenting with an ECG pattern in line with an acute anterior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.

In our case, the ECG pattern of ST-segment elevation in aVR with diffuse precordial ST-segment depression is particularly noteworthy, as CT imaging demonstrated patent coronary ostia without evidence of direct coronary occlusion or dissection extension into the coronaries. Although the possibility of a dynamic dissection flap intermittently prolapsing into one or both coronary ostia cannot be excluded, particularly given that the CT in our case was acquired without ECG gating and therefore captures only a single temporal snapshot of the coronary ostia only, the ECG pattern may equally reflect severe global subendocardial ischemia resulting from a combination of profound hemodynamic insults: profound cardiac compromise with systemic hypoperfusion, acute severe aortic regurgitation causing left ventricular volume overload and reduced diastolic coronary perfusion pressure, and extensive malperfusion involving all major aortic branch vessels.9 In such circumstances, marked oxygen supply-demand mismatch produces diffuse subendocardial ischemia manifesting as widespread ST-segment depression with reciprocal ST-segment elevation in aVR, even in the absence of epicardial coronary occlusion. Dorman et al10 similarly described a case of acute dissection causing global ischemic ECG changes rather than a localized infarction pattern, underscoring that dissection-related coronary compromise may occur through mechanisms beyond frank coronary occlusion.

Conclusions

Acute aortic dissection can present with an electrocardiographic pattern indistinguishable from LMCA occlusion, yet requires a fundamentally opposite management. While acute coronary syndrome mandates anticoagulation and coronary intervention, aortic dissection is a contraindication to anticoagulation and necessitates immediate surgical repair. Meticulous clinical examination—assessing for aortic regurgitation, blood pressure discrepancies, pulse deficits, and neurological findings—is essential for differentiation. Urgent point-of-care echocardiography and CT aortography provide a definitive diagnosis and guide subsequent management. This case underscores that when electrocardiographic findings suggest acute LMCA occlusion, meticulous clinical examination and prompt advanced imaging are critical to avoid potentially harmful therapies in the setting of a cardiac emergency.
