Early-Onset Myocardial Infarction in a 13-Year-Old With Homozygous Familial Hypercholesterolemia Managed by PCI
Authors: Fahad Irshaad Siddiqui, Ali Ghaffar, Inam Ur Rehman, Muhammad Zubair Zaffar, Abeera Hassan, Yasir Mehmood, Sugat Adhikari, Samama Noor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHomozygous familial hypercholesterolemia is an autosomal codominant genetic disorder leading to highly elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and premature atherosclerosis.Case SummaryA 13-year-old boy with no comorbidities had acute chest pain and cardiogenic shock. Electrocardiogram documented anterior ST-segment elevation. Echocardiography revealed left ventricular ejection fraction of 35%. Lipid panel showed total cholesterol of 616 mg/dL and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol of 541 mg/dL. Physical examination was performed with widespread xanthomas and corneal arcus. Coronary angiography revealed severe left main and 2-vessel disease. He received successful elective percutaneous coronary intervention of the left main to left anterior descending with TIMI flow grade 3.ConclusionsThis case points out the necessity of early diagnosis of homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia, intensive lifelong lipid-lowering therapy, and family cascade screening for averting premature coronary events.

A 13-year-old boy, previously well with normal growth and development milestones, presented to the emergency department with acute-onset central chest pain and dyspnea lasting 3 hours. He was a lean child with an estimated body mass index of 17.7 kg/m2. There was no history of hypertension, diabetes, or other comorbidities. He was the product of a consanguineous marriage. On arrival, the boy was in cardiogenic shock with a blood pressure of 70/40 mm Hg, heart rate of 122 beats/min, and oxygen saturation of 90% on room air. Past Medical History

The patient had no history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, congenital heart disease, or prior cardiac symptoms. However, he had cutaneous xanthomas since early childhood, which progressively enlarged and remained untreated due to limited access to health care. He had never received lipid-lowering therapy, nor had he been hospitalized or undergone surgical interventions in the past. Family history was significant for premature MI in the maternal uncle, who died suddenly in his early 30s.

Differential Diagnosis

At presentation with acute chest pain, dyspnea, and cardiogenic shock, myocarditis, Kawasaki disease, Takayasu arteritis, and congenital coronary anomalies were considered but excluded based on clinical features, laboratory findings, and coronary angiography. Angiography revealed significant obstructive atherosclerotic disease. Given longstanding xanthomas, markedly elevated cholesterol (LDL-C), and a family history of premature MI, the diagnosis was acute anterior wall MI due to homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HoFH).

Investigations

Laboratory evaluation showed markedly elevated high-sensitivity troponin I (4.68 ng/mL), normal erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein, and otherwise unremarkable renal, hepatic, and thyroid profiles. Lipid panel demonstrated total cholesterol of 616 mg/dL and LDL-C of 541 mg/dL, consistent with familial hypercholesterolemia (FH). Genetic testing was not performed due to limited availability and because the diagnosis was clinically obvious.

Cutaneous examination revealed multiple yellowish nodular swellings over the extensor surfaces of the elbows and forearms (Figure 2), knees (Figure 3), buttocks and thighs (Figure 4), and Achilles tendons (Figure 5B). Bedside echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 35% and severe hypokinesia of the mid and apical anterior wall, apex, and distal two-thirds of the anterior interventricular septum, with mild mitral regurgitation and no pericardial effusion. The aortic valve was specifically evaluated on echocardiography and found to be mildly thickened and calcified, consistent with early degenerative or lipid-related changes. There was mild to moderate aortic regurgitation, with a vena contracta of 4 mm and a pressure half-time of 413 milliseconds. Although no significant aortic stenosis was identified at this stage, these findings warrant periodic follow-up because progressive valvular calcification and eventual stenosis are well-recognized complications of FH.

Differential diagnoses including Kawasaki disease, myocarditis, Takayasu arteritis, and anomalous coronary anatomy were ruled out based on the absence of systemic inflammatory signs, normal inflammatory markers, and angiographic findings.

Coronary angiography revealed severe ostioproximal bifurcation disease of the left main stem (LMS), severe proximal and moderate mid-segment disease in the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, a nondominant left circumflex artery with mild mid-segment disease, and a dominant right coronary artery with severe proximal disease (Figure 6A).Figure 6Left and Right Coronary Angiograms(A) Left coronary angiogram (LAO cranial view) showing severe ostioproximal left main stem bifurcation disease extending into the proximal left anterior descending artery, with additional narrowing of the proximal left circumflex artery. Treatment

On admission, the patient was stabilized with intravenous inotropic support, high-flow oxygen, and diuretics. Antiplatelet therapy with aspirin was initiated alongside anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin at a dose of 80 U/kg, titrated to achieve an activated clotting time of 300 seconds. High-intensity statin therapy and ezetimibe were commenced to address the markedly elevated LDL-C. Beta-blockers and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors were deferred initially due to cardiogenic shock.

Once hemodynamically stable, coronary angiography was performed via the right femoral artery using a 6-F sheath. Revascularization was undertaken with an XB3 guiding catheter. Two BMW guidewires were positioned, one in the LAD and the other parked in the circumflex artery, to secure bifurcation access. Lesion preparation was achieved with a 2- × 12-mm balloon, followed by crossover stenting from the ostium of the left main stem into the mid LAD using a Resolute Onyx zotarolimus-eluting stent (3 × 38 mm). Postdilatation was performed with a 3.5- × 12-mm noncompliant balloon, achieving optimal stent expansion and TIMI flow grade 3 (Figure 6B). No adjunctive intravascular imaging was performed due to size and resource limitations. The angiographic findings are further demonstrated in Videos 1 and 2.

The total contrast volume was 120 mL and fluoroscopy time was 15 minutes, with no periprocedural complications observed. Hemodynamic stability was maintained throughout with double inotropic support. A staged percutaneous intervention of the right coronary artery was planned for a later date. The complete list of devices and equipment used during the intervention is shown in the equipment list.

Outcome and Follow-Up

During his hospital stay, the patient's hemodynamics stabilized, his chest pain resolved, and he was weaned off inotropes by day 3. Repeat echocardiography before discharge showed an improvement in LVEF from 35% to 45%. Postpercutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) electrocardiogram showed resolution of ST-segment elevation in the anterior precordial leads with evolving T-wave inversions, consistent with reperfusion and postinfarction changes (Figure 7). He was started on dual antiplatelet therapy, high-intensity statin, and ezetimibe, with outpatient referral for PCSK9 inhibitor evaluation and family cascade screening. Given the psychosocial impact of his visible xanthomas, the patient and his family were also referred for counseling to address emotional well-being, treatment adherence, and lifestyle modification.

Patient's perspective

The patient's parents reported longstanding xanthomas and corneal arcus that were noticeable to peers, leading to teasing and social discomfort at school. The family was anxious about sudden cardiac events after losing a close relative and faced financial barriers to specialized care. Counseling during hospitalization addressed both medical management and psychosocial support to improve quality of life and reduce stigma.

Discussion

Severe FH in childhood is an aggressive condition predisposing to early onset coronary artery disease and MI. In untreated HoFH, LDL-C levels >500 mg/dL are common, and coronary atherosclerosis may develop within the first decade of life.1 Case reports consistently highlight that late diagnosis and lack of lipid-lowering therapy increase the risk of acute coronary events before adulthood.2

Our patient presented with acute anterior wall MI and severe LMS and LAD artery disease requiring urgent revascularization. Similar cases have been described. Leclercq et al1 reported a 10-year-old patient with HoFH presenting with acute coronary syndrome requiring urgent coronary artery bypass grafting, but rapid atherosclerosis progression even in the grafted vessels was noted. Wang et al3 described an 8-year-old patient with HoFH and multivessel coronary artery disease managed with plasmapheresis, intensive statins, and balloon angioplasty. More recently, Deshpande et al4 documented a 10-year-old girl with acute coronary syndrome and severe LMS disease treated with complex PCI instead of coronary artery bypass grafting because of unfavorable surgical anatomy. These reports, together with our case, emphasize that PCI can be a viable option in children with severe coronary lesions, provided procedural expertise and pediatric cardiac support are available.

Our case required PCI from the LMS into the LAD using a drug-eluting stent after balloon predilatation, followed by postdilatation with a noncompliant balloon to ensure optimal stent expansion. TIMI flow grade 3 was achieved, consistent with good procedural success. Other authors have highlighted similar techniques, including provisional bifurcation stenting for LMS disease and the need for aggressive postdilatation to prevent stent malapposition.5 The femoral route, as in our case, remains the preferred access in most pediatric interventions because it accommodates larger guiding catheters required for LMS stenting; however, radial access has been reported in older adolescents.6

Intravascular imaging (IVUS/OCT) was not used in our case because of resource and size limitations; however, pediatric reports increasingly highlight its role in accurate vessel sizing and stent optimization.6 Growth-related challenges remain unique to pediatric PCI: the LMS diameter increases substantially as children grow, raising concerns about future stent under sizing.7 Bioresorbable vascular scaffolds have been explored as a potential solution, with Goktekin et al6 describing successful bioresorbable vascular scaffolds use in a 3-year-old patient with LMS disease. However, there is limited evidence for children.

Early symptomatic improvement and partial recovery of LVEF after PCI, as observed in our patient, are consistent with prior reports. However, restenosis and disease progression remain concerns in HoFH, necessitating lifelong intensive lipid-lowering therapy and family screening. Psychosocial support is also important due to the visible and chronic nature of the disease.8

In patients with HoFH and established ASCVD, LDL receptor–independent therapeutic strategies are increasingly necessary to achieve guideline-recommended LDL-cholesterol targets (<55 mg/dL). A recent systematic review underscores the importance of lipoprotein apheresis, liver transplantation, and angiopoietin-like protein 3 inhibition as essential adjuncts when conventional lipid-lowering therapies fail to adequately control lifelong LDL burden.9
