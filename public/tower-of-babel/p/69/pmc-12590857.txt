Combined glyphosate and chlorpyrifos-based pesticides impair innate and adaptive immune functions: an in vitro approach
Authors: Adrián David Friedrich, Daniela Belén Gareis, María Eugenia Ordoñez, María Victoria Regge, María Cecilia Santilli, María Natalia Rubinsztain, Mariana Gantov, María Sofía Amarilla, María Eugenia Gaillardou, Carolina Ines Domaica, Mercedes Beatriz Fuertes, Norberto Walter Zwirner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe widespread use of pesticides, including glyphosate-based herbicides (e.g., Roundup®, R) and chlorpyrifos-based insecticides (e.g., Clorpi48®, C), has raised concerns about their environmental and human health impacts. Growing evidence links pesticide exposure to cancer development. Given the critical role of immune surveillance in tumor growth control, environmental pollutants such as pesticides should also be evaluated for immunotoxicity. Moreover, while individual pesticides have been extensively studied, the effects of pesticide mixtures on human immune cells remain poorly explored. Here, we assessed the impact of environmentally relevant doses of R, C, or their combination (R+C) on immune cell functions.MethodsPeripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), NK cells, and T cells from healthy donors were exposed to environmentally relevant doses of R, C, or R+C. NK cell cytotoxicity, T-bet expression and IFN-g production were analyzed by flow cytometry, and immune synapse formation (LFA-1 localization) and perforin polarization were analyzed by confocal microscopy. T-cell proliferation, Th1 differentiation, and IL-2 signaling were also evaluated by flow cytometry. Oxidative stress was quantified using a fluorometric assay by measuring H2O2 production in PBMCs exposed to R, C, or R+C. Also, the role of oxidative stress in T-cell dysfunction was assessed.ResultsThe combination of R+C, but not the individual compounds, significantly impaired NK cell cytotoxicity, IFN-g production, and immune synapse formation, as evidenced by disrupted LFA-1 localization and defective perforin polarization. In T cells, R+C exposure inhibited proliferation, Th1 differentiation, IL-2 signaling, and IFN-g secretion by CD8⁺ T cells, all key functions for effective antitumor responses. Mechanistically, oxidative stress contributed to the antiproliferative effect, as scavenging of H2O2 by catalase addition restored T cell proliferation.ConclusionsEnvironmentally relevant doses of glyphosate and chlorpyrifos-based pesticide mixtures disrupt innate and adaptive immune effector functions that are critical for the control of neoplastic cells and nascent tumor foci, suggesting that current risk assessments underestimate the immunotoxicity of combined formulations. Introduction

The increase in the use of pesticides during the last 20 years has been accompanied by a rise in crop yields, but poses significant environmental and health risks [1, 2]. Glyphosate (GLY), the active ingredient in R, is the most widely used herbicide worldwide; meanwhile Chlorpyrifos (CPF), the active ingredient of C, is banned in many countries but remains detectable in the environment, in humans, and in wild animals, suggesting that it is still widely used. In fact, Lajmanovich et al. document high concentrations of CPF in aquatic animals in an Argentinean river, reinforcing the evidence that CPF based pesticides are still being used at least in Argentina [3]. Owing to the valuable work performed by the SPRINT Project, we now know that GLY and CPF are present in 34% and 21% of environmental samples, respectively, both in Europe and Argentina [4, 5]. Moreover, a 2023 study revealed that 84% of farmer urine samples from five European countries contained at least two different pesticides, with a median of three pesticides per sample and a maximum of 13 pesticides in a single sample [6]. These findings highlight that assessing single-pesticide toxicity no longer reflects real-world exposure [7].

GLY is classified as a probable carcinogen (Group 2 A) by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), and its exposure has been linked to diverse types of cancer, such as breast cancer and non-Hodgkin lymphoma [8, 9]. Additionally, GLY-based herbicides (GbH) have been associated with immune modulation in mice exposed to R(the most commonly used GbH)-rich air, promoting Th2-type inflammation [10]. Recent studies also indicate that GLY alters T-cell differentiation, increasing IL-4 and IL17A and decreasing IFN-γ production. In contrast, the carcinogenic potential of CPF remains less clear (IARC Group 2B), but CPF-based insecticides (CbI) exhibit immunotoxicity in various animal models [11–13]. Despite the frequent co-application of GLY and CPF in agriculture, no studies have evaluated their combined effects on human immune cells.

Pesticide formulations consist of active ingredients and excipients. For GbH, the addition of surfactants such as polyoxyethylene tallow amine (POEA) is essential due to the high hydrophilicity of GLY that prevents efficient plant cell wall penetration. Of concern, such excipients, although considered inert compounds, can increase GLY toxicity [14–16]. In 2016, the EU Commission recommended to Member States that POEA-type co-formulants should be banned from use in GbH [17], yet excipient toxicity remains understudied. For CbI, petroleum distillates are used as solvents together with different emulsifiers, but their safety for human health remains poorly characterized.

The occurrence of cancer depends not only on genetic predispositions and accumulation of tumor-driving mutations but also on environmental factors. Among them, pollutants such as pesticides might negatively affect key cell populations, such as NK cells and CD4 and CD8 T cells, responsible for the detection and elimination of neoplastic cells and nascent tumor foci Importantly, these immunomodulatory effects might occur independently of an eventual direct carcinogenic activity of the pesticides, suggesting that environmental exposure may contribute to cancer progression not only by initiating mutations but also by weakening immune surveillance mechanisms.

A recent study in a school aged cohort from Mexico that was highly exposed to pesticides demonstrated the occurrence of an increased systemic inflammation index (SII) and alterations in various hematological parameters, such as low lymphocyte counts [18]. Notably, the SII has been associated with an increased risk of developing cancer [19, 20]. Studying immune cell functions, such as T-cell proliferation, IFN-γ production, and NK cytotoxicity in humans highly exposed to pesticides, is essential for understanding pesticide–immunosurveillance crosstalk.

Given the critical role of immune surveillance for the control of neoplastic cells and nascent tumor foci, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of combined R and C (R + C) exposure on the phenotype and function of innate (NK cells) and adaptive (T cells) immune cells. For this purpose, we treated PBMCs, isolated NK cells, and T cells from healthy donors with R, C, or R + C and assessed their effector functions in response to prototypical stimuli. Our results demonstrate that combined exposure to R and C impaired the effector functions of both NK cells and T cells, which might be related to the increased prevalence of malignancies observed in individuals exposed to pesticides.

Materials and methods

Pesticides and doses

Stock solutions of pesticides were prepared as follows: Roundup Full II (R, Bayer-Monsanto) was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline and sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 μm pore filter, and Clorpi48 (C, Huagro) was dissolved in 96% absolute ethanol. The stock solutions were stored at −20 °C until use. Preliminary experiments were performed to identify doses of GLY and CPF corresponding to those detected in the environment in the SPRINT Project [5] that were also non-cytotoxic to human cells. We chose individual and combined pesticide doses within this range that maintained cell viability above 90%. Based on these results, we established the following concentrations used throughout this study: 8 µM (1.3 × 10³ ppb) for R and 15 µM (5.25 × 10³ ppb) for C. They reflect environmentally relevant exposures as reported in the SPRINT Project. The same concentrations were used in the R + C mixture. The extension of in vitro pesticide exposure were chosen considering the short half-life of GLY (5–14 h) and CPF (27 h) in the human body [21, 22]. The exception was the measurement of intracellular H2O2 which is a rapid phenomenon that had to be assessed within the first few hours after exposure.

Cells and cell culture

The NK cell-sensitive K562 cell line was obtained from ATCC (CCL-243). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 10% inactivated FBS (Natocor, Córdoba, Argentina), sodium pyruvate (Fluka), glutamine (Sigma‒Aldrich) and antibiotics (Sigma‒Aldrich) at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

Human samples

Buffy coats from healthy donors (HDs) were provided by the Hospital Churruca-Visca (Buenos Aires, Argentina). This study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of IBYME (protocol 9347, date of approval: March 3, 2023). Additionally, written informed consent was obtained from the participating subjects. Although we did not perform specific questionnaires to the blood donors to identify possible confounding factors, buffy coats were obtained from blood donors who met the rigorous health screening criteria required for blood donation. This protocol excludes individuals with acute illnesses, many chronic conditions, and those on specific medications, providing a baseline level of homogeneity. Moreover, every blood sample underwent standard serological and PCR testing for major blood-borne pathogens (e.g., HIV, HBV, HCV).

Cell separation

PBMCs were obtained by Ficoll-Paque™ Plus (GE Life Sciences) centrifugation of the blood samples. NK cells and T cells were isolated using RosetteSep (StemCell) and Ficoll-Paque™ Plus centrifugation. The purity of the isolated cells was always above 90%, as assessed by flow cytometry (FC) as CD3−CD56+ cells (NK cells) or CD3+ cells (T cells).

Flow cytometry

Nonspecific staining was blocked with 10% normal mouse serum. The cells were labeled with the following monoclonal antibodies (mAbs): APC-conjugated anti-CD56 (clone N901) from Beckman Coulter; PE- and FITC-conjugated anti-CD3 (clone UCHT1), PE-conjugated anti-CD25 (clone BC96), Brilliant Violet 421-conjugated anti-IFN-γ (clone 4 S. B3), Brilliant Violet 421-conjugated anti-CD11a/CD18 LFA-1 (clone m24), Brilliant Violet 421-conjugated anti-T-bet (clone 4B10), APC-conjugated anti-IL-2 (clone W19046A), PE-conjugated anti-perforin (clone.

B-D48) from BioLegend; PerCp-Cy5.5-conjugated anti-CD4 (clone OKT4) from Tonbo. Cell viability was determined with ZombieAqua from BioLegend. The m24 clone of the anti LFA-1 antibody used recognizes only the open conformation of LFA-1 which interacts with ICAM-1 [23]. The expression of the cytokines IL-2 and IFN-γ and the transcription factor T-bet was analyzed via intracellular FC using FOXP3 Fix/Perm buffer, FOXP3 Perm buffer (BioLegend) and the True Nuclear Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BioLegend), respectively. For assessment of IFN-γ production, cells were cultured in the presence of monensin and brefeldin A (BioLegend) during the last 6 h of culture in the case of NK cells and 4 h in the case of T cells and analyzed by FC. Samples were acquired in a MACSQuant16 Analyzer (Miltenyi Biotec), and data analysis was conducted with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences). The results were expressed as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI), as relative mean fluorescence intensity (rMFI) calculated as the ratio of the MFI produced by a specific mAb divided by the corresponding Fluorescence Minus One (FMO) control, or as the percentage of positive cells.

NK cell effector functions

For the assessment of T-bet expression and IFN-γ production, 5 × 105 PBMCs isolated from HDs were cultured in the absence (control) or in the presence of R, C or R + C for 48 h. During the last 6 h, recombinant IL-12 (10 ng/ml, Peprotech), recombinant IL-15 (1 ng/ml, BioLegend) and recombinant IL-18 (10 ng/ml, MBL International) were added to the cell culture together with, monensin and brefeldin A (BioLegend). The cells were then stained with the anti-CD3 and anti-CD56 mAbs, fixed, permeabilized and stained with the anti-IFN-γ mAb. For cytotoxicity analysis, 1,5 × 105 freshly isolated NK cells were cultured in the absence (control) or in the presence of R, C or R + C for 24 h. Then, cells were washed twice and cocultured for 4 h with 5 × 104 K562 cells previously loaded with eFluor670 (Thermo Fisher). The cells were harvested and stained with Zombie Aqua and with the anti-LFA-1 mAb. Dead K562 cells were identified as eFluor670+Zombie Aquahigh cells. The frequencies of IFN-γ+ NK cells and dead K562 cells were determined by FC.

NK cell: K562 cell conjugates and LFA-1 expression

For the assessment of the frequency of NK cell: K562 cell conjugates, NK cells were stained with CFSE and incubated with eFluor670-labeled K562 cells at a 3:1 ratio. After 1 h, the percentage of conjugates was assessed by FC and calculated as 100 x percentage of CFSE+eFluor670+ cells/percentage of CFSE+ cells. For LFA-1 expression determination, NK cells were cultured with eFluor670 dye-labeled K562 cells for 1 h at 37 °C in the presence of anti LFA-1 antibody. Cells were washed and analyzed by FC for MFI assessment corresponding to LFA-1 expression.

NK cell immune synapse analysis and Perforin localization

2 × 105 Freshly isolated NK cells were cultured in the absence (control) or presence of R + C overnight. Then, 2 × 105 efluor670-stained K562 cells were seeded in poly-D-lysine (Gibco)-coated Nunc Lab Tek Chamber Slides (Thermo Fisher) and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C to facilitate cell adherence. NK cells were washed, seeded at a 1:1 ratio with K562 cells and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. For LFA-1 staining, the cells were incubated in the presence of Brilliant Violet 421 anti-LFA-1 mAb. The cells were washed 3 times and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. For perforin staining, after coculture, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed, blocked and permeabilized with 1% BSA (Sigma) and 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. After washing, cells were stained with anti-perforin mAb in 1% BSA and 0,1% Triton X-100 overnight at 4 °C. The cells were washed, mounted with Aqua Polymount (Polysciences) and visualized in an LSM 880 laser-scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss). Confocal microscopy images were analyzed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health). The fold increase in MFI of LFA-1 at the immune synapse was calculated as the ratio between the MFI at the synaptic interface and the MFI at regions distant from the interface, selecting areas of similar size.

T-cell effector functions

For proliferation assessment, freshly isolated PBMCs were stained with CFSE and 5 × 105 cells were cultured anti-CD3 (clone OKT3)-coated wells and soluble anti-CD28 (clone CD28.2) mAbs (BioLegend) for 5 d in the absence (control) or presence of R, C or R + C. Then, cells were harvested and stained for CD3, CD4, CD25 (Biolegend) and IL-2 (Peprotech). When indicated, 1000 U/ml catalase (Sigma) was added to the medium.

For Th1 differentiation, 5 × 105 isolated T cells were cultured for 4 days in 250 ng anti-CD3-coated wells with 2 ng/ml recombinant human IL-12, 5 ng/ml recombinant human IL-2, 1 µg/ml anti-CD28 and 100ng/ml blocking anti-IL4 mAbs (clone 8D4-8) (BioLegend). After harvesting, the cells were stained with anti-CD4, anti-T-bet and anti-IFN-γ mAbs. The frequencies of IL-2+ and CD25+ CD4 T cells, CFSElow T cells, T-bet+CD4 T cells and IFN-γ+CD4 T cells were determined by FC.

Intracellular H2O2 measurement

Freshly isolated PBMCs were labeled with 10 µM dichlorofluorescein-diacetate (DCF-DA, Cayman Chemicals), washed twice with PBS and 6 × 104 cells were seeded by triplicates in 200 µl of AIM-Medium (Thermo Fisher) in black flat bottom 96 well plates in the absence or in the presence of variable concentrations of R, C or R + C. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 4 h and fluorescence intensity (excitation/emission: 485/535 nm) was assessed in a Spark 20 M multimode fluorometer (Tecan). Statistical analysis

Differences between data sets were analyzed with the two-sided Student’s t test when experimental groups passed normality tests or with Wilcoxon or Mann‒Whitney tests when experimental groups did not pass normality tests, one-way ANOVA (when more than two experimental groups were compared), or two-way ANOVA (when two variables were analyzed), with Dunnett´s, Tukey’s or Holm-Sidak´s multiple hoc tests using GraphPad Prism Software.

Results

Effects of pesticides on NK cell functions

To assess the impact of commercial formulations containing GLY (R), CPF (C) and their combination (R + C) on key immune cells involved in tumor immunosurveillance, we exposed isolated PBMCs to these compounds for 48 h and assessed the frequency of IFN-γ-producing NK cells (gated as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1A) after stimulation with IL-12, IL-15, and IL-18 during the final 6 h of culture. While R or C alone did not affect the production of IFN-γ, the combination R + C significantly reduced the frequency of IFN-γ-producing NK cells in a 42±10% (Fig. 1A and B). Since T-bet is crucial for IFN-γ production by NK cells, we assessed its expression following pesticide exposure. Effects of pesticides on NK cell immune synapse analysis

Since NK cell cytotoxicity critically depends on LFA-1 expression and its precise localization at the immune synapse (NKIS), we next examined whether the R + C mixture disrupts LFA-1 polarization, an adhesion molecule critical for NKIS formation. Confocal imaging revealed a striking difference in LFA-1 distribution. Control NK cells presented a 2-fold enrichment of LFA-1 at the NKIS, whereas R + C treatment caused diffuse LFA-1 localization (Fig. 3A and B). Notably, R + C-treated NK cells presented surface levels of LFA-1 comparable to those of controls (Fig. 3C and D).

We further investigated whether R + C also affects perforin granule polarization. Consistent with the LFA-1 findings, NK cells cultured with K562 cells efficiently clustered perforin granules near the NKIS, whereas R + C-treated NK cells displayed a disorganized granule distribution, with perforin scattered at distant sites from the NKIS (Fig. 3E). Effects of pesticides on T cell functions

Given the pivotal role of Th1 cells in antitumor immunosurveillance and the previously observed partial impairment of T-bet expression in NK cells induced by the pesticide mixture, we investigated whether R, C, or their combination (R + C) affect Th1 polarization in vitro. Under Th1-polarizing conditions, both C and R + C significantly reduced the frequency of T-bet+ CD4 T cells in a 8±4% (CD4+ T cells were gated as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1B), whereas R alone had no effect (Fig. 4A and C). Consistently, T-bet expression in these cells was also reduced by R + C (Fig. 4B and C). Also, compared to control cells, only R + C markedly decreased the frequency of IFN-γ+CD4 T cells in a 39±20% (Fig. 4D and F) and the expression of IFN-γ in these cells (Fig. 4E and F). Moreover, CD8+ T cells also exhibited a reduction in the frequency of IFN-γ producing cells, but only in a 11± 8%(Fig. 4G and I) and in the expression of IFN-γ after R + C treatment but not after individual pesticide exposure (Fig. 4H and I). Discussion

GLY and CPF are widely used as commercial formulations, such as R and C, which, in addition to the active ingredients, also contain excipients and surfactants of undetermined detrimental effects. Of note, current legislation in most countries does not oblige manufacturers to publicly disclose the full list of non-active ingredients nor their precise concentrations in the commercial formulations. This lack of regulatory transparency makes it exceptionally challenging to obtain a comprehensive and realistic understanding of human, animal, and environmental exposure to the complete cocktail of chemicals present in such commercial pesticide products.

There is a demonstrated higher incidence of different cancer types in highly fumigated areas and in pesticide applicators. Particularly, GbH exposure has been associated with an increased non-Hodgkin lymphoma incidence using the Agricultural Health Study (AHS) cohort [24]. Moreover, a recent epidemiological study demonstrated that US regions where a more diverse spectrum of pesticides is used in larger amounts are associated with an increased cancer incidence [25]. It also was observed that GLY was one of the ten most abundantly used pesticides in regions with an increased all cancer, colon cancer and pancreatic cancer incidence.

However, the effects of pesticides on the immune system, especially their commercial formulations and the combination of an herbicide and an insecticide, remain poorly or incompletely explored. Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that these compounds might negatively affect key cell populations responsible for the detection and elimination of neoplastic cells and nascent tumor foci, such as NK cells and CD4 and CD8 T cells, independently of their potential direct carcinogenic activity.

While exposure to individual pesticides had minimal or no effect on NK cell cytokine production, the combination R + C significantly impaired IFN-γ production by NK cells. This functional impairment was associated with reduced expression of T-bet in R + C-treated cells. T-bet is a key transcription factor that regulates not only cytokine expression but also the cytotoxic function of NK cells regulating perforin and granzyme transcription [26]. In fact, T-bet overexpression increases the cytotoxic capacity of NK cells [27]. In accordance with the observed effect of R + C on T-bet expression, exposure of NK cells to this combination of pesticides markedly decreased the cytolytic activity of NK cells. Mechanistically, this reduction was associated with a diminished capacity of NK cells to form conjugates with target cells, a prerequisite for effective cytotoxic responses. Further analysis of the NK cell immune synapse revealed that although total LFA-1 expression remained unchanged, its polarization at the NKIS was markedly disrupted by R + C, suggesting that the mislocalization of this integrin may underlie the observed defects in target cell engagement. Additionally, R + C exposure led to a disorganized distribution of perforin-containing granules, which failed to polarize toward the immune synapse, further compromising cytotoxic function. This is the first report that indicates a negative impact of pesticide on NKIS. These results suggest that the R + C mixture induces cytoskeletal alterations that impact on two key components of the NKIS and the subsequent development of the cytotoxic function of NK cells such as LFA-1 localization and perforin granule polarization [28].

In addition to NK cells, our results demonstrate that the R + C mixture also impairs T-cell-mediated responses, particularly Th1 polarization and proliferation. Under Th1-polarizing conditions, both C and R + C reduced the frequency of T-bet⁺ CD4 T cells, but only the combined treatment significantly decreased the proportion of IFN-γ⁺ CD4 T cells, highlighting the synergistic alteration of Th1 differentiation. Consistent with our findings, a recent study demonstrated that GLY impairs Th1 differentiation in vitro, although at a concentration 7.5 times higher than the one present in R used in our experimental setup [29]. Simultaneously, we observed that exposure to C or R + C reduced T-cell proliferative responses following polyclonal activation with anti-CD3/anti-CD28. While isolated CPF has been shown to exert antiproliferative effects on breast cancer tumor cells and adipocytes [30], this is the first report demonstrating the antiproliferative effects of CPF-based pesticides on human T cells in vitro. Notably, T cells from individuals occupationally exposed to CPF reportedly exhibit reduced proliferation upon stimulation with phytohemagglutinin. In our model, the observed impairment in proliferation was accompanied by a reduced frequency of CD25⁺CD4 T cells and, in the case of R + C exposure, decreased IL-2 production. Given the central role of the IL-2/CD25 axis in supporting T-cell expansion, these findings point to disrupted cytokine signaling as a potential mechanistic link between pesticide exposure and impaired T-cell responses.

Oxidative stress is a well-known disruptor of the immune system, exerting multiple deleterious effects on immune functions [31]. Epidemiological studies have shown a positive correlation between farmers’ exposure to GLY-based herbicides, such as Roundup, and urinary levels of 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), a biomarker of oxidative stress [32]. Also, elevated 8-OHdG levels have also been observed in different types of cancer patients [33]. In our in vitro studies, both R and C induced intracellular H₂O₂ production in PBMCs, with a probably additive effect of their combination. Importantly, the impaired T-cell proliferation observed upon combined exposure was rescued by catalase, indicating that oxidative stress mediates this immunotoxic effect. These findings support the notion that pesticide-induced oxidative stress can impair immune functions and compromise antitumor immunosurveillance.

As a limitation of the study, it is important to mention that we did not perform questionnaires to identify potential occupational and/or environmental pesticide exposure with blood donors to account for potential confounding factors. However, blood donors must meet rigorous health screening criteria, which exclude individuals with acute illnesses, many chronic conditions, or those on specific medications, providing a baseline level of homogeneity. Nevertheless, since blood donors attending the hemotherapy center that provided these samples are urban residents, we expect them to have low or negligible pesticide exposure.

Conclusions

Our findings provide compelling evidence about deleterious effects of commonly used pesticide mixtures on innate and adaptive immune cell effector functions that are critical for immunosurveillance against cancer. Such deleterious effects might impair the control of neoplastic cells and nascent tumor foci, and might partially explain the higher cancer incidence in highly fumigated areas. Our results also underscore the importance of evaluating commercial pesticide formulations and their combinations, rather than individual agents, to accurately assess their immunotoxic potential. l.
