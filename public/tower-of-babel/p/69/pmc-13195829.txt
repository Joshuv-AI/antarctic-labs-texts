Association of screen time and chronotype with depression and anxiety symptoms among Chinese adolescents
Authors: Ye Xu, Yi-fan Lin, Liuxun Li, Yanzhi Li, Liwen Yang, Herui Wu, Caixia Zhang, Wenyu Zhou, Lei Yang, Ciyong Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression and anxiety symptoms among adolescents have increased in recent years, making the identification of modifiable risk factors important. Prolonged screen time (ST) and later chronotype may be independently and jointly associated with adolescent mental health. This study aimed to examine the independent and joint associations of ST and chronotype with depression and anxiety symptoms among adolescents.MethodsData were drawn from the China Adolescent Health Screening Program (CAHSP), a large school-based health screening program conducted in Shenzhen, southern China, using a multistage cluster sampling design, including 10,083 adolescents (mean age: 13.7 [SD: 1. 6] years). ST was assessed using structured self-report items, while chronotype was measured using the Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire-5 (MEQ-5). Depression and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), respectively. Logistic regression models adjusted for relevant covariates were employed to examine independent and joint associations of ST and chronotype with depression and anxiety symptoms.ResultsExcessive ST and later chronotype were significantly associated with depression and anxiety symptoms. Significant interaction indicated that the association between excessive ST and mental health symptoms differed across chronotype groups. Stratified analyses confirmed that within each chronotype group, adolescents with excessive ST had higher odds of depression and anxiety, with the strongest associations observed in evening chronotype (E-type) adolescents (depression: aOR = 2.54, 95% CI: 2.01–3.22; anxiety: aOR = 2.49, 95% CI: 1.98–3.13). Joint analyses further confirmed that E-type adolescents with excessive ST showed the highest odds of screening positive for depression and anxiety symptoms (depression: aOR = 5.55, 95% CI: 4.38–7.02; anxiety: aOR = 4.71, 95% CI: 3.73–5.93) compared with the non-excessive ST and M-type group.ConclusionHigher levels of ST and later chronotype were associated with higher odds of screening positive for depression and anxiety symptoms among adolescents. Public health strategies to promote adolescents’ mental well-being should focus on encouraging healthy screen behaviors and promoting sleep hygiene, particularly for E-type adolescents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12991-026-00659-0.Keywords: Chronotype, Screen time, Depression symptoms, Anxiety symptoms, Adolescent

Introduction

Adolescence is a critical period characterized by significant physical, emotional, and social changes, often coinciding with the onset of mental health problems [1], in which depression and anxiety are common and debilitating conditions [2, 3]. Recent estimates indicate that the prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms among adolescents has reached 25.2% and 20.2%, respectively, and continues to rise [3, 4]. Such symptoms are associated with a wide range of adverse outcomes, including impaired social relationships, poor mental and physical health, and suicide [5, 6]. Given their high prevalence and serious consequences, investigating modifiable risk factors potentially linked to mental health in the youth is essential.

Screen time (ST), commonly defined as the amount of time spent engaging with screen-based devices such as smartphones, computers, and televisions, has emerged as a salient behavioral factor in adolescent development [7]. In China, where digital technologies are deeply integrated into educational activities and daily life, adolescents report particularly high levels of screen use, reflecting not only academic demands but also the need to maintain social connectedness and cope with academic-related stress [8, 9]. At the same time, adolescents’ screen use is shaped by multiple stakeholders. Parents often monitor or restrict screen use at home, schools typically impose limits on device use during school hours and promote balanced digital habits through educational guidance, and broader societal and policy initiatives emphasize the regulation of digital media exposure among minors [10, 11]. Despite these multilevel regulatory efforts, evidence indicates that ST among youth surged significantly during the pandemic and remains elevated [12]. A growing body of research has reported associations between higher levels of ST and poorer mental health outcomes, including increased risks of depression and anxiety [12, 13]. Nevertheless, findings across studies remain inconsistent, suggesting that the relationship between ST and adolescent mental health may not be straightforward [14, 15]. For example, Orben et al., using three large-scale social datasets, found that the relationship between digital technology use and adolescent well-being is negative but small, only explaining 0.4% of the variation in well-being [15], while Przybylski et al. using a representative sample of English adolescents found that digital screen use may be beneficial for mental well-being [14]. These conflicting results underscore the need for further research to better understand the complex relationship between ST and adolescent mental health.

Chronotype, defined as an individual’s preference for the timing of sleep and wakefulness, represents another important factor relevant to adolescent mental health [16]. Chronotype reflects the alignment between endogenous circadian rhythms and externally imposed schedules and is commonly categorized as morning (M-type), intermediate (I-type), or evening type (E-type) [17]. M-type individuals prefer an early bedtime and wake-up schedule, while E-types tend to stay up late and wake up later [18]. In China, adolescents are often subject to highly structured school schedules with relatively early start times alongside substantial evening academic demands [19, 20]. This sociocultural context may increase the likelihood of circadian misalignment and social jetlag, especially for those with an E-type who prefer later sleep-wake cycles [21, 22]. Evidence suggests that, compared with M-type and I-type individuals, E-type individuals tend to report higher levels of anxiety, depression, and poorer general well-being [23, 24]. However, some studies have reported different findings [18, 19]. Furusawa et al., for example, reported significant associations with depression symptoms only among I-type in Japanese male workers [19]. Such inconsistencies highlight the complexity of the relationship between chronotype and mental health and point to the need for further investigation.

Adolescence is characterized by heightened limbic system sensitivity, particularly in the nucleus accumbens, which is involved in reward processing, while the prefrontal cortex, responsible for executive control, remains immature [25, 26]. This developmental imbalance may heighten adolescents’ sensitivity to the immediate rewards offered by digital media while impairing the ability to self-control [27]. Meanwhile, social displacement theory suggests that excessive ST may compromise mental health by displacing time for essential protective activities such as face-to-face social interaction, physical exercise, and sleep [28, 29]. Within this framework, chronotype, particularly E-type, represents an important individual difference that may influence these processes. Prior studies indicate that E-type adolescents are more likely to engage in unhealthy behaviors, including prolonged ST, compared with their M-type counterparts [30]. Moreover, exposure to blue light emitted by screens can delay sleep onset by suppressing melatonin secretion, which may further exacerbate circadian misalignment among E-type adolescents [31, 32], and under rigid school schedules, such misalignment may persist as social jetlag, resulting in chronic alterations in sleep timing and duration [20, 22]. Accumulating evidence suggests that chronic sleep disruption may directly or indirectly contribute to emotional difficulties [33, 34]. Consequently, the co-occurrence of E-type profile and high exposure to screen activities may co-occur as exposure patterns that are jointly associated with poorer mental health outcomes.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the independent and joint associations of ST and chronotype with depression and anxiety symptoms among adolescents. By identifying subgroups characterized by higher observed symptom prevalence at the population level, this study seeks to provide an empirical basis for more targeted prevention and intervention strategies.

Methods

Participants and procedure

The study analyzed cross-sectional data collected in 2024 from the China Adolescent Health Screening Program (CAHSP) in Shenzhen, Guangdong Province, southern China. The program is conducted and administered by the Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital (ChiCTR2400081430, registered on February 29, 2024) and has been collecting health-related data annually since 2013. Detailed information on the study design is available in previous publications [35]. We randomly selected six districts of Shenzhen, and six primary schools, nine junior high schools, and three senior high schools were randomly selected. Each student completed an anonymous self-report questionnaire within a single class period, with the assistance of trained interviewers, which protected the privacy of the participants and avoided any potential information bias. A total of 100,83 students from 237 classes in 18 public schools completed the questionnaires with a response rate of 88.6%. This study was approved by the Shenzhen Second People’s Hospital Institutional Review Board (20211013002-fs01). Written informed consents were obtained from each participant who was at least 18 years old and from parents or guardians of those under the age of 18.

Assessment of Screen Time(ST)

Participants were asked about the average number of hours spent on a weekday and a weekend day for the following items: using computers, using smartphone (or tablet), and watching television (or projector). The average daily time was calculated using the following formula: (weekday ST*5 + weekend ST*2)/7 [36]. In this study, we used the 2-hour cut-off criteria recommended by the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines to define excessive ST among adolescents [37].

Assessment of Chronotype

Chronotype was assessed using the Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire-5 (MEQ-5), a self-report scale consisting of five items with a total score ranging from 4 to 25 [38]. The MEQ-5 has demonstrated good reliability and validity among Chinese adolescents [39]. Subjects were classified into 3 categories according to their total score: E-type (4–11 points), I-type (12–17 points), and M-type (18–25 points) [38].

Assessment of depression symptoms

Depression symptoms were assessed using the Chinese version of the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), a self-report questionnaire that consists of nine items based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-IV (DSM-IV) criteria for assessing symptoms of depression [40]. The PHQ-9 evaluates depression symptoms experienced in the past 14 days and is widely used in Chinese adolescents with good reliability and validity [41]. Each item is scored from 0 (not at all) to 3 (almost every day), resulting in a total score range of 0 to 27. Additionally, depression symptoms were defined as PHQ-9 scores ≥ 5 [40], analyzed as a dichotomous variable (yes or no), with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.90.

Assessment of anxiety symptoms

Anxiety symptoms were measured using the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) scale [42]. The GAD-7 questionnaire is a brief and widely used tool for measuring anxiety symptoms, consisting of 7 questions on anxiety symptoms over the last 14 days. It has good validity in Chinese adolescents [43], with the same response options and scoring as the PHQ-9. The total score ranged from 0 to 21, participants with GAD-7 ≥ 5 were categorized as having anxiety symptoms [42]. The GAD-7 demonstrated high reliability in this sample, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.92.

Assessment of potential covariates

Covariates were selected as potential confounders based on prior literature and were obtained through a structured questionnaire [44, 45]. The covariates included age, sex (1 = boy, 2 = girl), household socioeconomic status (HSS), living arrangement, academic performance, academic pressure, relationship with classmates, teachers, and parents, smoking, and drinking. Except for age (continuous variables), all variables were included as categorical variables. Household socioeconomic status (HSS) was assessed by asking participants about their household economic status (1 = good, 2 = average, 3 = poor). Living arrangement was measured according to the individuals they lived with (1 = living with parents, 2 = living with a single parent, 3 = living with others). Academic performance was evaluated by asking students to report their ranking in the final exam of the academic semester closest to the survey period (1 = good, 2 = average, 3 = poor). Relationships with classmates, teachers, and parents were assessed through asking, “How would you rate your relationship with your classmates, teachers, or parents?” (1 = good, 2 = average, 3 = poor). Smoking status and drinking status were collected by asking the students, “Have you used at least one cigarette during your lifetime?” and “Have you had at least one drink of alcohol during your lifetime?” (0 = no, 1 = yes).

Statistical analyses

First, descriptive analyses were conducted, with continuous variables presented as mean (standard deviation, SD) and categorical variables as number (percentage, %). Participant characteristics were compared based on depression symptoms and anxiety symptoms status using two-sample t-tests for continuous variables and Chi-squared tests for categorical variables. To enhance transparency regarding the underlying data structure, the continuous bivariate distribution of ST and chronotype was first visualized prior to categorization, and subsequently described stratified by depression and anxiety symptom status. Second, logistic regression models were employed to examine the associations of ST and chronotype with depression and anxiety symptoms separately. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for potential covariates that were significant at the 0.10 level in Table 1 or widely reported in previous literature. Model 3 was further adjusted for both ST and chronotype to assess their independent effects. Third, to assess whether the association between ST and mental health outcomes varied across chronotype groups, multiplicative interaction terms between ST and chronotype were included in the regression models. If interactions were significant, stratified analyses were conducted to estimate associations within each chronotype group. Finally, to assess the joint effects of ST and chronotype, participants were classified into six groups based on the combination of excessive ST (yes or no) and chronotype (M-type, I-type, or E-type). Multivariable logistic regression models, adjusting for the same covariates in Model 2, were applied to estimate the associations between these joint groups and depression and anxiety symptoms, with the group with non-excessive ST and M-type chronotype as the reference.

In addition, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. First, ST and chronotype were treated as continuous variables and modeled using restricted cubic splines to explore potential non-linear associations with depression and anxiety symptoms. Second, depression and anxiety symptom scores were analyzed as continuous outcomes, while ST and chronotype were retained as categorical variables, with multivariable regression models adjusted for the same covariates as in Model 2.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Missing values were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations. Five imputed datasets were generated, and estimates from association analyses were pooled. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of participants

Sample characteristics stratified by depression and anxiety symptom status are presented in Table 1. This study included 10,083 adolescents in the final analyses, with a mean (SD) age of 13.7 (1.6) years, and almost half were boys (53.5%). The prevalence of depression symptoms and anxiety symptoms was 40.1% and 37.4%, respectively. Adolescents with depression or anxiety symptoms were more likely to be girls, living with a single parent or others, have poor HSS, have poor academic performance, have above average academic pressure, have poor relationships with classmates, teachers, and parents, smoking, and drinking than those without symptoms.

Distribution of ST and chronotype

Figure 1 A presents the continuous bivariate distribution of ST and chronotype in the overall sample. The plot illustrates the spread and clustering of observations across the chronotype spectrum and ST range, providing an overview of the raw data structure prior to categorization. Higher densities were observed in the lower ST range, particularly among adolescents with I-type.

The distribution of ST and chronotype among participants according to the presence of depression and anxiety symptoms was presented in Fig. 1B-C. Overall, nearly half of the participants (48.4%) reported excessive ST. The I-type was most common (68.2%), followed by E-type (16.2%) and M-type (15.6%).

Marked differences were observed in mental health status. Adolescents with depression symptoms were more than twice as likely to exhibit an E-type chronotype compared with those without symptoms (24.2% vs. 10.8%), and a similar pattern was observed for anxiety symptoms (25.0% vs. 11.0%). Excessive ST was also substantially more prevalent among adolescents with depression (60.1% vs. 40.4%) and anxiety symptoms (59.0% vs. 41.9%).

Joint associations of ST and chronotype with depression and anxiety symptoms

Participants were classified into six groups based on excessive ST (yes or no) and chronotype (M-type, I-type, or E-type), with the combination of non-excessive ST and M-type as the reference group. As shown in Fig. 2, after adjusting for covariates, both depression and anxiety symptoms increased with excessive ST and later chronotype. Compared with the reference group, those with excessive ST and E-type chronotype had the highest odds of mental health symptoms, with approximately 5.6-fold higher odds of depression (aOR = 5.55, 95% CI: 4.38–7.02) and approximately 4.7-fold higher odds of anxiety symptoms (aOR = 4.71, 95% CI: 3.73–5.93).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. Restricted cubic spline analyses suggested non-linear associations with depression and anxiety symptoms, with patterns broadly consistent with the categorization used in the primary analyses (Fig. S1). Analyses using continuous depression and anxiety symptom scores produced results similar to the primary findings (Table S1).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale study to investigate the joint associations of ST and chronotype with depression and anxiety symptoms. Our findings revealed that the prevalence of depression symptoms and anxiety symptoms among Chinese adolescents was 40.1% and 37.4%, respectively, which were higher compared to the previous research [2]. One possible explanation is that, in the post-pandemic era, factors such as relative isolation, boredom, elevated parental scrutiny, and the loss of independence may have adversely impacted adolescents’ mental health [46].

Approximately 48.4% of participants reported daily ST exceeding 2 h. This pattern may partly reflect the sociocultural context in China, where adolescents increasingly rely on digital communication to maintain connections with peers and family members, potentially leading to more frequent use of electronic devices [8, 47]. Furthermore, cultural norms emphasizing academic achievement and social comparison, particularly within China’s highly exam-oriented education system, may motivate adolescents to engage in electronic device use as a coping strategy to manage academic stress [9]. Consistent with prior research, the present study found that adolescents with excessive ST were significantly more likely to experience depression and anxiety symptoms [48–50]. It is believed that excessive ST may displace time that could otherwise be devoted to protective activities, such as physical exercise [49], reduce face-to-face communication with family members, and limit opportunities for in-person social interaction, leading to heightened loneliness and depressive symptoms [51]. Additionally, prolonged ST may increase adolescents’ exposure to negative online content, including cyberbullying and distressing news, further exacerbating mental health issues [52].

Moreover, the current study showed that chronotype was associated with depression and anxiety symptoms, similar associations have been reported in population-based studies conducted in different cultural contexts [24, 53, 54]. Although the present study cannot test underlying mechanisms, prior research has proposed several neurobiological and psychological pathways that may help contextualize these associations. Individuals with later chronotype have been linked to heightened amygdala activation and reduced connectivity with the cingulate gyrus. These neural alterations have been proposed to underlie stronger emotional reactivity to negative stimuli and impaired the regulation of the amygdala by the cingulate cortex, which may help explain the observed associations with depression and anxiety symptoms [55]. Additionally, E-types tend to be more present-oriented and less future-oriented [56]. They are also more likely to adopt non-adaptive emotion regulation strategies, which can prolong or exacerbate negative emotions, potentially contributing to a higher likelihood of screening positive for these symptoms [57].

In addition, the associations between excessive ST and depression and anxiety symptoms varied across chronotype groups. While excessive ST was associated with higher observed burden of depression and anxiety symptoms across all chronotypes, the strength of these associations differed, with significantly stronger associations observed among adolescents with an E-type. In competitive academic environments, high educational demands may reinforce later sleep–wake patterns, while rigid early school start times often conflict with adolescents’ biological rhythms [20–22]. This circadian misalignment is particularly pronounced among E-type adolescents, who often have difficulty initiating sleep early enough on school nights and may spend additional wake time engaging in screen-based activities [58]. As a result, chronic sleep deprivation may occur, which may contribute to heightened emotional reactivity, impaired mood regulation, and increased odds of screening positive for depression and anxiety symptoms [59, 60]. Besides, exposure to blue light emitted by screens can further delay the onset of sleep by suppressing melatonin production, a hormone that regulates sleep-wake cycles [32]. This delay can intensify the existing circadian misalignment in E-type adolescents, leading to more pronounced sleep disturbances and greater emotional dysregulation [31]. These findings suggest that excessive ST and chronotype jointly characterize adolescents with a higher observed burden of depression and anxiety symptoms, rather than acting as isolated correlates. This effect aligns with previous findings [17, 61, 62]. Rong et al. found that E-type and problematic smartphone use were interactively associated with higher odds of non-suicidal self-injury [61]. Similarly, Hisler et al. examined chronotype as a moderator of the associations between screen-based activities and sleep, and found that individuals with a later chronotype exhibited stronger associations between screen media use and sleep outcomes [62]. To strengthen mechanistic inference and translational relevance, future studies should adopt promising research paradigms. Multimodal longitudinal designs integrating circadian biology (e.g., melatonin rhythms), sleep architecture, and emotional regulation processes may help disentangle the biological and behavioral pathways linking chronotype and ST to depression and anxiety. Furthermore, intervention studies targeting modifiable factors, such as optimizing school schedules, reducing evening screen exposure, or promoting chronotype-aligned sleep hygiene, may facilitate the translation of mechanistic insights into effective prevention strategies for adolescent mental health.

Furthermore, although the observed associations between ST, chronotype, and depression and anxiety symptoms were modest in magnitude, these effect sizes align with prior population-based studies of behavioral risk factors [49, 63, 64]. Importantly, in the context of highly prevalent exposures, such as prolonged ST and E-type, even modest increases in relative odds can translate into a substantial impact at the population level [17, 36]. From a public health perspective, widely distributed behavioral risks may cumulatively contribute to a significant mental health burden [65]. Therefore, these results should be interpreted not merely in terms of individual clinical prediction but regarding their broader implications for population-level prevention. Specifically, our findings highlight the necessity of integrated intervention strategies: to effectively mitigate the burden of depression and anxiety, public health initiatives should concurrently target the regulation of ST and the promotion of healthy sleep hygiene, particularly for adolescents with an E-type orientation.

There are several limitations of the study should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to infer causal directionality or capture within-person dynamic processes. Recent research has highlighted substantial heterogeneity in within-person associations between digital media use, sleep-related behaviors, and mental health, challenging assumptions of homogeneity implicit in between-person analyses. As such, our findings should be interpreted as population-level associations rather than evidence of mechanistic pathways. Second, the present study did not include direct measures of emotional regulation, self-control, or family supervision. Prior research suggests that these factors may play an important role in shaping the associations between sleep-related behaviors and mental health outcomes [33, 34, 66]. The absence of such measures may therefore oversimplify the complexity of the sleep-mental health link. Third, all key variables, including ST, chronotype, and depression and anxiety symptoms, were assessed using self-reported questionnaires. Although these measures have been widely used and validated in adolescent populations, self-report data are inherently susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias. These potential biases may have attenuated or inflated the observed associations. Future research would benefit from incorporating objective assessments, such as digital device usage logs, actigraphy-based sleep measures, or multi-informant reports, to further enhance measurement accuracy and reduce bias. Fourth, the sample was drawn from a single geographic region in China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to adolescents from other regions with different educational systems, sociocultural contexts, or patterns of screen use and sleep behavior. Future research employing multi-region and cross-cultural designs is needed to determine whether the observed associations persist across more diverse adolescent populations.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of Chinese adolescents, excessive ST and chronotype were both independently associated with higher odds of screening positive for depression and anxiety symptoms. Adolescents with an E-type showed the highest odds of screening positive, particularly when combined with excessive screen exposure, reflecting a pattern of co-occurring exposures associated with a greater symptom burden.

These findings underscore the importance of considering individual circadian preferences when evaluating the mental health correlates of screen use. Given the widespread use of digital devices among adolescents, targeted interventions should focus on promoting healthy screen habits and sleep hygiene. Strategies such as limiting screen use before bedtime and encouraging regular sleep schedules may help mitigate the adverse effects of screen exposure, particularly among E-type adolescents. Future longitudinal studies are needed to further explore the causal mechanisms underlying these associations and to develop effective prevention and intervention strategies aimed at reducing mental health risks in adolescents.
