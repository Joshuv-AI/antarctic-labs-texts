BCMA CAR T for AL Amyloidosis After Plasmacytic Relapse of Lymphoplasmacytic Lymphoma
Authors: Nadia Al Haddad, Eugene Brailovski, Jacqueline Dela‐Pena, Melinda Cook, Michael Rosenzweig, Heather Jolie Landau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionLambda immunoglobulin light chain (AL) amyloidosis associated with non‐IgM lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL) is rare and often refractory to conventional therapies. Treatment options for relapsed disease remain limited.MethodsHere we describe the case of a patient with a history of LPL‐associated AL amyloidosis who had previously been treated with multiple B‐cell‐ and plasma‐cell‐directed regimens. At the time of relapse, pathology demonstrated plasmacytic histology, with BCMA‐expressing plasma cells by immunohistochemistry and no detectable B‐cell infiltrate. The patient subsequently received investigational B‐cell maturation antigen (BCMA)‐directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy.ResultsFollowing BCMA‐directed CAR T‐cell therapy, the patient achieved a complete hematologic response.ConclusionThis case highlights the biologic complexity of LPL‐associated AL amyloidosis and supports the use of BCMA‐targeted therapy, especially when the relapsing or residual disease is comprised by the plasma cell component.Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06097832.Keywords: amyloidosis, CAR T, lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL), plasma cell disorder

Light chain (AL) amyloidosis results from the production of misfolded amyloidogenic immunoglobulin light chains that deposit in organs, leading to progressive dysfunction [1]. AL amyloidosis most commonly arises from clonal plasma cell disorders, whereas association with lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma (LPL) is exceptionally rare and difficult to treat [2, 3]. B‐cell maturation antigen (BCMA) is expressed on plasma cells and has emerged as a therapeutic target in plasma cell disorders. BCMA‐directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapies have demonstrated high efficacy in multiple myeloma and are being explored in relapsed/refractory AL amyloidosis [4]. However, data remain limited, particularly in cases arising in the context of LPL.

We report a case of relapsed AL amyloidosis initially associated with non‐IgM LPL, in which investigational BCMA‐directed CAR T‐cell therapy induced a deep hematologic response in the setting of predominantly plasmacytic relapse.

A 74‐year‐old woman presented in 2015 with nephrotic range proteinuria (3.5 g/day), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) > 60 mL/min and IgG lambda M‐spike level of 0.42 g/dL on serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP). Serum free light chains showed free lambda 8.25 mg/dL and free kappa 1.28 mg/dL. Bone marrow immunohistochemistry showed 5%–10% plasma cells and < 5% CD20+ cells, but aggregates where CD20 is positive in 50%–70% of cells. Flow cytometry identified CD138+ and CD38+ plasma cells, as well as a CD5+, CD19+, CD20+ cell population with overexpression of lambda light chains in both, suggesting LPL. MYD88 and CXCR4 mutations were not detected. No evidence of 1p deletion, 1q gain, 13q deletion, TP53 deletion or t(11;14). Kidney biopsy demonstrated diffuse amyloid infiltration of the mesangium and capillary walls. Immunofluorescence revealed +2 staining for IgG and lambda. She had an elevated brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) (183 ng/L) with a normal troponin (< 0.02 mcg/L) and no echocardiographic evidence of infiltrative cardiomyopathy. Therefore, she was diagnosed with AL amyloidosis with renal involvement (Stage I) and potential cardiac involvement (Boston University Stage II) [5, 6].

The patient did not achieve a hematologic response to either frontline bortezomib/dexamethasone or second‐line bendamustine/rituximab. Third‐line carfilzomib achieved a hematologic very good partial response (VGPR) by International Society of Amyloidosis hematologic response criteria, but proteinuria continued to rise, peaking at 5 g/day [7]. She received fourth‐line therapy with pharmacokinetic‐directed melphalan followed by autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HCT) as part of a clinical trial. Following HCT, serum free light chains normalized, but a small monoclonal protein (M‐spike 0.15 g/dL, IgG lambda) persisted, indicating ongoing hematologic VGPR. Three months following HCT, urine protein excretion decreased to 2.2 g/day, indicating a partial renal response based on validated criteria [6]. Six months following HCT, proteinuria further decreased to 1.5 g/day, consistent with a renal VGPR. The patient achieved a proteinuria nadir of 0.6 g/day 3 years post‐HCT.

Seven years later, lambda free light chains rose to 6.10 mg/dL (kappa of 1.96 mg/dL), accompanied by increasing proteinuria and declining serum albumin. Bone marrow biopsy showed < 5% lambda‐restricted plasma cells without B‐cell infiltrates. Over 80% of plasma cells had positive membranous/Golgi staining for BCMA by immunohistochemistry. Salvage therapy with fifth‐line daratumumab monotherapy and then the combination of daratumumab, ixazomib, and cyclophosphamide as sixth line did not result in a response.

She was eligible for and enrolled in a clinical trial studying an investigational BCMA‐directed CAR T‐cell therapy for patients with systemic AL amyloidosis. Pretreatment serum free lambda was 6.71 mg/dL, free kappa was 1.28 mg/dL, and IgG lambda M protein was 0.41 mg/dL. Serum albumin decreased to 2.8 g/dL, 24 h protein excretion increased to 2.481 g. N‐Terminal Pro‐BNP (NT‐Pro BNP) was 1884 pg/mL, consistent with stable Stage II cardiac involvement based on Mayo 2004 criteria [8]. Bridging therapy was not administered. She received lymphodepletion with cyclophosphamide (250 mg/m2) and fludarabine (20 mg/m2) (dose reduced for glomerular filtration rate 43 mL/min/1.73 m2) on Days 5, 4, and 3 (Figure 1A). Lymphodepletion was complicated by neutropenic fever and prerenal acute kidney injury (AKI) (Stage 1) in the setting of noninfectious diarrhea, which improved with intravenous hydration. Grade 1 cytokine release syndrome (CRS) occurred on Day 1 and Grade 2 CRS on Day 2 following CAR T‐cell infusion. She was treated with tocilizumab both days and two doses of dexamethasone on Days 2 and 3. IL‐6 peaked to 19,191 pg/mL from a baseline of 11 pg/mL. She developed a second episode of AKI (Stage 3) 48 h after infusion, with creatinine rising to 7.6 mg/dL from a baseline 1.3 mg/dL. Nephrology evaluation favored acute tubular necrosis (ATN), likely multifactorial, including prerenal azotemia progressing to ATN in association with CRS‐related hemodynamic changes and underlying renal vulnerability in the setting of amyloidosis. She was managed conservatively and did not require kidney replacement therapy. She also developed a catheter‐related deep vein thrombosis (Grade 2). Anticoagulation led to a large chest‐wall hematoma (Grade 2) requiring blood product support. She had no evidence of early or delayed neurotoxicity. Peak CAR‐T expansion occurred between Days 7 and 15 (Figure 1B). Free light chains normalized after 7 days, but a small M‐spike of 0.11 g/dL persisted following CAR T‐cell infusion. At Day +25, there was no measurable residual disease (MRD) in the bone marrow by flow cytometry (10−5). MRD was assessed using a single 10‐color tube using a previously described method [9]. Four months following CAR T‐cell therapy, she achieved a hematologic complete response (CR) (serum and urine immunofixation negative) and remains in CR at 12 months following BCMA‐directed CAR‐T treatment [7]. Creatinine returned to baseline (1.2 mg/dL) and she achieved a partial renal response with proteinuria improving to 1.1 g/24 h 10 months following CAR T‐cell therapy [10]. NT‐proBNP is 1123 pg/mL, consistent with stable cardiac involvement [11] (Figure 1C). The date cut‐off was March 1, 2026.

LPL is a B‐cell neoplasm defined by the clonal expansion of B cells, lymphoplasmacytic cells, and plasma cells within the bone marrow and in some cases, other tissues [2, 3]. LPL is classified based on the presence of bone marrow infiltration and the type of paraprotein produced as Waldenström macroglobulinemia (WM) or non‐WM LPL. WM, distinguished by an IgM paraprotein, accounts for the vast majority of LPL, while non‐WM LPL is uncommon with distinct clinicopathologic and molecular features [3]. Non‐IgM LPLs are typically composed of plasma cells and often show minimal overt B‐cell infiltration, although small clonal B‐cell populations may still be identifiable in a subset of cases. They usually secrete IgG or IgA and lack MYD88 and L265P mutations [3]. Despite these differences, management parallels WM, with treatment reserved for symptomatic disease and response assessment using WM criteria [2]. Recommended therapies include bendamustine, rituximab, Bruton's tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors, and high‐dose melphalan (HDM) with HCT, but complete hematologic responses are uncommon [2].

AL amyloidosis typically results from a plasma cell dyscrasia producing lambda light chains immunoglobulin in 75%–80% of cases and kappa light chains in < 25% [1]. Misfolded light chains deposit in organs, most commonly the heart, kidneys, and peripheral nerves, causing progressive dysfunction and death [1]. Treatment is aimed at eradicating the plasma‐cell clone to achieve both hematologic and organ responses, and the depth of hematologic response based largely on the light chain levels predicts both the likelihood of organ response and is associated with longer survival [7]. The addition of daratumumab to cyclophosphamide, bortezomib, and dexamethasone leads to increased rates of VGPR/CR and organ response [13]. It is also associated with an improved overall survival [14].

Rarely, AL amyloidosis can be associated with B‐cell lymphoproliferative disorder, including LPL. These cases are challenging to treat because therapies targeting the clone in LPL do not typically achieve the depth of response necessary to halt amyloidogenic light‐chain production, leading to organ progression [15]. Historically, HDM/HCT has provided the deepest and most durable responses but has also been associated with treatment‐related morbidity and mortality [16].

BCMA is universally expressed on plasma cells, making it an ideal immunotherapy target in patients with multiple myeloma [17]. The two Food and Drug Association approved BCMA CAR T‐cell therapies, ciltacabtagene autoleucel and idecabtagene vicleucel, have shown high efficacy in relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma [18, 19]. More recently, BCMA CAR T‐cell therapy in patients with AL amyloidosis has resulted in high rates of hematologic and organ responses, with manageable CRS and no reported neurotoxicity [20].

This case highlights the therapeutic and biologic complexity of AL amyloidosis associated with LPL. Our patient failed multiple commonly used B‐cell‐ and plasma‐cell‐directed therapies and achieved the most durable remission with HDM/HCT. While relapse was characterized by plasma cell disease without detectable lymphoma, the patient did not respond to daratumumab alone or in combination with a proteasome inhibitor. Remarkably, BCMA CAR T‐cell therapy resulted in the rapid eradication of the clonal disease, achieving a hematologic CR. While Grade 2 CRS was successfully managed with tocilizumab and dexamethasone, patients with renal involvement from AL amyloidosis are particularly susceptible to AKI. Renal function often depends on a narrow hemodynamic window, and slight decreases in intravascular volume can precipitate prerenal AKI and subsequent ATN. In addition, patients with cardiac amyloid involvement are prone to fluid overload due to restrictive cardiomyopathy, impaired diastolic filling, and low stroke volume. This combination creates a very tight therapeutic margin, where even small perturbations in fluid status, blood pressure, or nephrotoxic exposures can lead to rapid clinical deterioration and underscores the vulnerability of patients with amyloidosis and multi‐organ involvement. Notably, the median time to organ response in AL amyloidosis has been reported to range from approximately 6–12 months, but best renal and cardiac responses are often delayed, occurring more than 2 years after the initiation of effective therapy [1, 21].

Fludarabine pharmacokinetics may be altered in patients with renal impairment and dose adjustments are commonly employed to mitigate toxicity. In this patient, fludarabine was dose‐reduced based on impaired kidney function. The relationship between drug exposure, efficacy, and toxicity cannot be determined from a single case, but this is an area that is ripe for study. Identifying an optimal fludarabine exposure that maximizes efficacy and minimizes toxicity may be particularly valuable for this vulnerable patient population [22, 23].

To our knowledge, this is the first report of successful BCMA CAR T‐cell therapy in a patient with AL amyloidosis arising from non‐IgM LPL. Although the relapsing disease at the time of treatment demonstrated a predominantly plasmacytic phenotype, the patient, who was anti‐CD38 inhibitor naïve, had no response to a combination of anti‐CD38 antibody therapy plus a proteasome inhibitor. Yet, BCMA CAR T‐cell therapy induced a sustained hematologic CR with associated renal improvement. While longer follow‐up is needed to assess the durability and extent of renal recovery, the depth of clonal eradication already observed is unprecedented in this setting and represents a novel, promising therapeutic avenue for a historically difficult to treat population.
