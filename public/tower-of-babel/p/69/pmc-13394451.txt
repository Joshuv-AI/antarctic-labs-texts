MiR‐146a‐5p Suppresses M1 Polarization of Tumor‐Associated Macrophages via IRAK1/NF‐κB Signaling in Colorectal Polyps
Authors: Peng Song, Lianhui Wu, Huabin Qiu, Lei Luo, Jianzhao Qiu, Shuping Xiao, Jincai He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) play a pivotal role in shaping the inflammatory microenvironment of colorectal polyps. MiR‐146a‐5p has been implicated in innate immune regulation, but its function in TAM polarization and polyp pathogenesis remains unclear.MethodsForty paired colorectal polyp and adjacent normal tissues were analyzed for miR‐146a‐5p, IRAK1, and macrophage markers (CD86/CD206) by qRT‐PCR and immunohistochemistry. THP‐1‐derived macrophages were polarized to M1 or M2 phenotypes, transfected with miR‐146a‐5p mimic/inhibitor, si‐IRAK1, or IRAK1 overexpression plasmids, and treated with polyp‐conditioned medium (Polyp‐CM) where indicated. Macrophage polarization was evaluated by flow cytometry, qRT‐PCR, ELISA, and Western blot. Dual‐luciferase reporter assays in HEK293T cells validated direct targeting of IRAK1 by miR‐146a‐5p. Rescue experiments assessed the functional contribution of IRAK1 and NF‐κB signaling to M1 polarization.ResultsMiR‐146a‐5p was significantly downregulated in polyp tissues, whereas IRAK1 and M1 macrophage markers (CD86, iNOS) were upregulated, with a strong negative correlation between miR‐146a‐5p and IRAK1. Overexpression of miR‐146a‐5p in macrophages suppressed M1 polarization, decreased pro‐inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐6), and reduced NF‐κB activation, while miR‐146a‐5p inhibition enhanced these responses. Dual‐luciferase assays confirmed IRAK1 as a direct target of miR‐146a‐5p. Silencing IRAK1 or inhibiting NF‐κB rescued the pro‐M1 effects induced by miR‐146a‐5p loss. Conversely, IRAK1 overexpression promoted M1 polarization, which was attenuated by miR‐146a‐5p co‐expression. Polyp‐CM experiments corroborated these effects under tumor‐mimicking inflammatory conditions.ConclusionThese findings suggest that the miR‐146a‐5p/IRAK1/NF‐κB axis may represent a potential therapeutic target for modulating macrophage‐mediated inflammatory responses in colorectal polyps.

Introduction

Colorectal polyps represent an early pathological stage in colorectal tumorigenesis, and their development and malignant transformation are tightly linked to alterations in the local immune microenvironment [1]. Recent systematic reviews of colorectal adenomas indicate significant changes in immune cell infiltration, cytokine expression and stromal–immune interactions even before invasive cancer emerges [2]. These immune‐microenvironmental changes include increased expression of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and altered macrophage infiltration, suggesting that early inflammatory processes within polyps may contribute to neoplastic progression [3].

Within the polyp and early tumor niches, tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs) have emerged as central regulators of the local immune microenvironment [1]. These macrophages are highly plastic and, depending on environmental cues such as microbial products, cytokines, and tissue‐derived signals, can polarize into classically activated (M1) or alternatively activated (M2) phenotypes [4]. M1 macrophages are induced by stimuli such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ), and are characterized by secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines including IL‐6 and TNF‐α, elevated expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and generation of reactive oxygen or nitrogen species; as such, they contribute to chronic inflammation, epithelial damage, and stromal activation, forming a key component of the early inflammatory milieu in colorectal polyps [5]. In contrast, M2 macrophages, which can be driven by IL‐4, IL‐13, IL‐10 or tumor‐derived factors, express markers such as CD206 and Arg1, promote immunosuppression, tissue remodeling, and matrix remodeling, and are associated with tumor progression and immune escape [6]. Thus, the balance between M1 and M2 macrophage populations within the polyp microenvironment may be associated with inflammatory status and immune regulation in colorectal polyps [7].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) act as critical post‐transcriptional regulators by binding to the 3′ untranslated regions (3′‐UTRs) of target mRNAs, leading to translational repression or mRNA degradation, thereby modulating diverse cellular pathways including inflammation, immune responses, and tumorigenesis [8, 9]. Among these, miR‐146a‐5p has been identified as a key immunoregulatory miRNA, which is downregulated in various inflammatory and neoplastic conditions, including colorectal adenomas and colorectal cancer, suggesting its potential role in controlling early inflammatory processes in the gut mucosa [10, 11, 12]. Interleukin‐1 receptor‐associated kinases (IRAKs) are a family of kinases that play central roles in innate immunity and inflammation and are increasingly implicated in the development and progression of cancer [13]. IRAK1 functions as a central adaptor kinase in Toll‐like receptor (TLR) and interleukin‐1 receptor (IL‐1R) signaling, mediating NF‐κB activation and driving pro‐inflammatory responses [14, 15]. Dysregulation of the NF‐κB signaling has been implicated in the polarization of macrophages toward the M1 phenotype, which contributes to the inflammatory microenvironment of colorectal polyps [16]. In light of these findings, we performed bioinformatic predictions using TargetScan and other miRNA‐target databases, which suggested that IRAK1 is a putative direct target of miR‐146a‐5p. Based on this, we hypothesized that downregulation of miR‐146a‐5p may lead to IRAK1 upregulation, potentially leading to NF‐κB–mediated M1 macrophage polarization and influencing local inflammatory responses in colorectal polyps.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the expression of miR‐146a‐5p in human colorectal polyp tissues and elucidate its mechanistic role in TAM M1 polarization via the IRAK1/NF‐κB pathway. By integrating clinical sample analyses with in vitro macrophage polarization models and mechanistic assays, our work seeks to clarify the immune‐inflammatory regulatory mechanisms underlying polyp development, thereby providing novel insights into potential early‐intervention strategies targeting macrophage‐mediated inflammatory regulation in colorectal polyps.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Sample Collection

Forty colorectal polyp specimens were obtained from patients undergoing polypectomy between 2024 and 2025, along with 40 paired adjacent normal mucosal tissues serving as controls. Inclusion criteria were histopathologically confirmed colorectal polyps (adenomatous or non‐adenomatous), no history of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy, and availability of complete clinical data. The detailed clinicopathological characteristics and histological subtypes are summarized in Table 1. Exclusion criteria included inflammatory bowel disease, colorectal cancer, other malignancies, or recent use of immunosuppressive agents. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with written informed consent obtained from all participants. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Sanming First Hospital Affiliated to Fujian Medical University (Fujian, China) and carried out following institutional policies for medical and research ethics.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) Staining

Paraffin‐embedded colorectal polyp tissue sections (4 μm thickness) were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in citrate buffer (pH = 6.0) for 20 min using a microwave. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature to reduce nonspecific binding. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against IRAK1, CD86, and CD206. After washing, sections were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Finally, sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. For quantitative analysis, five randomly selected high‐power fields (×400 magnification) per section were evaluated. Staining intensity and the percentage of positively stained cells were analyzed using ImageJ software. A uniform threshold setting was applied for all images within each marker to ensure consistency. The evaluation was performed independently by two investigators who were blinded to the clinical information of the samples. The final score was calculated as the mean value of the two observers.

Cell Source and Culture Conditions

THP‐1 monocytes (ATCC TIB‐202) were used for macrophage experiments and cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. Differentiation into macrophages (M0) was induced by treating THP‐1 cells with 100 nM phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA) for 24 h. HEK293T cells were used for dual‐luciferase reporter assays and maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS under identical culture conditions.

Cell Treatments

THP‐1‐derived macrophages were polarized to M1 or M2 phenotypes to investigate the functional role of miR‐146a‐5p. M1 polarization was induced by LPS (100 ng/mL) and IFN‐γ (20 ng/mL) for 24 h, whereas M2 polarization was induced using IL‐4 (20 ng/mL) and IL‐13 (20 ng/mL) for 24 h. To simulate the polyp microenvironment, polyp‐conditioned medium (Polyp‐CM) was prepared individually from each freshly resected colorectal polyp specimen. Briefly, tissue samples were washed with sterile PBS, minced into small fragments, and cultured in serum‐free medium at a standardized ratio of approximately 1 g tissue per 10 mL medium for 48 h at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The supernatant was collected, centrifuged to remove cellular debris, and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before use. The resulting Polyp‐CM from each sample was used separately without pooling and applied to THP‐1‐derived macrophages for 48 h.

Cell Transfection

THP‐1 macrophages and HEK293T cells were transfected with miR‐146a‐5p mimic, inhibitor, or negative control (mimic NC or inhibitor NC), si‐IRAK1, si‐NC, pcDNA3.1‐IRAK1, and pcDNA3.1 using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Following 24 h of transfection, the culture medium was replaced, and cells were harvested 48 h later for subsequent analyses.

Rescue Experiments

To establish causal relationships between miR‐146a‐5p, IRAK1, NF‐κB signaling, and M1 macrophage polarization, rescue experiments were performed in THP‐1 macrophages subjected to M1‐polarizing stimulation or Polyp‐CM treatment. Cells were treated with combinations of miR‐146a‐5p inhibitor or mimic, si‐IRAK1, pcDNA3.1‐IRAK1, or NF‐κB inhibitor (BAY 11‐7082). The co‐culture and treatment duration was 48 h, after which cells were collected for analysis of M1/M2 markers and signaling proteins.

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay

Potential miR‐146a‐5p binding sites within the IRAK1 3′‐UTR were predicted using TargetScan. The corresponding wild‐type (WT) 3′‐UTR fragment and a mutant (MUT) version with disrupted seed sequences were amplified and cloned into the psiCHECK‐2 luciferase reporter vector, with sequences verified by Sanger sequencing. HEK293T cells were co‐transfected with WT or MUT reporter plasmids and miR‐146a‐5p mimic, inhibitor, or negative controls, along with a Renilla luciferase plasmid as internal control, using Lipofectamine 3000. Forty‐eight hours post‐transfection, firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using a dual‐luciferase reporter system, and relative luciferase activity was calculated as the firefly/Renilla ratio. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐10 levels in cell culture supernatants were measured using commercially available ELISA kits according to manufacturer instructions. Absorbance was read using a microplate reader.

Flow Cytometry Analysis of Macrophage Polarization

THP‐1‐derived macrophages were first washed twice with PBS and resuspended in staining buffer (PBS containing 2% FBS). Fc receptors were blocked using Human TruStain FcX (BioLegend, Cat# 422302) for 10 min at 4°C to reduce non‐specific antibody binding. Cells were then stained with the following fluorophore‐conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4°C in the dark: anti‐CD11b‐APC (M0 marker, BioLegend, Cat# 301312), anti‐CD86‐PE (M1 marker, BioLegend, Cat# 305406), and anti‐CD206‐FITC (M2 marker, BioLegend, Cat# 321106). After staining, cells were washed twice with PBS, resuspended in 300 μL PBS, and filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer to remove aggregates. Flow cytometry was performed using a BD FACSCanto II instrument, and data were analyzed using FlowJo v10 software. The percentages of M0 (CD11b+), M1 (CD86+), and M2 (CD206+) macrophages were calculated from at least 10 000 events per sample. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissue samples using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 15596026) according to the manufacturer's protocol, and RNA purity and concentration were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For mRNA analysis, 1 μg of RNA was reverse‐transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Cat# RR037A), while for miRNA detection, cDNA synthesis was performed using the Mir‐X miRNA First‐Strand Synthesis Kit (Takara, Cat# 638313). Quantitative PCR was conducted on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Cat# 4309155) for mRNA targets and miScript SYBR Green PCR Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 218073) for miRNA. Expression of miR‐146a‐5p, IRAK1, TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐1β, Arg1, IL‐10, and CD206 was measured, with U6 serving as an internal control for miRNA and GAPDH for mRNA. All the primers were listed in Table 2. All reactions were performed in triplicate, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western Blot Analysis

Total protein was extracted from cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Cat# P0013B) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Cat# 04693132001) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Cat# 04906837001) on ice for 30 min. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 23225). Equal amounts of protein (20–40 μg) were separated by 10%–12% SDS‐PAGE (Bio‐Rad, Cat# 4561094) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non‐fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against IRAK1 (Cell Signaling Technology, CST, Cat# 4504, 1:1000), p65 (CST, Cat# 8242, 1:1000), phospho‐p65 (Ser536, CST, Cat# 3033, 1:1000), IκBα (CST, Cat# 4814, 1:1000), phospho‐IκBα (Ser32, CST, Cat# 2859, 1:1000), iNOS (Abcam, Cat# ab15323, 1:1000), Arg1 (CST, Cat# 93668, 1:1000), CD86 (Abcam, Cat# ab53004, 1:1000), CD206 (Abcam, Cat# ab64693, 1:1000), and GAPDH (CST, Cat# 5174, 1:2000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (CST, Cat# 7074, 1:3000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 32106) and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH) and normalized to GAPDH.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Paired Student's t‐test was used for comparisons between colorectal polyp tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues. For in vitro experiments, an unpaired Student's t‐test was applied for two‐group comparisons, and one‐way analysis of variance (one‐way ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. Correlations were analyzed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.

Results

Downregulation of miR‐146a‐5p Correlated With Upregulated IRAK1 and M1 Macrophage Markers in Human Colorectal Polyps

To investigate the relationship between miR‐146a‐5p, IRAK1, and macrophage polarization in colorectal polyps, we analyzed 40 paired polyp tissues and adjacent normal mucosa. Quantitative PCR revealed that miR‐146a‐5p expression was significantly decreased in polyp tissues compared with matched normal specimens (Figure 1A). In contrast, IRAK1 expression was markedly elevated in the same polyp samples (Figure 1B). Correlation analysis demonstrated a strong negative association between miR‐146a‐5p and IRAK1 expression across all samples (Figure 1C, r = −0.3891, p = 0.0131), suggesting that miR‐146a‐5p downregulation may contribute to IRAK1 upregulation in colorectal polyps. Furthermore, IHC analysis showed that IRAK1 and the M1 macrophage marker CD86 were highly expressed in polyp tissues, whereas the M2 macrophage marker CD206 was decreased (Figure 1D). These findings indicate that the downregulation of miR‐146a‐5p is associated with IRAK1 upregulation and a shift toward M1 macrophage polarization in colorectal polyps, highlighting an association between miR‐146a‐5p, IRAK1, and the pro‐inflammatory microenvironment in colorectal polyps.

(B) qPCR analysis of IRAK1 mRNA expression in the same 40 paired samples. (C) Correlation analysis between miR‐146a‐5p and IRAK1 expression levels across all 40 paired samples. Establishment and Verification of the THP‐1 Macrophage Polarization Model

To confirm the successful induction of macrophage polarization, THP‐1 cells were differentiated into M0, M1, and M2 macrophages using specific stimuli. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated distinct surface marker profiles among the three subsets. M0 macrophages showed increased expression of the general macrophage marker CD11b (Figure 2A). As expected, M1 macrophages exhibited a marked increase in the pro‐inflammatory surface marker CD86, whereas M2 macrophages showed elevated expression of the anti‐inflammatory marker CD206 (Figure 2B). We next assessed transcriptional signatures associated with macrophage polarization. QRT‐PCR analysis revealed significantly higher mRNA levels of the M1‐associated inflammatory genes TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β in M1 macrophages compared with M0 cells (Figure 2C). In contrast, the M2 macrophages displayed pronounced upregulation of Arg1, IL‐10, and CD206 mRNA relative to M0 cells (Figure 2D). These findings were further validated at the protein level by ELISA. M1 macrophages secreted higher amounts of the pro‐inflammatory cytokines TNF‐α and IL‐6 but lower levels of IL‐10 compared to M0 cells, whereas M2 macrophages showed the opposite secretion pattern (Figure 2E).

(C) QRT‐PCR analysis of M1‐associated cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β) in M1 and M0 macrophages. (D) QRT‐PCR analysis of M2‐associated markers (Arg1, IL‐10, and CD206) in M2 and M0 macrophages. MiR‐146a‐5p Negatively Regulated M1 Macrophage Polarization

To investigate the role of miR‐146a‐5p in macrophage polarization, we first examined its expression levels. QRT‐PCR analysis showed that miR‐146a‐5p was predominantly expressed at low levels in M1 macrophages, whereas its expression was higher in M0 or M2 macrophages (Figure 3A). To explore the functional impact of miR‐146a‐5p on M1 polarization, THP‐1 cells were transfected with miR‐146a‐5p mimic or inhibitor, with corresponding negative controls (mimic NC, inhibitor NC). Successful overexpression or knockdown of miR‐146a‐5p was confirmed by QRT‐PCR (Figure 3B). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that overexpression of miR‐146a‐5p significantly reduced the proportion of CD86+ M1 macrophages, while knockdown of miR‐146a‐5p markedly increased CD86+ cells (Figure 3C). We next assessed the mRNA expression of M1‐ and M2‐associated markers. QRT‐PCR demonstrated that miR‐146a‐5p overexpression suppressed M1‐associated genes (TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐1β) and upregulated M2 markers (Arg1, IL‐10, CD206), whereas inhibition of miR‐146a‐5p produced the opposite effects (Figure 3D). Finally, ELISA assays confirmed that overexpression of miR‐146a‐5p decreased the secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines TNF‐α and IL‐6, while increasing the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10. In contrast, miR‐146a‐5p knockdown enhanced TNF‐α and IL‐6 secretion and reduced IL‐10 levels (Figure 3E). These results collectively indicate that miR‐146a‐5p acts as a negative regulator of M1 macrophage polarization, suppressing pro‐inflammatory responses and promoting an anti‐inflammatory profile.

MiR‐146a‐5p Directly Targets IRAK1 to Regulate NF‐κB Signaling

We next sought to uncover the molecular mechanism underlying miR‐146a‐5p suppressing M1 macrophage polarization. Among the several predicted targets of miR‐146a‐5p, IRAK1 was prioritized because it functions as a key adaptor kinase in TLR/IL‐1R signaling and is critically positioned to activate NF‐κB, a central pathway driving M1 polarization. As shown in Figure 4A, TargetScan predicted a conserved miR‐146a‐5p binding site within the IRAK1 3′‐UTR, and a corresponding mutant sequence was generated for validation. Dual‐luciferase reporter assays in HEK293T cells (Figure 4B,C) demonstrated that the miR‐146a‐5p mimic markedly reduced luciferase activity of the wild‐type IRAK1 3′‐UTR reporter, whereas the miR‐146a‐5p inhibitor increased reporter activity. These effects were abolished in the mutant constructs, confirming that IRAK1 is a direct target of miR‐146a‐5p. To verify whether this regulatory axis operates in macrophages, we manipulated miR‐146a‐5p or IRAK1 levels in M0‐polarized THP‐1 macrophages. As shown in Figure 4D, miR‐146a‐5p overexpression or IRAK1 knockdown reduced IRAK1 protein levels and suppressed NF‐κB activation, manifested by decreased phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα. Conversely, inhibition of miR‐146a‐5p increased IRAK1 expression and enhanced p‐p65 and p‐IκBα levels.

IRAK1 Silencing Counteracted the M1‐Promoting Effect of miR‐146a‐5p Inhibition

To determine whether IRAK1 mediates the pro‐inflammatory effects observed upon miR‐146a‐5p inhibition, THP‐1 macrophages were subjected to M1‐polarizing stimulation or Polyp‐CM treatment and divided into three groups: inhibitor NC, miR‐146a‐5p inhibitor, and inhibitor combined with si‐IRAK1. As shown in Figure 5A, Western blot analysis revealed that miR‐146a‐5p inhibition increased IRAK1 protein expression, whereas co‐transfection with si‐IRAK1 effectively reduced IRAK1 levels. Flow cytometry (Figure 5B) further demonstrated that the miR‐146a‐5p inhibitor elevated the surface expression of the M1 marker CD86, while IRAK1 silencing reversed this increase. Consistently, Western blot assessment of macrophage polarization markers (Figure 5C) showed that miR‐146a‐5p inhibition enhanced the expression of iNOS and CD86 and reduced the M2 marker CD206, whereas IRAK1 knockdown restored these markers toward a less inflammatory phenotype. Cytokine measurements by ELISA (Figure 5D,E) supported these findings, showing increased TNF‐α and decreased IL‐10 secretion in the inhibitor group, both of which were reversed by IRAK1 silencing. Collectively, these results suggest that IRAK1 plays an important role in the increased M1 polarization associated with miR‐146a‐5p loss.

Blocking NF‐κB Signaling Reversed the Enhancement of M1 Polarization Caused by miR‐146a‐5p Loss

To investigate whether NF‐κB mediates the pro‐M1 effects induced by miR‐146a‐5p inhibition, THP‐1 macrophages under M1‐polarizing stimulation were divided into three groups: inhibitor NC, miR‐146a‐5p inhibitor, and inhibitor combined with the NF‐κB inhibitor BAY 11–7082. As shown in Figure 6A, Western blot analysis revealed that miR‐146a‐5p inhibition increased the phosphorylation of p65 and IκBα, indicative of NF‐κB activation, whereas treatment with BAY 11–7082 effectively reduced these phosphorylation levels. Flow cytometric analysis (Figure 6B) demonstrated that CD86 surface expression was elevated upon miR‐146a‐5p inhibition, but this increase was attenuated by BAY 11–7082. Consistently, Western blot analysis of macrophage polarization markers (Figure 6C) showed that miR‐146a‐5p inhibition enhanced the expression of M1 markers iNOS and CD86 while reducing the M2 marker CD206, whereas NF‐κB inhibition reversed these effects. These results indicate that NF‐κB activation is required for the M1‐promoting effects observed upon miR‐146a‐5p loss.

Overexpression of miR‐146a‐5p Inhibited M1 Macrophage Polarization Induced by IRAK1/NF‐κB Signaling

To investigate the role of the miR‐146a‐5p/IRAK1/NF‐κB axis in M1 macrophage polarization, THP‐1 cells were subjected to M1‐polarizing conditions or Polyp‐CM stimulation and transfected with pcDNA3.1, pcDNA3.1‐IRAK1, or pcDNA3.1‐IRAK1 together with miR‐146a‐5p mimic. qRT‐PCR (Figure 7A) and Western blot analyses (Figure 7B) confirmed that IRAK1 expression was significantly upregulated following pcDNA3.1‐IRAK1 transfection, while co‐transfection with miR‐146a‐5p mimic partially reduced IRAK1 expression at both mRNA and protein levels. Correspondingly, NF‐κB activation, indicated by p65 and IκBα phosphorylation (Figure 7C), was enhanced by IRAK1 overexpression and attenuated by miR‐146a‐5p. Flow cytometry (Figure 7D) showed that IRAK1 overexpression increased the proportion of CD86‐positive cells, an effect partially reversed by miR‐146a‐5p mimic. Moreover, assessment of polarization markers (Figure 7E) revealed that IRAK1 overexpression upregulated the M1 marker iNOS and downregulated the M2 marker CD206, while miR‐146a‐5p co‐expression counteracted these changes. Collectively, these results indicate that miR‐146a‐5p suppresses IRAK1/NF‐κB‐mediated M1 macrophage polarization, supporting its role as a negative regulator of macrophage inflammatory activation.

Discussion

Colorectal polyps represent early pathological lesions in colorectal carcinogenesis and are frequently accompanied by chronic inflammation in the local mucosal microenvironment [17]. The immune landscape within these lesions plays a critical role in shaping inflammatory status and potentially influencing disease evolution [18]. Macrophages are highly plastic cells that can polarize toward pro‐inflammatory M1 or anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotypes depending on microenvironmental cues [19]. Importantly, the functions of M1 macrophages are context‐ and stage‐dependent; although traditionally considered anti‐tumoral in established cancers, they may contribute to persistent inflammation during early tumorigenesis [20, 21]. Our findings demonstrate increased M1 markers (CD86, iNOS) in colorectal polyps, supporting an association between M1 polarization and the inflammatory microenvironment rather than indicating a direct causal role in polyp progression.

MiRNAs have emerged as pivotal regulators of immune responses by fine‐tuning macrophage polarization and inflammatory signaling pathways [22]. Among them, miR‐146a acts as a critical negative feedback modulator in innate immunity and inflammation [23, 24]. It is induced by NF‐κB following inflammatory stimuli such as LPS, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α, thereby forming a regulatory loop that limits excessive cytokine production [10]. Functionally, miR‐146a‐mimic alleviated inflammatory responses of macrophages, with reduced levels of IL‐1β, IL‐6 and CCL‐2 and suppressed NF‐κB activation [25]. In inflammatory models of periodontitis, miR‐146a negatively regulated P. gingivalis ‐induced proinflammatory cytokine secretion through the TRAF6/p38 MAPK pathway [26]. Moreover, Du et al. [27] demonstrated that miR‐146a overexpression inhibited Candida albicans –induced p‐IκBα activity and NF‐κB p65 nuclear translocation, further supporting its role as a potent anti‐inflammatory regulator. Consistent with these findings, miR‐146a‐5p was downregulated in M1 macrophages, and its modulation significantly affected macrophage polarization and inflammatory responses. Importantly, miR‐146a‐5p has also been reported to be dysregulated in multiple malignancies, including colorectal cancer [11, 12], where it is associated with inflammatory regulation and immune modulation [28]. Given its established role in controlling NF‐κB–dependent pathways, miR‐146a may influence anti‐tumor immunity through mechanisms such as regulation of immune checkpoint molecules (e.g., PD‐L1) and modulation of CD8+ T cell–mediated responses [29, 30]. Although the present study did not evaluate PD‐L1 expression or T cell infiltration, these potential immunological implications warrant further investigation in colorectal lesions.

Mechanistically, IRAK1 serves as a central adaptor kinase downstream of TLR/IL‐1R signaling and activates the NF‐κB pathway, thereby promoting M1 macrophage polarization [31, 32]. Dysregulation of the IRAK1–NF‐κB axis has been implicated in sustaining pro‐inflammatory macrophage phenotypes in colorectal inflammation and early neoplastic lesions [33]. MiR‐146a is known to target key components of this pathway, including IRAK1 and TRAF6, acting as a negative regulator of inflammatory signaling [34, 35]. Consistent with this, we confirmed IRAK1 as a direct target of miR‐146a‐5p in our study. MiR‐146a‐5p overexpression reduced IRAK1 expression, suppressed p‐p65/IκBα phosphorylation, and decreased M1 markers, whereas its inhibition exerted opposite effects. Rescue experiments further showed that silencing IRAK1 or inhibiting NF‐κB abrogated the M1‐promoting effect induced by miR‐146a‐5p loss, supporting a functional miR‐146a‐5p/IRAK1/NF‐κB axis in macrophage polarization. While this axis represents a central inflammatory pathway, additional targets such as TRAF6, as well as parallel signaling pathways including MAPK and STAT, may also contribute to macrophage regulation and warrant further investigation [36, 37, 38].

Despite these insights, our study has several limitations. First, our in vitro findings were based on THP‐1‐derived macrophages, which may not fully reflect the heterogeneity of tissue‐resident macrophages in colorectal polyps. Although enhanced M1 polarization was observed, causality was not established, and in vivo studies are required to clarify its role in disease progression. Second, although we analyzed 40 human polyp specimens and summarized their clinicopathological characteristics, correlation or subgroup analyses between miR‐146a‐5p (or macrophage markers) and polyp features were not performed due to the limited sample size and cohort heterogeneity, which may have reduced statistical power. Third, the study focused primarily on the IRAK1/NF‐κB signaling pathway. However, other signaling cascades, such as TRAF6‐ or MAPK‐related pathways, may also participate in regulating macrophage polarization and warrant further investigation. Importantly, while our findings suggest that miR‐146a‐5p contributes to inflammatory regulation in early colorectal lesions, the present study does not determine whether it specifically participates in the adenoma–carcinoma sequence or reflects broader inflammatory processes. Future studies incorporating patient‐derived organoids, in vivo models, and single‐cell profiling of tumor‐associated macrophages could further clarify its stage‐specific role in colorectal tumorigenesis.

In summary, our results demonstrate that downregulation of miR‐146a‐5p in colorectal polyps is associated with IRAK1 upregulation and enhanced M1 macrophage polarization, creating a pro‐inflammatory microenvironment. Functional studies indicate that miR‐146a‐5p suppresses IRAK1/NF‐κB‐driven M1 polarization, providing a mechanistic link between miRNA dysregulation and inflammatory activation in early colorectal lesions. Clinically, these findings suggest that therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring miR‐146a‐5p expression or targeting the IRAK1/NF‐κB axis may help modulate local inflammation in colorectal polyps, which could be relevant to colorectal disease prevention.
