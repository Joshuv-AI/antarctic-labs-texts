Predicting spinal neurological prognosis in patients with surgical treatment for thoracic spinal stenosis based on neurological and neurophysiological evaluation
Authors: Tun Liu, Jia Li, Huaguang Qi, Wentao Wang, Bin Guo, Yongjie Zhang, Kuo Jiang, Songchuan Zhao, Jin Wang, Gang Wu, Gang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe aim to investigate the spinal neurological outcomes in patients who have undergone surgery for thoracic spinal stenosis (TSS) and to identify high-risk factors for poor spinal neurological prognosis. Methods Patients were categorized into four subgroups based on TSS causes: thoracic disk herniation (TDH), ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligaments (OPLL), ossification of the ligamentum flavum (OLF), and OPLL + OLF. The following data were collected: (1) demographic and clinical data; (2) neurological evaluation; and (3) neurophysiological evaluation by combining somatosensory- and motor- evoked potential (SSEP and MEP) baseline at both legs. The spinal neurological function was assessed by modified Japanese Orthopedic Association (mJOA) score at three time-point: before surgery, one-month and six-month after the surgery; Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent high-risk factors associated with poor spinal neurological prognosis. ResultsA total of 236 patients were diagnosed with TSS. The mean mJOA scores before and six months after the surgery were 7.0 and 7.9, respectively. The number of patients with declined spinal neurological outcomes during the short- and long- term follow-ups were 17 and 8, respectively. Based on multivariate logistic analysis, risk factors for poor spinal neurological prognosis were as follows: preoperative severe myelopathy [OR = 5.65, 95% CI (2.12, 13.04), p = 0.001], abnormal SSEP and/or MEP baseline [OR = 3.93, 95% CI (1.44, 8.22), p = 0.007], and OPLL and/or OLF [OR = 3.06, 95% CI (1.01, 7.36), p = 0.039]. A decision tree that includes the risk factors can be used to screen patients. ConclusionsSurgical treatment for TSS can improve spinal neurological function. Risk factors for poor prognosis were identified. Preoperative neurological evaluations and neurophysiological baseline signals can help predict poor prognosis of the TSS patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04369-0.

Introduction

Thoracic disk herniation (TDH), ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligaments (OPLL), ossification of the ligamentum flavum (OLF), and their combination (OPLL + OLF) are the main causes of thoracic spinal stenosis (TSS) [1]. It has been reported that TSS caused by OPLL and/or OLF occurs almost predominantly in East Asia [1, 2]. Compared with lumbar spinal stenosis and cervical myelopathy, the spinal neurological prognosis in surgical treatment for TSS patients is relatively poor due to the low prevalence and difficulty in the surgical procedures [3, 4]. Several high-risk factors for poor prognosis in TSS have been reported, including worse preoperative symptoms [1, 5]. However, they did not incorporate intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM) features in predicting postoperative spinal neurological status in TSS patients [1, 6].

IONM by combining somatosensory- and motor-evoked potentials (SSEP and MEP) enables continuous and real-time assessment of the spinal neurological status, allowing for early detection of spinal neurological injuries to prevent or limit further injuries. Previous studies have shown that preoperative SSEP waveforms correlate well with the clinical prognosis in cervical spondylotic myelopathy [7, 8]. Poor MEP baseline waveforms have been demonstrated correlate with preoperative motor deficits in patients during spinal surgery [9]. So, the incorporation of IONM data could have enhanced the accuracy of the prediction [10]. Furthermore, both preoperative neurological and neurophysiological assessment can be used to predict the feasibility of SSEP/MEP signals during scoliosis surgery [11]. However, there are limited studies that combined preoperative neurological evaluation with SSEP/MEP baseline waveforms for predicting spinal neurological prognosis in TSS surgery. Preemptive accurately identifying TSS patients who at higher risk for poor neurological prognosis can be beneficial, including more transparent communication on impending neurological deficits and planing for postoperative rehabilitation.

We hypothesized that combined neurological and neurophysiological evaluations can be used to predict poor neurological prognosis in TSS surgery. Therefore, we performed this study to evaluate our hypothesis.

Materials and methods

Patients

It is a retrospective cohort study of one single centre in a tertiary hospital in Xi’an, China. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee and Institutional Review Board (IRB, No. 201806003) of our hospital in Xi’an, China on August 2018. It was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry.cn (No. ChiCTR2000032155) on October 2018. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent to participate was obtained from all of the participants in the study. We conducted a retrospective analysis of 236 patients who underwent surgical treatment for TSS in our hospital from March 2018 to March 2024. Inclusion criteria were (1) patients with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) status ranging from I to III, (2) MRI studies showed evidence of thoracic spinal compression. Exclusion criteria were (1) patients suffered from stroke with residual neurological disorders, (2) TSS secondary to fracture, scoliosis, hematoma, tuberculosis, cyst, and other infected disease [2].

SSEP/MEP signals acquisition

Use of multi-channel SSEP/MEP can improve the successful rate of monitoring [5, 6]. For MEP monitoring, targeting muscle recording points including the deltoid, brachioradialis, and abductor pollicis longus (APL) for the upper limbs, and the flexor hallucis brevis and abductor hallucis (AH) for the lower limbs [7, 8]. Parameters for acquiring MEP signals were as follows: constant voltage (220–360 V); multiple trains (5–8 pulses); duration (300µs). We set the bandpass filter between 10 and 1500 Hz. The time base was 100ms.

For SSEP monitoring, stimulation sites included the median nerve at the upper limbs and the posterior tibial nerve at the lower limbs [12, 13]. Parameters were set as follows: posterior tibial nerves were stimulated at 25 mA. Single-pulse stimulation was performed with a frequency of 1 Hz, and the duration was 200µs.

While we recorded SSEP/MEP signals from both the lower proximal extremities and the lower distal extremities. However, we ultimately adopted the APL and AH muscles for signal recording, as these targeting muscles are well-established in the previous studies that could provide the optimal IONM signal during the spinal surgery [9]. Furthermore, we placed surface-stimulating electrodes at the ankles. According to the international 10–20 system, needle electrodes were placed over for signal recording.

SSEP and MEP baseline signals were acquired after the spine exposure was completed, and we defined them as IONM baseline waveforms [14].

Criteria for abnormal SSEP/MEP baseline waveforms

According to Hu et al., SSEP waveforms were classified as five types [7]. Abnormal SSEP waveforms were defined as abnormal latency or amplitude (Type III and Type IV) or absent waveform (Type V). MEP waveforms were classified as two types: normal or abnormal signals. Abnormal MEP waveforms were defined as an amplitude less than 3.6µV or absent waveform [12, 15].

A simultaneous abnormality in both SSEP and MEP was defined as abnormal SSEP and MEP, and an abnormality in either or both modalities was defined as abnormal SSEP or MEP [16]. Furthermore, IONM baseline waveforms were classified into four categories: Category A included normal SSEP and MEP on both sides, Category B included abnormal SSEP or MEP on one or both sides, and Category C included abnormal SSEP and MEP on either side [11] (Table 1).

Preoperative neurological evaluation

The lower extremity dimension of the modified Japanese Orthopedic Association (mJOA) score [17] was used to assess clinical neurological conditions in the lower extremities of the patients at two follow-up visits: 1 month and 6 months after the surgery [18]. According to the formula, the mJOA RR was categorized as improved (>0%), unchanged (= 0%), or deteriorated (≤ 0%).6 Moreover, a poor prognosis is defined as unimproved spinal neurological outcomes.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 statistics software (SPSS 26.0, Chicago, IL). Independent two-sample t-test was used to compare the difference between groups when the data were normally distributed, whereas Mann–Whitney U-test was performed to determine differences between the two groups when the data were skewed distributed.

Uni- and multi-variate logistic regression were performed to evaluate the relationship between different clinical features and poor spinal neurological prognosis. If variables had a p-value less than or equal to 0.2 in Univariate regression analysis, it was further analyzed in multivariate logistic regression. Odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated.

We categorized preoperative myelopathy in lower extremities as either mild or moderate (mJOA score ≥ 7), or severe (mJOA ≤ 6)1. To conduct our analysis, we divided IONM baseline waveforms into two categories: abnormal SSEP and/or MEP (i.e., categories B and C, respectively) and normal SSEP and MEP (i.e., category A). In multivariate logistic analysis, Age, sex, duration of symptoms, and estimated blood loss were dichotomized based on clinically appropriate cutoff values agreed upon by all the authors [1].

P values less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

A total of 236 patients with TSS were identified between March 2018 and March 2023, of which 35 patients were excluded. The excluded patients had TSS secondary to scoliosis (n = 11), tuberculosis (n = 9), fracture (n = 3), hematoma (n = 1), cyst (n = 1), and any other infection-related pathologies (n = 2). Additionally, 8 patients suffered from stroke with residual neurological disorders. Therefore, 201 patients were eligible and included in the analysis.

Demographic and perioperative data

Of the eligible patients (n = 201), there were 84 men and 117 women. The mean age of the patients was 53.5 years. The study included 41 patients diagnosed with TDH, 14 with OPLL, 120 with OLF, and 26 with OPLL + OLF. The number of patients with preoperative severe myelopathy (mJOA score ≤ 6 points) with TDH, OPLL, OLF, and OPLL + OLF were 17, 5, 32, and 11, respectively. Among the patients, 79.60% (n = 160) had TSS caused by OPLL and/or OLF. As is depicted in Table 2.

Surgical outcomes

The mean operation duration was 175 ± 53 min with a range of 95 to 245 min. The estimated mean blood loss (ESB) was 440 ml with a range of 200 to 1750 ml. A total of 156 patients had improved spinal neurological results (mJOA RR > 0%) after the surgery, 28 had unchanged neurological conditions (mJOA RR = 0%), and 17 had a decline in neurological conditions (mJOA RR < 0%).

Mean mJOA scores were 7.0 pre-surgery and 7.9 six months post-surgery, resulting in a mean mJOA RR of 23.1%. The patients’ spinal neurological function significantly improved after the surgery (P < 0.05). The average postoperative mJOA RR was 24.8%, 10.7%, 26.8%, and 11.2% in patients who were diagnosed with TDH, OPLL, OLF, and OPLL + OLF, respectively, as presented in Table 3.

High-risk factors associated with poor neurological prognosis

In univariate analysis, the following factors were associated with a higher probability of poor neurological outcomes: OPLL and/or OLF (p = 0.16), estimated blood loss ≥ 400 ml (p = 0.09), preoperative severe myelopathy (p<0.001), longer duration of symptoms (p = 0.15), and abnormal SSEP and/or MEP baseline (p = 0.14).

In multivariate logistic regression analysis, the independent risk factors were as follows: OPLL and/or OLF [OR 3.06, 95% CI (1.01, 7.36), p = 0.039], preoperative severe myelopathy [OR 5.65, 95% CI (2.12, 13.04), p = 0.001], and abnormal SSEP and/or MEP baseline [OR 3.93, 95% CI (1.44, 8.22), p = 0.007]. Decision tree for predicting declined spinal neurological prognosis

A decision tree with risk factors screens patients vulnerable to poor spinal neurological prognosis (Fig. 1). Among TDH patients presented with mild-to-moderate preoperative myelopathy (n = 24), preoperative neurophysiological evaluations were Category A, with neither short-term nor long-term follow-up presenting poor neurological prognosis. Among TDH patients with severe preoperative myelopathy (n = 17), neurophysiological assessments included Category A (n = 10) and Category B (n = 7). Notably, none developed long-term poor prognosis, indicating favorable prognosis in TDH patients. While OPLL/OLF patients with mild-to-moderate preoperative myelopathy (n = 112) uniformly demonstrated Category A waveforms, and presented with favourable prognosis. However, severe preoperative myelopathy OPLL/OLF patients (n = 48) exhibited different risk stratification: Category A (n = 20) maintained favourable prognosis (poor prognosis rate = 0), Category B (n = 23) showed moderate poor prognosis rate (17.4%), and Category C (n = 5) demonstrated a strikingly high poor prognosis rate (80%) in both short-term and long-term follow-up visits. Indicating unfavorable clinical prognosis in those patients.

Complications

Overall, the postoperative mJOA score declined in 17 patients (4 patients with TDH, 2 patients with OPLL, 5 patients with OLF, and 6 patients with OPLL + OLF). Four patients developed paraplegia after the surgery, and none of them showed any signs of recovery during the last follow-up visit. Additionally, a total of 13 patients presented mild or moderate neurological deficits after the surgery. At the 6-month follow-up, 9 and 4 patients achieved complete recovery, and partial recovery, respectively, as depicted in Table 5.

Discussion

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 201 patients diagnosed with TSS and evaluated the change in perioperative mJOA score. The outcome was categorized as improved or not improved [6]. The postoperative mJOA score improved significantly after the surgery, yielding a mean mJOA RR of 23.1%. High-risk factors for poor neurological prognosis were identified. A decision tree was developed, which included neurological and neurophysiological evaluations that can be used to screen patients who are susceptible to a poor spinal neurological prognosis.

The minimum clinically important difference (MCID) is defined as the minimum change in a measurement that a patient would identify as beneficial [16]. However, MCID of the mJOA score is estimated to be between one and two points and varies with myelopathy severity [17]. Additionally, Maki et al. reported that MCID was defined as gain in JOA score of 2.5 points or more [18, 19]. Given the relatively low mJOA RR observed in our study, we considered any mJOA improvement was clinically meaningful in surgical treatment for TSS patients.

Transient SSEP/MEP signals changes are critical for predicting transient postoperative neurological deterioration [5, 14]. If the duration exceeds the corresponding values, there is a greater probability of short term postoperative neurological decline. Permanent changes in MEP/SSEP signals are critical for predicting long-term postoperative neurological decline [5, 14]. If the duration exceeds the corresponding values, the probability of long-term postoperative neurological deterioration increases.

We focus on investigating postoperative spinal neurological deterioration in TSS patients, due to the surgical difficulties and intraoperative neurological injury risks. Preemptive accurate identification of the TSS patients who are at higher risk for postoperative neurological deterioration can be beneficial. Relieving the patient’s postoperative anxiety, and thus could increase postoperative satisfaction.

Predictive value of SSEP and/or MEP baseline

SSEP and MEP signals were categorized into A (normal SSEP and MEP) and C (abnormal SSEP and MEP), respectively. Category C were observed exclusively in the OPLL/OLF patients with severe myelopathy. Previous studies demonstrated that the sensitivity and specificity of IONM by combining SSEP and MEP are excellent in high-risk spinal surgery [22]. In our study, OPLL/OLF patients with category C are the most susceptible to poor prognosis. Furthermore, the incidence of unimproved mJOA score of the patients was high in both the short-term and the long-term follow-up visits, and the deficits were less likely to recovery, indicating that simultaneous SSEP and MEP abnormalities in baseline waveforms is a risk indicator. In contrast, patients with category A are the least likely to develop postoperative neurological deficits and have the lowest rate of poor neurological function. This finding demonstrates a high prognostic value for neurological prognosis because combining MEP with SSEP has a complementary role in detecting real-time signal feedback from the spinal cord [21, 23].

Abnormal SSEP or MEP was observed exclusively in the TSS patients with severe myelopathy. The incidence of neurological deficits in the patients was low in the short-term and was likely to recover partially or fully during the long-term follow-up visit.

Why patients with preoperative severe myelopathy are more likely to have poor prognosis?

Surgical treatment lead to significant improvement in spinal neurological status in TSS patients presenting with preoperative mild to moderate myelopathy. None of those patients developed spinal neurological dysfunction at the last follow-up visit.

However, the incidence of unimproved spinal neurological function at the short-term visit was relatively high. Especially, OPLL/OLF patients with abnormal SSEP and/or MEP baseline were more likely to develop declined mJOA score at the last follow-up visit. Three factors contribute to this phenomenon. Firstly, prolonged compression in the thoracic spinal cord can lead to more serious spinal neuronal damage. Consequently, patients with preoperative severe myelopathy are more susceptible to getting declined mJOA scores due to the preexisting damage, which might be less resilient to spinal neurological injuries, intraoperative blood loss, or fluctuation in blood pressure. Secondly, TDH distributes exclusively in the lower thoracic spine. In contrast, OPLL/OLF was occurs primarily in the mid-thoracic spine, followed by the lower thoracic segment [2]. The blood supply is less at the middle thoracic segment. Thirdly, TDH patients had a greater tolerance of the SSEP/MEP warning thresholds to predict a postoperative decline in functional status. However, OPLL/OLF patients had a lower tolerance for the signal change thresholds, including a lower amplitude cutoff value and a shorter duration of waveform change. Indicating that OPLL/OLF patients were more susceptible to developing declined mJOA scores.

Therefore, it is crucial to put more emphasis on OPLL/OLF patients who present with severe thoracic myelopathy symptom before the surgery, as these patients are susceptible to intraoperative spinal neurological injury, which can progress into long-term neurological dysfunction.

The relationship between preoperative severe spinal cord lesions and poor prognosis is consistent with previous literature [20, 21]. However, previous studies primarily focused on evaluating both the utility and reliability of intraoperative MEP in patients with preoperative severe motor deficits. They demonstrated that MEP predictive value in patients with a preoperative severe motor status was improved [21]. The PPV was higher in those patients [22]. Furthermore, rescue cases had a significantly higher MEP baseline waveform derivation rate [20].

Our study investigated the combined application of neurological and multi-modal neurophysiological evaluations to predict postoperative spinal neurological deterioration. Preemptive identification of high-risk poor prognosis patients is important [5]. Combined OPLL and OLF patients who presented with both severe preoperative myelopathy and abnormal neurophysiological waveforms exhibited persistent mJOA score decline throughout both the short-term and the long-term follow-ups, indicating irreversible neurological spinal deterioration in those patients. This represents an important patient subgroup whom required prioritized attention before the surgery. In contrast, other TSS patients pathologies demonstrate significantly lower poor prognosis rates.

Limitations

The study’s limitations include its single-institute retrospective design. First, this is a single-center retrospective cohort study, which may be subject to selection bias. In the future, we will perform a multi-center prospective study with larger sample size to validate our results. Second, the conclusions may not be universal and should be tested with external datasets in the future study. Third, MEP waveforms cannot be acquired without proper sedation or general anesthesia [24]. Therefore, MEP baseline signals can be obtained only when the patients are anesthetized. Fourth, 6-month follow-up visit may be possible relatively short, a longer follow-up period is needed in the future.

Conclusions

Surgery for TSS patients can significantly improve spinal neurological function. Risk factors for poor prognosis were identified. Preoperative neurological and neurophysiological evaluations can help predict the prognosis of patients who are susceptible to poor neurological outcomes.
