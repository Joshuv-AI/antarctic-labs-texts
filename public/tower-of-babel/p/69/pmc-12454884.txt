Assessment of quality of life in children with cerebral palsy: A parent-reported questionnaire-based study
Authors: Siddhesh Ajgaonkar, Amarshree A Shetty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe quality of life (QoL) of children with cerebral palsy (CP) needs to be assessed using a CP-specific questionnaire, such as the CP-QoL questionnaire. This study was conducted to assess the QoL of children with CP using the CP-QoL questionnaire and correlate QoL scores with patient demographics. The caregivers of 155 children (3–15 years) attending the NITTE centre of AB Shetty Memorial Institute of Dental Sciences were enrolled in the study. The caregivers were given the Kannada translation of the parent-proxy version of the CP-QoL questionnaire. Analysis and scoring were carried out using the prescribed methodology. For validation, Cronbach's alpha was computed for every question. Analysis was done on the impact of demographic profile on QoL. A CP-QoL survey was given 155 caregivers of children with cerebral palsy (96 male and 61 female). The youngsters under study had an overall QoL score of 25.24 ± 6.14. The children's QoL was significantly impacted by age (P < 0.05). The QoL was not substantially impacted by gender (P > 0.05). Overall, reliability and internal consistency were determined to be good. (Alpha Cronbach's > 0.7). Children with CP have a lower quality of life as assessed using the CP-QoL questionnaire.Keywords: Cerebral palsy, Cerebral palsy-quality of life questionnaire, Quality of life

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a collection of irreversible developmental motor and postural impairments that create activity restrictions due to non-progressive disruptions in the developing fetus or infant brain.1 Coexisting diseases such as epilepsy, starvation, growth retardation, and gastrointestinal disorders exacerbate the pre-existing neurodevelopmental morbidities.2 Globally published research has revealed the range of CP to be from 1.5 to 4 per 1000 live births; however, the prevalence range recorded in India is larger, ranging from 2.08 to 3.88 per 1000 live births.3 Early brain injury always causes motor sequelae. Still, the existence of CP-related comorbidities might impair functioning and have a poor influence on the Quality of Life (QoL) of children and adolescents with CP.4 According to Viswanath et al. (2023), 30–89 % of children with cerebral palsy have associated impairments, depending on the type of CP.5

Quality of life outcomes are seen as one of the most significant aims of services for children and adolescents with cerebral palsy. The World Health Organization defines quality of life as “an individual's perception of their position in life in context to their culture and value systems, as well as about their goals, expectations, standards, and concerns".6 Although QoL is a key concept in childhood, less is known about the QoL of children with impairments, particularly those at the severe end of the spectrum.7 Children with severe intellectual disabilities or communication challenges may struggle to provide accurate information, necessitating the use of proxies (typically mothers) to measure quality of life.8

Oral health in special needs patients is one of the most neglected areas of care. When compared to the overall population, this group has many more unmet dental needs. Maintaining good and optimal oral health in these physically handicapped and mentally challenged individuals is exceedingly tough, as they also have poor general health.9 Disabled children and adults living in long-term care facilities are typically dependent on others for personal care. Typically, this care is provided by parents, siblings, or other caretakers. As a result, it is critical that these caregivers understand and execute preventative measures to ensure the oral health of the group under their care.10 Unfortunately, the majority of caregivers are unaware of the necessity of adequate oral health care and hence do not practice it.11

To our knowledge, limited data is available regarding on caregivers' knowledge about oral health and quality of life in Dakshin Kannada district of Karnataka. Questionnaires have been created to statistically assess the impact of the disability on the patient's or primary caregiver's perspectives. It also aids in the evaluation of how existing interventions are effective in enhancing the individual's life. These improve patient care by broadening the boundaries of the benefit of therapy, indicating improved supportive measures, aiding in prognosis and decision-making, and allocating resources in health-care policymaking.12 CP-QoL child questionnaire is an Australian-based questionnaire that evaluates certain aspects of QoL according to specific age ranges, for children aged 4–12 years (parent proxy version).13

The NITTE Special Child Care (NSPECC) program is an integrated healthcare services platform that provides inexpensive and subsidized health services through the organization's coverage network. Since 2015, the program has partnered with the “Sarva Shiksha Abhiyana” (education for all) project of the Karnataka state government in India. The platform coordinates healthcare for children with special needs enrolled in the local Board of Education offices in Mangalore, Karnataka, as well as those seeking care at the university hospital.

The integrated effort enables an initial diagnostic screening and check-up for medical and dental issues, as well as on-site emergency, preventive, and minor dental treatments. Dental coverage is provided free of charge by postgraduate residents from the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, with appointments tailored to the requirements of children and caregivers. A key feature of the program is that it includes free transportation to and from the location for children and caregivers every two weeks, provided by the hospital administration.

The current study employs a validated, parent-reported questionnaire designed specifically to evaluate the quality of life of children with cerebral palsy. This tool also includes questions about oral health practices and feeding issues, providing a complete picture of the child's daily life and well-being. By identifying the areas that most significantly impact quality of life, this research intends to inform clinical practices, improve interdisciplinary treatment, and develop supportive initiatives for both children and their families.

Methodology

The Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, AB Shetty Memorial Institute of Dental Sciences conducted an awareness program for parents and caregivers of patients with cerebral palsy. This occasion served as a vital platform for increasing awareness about Cerebral Palsy (CP), extending support to individuals affected by the condition, and advocating for enhanced inclusivity and comprehension. After obtaining approval of the Institutional Review Board and ethical committee (Ref no ETHICS/ABSMIDS/583/2025), a cross-sectional questionnaire-based study was conducted among the interested attendees of the program, satisfying the inclusion and exclusion criteria. This study was based on the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

The caregivers of children aged 3–15 years, diagnosed with cerebral palsy and regularly attending therapy or school sessions, were included in the study. The participants were informed about the research and their assent was obtained for the same. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or caregivers in their native language. The explanation of the study objectives, participants’ voluntary consent to participate in the study, and their ability to withdraw from it at any time without any penalty or loss of routine care benefits were made clear in the consent form. The caregivers of children with comorbid conditions, such as severe intellectual disability that limits the parental reporting accuracy, as well as non-consenting parents, were excluded from the study.

The caregivers of children with CP were selected using a purposive sampling design due to CP's rarity and low prevalence rate. The premise of the CP-QoL child questionnaire was explained to the parent before administration. Before beginning the study, the lead investigator received interview training and presented the questionnaire to a small sample of parents. All responses were documented, regardless of literacy level. Items that did not apply to a particular patient were noted as NA (not applicable). The options for parental rating replies varied from very unhappy to very happy, using a Likert scale of 1–5. These were then transformed into the following scores: Very unhappy = 1, Unhappy = 2, unclear = 3, happy = 4, very happy = 5.

This 18-item questionnaire was derived from the 66-item CP-QoL questionnaire.14 The questions were selected to represent broader domains, including family, participation, health, pain, and access to services. The most representative questions for each domain were identified and compiled into the questionnaire. Content validity was established through expert review by pediatric dentists and pediatricians, who assessed the relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness of the items. Based on their feedback, necessary modifications were incorporated to ensure the questionnaire's validity for use in the target population.

Each question in the attached questionnaire begins with the words “How do you think your child feels about.” It was structured into two parts: the first part dealt with quality-of-life domains (11 questions): family and friends, participation, communication, health, use of special equipment, pain, and access to services. Part 2 consisted of 7 multiple-choice items related to oral hygiene practices and eating challenges (Annexure 1). Of these, four were dichotomous (yes/no) while the remaining three had multiple-choice response options. It took 15–20 min to complete the questionnaire. The questionnaire was printed in English and Kannada (local language). Individual proforma's with unique identification numbers were maintained.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive statistical analysis of the study was done based on the data obtained, using a Microsoft Excel sheet (Microsoft office version-2016). Further statistical analysis was performed using the latest version of SPSS. Mean quality-of-life scores were calculated for each item. The internal consistency of the questionnaire was measured using Cronbach's alpha.

Results

A total of 155 eligible children were recruited wherein 96 were males (60.90 %) and 61 were females (39.10 %).

When the findings of the survey were tabulated, it was noted that 51 % were unhappy getting along with other people and 40 % were unhappy getting accepted by other children outside of preschool or school. It had the highest mean score of 2.65 ± 0.79. Difficulties were also noted in their ability to play with peers (34.2 %), the way others communicated with them (52.9 %), ability to complete daily activities (45.2 %), and overall life satisfaction (38.1 %). In addition, 39.4 % reported feeling unhappy about participating in recreational activities, 35.5 % were unclear about their access to therapy (for example, physiotherapy, speech therapy, occupational therapy while 38.7 % reported feeling unhappy about the way they communicate with people they know well (using any means of communication). Health concerns were considerable, with 50.3 % unhappy about their physical health while 32.9 % were unhappy about brushing their teeth (Table 1).

Toddlers (26.08 ± 8.64) and adolescents (26.31 ± 6.86) had the highest QoL scores amongst all the age groups. But this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.709). The QoL scores were comparable between males (25.54 ± 6.13) and females (24.76 ± 6.26) (p = 0.442) (Table 2). Around 36.1 % of caregivers rated their child's oral hygiene as good, while 27.1 % rated it as fair, and 36.8 % rated it as poor. Over 96.1 % acknowledged the importance of brushing teeth with 31 % brushing their child's teeth once a day, 51.6 % brushing twice a day, and 17.4 % brushing after meals. Around 8.4 % of the caregivers felt the need for a routine dental checkup every 3 months, 45.2 % visited every 6 months, and 46.5 % acknowledged visiting the dentist only in the presence of pain. Almost 70.3 % of caregivers responded that their child faced difficulty when having food, while 25.8 % responded that their child experienced pain while having food. Additionally, 65.8 % observed food seeping out of the mouth in their child while eating (Table 3). The overall Cronbach alpha was 0.8356.

Discussion

Cerebral palsy is a lifelong impairment that affects the quality of life for both parents and children.15 Children with cerebral palsy need healthcare services, as well as significant time, effort, and care from caregivers to support their functioning because of the condition's long-term effects.16 Some severe cases necessitate complete reliance on mobility, self-care, and social engagement, and the condition's aftereffects impact not only the children but also their caregivers, adding to the caregiving load and possibly lowering the mothers' quality of life.17 The quality of care and parental comprehension of the condition have an impact on this outcome.15

More than half were unhappy with their ability to get along with people, while 40 % were unhappy getting accepted by peers. 38.1 % of patients were unhappy about their life in general, while 50.3 % were unhappy about their physical health. This resonated with another study18 wherein the youth expressed worries about potential health problems and a potential decline in their motor performance over time. Some participants said they were afraid of being reliant on other people and failing to achieve their own life goals. Willis et al.19 and Miklos et al.20 concluded that group environments that allowed kids and young people to build and validate their identities among peers with disabilities were crucial for promoting feelings of acceptance and relatedness.

In the current study, around 34.2 % and 39.4 %, respectively, expressed feeling unhappy about their ability to play games with other children and participating in recreational activities. Research indicates that by sharing experiences and reducing feelings of loneliness, participation in various activities gives patients a sense of community and connection to others. Their psychological needs and personal development are impacted by their involvement in social and physical activities; this is in line with earlier studies that have demonstrated the importance of providing young people with opportunities to interact with their peers and form friendships.18 Thus, participation in various social activities should be encouraged.

As many as 25 % of children with cerebral palsy (CP) are non-verbal or have complex communication needs, and up to 80 % of them have communication problems.21 In the current study, we observed that around 52.9 % were unhappy about the way other people communicate with them, while 38.7 % were unhappy about the way they communicate with others (using any means of communication). Children with complex communication needs can communicate effectively in any setting by creating a communicatively accessible environment, having competent communication partners use efficient techniques, including sign and gesture, communication boards, and speech generating devices. Since family-centred care depends on efficient communication and poor communication is linked to a markedly higher chance of suffering several avoidable safety incidents, it is crucial to investigate the application of these strategies for children with cerebral palsy.21

45.2 % were unhappy about their ability to complete daily activities, and 35.5 % were unclear about their access to therapy for example, physiotherapy, speech therapy, occupational therapy. This possibly reflects the concreteness of the experience, which parents with impaired children are generally aware of, as opposed to those from more abstract domains, such as sentiments of social acceptability.

Toddlers (26.08 ± 8.64) and adolescents (26.31 ± 6.86) had the highest QoL scores amongst all the age groups. Das et al.22 noted that children aged 6–9 had a higher QoL than those aged 4–6 and 9–12. Thus, the QoL was significantly impacted by age. This may be explained by the parents' lower acceptance of the condition when the children are young, but as the youngsters get older, their mobility and access to services decline. Gender did not significantly affect the QoL scores. This was similar to the results of a study by Das et al.22

While 36.1 % rated their child's oral hygiene as good and another 36.8 % rated it as poor, around 96.1 % acknowledged the importance of brushing teeth. Interestingly, the caregivers have shown an acceptable understanding of the frequency of dental hygiene. Around 51.6 % recognized that teeth should be brushed twice daily, and 31 % believed that it is better to brush teeth once a day. This was in concordance with the results of a study wherein 50 % of caregivers acknowledged brushing twice a day.11

When asked how many times they should have a dental check-up in a year, 46.2 % said only when there was a problem with their teeth. This trend has also been observed in an earlier study, where utilization and access to care were symptom-oriented.11 A Sri Lankan study, however, observed contrasting results with 93.5 % knowing that a dental visit every 6 months was required.23 Regular dental check-ups are essential for preventing dental disease. These visits allow dentists to implement clinical preventative measures such as dental prophylaxis, topical fluoride administration, and reinforce proper home dental care. Watt24 demonstrates how the majority of diseases are preventable. The difficult issue is to provide opportunities that are beneficial on an individual and community level. Though there is a significant advancement in surgical dental care, symptoms or treatment-oriented approaches can never eradicate oral illnesses.11

70.3 % of the caregivers reported that their child faced difficulty when eating. Further, 25.8 % and 65.8 % of caregivers, respectively, reported that their child experienced pain while having food, and observed food seepage out of the mouth in their child while eating. A recent systematic review confirmed the high prevalence (>80 %) and serious clinical consequences associated with swallowing difficulties in individuals with CP. This highlights the urgent need for better clinical treatment. The swallowing problems are not isolated problems; they have a significant influence on growth, nutritional status, general health, and quality of life. They also frequently exacerbate concerns, including respiratory troubles and gastroesophageal reflux.25

The QoL questionnaire's consistency and reliability are evaluated by computing the Cronbach's alpha score in each of its areas. For a questionnaire to be considered legitimate in a domain, its score must be more than 0.7. Our study's overall Cronbach's alpha was more than 0.7. This scale was therefore appropriate for measuring these domains. Similar results were obtained in a study by Waters et al., who noted that the Cronbach's alpha in their study ranged from 0.74 to 0.92.14

The study's merits include a population-based recruitment approach, which is necessary for assuming externally valid outcomes. Furthermore, combining persons within a narrow age range enhances interpretability. However, the study has drawbacks. To begin, the study used a purposive sampling approach to select individuals due to the rarity and low prevalence of CP. Although such an approach may yield a greater response rate, selection bias cannot be eliminated. Second, the sample size is modest; thus, larger investigations of children with CP may yield more robust results. Third, the study's sample size was limited to children aged 3 to 15, thus a larger study with age controls could provide a more thorough picture. Finally, caregiver interviews were conducted to assess the children's QoL.

To enhance caregivers' attitudes toward oral health, dental education and training initiatives should be prioritized. Basic oral health prevention approaches should be updated to reflect current evidence. This implementation may indirectly promote oral health attitudes, resulting in an improvement in the oral state of the impaired.

Conclusion

Children with CP have a lower quality of life. Validity testing using Cronbach's alpha suggests a good internal reliability for the overall CP-QoL questionnaire (caregiver version) indicating it to be a reliable tool for evaluating children with cerebral palsy's quality of life.
