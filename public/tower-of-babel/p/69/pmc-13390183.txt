Ureteral Metastasis From Endometrial Carcinoma Mimicking Upper Tract Urothelial Carcinoma

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma is a common gynecological malignancy [1]. Although most cases are diagnosed at an early stage, distant metastasis may occur in advanced disease. The most common metastatic sites include the pelvic and para‐aortic lymph nodes, vagina, and lungs [2].

Ureteral metastasis from endometrial carcinoma is extremely rare, with only a few cases reported to date. In previously reported cases, ureteral involvement was usually detected during follow‐up after treatment of the primary tumor [1, 3, 4, 5].

Ureteral lesions may mimic upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) on imaging studies, making definitive diagnosis challenging [6]. Herein, we report a rare case of ureteral metastasis from endometrial carcinoma identified at the initial presentation.

Case Presentation

A 64‐year‐old woman was referred to the Department of Gynecology with suspected endometrial carcinoma, which had been diagnosed based on endometrial biopsy.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated invasion of less than half of the myometrium, corresponding to cT1a disease (Figure 1a). Preoperative contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CECT) revealed left‐sided hydronephrosis, a mass in the mid‐ureter suggestive of left ureteral urothelial carcinoma (Figure 1b), and para‐aortic lymphadenopathy (Figure 1c). She was referred to our department for further evaluation.

(a) Pelvic MRI showing invasion of less than half of the myometrium, suggesting cT1a disease (arrow). (b) Abdominal axial CECT showing a mass in the left mid‐ureter (circle). (c) Abdominal coronal CECT showing left‐sided hydronephrosis and para‐aortic lymphadenopathy (arrow).

Serum creatinine (0.68 mg/dL) and the estimated glomerular filtration rate (66.3 mL/min/1.73 m2) were within normal limits. Serum tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (1.4 ng/mL), carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (13 U/mL), and cancer antigen 125 (27 U/mL), were also within normal limits. Urinalysis revealed numerous leukocytes and erythrocytes. Urine cytology was positive for malignancy. However, the cytological findings were insufficient to determine whether the cells represented urothelial carcinoma or adenocarcinoma. Cystoscopy showed no evidence of a bladder tumor. Ureteroscopy and retrograde urography demonstrated stenosis of the left mid‐ureter (Figure 2), without an obvious intraluminal tumor on ureteroscopy. Biopsy of the ureteral mucosa revealed atypical epithelium, and cytology of urine from the left renal pelvis also showed atypical cells.

Retrograde urography demonstrating stenosis of the left mid‐ureter (circle), without an obvious intraluminal tumor on ureteroscopy. Biopsy of the ureteral mucosa revealed atypical epithelium, and cytology of urine from the left renal pelvis found atypical cells.

Preoperatively, the patient was diagnosed with synchronous double malignancies, namely endometrial carcinoma and suspected left ureteral urothelial carcinoma, although metastatic involvement of the ureter or lymph nodes from endometrial carcinoma could not be excluded. The enlarged para‐aortic lymph nodes were considered to represent metastases from either endometrial carcinoma or suspected UTUC, and the primary origin could not be determined preoperatively.

Simultaneous surgery was planned, consisting of laparoscopic transperitoneal left nephroureterectomy by the urology team and total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo‐oophorectomy, omentectomy, and para‐aortic lymph node dissection by the gynecology team. During the urological procedure, left nephroureterectomy was completed without difficulty, although mild adhesions were observed around the left ureter.

During the open gynecological procedure, suspected vaginal invasion was noted, and intraoperative pathological examination revealed a positive vaginal surgical margin. The gynecology team determined that complete radical resection was not feasible. Therefore, systematic lymph node dissection was abandoned, and only para‐aortic lymph node biopsy and hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo‐oophorectomy were performed before postoperative systemic chemotherapy.

Histopathological examination revealed serous carcinoma of the endometrium with metastases to the bilateral ovaries and bilateral fallopian tubes (Figure 3). Para‐aortic lymph node biopsy confirmed metastatic uterine serous carcinoma. Histopathological examination of the ureteral tumor revealed adenocarcinoma morphologically similar to the endometrial carcinoma (Figure 4a). Tumor cells were diffusely distributed along the ureter from the renal hilum to the distal ureter and were also detected at the bladder‐side ureteral margin. Immunohistochemical staining showed PAX8 positivity in the ureteral tumor (Figure 4b), and the ureteral lesion was diagnosed as metastatic adenocarcinoma from endometrial carcinoma. Based on the presence of distant ureteral metastasis, the disease was classified as pathological stage pT3N2M1, corresponding to FIGO stage IVB according to the 2023 FIGO classification [7]. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 8 without complications and is currently receiving systemic chemotherapy in the Department of Gynecology.

Histopathological examination of the uterus demonstrated serous carcinoma of the endometrium characterized by papillary and tubular architecture, marked nuclear atypia, a high nuclear‐to‐cytoplasmic ratio, and frequent mitotic figures.

(a) Histopathological examination of the ureter showed diffuse intramural spread of adenocarcinoma extending from the renal hilum to the distal ureter. The tumor was composed of highly atypical cells with tubular and micropapillary architecture, resembling endometrial serous carcinoma. (b) Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated PAX8 positivity, supporting the diagnosis of metastatic endometrial carcinoma.

Discussion

Ureteral metastasis from endometrial carcinoma is extremely rare, with only a few cases reported to date [1, 3, 4, 5]. To the best of our knowledge, no previous reports have described a ureteral metastatic lesion identified simultaneously with the primary endometrial carcinoma at the time of initial diagnosis.

In the present case, CECT showed hydronephrosis and a mass in the left ureter. These findings are typically suggestive of UTUC. Urine cytology also supported the possibility of UTUC. Although urine cytology is useful for detecting malignant cells in the urinary tract, it cannot always determine the origin of the cells. Contamination of urine cytology by endometrial carcinoma cells has been reported [8, 9, 10]. Therefore, cytological findings should be interpreted with caution, especially in patients with a gynecological malignancy. Despite ureteroscopic biopsy with tissue sampling, a definitive diagnosis could not be established. In addition, UTUC could have significantly affected the prognosis and management. Therefore, simultaneous surgery with radical nephroureterectomy was considered appropriate. Because the diagnosis remained uncertain and UTUC could not be excluded, obtaining a definitive pathological diagnosis was prioritized over upfront systemic therapy.

Histopathological examination demonstrated diffuse intramural tumor spread along the ureter rather than a focal papillary lesion. This growth pattern likely resulted in ureteral wall thickening and hydronephrosis, explaining why the lesion closely mimicked UTUC on imaging. Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining demonstrated PAX8 positivity in the ureteral tumor, supporting the diagnosis of metastatic endometrial carcinoma. PAX8 is frequently expressed in endometrial carcinoma [11] and is generally not expressed in urothelial carcinoma. Therefore, PAX8 immunostaining may be particularly useful for distinguishing metastatic gynecological malignancies from primary urothelial carcinoma in diagnostically challenging cases.

Another important finding in this case was that the disease was ultimately diagnosed as FIGO stage IVB despite limited myometrial invasion on MRI. Preoperative CECT had already demonstrated para‐aortic lymphadenopathy, suggesting systemic disease. These findings demonstrate that local staging based on MRI alone may be insufficient and emphasize the importance of comprehensive systemic evaluation, including assessment of lymph‐node and distant metastases by CT imaging.

If ureteral metastasis had been accurately diagnosed preoperatively, radical nephroureterectomy might have been avoided. However, because UTUC could not be excluded despite ureteroscopic biopsy, urine cytology, and imaging findings, radical nephroureterectomy was considered the most appropriate management. This case highlights the importance of considering metastatic disease, in addition to synchronous primary urothelial carcinoma, when upper urinary tract abnormalities are identified in patients with gynecological malignancies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, ureteral metastasis from endometrial carcinoma is extremely rare and may mimic UTUC. Clinicians should be aware that gynecological malignancies may rarely involve the ureter. When upper urinary tract abnormalities are identified in patients with gynecological malignancies, metastatic disease should be considered in the differential diagnosis in addition to synchronous primary urothelial carcinoma.
