Contribution of Obesity to Polypharmacy in U.S. Older Adults
Authors: Alissa S. Chen, Ashwin K. Chetty, John A. Batsis, Kasia J. Lipska, Alexandra M. Hajduk
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Polypharmacy is highly prevalent among older adults and is associated with adverse drug events, treatment burden, and reduced quality of life.1,2 Obesity contributes to many of the chronic conditions that drive polypharmacy,3,4 but the extent of its contribution to polypharmacy has not been well quantified. In this study, we estimate the proportion of polypharmacy attributable to obesity in a nationally representative sample of U.S. older adults.

METHODS

We performed a cross-sectional analysis using data from the 2021–2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). We included adults aged ≥ 65 years with body mass index (BMI) ≥ 18.5 kg/m2 and excluded adults missing data on BMI or medication use. The independent variables were obesity, measured via BMI (≥ 30 kg/m2) or waist circumference (≥ 102 cm in men and ≥ 88 cm in women) and obesity class (class 1 = BMI 30 to < 35 kg/m2, class 2–4 = BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2). The dependent variable was polypharmacy, defined as self-reporting taking ≥ 5 prescription medications.

We compared those with and without obesity by age, sex, race, ethnicity, and clinical conditions. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test and continuous variables using the t-test. Using logistic regression, we calculated the adjusted relative risk of polypharmacy in participants with BMI-defined obesity compared to those without obesity, controlling for age, sex, education, smoking status (never, former, current), and alcohol use (not significant use, significant use, missing). We calculated the population attributable fraction (PAF) of polypharmacy due to BMI-defined obesity using this adjusted relative risk (aRR) and the prevalence of BMI-defined obesity among older adults with polypharmacy (P1) with the formula PAF = P1 * [(aRR – 1)/aRR] * 100.5 We additionally calculated the PAF of polypharmacy by obesity class and waist-circumference-defined obesity. Population-level estimates accounted for the complex survey design and were calculated using R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation).

Yale University institutional review board did not require review because this study did not involve human subjects. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

RESULTS

The study sample included 1,944 individuals, representing 53.2 million older adults (weighted mean [SD] age, 72.7 [5.2] years; 55% female; 3.9% Asian, 8.7% Black, 8.2% Hispanic, 73.5% White, and 5.7% other or multiple races or ethnicities) (Table 1). Almost two-fifths (38.7%, 95% CI 35.9%-41.5%) had BMI-defined obesity (22.6% with class I obesity and 16.1% with classes 2–4 obesity), 70.5% (95% CI 67.7%-73.2%) had waist circumference-defined obesity. The prevalence of polypharmacy was 41.8% (95% CI 38.9%-44.7%), representing 22 million older adults. Polypharmacy was more prevalent among people with BMI-defined obesity compared to those without BMI-defined obesity (51.1% [95% CI 47.0%-55.1%] vs. 35.9% [95% CI 32.9%-39.0%]; p < 0.001). The PAF of polypharmacy due to BMI-defined obesity was 14.8% (9.9%-18.5%), corresponding to approximately 3.3 million older adults with polypharmacy attributable to BMI-defined obesity (Fig. 1). The PAF for polypharmacy due to class I obesity was 4.9% (95% CI 1.3%-7.6%), for class 2–4 obesity was 9.7% (95% CI 7.0%-11.5%), and for waist circumference-defined obesity was 24.8% (95% CI 10.4%-34.4%). CONCLUSIONS

In this cross-sectional study, we found that obesity accounted for between 1 in 7 (BMI-defined obesity) and 1 in 4 (waist circumference-defined obesity) cases of polypharmacy among older adults. Obesity medications which provide significant weight loss can improve many obesity-related complications (e.g., diabetes, sleep apnea)6 and may potentially reduce obesity-related polypharmacy. However, the potential benefit of reducing polypharmacy needs to be weighed against the immediate increase in medication burden and side effects. Future studies should examine the impact of medications for obesity on polypharmacy among older adults with obesity.

Limitations include reliance on self-reported number of medications taken. Older adults in long term care settings were not included in this study, limiting generalizability to that population. There may be unmeasured confounding, e.g., due to wealth or access to care, that impacts the association between obesity and polypharmacy. There may be important differences in PAF within subgroups, but the evaluation of these differences is beyond the scope of this brief report. Cross-sectional data limits our ability to determine a temporal relationship between obesity and polypharmacy, although longitudinal studies have demonstrated this relationship.7

These findings underscore obesity as a potentially modifiable contributor to polypharmacy in older adults and highlight the importance of considering medication burden when evaluating treatment.
