The combined effects of heatwaves, air pollution and greenery on the risk of frailty: a national cohort study
Authors: Weifang Dai, Shanna Liu, Weina Xu, Yuqiang Shen, Xiao Yang, Qingli Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe associations between heatwaves and frailty, as well as the joint effects of heatwaves with air pollution and greenery, are currently unknown. This study leverages data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), which collected information from 6,400 older adults between 2011 and 2018. Our outcome variable was frailty, as measured by the frailty index (FI > 0.21). Heatwaves were defined based on maximum temperature, incorporating four thresholds (≥ 97.5%, 97.5%, 92.5%, and 90%) and three durations (≥ 2, 3, and 4 days). These variables were considered as time-varying variables, representing the one-year exposure preceding survival events. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and greenery (normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI)) were utilized as indicators of air pollution and greenery exposure, respectively, and were treated as time-varying indicators concurrent with heatwaves.Time-varying Cox proportional hazards models were employed to assess the independent effects, as well as the multiplicative and additive interactions of heatwaves, air pollution, and greenery on the risk of frailty. These effects were quantified using hazard ratios (HRs), a traditional product term representing the ratio of HRs, and the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI). Our findings indicate that heatwaves are associated with an increased risk of frailty, with HRs ranging from 1.035 (95% CI: 1.006–1.064) to 1.063 (95% CI: 1.028–1.101). We observed both a positive multiplicative interaction (HRs > 1) and an additive interaction (RERI > 0) between high level PM2.5 concentration, lack of greenery, and heatwaves. This study reveals that the combined effects exacerbate the adverse impact of heatwaves on the risk of frailty. Moreover, the combined effects of heatwaves, air pollution, and greenery exposure on frailty risk vary across age, gender, and educational attainment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73604-4.Keywords: Heatwave, Fratily, Elderly, China, Air pollution, GreenerySubject terms: Environmental social sciences, Natural hazards, Risk factors, Signs and symptoms

Introduction

Frailty, a growing health concern among the aging global population1, is a complex condition marked by an age-related decline in the resilience and functioning of multiple physiological systems, rendering individuals highly susceptible to a myriad of health issues2. Frailty has now been associated with increased mortality rates3, cognitive impairment4,5, heart failure6, myocardial infarction7, stroke8, and a disproportionate number of other adverse health outcomes9. Recognizing the multifaceted nature of frailty, prior research has categorized frailty status into three distinct levels: robust (FI ≤ 0.10), indicating good health status; prefrail (0.10 < FI < 0.21), a transitional phase requiring attention to prevent further deterioration; and frail (FI ≥ 0.21), indicating a severe decline requiring immediate intervention10,11. Our study focuses on evaluating changes in frailty status by comparing individuals’ frailty levels at baseline with a follow-up survey conducted two years later. Given the progressive nature of frailty and the escalating challenges associated with its mitigation and management, early identification, continuous monitoring, and targeted interventions aimed at modifying contributing factors are crucial in mitigating its detrimental effects3,12,13.

In recent years, the detrimental effects of elevated ambient temperatures on older adults have garnered significant attention. Research has consistently demonstrated that exposure to heat among elderly individuals is associated with heightened risks, including increased mortality rates14,15, increased visits to emergencies16, cognitive impairment17,18, reduced sleep duration19and the development of severe health conditions such as heart and lung diseases20,21. Notably, heat-related injuries may persist as a threat even after the body’s temperature returns to normal22. However, the intricate physiological mechanisms linking heat exposure to frailty and vulnerability remain largely unexplored23–25, with no research to date specifically investigating the impact of heatwaves on frailty among elderly individuals.

Given the potential interdependence of environmental factors on health outcomes, researchers are increasingly interested in the complex interplay and synergistic effects of these elements on human health. Previous studies have indicated a potential link between frailty and fine particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter of 2.5 micrometers or less (PM2.5)26, highlighting the standalone impact of air pollution on vulnerable populations.However, a growing body of research underscores that regular occurrences of heatwaves significantly amplify the detrimental effects of air pollution27,28. This finding indicates that when individuals are exposed to both heatwaves and air pollution simultaneously, the combined impact on health is more pronounced and damaging than the individual effects29. Studies have documented this phenomenon, revealing that such combined exposure can lead to increased risks of hypertension30, cognitive impairment17 and increased mortality rates31,32. Consequently, it is imperative that research should prioritize understanding the intricate interplay between heatwaves and air pollution, and its profound implications for public health.

Greenery has emerged as a myriad of benefits that encompass enhancements in cognitive function33, physical capacity34, and mental well-being35. Recent studies have further illuminated its role in mitigating the adverse effects of heat exposure and air pollution. Specifically, exposure to greenery has been found to significantly reduce the incidence of cardiovascular diseases and mortality associated with heat exposure36 while also purifying the air to provide health benefits37. Therefore, we speculate that greening exposure may have a joint effect on heat exposure and air pollution. Some studies have suggested that greater exposure to greenery is associated with better expected health outcomes in frail older adults38, but there is limited information on the combined effects of greenery and heat exposure on frailty, as well as air pollution. Moreover, although the results of these studies highlight the positive impacts of green spaces, it is important to recognize that not all of the studies have drawn definitive conclusions37,39. A limitation of these studies may be the misclassification of exposures when spatially coarse land cover information is used. In our study, sufficiently detailed geographic unit delineation would have been effective in remedying these deficiencies.

To date, the intricate interplay between extreme heat exposure, frailty, and the combined influence of heatwaves, air pollution, and greenery on frailty outcomes has been largely unexamined. Our research endeavors to fill this void by comprehensively investigating this matter. Specifically, we will examine not only how heatwave exposure affects frailty among older adults but also the potential for frailty under such conditions. Moreover, we aspire to uncover the intricate, possibly synergistic, effects that exposure to both greenery and air pollution during heatwaves may have on frailty outcomes. This investigation will encompass assessing how these exposures contribute to the likelihood of frailty states among elderly individuals. Ultimately, through this comprehensive analysis, we aim to provide valuable insights that can guide the development of targeted interventions and policies aimed at alleviating the adverse impacts of climate change on the health and well-being of older populations.

Method

Study population

The data for the study were collected from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), which is a comprehensive survey conducted across mainland China that aims to gather nationally representative samples of individuals aged 45 years and above for geriatric research. The survey included participants aged 45 years and older from 28 counties and districts and 450 villages and residents’ committees. The inaugural survey was carried out from 2011 to 2012 (Wave 1), followed by in-person interviews in 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), and 2018 (Wave 4).

In our study, a total of 6,400 individuals were included. The participants were selected on basis of the following criteria: (1) were 45 years old or older; (2) were interviewed in Wave 2, Wave 3, or Wave 4; and (3) had data on frailty and basic demographic independent variables. The specific process is illustrated in Fig S1. The CHARLS project was authorized by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee at Peking University. The IRB permission number IRB00001052-11015 was assigned for the main household survey, confirming that all research was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Research involving human participants must adhere to the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants provided written consent for the use of their data in the research.

Assessment of heatwaves

In this study, daily maximum air temperatures from a dense network of meteorological monitoring stations across China from 2010 to 2018 were collected to assess participants’ exposure to heat waves. The data were downloaded from the Resource and Environmental Science Data Centre of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (http://www.resdc.cn/data.aspx?DATAID=230). Environmental exposures were assessed against the environmental exposures matched to the participants’ city of residence. Given that specific residential location details were not provided, this study assumed that participants living in the same city faced similar levels of environmental exposure. There is no universally accepted definition of heatwaves. On the basis of previous surveys, we defined the temperature threshold as the current local air temperature and recognized that the duration of residence may affect residents’ tolerance to high temperatures.

Currently, there is a notable inconsistency in the definitions of heatwaves across previous surveys, with many adopting diverse temperature thresholds and consecutive day requirements to characterize them. In our study, we opted for daily maximum temperature as the primary metric for defining heatwaves, primarily because of its ability to capture the peak temperature conditions that encompass the entirety of daytime heat exposure. Furthermore, we included in the appendix a detailed presentation of our methodology, which utilized daily minimum temperature as the primary metric for defining heatwaves. This choice allows for a more comprehensive understanding of the heatwave intensity and potential impacts. We employed 12 distinct definitions of heatwaves (HW1 to HW12, detailed in Table 1) to represent heatwave exposure. For example, HW1, known as 97.5th-2D, signifies a heatwave occurrence when the daily maximum temperature exceeds the 97.5th percentile of the city-specific daily maximum temperature threshold for at least two consecutive days. Our approach defines heatwave exposure as the number of ‘heatwave days’ which are days that exceed the specified threshold and persist for a predetermined duration. To analyze heatwave exposure, we initially evaluated various time windows (Table S2–3) and discovered that exposure over a one-year period had the most pronounced impact on the outcome of interest. Therefore, we restricted the definition of heatwaves to encompass only the number of heatwave days occurring within the year preceding the occurrence of frailty, loss to follow-up, or the conclusion of the follow-up period (2018 wave) as a means to accurately reflect extreme heat exposure. Notably, in our investigation, heatwave exposure was a time-varying variable.

Assessment of air pollution

The yearly average levels of air pollutants (specifically PM2.5) were acquired from the China High Air Pollutants (CHAP) database, which is accessible at https://weijing-rs.github.io/product.html40. The data were collected at a resolution of 1 km (0.1° × 0.1°), making it a reliable and detailed database specifically designed for China. The model was created by combining ground-level air pollution monitoring data with satellite-derived aerosol optical depths and other geographical and temporal predictors, such as urban, forest, and meteorological data. The model obtained 10-fold cross-validation R2 values, and the root-mean-square error (RMSE) was 92% (10.76 µg/m3)41,42.

Assessment of greenery exposure

The dataset contains the maximum normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) for China on a yearly basis, with a spatial resolution of 30 meters. A dataset of maximum yearly NDVI values starting in 1986 was generated using Landsat 5/7/8/9 remote sensing images from the United States. The calculations are performed on the Google Earth Engine (GEE) remote sensing cloud computing platform. The dataset can be accessed at http://www.nesdc.org.cn. During the computation process, the NDVI is computed for every Landsat 5/7/8/9 remote sensing image in each year. The highest NDVI value for each pixel location in a year is then found, allowing for the creation of the yearly maximum NDVI. The formula for computing the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) is as follows: the NDVI is calculated by subtracting the red (R) band value from the near infrared (NIR) band value and then dividing the result by the sum of the NIR and R band values. NIR stands for the reflectance of near-infrared wavelengths, whereas R stands for the reflectance of red wavelengths. The phrase ‘band reflectance’ is also employed. The calculation of the NDVI for Landsat 5 involves the use of the B4 and B3 bands, whereas for Landsat 8, it involves the use of the B5 and B4 bands. Both calculations have a spatial resolution of 30 m43.

Assessment of frailty

In this investigation, our outcome variable was frailty, as measured by the frailty index (FI)13. The selection criteria for the FI indicators were based on established guidelines44, variables must have missing values < 5%; they must represent health-related deficits occurring in > 1% of the population; the prevalence of these deficits should generally increase with age; the deficits should not saturate prematurely (i.e., < 80% of the population affected); and the deficits comprising the FI must span a broad range of health systems. Following these criteria and informed by relevant domestic and international research45,46, we constructed a 39-item FI scale encompassing various health deficits (Table S1), including 13 types of chronic diseases, 4 disabilities, and 1 self-rating of health, 6 activities of daily living (ADL) items, 5 instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) items, 3 mobility-related items, and 5 muscle strength-related items under disability; depression assessed via the CESD-10 scale, with a score > 10 indicating the presence of a health deficit; and cognitive decline defined via the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status-modified (TICS-m), which uses a reverse-scoring system where each incorrect answer is worth 1 point, with the health deficit score calculated as the actual score divided by 21. For all the items except cognition, each accumulated health deficit contributed 1 point to the health deficit score. The Frailty Index (FI) was subsequently determined as the proportion of the health deficit score to the total measurement indicators, with a scale spanning from 0 to 1, enabling the classification of three frailty states: prefrailty (FI > 0.10, and FI ≤ 0.21), robust (FI ≤ 0.10), and frailty (FI > 0.21)47,48. Drawing upon the analysis framework used in other publications, by comparing frailty status between visits, we established definitions of frailty, including frailty robustness or prefrailty at baseline and frailty (FI > 0.21) at follow-up49,50.

Covariates

This survey included several potential complicating variables. Climate information, such as annual precipitation and relative humidity, was acquired from the website of the China Meteorological Administration (http://www.nesdc.org.cn). This study examined various sociodemographic factors, such as age (measured in years), sex (female or male), place of residence (urban or rural), economic status (divided into four economic zones: eastern, northeastern, central, and western), geographic subdivisions (central, eastern, northwestern, and northeastern)51, educational attainment (illiterate, elementary school and below, secondary school and above), marital status (married, unmarried, and other, such as divorced, widowed, cohabiting, etc.), and health insurance status (whether or not entitled to medical reimbursement). Health behaviors and lifestyle features include factors such as body mass index (BMI), frequency of alcohol intake (abstaining from alcohol for a year, consuming alcohol no more than once a month, consuming alcohol more than once a month), and smoking habits (never smoked, former smoker, current nonsmoker, current smoker). The assessment of one’s health state is also taken into account, which includes the individual’s self-perception of their health as either good or excellent, fair or poor. In addition, the study considered the variables of cooking energy and social activities (which encompassed a variety of interactions such as communicating with friends, participating in community clubs, reading newspapers or books, owning livestock or pets, playing cards or mahjong, listening to the radio or watching television, and engaging in volunteer work). Several studies have suggested that the amount of sleep a person has can affect their frailty index. Consequently, we included the number of hours of nocturnal sleep and the duration of naps in our analysis. To evaluate the duration of sleep, both during the night and during naps, we utilized the Sleep Duration Questionnaire (IPAQ). When assessing indoor air pollution, distinguishing between solid and clean fuels is crucial. Coal, a type of solid fuel, tends to contribute to pollution52. To ensure the precision of our analyses, we utilized directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) as a means of selecting both contextual and individual factors that potentially confound the association between exposure to air pollution and disease outcomes (Fig. S2).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive description

For descriptive analyses, categorical variables are presented as frequencies and proportions, whereas continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations. Variables that did not follow a normal distribution were described using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Furthermore, we utilized time-varying Cox proportional risk regression models to determine the correlation between frailty incidence and heatwaves.

Basic risk models

In this survival analysis, the duration of survival was calculated from the date of participant enrollment until the earliest occurrence of three potential endpoints: the date of the interview assessing frailty status, the date of death, or the date of the final interview prior to the conclusion of the study. We adopted a time-varying Cox proportional hazards regression model to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) between the incidence of frailty and heatwaves: h(t, x) = h0(t) exp (β1 × 1 + βmxm(t) + ui), where h0(t) signifies the baseline hazard function and x1 comprises time-invariant covariates (e.g., sex and gender), and βmxm(t) encompasses the coefficients associated with time-varying variables, such as heatwave exposure, air pollution, and greenery exposure. To address potential biases stemming from residual spatial clustering, we incorporated a random intercept at the county level within the time-varying Cox model53. Given the violation of the PH assumption, we stratified the model by geographic region for refinement.

Initially, we conducted an analysis to explore the independent effects of heatwaves, air pollution and greenery on frailty (each 10-unit increase in PM2.5 or each 0.1-percent decrease in the NDVI). We subsequently expanded the stratified analysis by several demographic subgroups, such as gender, age, place of residence, and education status. The differences in hazard ratios among subgroups were tested by adding a cross-product term between modifier and heatwave exposure. To test for differences in HRs among these subgroups, we added a cross-product term between the modifier (e.g., gender, age group) and heatwave exposure in our models. All models were adjusted for age, gender, meteorological variables (temperature, relative humidity), marital status, educational level, health status, patients’ body mass index (BMI), place of residence, social activity, public insurance, lifestyle habits (including alcohol consumption and smoking), cooking fuel, economic region category, and sleep duration (including both nighttime sleep and napping).

Measures of multiplicative interaction

This research used separate mixed Cox models to investigate the multiplicative effects of heatwave exposure, air pollution, and greenery on frailty. This effect was subsequently confirmed through the computation of a cross-product term, which considered each 10-unit increase in PM2.5 or each 0.1-percent decrease in the NDVI × each unit increase in heatwave days). We also studied the interactive impacts of heatwaves, PM2.5 and greenery on frailty, which are modified by sex, age, place of residence, and education status.

Measures of additive interaction

The additive effect between heatwaves and air pollution or green space exposure on frailty was evaluated using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI)54. The assessment of this additive interaction was conducted across four categories, which were determined by the combination of heatwaves, air pollution, and greenery exposure levels. Heatwaves, air pollution and greenery were divided into binary variables on the basis of their median exposure values. The RERI was computed using the following formula: RERI = HR11 − HR10 − HR01 + 1. The categories were defined as low level for values below the median and high level for values equal to or above the median. The RERI > 0 indicates a positive interaction on an additive scale, suggesting that the combined effect of factor of interest and exposure is more significant than single exposure due to a synergistic relationship between the factors. Conversely, if the RERI < 0, it indicates less than an additive combined effect, denoting that the antagonistic effect exists.We also used subgroup analysis for the additive impact of heatwaves, PM2.5 and greenery on frailty.

Sensitivity analyses

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we implemented several analytical strategies. Firstly, we adjusted for the year of enrollment as a categorical variable to control for potential base time residuals. Secondly, we excluded participants lost to follow-up to assess whether the main findings were influenced by this factor. Additionally, we conducted the warm-season mean temperature bias to examine possible confounding by meteorological factors. We repeated the main analyses using the number of heatwave events as the measure of heatwave exposure, and also incorporated the HW definitions based on the daily minimum temperature into the analysis. All the statistical analyses were performed using Stata MP18.

Independent effect of heatwave, air pollution and greenery on frailty

Notably, Models 1, 2, and 3 exhibited similar effect estimates, indicating that the adjustment for different covariates did not alter the primary results. A significant association was observed between the incidence of frailty and heatwave exposure, with hazard ratios (HRs) ranging from 1.035 (95% CI: 1.006–1.064) to 1.063 (95% CI: 1.028–1.101). These findings suggested that individuals exposed to heatwaves had a 4.8–7.4% increased risk of developing frailty compared with those who were not exposed. Additionally, a significant association was found between the incidence of frailty and air pollution exposure, with an HR of 1.559 (95% CI: 1.405–1.732) (Table 3). The results of the subgroup analyses revealed that age, sex, and education level significantly moderated the risk of frailty. Specifically, males, as well as less educated and older individuals, were more susceptible to the effects of heatwaves (Fig. 1). The statistical test results for the subgroup comparisons are presented in Table S4.

Multiplicative interactions between heatwaves, air pollution, and greenery

The multiplicative interactions between heatwaves, air pollution, and greenery indicators on the risk incidence under various heatwave definitions were analyzed (Table 4; Fig. 2). Specifically, the combined multiplicative interactions between all heatwaves (HWs) and PM2.5 were positive and statistically significant, indicating the presence of a synergistic interaction. The hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs ranged from 1.035 (1.026, 1.044) to 1.066 (1.046, 1.087), suggesting that the risk of frailty increased by 3.5–6.6% in individuals exposed to both heatwaves and PM2.5 compared to those exposed to only one or none. Similarly, the combined multiplicative interactions between six specific HWs (HW2, HW3, HW5, HW6, HW8, HW9) and greenery were also positive and statistically significant, indicating a synergistic interaction. The HRs with 95% CIs ranged from 1.013 (1.002, 1.024) to 1.016 (1.008, 1.025), indicating a slight increase in frailty risk. Furthermore, the combined multiplicative interactions between all HWs, PM2.5, and greenery were positive and statistically significant, indicating a synergistic interaction with HRs ranging from 1.001 (1.000, 1.002) to 1.005 (1.003, 1.007). Notably, the synergistic effects of heatwaves with high levels of PM2.5 exposure, heatwaves with low levels of greenery, and heatwaves with low levels of greenery were more pronounced in males than females, individuals aged less than 60 years than those aged 60 years or older, uneducated participants than educated participants, and those living in rural areas than urban areas (Fig. 3 and Table S5).

Additive interaction of heatwaves, air pollution, and greenery on the risk of frailty

The results of the additive interaction analysis indicated that PM2.5 and heatwaves synergistically affected the risk of frailty, with statistical significance found for six specific heatwaves (HW2, HW3, HW5, HW6, HW8, and HW9) (Table 5). The RERI values, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), ranged from 0.654 (0.089, 1.219) to 1.721 (0.725, 2.716). Additionally, the results of the additive interaction analysis indicated that greenery and heatwaves synergistically affected the risk of frailty, with statistical significance found for three specific heatwaves (HW2, HW3, and HW5). The RERI values, with 95% CIs, ranged from 0.550 (0.060, 1.041) to 0.568 (0.050, 1.085). Strong positive and synergistic combined effects of heatwaves and PM2.5 exposure were observed among males, uneducated participants, those living in rural villages, and individuals aged less than 60 years (Table S6). Furthermore, a significant synergistic effect of heatwaves and reduced greenery exposure was detected in males and rural residents when the heatwaves were defined by the 92.5th percentile for a duration of ≥ three days (Table S7).

Sensitivity analyses

The sensitivity analyses revealed a significant positive association between heatwaves and the incidence of frailty (Table S8). To verify the strength of the interactions, we utilized the same methodology and found that the multiplicative and additive effects of heatwaves, air pollution and greenery exposure on the incidence of frailty remained statistically significant (Tables S9–S11). Moreover, the impact estimates of heatwave events(Tables S12–S14) and heatwaves based on the minimum daily temperature were found to align with the impact estimates obtained from the main analyses (Tables S15–S17).

Discussion

This study represents one of the initial investigations aimed at estimating the combined effects of heatwaves, air pollution, and greenery on the risk of frailty. The study revealed a significant association between heatwave exposure and the incidence of frailty, with hazard ratios (HRs) ranging from 1.048 to 1.063. These findings suggest individuals exposed to heatwaves had a 4.8–7.4% increased risk of developing frailty compared with those who were not exposed. Furthermore, the results revealed that age, sex, and education level significantly moderated the risk of frailty. Specifically, males, as well as less educated and older individuals were more susceptible to the effects of heatwaves. Additionally, the study analyzed the effects of multiplicative and additive interactions between heatwaves, air pollution, and greenery indicators on the risk of frailty. The combined multiplicative interactions between all heatwaves and PM2.5, as well as the interactions between heatwaves, PM2.5, and greenery, were positive and statistically significant. Moreover, the combined multiplicative interactions between specific heatwaves (HW2, HW3, HW5, HW6, HW8, and HW9) and greenery were also positive and statistically significant. The results of the additive interaction analysis further indicated that PM2.5 and greenery interacted synergistically with heatwaves on the risk of frailty, with statistical significance found in the HWs (HW2, HW3, and HW5). The synergistic effects were more significant for males, younger individuals, uneducated participants, and those living in rural areas.

This is one of the few studies of its kind that has examined multiple definitions, specifically considering various definitions of heatwaves on the basis of daily maximum temperatures. Most research in this field has relied solely on daily maximum temperature as the defining criterion, with 12 different definitions being employed due to its widespread use and recognition in the scientific community. However, in one investigation on the influence of sleep patterns and heatwave exposure, the daily minimum temperature was also used as a criterion. The supplementary materials used in this study further provide research results on the basis of daily minimum temperatures and the frequency of heatwaves, revealing that the association between heatwaves and frailty persists. This indicates that considering both daily maximum and minimum temperatures, as well as the frequency of heatwaves, is essential for a comprehensive understanding. Therefore, it is crucial to consider multiple criteria simultaneously. Despite the varying definitions employed, the number (days) of heatwaves, particularly when characterized by daily maximum temperature, was found to be significantly associated with the occurrence of frailty.

Factors such as sex, education, and age influenced the connection between heatwave exposure and frailty status, according to additional subgroup analyses. Overall, our findings align with previous research, which has consistently highlighted the heightened risk faced by men during intense heatwaves, attributed partly to biological differences and socioeconomic factors55,56. Additionally, our findings indicate that a low level of education, a proxy for socioeconomic status (SES), as a significant contributor to increased vulnerability to heatwave exposure57,58.This observation is supported by a meta-analysis of 15 studies conducted in China, which revealed that individuals with less than six years of education had a greater likelihood of nonaccidental death during heatwaves than did those with higher educational attainment14. This disparity may stem from a combination of factors, including lower SES, greater exposure to physically demanding labor, limited access to healthcare services, and a reduced ability to mitigate the impacts of high temperatures. Notably, older individuals (aged 60 years and above) demonstrated increased vulnerability to the direct effects of heatwaves on frailty, and an interesting reversal emerged in the context of interactive effects involving air pollution and reduced greenery. Specifically, younger individuals (younger than 60 years) were found to be more susceptible to the combined effects of heatwaves, air pollution, and limited green space. Older adults are well-documented to be particularly sensitive to heatwaves because of factors such as diminished thermotolerance and potential comorbidities59. However, our study highlights a distinct pattern in the context of environmental stressors. The heightened sensitivity among younger individuals in the presence of air pollution and reduced greenery suggests that these factors may exacerbate the negative health impacts of heatwaves in this demographic.

This study revealed a positive interaction effect between heatwaves and high PM2.5 concentrations on the incidence of frailty, interpreted as synergistic effects. However, in the present analysis, no specific interaction patterns were observed. A growing number of epidemiological investigations have indicated that heatwaves and air pollution independently impact the incidence of frailty, which is consistent with our findings60,61.Heatwaves are often accompanied by expedited photochemical reactions, inducing the formation of secondary pollutants and altering the distribution and accessibility of airborne contaminants. Moreover, air pollution intensifies heatwaves by warming the atmosphere and diminishing the diurnal temperature range, thereby prolonging heatwave exposure62. Cao et al.63found a relationship between air pollution and surface heatwaves in Chinese cities of varying sizes, emphasizing the pivotal role of particulate matter in shaping urban thermal environments. From a mechanistic perspective, air pollution has been shown to facilitate the occurrence of oxidative stress (OS), which triggers various pathways of cytotoxic expression. These variables include cellular harm, the initiation of programmed cell death pathways, increased production of transcription factors and breakdown of proteins, diminished functioning of mitochondria, and hindered repair mechanisms64. Moreover, studies have shown that air pollutants contribute to the degradation of muscle proteins65, and elevated levels of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and IL-6 accelerate the development of weakness64,66,67. Additionally, Fougère et al. identified a link between air pollution and gene expression. This association was found to hinder the action of natural antioxidant enzymes and lead to dysfunction in various organs and systems68. Studies have consistently demonstrated that heatwaves exacerbate the adverse health effects of air pollution via multiple pathways, notably by intensifying the inflammatory reactions and oxidative stress induced by pollutants. These pathological mechanisms underscore the intricate interplay between heatwaves and air pollution, which jointly contributes to detrimental health outcomes67,69.The results of this study suggest that a combination of decreased vegetation and increased exposure to heatwaves has a mutually reinforcing effect on frailty. Owing to the scarcity of prior research in this particular area, no studies have been conducted on outcomes related to frailty. The detrimental impacts of heatwaves with decreased greenery on unfavorable pregnancy outcomes, hypertension, and cognitive impairment have been consistently reported in multiple studies17,30,70. These studies have shown that a decrease in the abundance of greenery exacerbates the impact of heatwave exposure on organisms. This may be because greener people help decrease the likelihood and severity of heatwave exposure. Possible reasons could include the extension of sleep duration (via full-night rest or napping) and the decrease in exposure to high temperatures, road noise, and air pollution. This is because greenery can impact health by reducing exposure, even when individuals are unable to physically immerse themselves in these areas71,72.

The study’s primary strengths are its comprehensive approach and depth of analysis. By utilizing a diverse range of heatwave definitions and integrating the long-term, locally adapted context of the population into our analysis, we ensure the robustness and comprehensiveness of our study. The utilization of nationally representative cohort data further enhances the generalizability of our findings, minimizing biases inherent in smaller sample sizes or cross-sectional designs.

Crucially, our investigation delves into a previously unexplored area, examining the collective impacts of heatwaves, air pollution, and greenery exposure on the risk of frailty. This depth of inquiry underscores the innovative contribution of this study to the field. Nevertheless, despite these strengths, we acknowledge several limitations that require attention in future research. First, the reliance on regional exposure data, without individual-level assessment, constitutes a notable constraint that limits the precision of our exposure estimates. Second, the retrospective evaluation of survival events, without the precise timing of frailty onset and progression, may have introduced some information distortion. Finally, the inability to incorporate detailed lifestyle factors, such as exercise routines and dietary conditions, into our models could have influenced the accuracy of our estimates. These limitations underscore the importance of ongoing research efforts to refine exposure assessment methods, improve data collection protocols, and integrate a broader range of covariates into analytical models. Such advancements will undoubtedly deepen our understanding of the intricate relationships between heatwaves, air pollution, greenery, and frailty status, informing more targeted interventions and policies.

In conclusion, our study has important implications for researchers, policymakers, and the public. Researchers highlights the need to consider multiple definitions of heatwaves and the complex interplay between environmental factors when investigating their impacts on health. Policymakers should be aware of the urgent need to address heatwaves and air pollution in urban planning and environmental policies while also promoting urban greening as a means of mitigating their negative effects on health. Finally, the public should be informed about the risks associated with these environmental exposures and encouraged to take proactive steps to reduce their exposure and support initiatives that promote healthy and sustainable urban environments.

Conclusion

This investigation revealed the harmful impacts of intense heatwave exposure on the risk of frailty. High levels of air pollution and reduced greenery jointly exacerbate frailty risk, with variations across age, gender, residence, and education. Policy interventions to reduce pollution and increase greenery could help mitigate the negative impacts of heatwaves on health.
