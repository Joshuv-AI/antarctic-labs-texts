Aortic Root Abscess Secondary to Coxiella burnetii Following TAVR
Authors: Parnia Behinaein, Omar A Jarral, Stevan Pupovac, Mei Chau, Chad Kliger, Marta L Feldmesser, Nirav C Patel, S Jacob Scheinerman, Alan R Hartman, Derek R Brinster
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundQ fever endocarditis, caused by the intracellular pathogen Coxiella burnetii, is uncommon yet highly destructive. Patients typically present with negative blood cultures and vague symptoms that delay diagnosis.Case SummaryA 67-year-old man with significant cardiovascular comorbidities, including prior transcatheter aortic valve replacement, permanent pacemaker, and reduced ejection fraction, presented with chest pain. Imaging revealed an aortic root abscess extending to the anterior mitral leaflet. Although blood cultures were negative, molecular diagnostics (Karius testing) and serology confirmed C burnetii. Surgical treatment required a complex hemi-Commando procedure, including radical debridement, aortic root homograft replacement, and reconstruction of the aorto-mitral curtain. Prolonged antibiotic therapy (doxycycline, hydroxychloroquine) was initiated for 2 years. Postoperatively, the patient’s condition stabilized with clinical improvement.DiscussionThis case highlights the destructive nature of Q fever endocarditis and emphasizes the importance of prompt diagnosis, multidisciplinary management, and aggressive surgical intervention in patients with extensive structural damage.Take-Home MessagesQ fever endocarditis is rare but destructive, necessitating high clinical suspicion despite negative blood cultures and advanced molecular diagnostics for diagnosis. Extensive surgical intervention, such as the Commando procedure, combined with multidisciplinary collaboration, significantly improves outcomes in severe cardiac involvement.Key words: aortic root abscess, Coxiella burnetii, culture-negative prosthetic valve infection, hemi-Commando procedure, Q fever endocarditis, TAVR

Q fever endocarditis is an uncommon but severe manifestation of Coxiella burnetii infection, representing 5% to 10% of all culture-negative endocarditis cases. Transmission occurs via inhalation of aerosols from parturient sheep, goats, or cattle.1 Geographically, Q fever outbreaks and higher incidence rates have been documented in parts of the southwestern and western United States, southern France, the Netherlands, and Australia.2,3

Patients frequently present with subtle or nonspecific symptoms, which can delay diagnosis. Left untreated or insufficiently treated, Q fever endocarditis can result in aortic root abscess, valve dehiscence, and conduction abnormalities.4 Here, we report a case of prosthetic valve Q fever complicated by a large aortic root abscess that required a hemi-Commando reconstruction.

History of Presentation

A 67-year-old man who worked as a truck driver and routinely transported livestock presented with a 3-month history of intermittent chest pain. The pain was initially mild but progressively worsened, accompanied by malaise and a 15-lb weight loss. Despite these symptoms, he sought no cardiac or infectious evaluation, delaying diagnosis. Examination revealed a new systolic murmur consistent with prosthetic valve dysfunction.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included culture-negative endocarditis (Coxiella, Bartonella, HACEK [Haemophilus, Aggregatibacter, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, and Kingella], Brucella), structural transcatheter valve degeneration, and device-related infection. Given the new large periannular abscess, a high index of suspicion for an infectious etiology remained, culminating in specialized diagnostic testing that revealed Q fever.

Management

Initial medical management

Empirical vancomycin and ceftriaxone were initiated, escalated to vancomycin and piperacillin-tazobactam, and finally streamlined to doxycycline (100 mg q12 h) plus hydroxychloroquine (200 mg q8 h) once Coxiella was confirmed.5 Surgical intervention was urgent given extensive root abscess.

Preoperative LVEF was 30%, and separation from cardiopulmonary bypass demonstrated biventricular dysfunction. Given the extensive surgical reconstruction, a prophylactic IABP was inserted before chest closure. Contemporary series of extensive aorto-mitral reconstructions report that prophylactic IABP in patients with severe left ventricular dysfunction or root abscess reduces early circulatory failure mortality by half without increasing embolic or bleeding events.6 Compared with venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, which is associated with a thromboinflammatory burden that is undesirable in infected fields, IABP offers rapid deployment, diastolic coronary augmentation, and afterload reduction, making it the least invasive, evidence-supported bridge to myocardial recovery in this setting. Owing to pronounced inflammation and coagulopathy, the chest was initially left open, secured with sterile dressings and bridging devices. On postoperative day 4, the patient returned to the operating room for chest washout, placement of antibiotic-impregnated beads, reinspection of the graft, placement of epicardial pacing leads due to complete heart block, and final chest closure once stability was assured.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was initially hemodynamically unstable postoperatively, but stabilized with vasoactive drugs and ventilatory optimization. He was rapidly extubated after chest closure on day 4. Antibiotics were later switched to oral therapy, and the patient was discharged home on day 23 with plans for prolonged antibiotic treatment lasting 24 months and heart failure management using sacubitril-valsartan, beta-blockers, and sodium-glucose transport protein 2 inhibitors. The patient was subsequently noncompliant with antibiotics and did not attend follow-up.

Discussion

Q fever endocarditis

C burnetii typically manifests as a subclinical or flu-like illness, but it can evolve into an indolent endocarditis, predominantly affecting prosthetic valves or patients with underlying valvulopathies.7 Owing to low bacterial load and intracellular location, routine blood cultures are often negative, necessitating specialized molecular or serologic testing (eg, phase I and II IgG/IgM titers, polymerase chain reaction). Accurate identification requires specialized testing. Indirect immunofluorescence assay is the reference standard (phase I IgG ≥1:800 defines chronic infection), whereas polymerase chain reaction detects bacterial DNA in blood or tissue.8,9 Despite the typically slow and indolent progression of C burnetii, the extensive destruction seen in this case likely resulted from delayed diagnosis coupled with prosthetic valve–related vulnerability. Prosthetic TAVR valves are predisposed to aggressive periannular extension and abscess formation, exacerbating tissue destruction beyond the typical pathology seen with Coxiella alone. Given aerosol transmission of C burnetii, individuals with prosthetic valves or cardiac devices should exercise caution when exposed to livestock, though current guidelines recommend emphasizing preventive measures, protective equipment, and prompt clinical assessment rather than strict avoidance. Positron emission tomography–computed tomography is strongly recommended for suspected prosthetic valve endocarditis, particularly with TAVR valves and fastidious organisms, but was omitted here because of rapid echocardiographic and molecular diagnosis. Early positron emission tomography–computed tomography might have reduced diagnostic delay and periannular destruction.

Why the hemi-Commando procedure?

Intraoperative inspection demonstrated infection extending to the anterior mitral leaflet and intervalvular fibrous body, while the posterior mitral valve leaflet, annulus, and subvalvular apparatus remained intact. Salvaging these native posterior structures allowed patch augmentation rather than complete mitral valve replacement, allowing a hemiCommando rather than a full Commando reconstruction. This approach enabled removal of all infected material, shortened cross-clamp and bypass times, and preserved left ventricular–annular dynamics (compared with a full Commando)—advantages deemed critical in a patient with depressed ejection fraction and elevated operative risk.

Performing TAVR in younger patients potentially increases lifetime exposure to bloodstream infections and risk of complex prosthetic valve endocarditis (compared with other valve choices such as the Ross procedure or a mechanical valve). Such infections often cause periannular invasion and extensive structural damage, significantly elevating surgical complexity and operative risk. In this case, destructive endocarditis and abscess formation extended from the prosthetic aortic valve into the aorto-mitral curtain and anterior mitral leaflet, leaving no option but a radical reconstruction. The hemi-Commando procedure provides a means for removing all infected tissue, reconstituting annular continuity, and preventing recurrence.10 Recent studies confirm favorable outcomes for the hemi-Commando procedure in aorto-mitral infections. Valve-sparing approaches remain highly individualized.10,11 In cases where the posterior mitral valve leaflet is extensively involved—often due to direct infectious extension across the aorto-mitral continuity—complete resection of the posterior mitral valve leaflet may become unavoidable. This may necessitate a full Commando procedure for complete aortic and mitral reconstruction.12

In our case, we elected to use a cryopreserved homograft for aortic root replacement, given its theoretical advantage of improved resistance to reinfection. Biologic inertness of homografts may diminish reinfection risk in contaminated fields.13

Long-term antibiotic therapy

Guidelines recommend prolonged antibiotic therapy with doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine for at least 18 to 24 months to effectively eradicate C burnetii and prevent relapse.14 Close monitoring for hydroxychloroquine toxicity is essential.

Multidisciplinary care

This patient's outcome reflects tight collaboration among cardiothoracic surgery, infectious disease, cardiology, and electrophysiology.15 Each specialty played a key role—from diagnosing a rare culture-negative organism to meticulously rebuilding the aortic and mitral substructures and ensuring safe lead extraction.

Follow-up and prognosis

At his 3-month follow-up appointment, the patient was clinically stable on doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine and optimized heart failure therapy. However, he subsequently discontinued antibiotics and was lost to follow-up.

Conclusions

This case underscores that Q fever endocarditis can be profoundly destructive in patients with prosthetic valves or cardiac devices, often requiring the hemi-Commando procedure—a radical form of surgical debridement and reconstruction. Negative routine cultures should not preclude consideration of C burnetii. Early recognition, advanced molecular diagnostics, aggressive surgical intervention, and prolonged antibiotic therapy can achieve infection control and salvage cardiac function in these exceptionally challenging cases.
