EGFR alteration is an adverse prognostic factor in IDH-mutant astrocytoma
Authors: Cheyanne C Slocum, Phuong Nguyen, Meenakshi Vij, Raymund L Yong, Jorge Samanamud, Satomi Hiya, Carolina Maldonado-Díaz, Melissa Umphlett, Thenzing J Silva-Hurtado, Kimmo J Hatanpaa, Mariano S Viapiano, Matija Snuderl, Kalil G Abdullah, Samuel K McBrayer, Dolores Hambardzumyan, Jamie M Walker, Nadejda M Tsankova, Timothy E Richardson
Published: 2025

Under current diagnostic and grading systems, adult-type diffusely infiltrating gliomas are subdivided based on the presence or absence of IDH1/2 mutation and 1p/19q co-deletion into glioblastoma (IDH-wildtype), astrocytoma (IDH-mutant with retained 1p/19q), and oligodendroglioma (IDH-mutant with 1p/19q co-deletion). Grading of these lesions was based exclusively on histologic features until the publication of cIMPACT-NOW Updates 3 [1] and 5 [2] which were ultimately incorporated into the 5th edition of the CNS WHO Guidelines [3]. These introduced molecular criteria for WHO Grade 4 designation in glioblastoma (EGFR amplification, gain of chromosome 7 with concurrent loss of chromosome 10 [+ 7/− 10], and TERT promoter [TERTp] mutation) and IDH-mutant astrocytoma (homozygous CDKN2A/B loss), even in the absence of high-grade histologic features. EGFR amplification, + 7/− 10, and TERTp mutation have also rarely been identified in IDH-mutant astrocytoma cases, however the frequency of these alterations is unclear, and evidence regarding their prognostic significance in IDH-mutant tumors is mixed [4–7].

Using data from four independent glioma datasets, including cohorts from the Genomics Evidence Neoplasia Information Exchange Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (GENIE-DFCI) (n = 251) [8, 9], Glioma Longitudinal Analysis (GLASS) Consortium (n = 77) [10], Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) (n = 192) [11], and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (n = 270) [12], we established a combined cohort of 790 IDH-mutant astrocytomas (Fig. 1a). Clinical and molecular data were downloaded from the cBioPortal interface [13], manually reviewed, and analyzed as previously described [5, 14–16], to determine the impact of individual molecular alterations on progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in IDH-mutant astrocytomas (Supplemental Methods).

The presence of TERTp mutation and + 7/− 10 were exceptionally rare in our combined cohort and had no significant effect on survival in univariate models. 2.5% of cases (20/790) had EGFR alterations, including amplification (1.8%; cohort range 0.7–7.8%) or activating mutation (0.8%; 0–2.6%) (Table S1). Homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B was more common, present in 12.3% (97/790) of cases (10.8–22.1%). Four cases (0.5%) had both homozygous CDKN2A/B deletion and EGFR alteration while the majority of cases were wildtype for both genes (85.7%; 677/790) (Fig. 1b). Of the EGFR alterations present, the majority (14/20) were amplifications while the remainder (6/20) were pathogenic mutations resulting in constitutive receptor activation (Fig. 1c). Similar to CDKN2A/B deletion, the presence of EGFR alteration was associated with higher histologic grade as well as elevated overall copy number variation and tumor mutation burden, suggesting that EGFR amplification was associated with genomic instability and tumor progression [17]. No differences were noted in the age, sex, or frequency of MGMT promoter methylation between the three molecularly-defined groups (Table S2).

Compared to IDH-mutant astrocytomas with wildtype/retained CDKN2A/B and EGFR, PFS and OS were significantly lower in cases with homozygous CDKN2A/B deletion or EGFR alteration (PFS: p = 0.0004 for CDKN2A/B deletion and p = 0.0046 for EGFR alteration; OS: p < 0.0001 for both CDKN2A/B deletion and EGFR alteration) (Fig. 1d, e). Of note, EGFR alteration only significantly impacted survival in the isolated GENIE-DFCI and GLASS cohorts (with a trend toward shorter survival in the MSKCC cohort and no significant effect in the TCGA cohort), while CDKN2A/B deletion had a significant impact on OS within each separate cohort, likely due to differences in frequency of the molecular alterations (Figure S1a–d). Patients with EGFR-altered IDH-mutant astrocytoma had significantly longer survival compared to IDH-wildtype glioblastoma (p < 0.0001) (Figure S1e). When separated by histologic grade, the difference in PFS and OS was significant only in cases with histologic grades 2–3 (PFS: p = 0.0005 for CDKN2A/B deletion and p = 0.0007 for EGFR alteration; OS: p < 0.0001 for CDKN2A/B deletion and p = 0.0002 for EGFR alteration) but not grade 4 histology (Fig. 1f, g and Table S2). The type of EGFR alteration was not relevant to the impact on overall survival, as both EGFR amplification (p = 0.0008) and pathogenic mutation (p = 0.0029) resulted in shorter overall survival compared to EGFR & CDKN2A/B wildtype cases (Fig. 1h), and no difference was found between cases with EGFR amplification compared to cases with EGFR mutations (Figure S1f).

A significant limitation of this study is that due to the relative rarity of EGFR alterations in IDH-mutant astrocytomas, we had to combine multiple cohorts with differences in patient survival, EGFR alteration type/frequency, and method of molecular analysis. Because there was significant variation in patient survival between 2021 WHO grades (Figure S1g) and database sources (Figure S1h), we performed multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression using database source as a covariate to determine the independent effect of the molecular features. This analysis showed that age (Hazard Ratio [HR]: 1.01 [95% confidence interval [CI] 1.00–1.03]; p = 0.0305), CDKN2A/B deletion (HR: 1.67 [95% CI 1.18–2.35]; p = 0.0034), and EGFR alteration (HR: 1.92 [95% CI 1.03–3.61]; p = 0.0415) were independently associated with worse PFS (Fig. 1i), while age (HR: 1.02 [95% CI 1.00–1.03]; p = 0.0195), histologic grade (HR: 1.33 [95% CI 1.03–1.71]; p = 0.0028), CDKN2A/B deletion (HR: 2.04 [95% CI 1.49–2.79]; p < 0.0001), and EGFR alteration (HR: 2.99 [95% CI 1.62–5.54]; p = 0.0005) were independently associated with worse OS (Fig. 1j). This independent association of EGFR alteration with worse survival was seen for both EGFR amplification (HR: 2.93 [95% CI 1.43–6.00]; p = 0.0032) and mutation (HR: 3.56 [95% CI 1.31–9.70]; p = 0.0129) (Fig. 1k).

While relatively uncommon, EGFR amplification and pathogenic activating mutations may occur in IDH-mutant astrocytoma and are associated with worse PFS and OS, similar in magnitude to, but independent of, CDKN2A/B deletion. While these EGFR alterations translate to significantly worse survival in patients with IDH-mutant astrocytoma, survival in patients with IDH-wildtype glioblastoma remains significantly worse. Overall, the presence of EGFR alterations in IDH-mutant astrocytoma, especially in the absence of CDKN2A/B deletion or grade 4 histology, result in significantly more aggressive behavior, necessitating closer follow-up and more aggressive therapeutic intervention. These data suggest that the presence of EGFR alterations may be sufficient to upgrade these tumors to WHO grade 4 and should be considered for future WHO grading systems if validated in independent datasets [18]. As such classification evolves, the potential benefit of EGFR-targeted therapies in this rare molecular subset of IDH-mutant astrocytomas remains unknown. However, preclinical research may be justified given their prognostic and therapeutic implications.
