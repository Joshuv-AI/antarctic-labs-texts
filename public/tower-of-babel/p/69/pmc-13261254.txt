Statin use and cognitive outcomes in older adults
Authors: Raiza Rossi, Mufti Mushfiqur Rahman, Zafer Akman, Laura T. Moreines, Armin Nouri, Mohammad Al Mouslmani, Chidubem Ezenna, Kaitlyn A. Stoehr, Shefa Arya Nezhad, Stephen Y. Wang, Abdulla A. Damluji, Michael G. Nanna
Published: 2026

Introduction

As the global population ages and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) continues to increase in older adults [1,2] preserving cardiovascular and cognitive health has become a public health priority. Given the strong connection between cardiovascular and brain health [3] and the increasing prevalence of cognitive impairment and dementia among older adults, targeting cardiovascular risk factors such as low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is a promising strategy to maintain cognitive function. Therefore, HMG CoA reductase inhibitors, also known as statins, represent a promising therapeutic option to promote long-term brain health [4].

Paradoxically, reports of cognitive adverse effects among statin users have surfaced in clinical settings, prompting the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to update statin labeling in 2012 to address potential cognitive side effects [5]. However, more recent evidence suggests potential cognitive benefits associated with the use of statins [4]. To further clarify this association, two ongoing randomized clinical trials (RCTs) are currently underway: the PRagmatic EValuation of evENTs And Benefits of Lipid-lowering in oldEr adults (PREVENTABLE) trial in the United States, enrolling adults aged ≥75 years without diagnosed atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (CVD), dementia, or disability [6], and the STAtins in Reducing Events in the Elderly (STAREE) trial in Australia, enrolling adults aged ≥70 years without CVD, diabetes, or dementia [7].

In the context of inconsistent evidence and pending trials, results continued research to better define the cognitive safety and potential neuroprotective role of statins is required, particularly in older adults. Thus, we set out to examine the association between statin use and cognitive outcomes among older adults in the Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial Memory and Cognition in Decreased Hypertension (SPRINT-MIND) study, a sub-study of the SPRINT trial, a multicenter randomized clinical trial conducted from 2010 to 2018, that included adjudicated cognitive outcomes [8].

Methods

We conducted a secondary analysis of the SPRINT-MIND study, which evaluated the risk of probable dementia in hypertensive, non-diabetic, 50 years and older adults receiving standard systolic blood pressure (SBP <140 mmHg) vs. intensive (SBP <120 mmHg) blood pressure (BP) treatment [8]. The data were de-identified and obtained through an approved process from the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute Biologic Specimen and Data Repository Information Coordinating Center, therefore human subjects approval was not required. Our analysis examined the association between statin use and cognitive outcomes among older adults, independent of blood pressure treatment intensity.

We included adults aged 65 years and older with complete cognitive outcome data, determined by in-person assessments of global cognition with the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), memory (Logical Memory I and II), and processing speed (Digit Symbol Coding), with follow-up functional evaluation using the 10-item Functional Activities Questionnaire for participants meeting predefined MoCA cutoffs or showing a ≥5-point decline performed at baseline, at 2 and 4 years of follow-up, and a study closeout if longer than 1 year from the 4-year follow-up visit [8]. The primary cognitive outcome was occurrence of probable dementia (PD), with secondary outcomes of occurrence of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and a composite of PD or MCI. Participants were diagnosed as no cognitive impairment, MCI or PD based on standardized criteria by two adjudicators [8].

We used multiple imputation by chained equations to address missing covariates. Propensity score matching was then applied to balance baseline characteristics between statin users and non-users, and Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association between statin use and occurrence of PD, MCI and composite PD or MCI. Analyses were performed with STATA version 18 (StataCorp. 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). For all analyses, p-values were reported for two-tailed tests with an alpha of 0.05.

Results

The propensity score matched cohort included a total sample of 2,416 patients, evenly distributed between non-statin users and statin users (1,208 per group). The mean age of the cohort was 69.9±3.3 years. Overall, 36.6% of participants were female, 70.4% were White, and 25.5% were Black. There were no significant differences between groups in baseline characteristics.

There were no significant differences in occurrence of PD between non-statin and statin users (2.9% vs. 3.1%, p=0.880), and MCI (6.7% vs. 8.1%, p=0.237). Similarly, the composite outcome of PD/MCI occurred at similar rates in both groups (8.9% vs. 10.0%, p = 0.384) (Fig. 1). Multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models showed no significant associations between statin use and cognitive outcomes. Statin use was not significantly associated with PD (hazard ratio [HR] 1.06, 95% CI 0.63-1.82, p=0.810), MCI (HR 1.31, 95% CI 0.94-1.82, p=0.105), or the composite PD/MCI outcome (HR 1.26, 95% CI 0.94-1.69, p=0.116).

Discussion

In this analysis of SPRINT-MIND participants, statin use was not significantly associated with the risk of dementia or MCI in older adults, regardless of blood pressure treatment intensity. While a non-significant trend toward higher dementia risk among statin users was observed, this is likely attributable to residual unmeasured confounding. This observation underscores the importance of future studies that are adequately powered to detect clinically meaningful differences.

Overall, these findings are consistent with recent observational data demonstrating the neurocognitive safety of statins and contribute to a growing body of evidence suggesting neutral effects of statin therapy on cognitive outcomes [4]. Clinically, these results reinforce statin therapy continuation when indicated, supporting cardiovascular risk reduction without apparent compromise of neurocognitive safety. This evidence is particularly relevant for aging populations in whom both vascular disease and cognitive decline often intersect.

This study has several limitations inherent to its observational design, including potential residual confounding and the inability to infer causality. Moreover, propensity score matching required restricting the age range of the SPRINT-MIND population and potentially underrepresenting the oldest adults at highest risk for cognitive decline. In addition, SPRINT-MIND participants were healthier than the typical older adult, particularly those aged >75 years [8], which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Finally, although SPRINT-MIND captured medication names for statins, it did not include specific doses of each medication which would have guided understanding of any dose-response relationship.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide timely reassurance for clinicians and patients. Current cardiovascular guidelines generally do not recommend avoiding or discontinuing statins due to cognitive concerns, and our results further support this guidance [4,9,10]. These results highlight the need for continued investigation into the interplay between lipid-lowering therapy, vascular health, and cognition to inform future prevention strategies for healthy aging.

Conclusion

In this secondary analysis of SPRINT-MIND, statin use was not associated with the risk of dementia or mild cognitive impairment among older adults, independent of blood pressure treatment intensity. These findings provide additional reassurance regarding the neurocognitive safety of statins when used for cardiovascular risk reduction in later life. While residual confounding and limited generalizability must be acknowledged, the results align with current guideline recommendations that cognitive concerns should not deter statin therapy when clinically indicated. Ongoing randomized trials will be critical to definitively clarify the role of statins in preserving cognitive health in aging populations.
