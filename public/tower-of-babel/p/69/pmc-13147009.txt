Elevated serum TMAO levels associate with higher risk of arteriovenous vascular access thrombosis in hemodialysis patients
Authors: Meng-Kan Chen, Yu-Chin Chang, An-Kuo Chou, Mu-Yang Hsieh, Ching-Hua Kuo, Po-Hsun Huang, Ting-Ju Lai, Chih-Cheng Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundArteriovenous access thrombosis is a major cause of morbidity in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis, and conventional clinical and anatomical risk factors do not fully explain its occurrence. Trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), a gut microbiota–derived metabolite that accumulates in kidney failure and exhibits prothrombotic properties, has been linked to cardiovascular events in dialysis populations. Its relevance to dialysis arteriovenous access outcomes, however, remains unclear.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter prospective cohort study including 375 adult patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis at 12 centers in Taiwan. Baseline serum TMAO concentrations were measured using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry and analyzed as tertiles and as a log-transformed continuous variable. Participants were followed for a median of 24 months. The primary outcome was time to first arteriovenous access thrombosis. Associations were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models with multivariable adjustment and restricted cubic spline analyses.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 24 months, 88 patients (23%) experienced arteriovenous access thrombosis. Thrombosis incidence increased stepwise across TMAO tertiles (13%, 23%, and 34%; P < .001). In unadjusted analysis, patients in the highest TMAO tertile had a higher risk of thrombosis compared with the lowest tertile [unadjusted hazard ratio 3.10; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.75–5.51]. This association remained significant after multivariable adjustment (adjusted hazard ratio 2.87; 95% CI 1.60–5.17). Results were consistent in competing-risk analyses treating death as a competing event. Addition of TMAO to a baseline clinical model yielded modest improvement in discrimination (ΔC = 0.011, 95% CI: −0.047 to 0.070; P = .051).ConclusionHigher serum TMAO levels were independently associated with an increased risk of arteriovenous access thrombosis in patients undergoing hemodialysis. These findings suggest that TMAO reflects a systemic prothrombotic milieu relevant to dialysis arteriovenous access and support further studies to determine whether TMAO represents a modifiable pathway or a biomarker of uremic thrombotic susceptibility.Keywords: arteriovenous access, hemodialysis, thrombosis, TMAO

What was known:

Arteriovenous access thrombosis remains a frequent and clinically important complication in hemodialysis patients, and conventional clinical and anatomical risk factors incompletely explain its occurrence. Trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), a gut microbiota–derived metabolite, accumulates in kidney failure and has been linked to cardiovascular risk.

This study adds:

In a multicenter prospective cohort of hemodialysis patients, higher baseline serum TMAO levels were associated with an increased risk of subsequent arteriovenous access thrombosis, independent of established clinical and dialysis-related factors. Associations with access stenosis, access failure, or mortality were not observed.

Potential impact:

These findings support further evaluation of TMAO as a biomarker of thrombotic risk in dialysis arteriovenous access. They also highlight the need for longitudinal and mechanistic studies to clarify whether TMAO reflects a modifiable pathway or serves as a marker of broader uremic thrombotic susceptibility.

INTRODUCTION

Arteriovenous (AV) access thrombosis remains one of the most frequent and clinically consequential complications in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis, leading to repeated interventions, hospitalizations, access loss, and increased healthcare costs [1]. Despite advances in access surveillance and interventional techniques, thrombosis rates remain substantial worldwide, affecting both arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) and arteriovenous grafts (AVGs) [2]. Epidemiological data indicate that established clinical and anatomical risk factors—such as access type, stenosis, and comorbid vascular disease—account for only part of the observed thrombotic burden, underscoring an unmet need to identify additional systemic contributors to thrombosis risk in this population [3].

Patients with kidney failure are characterized by a profoundly altered metabolic milieu, including accumulation of uremic solutes that may influence vascular and thrombotic biology. Trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO) is a gut microbiota–derived metabolite generated from dietary precursors such as choline and L-carnitine and subsequently oxidized in the liver [4]. Under normal renal function, TMAO is efficiently cleared; however, in hemodialysis patients, circulating concentrations are markedly elevated [5–7]. Prior clinical studies have associated higher TMAO levels with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in dialysis populations, and experimental data suggest that TMAO may promote platelet hyperreactivity and endothelial dysfunction [8–11]. Nevertheless, whether TMAO is relevant to dialysis-specific thrombotic complications, particularly AV access thrombosis, remains insufficiently studied [12].

To date, clinical investigations of AV access outcomes have focused primarily on local anatomical factors and procedural characteristics, with limited evaluation of systemic metabolic contributors. As a result, the potential role of gut-derived metabolites in access thrombosis represents an important knowledge gap with direct clinical relevance.

We therefore hypothesized that higher circulating serum TMAO levels are associated with an increased risk of subsequent AV access thrombosis in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the association between baseline serum TMAO concentrations and incident AV access thrombosis in a multicenter prospective cohort of maintenance hemodialysis patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study participants and design

This multicenter prospective study investigated the clinical and biological factors related to outcomes of AV access in patients on maintenance hemodialysis. The study was approved by the institutional review board (108-115-F) and all participants provided informed consent to participate in the study. Patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis were recruited from 12 hemodialysis centers. The patient inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 18 and 90 years, (2) maintenance dialysis for more than 6 months, (3) use of a functional AVF or AVG at study entry, and (4) no hospitalization in the previous 3 months. Patients with a prior history of AV access thrombosis were not excluded, as they represent a clinically relevant subgroup at increased risk of recurrence. All eligible patients receive 4 h of hemodialysis three times a week using a synthetic dialysis membrane (polyamide or polysulfone). The dialysate in all patients was a standard ionic composition and bicarbonate-based buffer. The adequacy of dialysis was assessed monthly using the single pool Kt/V of urea nitrogen.

Data collection

We obtained information on demographics, comorbidities, medications, dialysis-related data, and laboratory data from the medical records of the dialysis centers. Cardiovascular diseases were defined as a composite history coronary artery disease (CAD), peripheral artery diseases, or stroke. All patients received prospective clinical follow-up of their hemodialysis AV access under the same protocol at all respective hemodialysis centers [13]. Follow-up surveillance of AV access patients included physical examination, dynamic venous pressure monitoring at each hemodialysis session, and transonic examination of access blood flow rate immediately after intervention, and then monthly, if available (Table S1) [13]. Patients with abnormal clinical or hemodynamic findings were referred for fistulography and intervention as needed. An AV access coordinator obtained follow-up data via review of dialysis records, angiography, and surgical reports every 3 months. Data were collected at baseline and updated quarterly. Follow-up began at the date of baseline blood sampling for TMAO measurement and continued for up to 24 months, or until the occurrence of death, switch to peritoneal dialysis, kidney transplantation, transfer to a nonparticipating center, or completion of the 24-month follow-up period. There were no missing data for baseline covariates or outcome events included in the present analyses; therefore, no imputation procedures were required.

Laboratory methods

After a 12-h fast and temporary cessation of medications, 10 ml of blood was drawn from each patient before the dialysis session. The blood sample was drawn directly from the AV access, which was arranged on a midweek dialysis day. They were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C immediately after collection. The serum samples were then stored at –80°C until use. Plasma biochemical parameters, including cholesterol, triglycerides, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), calcium, phosphate, and albumin, were analyzed by standard laboratory procedures. Single-pool Kt/V of urea nitrogen was calculated after enrollment using the second-generation logarithmic formula of Daugirdas. Residual renal function was estimated from an interdialytic urine collection and expressed as weekly renal Kt/V (RKt/V). TMAO concentrations were measured using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) at a centralized laboratory. All samples were analyzed in batches after completion of participant enrollment to minimize inter-assay variability. Laboratory personnel were blinded to clinical characteristics and outcome data. Details of the TMAO assay procedure, including sample preparation, chromatographic conditions, and validation parameters, are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was time to the first AV access thrombosis event. AV access thrombosis was defined as complete occlusion of AV access resulting in cessation of blood flow and requiring thrombectomy procedures or access abandonment. In clinical practice, complete access occlusion is typically accompanied by thrombus formation, although underlying stenosis frequently serves as a predisposing lesion. In this study, we did not differentiate whether the occlusive event was primarily triggered by progressive stenosis or acute thrombosis; all complete occlusions were adjudicated as thrombosis events for analysis.

Secondary outcomes included access stenosis, access failure, and death. Access failure was analyzed as a separate secondary outcome and was defined as permanent abandonment of access or the need for surgical revision. Access stenosis was defined as a lesion with >50% diameter stenosis anywhere within the access circuit with correlated evidence of dysfunction [14]. The study personnel ascertaining outcome events were blinded to the clinical and biochemical data of study participants

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the study participants were compared according to the tertile of serum TMAO concentrations using analysis of variance for normally distributed continuous variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Serum TMAO concentrations exhibited a left-skewed distribution; therefore, values were natural log–transformed [ln (TMAO)] for parametric analyses. Access patency was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the association between TMAO and outcomes, modeling TMAO as both a categorical variable (tertiles) and a continuous variable using ln (TMAO). The proportional hazards assumption was verified using log-minus-log survival plots and Schoenfeld residuals. Given the limited number of thrombosis events, we adopted a parsimonious multivariable adjustment strategy to minimize overfitting while accounting for clinically relevant confounders. The primary multivariable model was hypothesis-driven and included age, sex, dialysis vintage, access type (AVF vs. AVG), diabetes, CVD history, dialysis adequacy (Kt/V), prior access thrombosis, antiplatelet therapy, and serum albumin. These variables were selected based on clinical relevance and prior evidence rather than solely on univariable statistical significance. Variables identified in exploratory univariable analyses (e.g. systolic blood pressure, hemoglobin, BUN) were evaluated in sensitivity models. To assess nonlinearity, we applied restricted cubic spline (RCS) Cox models implemented using the rms package in R. Models with three and four knots were fitted, and knot locations were placed at default quantiles of the ln (TMAO) distribution. Hazard ratios were plotted across the observed range with the median as reference. To account for the high competing risk of death in maintenance hemodialysis, Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models treating death as a competing event were additionally performed. To evaluate the incremental predictive value of TMAO beyond established clinical predictors, we constructed a baseline model including access type (AVF vs. AVG), diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular disease. We then assessed model discrimination using Harrell’s C-statistics before and after the addition of ln (TMAO). The change in C-statistic (ΔC) was calculated. In addition, reclassification performance was evaluated using the integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) and continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI). Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine whether the association between high versus low TMAO tertiles and AV access thrombosis varied across major clinical subgroups, including diabetes, CAD, antiplatelet therapy, access type (AVF vs. AVG), and prior history of access thrombosis. Multiplicative interaction terms were introduced into Cox models to formally test effect modification. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P < .05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp.) for primary statistical analyses and R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with the rms package for RCS modeling.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

Between 25 March 2020 and 21 July 2022, a total of 420 patients were prospectively evaluated. Forty-five patients were excluded due to recent hospitalization, dialysis duration of less than 6 months, or a recent acute illness. The final study cohort comprised 375 patients with a mean age of 66 years. The median shunt age was 4.6 years [interquartile range (IQR), 1.0–6.6 years], and 74 patients (20%) had graft-based AV access. Baseline serum TMAO levels and associated factors

TMAO concentrations exhibited a wide range, from 2.0 to 1084.1 µM. When stratified by tertiles, the lowest tertile included patients with TMAO levels below 77.6 µM, while the highest tertile ranged from 164.5 to 1084.0 µM. Table 1 compares the demographic and clinical characteristics of participants across TMAO tertiles. No significant differences were observed in clinical, medication, access, or biochemical parameters, except for diastolic BP, shunt age, and BUN. When ln (TMAO) was analyzed as a continuous variable, it showed positive correlations with shunt age, BMI, history of hyperlipidemia, history of CAD, use of lipid-lowering agents, and BUN. In contrast, ln (TMAO) was negatively correlated with systolic SBP (Table S2).

Follow-up and events

The median follow-up duration was 24 months, consistent with the prespecified follow-up period. Early censoring occurred in 35 patients due to death (n = 27), transfer (n = 7), or kidney transplantation (n = 1). AV access thrombosis occurred in 88 patients (23%), and symptomatic stenosis was observed in 205 patients (55%). AV access was abandoned in 25 patients (7%). Patients who developed access thrombosis had higher median TMAO levels than those who did not. Nonetheless, the trend of difference remained when patients were stratified by access type (Fig. 1). Table 2 presents clinical and access-related events stratified by serum TMAO tertiles. Higher TMAO levels were associated with an increased incidence of thrombosis events but not with incident access stenosis, access failure, or all-cause mortality. Kaplan–Meier analysis (Fig. 2) demonstrated a graded association between AV access thrombosis and TMAO tertiles (high: 34%; middle: 23%; low: 13%; P < .001), whereas no significant association was observed between TMAO levels and stenosis events.

Univariable analyses

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis identified several factors associated with an increased risk of AV access thrombosis, including low systolic BP, comorbidities (CAD and stroke), AV graft use, low hemoglobin concentration, low BUN levels, and serum TMAO concentration [ln (TMAO) and TMAO tertiles] (Table 3). In unadjusted Cox regression analysis, patients in the highest TMAO tertile had a 3.1-fold higher risk of access thrombosis compared to those in the lowest tertile [unadjusted hazard ratio (HR), 3.10; 95% CI: 1.75–5.51; P < .001].

Given the potential competing risk of death in maintenance hemodialysis patients, we additionally performed Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models treating death as a competing event. The results were consistent with those from the Cox models. Higher TMAO levels remained significantly associated with an increased risk of thrombosis both as tertiles and as a continuous variable, with similar effect estimates and levels of statistical significance (Table 3).

Multivariable analyses

Multivariable analyses were performed using prespecified adjustment models. In the basic model (adjusted for age, sex, dialysis vintage, access type, and prior thrombosis), higher ln (TMAO) remained independently associated with thrombosis risk [adjusted HR (aHR), 1.53; 95% CI: 1.19–1.98; P = .001]. Patients with the highest TMAO tertile had approximately a three-fold increased risk compared with the lowest tertile. In the primary multivariable model (further adjusted for diabetes, cardiovascular disease, antiplatelet therapy, dialysis adequacy, and albumin), the association remained robust. The highest TMAO tertile was associated with a significantly increased thrombosis risk (aHR, 2.87; 95% CI: 1.60–5.17; P < .001), and ln (TMAO) remained independently associated (aHR, 1.52; 95% CI: 1.17–1.97; P = .002). Further adjustment for residual kidney function in the extended model did not materially alter the results (Table 4).

Fine–Gray competing-risk analyses yielded comparable findings across all multivariable models (Table 4). The association between higher TMAO levels and AV access thrombosis remained statistically significant, and the magnitude and direction of the subdistribution hazard ratios were similar to those obtained from the cause-specific Cox models. These findings indicate that the observed association was not materially influenced by the competing risk of death (Table 4).

In adjusted sensitivity analyses (Table S3), additional adjustment for systolic blood pressure, hemoglobin, or BUN did not materially alter the association between ln (TMAO) and thrombosis risk. Furthermore, exclusion of participants with prior thrombosis yielded consistent results. These findings support the robustness of the observed association.

Incremental predictive performance

We further evaluated whether TMAO improved risk discrimination beyond established clinical predictors. The baseline model including access type, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease demonstrated modest discrimination (C-statistic 0.581; 95% CI: 0.540–0.622). Addition of ln (TMAO) increased the C-statistic to 0.592 (95% CI: 0.551–0.634), yielding a ΔC of 0.011 (95% CI: −0.047 to 0.070; P = .051). Reclassification analyses showed an IDI of 0.008 (95% CI: −0.001 to 0.030; P = .124) and a continuous NRI of 0.051 (95% CI: −0.066–0.168; P = .244) (Table S4).

Dose–response relationship analysis

RCS modeling was performed to explore the functional form of the association between ln (TMAO) and AV access thrombosis. RCS analysis with three knots demonstrated a monotonic dose–response relationship between ln (TMAO) and thrombosis risk. While four-knot models showed minor local variations, these were likely attributable to random variation given the limited number of events (n = 88) and should be interpreted cautiously. Given the relatively small number of events and the principle of parsimony, the three-knot model was preferred as the primary representation of the dose-response relationship (Fig. 3). These findings support the use of ln (TMAO) as a continuous variable in our Cox proportional hazards models and are consistent with an approximate linear association across the observed range.

Subgroup analyses

Given the differing baseline thrombosis risks between AVF and AVG patients, access-type–stratified analyses were performed. In unadjusted analyses (Table S5 and Fig. S1), higher TMAO tertiles were associated with increased thrombosis risk in both AVF and AVG patients. Among AVF patients, those in the highest tertile had a threefold higher risk compared with the lowest tertile (unadjusted HR 3.24; 95% CI: 1.57–6.69; P < .01). A similar, though less precise, association was observed among AVG patients (unadjusted HR 2.65; 95% CI: 1.03–6.77; P = .04). In multivariable models adjusting for clinical covariates (Tablex S6), the association between ln (TMAO) and thrombosis remained significant in AVF patients. In AVG patients, effect estimates were directionally consistent but attenuated, likely reflecting the smaller sample size and wider confidence intervals.

To evaluate potential effect modification, additional subgroup analyses were performed according to diabetes, CAD, antiplatelet use, prior thrombosis, and access type (Fig. 4). The positive association between high TMAO and thrombosis was consistent across all subgroups except for CAD status and history of prior thrombosis. A significant interaction was observed for CAD status (P for interaction = .048). Among patients without CAD, the unadjusted HR for high versus low TMAO tertiles was 1.72 (95% CI: 0.86–3.43), whereas among those with CAD it was 10.56 (95% CI: 2.49–44.72). A significant interaction was also observed for prior thrombosis (P for interaction = .027), with stronger associations among patients with prior thrombosis (unadjusted HR 7.20; 95% CI: 1.65–31.41) compared with those without prior thrombosis (unadjusted HR 2.34; 95% CI: 1.23–4.47).

DISCUSSION

Main findings

In this multicenter prospective cohort study, we found that elevated baseline TMAO concentrations were independently associated with a substantially higher risk of subsequent AV access thrombosis. Importantly, this association was specific to thrombotic events and was not observed for access stenosis, access failure, or mortality. The dissociation between thrombosis and stenosis observed in our study suggests that TMAO may primarily reflect a systemic prothrombotic milieu rather than a driver of progressive anatomical narrowing of the access circuit. This association was robust across multivariable models, demonstrated a graded dose–response relationship, and was consistent across access types and most clinical subgroups.

TMAO concentration and its associates

A notable finding in our study is the wide interindividual variability in serum TMAO concentrations (with a minimum concentration of 2.0 µM to a maximum concentration of 1084.0 µM) among hemodialysis patients, like that reported in previous studies [8, 9, 11]. TMAO is generated from dietary precursors such as choline and L-carnitine, which are metabolized by intestinal microbes into trimethylamine and subsequently converted to TMAO in the liver [12]. Unlike protein-bound uremic toxins, TMAO is a small, water-soluble molecule (75 Da) that is freely filtered by the kidneys, likely to undergo both glomerular filtration and tubular secretion under normal renal function. However, in dialysis patients, TMAO elimination is primarily dependent on extracorporeal clearance [4]. Despite its small molecular size, our data indicates that TMAO levels were not associated with urea clearance, suggesting that standard hemodialysis is not the main determinant of the interindividual variability. This aligns with prior findings that hemodialysis only transiently lowers TMAO concentrations, which then rapidly rebound postdialysis, likely due to redistribution between intracellular and extracellular compartments [4]. Additionally, residual renal function may play a critical role in TMAO clearance. Patients with some remaining kidney functions likely retain partial tubular secretion capacity, contributing to lower circulating TMAO levels compared to anuric patients. The HEMO study previously demonstrated that increasing dialyzer urea clearance failed to improve survival, likely due to the inability of standard dialysis to efficiently remove non-urea uremic solutes [8]. Our findings reinforce this concept and suggest that alternative strategies may be required to optimize TMAO clearance and mitigate its potential cardiovascular effects.

Interestingly, we observed no significant correlation between TMAO and serum phosphate, suggesting that dietary phosphate intake may not be a major determinant of circulating TMAO levels in our population. However, TMAO was significantly associated with BUN, indicating a link between overall uremic burden and TMAO retention. Given that both TMAO and BUN are nitrogenous waste products, this association likely reflects reduced clearance rather than direct metabolic interplay. The relationship between TMAO and systolic BP warrants further investigation. In contrast to prior studies reporting a positive association between TMAO and hypertension, our data showed an inverse relationship between TMAO and systolic BP [15]. While the underlying mechanism remains unclear, this finding may be influenced by differences in volume status, antihypertensive medication use, or confounding by residual kidney function. Additionally, previous studies have linked TMAO to CAD and vascular dysfunction, suggesting a potential role in cardiovascular pathology among hemodialysis patients [5, 7, 12].

TMAO as a systemic contributor to access thrombosis

In our study, the cumulative risk of thrombosis at 2 years was 23%, with a notable difference between AVF (17%) and AVG (51%). These findings closely align with data from the United States Renal Data System (USRDS), where the 1-year thrombosis risk was 15% for AVF and 49% for AVG [16]. The mechanisms underlying AV access thrombosis involve complex interactions among blood stasis, vessel injury, and hypercoagulability. Traditionally, thrombosis prevention and management have focused on detecting and treating stenosis, as outflow vein narrowing is a well-established risk factor for thrombotic events [17]. However, our findings suggest that a prothrombotic uremic milieu, rather than stenosis alone, plays a central role in AV access thrombosis [18]. In the Kaplan–Meier analysis, patients with high TMAO levels had a significantly greater risk of thrombosis (34% vs. 13% in the low TMAO group, P < .001), but the difference in stenosis rates was less pronounced (59% vs. 47%, P = .048). This suggests that while TMAO may contribute to stenosis, its primary effect is the creation of a prothrombotic environment rather than the promotion of vascular narrowing. Our Cox regression analysis demonstrated that patients with high TMAO levels had approximately three-fold higher risk of thrombosis compared to those with low TMAO levels, while even those in the middle TMAO group had a 1.93-fold elevated risk. Notably, the impact of TMAO on thrombosis risk was more pronounced than that of traditional risk factors, underscoring the potential contribution of systemic metabolic disturbances in uremia as independent drivers of thrombosis beyond conventional vascular abnormalities. This finding aligns with previous studies reporting that uremia and its associated toxins contribute to a prothrombotic state, which may further exacerbate thrombotic complications in dialysis patients [18–20]. A recent cohort study from China also reported an independent association between higher serum TMAO levels and AV access dysfunction in hemodialysis patients [21]. However, that study included a relatively small sample (n = 80), applied a binary stratification of TMAO levels, and did not distinguish among specific types of access dysfunction such as thrombosis, stenosis, or access failure. In contrast, our multicenter prospective study extends these findings by focusing specifically on adjudicated AV access thrombosis as the primary outcome, demonstrating a graded dose–response relationship across TMAO tertiles and continuous modeling.

Possible mechanisms

The association between TMAO and AV access thrombosis may be explained by several potential mechanisms. First, TMAO has been identified as a prothrombotic metabolite in both clinical and laboratory studies, where elevated levels have been linked to increased platelet responsiveness, enhanced endothelial tissue factor activation, and a greater propensity for thrombotic events [10]. Experimental evidence demonstrates that TMAO promotes platelet activation, increases intracellular calcium release, and accelerates thrombosis in vivo [10]. Additionally, TMAO has been shown to impair endothelial function by inducing oxidative stress, upregulating vascular adhesion molecules, and reducing nitric oxide bioavailability, thereby creating a prothrombotic vascular environment [22–24]. Moreover, TMAO directly induces tissue factor expression in endothelial cells, further amplifying coagulation and increasing thrombosis risk [25]. Human studies support these findings as well, with epidemiological data linking high TMAO levels to increased risks of cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction and stroke [5–7].

Second, the increased stenosis rate in our high TMAO group suggests that TMAO may contribute to intimal hyperplasia, a pathological process characterized by vascular smooth muscle proliferation and endothelial dysfunction. Experimental studies have shown that TMAO promotes vascular inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial cell activation, all of which are implicated in the development of intimal hyperplasia [23, 26]. TMAO has been reported to upregulate vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 expression, enhance monocyte adhesion to endothelial cells, and reduce nitric oxide bioavailability, leading to endothelial dysfunction and increased vascular remodeling [24]. Additionally, TMAO has been linked to the proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells, which are key contributors to neointimal thickening and luminal narrowing [27]. Intimal hyperplasia is a well-recognized cause of AV access dysfunction, and its progression can lead to impaired blood flow and an elevated risk of thrombosis.

Third, although experimental evidence supports a prothrombotic role for TMAO, our observational findings do not establish relationship. It is possible that TMAO reflects an underlying pathological process that predisposes to thrombosis rather than acting as the primary driver itself. In our cohort, both higher TMAO levels and AV access thrombosis were associated with comorbid vascular disease, raising the possibility that a shared systemic disturbance—such as uremic toxin accumulation, endothelial dysfunction, or chronic inflammation—may underlie both phenomena. In this context, TMAO may function as a biomarker of a broader prothrombotic milieu rather than a direct causal mediator. Further studies are required to clarify whether TMAO exerts independent mechanistic effects or primarily reflects underlying thrombotic susceptibility in hemodialysis patients.

Other factors associated with thrombosis

Several clinical factors were associated with increased AV access thrombosis risk. A history of vascular disease (CAD or stroke) was correlated with both higher TMAO levels and thrombosis, suggesting it may confound their relationship. Lower systolic and diastolic BP, as well as reduced hemoglobin, were also linked to higher thrombosis risk—possibly reflecting compromised perfusion, oxygen delivery, or endothelial function. Interestingly, lower BUN levels were associated with thrombosis, potentially indicating malnutrition or reduced protein intake, both of which are linked to adverse vascular outcomes in dialysis patients [28]. These variables were included in the multivariable analysis to account for potential confounding. Despite these adjustments, including age, diabetes, vascular disease, hemoglobin, albumin, BUN, and residual kidney function, the association between TMAO and thrombosis remained statistically significant. This suggests an independent relationship, although residual confounding from unmeasured metabolic or vascular factors cannot be entirely excluded.

Subgroup findings and clinical context

The association between TMAO and thrombosis differed by access type. Among AVF patients, only those in the highest TMAO tertile had a significantly increased risk of thrombosis. In contrast, both middle and high TMAO tertiles were associated with increased risk among AVG patients. These differences may reflect the underlying pathophysiology: AVF thrombosis is often driven by progressive stenosis from neointimal hyperplasia, possibly requiring a higher systemic thrombotic burden, while AVG thrombosis is more sensitive to acute thrombotic triggers and hemodynamic stress [29]. Additionally, AVG patients typically have a higher baseline risk due to vascular disease and systemic inflammation, which may amplify TMAO’s effects. The smaller number of AVG patients (n = 74) may have also limited statistical precision in subgroup analyses.

In prespecified subgroup analyses, the association between TMAO and thrombosis was broadly consistent across clinically relevant strata. Significant interactions were observed for CAD and prior access thrombosis, with stronger associations in these high-risk groups. Because both CAD and prior thrombosis are linked to antiplatelet therapy, we examined whether differential antiplatelet use might account for these findings. Antiplatelet therapy was more frequent among patients with CAD (61.5% vs. 24.0%) and among those with prior thrombosis (47.8% vs. 31.4%). However, the association between TMAO and thrombosis remained significantly irrespective of antiplatelet use, and no interaction was observed by antiplatelet therapy. These findings suggest that the amplified associations in CAD and prior thrombosis subgroups are unlikely to be explained solely by pharmacologic differences and may reflect heightened thrombotic vulnerability in patients with advanced vascular disease. Given the limited subgroup sample sizes and wide confidence intervals, these interactions should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its observational design precludes causal inference, and residual confounding by unmeasured factors—such as diet, gut microbiota composition, inflammation, or platelet function—cannot be excluded. Second, TMAO was measured at a single baseline time point, which may not capture temporal variability. However, given the markedly reduced renal clearance in hemodialysis patients, circulating TMAO levels are persistently elevated, and nondifferential misclassification would likely bias results toward the null. Future studies incorporating repeated TMAO measurements are needed to better define exposure trajectories and their relationship with AV access outcomes. Third, secondary outcomes had fewer events, limiting power to detect modest associations. Fourth, we were unable to adjust for certain potentially relevant factors, including inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein and episodes of intradialytic hypotension, as these data were not systematically collected in this cohort. Residual confounding from unmeasured inflammatory or hemodynamic factors therefore cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, the study population was derived from Taiwanese dialysis centers, which may limit generalizability to other ethnic or dietary contexts. Although this multicenter design enhances generalizability, the moderate sample size and number of events may limit precision in subgroup analyses and warrant confirmation in larger cohorts.

Clinical implications and future directions

Elevated TMAO was independently associated with AV access thrombosis; however, the incremental improvement in predictive discrimination beyond established clinical variables was modest. While TMAO may help identify patients with increased thrombotic susceptibility, its role as a standalone clinical prediction tool appears limited. Nonetheless, these findings suggest that TMAO may reflect an underlying prothrombotic milieu relevant to AV access outcomes and warrant further mechanistic investigation. While these findings raise interest in targeting TMAO-related pathways—through dietary modification, gut microbiota–directed approaches, or other metabolic interventions—causal inference cannot be established from observational data alone. Future studies incorporating repeated TMAO measurements, intermediate thrombotic phenotypes (such as platelet activation or coagulation markers), and interventional or genetic approaches will be necessary to determine whether TMAO is a modifiable mediator of AV access thrombosis or a marker of broader uremic thrombotic risk [12].

CONCLUSION

In summary, this multicenter prospective cohort study demonstrates that higher serum TMAO levels are independently associated with an increased risk of AV access thrombosis in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. These findings identify TMAO as a novel biomarker associated with thrombotic risk in dialysis AV access. Future studies incorporating repeated TMAO measurements, intermediate thrombotic phenotypes, and interventional or genetic approaches are warranted to determine whether TMAO represents a modifiable mediator or a marker of broader uremic thrombotic susceptibility.
