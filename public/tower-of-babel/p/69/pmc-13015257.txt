Lipochaperoning folding of water-soluble functional membrane protein in genetically and biosynthetically tuned Escherichia coli
Authors: Nataliia Bogdanova, Sergey Ryabichko, Lei Zheng, Mikhail Bogdanov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHydrophobic membrane proteins are widely believed to require lipid bilayers for proper folding, collapsing or aggregating in aqueous environments. Using lactose permease (LacY) as a model membrane protein, we show instead that folding in vivo can be optimized for unusual specific molecular interactions with particular phospholipids during functional folding and final localization. Co-overproducing LacY with lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), a normally short-lived intermediate in phospholipid biosynthesis, unexpectedly yields a water-soluble, native LacY in the cytoplasm. This approach obviates SIMPLEx and QTY protein redesign strategies, enabling soluble production of unmodified membrane proteins in vivo. Although surplus LacY spills from the cytoplasmic leaflet together with LPA into cytoplasm, LPA-driven activation of only principal ClsA and buildup of cardiolipin (CL) ensure the SecYEG translocon capacity for proper membrane insertion of LacY. These findings challenge current models of membrane protein biogenesis and provide new opportunities to study membrane proteins in both membrane and aqueous contexts.

Introduction

Membrane proteins constitute a substantial portion of the cellular proteome and are essential to diverse biological processes, including signal transduction, molecular transport, and energy production and conversion. Although membrane proteins account for nearly 30% of all predicted open reading frames and more than half of current therapeutic targets1 they represent only ∼1% of the total cellular protein mass. Their intrinsic hydrophobicity and strong tendency to aggregate in aqueous environments have long hindered structural and functional studies. Despite major advances in X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy, and NMR spectroscopy, producing and purifying stable, functional membrane proteins remains one of the most formidable challenges in structural biology.

Current dogma holds that integral membrane proteins must be embedded within a lipid bilayer to fold correctly and remain stable; consequently, “soluble” forms of such proteins are often assumed to be non-natural or misfolded. These challenges are particularly acute in Escherichia coli, one of the most widely used hosts for membrane-protein production. Because its cytoplasm lacks native membrane structures, membrane proteins commonly misfold or form insoluble inclusion bodies when expression exceeds the capacity of the membrane insertion machinery. In E. coli, overexpression frequently overwhelms the SecYEG translocon and inner membrane (IM) capacity, causing formation of loosely membrane-associated, partially folded species. A variety of synthetic biology and protein engineering strategies have been developed to mitigate misfolding and toxicity during expression in the E. coli IM. Controlled expression systems, co-expressed molecular chaperones, and lipid modifying interventions have all improved yields of functional membrane proteins, although none is universally applicable. However, methods enabling in vivo production of membrane proteins in a detergent-free, soluble form remain extremely limited.2 One approach uses solubility-enhancing fusion tags3 for instance, a fusion of the small membrane protein EmrE to a truncated apolipoprotein A-I (ApoAI) yields a water-soluble complex.4 Yet the tag significantly perturbed EmrE’s folding, ligand binding, conformational landscape and antibiotic-resistance profile.5 Alternatively, membrane proteins can be redesigned by substituting lipid-facing hydrophobic residues with hydrophilic ones, exemplified by the “QTY code” (specific amino acid substitutions, Q for glutamine, T for threonine, and Y for tyrosine), which has generated water-soluble variants of several proteins that retain overall structure and ligand binding.6,7 Nevertheless, this strategy requires extensive protein-specific substitutions: too few fail to confer solubility, while too many destabilize structure or function, limiting its general applicability.

Unexpectedly, multiple studies have suggested that non-integrated, non-membrane-embedded forms of α-helical membrane proteins can arise naturally under conditions of overexpression. When the SecYEG translocon or membrane capacity becomes saturated, excess polypeptides fail to integrate and instead accumulate as loosely membrane-associated or partially folded species. Although these proteins do not pass the classical “100,000 × g solubility test,” they can be stripped from membranes with urea, alkali, or sonication8 and, upon urea removal, some (e.g., LacY and melibiose permease) remain in soluble monomeric or oligomeric forms.9,10,11 Whether such species occur in vivo or represent artifacts of overexpression and membrane saturation has remained unclear. Importantly, conformation-specific antibodies recognize only the tightly membrane-integrated monomeric forms, indicating that the loosely membrane-associated and soluble aggregates adopt structures incompatible with native membrane insertion.11,12

Proper membrane insertion and folding are complex processes involving specific cellular machinery, such as the signal recognition particle (SRP) system and the SecYEG translocon, which normally direct the protein to the IM.13,14 When expression is forced into the cytoplasm, this pathway is bypassed, typically resulting in misfolding. In principle, however, if hydrophobic transmembrane segments are transiently shielded by endogenous or engineered “lipochaperones,” properly folded, soluble membrane proteins might be achievable in vivo. We hypothesized that elevated levels of native detergent-like lysophospholipids could sequester the hydrophobic α-helices of membrane proteins and stabilize their extramembrane domains. Under such conditions, overexpressed membrane proteins might “spill over” from the membrane-insertion pathway and remain soluble yet native in the cytoplasm.

To test this hypothesis, we overexpressed LacY, a model system widely used to study membrane-protein folding, in an E. coli strain engineered to overproduce lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), a native phospholipid precursor, while using rifampicin to suppress chromosomal gene expression. Remarkably, under these conditions, LacY, a hydrophobic membrane transporter that normally requires detergent for solubilization, accumulated in the cytoplasm in a water-soluble form. Upon reconstitution into liposomes, this soluble LacY structurally and functionally complemented permease located in the cytoplasmic membrane.

These findings demonstrate that hydrophobic transmembrane proteins can adopt non-denatured, functional conformation in complex with endogenous lipochaperones. Thus, membrane proteins can fold correctly and remain soluble in aqueous environments when transiently exposed to an appropriate lipid environment that becomes saturated, releasing excess protein in a native state. This simple, detergent-free strategy enables cytoplasmic production of integral membrane proteins without large fusion tags, extensive sequence redesign, traditional detergents, amphipols, nanodiscs, or peptidiscs and may provide a general route for rapid preparation of membrane proteins for structural and functional studies.

Results

Experimental rationale

Overproduction of membrane proteins in the IM rather than as misfolded aggregates in inclusion bodies is generally preferred, but often limited by saturation of the translocation machinery and insufficient membrane space to accommodate newly synthesized proteins, ultimately resulting in low yields. Nevertheless, several strategies have been documented for achieving soluble expression of integral membrane proteins in the E. coli cytoplasm, although these successes have only been realized through careful protein engineering. However, in the case of strategically modified membrane proteins, the challenge is even greater; not only are they difficult to produce, but maintaining them simultaneously in a soluble and functionally active state is also problematic. While cytoplasmic production of “soluble” full-length native integral membrane proteins in E. coli would be highly valuable for many applications, it has yet to be convincingly demonstrated. This underscores the need to develop robust expression systems capable of producing α-helical membrane proteins in soluble form, despite their intrinsic incompatibility with the aqueous, detergent-free environment of the E. coli cytoplasm due to their structural characteristics.

In this manuscript, we explore the possibility that a newly synthesized protein may “hijack” detergent-like lysoglycerophospholipids and “spill” them off the IM into the cytoplasm. By orchestrating the assembly of lysophospholipid around the hydrophobic transmembrane domains the hijacked protein could be surrounded by a protective, detergent-like coat. This mechanism would prevent aggregation and allow membrane proteins to remain soluble in the cytoplasm, even at high concentrations. The phenomenon of lysoglycerophospholipids such as lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE) acting as molecular chaperones for soluble proteins has been documented through in vitro studies.15 LPE exhibits chaperone-like properties by assisting in the folding of bona fide hydrophilic (water-soluble) cytoplasmic proteins and preventing their aggregation. Its ability to support protein folding is attributed to its unique amphipathic structure, which resembles that of a detergent and enables interactions with both polar and nonpolar regions of unfolded proteins. LPE has been shown to promote the functional refolding of chemically denatured soluble proteins such as citrate synthase and α-glucosidase, substrates typically refolded by classical molecular chaperones like GroEL, DnaK, Hsp90, and small heat shock proteins (sHsps). However, this mechanism is distinct from the role of its parent phospholipid, phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), which can assist in the folding of certain polytopic membrane proteins, such as lactose permease. In that context, PE acts as a lipochaperone supporting folding through a membrane insertion-dependent mechanism.16,17,18,19,20 Although LPA, like LPE, is best known for its widespread signaling and structural functions,21 it can also exhibit the capacity to promote protein folding, suggesting its previously underappreciated chaperone-like role. However, this capacity has never been demonstrated despite its very attractive structural features (unique conical shape with a large, positively curved head group) and hydrophobic tail, and the demonstrated ability to act as a “folding effector” creating a more energetically favorable environment for membrane protein.22 “Lipochaperoning” term is appropriate and reflects an established functional concept. Lipochaperoning refers to the ability of specific lipid molecules to assist protein folding and prevent aggregation, analogous to protein-based molecular chaperones. Lipochaperones can transiently bind to non-native proteins, reducing the likelihood of misfolding or harmful aggregation. Similarly, lysophospholipids such as LPA function like molecular chaperones by “masking” hydrophobic regions of newly synthesized proteins. Traditionally, molecular chaperones are proteins that bind non-covalently to folding intermediates, assisting productive folding without remaining bound to the mature protein. In our experiments, phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) satisfies these functional criteria: it interacts non-covalently with nascent LacY, facilitates proper folding, and is no longer required once folding is complete. Following the functional definition proposed by John Ellis,23 the classification of a molecule as a molecular chaperone depends solely on its function, not its chemical identity. The core idea of our and John Ellis’s arguments is that the classification of a molecule as a “molecular chaperone” should depend solely on its function and action (assisting protein folding non-covalently), not its chemical structure (i.e., it does not have to be a protein). Thus, lipids that fulfill this role can be termed “lipochaperones,” distinguishing their function from merely providing a solvent environment.

The positional asymmetry of fatty acids in bacterial glycerophospholipids is determined by a universally conserved two-step pathway involving the sequential acylation of sn-glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P) to form the central intermediate phosphatidic acid (PA). The first acylation step, catalyzed by G3P acyltransferase (GPAT), known as PlsB in bacteria, produces 1-acyl-glycero-3-phosphate (LPA). We decided to take advantage of Jack Coleman’s strain SM2-1, a temperature-sensitive E. coli mutant with a defective plsC gene. This conditionally lethal E. coli mutant can grow at a permissive temperature (e.g., 30°C) but not at a non-permissive temperature (e.g., 42°C) because the essential plsC gene is required for membrane lipid biosynthesis and essential for cell survival.24 PA is a crucial intermediate for creating all other membrane phospholipids; however, its acylation is prevented in SM2-1, defective in plsC. A temperature-sensitive mutation in the plsC gene should cause a mutant E. coli strain to accumulate the precursor molecule, 1-acyl-sn-glycerol-3-phosphate (LPA), at a non-permissive temperature (e.g., 42°C). Lysophospholipids are formed from normal phospholipids in the E. coli envelope through reactions such as PldA-mediated diacylation, as a product of PagP-mediated transfer of the sn-1 palmitate residue from glycerophospholipids to lipid A or lipoprotein acyltransferase (Lnt)-catalyzed transacylation.21 However normally E. coli does not accumulate lysophospholipids because they are promptly repaired by the LplT/Aas system, where LplT flips them across the IM, and Aas acylates them back to diacyl forms.25,26,27 This phospholipid repair system is crucial because lysophospholipids are detergent-like molecules that can severely disrupt membrane structure and integrity, making their rapid removal and recycling essential for bacterial viability. However, we demonstrated that reacylation of LPA cannot be carried out by the E. coli LplT transport system.25,28 The interference of this system with a critical intermediate step in the de novo biosynthesis of membrane glycerophospholipids was possibly avoided during evolution. This hypothesis explains why SM2-1 strain can accumulate LPA after a shift to non-permissive temperature. Thus, the generation of LplT/Aas null mutant in the plsC background for hyperproduction of LPA in E. coli was not required.

To overload SecYEG with newly synthesized LacY, the expression of LacY from the T7 promoter was combined with the ability of rifampicin to inhibit selectively the host RNA polymerase. This combination permits the exclusive expression of a gene from a plasmid under the control of a T7 RNA polymerase promoter. In this strain, expression of the gene encoding the target is driven by the bacteriophage T7 RNA polymerase (T7 RNAP), which transcribes much faster than E. coli RNAP.29 The expression system consists of two ori-compatible plasmids, pGP1-2 and pT7-5-LacY. pGP1-2 drives the expression of T7RNA polymerase. It consists of the gene of phage T7 polymerase under the control of the inducible λ PL promoter and the gene for the heat-sensitive repressor λ. LacY is under the control of the T7 promoter and expressed from the pT7-5 plasmid. LacY can constitute up to 30% of the IM E. coli proteome (Bogdanov, unpublished).

PlsC– mutant hyperexpressing LacY accumulates abnormally high level of LPA and anionic glycerophospholipids PG and CL on expense of PE

The logarithmically grown SM2-1 cells harboring pGP1-2 and pT7-5-LacY plasmids were incubated at 42°C for 20 min to inactivate temperature-sensitive λ repressor first, then rifampicin (100 μg/mL) was added to inhibit host RNA polymerase, then the cells were outgrown for additional 10 min at 42°C and followed by additional growth for 60 min at 37°C. Phospholipids were extracted and separated as described under STAR Methods. Growth under these conditions resulted in a sharp increase in total LPA content accompanied by a corresponding decrease in the level of PE and accumulation of PG and CL (Figure 1B). LPA levels in plsC– cells typically account for 18%–20% of total lipids and can occasionally reach up to 30%. The fact that the cells are still viable after the shift to the nonpermissive temperature is unexpected because at this point 20% of the total phospholipids present are detergents like LPA. The cells compensate for the having high level of LPA and hyperproduction of LacY by a change in the composition of other phospholipids synthesized, particularly PG and CL. In bacteria, lysoglycerophosphoilipids are typically present only in trace amounts, functioning as intermediates in phospholipid biosynthesis and turnover. However, abnormally high levels of the detergent-like lysolipid LPE up to 30% have been observed quite often in Gram-negative bacteria, and are therefore not without precedent.21 In E. coli, PlsC primarily incorporates long, unsaturated fatty acids (such as cis-vaccenic fatty acid) into the sn-2 position of the phospholipid backbone, thus accumulated LPA is consistently decorated by saturated fatty acid.

Co-overproduction of LPA and LacY leads to the appearance of a unique soluble and properly folded form of permease in the cytoplasm

Overexpression of LacY at a high rate via the T7 RNA polymerase system yields unexpected results in an E. coli strain with an abnormally high level of LPA. When cytoplasm and membrane fractions were isolated from E. coli plsC– cell cultures and analyzed by western blotting with polyclonal antibodies (pAb) (Figure 2A), an immunoreactive form of this permease but not endogenously expressed outer membrane protein OmpF (Figure 2B) was detected in the cytoplasm of these cells but not detected at all in the cytoplasm of plsC+ counterpart (Figure 2A). To demonstrate and quantitate the distribution of LacY between the IM and cytoplasmic fractions, two separate gels were run (Figures 2A and 2C) with different loading methods based on either total protein amount or volume of fractions. The calculation (as described in STAR Methods) demonstrates that more than 80% of expressed LacY is localized in the cytoplasm, while a smaller portion remains in the IM (Figure 2C). Based on past success in utilizing conformation-specific monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) to study proper LacY folding,12 we employed antibodies directed against distinct epitopes of native LacY. mAb4B1 inhibits all reactions involving net H translocation (active transport) during LacY function30,31 and recognizes a properly folded continuous epitope formed by extramembrane periplasmic domain P7.16,30,31 The retention of LacY recognition by mAb 4B1 and the uphill transport function of LacY are tightly coupled. The recognition of the lactose permease by mAb4B1 is tightly coupled to the protein’s uphill transport function because the antibody binds to and inhibits a specific conformational state of LacY that is essential for active transport.30,31,32,33 Remarkably, all soluble LacY molecules contain the native continuous epitope, which is efficiently recognized by conformation-specific 4B1 antibodies. As shown in Figure 2D, mAb4B1 recognized the soluble 33 kDa monomer of LacY, indicating that the soluble form of permease is properly folded with respect to functionally and structurally important 4B1 epitope. “Less is better” or “the Winner takes it all”: The high level of LPA and CL ensures the capacity of the SecYEG translocon to integrate the produced proteins properly in the membrane

For E. coli, it has been shown that the production of both homologous and heterologous membrane proteins can lead to the saturation of the SecYEG translocon capacity, which negatively affects the fitness of the E. coli cell as well as membrane protein production yields due to improper folding or exceeding the functional capabilities of the translocon34 (34). A pAbs can detect all (folded properly and misfolded) forms of LacY.11,12 Correspondingly we detected very high amount of permease in the IM fraction (Figure 3A) from plsC+ cells; however, mAb4B1 recognized near similar amounts of the 33 kDa monomer of LacY in plsC+ and plsC– (Figure 3A), indicating that all LacY inserted in membrane with elevated level of LPA, PG and CL and contains the native continuous epitope efficiently recognized by conformation-specific antibodies antibody. In plsC+ cells, the majority of LacY is misfolded and and aggregated (Figure 3B) The partially degraded form was surprisingly recognized by mAb4B1 Abs (Figure 3A). Near all LacY seems to be properly folded within the membrane of plsC–cells when LacY and LPA, PG, and CL are co-synthesized. These findings are consistent with hypotheses that SecYEG-translocon capacity is hardly saturated in these cells, leading to enhanced properly folded membrane protein production yields in the IM. A high level of LPA and CL/PG somehow ensures the capacity of the Sec-translocon to integrate the produced proteins properly in the membrane. LPA activates ClsA in vitro

Among three redundant CL synthases in E. coli, ClsA and ClsB utilize two PG molecules for CL synthesis; however, ClsC uses phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) as a second molecule with PG to form CL.35,36 To investigate the mode of elevated CL synthesis and to verify whether elevated level of LPA is responsible for high level of CL and low level of PE in cells hyperproducing LPA and LacY, we utilized a BKT29 ΔpgsA, ΔclsABC strain lacking both PG and CL but expressing one Cls at the time. The CL synthetic capability of the membrane fraction was determined using purified radioactive 32PG and isolated IM fraction from BKT29. As shown in Figure 4, LPA surprisingly activates only principal E. coli cardiolipin synthase ClsA. Soluble lactose permease is monomeric

When the soluble lactose permease was chromatographed on Superdex 200, LacY was eluted as one peak. The observed molecular weight of the peak was in good agreement with the expected masses for the monomer (33.4 kDa) (Figure 5A). As shown on this Figure soluble LacY elutes as distorted peak centered at 29–33 kDa within the column’s fractionation range. It is well known that this protein is in equilibrium between a monomer and a dimer, which are resolved very often even by SDS-PAGE because LacY dimerization is highly stable against the denaturing conditions.37 LacY monomers and dimers are present simultaneously, the equilibrium is dynamic, meaning the proteins are constantly associating and dissociating, and both the monomeric and dimeric species will be present in the sample loaded onto the gel or gel filtration column. This can result in a distorted peak that is not perfectly symmetrical or even asymmetrical during purification, particularly with size-exclusion chromatography, because the equilibrium is disrupted during the separation process. Similar results were obtained with chromatography on Sephacryl SF-200 (Bio-Rad), suggesting the results are not due to interaction with the gel matrix (data not shown). Furthermore, chromatography on Superdex 200 results in purification of the soluble permease (Figure 5B). Purified soluble lactose permease catalyzes membrane potential driven lactose uptake after reconstitution into proteoliposomes

The ability to take this soluble, folded membrane protein and reconstitute it into functional proteoliposomes is a critical step to validate our assumptions. If successful, this process will confirm that the protein is not denatured and disabled but correctly folded and functional, ready to be reintegrated into a lipid bilayer. When purified LacY in 50 mM KPi (pH 7.5) was mixed with E. coli total phospholipids and proteoliposomes prepared in KPi (pH 7.5) were diluted into equimolar NaPi (pH 7.5) in the presence of valinomycin so that a membrane potential (interior negative) is generated, accumulation of [14C] lactose was observed (Figure 6A) (solid circle symbols). Lactose accumulates at an initial rate and to a steady-state level comparable to that of proteoliposomes reconstituted with an equivalent amount of permease purified from the membrane.37 However, when the proteoliposomes are diluted into equimolar KPi (pH 7.5) so that no ΔΨ is generated, or nigericin is added during dilution, lactose accumulation is negligible (Figure 6B, solid square symbols). The addition of nigericin resulted in the inward diffusion of potassium in exchange for a proton, dissipation of the membrane potential (high buffering capacity prevents ΔpH formation). These experiments further demonstrated that these soluble proteins could be reconstituted into proteoliposomes and remain functional. These results supported the “heretical notion” that certain very hydrophobic membrane proteins could retain their native, functional conformation in an aqueous solution if overproduced along with a detergent-like molecule, LPA. Highly purified soluble LacY appears to be exclusively lipidated with LPA. Discussion

Escherichia coli remains one of the most popular and widely used bacterial hosts for the production and purification of membrane proteins for biochemical and structural analyses. Most prokaryotic membrane protein structures found in the Protein DataBank (PDB) have been obtained after production of the corresponding protein in E. coli.38 Historically, studying hydrophobic integral membrane proteins outside their natural membrane environment has been extremely challenging. The highly hydrophobic nature of membrane proteins leads to challenges in producing a homogeneous, properly folded, stable, and pure sample for structural studies. Circumventing these expression problems will be possible through the introduction of principally novel protein production protocols.

The results presented here demonstrate that LacY, a hydrophobic transmembrane protein that normally requires detergent for solubilization, can be overproduced and purified in soluble properly folded form from the cytoplasm of E. coli cells synthesizing high amounts of detergent-like lysophospholipid LPA. These results support a provocative conclusion that hydrophobic membrane proteins can adopt a native-like state in a detergent-free solution. This conclusion definitely departs from the established view and may prompt a reevaluation of current folding models. Importantly, soluble permease behaves as a monomer during size-exclusion chromatography (Figure 5). This unexpected form of permease is different from previously described, urea-soluble LacY, which can be dissociated from the IM with 5.0 M urea and which appears after the IM is saturated with newly synthesized permease.9,11,12 When LacY is highly overproduced, it was found integrated into the membrane as a monomer as well as both in the cytoplasm and in association with the membrane in a non-integrated form as dimers and trimers. Although monomeric, dimeric, and trimeric forms of LacY were found in the membrane fraction, only dimeric and trimeric forms were stripped by urea or isolated from the cytoplasm. However, the conformation-specific mAbs 4B1 and 4B11 recognized only the tightly membrane-associated 33 kDa monomer of LacY, but not dimers and trimers which were failed to present the specific native continuous and discontinuous epitope structures recognized by these antibodies.12

This soluble form of permease isolated from cells hyperexpressing LPA (Figures 1 and 2) is also different from previously described membrane proteins which upon overproduction can induce the proliferation of intracellular bona-fide bilayer membranes (ICMs) within the cell (reviewed by Royes et al.39). Interestingly, certain overproduced integral membrane proteins can “hijack” membrane biogenesis pathways in prokaryotic cells, leading to the formation of intracellular membranes or vesicles enriched in cone-shaped phospholipids. The large-scale overproduction of few endogenous integral membrane proteins, i.e., the fumarate reductase, ATP synthase or its b-subunit, the mannitol permease MtlA, sn-glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase PlsB, chemotaxis receptor Tsr induce the formation of membrane stacks or tubules or bilayer vesicles in the cytoplasm, close to and continuous with the inner (cytoplasmic) membrane.8,39,40 Such inducible ICMs housing newly synthesized membrane proteins can provide more than an order of magnitude larger membrane surface area than E. coli IM. E. coli PS decarboxylase (Psd)41 and heterologously expressed Acholeplasma laidlawii monoglucosyldiacylglycerol synthase42 have been reported to exist as non-integrated after overproduction. Non-integrated forms of PTS Enzymes II were also identified.43 However, these forms of integral membrane proteins are very often different from their membrane-integrated counterparts with respect to kinetics and various physical characteristics.44 Whether ICMs function as “emergency membrane protein biosynthetic” platforms or represent “storage” forms of these proteins, providing a reservoir of membrane proteins that can be recruited back to the cytoplasmic membrane to allow rapid increases in transport or biosynthetic activities is still unknown.

There is a complex relationship between membrane protein production and an imbalance of phospholipids that can trigger significant biological changes. Bacterial host C43(DE3) over-expressing ATPase subunit reacted simply to the overproduction of a membrane protein by converting PG into CL,45 most likely to maintain the new lipid/protein ratio. It is not clear yet whether alteration in the IM phospholipid balance needs to be compensated by the biosynthesis of the complementary type of phospholipids, either via enzymatic and/or genetic regulation, and how specific phospholipids are recruited by membrane proteins. The enrichment in CL through activation of ClsA by LPA (Figure 4) most likely reflects an alteration that promotes the recruitment of the desired phospholipid to the translocon. It is possible that the constant overexpression of membrane proteins continuously alters phospholipid homeostasis and pushes E.coli to produce and present a desired phospholipid CL to the SecYEG translocon to prevent its jamming by overproduced membrane protein. The enrichment for specific phospholipids CL could also be explained by the generation of special membrane microdomains in the vicinity of the translocon during the de novo biosynthesis of phospholipids, with impaired accessibility of LPA to the LplT/Aas regenerating system and increased accessibility to SecYEG translocon. CL contributes to the efficiency, turnover, proton-motive force (PMF) stimulation, and spatial organization of the bacterial SecYEG-SecA translocon through specific lipid-protein molecular interactions and a multifaceted mechanism that ensures proper docking and optimal performance of the motor protein.13,14,46,47,48,49 CL enhances the SecYEG translocon’s efficiency by stabilizing its “back-to-back” dimeric configuration, which creates a high-affinity binding site for SecA, and by directly stimulating SecA’s ATPase activity. When bound to the membrane, the SecYEG dimer, stabilized by CL, provides a robust platform for SecA binding. This interaction is significantly stronger (30-fold increase in affinity) and more productive than the interaction with a monomeric SecYEG and occurs even the absence of nucleotide, thereby enabling a productive interaction to occur before the energetic phase of translocation begins.13,14 This ensures that the complex is primed to accept a preprotein substrate. This conformational priming and intermolecular coupling are strictly dependent on CL and lead to more frequent and stable interactions between the motor protein Sec A and the SecYEG protein-conducting channel, thereby indirectly accelerating the translocation process. CL plays a crucial role in coupling ATP hydrolysis to translocation by specifically relieving the allosteric inhibition of SecA’s ATPase activity by magnesium ions, an inhibition that prevents wasteful ATP consumption when A is unbound in the cytoplasm. The interaction between the positively charged N-terminus of SecA and anionic lipids such as CL induces a conformational change in A, priming it for high-affinity binding to SecYEG and enhancing its catalytic activity.47 CL also contributes to the spatial organization of the export machinery. It helps localize the SecYEG translocon to specific membrane microdomains, forming specialized “export factories” that concentrate the translocation machinery and enable faster processing of multiple substrates. The co-localization of SecYEG and motor protein SecA in these lipid-rich domains increases local reaction rates and enhances the overall efficiency of protein export and membrane protein insertion. Fluorescent tagging experiments further support this model: in CL-deficient mutants, SecYEG distribution shifts from a characteristic spiral pattern to a more scattered localization, which is associated with reduced export efficiency.48 Given that E. coli cells are rod-shaped with distinct curvature at the poles, cell poles regions characterized by high negative membrane curvature, and that several proteins show polar enrichment, remarkably, among several polar proteins, only ClsA exhibited strict CL-dependent localization.49 Whether LPA is a small, conical-shaped phospholipid with a large, positively curved head group antagonizing a scattered localization by creating local membrane stress and forcing ClsA to localize with SecYEG into spiral-like structures48 is unknown; however, it represents a very interesting spatial perspective.

Since the translocon is neither jammed nor overloaded (Figure 3), it likely balances the yield of properly inserted LacY in the inner membrane with the release of surplus LacY into the cytoplasm. Alternatively, the amount of properly inserted LacY could be modulated by changes in the IM’s capacity to accommodate LacY, for example through alterations in lipid packing (Figure 8). We have demonstrated that CL specifically contributes to a more loosely packed organization of the IM.50,51 Using CL, a relatively less abundant membrane lipid, allows the cell to fine-tune bilayer physical properties efficiently as it can be more easily regulated.Figure 8Intracellular logistics of newly synthesized LacYLacY is able to adopt water-soluble conformations after “spilling off” the cytoplasmic leaflet of the membrane together with LPA. Interestingly, LPE promotes protein refolding in vitro at concentrations lower than its CMC.15 LPA is a simple glycerophospholipid consisting of a glycerol backbone, a single fatty acyl chain, and a negatively charged phosphate head group. As an amphipathic molecule with one hydrocarbon chain, LPA acts like a detergent in an aqueous solution. Micelle formation is characterized by the CMC which value was reported to be ∼25–50 μM for LPA by pyrene-based method which is also in good agreement with the value determined previously by microcalorimetry.52 We don’t know yet whether the in vivo concentration of LPA required for the maintenance of the soluble form of LacY coincides with its critical micelle concentration (CMC). Thus, LacY might interact with individual monomeric detergent-like LPA molecules rather than with an amount of LPA equivalent to a whole micelle and form a lipid-protein complex (Figure 8, inset). If the concentration of LPA required for maintenance of LacY in a soluble state coincides with its CMC, it might interact with an amount of this lipid equivalent to a whole micelle rather than with individual monomeric LPA molecules. Although most of LPA molecules are probably localized in the membrane, the micromolecular concentrations are probably present in the cytoplasm. There are probably several hydrophobic sites on the “metastable” membrane protein that need to be transiently or permanently bound to LPA after exit from the translocon. The unique ability of LPA to effectively drive folding of disordered peptides was recently described. The interaction with associated LPA resulted in the gain of ordered secondary structure elements with an affinity in the low micromolar range53 could be important for stabilizing the intrinsically disordered (ID) large cytoplasmic loop of LacY. LPA-ID peptide complex is formed under crowding conditions reminiscent of E. coli cytoplasm, which is inherently a crowded environment, with macromolecules occupying up to 30% of its volume, influencing molecular interactions. For E. coli, it is around 300 mg/mL, which corresponds to a volume fraction of 25%–30%.54

According to our hypothesis, either “micellar” or “soluble” non-integrated forms of the membrane protein may be generated during membrane biogenesis through a relatively simple putative mechanism. An imbalance in the biosynthesis of lipid and protein, along with the “hijacking” of LPA during the membrane integration process, may facilitate the formation of a complex with individual lipid molecules and stabilize an α-helical bundle of the membrane protein in a soluble-like state, which can be “spilled off” the cytoplasmic leaflet of IM. Our results lay the foundation for a new lipid-protein membrane homeostasis paradigm and support the development of alternative expression systems by re-routing the lipid and membrane protein biosynthetic pathway to the bacterial cytoplasm. This strategy aims to reduce protein misfolding and the formation of inclusion bodies. While previous efforts to enhance functional overexpression of membrane proteins have primarily focused on modulating expression levels through promoter engineering, transcriptional/post-transcriptional tuning, or fusion protein design, we propose an innovative approach centered on enhancing E. coli’s capacity to produce lysophospholipids in substantial amounts. Our experimental data provide a broadly applicable strategy to improve the expression of soluble forms of membrane proteins within the cytoplasm. Under conditions of co-hyperproduction with specific lysolipids acting as lipochaperones and fruitful mislocalization to the cytoplasm, integral MPs can fold correctly in the aqueous environment of cytoplasm rather than reside exclusively within the lipid bilayer of the membrane.

Although the approach described has been applied to only this far, to the lactose permease, it is apparent that the described approach may represent a general method for rapid isolation, reconstitution, and structural analysis of membrane proteins.

Limitations of the study

The objective of this study is to challenge the prevailing assumption that integral membrane proteins must reside within a lipid bilayer to achieve proper folding and activity. By using LacY, a well-characterized model transporter, we show that it can adopt a soluble, functional conformation in the E. coli cytosol under defined in vivo biosynthetic conditions. Our present work serves as a “proof of principle” study that demonstrates this ability for very hydrophobic membrane protein. Despite its novelty, this work has several limitiations, (1) the effects of specific lipids on membrane protein folding, structure, and function are often protein- and host-context dependent and therefore may not be universally generalizable across all proteins, or even across all functional states of LacY. Additional research will be required to determine whether these findings extend to other membrane proteins and growth conditions; (2) the molecular organization of the LPA-LacY complexes remains to be resolved. Defining the interactions between LPA and LacY will be essential for understanding how membrane proteins fold in non-bilayer environments; and (3) the mechanism by which LPA enables LacY to bypass oversaturated SecYEG translocon is unclear. LPA may influence the organization or activity of the SecYEG translocon directly or indirectly through altered levels of PG and CL. Addressing these questions will strengthen our mechanistic understanding and broaden the relevance of the findings reported here. Nevertheless, this study opens new directions for producing, stabilizing and studying membrane proteins in fully aqueous environments without traditional detergents, nanodiscs or peptidiscs that keep proteins stable in water and offering a conceptual shift in how membrane protein folding can be achieved in vivo.

Experimental model and study participant details

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

E. coli strain SM105 (thr-1, araC14, tsx-78, Δ(gal-attλ)99, hisG4(Oc), rfbC1, rpsL136(strR) xylA5, mtl-1, lacY1, eda50, rfbD1, thiE1) and SM2-1 (plsC101(ts) metC162::Tn10, serA) were utilized as described. Strain BKT29 was engineered by introducing constructs ΔclsA, ΔclsB, ΔclsC, and ymdB::KanR into the E. coli strain UE54 (after removal of its kanamycin resistance [KanR] cassette). UE54 is null for the pgsA gene and is therefore unable to synthesize phosphatidylglycerol (PG).55 The resulting strain, designated BKT29, was subsequently transformed with plasmid pBAD-cls A, -B, or –C35 (35). Cells were grown in LB medium containing 10 g/L Bacto-tryptone, 5 g/L Bacto-yeast extract, and 10 g/L NaCl. E. coli strain SM2-1, harboring two compatible plasmids (pGP1-2 and pT7-5 (lac Y)),12,56,57 was cultivated at 30°C in Luria broth supplemented with 50 μg/mL of kanamycin and 50 μg/mL of ampicillin to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.5. To induce expression, the culture was shifted to 42°C for 20 min to inactivate the temperature-sensitive λ repressor carried on plasmid pGP1-2. Rifampicin (100 μg/mL) was then added to inhibit host RNA polymerase. Cells were further incubated for 10 min at 42°C, followed by an additional 60-min incubation at 37°C.

Method details

Determination of steady-state phospholipid composition by radiolabeling

To determine the steady-state phospholipid composition, cells were uniformly labeled with 1 μCi/mL of [32P]PO4. After labeling, cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in 0.2 mL of acidic solution (0.5 M NaCl in 0.1 N HCl). Lipids were extracted by adding 0.6 mL of solution 2 (chloroform:methanol, 1:2, v/v), followed by vortexing for 30 min. Subsequently, 0.2 mL of resuspension buffer was added, and the sample was vortexed for an additional 10 min. Phase separation was achieved by centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 5 min. The lower chloroform phase, containing the extracted phospholipids, was collected. An aliquot corresponding to approximately 1,000–2,000 counts per minute (cpm) of radiolabeled phospholipid was used for thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analysis. 32P-labeled phospholipids were resolved by one-dimensional thin-layer chromatography (TLC) using HPTLC 60 silica gel plates (0.25 mm thickness), EMD, Gibbstown, NJ), activated and developed with a solvent consisting of chloroform:methanol:acetic acid (65:25:8 v/v). Two-dimensional TLC was also performed to resolve individual 32P-labeled phospholipid species. Separation was carried out using solvent 1 (chloroform/methanol/ammonium hydroxide [65:25:4, (vol/vol)]) in the first dimension and solvent 2 (chloroform/methanol/acetic acid/water [170:25:25:6, (vol/vol)]) in the second dimension. Solvent 1 was prepared using 28–30% aqueous ammonia. Radiolabeled lipids were detected using a Typhoon FLA 9500 phosphorimager and quantified with ImageQuant software. All TLC analyses were performed in at least two independent experiments to ensure reproducibility.

Cell disruption and differential centrifugation of E. coli cells

The cells were lysed using a French press at 1,000 kg/cm2 (16,000 psi) in 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol (BME) in the presence of either 0.5 mM Pefabloc or cOmplete protease inhibitors. The whole cell lysate (WCL) was centrifuged at a moderate speed (e.g., 30,000 × g for 20 min) first to pellet unbroken cell debris. The resulting supernatant is the crude lysate containing both soluble (cytoplasmic) and membrane-associated proteins. The crude extract was centrifuged at a high speed (e.g., 200,000 × g for 1 h at 4°C) to separate the fractions. The resulting supernatant will contain the soluble cytoplasmic proteins (soluble fraction, Cyt), while the pellet will contain the inner membrane (IM).16,58,59 To demonstrate and quantify the distribution of LacY between the IM and cytoplasmic fractions, two separate gels were run with different loading methods based on either total protein amount or volume. ImageJ software was used to measure protein band intensities in both the “cytoplasmic” and “membrane” lanes on western blots from two different (“equal protein” and “equal volume”) sample preparations. The densitometric value of each band was multiplied by the total volume of its corresponding fraction to calculate the total mass of protein in that fraction. The percentage distribution of LacY between IM and cytoplasm was calculated as % of LacY in either Cyt or IM fractions = Total mass of Cyt or IM total mass signal/total mass of Cyt signal + total mass of IM signal) x100.

Isolation of soluble LacY

Purification of protein LacY was carried out at 4°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, and the resulting pellet (∼10 g wet weight) was resuspended in 50 mL of buffer containing 50 mM potassium phosphate (pH 7.5, 5 mM MgSO4,10 mM β-mercaptoethanol (BME), 0.5 mM Pefabloc or cOmplete protease inhibitor, and 30 μg/mL DNase I. Cells were disrupted using a French press as described above, and unbroken cells were removed by centrifugation at 30,000 × g for 20 min. The cytoplasmic fraction was obtained by ultracentrifugation at 200,000 × g for 1 h. This fraction was concentrated using Vivaspin (10,000 MWCO) concentrators for subsequent gel filtration chromatography, or used directly for reconstitution into liposomes. Purified samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and visualized using Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining and western blot analysis. Fractions containing LacY were pooled and further concentrated to approximately 1 mg/mL using Vivaspin concentrators by centrifugation. Soluble LacY was purified further by size exclusion chromatography on Superdex 200 200SF. The column buffer contained 100 mM KPi (pH 7.3)//1.0 mM DTT. A column was used and run at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The calibration standard curve for size-exclusion chromatography was measured using the standard substances, creatine phosphokinase (80 kDa), carbonic anhydrase (29 kDa), ovalbumin (44 kDa), and lysozyme (14 kDa). Purified samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and visualized by Coomassie Blue staining and Western blot analysis.

Reconstitution of soluble permease

E. coli total lipid extract was dissolved in chloroform/methanol (9:1, v/v). The solvent was removed first under a stream of oxygen-free nitrogen and then under vacuum to form a thin film of dry lipids. The dried lipids were rehydrated in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (KPi; pH 7.5), containing 2 mM of β-mercaptoethanol (βME) and 1.5% of octyl-β-D-glucopyranoside (OG), to a final lipid concentration of 50 mg/mL. OG was removed by overnight dialysis against buffer 50 mM KPi (pH 7.5), containing 50 mM MgCl2. The resulting liposomes were either used immediately or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for future use. For reconstitution with LacY, frozen liposomes were thawed as needed, and OG was added to a final concentration of 1.5%. The liposomes were mixed with purified soluble LacY at a lipid-to-protein ratio of 500:1 (w/w; final LacY concentration of 0.1 mg/mL). The mixture was incubated at 30°C with gentle agitation for 10 min, then placed on ice for 20 min. To remove OG and form proteoliposomes, the mixture was diluted at least 50-fold into 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) and 2 mM βME, and centrifuged at 190,000 × g for 45 min. The supernatant was aspirated, and the resulting proteoliposomes (final concentration: 50 mg/mL phospholipid, 100 μg/mL LacY) were resuspended in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) and used immediately for transport assays.

Lactose transport assay

To measure membrane potential-driven active transport, 10 μL of proteoliposomes containing 50 mM potassium phosphate, pH 7.5, were diluted into 990 μL of 50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.5, containing 0.1 mM [14C] lactose (250 mCi/mol, 5 μCi/mL) and 10 μM valinomycin. To dissipate the membrane potential, 10 μM nigericin was added. Aliquots of 100 μL were removed at various times, quenched with 3 mL of ice-cold 50 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.5, 100 mM LiCl, immediately filtered through GFTS filters, and washed using 5 mL of the same buffer. Filters were dried and counted by liquid scintillation. A background value, determined by assaying liposomes prepared in the same way, but lacking LacY, was subtracted routinely. All incubations were at 30°C.

In vitro assay of CL synthase

Strain BKT29 was created by introducing ΔclsA, ΔclsB, ΔclsC, and ymdB::KanR into UE54 (after removal of its KanR cassette), which is null for the pgsA gene and therefore lacks the ability to make PG. The resulting BKT29 was transformed with plasmid pBAD-cls A, -B, or -C. These cells were grown from an overnight culture to OD600 of ∼0.05 at 37°C and then induced for the respective cls gene expression with 0.025% arabinose. After induction, cells were grown for two more hours until harvesting by centrifugation at 3,500 x g at 4°C. The cells were then washed with Phosphate Buffered Saline PBS (pH 7.4). Afterward, the pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of PBS and lysed by passing through a French press at 18,000 psi. Cell-free lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 g for 20 min at 4°C. The membrane fraction was isolated by ultracentrifugation of the cell-free lysate at 100,000 × g for 2 h at 4°C. E. coli AL95 strain (pss 93: kan lacY::Tn9), lacking the ability to synthesize PE, was used to make 32P-labeled PG.28,33 32P-labelled PG were prepared by growing this strain in LB medium containing 5 μCi/ml [32P]PO4 and 50 mM MgCl2.25,28 The cells from 50 mL of culture were harvested, and the lipids were extracted as described above. The total extracted lipids were loaded onto Silica Gel G thin-layer plates and developed with chloroform/methanol/ammonia/H2O (60:37.5:1:3 v/v). The dried plate was exposed to an X-ray film for 2 h. The phospholipid bands were visualized by developing the film, and the band corresponding to 32P-labeled PG on the TLC plate was scraped and extracted as described above. Dry silica gel powder scraped from a TLC plate should first be saturated with 0.5 N NaCl in 0.1 N HCl alone by vigorous vortexing. The reaction mixture of 100 μL consisted of PBS, 0.1% Triton X-100, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol,10,000 cpm of 32PG in the reaction, 50 mM of DOPG, and 2 mg/mL of membrane prepared from arabinose-induced BKT29 transformed with either plasmid pBAD-A, -B, or -C. This mixture was supplemented or not with 50 mM LysoPA. The final assay mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 60 min or 120 min. To quench the reaction, 0.3 mL of chloroform/methanol 2:1 (vol/vol) was added to form a two-phase Bligh-Dyer solution.

Statistical analysis

The results of quantitative experiments are shown as means for independent experiments performed multiple times, as indicated. Values are means (±S.D.) from three experiments, where each determination was done in duplicate. Variation between duplicates was ±3%. The statistical significance of mean differences was assessed by means of the two-tailed Student’s t test. Images represent three independent experiments.
