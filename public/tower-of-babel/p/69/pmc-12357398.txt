A systematic scoping review of health-seeking behavior and healthcare utilization in tribal communities of odisha, india: concentration on maternal and child health
Authors: N Siva, Kasturi Mohanty, Rashmipriya Rath, Shubhasmita Mishra, Monalisa Mishra, Krishna Samal, Sasmita Das, Navaneeta Rath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal and child health among tribal populations in Odisha, India, is significantly influenced by socio-economic, cultural, and infrastructural factors. Cultural practices, reliance on traditional medicine, and limited awareness of modern healthcare benefits shape health-seeking behavior. This scoping review synthesises evidence on health-seeking behaviour, healthcare utilisation, awareness of healthcare services, government policies, and barriers in tribal communities in Odisha, India.MethodsThis scoping review was conducted following the Joanna Briggs Institute guidelines. We followed the Arksey and O’Malley methodological framework and applied the PAGER framework (Patterns, Advances, Gaps, Evidence for Practice, Research Recommendations) for quality of reporting. Studies were identified through systematic searches of international and Indian databases, Indian journal websites, organisational websites, repositories, and registries, focusing on health-seeking behaviour and healthcare utilisation among Odisha’s tribal communities. Only English-language articles published between January 2011 and July 2024 were included. The methodological quality of the selected studies was independently assessed by two reviewers using the JBI Quality Assessment Checklist.ResultsA total of 39 studies, encompassing 36,613 participants, were included in the review. The findings highlight significant barriers to healthcare access among tribal communities, including poverty, illiteracy, cultural practices, geographic isolation, distance to healthcare centres, transportation availability and mistrust of government services. While some tribes have shown progress in adopting modern healthcare services, many continue to rely on traditional medicine and indigenous practices. Socio-cultural factors, such as patriarchal norms and religious rituals, further influence healthcare-seeking behavior. Government initiatives like the National Rural Health Mission and the Integrated Child Development Services have had some success in improving healthcare utilisation among tribal populations. However, strengthening community support, conducting village-level awareness campaigns, and implementing targeted educational interventions can play a transformative role in enhancing healthcare access and overall well-being.ConclusionImproving maternal and child health in Odisha’s tribal populations requires culturally sensitive approaches integrated with modern healthcare strategies. Enhancing awareness, infrastructure, and community health workers’ roles can bridge access gaps while respecting tribal traditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24079-z.Keywords: Mother, Child, Health-seeking behaviour, Healthcare utilisation, Socio-cultural barriers, Healthcare policies, Healthcare access, Tribal health, Odisha

Introduction

Odisha stands out among Indian states for its substantial and diverse tribal population, which comprises 22.85% of the Scheduled Tribe population, including 62 distinct Scheduled Tribe communities and 13 Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups [1, 2]. The tribal communities in Odisha, with more than 62 different tribes, predominantly reside in remote, forested, and hilly regions that have limited access to modern healthcare services [1, 3]. These indigenous communities are often socially and economically marginalised, making them disproportionately vulnerable during public health emergencies [4]. Despite numerous government interventions, these communities continue to face significant health challenges, leading to higher rates of morbidity and mortality compared to the general population [1, 3]. According to the National Family Health Survey (NFHS), tribal populations in the state experience an infant mortality rate of 41.6 per 1,000 live births, which is significantly higher than the national average. Additionally, the under-five mortality rate stands at 50.3 per 1,000 live births, reflecting critical gaps in maternal and child healthcare services [5]. Nutritional deficiencies are also prevalent, with 55.9% of tribal children being underweight and 79.8% suffering from anaemia [6]. Women in these communities are similarly affected, with 64.9% experiencing anaemia, contributing to adverse maternal health outcomes [3, 7, 8].

Antenatal, maternity, and postnatal care are critical components of improving maternal and child health in tribal populations, where unique challenges persist [9, 10]. Widespread communicable diseases such as malaria, tuberculosis, and sexually transmitted infections, coupled with poor living conditions, inadequate sanitation, and limited access to clean water, significantly impact maternal and neonatal health [1, 9]. The growing burden of non-communicable diseases like hypertension and diabetes, along with the prevalence of genetic disorders such as sickle cell anaemia, further complicates maternal healthcare delivery [1, 5]. Traditional beliefs and cultural practices often delay the utilisation of essential maternal health services, including antenatal check-ups, safe deliveries, and postnatal care [11, 12]. Addressing these barriers is vital for ensuring timely and comprehensive maternal and child healthcare. India’s commitment to Universal Health Coverage and the Sustainable Development Goals emphasises equitable access to healthcare, focusing on “healthy lives and well-being for all.” The National Health Policy 2017 and Article 21 of the Indian Constitution, recognising healthcare as a fundamental right, underline the urgency of providing accessible, affordable, and high-quality maternal and child health services to marginalised tribal populations [13, 14].

The Indian and Odisha governments have introduced numerous healthcare policies and schemes to enhance maternal, child, and adolescent health through comprehensive antenatal, intranatal, and postnatal care. At the national level, initiatives like Ayushman Bharat, with its Health and Wellness Centers and Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana, provide primary care and financial protection for medical expenses. Programs such as Janani Suraksha Yojana and Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram promote institutional deliveries and offer free maternal healthcare services, including diagnostics and transportation. Additionally, schemes like Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana and Mission Indradhanush focus on improving maternal nutrition and ensuring universal immunisation. For adolescent girls, the Rashtriya Kishor Swasthya Karyakram addresses nutritional needs, menstrual hygiene, and reproductive health [1, 10, 11, 15]. Odisha-specific schemes like the Mamata Scheme and Nirmal Scheme incentivise institutional deliveries and provide transport assistance for tribal and rural populations. Moreover, the Biju Swasthya Kalyan Yojana ensures financial assistance for healthcare, contributing significantly to reducing maternal and child mortality and promoting healthier communities. Additionally, The Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram focuses on early identification and intervention for common childhood illnesses and developmental delays. This program screens children from birth to 18 years for 32 health conditions, providing free treatment and follow-up care to ensure optimal growth and development [16].

Despite the availability of modern healthcare services, many tribal communities in Odisha continue to rely on traditional health practices deeply rooted in their cultural and spiritual beliefs, which significantly influence antenatal, maternity, postnatal care, and overall maternal and child health [17–19]. Local healers, known as disaris or vaidyas, play a central role, utilising Indigenous knowledge of over 325 medicinal plants to address health needs through herbal remedies, rituals, and magico-religious interventions [20]. These practices, deeply rooted in cultural and spiritual beliefs, provide a sense of familiarity and community identity [18]. However, illnesses are often attributed to supernatural causes such as spirits, sorcery, or cultural taboos, which can delay timely access to essential modern healthcare [15]. This delay is particularly critical during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period when immediate medical care is vital to prevent complications and ensure the well-being of mothers and children [9].

Mother and child health among Odisha Tribal communities in Odisha experience significant healthcare accessibility challenges due to geographical remoteness, poverty, dependence on daily wages, and deep-rooted cultural beliefs [1]. Limited access to healthcare facilities, poor transportation networks, and low literacy levels contribute to high maternal and infant mortality rates and delayed and inadequate utilization of healthcare services [11, 15]. Historically, these communities have relied on traditional healers and indigenous medicine, leading to minimal utilisation of modern healthcare services [20]. Social factors, such as gender norms and economic constraints, hinder access to institutional care [21]. The healthcare infrastructure remains inadequate, with many villages lacking primary health centers or skilled professionals [22]. These barriers often delay or prevent tribal mothers and children from receiving essential antenatal, maternity, and postnatal care [1, 10]. Studying health-seeking behavior is crucial for understanding barriers to healthcare utilisation, informing policy interventions, and developing culturally appropriate strategies to improve maternal and child health outcomes.

Researchers conducted a comprehensive scoping review to understand the health-seeking behaviour, healthcare utilisation, awareness, knowledge, perceptions, and barriers tribal mothers face in accessing healthcare services. The review also examined reasons for relying on traditional medicine practices to address maternal and child health issues. Findings emphasised the importance of culturally sensitive health education, community engagement, and involving traditional healers in public health initiatives. Strengthening Health and Wellness Centers, increasing the availability of trained healthcare personnel, and promoting trust in modern healthcare were identified as crucial strategies. Leveraging government initiatives and fostering community participation can bridge these gaps, combining traditional knowledge with modern medical care to ensure better maternal and child health outcomes and a healthier future for tribal communities in Odisha.

Methods

The review was carried out following the scoping review methodological framework outlined by Arksey and O’ Malley [23]. This framework consists of five stages, which outline the methodology and guide the reporting of findings.

Stage 1: identifying the research question

The primary review question guiding this study was: What are the patterns, determinants, and barriers influencing health-seeking behaviour and healthcare utilisation for maternal and child health among tribal populations in Odisha, India? Our research team consisted of seven multidisciplinary experts from nursing, sociology, public health, and health policy, ensuring a comprehensive approach to analysing the complex dynamics within tribal healthcare systems.

Stage 2: identifying relevant studies

The literature search was conducted in two phases: global and Indian databases, complemented by other methods to ensure comprehensive coverage.

Global databases

A thorough search was performed in international databases, including CINAHL via EBSCOhost, Scopus, ScienceDirect, MEDLINE via PubMed, Web of Science, ProQuest, Microsoft Academic, and DOAJ. Both free-text keywords and database-specific subject headings were employed, ensuring a robust exploration of relevant literature.

Indian databases and additional methods

Indian databases, such as the Indian Citation Index, J-Gate, and the Journal of Clinical and Diagnostic Research, were also searched. Institutional and non-institutional repositories, including Shodhganga, NISCAIR, and ePrints@IISc, were explored to identify additional studies. Searches extended to public health journals, sociology and anthropology journal websites, and organisations like WHO, UNICEF India, and Semantic Scholar. To ensure inclusivity, reference lists from the included studies and relevant systematic reviews were screened for additional pertinent articles.

Stage 3: study selection process

The inclusion and exclusion criteria for this scoping review were developed using the PCC (Population, Concept, Context) framework, as recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute, to guide scoping reviews. The Population (P) of interest includes tribal mothers, children, and adolescents (both girls and boys) residing in Odisha, India, who are affected by a range of maternal and child health issues. These issues include physical conditions such as pregnancy-related complications, neonatal health problems, malnutrition, and infections, as well as psychological conditions such as postpartum depression and anxiety. The Concept (C) focuses on the exploration of health-seeking behaviours, healthcare utilisation patterns, and the various determinants that influence health outcomes among these populations. This includes an examination of cultural beliefs, traditional practices, barriers to accessing care, and financial or systemic constraints. The Context (C) is limited to studies conducted within Odisha, India, specifically in rural, tribal, or community-based settings where healthcare access and utilisation among tribal populations is a central focus. The review includes prospective observational studies, cross-sectional, cohort, case-control studies, and secondary data analyses from the NFHS published in English from January 2011 to July 2024, excluding qualitative studies, reviews, letters, case reports, grey literature, and studies focused on fathers, extended family members, or traditional healers.

The Rayyan software was utilised to identify and remove duplicate records from the exported database search results. After eliminating duplicates, two independent authors screened the titles and abstracts to determine study eligibility, categorising them as “include,” “exclude,” or “uncertain.” In cases of discrepancies, a third reviewer resolved the conflicts through discussion. To enhance the reliability of the screening process, a predefined screening form based on the eligibility criteria was employed (Level 1: Screening). Following this, the full texts of the selected articles were retrieved and reviewed by two independent authors to extract detailed information, including study design, participant characteristics, sample size, type and duration of interventions, follow-up period, outcome parameters, and methods used for outcome validation (Level 2: Selection). The appropriateness of the studies for inclusion in the scoping review was evaluated based on the relevance of their outcome measures to the review’s domain of interest (Level 3: Eligibility). Any disagreements at this stage were resolved through consensus among the reviewers (Level 4: Inclusion). Stage 4: charting the data

Risk of bias in individual studies

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Quality Assessment Checklist [24], which evaluates eight domains: [1] clearly defined inclusion criteria [2], detailed description of study subjects and setting [3], valid and reliable exposure measurement [4], use of objective, standard criteria [5], identification of confounding factors [6], strategies to address confounding factors [7], valid and reliable outcome measurement, and [8] appropriate statistical analysis. The overall risk of bias was found to be mild to moderate; however, a majority of the studies demonstrated a high risk of bias in specific domains, particularly in the identification of confounding factors and the strategies implemented to address these confounders.

Data extraction

Following title and abstract screening, selected articles were manually retrieved for full-text review. Two independent reviewers from the research team extracted key findings from the eligible studies for synthesis. The data extraction process included detailed information on study objectives, design, participant characteristics (including age and sample details), types of health issues, health-seeking behaviours, healthcare utilisation patterns, transportation methods, treatment types, barriers to care-seeking and access, and knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions toward modern healthcare practices.

To ensure rigor and consistency, all articles were assessed by two primary reviewers, with 10% of randomly selected articles independently reviewed by two additional reviewers for validation. Discrepancies were resolved through mutual consensus. The general characteristics of the selected studies were systematically documented. This included author(s), year of publication, demographic details, study duration, research design, participant characteristics, health-seeking behaviours, healthcare utilisation, transportation methods, treatment approaches, cultural practice rationales, barriers to care-seeking and access, and study findings, providing a comprehensive overview for synthesis.

Stage 5: collating, summarizing and reporting the results

Following Arksey and O’Malley’s approach, we systematically charted the extent, nature, and distribution of the included articles. A narrative synthesis was performed across all studies to identify common appropriate findings based on the purpose of the study. Additionally, we utilised the PAGER Framework (Patterns, Advances, Gaps, Evidence for Practice, and Research Recommendations) for quality of reporting (Table 1).

Results

A total of potentially eligible articles were screened from 6 international databases, one Indian database and other sources. A total of 874 studies were identified from international databases, along with 115 studies from national databases and websites. After predefined Level 1 screening, 705 studies were excluded, and 130 were selected following the removal of duplicates. During Level 2 full-text screening, 79 articles were excluded for not meeting the eligibility criteria. Ultimately, 39 studies were included in the review. Study characteristics

In total, 39 studies were included in the review [3, 6–11, 15, 17–19, 21, 22, 25–50], comprising 33 primary studies and six secondary analysis studies. The studies encompassed 13 Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups (PVTGs), 12 Scheduled Tribes in Odisha, and 15 Scheduled Tribes residing across Odisha and Jharkhand, focusing on various health-related concepts. Health issues

Common physical health problems include malnutrition, anaemia, respiratory infections, diarrheal diseases, tuberculosis, malaria, and skin diseases like scabies. Maternal health challenges include high maternal mortality and morbidity, pregnancy complications, and inadequate access to antenatal care (ANC) services, often linked to anaemia, malnutrition, and poor hygiene. Reproductive health concerns, such as dysmenorrhea, sexually transmitted infections, and HIV/AIDS, were also highlighted. Among children, issues like undernutrition, low birth weight, high infant and under-five mortality, and infectious diseases, including measles and acute respiratory infections, were prevalent. Adolescents face unique challenges, including delayed menarche, menstrual irregularities, and psychosocial problems such as depression and anxiety. The studies also emphasised poor health literacy, socio-economic disadvantages, and cultural beliefs in traditional healers and spirits as barriers to accessing healthcare. These findings underscore the significant burden of malnutrition, infections, chronic illnesses, and reproductive and mental health challenges among tribal populations, compounded by limited healthcare access and socio-cultural constraints.

Health-seeking behaviour

Health-seeking behaviour among tribes in Odisha is shaped by a complex interplay of cultural beliefs, socio-economic factors, and access to healthcare services. The Kutia Kandha tribe, for instance, faces significant barriers to healthcare, particularly in child dental services, with participants showing low motivation for oral health checkups and expecting free medicines. Mothers in these communities often self-treat before seeking professional help, and only a small percentage (7.33%) utilise government medical facilities [35]. Delays in seeking care are common, largely due to accessibility issues, and treatment choices are heavily influenced by illness severity [39]. Perceptions of illness, particularly malaria, also vary among mothers, impacting the timely seeking of care [6, 39]. On the other hand, the Saora tribe demonstrates greater awareness of oral health, with 29.7% of pregnant participants visiting a dentist [22, 35]. The knowledge of healthcare services, especially concerning maternal and child health, is generally low across tribal groups, compounded by factors like poverty, illiteracy, and malnutrition [3, 39, 47]. Among young girls, there is a lack of awareness about contraception, menstruation, and sexual health, contributing to poor menstrual hygiene and limited engagement with healthcare providers [7, 28, 29, 41, 50].

Moreover, 22% of tribal children are deprived of vaccinations, and many children under five lack essential vitamin A supplementation [6, 9, 21, 25]. Healthcare-seeking behaviour is often influenced by social and familial support systems, where gender discrimination also plays a role [39]. Male children are more likely to receive medical attention than female children [44, 46]. Furthermore, despite the availability of Anganwadi centres and government health programs, such as Safe Motherhood Assurance, utilisation remains low due to cultural beliefs, financial constraints, and lack of awareness [9, 28, 39]. Many tribal individuals still rely on herbal medicine and traditional healing practices, which are deeply intertwined with their cultural and spiritual beliefs [18, 26, 37]. Additionally, education plays a crucial role in improving health-seeking behaviour [11]. Women with higher levels of education and autonomy are more likely to seek modern healthcare services, particularly in maternal health [17, 50]. However, the high reliance on traditional practices persists, particularly in remote areas, where healthcare access is limited [40, 42]. Healthcare utilisation and barriers to healthcare access

Distance

Distance to healthcare centres significantly impacts healthcare access and utilisation among tribal populations. Distance to healthcare facilities contributes to poor oral health quality [22, 35]. Most children sought treatment within one kilometre of home. A total of 48.6% of boys and 47.3% of girls accessed nearby sources [39]. Many tribes reside in remote areas far from government hospitals or PHCs, with the median distance to PHCs around 5 km and private doctors approximately 8 km [26]. In some cases, vision centres are 13–26 km away, while secondary healthcare centres are 45–63 km distant [38]. Geographical remoteness and difficult terrain further complicate access, with a lack of public transportation being a major barrier [9, 37, 43, 45]. Even when healthcare facilities are relatively closer, resource constraints and unavailability of health personnel during emergencies reduce their effectiveness [35, 46, 47]. Juang communities in Cluster 1 face poor communication infrastructure despite proximity to health centres, while Cluster 2 villages have better access to modern healthcare services [47]. Geographical remoteness, with many tribes residing far from government hospitals or PHCs in interior areas, limits access to modern healthcare facilities. This lack of proximity to health institutions increases out-of-pocket expenditures, hinders service utilisation, and contributes to higher pregnancy-related mortality and morbidity [21]. Socio-economic disparities further exacerbate these challenges [15]. Distance significantly impacts healthcare-seeking behaviour and utilisation among tribal populations [32].

Transportation

Transportation and medical facilities in tribal areas present significant challenges that hinder access to healthcare [35, 43]. Many regions lack self-transport options, and public transport is either unavailable or difficult to access, often leaving walking as the only alternative [22, 34, 38]. Roads in these areas vary in quality, with 73.7% being concrete, 22.1% mud, and 4.2% lacking proper road infrastructure. Seasonal factors, such as monsoon floods, disrupt communication and transportation networks, further compounding the problem [9]. In emergencies, 78.1% of the population is aware of transport options, relying on public transport, personal vehicles, or assistance from family and friends to reach local hospitals [37]. However, irregular terrain, particularly in the Kalahandi Balangir Koraput region, further limits access to maternal and other health services, leaving many communities underserved [45].

Cultural practices

Many tribes rely on traditional healers (Gunias or Disaris) and herbal remedies for pregnancy-related issues, often delaying or avoiding modern medical interventions. Home births assisted by traditional birth attendants (Dais) are preferred over institutional deliveries due to deep-rooted beliefs, increasing the risk of complications [9, 12, 15]. Rituals like placental burial discourage hospital births, while postpartum confinement and dietary restrictions weaken new mothers and prevent them from seeking postnatal care [9, 11, 19]. Newborn care practices, such as discarding colostrum and applying mustard oil or ash to the umbilical cord, contribute to malnutrition and infections [11, 14, 49]. Childhood illnesses are often attributed to supernatural causes, leading families to seek exorcisms or religious rituals instead of medical treatment [10, 48]. Harmful practices like scarification and branding are still used to treat ailments in children with any type of fever and cause severe infections [10]. Adolescent girls face seclusion during menstruation with limited access to menstrual hygiene and reproductive health education [41, 44, 46].

Reasons for cultural practice

Cultural beliefs in tribal communities often lead to misconceptions about health and healthcare practices, impacting maternal, child, and adolescent girls’ health. For example, malaria is sometimes associated with dirty environments or damp climates rather than mosquito bites, limiting the use of modern treatments [6]. Health practices are also closely tied to social and religious rituals designed to protect individuals from perceived threats, such as evil spirits [18, 47]. In some communities, practices like menstrual isolation, rooted in religious beliefs and societal taboos, restrict women’s access to healthcare during these times [36]. Patriarchal structures further limit women’s autonomy in making healthcare decisions, particularly regarding marriage, childbirth, and family size [19, 47]. Additionally, low literacy rates and limited education contribute to the persistence of traditional practices, leaving many unaware of modern healthcare options [31]. Geographic isolation and economic constraints exacerbate reliance on traditional remedies, such as herbal treatments and spiritual healing, due to limited access to healthcare facilities [40]. Moreover, cultural practices promote social cohesion and a strong sense of identity, which can make it challenging for individuals to embrace changes in healthcare practices [17, 33]. Economic hardships and inadequate infrastructure further hinder access to modern healthcare, while migration introduces new influences that challenge traditional practices [8]. Change in healthcare practices tends to be gradual, particularly in rural areas where longstanding traditions remain deeply rooted. Community health workers, such as Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHA) and Axillary Nurse Midwifery (ANM), play a crucial role in bridging the gap between communities and healthcare services but often face challenges due to knowledge gaps [51].

Discussion

This scoping review included 39 studies involving 36,361 tribal mothers and children to examine health-seeking behaviour, healthcare utilisation, and awareness of healthcare services among tribal populations in Odisha, India, while addressing socio-cultural and infrastructural barriers. This review identified critical gaps in maternal health, with tribal women experiencing high rates of maternal mortality despite increasing institutional deliveries.

Socio-economic barriers

NFHS surveys highlighted socioeconomic and demographic factors, particularly education and wealth, as critical determinants of healthcare access, yet disparities persist, especially in Odisha [10–12, 15, 27, 31, 48]. Low utilisation of healthcare services, coupled with lack of education, socio-economic barriers, and cultural practices [10, 11, 15]. The health-seeking behaviour of tribal populations in Odisha is heavily influenced by socio-economic, cultural, and infrastructural factors [26, 39, 47]. A systematic review on community participation in the development, implementation, and evaluation of health services highlighted that social mobilisation efforts, particularly those aimed at engaging family and community leaders, play a crucial role in promoting maternal health services and addressing cultural barriers that deter women from seeking care [52]. In line with previous studies, our review found that many tribal individuals tend to delay seeking formal healthcare, often opting for self-treatment or relying on traditional healers [18, 53]. This pattern is consistent with findings from other studies across India, where tribal communities exhibit low levels of healthcare utilisation, particularly for preventive services such as ANC and vaccination [1, 12]. The low utilisation of government healthcare services (e.g., only 7.33% of participants in our review sought care from government facilities) reflects a broader issue observed in tribal populations across the country [54].

Maternal health

Health-seeking behaviour in tribal and underserved populations reflects significant gaps in knowledge and practices. During the postnatal period, minimal bottle feeding was observed with exclusive breastfeeding till 6 months of infant age [49], while adolescent girls exhibited poor awareness of menstrual hygiene, sexual health, and nutritional needs [28, 29, 36, 41, 46, 50]. High levels of postnatal depression and inadequate family counselling were reported [33]. Migrant tribal populations faced high poverty levels [40]. Undernutrition, anaemia, and micronutrient deficiencies are widespread, with tribal women experiencing a higher prevalence of poor nutritional status, which affects their reproductive health and increases the risk of pregnancy complications [7, 8, 19, 26, 45]. Low utilisation of ANC, coupled with inadequate postnatal care, including postnatal anxiety, stress and depression, remains a significant concern [15, 33]. The lack of education, awareness, and belief in indigenous treatment and cultural practices contribute to the underutilisation of healthcare services [13]. This behaviour mirrors findings from other tribal populations in India, where health-seeking behaviour is often delayed, exacerbating the risk of morbidity and mortality [1].

Child health

The health issues among children in tribal communities are further compounded by socio-economic challenges such as poverty, parental illiteracy, and poor living conditions. These factors exacerbate the burden of infectious diseases among children like malaria and dengue, which remains a significant reason for morbidity and mortality in tribal areas [1, 13]. Although ANC visits have reduced infant undernutrition and improved stunting, underweight, and wasting rates over time among children, continued efforts to address barriers to healthcare access and empower communities through education and socioeconomic interventions are essential [10, 11, 15, 27, 31, 48]. Malaria and malnutrition often coexist in these communities, creating a vicious cycle that requires integrated intervention strategies [6]. Moreover, diseases like sickle cell anaemia, which disproportionately affect tribal populations, require better management strategies, including increased social awareness and improved access to healthcare services [37].

Healthcare system initiations

Geographical isolation, lack of transportation, deeply embedded cultural practices, socio-economic barriers and the inadequate presence of trained healthcare personnel in tribal areas contribute to low healthcare utilisation [15]. These findings are in line with research that points to the systemic inadequacies in healthcare delivery in tribal regions, which continue to persist despite government efforts [1]. Poverty remains a significant challenge, with many unable to afford out-of-pocket healthcare expenses, even with government schemes providing free medicines [21]. Government policies aimed at improving healthcare access in rural and tribal areas have had mixed results [10, 11, 15, 45].

Successful healthcare models aimed at improving education, awareness, and health-seeking behavior among tribal populations in India [45], such as the National Rural Health Mission and the Integrated Child Development Services, have been designed to address healthcare access and maternal-child health issues [30, 45]. Particularly in Odisha, the government have significantly enhanced access to modern healthcare and government services [1]. The Swasthya Seva Initiative in Odisha, with its network of trained community health workers (CHWs), has been pivotal in promoting health education and encouraging the use of government healthcare facilities. The NRHM has deployed ASHAs to raise awareness about maternal and child health, preventive measures, and available government schemes. Mobile Health Units (MHUs) and Telemedicine Services have bridged the distance barrier, providing healthcare services and consultations in remote areas.

Additionally, the establishment of Village Health Sanitation and Nutrition Committees (VHSNCs) has fostered community-level awareness and trust in government healthcare facilities. The Maa Durga Health Insurance Scheme has also played a key role in ensuring financial accessibility to healthcare services. These models, alongside initiatives like Rural Health Training Centers (RHTCs), have improved healthcare utilization among the tribal population by raising awareness, providing timely medical interventions, and promoting the use of government health services.

Our review found that despite the availability of various health schemes, such as Anganwadi centres offering basic health check-ups and nutritional support, there is still a significant gap in awareness among tribal communities. Many tribal individuals continue to rely on traditional medicine, herbal remedies, and faith-based practices for treating common ailments [18]. This lack of awareness is particularly evident in the Kutia Kandha tribe, where participants showed low motivation for regular oral health check-ups and expected free medicine from researchers rather than seeking professional care [35]. As per the NFHS survey, the low percentage of tribal women accessing ANC (10–14%) and the underutilisation of government facilities for general healthcare indicate that policy interventions have not fully addressed the barriers faced by these communities [11, 12, 15].

Policy implications

Traditional healers are often the first point of contact for healthcare; there is a pressing need to integrate these practices into modern healthcare systems [4, 54]. While integrating traditional and modern healthcare systems presents a potential way forward, reconciling these two approaches remains a significant challenge, highlighting the need for culturally sensitive interventions to improve health access and outcomes in these communities [20]. This integration could help bridge the gap in service delivery, especially in remote areas where modern healthcare is not readily available and transportation issues are present [35, 43]. Integrating traditional and modern healthcare systems can bridge existing gaps in healthcare access among Odisha’s tribal populations [1]. Traditional healers play a crucial role in community health,, and leveraging their influence alongside modern medical practices can enhance healthcare utilization [15]. Culturally sensitive interventions, such as training traditional healers to identify critical conditions requiring medical attention, can improve maternal and child health outcomes [20]. Strengthening healthcare infrastructure, increasing community engagement, and incorporating indigenous knowledge into public health initiatives can foster trust and acceptance [52].

Furthermore, implementing community support and village campaigns can play a transformative role in improving the health-seeking behaviour of tribal populations in Odisha, India. Training healthcare workers, such as community nurses, to recognise and respect traditional healthcare practices while offering evidence-based interventions is essential. This approach would enhance community trust through evidence-based medicine and improve the effectiveness of sustainable healthcare programs [51, 52]. Educational interventions can highlight the advantages of maternal and child health schemes, reducing reliance on traditional or indigenous practices [11]. Consistent advocacy, along with primary healthcare services and health education campaigns, can address cultural barriers and encourage care-seeking behaviour among mothers [12]. By tackling inadequate healthcare services and cultural perceptions, these interventions can significantly improve access to preventive and curative care, ensuring better health outcomes for tribal communities [10, 15, 51].

Limitations

This scoping review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. Firstly, access to full-text articles was limited, especially for studies behind paywalls or in non-English languages, which may have resulted in the exclusion of relevant research. The focus on tribal populations in Odisha may also limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions or tribal communities across India, as healthcare contexts and socio-economic factors can vary significantly. Some studies may have been prone to reporting bias, particularly concerning sensitive health topics or socially stigmatised behaviours, which could have affected the accuracy of the data. Variations in how health-seeking behaviour and healthcare utilisation were defined and measured also hindered direct comparisons and synthesis of findings. Cultural sensitivity was another challenge, as the complexities and nuances of indigenous beliefs and practices may not have been fully captured, potentially influencing the accuracy of the findings. Finally, many of the studies were cross-sectional, which limits the ability to track trends over time or establish causal relationships.

Implications

This study highlights critical areas requiring intervention to improve health-seeking behaviour and healthcare utilisation among tribal populations in Odisha. The findings suggest the need for culturally sensitive healthcare models that integrate traditional beliefs with modern medical practices to enhance acceptance. Strengthening education and awareness programs, especially among women, is essential to address misconceptions and promote informed decision-making regarding health. Improved infrastructure, increased accessibility to healthcare services, and targeted government policies can bridge the gap in healthcare delivery. The role of community health workers like ASHA and ANM should be enhanced through training and support to foster trust and effectiveness in delivering services. The review findings highlight the critical need for future research incorporating qualitative perspectives to gain deeper insights into the cultural, social, and behavioral factors shaping health-seeking behavior among tribal populations. Additionally, addressing socio-economic disparities, such as poverty and illiteracy, can lead to better utilisation of available resources. These implications highlight the importance of a multi-faceted approach to healthcare interventions in tribal regions, emphasising inclusivity, education, and cultural sensitivity.

Conclusion

This review highlights persistent socio-economic, cultural, and structural barriers affecting healthcare utilization among Odisha’s tribal populations. While these challenges are well-documented, our findings emphasise the urgent need for context-specific, scalable interventions. Culturally inclusive healthcare models integrating indigenous practices with modern medical services can improve acceptance and accessibility. Strengthening local healthcare infrastructure, leveraging digital health solutions, and fostering community-led health initiatives are essential to bridging existing gaps. Future policies should prioritize equity-driven approaches, ensuring sustainable and culturally competent healthcare delivery to these underserved populations.
