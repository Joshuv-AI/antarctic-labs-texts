Integrated 16 S rRNA and transcriptome analysis reveal molecular and microbial mechanisms of cold-tolerant germination in hulless barley
Authors: Panrong Ren, Jie Wang, Lei Gong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElucidating the mechanisms underlying cold-tolerant germination is crucial for enhancing crop resilience to low temperatures. Hulless barley (Hordeum vulgare var. coeleste L.), with remarkable natural cold adaptation, serves as an ideal model to study cold stress tolerance mechanisms in gramineous crops. In this study, cold-tolerant variety 37 and cold-sensitive variety 44 were screened and used to investigate the molecular mechanisms of cold-tolerant germination, via seed germination assays, combined with phytohormone determination, transcriptome sequencing and 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing.ResultsLow temperature significantly inhibited hulless barley seed germination: the germination rate of cold-sensitive variety 44 decreased by 69%, while that of cold-tolerant variety 37 only decreased by 2%. Transcriptome analysis identified 2,647 and 2,392 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in variety 37 and 44, respectively. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) revealed a green module significantly positively correlated with gibberellic acid (GA) content, containing 10 core genes such as late embryogenesis abundant protein (LEA) and Homeobox genes. 16 S rRNA sequencing showed that the cold-tolerant variety 37 had enriched abundances of dominant endophytes including Sphingomonas and Pelomonas, with correlation coefficients of 0.70 and 0.87 with GA content, respectively. Additionally, exogenous GA treatment significantly increased germination rates under cold stress by 176.67% in cold-sensitive variety 44.ConclusionsThis study confirms that the enhanced cold tolerance of hulless barley during seed germination originates from the synergistic interaction between beneficial endophytes (Sphingomonas, Pelomonas), GA, and core genes (e.g., LEA, Homeobox). Exogenous GA application can significantly restore the germination ability of cold-sensitive varieties. These findings provide a critical theoretical basis for improving cold tolerance in hulless barley germplasm.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12124-5.

Background

Hulless barley (Hordeum vulgare var. coeleste L.), colloquially referred to as “qingke” in China, is well-adapted to high-altitude regions of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, where its exceptional tolerance to cold, hypoxia, and poor soil conditions renders it a vital staple that sustains local communities. Barley ranks as the world’s fourth largest cereal crop, boasting a cultivation history of over 4,000 years [1]. With a genome size of approximately 4.5 Gb and over 36,151 protein-coding genes [2], hulless barley exhibits substantial genetic diversity, particularly in its resistance to low temperatures, salinity, and other environmental challenges [3]. Seed germination marks the onset of crop development, and exposure to low temperatures often results in delayed germination and hindered seedling growth, ultimately impacting final yield and quality [4]. Globally, the escalating occurrence of spring frost events due to climate change poses a heightened risk of cold stress on hulless barley production. Therefore, a comprehensive exploration of the molecular mechanisms governing hulless barley seed germination under cold stress is imperative for developing resilient varieties and safeguarding food security.

Low temperatures have a detrimental impact on seed germination and seedling establishment in crops like hulless barley due to disruptions in cell membrane integrity, excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and interference with energy metabolic pathways [5]. Hulless barley responds to low-temperature stress by activating cold-responsive genes, such as the COLD-REGULATED (COR) gene, modulating the balance between abscisic acid (ABA) and gibberellin (GA), and enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity [6]. GA, a key hormone governing seed germination, functions by alleviating dormancy, facilitating endosperm degradation, and promoting cell elongation [7]. However, GA biosynthesis and signaling are significantly impaired under low temperature conditions, leading to reduced expression of crucial GA biosynthesis genes like GA 3-oxidase (GA3ox) and GA 20-oxidase (GA20ox), resulting in decreased active GA levels and delayed germination [7]. Application of an amount of exogenous GA (e.g., 300 mg/L) substantially enhances the germination rate and potential of rapeseed seeds, particularly under low temperatures [8]. Cold-resistant hulless barley varieties elevate active GA content by upregulating GA synthesis genes (e.g., GA3ox2 and GA20ox1) at low temperatures, thereby accelerating α-amylase activation, endosperm nutrient mobilization, and promoting germination [9]. For instance, transgenic hulless barley overexpressing the HvGA3ox2 gene exhibited a 40% higher germination rate at 5℃ compared to the wild type, along with more vigorous seedling growth [10]. The interplay between GA and ABA is critical for cold tolerance regulation. Cold-resistant varieties optimize the GA-ABA balance, reducing the ABA/GA ratio to counteract the inhibitory effects of ABA on germination effectively [11].

Seed-associated microbes are crucial for enhancing plant stress tolerance. Recent studies utilizing 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing have revealed that the endophytic microbial composition in hulless barley seeds is notably impacted by genetic factors, with Pantoea, Curtobacterium, and Pseudomonas being among the core microbiota, which contribute to the cold resilience of the host plant by synthesizing phytohormones such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), antioxidant enzymes, and extracellular polysaccharides [12]. Notably, key microbes like Sphingobium and Mortierella, recruited in response to drought conditions in the sub-root zone, have been shown to bolster the cold tolerance of hulless barley roots by stimulating glutathione accumulation [13]. Furthermore, the seed microbiome can indirectly enhance cold tolerance by modulating the expression of host genes. For instance, seed endophytes can trigger the upregulation of defense-related genes like Pathogenesis related (PR-1) [14] and Plant defensin (PDF1.2) [15], thereby fortifying hulless barley’s ability to withstand low-temperature stress.

Transcriptome analysis is a crucial method for elucidating cold tolerance mechanisms in plants [16]. Recent studies have utilized RNA-seq technology to identify pivotal genes and pathways involved in the response to low temperatures during hulless barley seed germination. For instance, in hulless barley, exposure to low temperature stress has been found to up-regulate the CBF (C-repeat binding factor) gene family. Among these, Hvcbf10a has been shown to enhance cold tolerance by modulating lipid metabolism-related genes such as HvGDSL (GDSL-type esterase/lipase) [17]. Furthermore, the application of split-root zone drought irrigation has been shown to enhance the antioxidant capacity of hulless barley roots by up-regulating genes involved in glutathione synthesis (e.g., Glutathione synthesis, GSH1) and those encoding antioxidant enzymes (e.g., Ascorbate peroxidas, APX)-thereby improving the crop’s low-temperature resilience [13]. Transcriptomic analysis has revealed a notable enrichment of genes linked to carbohydrate metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and redox processes under low-temperature conditions, underscoring the significance of energy metabolism and membrane lipid remodeling in the response to cold stress [18]. Particularly noteworthy is the pivotal role of chloroplasts in low-temperature signaling, as evidenced by the cold-sensitive phenotype observed in mutants lacking functional chloroplasts (e.g., albina and xantha), which also exhibited suppressed activation of the CBF pathway [18].

However, most studies have focused on isolated roles of hormones, endophytes, or transcriptomic changes in cold tolerance, with few integrating these three components to explore their synergistic interactions. To address this gap, a cold-tolerant hulless barley variety 37 and a cold-sensitive hulless barley variety 44 were selected in this study. Germination rates were assessed at various temperatures. Additionally, seed samples were collected three days post-treatment for phytohormone content analysis. 16 S rRNA sequencing and transcriptome analysis were conducted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying hulless barley’s cold tolerance. These findings aim to establish a scientific foundation for enhancing cold tolerance in hulless barley germplasm through cultivation practices.

Methods

Experimental design

In this investigation, a total of 151 hulless barley varieties sourced from diverse regions were stored at room temperature (22℃) in the Biotechnology Laboratory of the School of Agriculture and Bioengineering, Longdong University and used to test germination rate (Table S1). Seeds meeting criteria of full grains, uniform size, and free from pests and diseases were selected. These seeds were disinfected with 5% sodium hypochlorite for 10 min, rinsed thrice with distilled water, and then evenly distributed in petri dishes lined with two layers of filter paper. Subsequently, petri dishes were placed in a constant-temperature light incubator set at 4℃ (cold-stress) after the addition of an appropriate amount of distilled water. The photoperiod consisted of 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness, with a relative humidity of approximately 70%. This process was replicated three times, each with 50 seeds variety. Daily monitoring of seed germination was conducted, and the white tip emergence of seeds was taken as the standard for germination. The germination rate was calculated using the formula: germination rate (%) = (number of germinated seeds on day 7/total number of test seeds) × 100%. Two varieties, cold-tolerant 37 and cold-sensitive 44 were selected from 151 hulless barley varieties based on the germonation rate under cold stress for further study. The seeds of two hulless barley varieties were incubated at two different temperatures-normal (22℃) and cold (4℃)-for a continuous 3-day period (It was determined through pre-experiments that incubating the seeds for 3 consecutive days could allow sufficient time for the seeds to exhibit distinct phenotypic and physiological responses to different temperature conditions.), after which the seed samples were collected to quantify phytohormone content, extract total RNA for transcriptome sequencing, and isolate total DNA for 16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing (Fig. 1).

To validate the role of GA in enhancing cold-tolerant germination of hulless barley seeds, cold-tolerant variety 37 and cold-sensitive variety 44 were used as experimental materials. Following a previously described seed pretreatment protocol, 50 surface-sterilized seeds per replicate were soaked in a 100 mg/L GA3 solution [19] and incubated in darkness at 25 °C for 6 h. Post-imbibition, seeds were distributed in Petri dishes lined with two layers of filter paper, each receiving 10 mL of the same GA3 solution to maintain moisture. These dishes were then placed in a constant-temperature incubator at 4 °C, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and 70% relative humidity. Three biological replicates were established for each treatment. Seed germination was monitored daily from day 1 to day 7 and germination rate was calculated by the same method as described earlier.

Determination of hormone content

The ground hulless barley seed sample weighing 0.2 g was measured and combined with ten volumes of acetonitrile solution. The mixture was then incubated overnight at 4℃, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. The precipitate underwent two additional extractions with five volumes of acetonitrile each. The combined supernatants were passed through a C18 solid-phase extraction column, vigorously shaken for 30 s, and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 5 min. Subsequently, all supernatants were consolidated, concentrated, and dried using vacuum centrifugation. The dried residue was reconstituted with 200 µL of methanol, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and stored at −20℃ until analysis.

Liquid chromatographic conditions involved the utilization of a Poroshell 120 SB-C18 reversed-phase column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 2.7 μm) (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, United States) maintained at 30℃. The mobile phase consisted of a mixture of methanol with 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (A) and water with 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (B), with an elution gradient specified in Table 1. The injection volume was 2 µL.

Mass spectrometry parameters included monitoring ionization in both electrospray ionization (ESI) positive and negative ion modes, utilizing multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) scan type. The air curtain gas was set at 15 psi, with spray voltages of + 4,500 V and − 4,000 V. Nebulization gas pressure was maintained at 65 psi, while auxiliary gas pressure was set to 70 psi, and the nebulization temperature was set to 400℃.

The instrument measures the concentration of the final extract, from which the hormone content in the original sample is determined using the formula: Hormone content in the sample (ng/g) = detection concentration (ng/mL) × dilution volume (mL)/weighing mass (g). The dilution volume refers to the volume of the solution utilized for the sample’s ultimate dissolution, while the weighing mass represents the mass of the sample at the extraction moment. The detection concentration is automatically derived from the instrument by inputting the peak area of the substance to be analyzed into the standardized equation.

16 S rRNA sequencing

Microbiome DNA was extracted from 100 hulless barley seeds using the CTAB method and assessed for quality via agarose gel electrophoresis, with DNA quantification done using a UV spectrophotometer. This DNA served as a template for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification with primers 341 F (5’-CCTACGGGGNGGCWGCAG-3’) and 805R (5’-GACTACHVGGGGTATCTAATCC-3’) using the Phusion Hot start flex 2X Master Mix. The PCR reaction mixture contained 12.5 µL of the Master Mix, 2.5 µL each of the primers (10 µmol/L), 50 ng of template DNA, and Sterilized ddH2O to reach a final volume of 25 µL. The PCR program included pre-denaturation at 98℃ for 30 s, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 98℃ for 10 s, annealing at 54℃ for 30 s, and extension at 72℃ for 45 s, with a final extension at 72℃ for 10 min. The PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel and purified using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Brea, United States). Purified products were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and Illumina library quantification kits for libraries construction. Qualified libraries (concentration > 2 nM) were diluted, denatured, and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencer for 2 × 250 bp paired-end sequencing. Subsequent data processing involved demultiplexing, quality control, and denoising using the DADA2 plugin in QIIME2 to generate Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) and their abundance tables. Alpha and Beta diversity analyses were conducted based on the ASV data. ASVs were annotated using the SILVA database (Release 138) for species classification, and statistical analysis of species abundance in each sample was performed. The Mann-Whitney U test was applied to conduct species difference analysis based on the genus-level species abundance in each sample, and species with a P-value less than 0.05 were classified as differential ASVs. The PICRUSt2 (v2.6.0) was used to predict the functions of ASVs in cold-tolerant varity 37 and cold-sensitive variety 44. The t-test method was applied to analyse significant functional items with a P-value < 0.05.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 50 seeds per sample of two hulless barley varieties (cold-tolerant variety 37 and cold-sensitive variety 44) using the TRIzol method (Thermo Fisher, cat. 15596018), followed by isolation and purification. The concentration and purity of the total RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop ND-100 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE, USA), while RNA integrity was evaluated with a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, CA, USA) to ensure a concentration > 50 ng/µL and RNA integrity number (RIN) value > 8.0 Samples meeting the criteria of RNA concentration > 50 ng/µL, RIN value > 8.0, and total RNA > 1 µg were deemed suitable for subsequent experiments.

Polyadenylated mRNA was enriched through two rounds of purification using oligo(dT) magnetic beads (Dynabeads Oligo (dT), cat. 25-61005, Thermo Fisher, USA). The enriched mRNAs were fragmented at 94℃ for 5–7 min using the NEBNextR Magnesium RNA Fragmentation Module (NEB, cat. E6150S, USA). Subsequently, the fragmented RNA (1 µg, 50 ng/µL) underwent reverse transcription using the Invitrogen SuperScriptTM II Reverse Transcriptase (cat. 1896649, CA, USA) to generate cDNA. The cDNA was synthesized using E. coli DNA polymerase I (NEB, cat. m0209, USA) and RNase H (NEB, cat. m0297, USA) for double-stranded synthesis and conversion into DNA duplexes. The duplexes were end-repaired by incorporating dUTP Solution (Thermo Fisher, cat. R0133, CA, USA) to generate blunt ends, followed by addition of an A base at each end for subsequent ligation to adapters with a T base overhang. The resulting fragments were size-selected and purified using AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences, Brea, United States).

A library with fragment sizes of 300 bp ± 50 bp was constructed through PCR amplification: initial denaturation at 95℃ for 3 min, followed by 8 cycles of denaturation at 98℃ for 15 s, annealing at 60℃ for 15 s, and extension at 72℃ for 30 s, with a final extension step at 72℃ for 5 min. The constructed library was sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 platform (LC Bio Technology CO., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) using the manufacturer’s recommended protocol under PE150 paired-end mode.

Initially, sequencing data underwent quality control using FastQC, followed by Trimmomatic for filtering and removal of splice sequences. Subsequently, the hulless barley genome file (https://ftp.ensemblgenomes.ebi.ac.uk/pub/plants/release-61/fasta/hordeum_vulgare/) was indexed using Hisat2 (v2.2.10) to create indexes for comparison with the transcriptome data, resulting in SAM files. Conversion of SAM files to BAM files and sorting were accomplished using Samtools (v0.1.19). The BAM files were then utilized for assembly with Stringtie (v1.3.3) to produce gff files for estimating transcript expression abundance, quantified by Fragments Per Kilobase of exon per Million mapped fragments (FPKM) values. Genes exhibiting FPKM values > 1 were classified as expressed genes. Subsequently, differential expression analysis was conducted on the expressed genes employing DESeq2 (v3.11) software to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), applying criteria of |log2(Fold Change)| ≥ 3 and adjusted P-value < 0.01.

WGCNA analysis

The co-expression network analysis of expressed genes and trait values was conducted using the WGCNA (v0.0.6.230118) plugin within the Tbtools (v2.034) software. Initially, genes with FPKM > 1 expressed in at least 90% of the samples were selected from the transcriptome for analysis. Subsequently, cluster analysis was employed to identify and remove outlier samples. To build a scale-free topology, we first scanned soft-threshold powers and selected the smallest value that yielded an R² ≥ 0.8 or had reached a plateau. With this power, modules were identified under the WGCNA default parameters: minimum module size = 30 genes, cut height for module merging = 0.25, which ultimately grouped the differentially expressed genes into 8 modules. Subsequently, correlation analysis was performed between the characterized phenotypic data and modules, identifying modules significantly associated with the GA content of the target traits based on module eigengene (ME) values. The selected modules underwent gene function enrichment analysis using the AgriGO v2 online platform (http://systemsbiology.cau.edu.cn/agriGOv2) to elucidate their biological functions. To identify core genes linked to cold-resistant germination, a regulatory network was visualized using Cytoscape (v3.10.1) software, with target genes selected based on their significance and connectivity within the modules.

Real-time quantitative PCR

To validate the 10 core genes identified, we assessed their expression in cold-tolerant and cold-sensitive hulless barley subjected to mock (22℃) and cold (4℃) treatments. RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596026), followed by cDNA synthesis using the PrimeScript® RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). The cDNA served as a template for quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis, with three replicates per sample. The qRT-PCR protocol included an initial 30 s pre-denaturation at 95℃, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95℃ for 5 s and annealing/extension at 58℃ for 30 s. The melting curve analysis comprised stages at 95℃ for 15 s, 58℃ for 30 s, and 95℃ for 15 s. Relative gene expression was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method with HvActin (accession number: AY145451) as the internal reference gene. Primers for qRT-PCR were designed using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool [20], and their sequences are detailed in Table S2.

Results

Inhibition of hulless barley seed germination by low-temperature stress

Hulless barley seed germination was severely impeded by low-temperature stress. Figure 2A illustrates that cold-tolerant variety 37 exhibited minimal sensitivity to cold stress, with the majority of seeds germinating normally, whereas cold-sensitive variety 44 experienced a substantial reduction in germination rate at 4℃ relative to the control. In Fig. 2B, the germination rate of cold-tolerant hulless barley variety 37 was 100% and 98% under mock and cold conditions, respectively. The germination rate decreased by 2% under cold stress compared to the room temperature control, with a t-test P-value of 0.02572, signifying a highly significant difference. Conversely, cold-sensitive variety 44 displayed a germination rate of 99% at room temperature, which significantly dropped to 30% under cold treatment, with a P-value of 1.18 × 10−7, indicating a highly significant inhibitory effect of cold stress on the germination rate of the cold-sensitive variety.

The role of gibberellic acid in regulating hulless barley seed germination under cold stress

To elucidate the hormonal response to cold stress, we analyzed the contents of gibberellin (GA), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), jasmonic acid (JA), salicylic acid (SA), trans-zeatin (t-zeatin), and abscisic acid (ABA) in the seeds of hulless barley varieties 37 and 44 following a 3-day cold treatment. Our findings revealed that following cold stress, both cold-tolerant variety 37 and cold-sensitive variety 44 exhibited significantly elevated levels of IAA (Fig. 3B), JA (Fig. 3C), SA (Fig. 3D), and ABA (Fig. 3F) relative to mock treatment. Notably, GA and t-zeatin contents exhibited distinct inter-varietal variations under cold stress: variety 37 displayed a substantial 492.33% increase in GA content (0.0965 to 0.5716 ng/g) and a non-significant 13.62% decrease in t-zeatin content (0.0257 to 0.0222 ng/g) between mock and cold stress treatments. In contrast, variety 44 showed a significant 26.62% decrease in GA content (0.3513 to 0.2578 ng/g) and a highly significant 506.06% increase in t-zeatin content (0.0033 to 0.020 ng/g) under similar conditions (Fig. 3A, E). These results underscore the pivotal role of GA in regulating hulless barley seed germination in response to cold stress.

To investigate the potential of gibberellic acid (GA) in enhancing seed germination, germination tests were conducted on cold-tolerant variety 37 and cold-sensitive variety 44 under cold stress, with the application of 100 mg/L exogenous GA. Figure 2A and B illustrate that the addition of exogenous GA led to a significant increase in the germination rate of both hulless barley varieties under cold stress. Specifically, the germination rates for cold-tolerant variety 37 and cold-sensitive variety 44 were 93% and 83%, respectively. For the cold-sensitive variety 44, exogenous GA treatment resulted in a marked 176.67% increase in germination rate compared to the cold-stress treatment without GA supplementation.

Identification of seed endophytes after cold stress

Seed endophytic bacteria play a crucial role in seed germination. Three days after the mock treatment and cold stress treatment, seeds were gathered and subjected to 16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing. Evaluation of sequencing quality revealed a Q30% exceeding 93% across all samples (Table S3), ensuring reliable sequencing quality for subsequent analyses. Alpha diversity, which characterizes diversity within a specific environment or ecosystem, primarily reflects species richness, evenness, and sequencing depth. Analysis of alpha diversity indicated that the Good’s coverage index approached 1 for all samples, signifying adequate sequencing depth to encompass the majority of microbial species present (Table S4). The range of Observed ASVs varied from 174 to 269 in all samples except mock_37_1 and mock_44_3. Leveraging species annotation data from the SILVA database (Release 138), the genus-level composition of the top 30 genera by relative abundance was depicted for all samples (Fig. 4A). Subsequent examination focused on changes in the relative abundance of the five predominant genera (Comamonas, Pelomonas, Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas, and Chloroplast_unclassified) (Fig. 4B). Findings revealed that exposure to low temperatures led to a notable increase in both Comamonas and Chloroplast_unclassified sequences in varieties 37 and 44, accompanied by a marked decrease in Pseudomonas abundance. Noteworthy varietal distinctions were observed in the abundance shifts of Pelomonas and Sphingomonas under cold stress: while both increased significantly in the cold-tolerant variety 37, they decreased notably in the cold-sensitive variety 44.

The correlation between phytohormone content and the prevalence of the five major bacterial genera is depicted in Fig. 4C. Specifically, a significant positive correlation was observed between the levels of gibberellic acid (GA) and the abundance of Sphingomonas and Pelomonas, with correlation coefficients of 0.70 and 0.87, respectively. This suggests a potential association of these genera with the promotion of seed germination mediated by GA. Under cold stress conditions, as illustrated in Fig. 4D, there was a notable increase of 64.73% in the relative abundance of Pelomonas in the cold-tolerant variety 37 compared to its control, whereas a significant decrease of 29.30% was observed in the cold-sensitive variety 44. A similar trend was observed for Sphingomonas, with a substantial increase of 118.74% in abundance under cold stress in variety 37, and a significant decrease of 44.49% in variety 44.

Functional prediction analysis revealed that in the salt-tolerant variety 37 (Fig. S1), the pathway for “Chorismate biosynthesis from 3-dehydroquinoline” was implicated in Chorismate production. This pathway also led to the synthesis of auxin precursors, known for their role in scavenging reactive oxygen species and mitigating oxidative stress. Furthermore, pathways such as “D-galactose degradation I”, “D-galactose degradation I”, and the “Superpathway of D-galactose and D-galactose degradation”, integral components of the uronic acid metabolic pathway, directly contributed to enhancing plant stress tolerance. Additionally, the pathway for “Guanosine ribotides de novo biosynthesis”, a key process in purine nucleotide metabolism, played a crucial role in GA signal transduction and the activation of antifreeze genes. Moreover, the “Superpathway of pyridoxal 5’-phosphate biosynthesis and salvage” pathway facilitated the synthesis and regeneration of pyridoxal 5’-phosphate (PLP), essential for catalyzing P450 monooxygenase activity and promoting GA synthesis. In contrast, in the cold-sensitive variety 44, the predicted functions mainly involved bacteria-specific or less prominent plant metabolic pathways, with no significant association observed between GA signaling and cold tolerance (Fig. S2).

WGCNA analysis for mining core genes related to seed germination

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying cold-tolerant germination in hulless barley, transcriptome sequencing was conducted on seeds subjected to a 3-day mock treatment and cold stress treatment. In the cold-tolerant variety 37, 2,647 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, comprising 360 up-regulated and 2,287 down-regulated genes (Fig. 5A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of these DEGs in pathways associated with stress responses, such as ‘cold acclimation (GO:0009631)’, ‘systemic acquired resistance (GO:0009627)’, ‘response to oxidative stress (GO:0006979)’, and ‘response to cold (GO:0009409)’ (Fig. 5B). In contrast, the cold-sensitive variety 44 exhibited 2,392 DEGs, with 660 up-regulated and 1,732 down-regulated genes (Fig. 5A). GO analysis highlighted enrichment in pathways related to ‘response to oxidative stress (GO:0006979)’, ‘L-arabinose metabolic process (GO:0046373)’, and ‘sucrose catabolic process (GO:0005987)’ (Fig. S3). These findings suggest that, compared to the cold-sensitive variety 44, the cold-tolerant variety 37 not only activated gene expression in response to cold stress but also triggered a broader spectrum of systemic defense responses to support seed germination.

To identify core genes associated with cold-resistant germination of hulless barley seeds regulated by gibberellic acid (GA), we conducted weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) using GA content as the target trait. The soft threshold was set to 22 for network construction (Fig. S4 and S5). Genes were grouped into eight modules based on their expression profiles under various treatments (Fig. S6). Correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation (correlation coefficient = 0.82, P = 0.0011) between the gene expression pattern of the green module and GA content, indicating their involvement in GA-regulated seed germination (Fig. 5C). By analyzing the topological overlap matrix (TOM) and connectivity of modular genes, we identified ten candidate core genes through visualization analysis in Cytoscape (Fig. 5D). These genes include two late embryogenesis abundant protein genes (LEA, HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG0380150, HORVU.MOREX.r3.3HG0305440), a homeobox protein gene (HORVU.MOREX.r3.6HG0559530), a glycosyltransferase gene (GT, HORVU.MOREX.r3.2HG0118820), a high affinity nitrate transporter gene (NRT, HORVU.MOREX.r3.6HG0591170), a detoxification protein gene (HORVU.MOREX.r3.4HG0341050), an anthocyanin 5-aromatic acyltransferase gene (5AT, HORVU.MOREX.r3.7HG0696550), an aluminum-activated malate transporter-like gene (ALMT, HORVU.MOREX.r3.7HG0710910), an aspartic proteinase nepenthesin-1 gene (APN, HORVU.MOREX.r3.7HG0640320), and a FBD-associated F-box protein gene (HORVU.MOREX.r3.7HG0725660). Validation through quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) demonstrated that cold stress up-regulated the expression of these ten genes in both hulless barley varieties, with significantly higher up-regulation observed in the cold-tolerant variety 37 compared to the cold-sensitive variety 44 (Fig. S7), consistent with the transcriptome sequencing results.

In addition, we performed a correlation analysis between the 10 screened cold-tolerance-related core genes and the abundances of the dominant bacterial taxa (Pelomonas, Sphingomonas) and the non-dominant taxon (Pseudomonas) in the cold-tolerant variety 37 (Fig. S8). The results revealed that Pelomonas and Sphingomonas exhibited predominatly positive correlations with these genes, with the overall correlation coefficients of Pelomonas generally higher than those of Sphingomonas. In contrast, Pseudomonas showed mostly negative correlations with the genes (serving as the negative control group). This suggests that under cold stress, the seed endophytes Pelomonas and Sphingomonas may exert their functions by promoting the expression of core genes, though this mechanism rquires further validation.

Discussion

Hulless barley, recognized as “the last crop before desertification,” thrives in harsh conditions and is predominantly found in high-altitude regions such as Tibet and Qinghai in China, serving as a crucial grain crop. With the increasing frequency of extreme weather events, improving hulless barley seed germination under low temperatures has become a key focus of research. Perea-Brenes et al. demonstrated that plasma treatment significantly boosts the germination rate of hulless barley under cold stress and other adverse conditions [21]. Chang et al. highlighted the role of exogenous melatonin in modulating antioxidant enzyme activity to counter cold stress [22]. Panahi et al. underscored the significance of the ABA signaling pathway, ROS signaling, and Ca2+-related signaling in responding to cold stress in hulless barley [23]. Additionally, Kour et al. identified the beneficial effects of the Serratia nematophilic EU-PE75 in enhancing hulless barley growth under low-temperature stress [24]. Our study elucidates the pivotal functions and interactions of the GA signaling pathway, core gene networks, and specific endophytic flora in regulating the cold-tolerant germination of hulless barley. This was achieved through a comparative analysis of physiological, transcriptomic, and microbiomic characteristics between the cold-tolerant hulless barley cultivar 37 and the cold-sensitive cultivar 44 during the low-temperature germination phase.

GA promotes hulless barley seed germination under low temperature stress

During seed germination, low temperatures delay seed swelling and water absorption, and weaken enzyme activity related to seed germination, thereby inhibiting germination [25]. In this study, low-temperature stress significantly decreased the germination rate of hulless barley, with a more pronounced effect observed in the cold-sensitive variety 44 than in the cold-tolerant variety 37 (Fig. 2). Tarnawa et al. reported that hulless barley seeds exhibited the highest germination rate (97.5%) at 5℃, lower at 20℃ (86.5%), and no germination at 35℃ [26]. This contrasts with our findings where germination at 4℃ was lower than at 22℃, likely due to differences in experimental materials. Mei et al. identified the optimal germination temperature for hulless barley as 15–20℃, with 0℃ pretreated hulless barley seeds showing enhanced tolerance to high temperatures or salt stress [27].

Gibberellic acid (GA), a key phytohormone in the regulation of seed germination, counteracts the inhibitory effects of abscisic acid to break seed dormancy and promotes germination by inducing the synthesis or release of hydrolase, or by interacting with other phytohormones [28]. Exogenous GA treatment (Fig. 2) enhanced the germination rate of two hulless barley varieties under cold stress. Zhu et al. conducted a seed germination test on cold-tolerant oilseed rape ZY15 and cold-sensitive oilseed rape HY49, observing a decreasing trend in germination rate under cold stress, with ZY15 showing a smaller decrease than HY49. Pretreatment with 300 mg/L GA significantly increased the germination rate of both varieties, particularly HY49, with no significant difference in germination rates between control and low-temperature treatments [8]. Zhang et al. demonstrated that cold stress inhibited tomato seed germination, but exogenous betaine treatment increased GA content and antioxidant enzyme activity, thereby enhancing tomato seed germination under cold stress [29]. Cui et al. found that a growth regulator solution (containing gibberellin, indole-3-acetic acid, and oleuropein lactone) at 100 mg/mL promoted maize seed germination and seedling growth under cold stress [30]. Li et al. showed that exogenous GA3 pretreatment of winter wheat seeds improved germination, increased amylase activity for starch degradation, and promoted seedling growth [31]. Xia et al. compared the effects of exogenous substances (hydrogen sulfide, nitric oxide, 2,4-epi-oleuropein lactone, and GA3) on cotton seed germination under low temperatures. They found that GA3 enhanced enzyme activities, reduced the accumulation of reactive oxygen species, and improved low-temperature tolerance during germination [32]. These findings collectively underscore the crucial role of GA in mitigating low-temperature stress during crop germination.

Role of the seed endophytes Sphingomonas and Pelomonas in cold-tolerant germination

Seed endophytes-microorganisms that form a mutualistic symbiosis with host plants-play a crucial role in enhancing seed germination and growth under adverse conditions through various mechanisms. For instance, Lai et al. discovered that the Pseudomonas sp. CC-LS37 could increase endogenous GA levels in rice seeds under low temperatures, leading to an 18% higher germination rate [33]. Furthermore, endophytes can alleviate seed dormancy by either suppressing ABA synthesis or degrading ABA. For example, the wheat endophyte Bacillus can expedite seed germination by down-regulating the expression of the ABA synthesis gene NCED (9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase) [34].

In our investigation, we observed a significant enrichment of bacteria belonging to the genera Sphingomonas and Pelomonas in the cold-tolerant variety 37 under cold stress through 16 S rRNA gene sequencing (Fig. 4B, D). The abundance of these bacteria exhibited a strong positive correlation with GA content (Fig. 4C). Sphingomonas has been shown to produce indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) via a tryptophan-dependent pathway, directly stimulating radicle elongation and cell division. For instance, Sphingobium yanoikuyae SJTF8 enhanced the germination rate and seedling biomass in rice seeds by secreting IAA, resulting in a 20–30% increase in germination rate compared to the control group [35]. Halo et al. demonstrated that Sphingomonas sp. LK11 synthesized GA4 under salt stress, which increased GA content and counteracted the inhibitory effects of ABA by enhancing the GA signaling pathway. Co-treatment of this strain with exogenous GA4 in tomato seeds restored the germination rate under salt stress to 85% of the control level while reducing ABA content [36].

Moreover, Sultana et al. utilized S. panaciterrae PB20 to treat spinach seeds, resulting in a 2–3 days reduction in germination time, a 60% increase in germination uniformity, and enhanced seedling tolerance to drought and salinity [37]. Matsumoto et al. illustrated that Sphingomonas melonis interfered with the synthesis of Burkholderia plantarii’s virulence factors by secreting aminophenolic acid, thereby reducing seed infestation. This colonization decreased the incidence of bacterial spike blight by over 50% in rice seeds, indirectly promoting germination [38].

Notably, our study is the first to report the association of Pelomonas endophytes with seed germination under cold stress in a graminaceous crop (hulless barley). Pelomonas aquatica A1S1S, isolated from six stress-resistant highland plants by Kaur et al., demonstrated the ability to produce high levels of gibberellins, offering a novel approach to reduce reliance on chemical fertilizers [39]. Ishizawa et al. discovered that the Pelomonas saccharophila MRB3 strain could lyse cells spontaneously to release NH4+ and PO43− ions, promoting puffball growth. This growth-promoting effect was confirmed across various inoculum levels and media, including natural pond water [40]. Additionally, Makino et al. isolated four plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) strains, among which Pelomonas saccharophila MRB3 significantly enhanced duckweed growth, increasing biomass and chlorophyll content [41]. Wang et al. compared the endophytic bacterial communities of two heteromorphic seed types (brown and black) of the saline-alkali plant Suaeda glauca. They observed that brown seeds were enriched with Pelomonas and other facultative bacteria, while black seeds harbored Marinilactibacillus and other enzyme-producing bacteria. Disparities in seed shell structure (e.g., hard outer seed coat of black seeds) and chemical properties (e.g., high phenolic content of brown seeds) influenced seed germination and adaptation to adverse conditions [42].

However, the 16 S rRNA sequencing data only reflects the correlation between the abundance of Sphingomonas/Pelomonas and hulless barley cold-tolerant germination, rather than establishing a direct causal relationship. To validate the role of Sphingomonas and Pelomonas in enhancing cold-tolerant germination, follow-up experiments are needed (e.g., inoculating these two endophyte strains into axenic hulless barley seeds and comparing germination rates, GA content, and related gene expression with non-inoculated controls under cold stress).

Functional analysis of core genes for cold-resistant germination

Transcriptome sequencing is a crucial technology for identifying candidate genes responsive to low-temperature germination, and it has been extensively applied in crops such as hulless barley [43], wheat [44], rice [45], and maize [46]. However, conventional differential gene analysis tools, such as DESeq2 and edgeR, primarily focus on individual gene expression changes. For studies with large sample sizes and complex groupings, these traditional approaches (e.g., differential expression analysis and pathway enrichment analysis) often fail to fully uncover the synergistic regulatory patterns of gene networks. In this investigation, we successfully established gene co-expression modules associated with gibberellic acid (GA) content using the WGCNA method, from which we identified 10 core genes (Fig. 5). Notably, the late embryogenesis abundant (LEA) protein was found to safeguard cellular structures and proteins during seed germination under abiotic stress [7, 47]. The high-affinity nitrate transporter (NRT) gene encodes a protein located in the cell membrane, and this protein has been implicated in lateral root development under adverse conditions [48]. For instance, in larkspur (Lotus japonicus), the LjNRT2.1 gene stimulates lateral root meristemization by activating the NIN-LIKE PROTEIN (NLP4) transcription factor [49]. Aluminum-activated malate transporter-like (ALMTL) genes encode proteins located on the cell membrane; these proteins enhance the secretion of Ca2+ and malic acid, thereby improving nutrient uptake and seed vigor under stress [50]. Overexpression of the Homeobox gene in Arabidopsis boosts seed germination, with its homolog in soybean regulated by gibberellins [51]. Additionally, the Arabidopsis homeobox gene AtHB25 directly activates GA biosynthesis genes, elevating GA1 and GA4 levels in seeds, thereby promoting germination and extending seed viability [52]. The F-box protein gene associated with F-box Domain (FBD) negatively modulates ABA-mediated germination inhibition by suppressing core transcription factors ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE (ABI3) and ABI5 in the ABA signaling pathway [53]. Studies have shown that anthocyanins, as potent antioxidants in plants, play a crucial role in scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) during seed germination under abiotic stress. For instance, the maize Bronze (ZmBZ1) gene encoding anthocyanin 3-O-glucosyltransferase enhances ROS scavenging enzyme activities, mitigating oxidative damage under stress [54]. Moreover, the DELLA protein indirectly up-regulates anthocyanin 5-aromatic acyltransferase (5AT) expression, enhancing ROS scavenging and promoting seed germination by counteracting the inhibitory effects of MYBL2 and Jasmonate ZIM-domain (JAZ) proteins on anthocyanin synthesis [55]. Glycosyltransferases, such as UGT75D1 and UGT71C5, are involved in glycosylation modifications of phytohormones like ABA and GA, regulating hormone activities and influencing seed germination and stress responses [56]– [57]. Furthermore, the aspartic protease gene, Aspartic protease nepenthesin-1, identified in this study, has been shown to inhibit toxin production by degrading fungal phytase, indirectly facilitating seed germination [58]. This suggests a potential dual role of this gene in both plant defense and the regulation of germination. Notably, these 10 core genes were identified solely via bioinformatics approaches (WGCNA and correlation with GA content). No direct functional validation experiments have been performed to confirm their roles in hulless barley cold-tolerant germination. Such validation-using techniques like CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing, virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS), or transient overexpression-is essential to confirm the core genes’ role in cold-tolerant germination.

The working model of GA-mediated seed germination

Global climate change has significantly impeded hulless barley seed germination under low-temperature stress. Identifying cold-tolerant hulless barley varieties and elucidating their cold-tolerance mechanisms are crucial for developing cold-resistant crop germplasm. Our study revealed a positive correlation between the seed endophytes Sphingomonas and Pelomonas and gibberellic acid (GA) content. Transcriptome and amplicon sequencing analyses following low-temperature stress exposure demonstrated that cold stress triggers reactive oxygen species (ROS) production within seeds, consequently stimulating GA synthesis as a signaling mechanism (Fig. 6). Elevated GA levels activate the calcium (Ca2+) signaling pathway, leading to the upregulation of Calcineurin B-like (CBL7) and Ca2+-dependent protein kinases (CDPKs). CBL7 induces NRT gene expression, ultimately promoting seed germination. CDPKs modulate AMLT genes on the cell membrane, facilitating the transport of calcium and malic acid ions extracellularly, and regulate CBF or Dehydration-Responsive Element Binding factor (DREB) genes in the nucleus to enhance downstream LEA gene expression, thereby promoting seed germination. Intracellular ROS bursts also influence CDPKs and Enolase (ENO2), the latter of which promotes DREB gene expression for seed germination. Additionally, Homeobox gene expression under cold stress activates DREB gene expression. When ROS accumulation jeopardizes cellular functions, phenylpropanoid acids synthesize anthocyanins via the 5AT gene, and flavonoids are produced through glycosylation by glycosyltransferases to mitigate ROS-induced cellular damage. Furthermore, intracellular detoxification gene expression aids in superoxide anion removal.

Conclusions

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive comparison of the physiological responses, transcriptome profiles, and microbiome alterations between the cold-tolerant hulless barley variety 37 and the cold-sensitive variety 44 during low-temperature germination. Our investigation aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms and microbial regulatory pathways underlying cold tolerance in hulless barley seed germination. Our findings revealed that low-temperature stress markedly inhibited hulless barley seed germination, with a more pronounced effect observed in the cold-sensitive variety 44. Notably, gibberellin (GA) emerged as a key player in regulating cold tolerance, with the cold-tolerant variety 37 exhibiting enhanced seed germination through the maintenance of elevated GA levels. Furthermore, exogenous GA treatment notably improved seed germination under cold stress conditions, particularly in the cold-sensitive variety 44. Analysis of the seed endophytic microbiota revealed that the cold-tolerant variety 37 exhibited a significant enrichment of the predominant flora Sphingomonas and Pelomonas under cold stress conditions. The abundance of these flora displayed a significant and positive correlation with GA content. Through transcriptome analysis coupled with weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), we identified 10 core genes (e.g., LEA, Homeobox, NRT, GT, Detoxification, 5AT, ALMT, APN1, FBP) that exhibited significant and positive correlations with GA content. These genes were up-regulated to a greater extent in the cold-tolerant variety 37 and were implicated in pathways related to stress responses, hormone metabolism, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging. In summary, our study elucidated the synergistic regulatory mechanism of GA-mediated cold-resistant germination in hulless barley, encompassing hormone regulation, core gene networks, and endophytic interactions. These findings offer a valuable theoretical foundation and genetic reservoir for the breeding of cold-resistant crop germplasm.
