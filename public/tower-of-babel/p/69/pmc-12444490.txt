Long-term primary pharmacotherapy of giant prolactinomas: A comparison of different cabergoline dosages
Authors: Hána Václav Jr., Vaněčková Manuela, Kršek Michal, Krausová Adéla, Ježková Jana, Kosák Mikuláš, Diblík Pavel, Netuka David, Masopust Václav, Májovský Martin, Liščák Roman, Hána Václav
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesDopamine agonists serve as first-line therapies for most giant prolactinomas. The objective of this study was to assess the differential impact of dopamine agonist dosing strategies on biochemical (prolactin normalisation) and radiological (tumour size reduction) responses in patients with giant prolactinomas.DesignA single-centre retrospective real-life 23-year follow-up study.MethodsThirty-three patients with giant prolactinomas (≥ 4 cm) were treated with primary pharmacotherapy. We assessed pituitary function, the effect of therapy and different dosing regimens on prolactin normalisation and tumour size, the effect of surgery for complications and the effect of radiotherapy in resistant patients.ResultsOut of thirty-three consecutive patients (mean age 42 years), 27 were men, and 6 were women. The baseline mean prolactin concentration was 7506 µg/L. The treatment of choice was cabergoline in 30 patients, terguride in 2 patients, and bromocriptine in 1 patient. In patients receiving a high dose of cabergoline (3.5 mg weekly), we observed a faster normalisation of prolactin but not faster reduction in tumour size than in patients receiving a low dose (1–2.5 mg weekly). A total of 9/33 (27 %) patients underwent surgery for complications, 3 of whom were irradiated by Leksell gamma knife for partial resistance. In 4/33 patients we were able to stop pharmacotherapy after 10–20 years of treatment. The remaining 29/33 patients remained on pharmacological treatment.ConclusionsDopamine agonists are safe and only required treatment in 2/3 of patients treated with pharmacotherapy as a first-line treatment. Higher doses of cabergoline accelerate prolactin normalisation but do not confer additional benefit in early tumour shrinkage.

Introduction

Giant prolactinomas are defined as prolactin-secreting pituitary adenomas (PitNETs) ≥ 4 cm, usually with very high concentrations of prolactinemia over 1000 µg/l and without cosecretion of other hormones, such as GH, ACTH or TSH [1]. They represent less than 3 % of all prolactinomas and are diagnosed predominantly (90 %) in males [1].

The high effectiveness of the dopamine D2 receptor (D2) agonist pharmacotherapy in the treatment of prolactinomas has been proven by numerous studies. D2 agonists have become the first-line therapy even in cases of giant prolactinomas, where the normalisation of prolactinaemia is assessed in more than 80 % of prolactinomas and significant shrinkage of the tumour in up to 90 % of prolactinomas [[2], [3], [4]]. Neurosurgery is only considered for prolactinomas containing pharmacoresistant tumours and in cases of complications such as rhinorrhoea and symptomatic tumoral apoplexy. In cases of asymptomatic apoplexy on MRI pharmacotherapy remains the treatment of choice.

Several studies have described baseline characteristics, treatment modalities, and outcomes in giant prolactinomas, including 47, 71, 84 patients with this rare diagnosis, but a clear optimal pharmacotherapy regime has not yet been defined [[5], [6], [7]]. This study aimed to determine whether higher doses of cabergoline result in faster tumour size reduction and prolactin normalisation in giant prolactinomas.

Patients and methods

Patients

The study group consisted of 33 patients (27 (82 %) males and 6 females) who were diagnosed with giant prolactinoma (≥ 4 cm) at the Endocrinology Department of the 3rd Department of Internal Medicine, 1st Faculty of Medicine, Charles University, Prague, Czech Republic, between 1997 and 2020 and subsequently started pharmacotherapy as a first-line treatment. The other 5 patients who were diagnosed with giant prolactinomas and underwent first-line surgical treatment were not included. Due to the retrospective design of the study and the rarity of the disease, patient allocation to groups could not be randomized. Group sizes were determined based on the available clinical data and the number of patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period. Other hormone secreting adenomas (such as mammo-somatotropinomas) at baseline were not included in the study.

The mean age at the time of diagnosis of the whole group was 42 ± 14 (mean ± SD) years, with an age range of 23–71 years. The length of follow-up ranged from 30 months to 23 years (mean 11.4 years, median 11 years). The analysis was performed retrospectively in patients who had signed an informed consent with scientific data collection and analysis.

Pharmacotherapy

Patients were treated with D2 agonists, predominantly cabergoline (n = 30) and terguride (n = 2), with one patient treated with bromocriptine (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Therapy used in the treatment of giant prolactinomas.

Therapy used in the treatment of giant prolactinomas.

The initial dose of cabergoline was as follows: 3.5 mg/week (18 patients), 2.5 mg/week (2 patients), and 1–2 mg/week (10 patients). Terguride was initially given to 2 patients (10.5 mg/week and 21 mg/week). Bromocriptine (12.5 mg/week) was given to 1 patient.

The maximal dose during the follow-up was for cabergoline at 10.5 mg/week (3 tbl daily) and bromocriptine at 52.5 mg/week (3 tbl daily).

For cabergoline, the chronic dose (minimal enough to maintain normoprolactinaemia) was 0.25–8.75 mg/week (0.5–2.5 tbl daily) in 31 patients. For bromocriptine, the chronic dose was 8.75–52.5 mg/week in 2 patients.

One patient, who had started on cabergoline due to sleepiness and tiredness, switched to bromocriptine with regression of side effects. Another patient presented with headache and throbbing of the head while on cabergoline, and this side effect almost completely disappeared after switching to bromocriptine. One patient who started on bromocriptine and 2 patients who started on terguride were later successfully switched to cabergoline. Treatment was initiated with terguride due to the unavailability of cabergoline at the time. Once cabergoline became accessible, therapy was switched, as cabergoline has been shown to be more effective, better tolerated, and has a longer half-life compared to terguride [8].

Pharmacotherapy was stopped in 4 patients with stable normoprolactinaemia, and MRI was performed after 10, 15, 15, and 20 years (3 in the low dose cabergoline group without surgery or Leksell gamma knife (LGK) and 1 on terguride, cabergoline and LGK, without sugery). The other patients had been receiving chronic therapy for 2.5–23 (mean, median 12) years.

Complications during pharmacotherapy in relation to the initial dosage of cabergoline

The use of a high dose of cabergoline (10.5 mg weekly) was associated with 2 cases of intratumoural bleeding and 2 cases of CSF leakage in 18 patients. Bleeding and CSF leakage were observed at lower dosages in 1 and 1 patients, respectively, out of 12 patients.

Hormone measurements and assays

All patients had complete pituitary hormone evaluations before starting and during therapy. Macroprolactinaemia was excluded in all patients. Hormonal assays in this study were routinely used, which included mostly chemiluminescent methods that were regularly calibrated and standardised. Serum prolactin was measured by a chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (CMIA) Centaur (Siemens) from 1997 to 2015 and Alinity (Abbott) from 2015 to 2020. The normal range for prolactin is 3.5–19.4 µg/l in men and 5.2–26.5 µg/l in women (Alinity) with intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) 2,3–3,8% and inter-assay CV 3,3–4,7%. Units of µg/L can be converted to mU/L by multiplying by 21.2. Different methods for prolactin measurement were used during the long course of follow-up, but for evaluation of pharmacotherapy effectiveness (relative prolactin decrease in 6 months), the method remained unchanged. Multiple regression analysis conducted during the method transition showed a strong correlation, with correlation coefficient R = 0.99009061.Fast normalisation was defined as prolactinemia normalisation in less than 6 months of therapy.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed using 1.5 T and 3 T MRI scanners. The sequences employed included pre-contrast and post-contrast T1 sequences in the coronal and sagittal planes, as well as T2-weighted sequences in the coronal plane. Tumor size was measured in three dimensions, always on the slice where the lesion appeared largest.

Fast regression of the tumour was considered when the maximal diameter was reduced by ≥ 20 % in less than 6 months. Although there is no official definition of fast regression in prolactinomas, a similar cut-off is well established in patients with acromegaly [9]. We therefore adopted this criterion for consistency in our analysis.

A static perimetry from Medmont (Australia) was used.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, and as medians with minimum and maximum values for non-normally distributed continuous variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Comparisons between two groups of prolactinomas, based on tumour reduction and prolactin normalisation, were performed using the two-sample t-test for normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann–Whitney test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, as appropriate. Categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi-squared test of independence. All statistical analyses were conducted using Statistica 14 (StatSoft, USA).

Pituitary functions before and during therapy

Hypogonadism was the dominant symptom present in 28/31 patients (2 postmenopausal women were not evaluated). Three patients (1 female with a persistent menstrual cycle and 2 males with normal testosterone levels of 11 and 16 nmol/l, with macroprolactinemia were excluded) had a preserved gonadotrophic axis at the initial examination. During therapy, the gonadotrophic axis was recovered in 11/28 patients (39 %), and new hypogonadism developed in 1 female patient and in 1 male patient.

Hypothyroidism and hypocortisolism were initially present in 15 (45 %) and 7 (21 %) patients, respectively, and none of them recovered.

One patient developed cosecretion of prolactin and growth hormone after six years of cabergoline therapy (IGF-1: 887–1200 µg/l, GH nonsuppressible in the OGTT 12 mIU/l).

Visual field and diplopia before and during therapy

Visual field defects were present in 26/33 patients before therapy and improved or normalised in 25 patients, including 3, who had to undergo surgery due to adenoma bleeding.

Diplopia was present before therapy in 5 patients, 3 of whom completely regressed, and 2 of whom experienced partial regression.

Depending on the cabergoline dose administered, we observed improvements in 8/9 patients receiving a low dose and 15/15 patients receiving a high cabergoline dose.

MRI before and during therapy

All the patients had giant prolactinomas with the largest diameter ≥ 4 cm. The two largest tumours were 53x76x65 mm and 71x40x60 mm in size. Suprasellar extension was present in all 33 patients, intrasphenoidal progression was present in 32 patients, parasellar extension was present in 33 patients, presellar invasion was present in 11 patients, clivus invasion was present in 18 patients and temporal lobe invasion was present in 8 patients. Cystic/degenerative changes in prolactinomas before therapy were described in 10 patients. When haemorrhages were present in the tumor (visible on the MR images), this was always mentioned in the radiological report. The T1W and T2W characteristics of a haemorrhage vary depending on its age.

Treatment modalities

As in other studies, we documented the excellent effect of primary pharmacotherapy, which was the only therapy used in 73 % (24/33) of patients [3]. In the remaining patients, surgery was required; 3 patients had apoplexy into the tumour with enlargement and worsened perimetry (all close to diganosis), 3 had CSF leakage (after 3 months in 2 patients and after 5 years in one patient), and 3 had partial pharmacoresistance, i.e. elevated prolactin levels despite high doses of cabergoline or intolerance of high cabergoline doses (mean 3 years after diagnosis). In all nine cases the endoscopic transsphenoidal approach was used. In cases of CSF leak (often confirmed by presence of β2-transferrin in nasal fluid) an additional lumbar drainage was inserted to decrease intracranial preasure. D2 agonist therapy was continued after surgery. For three pharmacoresistant patients (2 after prior surgery), stereotactic radiosurgery with a LGK was also employed. LGK was used either to treat pharmacoresistant residual tumors or in effort to diminish and exit pharmacotherapy where residual small tumoral remnants (in cavernous sinuses) were remaining after a neurosurgical resection.

The initial prolactin levels were 250–45 500 µg/l (normal range for prolactin is 3.5–19.4 µg/l in men and 5.2–26.5 µg/l in women), with a mean of 7506 µg/l and a median of 4265 (IQR 1815–9100). The prolactin concentration was lower than 1000 µg/l in only 3 patients.

During therapy, prolactinaemia decreased in all patients and normalised in 31/33 patients. In 24 patients who were on pure pharmacotherapy, fast prolactin normalisation in less than 6 months occurred in 14/24 patients, in the interval of 6–12 months in 2 patients, at > 12 months in 6 patients and never normalised in 2 patients. One of these two patients with never-normalised prolactin had only slightly elevated prolactin (32 µg/l), so we continued pharmacotherapy. In the second patient, with very elevated prolactin (620 µg/l), we observed substantial regression of the prolactinoma on MRI, with residual tissue in the left cavernous sinus, as shown in Fig. 2. In the group of 9 patients who needed surgery (3 for bleeding into the tumour, 3 for CSF leakage and 3 for partial resistance), 4 had prolactin normalised in less than 6 months (2 had early surgery), one in 6–12 months and 4 after 12 months.

The effect of initial high-dose cabergoline on prolactin levels

The initial prolactin levels did not differ significantly between the groups. In the group with an initial dose of 3.5 mg, the median prolactin level was 5000 µg/l (range 440–32500), and in the group with 1–2.5 mg cabergoline, the weekly median prolactin level was 4035 µg/l (range 470–45522), p = 0.472 (Mann‒Whitney test).

Administration of a high initial dose of cabergoline (3.5 mg weekly), fast normalisation of prolactin was observed in 11/15 (73 %) of the assessable patients. A lower dosage (1–2.5 mg weekly) led to fast normalisation in only 4/11 of the assessable patients (36 %), p = 0.059.

The effect of pharmacotherapy on the size of tumours

Patients receiving a higher initial dose of cabergoline had larger tumours, with a mean maximal diameter of 55.8 ± 11,26 (SD) mm (range 40–76 mm), than did those receiving lower doses, with a mean maximal diameter of 45.1 ± 4.8 (SD) mm (range 40–55 mm), p = 0.004.

We observed a reduction in the size of the tumours in all patients. Fast regression of the tumour (reduction in the maximal diameter of the tumour ≥ 20 % in less than 6 months) was present in 22/29 (76 %) of the patients (4 could not be evaluated, 3 had early surgery, and 1 had radiotherapy). Slow regression (reduction in the maximal diameter of the tumour < 20 % in less than 6 months) occurred in 7/29 (24 %) of the patients.

In patients taking a high dose of cabergoline, fast tumour regression was observed in 10/15 (67 %) of the patients, and a lower dose was observed in 10/11 (91 %) of the assessable patients (p = 0.15). Discrepant effects of cabergoline on prolactin levels and tumour size

We observed different impacts of pharmacotherapy on the decrease in prolactin and shrinkage of the tumour (Table 2). Concordant fast normalisation of prolactinaemia and fast shrinkage were present in 38 % (11/29) of patients. In contrast, only 3 % (1/29) showed a slow effect on both parameters. A total of 41 % (12/29) of patients had slow normalisation of prolactin but fast size reduction. In 17 % (5/29) of patients, prolactin quickly normalised, but regression of the tumour was slower.

The effect of pharmacotherapy on hypopituitarism

Cabergoline therapy with normalisation of prolactinaemia led to gonadotrophic axis recovery in 39 % (11/28) of assessable patients, and new hypogonadism developed in 7 % (2/28) of patients (1F and 1 M). Hypothyroidism and hypocortisolism were initially present in 45 % (15/33) and 21 % (7/33) of the patients, respectively, and none of them recovered.

Complications of pharmacotherapy

The side effects observed during pharmacotherapy with cabergoline were as follows: 1 patient initially complained of sleepiness, but this symptom disappeared with continuous therapy. One patient temporarily presented with anxiety, delusions, and fear, and after a short course of psychopharmacotherapy, symptoms disappeared. We then gradually reintroduced and increased the dose of cabergoline. One patient received cabergoline, which led to the presence of headaches and anxiety, but he tolerated bromocriptine well. There was also transitory nausea during initial therapy in 1 patient, but it disappeared.

Therapy with terguride was accompanied by anxiety and depression in one patient, but symptoms disappeared after replacement with cabergoline.

Patients receiving higher doses of D2 agonists were subjected to regular echocardiography, and no clinically significant valve abnormalities were detected.

Discussion

Giant prolactinomas are rare tumours that appear predominantly in males, with large extrasellar progression and symptomatology corresponding to prevailing progression of the tumour (headache, visual field defects) and hypogonadism. In our study, these symptoms were confirmed to be prevalent. In three patients, we observed the exception of this typical rule for prolactinomas, namely, a preserved gonadotrophic axis despite extremely high prolactin levels. There is no clear explanation for this. A rare case with a germline prolactin receptor loss of function leading to familial hyperprolactinaemia in which the patient was fertile and had regular cycles until 39 years of age has been described in the literature. She subsequently developed oligomenorrhea [10]. Similarly, we speculate that in our patients with a preserved gonadotropic axis, young age (23, 28, 30 years) could be a protective factor against oligomenorrhea or male hypogonadism. Hyperprolactinemia causes hypogonadism via pulsatile gonadotropin-releasing hormone secretion inhibition. This mechanism is not age dependent. However, in some younger patients, GnRH pulsatility may be stronger, and the hypothalamic-pituitary–gonadal axis may work more dynamically [11].

Pharmacotherapy for giant prolactinomas is an established first-line therapy unless there are any complications, such as acute bleeding or CSF leakage. The surgical operation of such large tumours that invade surrounding structures can never remove all the pathologic tissue, so the current position of neurosurgery is in treating complications and debulking pharmacoresistant tumours.

We documented differences in response to therapy in our group. Some of these large tumours that have extremely high prolactin levels normalised prolactin quickly, whereas others normalise it slowly. Differences in tumour reduction were also observed and were either quick or slow. There was no clear correlation between the effect on prolactin and the shrinkage of the tumour.

The dominant pharmacotherapy in our cohort of patients was cabergoline with different initial doses. Higher initial doses of cabergoline (3.5 mg/week) were administered in more than half of the patients. The presence of significantly larger tumours and severe visual field defects influenced our decision to administer high doses, which usually results in prompt improvement of vision in a matter of days. High initial cabergoline doses had a stronger effect on the normalisation of prolactin levels than lower doses did. A high dose of cabergoline normalised prolactin quickly (in less than 6 months) in 73 % of patients in comparison with 36 % of patients receiving lower dosages (p = 0.059). There was not a stronger effect observed with high doses of cabergoline on the speed of tumour shrinkage on MRI, but the “high-dose cabergoline group” had a significantly larger initial tumour size, which could represent greater proliferative activity and therefore a worse result. Another explanation could be the possibility of tumour fibrotisation in response to D2 agonist therapy; thus, we speculate that fibroproduction under higher doses of D2 agonist therapy has a reduced effect on tumour size [12].

This long-term study on the different effects of D2 agonists on prolactinaemia and the size of tumours suggest that we should be cautious and wait a longer period of time when considering surgery for prolactinoma in patients who do not exhibit a fast response to medical therapy. Using higher doses of cabergoline leads to faster normalisation of prolactinaemia, although not to faster tumour reduction after 6 months of pharmacotherapy. Nevertheless, in the long term, we observed normalisation of prolactinaemia in 94 % (31/33) of patients with shrinkage in all patients, although 3 underwent surgery due to partial resistance to pharmacotherapy, and three (2 after surgery) also underwent stereotactic radiosurgery for the same reason.

We observed partial resistance to D2 agonists in 12 % of patients where surgery and stereotactic radiosurgery were successful second-line treatments. Continuing pharmacological therapy is necessary in these patients. The complications observed during pharmacological therapy for giant prolactinomas include intratumoural haemorrhage or CSF leakage (in our cohort, they occurred in 9 % and 9 %, respectively), and tight cooperation with neurosurgeons is necessary.

Medical therapy in patients with giant prolactinomas is necessary in the long term. The therapy was stopped in our cohort in only 4 patients (after 10, 15, 15 and 20 years), and the remaining patients (29/33) continued medical therapy for 2.5–23 years.

Successful therapy can even restore pituitary gonadotropin function in giant prolactinomas, as in 39 % of the patients in our cohort.

An additional hormonal cosecretion, such as GH + prolactin, can appear, as it did in one of our patients during the follow-up. This phenomenon is observed in approximately 4 % of patients, and regular screening of other hormones, such as GH/IGF-1, is recommended [13].

This study provides an analysis of the long-term pharmacotherapy of giant prolactinomas and their complications. The preferences of this study are the long-term follow-up of up to 23 years, the effects of different cabergoline doses on prolactinaemia and the size of the tumour, and the long-term safety of the therapy.

We are aware of the shortcomings of this study. As a retrospective analysis, it lacks randomisation and blinding. On the other hand, real-life, rare and heterogeneous diagnoses, such as giant prolactinomas, in prospective randomised studies would be very cumbersome. We are aware of the difficulty of standardising MRI measurements, the different dopamine agonists available historically and improvements in surgical techniques in the long term. Patients with incomplete historical follow up were not included in the study. Also 2 different prolactin measurements were used in this study, but with an excellent correlation coefficient when directly compared.

Conclusion

D2 agonists represent a first-line treatment for most giant prolactinomas. This study evaluated different D2 agonist dosing regimens in a single centre via a retrospective 23-year study. In our cohort, higher doses of cabergoline (3.5 mg weekly) led to fast prolactin normalisation but not size reduction. This effect could be distorted in our cohort by the larger initial tumour size in the group receiving higher cabergoline doses. For most patients, pharmacotherapy was the only treatment, but 27 % had to undergo surgery for complications, and 9 % were irradiated by LGK for partial resistance. Even with high prolactin levels, 9 % of patients had partially preserved gonadotroph function. One patient developed prolactin and GH cosecretion during follow-up. We observed no fatal complications.
