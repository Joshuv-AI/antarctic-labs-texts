Implementing Equitable Hypertension Care Using a Remote Patient Monitoring Program
Authors: Amber E. Johnson, Kevin Pearlman, Ria Sood, Alicia Dawdani, Alexis Aranda-Hernadez, Mustafa Agha Al Kola, Seyed Ehsan Saffari, Ahmad Bearat, Corey E. Tabit, Lisa M. Vinci, Tamar S. Polonsky, Sachin D. Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSystems-level approaches can promote equitable hypertension outcomes. Remote patient monitoring (RPM) is a patient-centered hypertension solution, but successful implementation strategies require further exploration.ObjectivesThis study was designed to determine social and institutional factors of RPM utilization at an academic medical center.MethodsWe used a mixed methods study design. Using logistic regression, we retrospectively assessed the relation of sociodemographic variables with RPM engagement, achievement of normotension, and maintenance of normotension at 180 days post-RPM. We completed Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier analyses to investigate the association of level of engagement and achieving normotension outcome. To augment the quantitative analysis, we concurrently interviewed RPM staff members to assess systems-level themes of RPM program implementation. The interviews were recorded, transcribed, and inductively coded using a validated implementation framework.ResultsWe analyzed 1744 RPM program participants (mean age 63.1 years, 64.7% female, 82.6% Black) between May 2022 and August 2024. Patients with higher RPM engagement were older than those with lower engagement (65.6 years vs 62.3 years). The high engagement group differed by race, with more Black individuals in the high engagement group. High engagement (OR: 2.06; 95% CI: 1.51-2.84; P < 0.001) was associated with likelihood of normotension and had a shorter time to normotension (HR: 2.52; 95% CI: 2.14-2.98; P < 0.001). Ten key staff provided important insights of barriers and facilitators of RPM implementation that converged with statistical analyses.ConclusionsAn RPM program successfully improved blood pressure care, especially among patients with higher program engagement. These findings reveal opportunities for achieving equitable hypertension outcomes.

Hypertension is a global epidemic associated with morbidity and disparity.1,2 As the leading modifiable cause of cardiovascular disease, hypertension is associated with heart failure, stroke, atrial fibrillation, renal failure, dementia, and sudden cardiac death.3 Control of hypertension in the United States plateaued a decade ago and current recommendations of blood pressure (BP) <130/80 mm Hg are achieved by only 19% of patients.3,4 Furthermore, hypertension disproportionately affects people of minoritized social status. Hypertension prevalence is higher among Black (57%) and Latino (45%), compared to White (44%) persons in the United States.5 In addition to a higher prevalence of hypertension among racially minoritized populations, its control is also affected by racial/ethnic disparities.6 Among men using antihypertensive medication, hypertension control rates are lowest among Latino (44%) followed by non-Latino Black (52%) and non-Latino White (59%) persons.5 These disparities have been attributed to inadequate hypertension awareness and treatment among persons of minority race.6

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention created strategies to address hypertension and its predisposing social factors.7,8 Similarly, professional society guidelines encourage patient self-management,4,9 defined as self-monitoring and interfacing with personal data to inform medical decisions and health services utilization.10 Hypertension disparities can be partly addressed by enabling equitable access to self-management tools, such as home-based monitoring.11 Strategies to facilitate equitable and improved access to proven hypertension management tools are needed. Data suggest that monitoring BP in an ambulatory setting is associated with long-term health improvement in vulnerable populations.12 Bridging the gap in hypertension equity requires interdisciplinary research, end-user engagement, and strategic dissemination of effective tools.13 Interventions that account for the unique social and health care contexts and inequities specific to at-risk communities will likely help address the hypertension challenges unique to these groups.14 Research suggests that health system-level approaches may enhance hypertension self-management. Systems-level approaches have achieved success in translating evidence-based physical activity, weight management, and diabetes prevention strategies into clinical and community practice.15

The University of Chicago Medicine (UCM) has implemented a mobile health (mHealth), internet-based, self-monitoring platform to improve access to care and hypertension outcomes among its diverse patient population.16,17 Essential components of the remote patient monitoring (RPM) hypertension self-management system include 1) patient self-measurement of BP at home; 2) automatic transmission of BP readings to the patient’s electronic health record (EHR); 3) clinician evaluation and potential intervention based on patient-generated data; and 4) algorithm-guided titration of antihypertensive therapy. Others have shown success in RPM programs, demonstrating the potential efficacy in clinical practice.18 However, the association between structural and health systems-level factors and desired hypertension outcomes remains less well understood.

Understanding the key mechanisms by which RPM can influence and sustain hypertension self-care could enhance implementation of the intervention more broadly. In this study, we hypothesized that patient sociodemographic characteristics and health system/structural factors would affect RPM utilization and outcomes. The overall aim of this study was to identify factors associated with RPM program implementation and its impact on BP control in a diverse patient population.

Methods

Setting

The University of Chicago is a leading academic institution with a main campus and satellite facilities. The study was conducted at the main UCM hospital in Chicago, Illinois. This academic center treats many patients from a large catchment area including the South Side of Chicago and surrounding communities.

Intervention

During an outpatient clinic visit in which a patient was diagnosed with uncontrolled hypertension, a UCM physician or advanced practitioner could refer the patient to RPM. Referrals could be initiated through one of 2 pathways. The first was a “warm handoff,” in which the clinician personally escorted the patient to see the designated RPM medical assistant trained as a Digital Navigator for completion of the enrollment process. The second was an asynchronous referral placed through an RPM order within the EHR; in this case, the order triggered direct outreach by the Digital Navigator, who would help the patient schedule a separate enrollment appointment.

During the RPM enrollment encounter, the Digital Navigator assisted the patient in installing a mHealth application and pairing it with a Bluetooth-enabled BP monitor provided to the patient free of charge. The mHealth application was then synchronized with the patient’s EHR portal (MyChart, trademark of Epic Systems Corporation), enabling automatic upload of BP readings into the patient’s chart. Patients were encouraged to upload daily BP readings from home while receiving weekly outreach calls from a population health nurses or clinical pharmacists to encourage program engagement, provide lifestyle counseling, and titrate antihypertensive medications using a standardized protocol. The UCM RPM medication titration protocol is available in Supplemental Figure 1. Since the RPM program’s inception in May 2022, it has been adopted by UCM clinics—including primary care, nephrology, and cardiology—demonstrating its feasibility and scalability.

Study design

This was a convergent parallel mixed methods study design.19 A quantitative analysis was conducted, along with qualitative interviews with key RPM staff. As results were obtained, the study team related convergent findings that informed key insights about RPM implementation. The analysis concluded with integration of the sociodemographic and institutional-level contributors to our clinical hypertension outcomes. Implementing Equitable Hypertension Care Using a Remote Patient Monitoring Program: A Mixed Methods Study

This study employed a convergent mixed methods design to assess the overlap between an observational analysis of sociodemographic variables with the descriptive insights from key program staff.

The findings will guide future qualitative assessment of patients’ perspectives and will lead to the hybrid implementation/effectiveness testing of enhanced remote patient monitoring (RPM). ADI = Area Deprivation Index; other abbreviation as in Figure 1.

We used qualitative methodology to perform intervention mapping strategies with key staff who had been involved with the RPM program. Intervention mapping is a validated process for developing and refining processes and understanding the criteria for ideal implementation.23 Staff were recruited based on convenience sampling with a goal of ten 30-minute interviews. Interviews were conducted virtually via Zoom by a trained qualitative researcher (A.E.J.), using the constant comparative method to understand facilitators and barriers to program utilization and hypertension control. Staff interviews were conducted following a semistructured interview guide that was developed (by A.E.J.) based upon the overarching research goals of understanding RPM implementation. The interview guide is available in the Supplemental Material. Confidentiality, transparency, and consent to record were established prior to interviews. Recordings were transcribed using voice recognition notetaking software, then manually checked twice for accuracy by authors A.E.J. and R.S. Data were coded and analyzed via Rapid Qualitative Analysis,24 a technique used for timely yet rigorous analysis by utilizing structured templates and summary matrices to distill key themes across interviews. Three coders (A.E.J., R.S., and A.D.) developed and agreed upon a structured summary template based on broad areas of inquiry within the interview guide (ie, role of technology, barriers, and facilitators to RPM use). Thus, the codes summarized qualitative themes using a deductive process based upon the overarching research goals as reflected in the interviews. During coding, unique observations were added to the predefined categories, such that the Rapid Qualitative Analysis also allowed for an inductive analytical process where appropriate. Each coder independently summarized a distinct subset of transcripts. Summarized data across all interviews were entered into a single matrix organized by the summary template domains. The analysis team met to review the matrix, identify themes and unique insights across interviews, and organize themes into categories. Disagreements were adjudicated until consensus was reached. Additionally, we used the validated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) framework to interpret the qualitative findings through an implementation science lens, assessing RPM innovations, settings, individuals, and implementation domains.25 Participants were provided $50 gift cards for study compensation.

RPM patient population

Eligible patients for the RPM program were adult patients (aged 18-85 years) actively receiving care at a UCM primary care, nephrology, or cardiology clinic. Inclusion required a diagnosis of hypertension (International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision: I10) or elevated BP (International Classification of Diseases-10th Revision: R03.0) in addition to having a clinic visit within the prior 6 months or on the day of enrollment. The enrolling clinician was also required to be the provider managing the patient’s BP. Participants also needed a smartphone (Apple or Android) with Bluetooth capability to use the BP management mHealth application. Ineligibility for RPM included current or planned pregnancy, a history of severe cardiovascular or renal disease (eg, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, end-stage kidney disease, stroke within the last 6 months, myocardial infraction, or hypertensive emergency), a baseline BP >180/110 mm Hg, institutionalization, significant sensory or neurocognitive deficits, or inability to operate a smartphone or BP monitor. Patients completed RPM after achieving BP goal and sustaining it for 4 weeks or disenrolled from the program if they were determined by RPM nurses or pharmacists to be consistently unreachable via phone or MyChart. The study was approved by the University of Chicago Institutional Review Board (IRB24-1375). Patient informed consent was waived due to retrospective analysis.

Statistical analysis

We aimed to investigate the efficacy of the RPM program for achieving normotension. The primary outcome was achievement of normotension defined as BP <140/90 as a dichotomous variable. We adjusted for RPM enrollment duration to account for variation across patients. Level of engagement was therefore defined as either high or low based on the number of uploaded BPs per day using a median cut off.26 Patient characteristics were described using mean and SD for continuous variables and frequency and percentage for categorical variables and were compared between the high and low engagement groups using 2-sample t-test (normality assumption was tenable) and chi-square tests for continuous and categorical variables, respectively.

We evaluated the association of level of engagement with the RPM program and likelihood of reaching normotension via logistic regression analysis. We assessed effect modification by sociodemographic variables: sex, age, ethnicity, race, and Area Deprivation Index as determined by patient EHR data by examining two-way interactions between RPM engagement and the abovementioned parameters in both logistic regression and Cox regression models. The Area Deprivation Index is a validated measure of neighborhood socioeconomic adversity that incorporates ZIP code-level variables to provide standardized neighborhood rankings by socioeconomic status, thus enabling the evaluation of outcomes by geographic location based on level of disadvantage.27 The goodness-of-fit for the logistic regression analysis was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, which indicated no significant deviation from the model. We also assessed the time to normotension outcome with Cox regression analysis, and HR and 95% CI were calculated. Proportional hazard assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals, and no major deviation was observed. Multicollinearity assumption in the regression models was assessed via variance inflation factor analysis and no variation was observed. Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to calculate the time to normotension (95% CI) within RPM by level of engagement. For the logistic and Cox regression analysis, we excluded patients who were normotensive at the time of RPM enrollment (based on their first uploaded BP reading). In the time to event model, censoring was determined as those with only one BP recorded over the duration of RPM enrollment and those who did not reach normotension at the end of RPM. Kaplan-Meier curves were compared using log-rank analyses. All analyses were completed using R software (R Core Team [2024]). An alpha level of 0.05 determined statistical significance.

To analyze BP trajectories during and after RPM participation, we conducted a piecewise linear mixed-effects regression analysis. Time was normalized to standardize different participation durations. The model included a change point at transition point from RPM to post-RPM period. We fitted a linear mixed-effects model with time-piece (normalized time), post-change indicator (binary variable for post-RPM period), and their interaction as fixed effects. Subject-specific random intercepts were included to account for within-subject correlation of repeated BP measurements. Separate models were fitted for high and low engagement groups to compare their BP trajectories. This approach allowed us to estimate different slopes for the RPM and post-RPM periods while accounting for the longitudinal nature of the data.

Regarding the qualitative analysis, between January and April 2025, we interviewed 5 nurses, 2 pharmacists, and 3 Digital Navigators, each providing their perspectives on RPM implementation. Using the interview guide questions, coauthors A.E.J., R.S., and A.D. coded the 10 transcripts along the following domains: staff role within RPM; patient-level issues, staff-level problems; the role of technology; additional barriers and facilitators to RPM use; possible ways to improve RPM for future iterations. A.E.J. and A.D. then used Rapid Qualitative Analysis to create a matrix of codes that were then synthesized into key interview takeaways. These findings were then further characterized using the CFIR domains of innovation, settings, individuals, and implementation. Mapping RPM implementation using convergent mixed methods

The core innovative components of the RPM Program are the design of its patient-centered activities and its EHR-based delivery. Staff agree that RPM implementation benefits from its synergy with the EHR. Examples of this synergy include staff using the EHR to communicate patient care delivery with other multidisciplinary team members and to communicate directly with patients about medication changes or required follow-up. However, the reliance on the EHR can create a barrier for patients who are less facile with or attentive to their online patient portal.“I think, you know, the patients who still want to participate, and they definitely try, I don't think they should be penalized in or it's essentially, in my mind, it's ageism, in a sense, if they're not able to use the technology, and yet, they're being enrolled in this program that's very tech heavy”(RPM pharmacist).

“I think, you know, the patients who still want to participate, and they definitely try, I don't think they should be penalized in or it's essentially, in my mind, it's ageism, in a sense, if they're not able to use the technology, and yet, they're being enrolled in this program that's very tech heavy”(RPM pharmacist).

Another example of RPM innovation is that the program allows patients agency over their care and can facilitate trust in the health care system as patients establish rapport with the RPM staff.“…so at the end of the shift, they send us a list of all the patients that was enrolled. They are assigned to each nurse, and that nurse follows that patient from beginning of enrollment to completion. And if somehow that patient needs to be reenrolled three months from now, we try to then put them back with that same nurse... They love to have the same consistent caretaker. They love that they can trust that person is not hopping around. So a lot of times, we've got a lot of patients. It's like, “I've been waiting for your call,” or, … “No, keep me in this group,” because they tend to start to trust them”(RPM Nurse)

The qualitative interviews assessed components of RPM’s inner setting domain, specifically how staff use technology to interact with patients. Following enrollment, patients receive weekly outreach from nurses or pharmacists to assess program and medication adherence, provide counseling regarding lifestyle modification, and titrate medications using a standardized protocol. We found that the program has helped circumvent barriers to care delivery such as transportation to clinic for BP management. Central components of the RPM infrastructure include electronic communication between multidisciplinary team members (Digital Navigator, nurses, and pharmacists). The quantitative analysis supports the staff report that patients complete the program within a few weeks, with a median RPM duration of 36 days for the high engagement group. However, our qualitative assessment revealed a perceived difference in the nurses’ and pharmacists’ management of the antihypertensive medications.“The population health nurses are maybe more hesitant to make medication changes and talking to them, it sounds like they're not [completing] patients as quickly, which would make sense, if they're not changing medication”(RPM Pharmacist).

As reflected by the themes within the inner domain, staff roles affect the RPM program’s implementation. The RPM Digital Navigator role is critical to RPM’s implementation. During qualitative interviews, all staff emphasized the importance of Digital Navigators in RPM’s success. Digital Navigators serve as patients’ introduction to the RPM program. They provide basic education about hypertension management, outline specific steps for successful BP measurement, and assist with troubleshooting. They also ensure proper synchronization between the mHealth application and the patient portal for access to the EHR, thus enabling automatic upload of BP readings into the patient’s chart.“I’m one of the Digital Navigators, we are more tech people. So we are the ones that actually help the patients navigate through their [EHR], help them to download, troubleshoot, if they are having any issues uploading”(RPM Digital Navigator).

Our RPM program was implemented at UCM among mostly Black patients living on Chicago’s South Side. We found that the intervention enhanced hypertension self-management for our patient population. The CFIR outer setting domain describes how one’s community, the health system, and the greater society can affect program implementation. Contrary to our anecdotal and preliminary data, this analysis revealed that RPM engagement was high among patients older than 65 years and patients of Black race. Our staff interviews support these results by describing how younger hypertensive patients were more difficult to contact and were often less willing to take medications. Regarding the overall implementation process, our qualitative results illustrate a lack of program fidelity in that RPM is not always being used as intended. Staff describe needing to deviate from the protocol, especially when patients get enrolled in RPM but do not meet the inclusion criteria. RPM was designed for patients diagnosed with uncontrolled hypertension or elevated BP, but staff describe a significant number of referrals for patients with normal BP. In these cases, patients are enrolled in RPM, but their home BPs are at goal without requiring medication titration. The exclusion criteria for our analysis cohort reflected that many people had controlled BP at the time of RPM enrollment.“I think sometimes there's just kind of a lack of understanding of what our exclusion criteria and inclusion criteria is. The treatment protocol was not adhered to consistently, especially for patients with complex medical problems and multimorbidity. In these instances, nurses often sought the assistance of RPM pharmacists for advice. For example, the interview results are supported by the data showing that an inverse relationship between renal dysfunction and likelihood of normotension. Nevertheless, with the RPM program, BP goals are eventually met irrespective of patients’ level of program engagement or strict staff adherence to the protocol.

The “warm handoff” during RPM referral is another implementation construct that is delivered with variable fidelity. The ideal enrollment process was designed such that the clinician would personally escort the patient to the Digital Navigator for RPM enrollment. However, staff describe asynchronous referral as the norm. In the case of the asynchronous referral, an RPM order is followed by outreach from a Digital Navigator, who would help the patient schedule a separate enrollment appointment. Staff agree that “warm handoffs” facilitate patient engagement and subsequent patient outreach. All staff described problems relating to difficulty contacting patients in RPM. We see this reflected in the median of 0.103 readings per day (roughly 1 BP reading every 10 days) despite RPM being meant to generate daily BP readings for patients.“What I'm kind of noticing is that there's maybe a disconnect between the provider who's ordering [RPM] versus, you know, what the patient may understand their perception of what the program is… Sometimes you can tell, you can tell we get on their nerve with these calls. Sometimes it's like, ‘Oh, it's you again.’ Or sometimes like, ‘Oh, hey XXX. I was wondering if you were going to call,’ but sometimes it's like, you can tell. It's like, ‘I don't want to be bothered right now’”(RPM Nurse).

Discussion

In this study, we used a convergent parallel mixed methods design to assess the implementation of RPM, a patient-centered mHealth intervention for home-based hypertension self-management. We completed a quantitative analysis of 1,744 patients to elucidate sociodemographic associations with BP outcomes. In addition, we qualitatively interviewed 10 key RPM staff to assess their perceptions of the clinical process. The convergent analysis illustrated similarities between our quantitative and qualitative findings. These data will assist the implementation of current RPM delivery as well as the development of future RPM iterations. In a learning health system, administrative leadership and clinical staff must work together synergistically to deliver optimal care to the patient population.28 This analysis exemplifies a framework-based approach to optimize care, achieve quality improvement, and deliver patient-centered innovation.

Our findings are supported by other recent literature leveraging a multidisciplinary approach to outpatient BP care. A Los Angeles-based barbershop BP initiative has proven that pharmacist-led, community-based interventions can provide effective hypertension management for marginalized groups.29 However, critics of such programs argue that implementation barriers often limit widespread scalability and adoption, especially in more traditional clinical settings such as a primary care office.30 Our RPM program employs key features of the barbershop intervention, such as pharmacist-led medication titration, while also integrating traditional aspects of hospital-based care (eg, EHR integration and clinician BP monitoring). Technology can bridge community-based components with health system components, thereby enabling a synergistic patient-centered hypertension care model. Our analysis identified effective mechanisms of the RPM program as well as insights for improvement. The ability for RPM to achieve normotension despite infrequent home BP measurements illustrates opportunities to revise the program scope and enhance the patient outreach process. Our data may also reflect RPM’s benefit beyond recording BP, specifically lifestyle modification and rapport with clinical staff.

While our RPM program has demonstrated general success in a diverse patient population, this study highlights important variations in outcomes based on patient demographics. Our future work will focus on designing and testing ways to enhance the RPM program to best serve patients at risk for implementation barriers. Digital interventions that require internet, smartphones, or EHR may be adapted differently across sociodemographic groups. Other studies have shown that marginalized patient groups, including those residing in resource-limited neighborhoods, required special assistance in learning to use a computer for their medical needs.31 Low levels of computer literacy may overlap with relatively low educational status among people living in medically underserved areas.31 We have previously shown that area of residence also corresponds with disparate access to the high-speed broadband internet often required for optimal mHealth use.32

Underlying mechanisms of race-based differences in hypertension outcomes likely include systemic barriers and structural drivers of care.6 Prior to this study, we thought that our Black patients would have lower RPM engagement. On the contrary, we found that the high engagement group consisted of significantly more Black patients whereas the low engagement group had significantly more White patients. Our health system is now empowered with the knowledge that our Black patients could serve as an ideal patient population for RPM. Potential facilitators of RPM use include patient agency over their BP improvement. We anticipate that future patient-level qualitative data will inform whether low-tech options are essential to home-based BP monitoring for this vulnerable patient population. Future quantitative work may also determine the extent of phone calls and digital outreach in building patient trust.

Learning health systems must implement clinical tools and protocols that are evidence-based, theory driven, and equitable.33 As UCM aims to improve the equitable outcomes of its diverse patient population, our health system’s leaders and staff will continue to explore equity-focused quality improvement initiatives. Health equity must include long-term commitments from our academic institutions and health systems. Such commitments are essential not only to improve outcomes that impact the lives of our individual patients but also to support the economic wellbeing of our institutions and broader society. The RPM intervention consists of a BP monitoring program that strives to incorporate a systems-based approach that: 1) acknowledges contextual domains that impact hypertension management; and 2) integrates research and practice into program adoption. Staff interviews revealed important recommendations for enhancements in patient and provider education, improved patient outreach and rapport building, and increased medication oversight from pharmacists. Interviews underscored that staff roles within RPM would benefit from improved clarity and decreased redundancy such that education, care coordination, and medications are managed more efficiently.

Study strengths include the convergent mixed methods design34 that elucidates implementation outcomes and the contextual factors that modify the RPM treatment effect. The CFIR framework also revealed implementation domains that will inform plans for broader RPM delivery. Despite these strengths, our study is not without limitation.

Study Limitations

As an urban health system, our findings may have limited applicability to other settings. While our center’s experience may not be transferrable to all clinical contexts, validity is enhanced by the common prevalence of hypertension and widely reported barriers to care that transcend patient populations.35 Our statistical analysis is limited by the availability of patient data in the EHR and notably does not capture manually recorded BPs. Written and manual BP measurements were not recorded, but may have contributed to post-RPM BP improvement as measured in the EHR. Another major limitation is that our quantitative analysis does not account for patient use of antihypertensive medications and its relation to BP outcomes. Unfortunately, the current data set did not allow for the capture of such data, but future analyses will study medication use. In addition, we were unable to quantitatively determine the clinical indications for RPM referrals or reasons for RPM disenrollment (eg, white coat hypertension). In this analysis, we defined normotension as <140/90 mm Hg. This BP target was based on the definition of normotension in the primary care program, which differed from the goal of <130/80 mm Hg in specialty clinics. Although our results may be biased by heterogeneous targets depending on the clinic, overall results demonstrate significant BP improvement from baseline. Validity of our numerical findings is further supported by staff interviews. Because of the convergent design, interview questions were largely influenced by quantitative results rather than researcher bias or personal motivation. Credibility of the qualitative study findings is enhanced by thematic saturation with respondents’ descriptions of similar RPM experiences.
