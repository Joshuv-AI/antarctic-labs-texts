An Algorithm at the Table at Multidisciplinary Diagnostic Meetings: A Dramaturgical Analysis
Authors: Justien Dingelstad, Francisca Grommé, Claartje ter Hoeven, Iris Wallenburg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis paper focuses on how a radiological classification algorithm temporarily became part of existing diagnostic deliberations between different medical specialists. It describes a participatory ethnographic case study in which the output of the algorithm was presented by the first author during weekly multidisciplinary neuro‐oncology meetings. Following the dramaturgical framework of deliberations by Hajer and adhering to the sociology of diagnosis, we focus on how the algorithm interacted with the established deliberative dynamics of the meetings. We show that by the algorithm's presence, physicians were prompted to speculate about what the algorithm could mean for their collective diagnosis. They did not foresee an independent or leading role for the algorithm yet speculated about how, when and by whom the algorithmic output should be presented. Some physicians followed dominant AI tropes of self‐evidence and objectivity, whereas others sought ways to contextualise and question the algorithmic output. We argue that following a dramaturgical framework unveils important questions that must be addressed to make algorithms fit into existing diagnostic practices, such as who represents the algorithm, when and how.Keywords: algorithms, diagnosis, dramaturgy, ethnography, healthcare

Introduction

At the neuro‐oncology multi‐disciplinary diagnostic meeting, the radiologist discusses the first patient included in the algorithmic test. She ends her explanation by stating: ‘I think it’s a glioblastoma [type of brain tumour].’ She turns to me, and I say: ‘According to the algorithm, it’s an oligodendroglioma [other type of brain tumour].’ Everybody turns to the pathologist, who says, ‘Well, we’re not 100% sure, but we’re pretty certain it’s an oligodendroglioma.’ Other physicians make ‘oohs’ sounds of excitement because the algorithm aligns with the pathologist and not with the radiologist.

This situation occurred during a participatory ethnographic study of a field test of an algorithm classifying a serious and profound condition: brain tumours. In the Dutch academic hospital where the study took place, brain tumours are diagnosed by physicians from different disciplines during weekly multidisciplinary meetings. Researchers at the radiology department developed ‘PredictAI’ (pseudonym), a deep learning algorithm meant to classify gliomas, the most common type of aggressive, primary brain tumour (Lapointe et al. 2018). The researchers wanted to test the algorithm in clinical practice as a first step in exploring how deep learning algorithms could be applied in collective diagnosis. This follows a broader trend of hospitals developing algorithms to assist in diagnosis and testing them in practice (Lebovitz et al. 2022; Avnoon and Eyal 2025). Hospital administrators and medical staff hope these diagnostic algorithms will, for example, save time by automating decision‐making or make diagnoses more consistent by eliminating bias (Raphael 2022).

In such hopes, diagnosis is often framed in behavioural‐psychological terms as a rule‐based, individual thought process (Berg 1997), which algorithms could execute equally well or even better. Sociologists of diagnosis (Brown 1995; Jutel 2009; Bell et al. 2024), however, argue that diagnostic practices cannot be reduced to linear, individual thinking and instead involve many actors (e.g., nurses, physicians, technicians and patients), apparatuses, methods, experiences and perspectives, all coming together in complex, situated attempts to fit symptoms into pre‐agreed categories (Maslen 2017). In this paper, we attend to this scholarship.

Specifically, we focus on collective diagnosis in multidisciplinary meetings and try to understand how and why algorithms fit into existing deliberative practices. We follow the framework of dramaturgy as developed by sociologists and science and technology studies (STS) scholars (Hajer 2005; Ramsey et al. 2023). Dramaturgy approaches deliberative practices through the lens of theatre (Goffman 1956), emphasising that such practices always involve material and discursive elements of performance (following concepts such as setting, scripts, roles and audiences), shaping the course of deliberation (Hajer 2005; Stacey et al. 2025). We study the question: How did the algorithm become part of the multidisciplinary diagnostic meetings, and what can we learn about the (potential) role of algorithms in such decision‐making practices?

We address this question through an 18‐month participatory ethnographic study. The first author (hereafter JD), a social scientist, was part of the research team conducting a test initiated by a radiologist (hereafter Radiologist A). Radiologist A aimed to understand how physicians and nurses participating in multidisciplinary meetings would react to the algorithm without any consequences for patient care. On invitation of Radiologist A, JD participated in weekly multidisciplinary meetings for several months, during which she reported the outputs of the radiological algorithm (described in detail below). She facilitated the set‐up of the test, prepared the medical ethics review and set up the digital infrastructure to download the outputs of the algorithm. JD made extensive field notes during the multidisciplinary meetings, of which the opening statement is part. In addition, she interviewed 21 physicians and nurses regularly attending the meetings.

Our analysis of this material follows the dramaturgical framework for deliberative practices by Hajer (2005). Using the method of abductive analysis (Timmermans and Tavory 2012), we illustrate the particular dramaturgy of the multidisciplinary meetings and how it supports the sharing of information and collective discussion and decision‐making in the face of the inherent complexity and uncertainty of brain tumour diagnosis (List et al. 2025). Based on this analysis, we explain how the algorithm was made part of the dramaturgy and assigned a role and script in the sharing of information. As we will show, this new role and script temporarily changed the existing performance of diagnosis and prompted physicians and nurses to speculate about possibilities to make the algorithm part of their meetings. These speculations help understand how the algorithm can enrich existing diagnostic practices.

Our study contributes to sociological studies of algorithms in diagnostic practices (e.g., Avnoon and Oliver 2023; Avnoon and Eyal 2025; Tyskbo and Nygren 2024). We reiterate their findings that medical practitioners consciously reflect on and respond to novel diagnostic algorithms in their work practices. Instead of ‘replacing’ or ‘reinventing’ professional work, as commonly held narratives about AI in healthcare suggest (Susskind and Susskind 2015), these technologies add to existing practices and reshape them in subtle and specific ways. Studies have, for example, shown that, in practice, medical practitioners' concerns about accuracy and opacity often result in reluctance to integrate algorithms into their daily work (Avnoon and Oliver 2023; Avnoon and Eyal 2025; Lebovitz et al. 2022). Our dramaturgical analysis provides an additional perspective to understand these reflections and responses. Following this analysis, we argue that allowing algorithms to enrich existing diagnostic practices requires understanding and possibly rewriting the existing dramaturgy of diagnosis, approaching the algorithm as a material, relational and discursive object. This involves considerations of when, how and by whom the algorithmic output is presented.

Theory

This study builds on two streams of literature: the sociology of diagnosis and a dramaturgical framework. Below, we elaborate on both and explain how they help us understand diagnostic practices in multidisciplinary meetings and how the algorithm temporarily became part of them.

The Sociology of (Brain Tumour) Diagnosis

Sociologists of diagnosis describe diagnosis as trying to organise ‘… “unorganised illness”: an agglomeration of complaints and symptoms which may be unclear, unconnected, and mysterious’ (Brown 1995, 39). This is done by assigning illness to agreed‐upon categories, which open up corresponding treatments and offer patients a prognosis. Although diagnoses can be relatively straightforward, there are many cases of complex, unconnected and uncertain symptoms that are difficult to fit into pre‐agreed categories. Here, a multiplicity of actors are involved (physicians, nurses, technicians and patients), all bringing their own explanations, methods, understandings and beliefs (Jutel 2009). Moreover, categories are often diffuse to begin with and keep changing due to new research insights or changes in (international) standards and protocols (Bell et al. 2024). Agreeing upon a diagnostic category therefore involves negotiation and compromise (Jutel 2009). The sociology of diagnosis thus studies diagnostic practices as complex, situated deliberations.

This study focuses on brain tumour diagnosis. Brain tumours present themselves in symptoms such as epileptic seizures, (partial) paralysis, speech or visual disturbances or personality changes (Gross 2009). There are several specialised physicians involved in ‘organising’ these symptoms, most importantly neurologists, neurosurgeons, radiologists and pathologists (Gross 2009; Tyskbo and Nygren 2024). These specialities use specific methods and apparatuses to gather information about the tumour. Neurologists and neurosurgeons perform consultations with patients, thereby sensing their symptoms. Neurosurgeons can perform surgery (resection and/or biopsy) and, in doing so, observe and touch the tumour. Radiologists use MRI and CT scans to analyse tumour location and characteristics. Pathologists analyse the tissue obtained during surgery for cellular and molecular characteristics. These different physicians usually come together at multidisciplinary meetings or ‘tumour boards’ (List et al. 2025), where a formal diagnosis is established collectively (Oborn and Dawson 2010).

Dramaturgy of Deliberation

Diagnosis in multidisciplinary meetings thus involves a process of deliberation between different physicians to agree upon categorisation. To understand how the algorithm became part of the multidisciplinary meetings, we are interested in the specific dynamics of these deliberations. We therefore follow dramaturgy, an analytical approach used to study the dynamics of social interactions. Dramaturgy has a long history in social science, with its origins usually attributed to Goffman (1956). He proposed dramaturgy as a metaphor, rooted in theatrical studies, including concepts such as stage, audiences and scripts, to show the intricate, often unspoken techniques, strategies and rituals constituting daily interactions (Ramsey et al. 2023). As dramaturgy is widely used in social sciences, the types of interactions studied vary.

We build on the work of Hajer (2005), who has written extensively about deliberation using the framework of dramaturgy. He argues that this framework allows one to study how particular set‐ups structure the flow of a conversation and who is allowed to participate in it.

Hajer (2005) developed a dramaturgical framework that distinguishes between four main theatrical concepts: scripting, staging, setting and performance. Ever since, other sociologists and political scientists have elaborated on this framework in their studies of deliberative practices (Freeman et al. 2016; Oomen et al. 2022; Stacey et al. 2025). First, scripting determines the characters of the play (Hajer 2005) and captures who is allowed to speak, how, to whom and when (Oomen et al. 2022). Scripting, thereby, constructs characters as, for example, lead or support roles, protagonists or antagonists (Freeman et al. 2016; Stacey et al. 2025). Second, staging refers to the deliberate organisation of interaction (Hajer 2005), guiding the sequence of events into a ‘step‐by‐step braiding’ (Oomen et al. 2022, 12) of what happens when. Third, setting refers to the physical environment where the interaction takes place (Hajer 2005), including artefacts or props present or that participants bring or remove (Freeman et al. 2016; Oomen et al. 2022). Last, performance refers to the way in which the interaction itself (re)produces social realities like knowledge or understandings of a problem (Hajer 2005), also shaping future interactions (Freeman et al. 2016). Hajer emphasises that this framework allows one to study under what conditions which voices emerge in discussions, how contributions relate to each other and how contributions influence final decision‐making.

A final dimension to the dramaturgical framework is the concept of the ‘dominant symbolic order’ in which a deliberation takes place. Hajer and colleagues argue that dramaturgical performances correspond to and navigate ‘the realm of collectively held symbols and notions’ (Stacey et al. 2025, 6) shaping dominant narratives about what the world is and should be. Dramaturgy henceforth does not only include the scripting, setting and performance of a particular deliberation but also the dominant, wider symbolic order in which it takes place (Hajer 2005; Oomen et al. 2022). This symbolism is often expressed by participants through references to existing symbols and tropes (Hajer 2005): commonly recurring motifs or clichés.

In our analysis, we use the dramaturgical framework to deepen our understanding of how deliberation takes place within multidisciplinary meetings and how the algorithm interacts with the established dynamics. Before we turn to this analysis, we detail how we performed our case study.

Methods

The PredictAI Algorithm

PredictAI was developed in‐house by a data science PhD student, supervised by Radiologist A and a medical imaging scientist. The algorithm subclassifies the most common type of primary malignant brain tumour: glioma (Lapointe et al. 2018). PredictAI distinguishes three main subtypes of glioma following the World Health Organization (WHO) classification guideline, based on two molecular tumour characteristics (IDH mutation and 1p/19q co‐deletion) and tumour grade (2–4). The three subtypes are glioblastoma (IDH wildtype), astrocytoma (IDH mutant) and oligodendroglioma (IDH mutant and 1p/19q co‐deleted) (Louis et al. 2021). The first is always Grade 4, whereas the latter two can be Grade 2 or 3, with grades referring to how likely a tumour is to grow and spread (see Figure 1).

2021 WHO classification guideline of gliomas. Source: Torp et al. (2022).

PredictAI consists of a single multitask convolutional neural network (CNN) in which an MRI scan passes through a network of 64 layers, comparing different features of the images to arrive at a final WHO classification (see Figure 2). It was developed in a few iterations by a data scientist, using a dataset consisting of retrospectively collected patient data of newly diagnosed, preoperative patients. In the development of the algorithm the accuracy of the classifications was compared to pathology, which by convention is established as the ‘gold standard’ diagnosis for brain tumour diagnosis in research (Mol and Law 2004). At the time of the study, PredictAI performed at an accuracy of about 80%–85%, depending on the glioma subtypes. 1

Participatory Ethnography

Our research approach adheres to sociology and STS scholarship engaging in participatory ethnography in clinical settings, where clinical practitioners work closely with social scientists to study social dynamics in medical practice (e.g., Mol and Elsman 1996; Pols and Willems 2011). This study was conducted at a large academic hospital in the Netherlands, where both the data scientist and Radiologist A were employed at the time of the study. Radiologist A wanted to test PredictAI in a real‐life clinical setting to learn about how it would fit in and, in case of positive experiences, further develop deep learning algorithms for diagnosis. During meetings preparing a grant proposal for a different project, Radiologist A got to know two of the authors who were part of a multidisciplinary research group studying AI in various ‘real life’ settings, including healthcare. Upon discussing Radiologist A's ideas for PredictAI, the authors showed their interest, wanting to study how algorithms reshape medical work practices. As both Radiologist A and the authors wanted to learn from this project, it was agreed that JD would join Radiologist A as a part‐time unpaid research assistant, eventually for 1.5 years.

JD and Radiologist A spent half a year preparing the test (including the medical ethics review and setting up software infrastructure to download the algorithmic output) before JD joined the weekly multidisciplinary meetings for over 8 months. About 1–5 physicians from each speciality attend the weekly meetings, on a rotating basis depending on their schedule. The weekly composition of attendees therefore varies. At the first meeting, Radiologist A introduced JD as a social scientist and explained the study. Afterwards, an email containing the same information was sent around to all regular attendees. During the first 1.5 months, JD only observed the meetings. During the following 6.5 months, JD participated by reporting the output of the algorithm (details will follow in the Findings section). After each multidisciplinary meeting, JD wrote extensive field notes. After participation in the meetings ended, JD interviewed 21 physicians who had regularly attended. This includes two neurosurgery nurse practitioners, eight radiologists, two pathologists, two neurosurgeons, three neurologists and four radiation oncologists. The interviews were recorded and transcribed with permission. We also included the dissertation on PredictAI and other papers about the algorithm by the data scientist.

It is important to note that although experimental studies in a medical context are common practice (Kuijper et al. 2024), the PredictAI experiment was described by many physicians as ‘unique’ because of the real‐life test of the algorithm. Many physicians characterised Radiologist A as a renowned clinical and research practitioner in the neuro‐oncology community in the hospital and suggested that her position made this experiment possible.

Analysis

All four authors collaborated during the analytical phase, regularly convening to reflect, think and write together. We first analysed the data to look for re‐emerging themes. Here, the collective, deliberative diagnostic practices that the algorithm became part of immediately stood out. We therefore decided to focus on the deliberation of diagnosis and the (temporary) role of the algorithm in this. This iterative, abductive analysis (Timmermans and Tavory 2012) consisted of four parts. First, we reconstructed the set‐up of the test, including how and why PredictAI was developed and how the idea for the test emerged and was put into practice. Second, we followed the framework by Hajer (2005) to understand the dynamics of the multidisciplinary meetings at the Dutch academic hospital. Third, we studied how the algorithm became part of these dynamics. Last, we analysed how physicians reflected on the test as part of their meetings, drawing on the insights from the dramaturgical framework. In the following, we detail our findings, largely following this four‐step analysis.

Findings

The Dramaturgy of Multidisciplinary Meetings

We first provide contextual background to the multidisciplinary meetings at the academic hospital before turning to our dramaturgical analysis. The meetings are organised on a weekly basis, bringing together all specialities involved in brain tumour diagnosis and treatment (neurosurgeons, neurologists, radiologists, pathologists and radiation oncologists). The aim is to discuss and ultimately decide on the diagnosis and treatment of particularly complex, rare or uncertain cases of (potential) brain tumours (cf. List et al. 2025). This involves patients who mostly have been referred by other hospitals and either have already had brain surgery (the majority) or qualify for it. The meetings are hosted by the neurosurgeons, in a meeting room in their department, and are managed by a neurosurgery nurse practitioner. They facilitate the discussions by calling out the next patient up for discussion, taking notes and keeping track of time. As 10–25 patients are discussed within 1–1.5 h, discussions go quickly. Between one and five physicians from each speciality attend the meetings, in rotation depending on their schedule. According to the interviewed physicians, this has been the set‐up of the meetings for over 20 years.

Following our dramaturgical analysis, we will describe the particular deliberative dynamics of these multidisciplinary meetings that the algorithm later became part of. First, the setting of the room involves long tables with chairs in a U‐shape, facing two large screens on a wall (see Figure 3). On one end, the nurse practitioner is seated with a remote keyboard, controlling one screen showing the patient file. Next to them, on one long side of the U, sit neurosurgeons, the largest group attending. On the bottom of the U sit the pathologist(s), alone or supervising a resident, the only one(s) bringing a laptop to the meetings, and radiation oncologists, usually two present. On the other long side of the U sit the neurologists, the second‐largest group, and at the end, the radiologist(s). The latter also attend alone or with a supervised resident, sitting behind a desktop, with their screen duplicated on the second large screen, showing MRI and/or CT scans of the patient. Chairs are lined up around the room, mostly for latecomers and residents. The meeting does not start until at least one representative of each speciality is present.

Layout of the meeting room, including author JD. Precise numbers of attendees differ per week, but the ratio in the image is representative.

In the following, we distinguish three staged parts making up the discussion of a single patient, as follows inductively from our dramaturgical analysis. A closer look at these parts helps to understand the meaning of this dramaturgy and how the algorithm was positioned in it.

Part I: The Information‐Sharing Sequence

First, several physicians share their input, in sequential order. This follows a clear script in which each speciality speaks specific lines of text. A patient is first introduced by either the nurse practitioner, a neurosurgeon or a neurologist, depending on who was last in contact with the patient. They perform the classic patient presentation junior medical practitioners learn during their training (Bosk 2003), stating a patient's sex, age and medical history, including symptoms. This is followed by the radiologist presenting radiological images of the tumour they selected beforehand and providing a radiological diagnosis based on the images. In the case of a patient postsurgery, the pathologist takes the next turn. They explain the results of their analysis of the tissue obtained during surgery and their corresponding diagnosis. The rest of the physicians are in the audience during the sequence. The information‐sharing sequence is tacitly performed, following this agreed‐upon script. There is no one announcing the start of the sequence or calling the next physician.

The sequence was described by some specialists during interviews, especially radiologists, as a ‘gameshow’ or ‘quizshow’. When discussing postsurgery patients, the pathologist's diagnosis is often known before the MDM, yet the sequence of the radiologist stating their diagnosis before the pathologist is never broken. In fact, during the sequence, there is a build‐up of tension from the moment the patient is introduced to the pathologists' diagnosis:

The radiologist says she could not figure out this particular case. She mentions several possible diagnoses but ends by stating ‘I really don’t know what to make of this.’ Already the heads of the other physicians start to turn expectantly towards the pathologist. He smiles mischievously and says: ‘luckily we have the pathologist.’ I can feel the suspense in the room. The pathologist explains it’s a rare tumour, specifying a sub‐sub‐classification. The radiologist makes a gasping sound, conveying both surprize and excitement.

(field notes)

This excerpt reflects a classic narrative arc building up tension towards a ‘climax’ and resolving it (Boyd et al. 2020). In this case, the scripted and staged narrative arc reflects the established knowledge hierarchy of the multidisciplinary meetings (Carr 2010): a dramatic build‐up towards the diagnosis of the pathologist establishing—if not contested—the final diagnosis. This hierarchy reflects the order in which specialities usually get involved when a patient is referred to the hospital. All specialists referred to the molecular pathology diagnosis as the ‘gold standard’: The diagnosis attributed most weight when collectively deciding upon a final diagnosis (Mol and Law 2004). It is therefore not a coincidence that the pathologist has the literal last words that resolve the dramatic tension. Many physicians indeed described this comparative build‐up as ‘fun’ and ‘exciting’ because of the tension of finding out ‘what it will be’. Some physicians, however, question the staging and scripting:

I find that whole MDM is kind of set up like a quiz show … we [radiologists] are always in the ‘hot seat’, it’s nonsensical. Why should I show an image and say out loud what I think it is, whilst the pathologist is sitting there already with their results? … [but] we’re so stuck in that template we don’t even put it up for discussion.

(interview Radiologist A)

A neurosurgeon similarly wondered: ‘Is it useful for a radiologist to spend so much time on it [classification], when 10 seconds later they get the final pathologists' result, which is the gold standard?’ (interview Neurosurgeon A). Following our dramaturgical framework, we try to understand how setting, staging and scripting facilitate deliberation (Hajer 2005). From this perspective, we argue the sequence is more than either ‘fun’ or ‘useless’; it plays a crucial role in the deliberative dynamics of the multidisciplinary meetings. Having a clear sequence in which each speciality takes a turn and shares their expertise brings everyone present onto the same page, from which an exchange of argument can take place. Some interviewees described this part of meetings as ‘laying out pieces of a puzzle’. Yet this is not a free‐for‐all; the strict sequence presorts how the ‘pieces of the puzzle’ should be weighed: in accordance with the knowledge hierarchy (Carr 2010) that the sequence reflects. Although this generally helps to organise the disorganised information in a quick and transparent way (Jutel 2009), the hierarchy is put aside in the case of uncertainty and/or disagreement, as we will show below.

Part II: The Plenary Discussions

Plenary discussions unfold after the sequence, the second staged part. These discussions are not clearly scripted; all physicians can speak, meaning there is no clear boundary between audience and performers. Oftentimes, in the discussions, the pathologist's diagnosis is endorsed, and the conversations mainly centre on possibilities for treatment and follow‐up of the patient. However, this diagnosis can be up for discussion:

A neurosurgeon introduces the patient, followed by the radiologist who says, ‘it’s a hard tumour to pin down.’ Scrolling through different images on the screen, he discusses options for diagnosis and ends by stating he leans towards a glial tumour. The pathologist then takes her turn, saying she ‘found a choroid plexus tumour,’ but disclaiming this is based only on little bits of tissue she received. The surgeon and radiologist immediately join in to make clear they do not agree with her. The surgeon says: ‘But it looked purple, so it seemed more glioma‐like.’ The radiologist says he agrees based upon his images it looks ‘mixed glial.’ Another surgeon joins the discussion, asking how they should proceed. The pathologist wonders out loud if she has enough tissue for extra analysis. The surgeons discuss which kind of test would be needed. The radiologist asks for the symptoms of the patient, which the surgeon provides. Eventually they all agree they will try to do additional testing with the tissue.

(field notes)

Physicians will thus question a ‘gold standard’ diagnosis if it does not convince them. This happens mostly when a radiologist is certain about a classification based on their images, but the pathologist provides a different classification. Sometimes, a neurosurgeon disagrees based on the physical appearance of a tumour, or neurologists question the pathologist because of a patient's symptoms not matching the classification. However, rather than trying to convince others of their own perspectives (cf. Hajer 2005; Oomen et al. 2022), physicians usually go back and forth, exploring different explanations and options together.

Part III: The Formalisation of a Diagnosis

The plenary discussions end with the third and final staged part of patient discussions: the formalisation of a diagnosis. This happens by the nurse practitioner writing a diagnosis down in a designated field in the patient file, visible to all physicians on the large screen. From this diagnosis, other decisions about the patient will follow (Oborn and Dawson 2010). Physicians pay close attention during this stage, usually looking at the screen and interrupting if they do not agree with what is written. Only once the diagnosis is formalised in writing does the discussion of the next patient start.

Almost all physicians emphasised the communality of the three stages of discussing a patient. According to a radiologist:

[Diagnosis] is not something you can do alone. Because there are so many specialisations involved with a patient, I think it’s especially important that we’re all aligned. The expertise of everything is brought together. The neurosurgeon saw the tumour … the radiologist knows how much tumour is left, the pathologist sees what kind of tumour it is, the neurologist knows about the systemic treatment and the radiation oncologist knows about the radiotherapeutic options. When we’re all sitting together, that is literally brought together. I think that’s the whole point of the meetings.

(interview Radiologist D)

Several physicians felt the stages are important because there is no single ‘correct’ diagnosis. In the multidisciplinary meetings, physicians try to ‘understand each other’ and work towards ‘consensus’ (interviews). Radiologist A explained why this is important:

You never have 100% certainty, and if you do have 100% certainty about the diagnosis, you're never 100% certain how the patient is going to respond to treatment. So you're always in dilemmas … You don't want to make decisions alone, not have that responsibility yourself. You want to make decisions jointly and know ‘we've all thought about this together.

(interview Radiologist A)

The dramaturgy of the multidisciplinary meetings supports this ‘thinking together’. Going through the stages of sharing information, plenary discussions and formalisation of a diagnosis ensures all specialists are literally brought together and have a structured, efficient way to reach consensus about a diagnosis. As the cases discussed in the multidisciplinary meetings are often highly complex, this dramaturgy also diminishes uncertainty for the physicians involved: It is clear to all present how and why a decision is made (cf. List et al. 2025). The dramaturgy thus creates a performance in which a diagnosis is collectively produced and sustained (cf. Oborn and Dawson 2010).

Setting Up the Test of PredictAI

Radiologist A wanted to test PredictAI in multidisciplinary meetings, explaining: ‘[I wanted to] see ‘what will we run into when we put this [PredictAI] to practice?’ … and also enthuse everybody a little, make them interested’ (interview Radiologist A). She envisioned a test in which results of PredictAI would be presented during multidisciplinary meetings, without any consequences for the diagnosis and treatment of patients. Radiologist A could, however, not participate as she rarely attended the meetings due to other clinical and research responsibilities. She suggested JD observe physicians’ ‘reactions and responses’ (field notes), whereas the radiologist on duty presented PredictAI's output. However, many radiologists responded hesitantly, unsure how they would present the output and what this would mean for their high workload. Radiologist A proposed that JD would present the algorithmic output instead, and JD agreed. For this presentation, Radiologist A envisioned specific scripting:

I had an order in mind: ‘First, ‘this is what algorithm thinks,’ and then ‘this is what pathologist thinks.’ Particularly because I wanted people to hear our [radiological] assessment first. And also, because the algorithm theoretically should be between radiology and pathology

(interview Radiologist A)

Radiologist A envisioned a sequence in which the radiologist provided their diagnosis first, then JD on behalf of the algorithm and, last, the pathologist. The algorithm thus got assigned a role in the sequence represented by JD, and thereby received a metaphorical and literal seat at the table, right next to the radiologist. A digital infrastructure was set up so JD could download each patient's PredictAI output in PDF on her own computer (see Figure 4) and read it out loud in the sequence, right after the radiologist and before the pathologist.

The Algorithm as (Potential) Part of the Dramaturgy of Multidisciplinary Meetings

We now turn to how the algorithm became part of the dramaturgy for about 6.5 months. We present two vignettes as starting points for reflecting on the setting, scripting and staging, and the symbolic order.

Setting, Scripting and Staging: The Algorithm as Part of the Sequence

At my first MDM represent the algorithmic output, I walked into the meeting room with the radiologist. He sat down behind the desktop, and I sat next to him and opened my laptop. The meeting started. The first few patients were not part of the study. 2 Then we got to a patient that was included. A neurosurgeon introduced the patient. The radiologist took over and clarified some images. I wasn’t sure when he was finished, so at some point the pathologist already started talking. Nervously, I turned to the radiologist and asked him if I could give the output of the algorithm. He said ‘yes go!’, so I interrupted the pathologist. Several other physicians joined in, cheering me on, saying things like ‘Go!’ and ‘Just shout, no worries!’ I said ‘the algorithm predicts IDH‐mutated, co‐deleted, grade 2.’ This corresponded to the radiologist, which was why I assumed the room gave a small cheer. The pathologist enthusiastically said ‘Yes that’s right!’ The physicians continued the discussion of the patient. A bit later, the second included patient was discussed. After the radiologist finished, I took the floor more confidently. The output of the algorithm differed from both the radiologist and pathologist (who proposed the same diagnosis), and many physicians responded with a ‘oooh’, signifying disappointment.

During the first few meetings that followed, responses to the algorithm remained similar. When the output corresponded with the pathologist, physicians made noises of excitement, and the other way around, they made noises of disappointment. From about two months into the test, these responses gradually wore off. When the algorithm corresponded to the diagnosis of the pathologist, usually nobody responded and the discussion immediately moved on. When the algorithm was ‘incorrect’, however, a few frowns or noises of disappointment remained. Sometimes physicians asked how the algorithm could have missed certain characteristics in imaging that clearly pointed to a certain tumour.

(field notes)

In terms of setting, JD always entered the room with the radiologist and sat next to them at one end of the U‐shaped table setting (see Figure 3). She sat behind her laptop, from which she dictated the algorithmic output for patients. When it comes to scripting, the author was actively encouraged by the physicians to take up a position in the sequence, and subsequently, the algorithm became part of its comparison. The vignette illustrates this: Physicians often responded with approving or disapproving sounds. One of the radiologists told JD during one of the first multidisciplinary meetings: ‘It is really like a game, you see how the neurologists were keen to know what the algorithm said?’ (field notes). The vignette also illustrates that pathologists often directly responded to the algorithmic output by stating ‘that's correct/right’ or ‘indeed’. According to most physicians, a person representing the algorithm had an important function: It emphasised that this was a test. A pathologist explained: ‘I think [JD's presence] was important because that person is a confirmation that people are doing a study.’ (interview Pathologist A). Most physicians similarly felt a physical presence emphasised that this was an ‘experiment’ and ‘a tryout’, precisely because ‘that person’ was someone ‘other’ than the physicians regularly attending the meetings, creating a distance that allowed reflection. Overall, physicians were positive about this set‐up, finding it ‘fun’ and ‘interesting’ to reflect on possibilities ‘for the future’ or ‘where technology is going’ (interviews). This shows that the representation of the algorithm by an outsider facilitated physicians to reflect on their own deliberative practices and the potential role of an algorithm in these.

Many physicians were, however, less convinced about assigning the algorithm a separate script or otherwise incorporating it in the staging of patient discussions long‐term. Interestingly, whereas Radiologist A hoped that the set‐up of the test would stimulate people to see the algorithm as not necessarily part of radiology, almost all physicians felt it was and, if anything, should be represented by a radiologist. Almost no physician suggested the algorithm should receive a separate role long‐term or connected it to ‘gold standard’ status. In that sense, the existing knowledge hierarchy (Carr 2010) was not modified by the algorithm's presence.

Nevertheless, physicians still considered the algorithm useful to ‘support’ or ‘aid in’ diagnosis (interviews). Opinions on how this should happen, however, diverged. Several pathologists, neurosurgeons and neurologists suggested the algorithmic output could be integrated into the script of the radiologists: ‘You [JD] don't have to sit there … if the algorithm works well enough, then the radiologist can say “I think it's this, and this is the output of the algorithm.”’ (interview Neurosurgeon A). Some pathologists, neurologists, radiation therapists and radiologists agreed that the algorithm could be part of the radiologist's analysis yet did not need a separate script. They felt that, instead, the results could be written in the patient file and consulted by the radiologist to inform their preliminary diagnosis. Some radiologists, however, felt the algorithm should be separated from them through the setting, for example, by projecting the output on one of the large screens when radiologists discussed their images. They felt this would emphasise the separate analysis of the algorithm, distinct from their own.

Symbolic Order: Dominant Algorithmic Tropes

During the entire study, specialists made comments about AI around me, usually a version of the question ‘Is this AI going to outperform us?’ Or ‘Is this AI going to steal jobs?’ They often said this when I was packing up after a meeting, or on the way out. Over time, as people grew comfortable around me, they sometimes also joked about algorithms when I entered the room. On one occasion, I entered the room and one of the neurosurgeons said ‘AI, AI, AI!’[Artificial Intelligence]. Other physicians laughed. Another surgeon asked me: ‘Are you even real?’ The first neurosurgeon said, ‘Maybe you’re like the terminator’ and the radiologist I entered with said ‘With those red eyes, and a little solar panel in the back of the head.’ They all laughed.

Once, 10 weeks into the test, the algorithm’s output made it into the discussion that followed the sequence. The radiologist explained she thought the tumour might be glioblastoma but was not 100% sure because ‘the imaging was not completely typical.’ I gave the output of the algorithm, which was also glioblastoma. Then the pathologist said, ‘According to our analysis, it’s metastasis.’ When she said it, she looked at me with a bit of a pitiful look, saying ‘So I’m sorry, we did not see a glioblastoma.’ The other physicians didn’t seem fully convinced by this. Radiologists, neurosurgeon and neurologist discussed if it was really metastasis or if it could still be a glioblastoma. Then a neurosurgeon said, ‘Well the AI thought it was a glioblastoma?’ Several other physicians nodded in agreement. However, the radiologist on duty said, ‘Well the algorithm doesn’t distinguish between metastasis and glioma, so it might not be correct.’ The other physicians nodded and did not bring up the algorithm again.

During my last MDM, the radiologist discussed the first patient that was included in the study, and ended by stating ‘I think it’s a glioblastoma.’ She turned to me, and I said ‘This is my last MDM.’ People made sad ‘aaah’ sounds and a neurosurgeon threw up his hands in a dramatic gesture of disappointment. I said: ‘According to the algorithm it’s a mutant, co‐deleted, grade 4 [not a glioblastoma].’ Everybody turned to the pathologist, who said ‘Well we see it’s mutated, the grade we didn’t know 100% for sure, but we’re pretty certain it’s a grade 4.’ There were some ‘oohs’ of excitement, which I assumed were because the algorithm aligned with the pathologist and not with the radiologist. The radiologist raised her eyebrows in confusion … When I was packing up after the MDM, a neurologist sitting close by turned to me and said, ‘This was your last one. Isn’t it nice that your machine beat the human in your last meeting?

(field notes)

Overall, physicians took the test seriously, attempting to integrate the algorithm into the sequence and reflecting genuinely on a potential role for the algorithm. At the same time, the vignette illustrates the frequent usage of irony and sarcasm. Physicians made sarcastic comments in response to the algorithm or asked ironic questions to JD before and after the meeting, and this continued in the interviews afterwards. In dramaturgy, irony and sarcasm are perceived as a means to create detachment from a performance, especially when it comes to sensitive topics. It allows relating to and working through this sensitivity, with sarcasm being more hostile than irony (Clift 1999). The irony and sarcasm used by the physicians usually involved references to dominant tropes of artificial intelligence, such as losing one's job to automation or robots (such as the terminator) taking over the world (Hermann 2023). This illustrates that the test took place not only within the confined social space of the meeting room or even the hospital but also within the symbolic order (Stacey et al. 2025) of dominant narratives about artificial intelligence. For instance, a radiation oncologist said half‐seriously, half‐jokingly:

In 20 years, we do not have radiologists nor radiation oncologists. We just put a patient in an MRI that prints a diagnosis on a ticket. Then they go to the next room where we put that ticket in the radiation machine. It will go automatically.

(interview Radiation Oncologist C)

Although, like this radiation oncologist, most physicians were not seriously convinced that this algorithm would replace them, they were still confronted with this uncomfortable scenario through popular media or their social circles. On two occasions, when leaving the meetings with JD, two different radiologists said their friends made jokes about them losing their jobs to artificial intelligence.

Although such tropes of automation and robotic replacement are rather extreme, there were also more subtle tropes that physicians referenced. The vignette illustrates an instance during the meetings of uncertainty about a diagnosis from the pathologist, and a neurosurgeon suggesting a review of the output of the algorithm in response. This suggestion represents a point of view shared by many physicians during the interviews: In cases of uncertainty, the algorithm could provide more information about whether discussions are ‘on the right track’ or if ‘alternatives’ needed to be considered. However, several physicians felt this was not the ‘intention’ of the algorithm. A neurologist stated: ‘We all discuss together and [the algorithm], as I interpreted it, was not meant to discuss about, more like a given’ (interview Neurologist B). Other physicians described the algorithm as too ‘rigid’, having ‘too few options’ or being ‘too limited’ for tumour diagnoses that are complex and uncertain. In such interpretations, physicians endorsed tropes of algorithms as ‘independent’, ‘objective’ or ‘self‐explanatory’ (e.g., Mayer‐Schonberger and Cukier 2013; Susskind and Susskind 2015). When discussing the PredictAI algorithm, Radiologist A and the data scientist also adhered to such AI tropes. The data scientist stated he wished he could extend the algorithm to include more factors so the recommendations could become more specific and need fewer ‘middlemen’ (meaning physicians). In dramaturgical terms, the data scientist hoped the algorithm could play an independent role in diagnostic practices, unlike the speculations of the physicians. Radiologist A had a different motivation for extending the algorithm:

[PredictAI] gives a very binary prediction. But you want to know: was this 50% certain or 90% certain? That is something I really miss, because that is what we’re used to in healthcare. We know there’s always a range, always uncertainty. And if it’s put so binary, it does not correspond with the rest [of us]. We’re used to asking: ‘How did this reading come about, what method was used?’ … so, I think an essential next step is to be able to give that extra information.

(interview Radiologist A)

Radiologist A emphasises the importance of questioning and interpreting the algorithm to relate to the inherent uncertainty of medicine and wants to accomplish this by adding confidence scores. Some physicians similarly felt the algorithm required more explanation and interpretation but sought this in the setting, scripting or staging of the MDM. Some suggested someone who knows the algorithm should be available to ‘substantiate the output’, ‘give explanations’ or even ‘read the room’ (interviews). A neurosurgeon explained:

You have to be able to answer some questions, so there has to be someone who understands what the [algorithm] is about and who also understands what it is based on and can explain it. They don't necessarily have to be there [at the meetings], the radiologist can state the results and if there are additional questions, then someone is available.

(interview Neurosurgeon A)

This neurosurgeon argues that a person does not always need to be there. Several other physicians suggested that someone could be ‘on stand‐by’ or ‘available to call’ to give explanations.

A few physicians speculated beyond the dominant symbolic order. Neurologist B, who initially interpreted the algorithm as a ‘given’, later in the interview explained she felt this should not necessarily be the case:

… the algorithm has a right to interpretation from everyone to do it justice … In the end it’s just another piece of the puzzle, not the absolute truth. Just like the pathologist does not hold the truth. That’s why the pathologist is also at the multi‐disciplinary meeting, right? To not just throw [diagnoses] around, but to complete the puzzle.

(interview Neurologist B)

Other physicians similarly described the algorithm as a ‘piece of information’, ‘extra support’ or ‘additional perspective’ that should be ‘valued as part of the diagnostic process’ (interview Radiologist D), to give more ‘certainty’ or ‘confirmation’ (interviews). Some even opposed this dominant symbolic order of AI, like a nurse practitioner:

Let's face it: [the algorithm] is not meant to steal your job, it's meant to help you, because sometimes we also just don't know, it's just very complicated and you do want to have a bit more certainty with these kinds of serious diseases.

(interview Nurse Practitioner B)

These physicians see a role for the algorithm that fits into the dramaturgy of their meetings: A dramaturgy geared towards reaching consensus in the face of complexity and uncertainty. They wish for an algorithm that can support these deliberations, not because it is complete or self‐evident, but because it can deepen the discussions. They contextualise and question all information brought forward in the discussions and want to do the same with the algorithmic output.

Discussion

In this paper, we described a participatory ethnographic case study, where the output of a brain tumour classification algorithm (pseudonym ‘PredictAI’) was presented by the first author—JD—during weekly multidisciplinary diagnostic meetings. We addressed the question: How did the algorithm temporarily become part of the multidisciplinary diagnostic meetings, and what can we learn about the (potential) role of algorithms in such decision‐making practices?

We showed that physicians appreciated JD representing the algorithm in person to emphasise that this was a test. They did not, however, imagine a separate, let alone, leading role for the algorithm long‐term. Instead, most physicians felt the algorithm ‘belonged’ to radiologists, taking the output into consideration in the diagnoses they presented at the meetings. A few radiologists emphasised that the output should be presented separately from theirs, for example, by projecting it on a screen. Some specialists suggested the algorithm need not be included in the discussions and instead could be written in a patient file. In their speculations, some specialists adhered to the dominant symbolic order (Stacey et al. 2025) of AI as ‘self‐evident’ and ‘objective’ (Mayer‐Schonberger and Cukier 2013). These physicians perceived the algorithm as too rigid and binary to contribute to their complex and uncertain deliberations. As a solution, some suggested more explanatory algorithmic features, such as confidence scores. Other physicians, however, speculated beyond this dominant symbolic order, instead suggesting that the algorithmic output needed more contextualisation and interpretation, for example, provided by an algorithmic expert present or on standby.

Based on these insights, we conclude that when algorithms are introduced to collective diagnostic practices, they become part of existing dramaturgies of deliberation, with existing local scripts, settings, staging and symbolic orders. Algorithms will interact with these existing dramaturgies. In this particular setting, the algorithm remained mostly on the sidelines of the deliberations, fulfilling the role of a provoking thought experiment. This was largely due to the explicit experimental set‐up of the study (Kuijper et al. 2024), which made it clear from the beginning that the algorithmic output was not to influence diagnostic decisions. However, we feel this can also be attributed to the positioning of the algorithm within the collective deliberations. We showed the dramaturgy of the neuro‐oncology multidisciplinary meetings supports collective decision‐making in the face of the inherent uncertainty and complexity of brain tumour diagnosis (List et al. 2025). Specialists’ reflections on the algorithm as too ‘rigid’ and ‘limited’ illustrate that simply reading algorithmic output out loud, thereby presenting it as a self‐evident piece of information, is most likely incongruent with this dramaturgy. Instead, the algorithm should be positioned in a way that supports collectively making sense of complex and uncertain information.

These insights contribute to sociological and broader social science literature on algorithms in diagnostic practices (e.g., Lebovitz et al. 2022; Raphael 2022; Avnoon and Oliver 2023; Tyskbo and Nygren 2024). We reiterate their findings that medical practitioners consciously reflect on and respond to novel diagnostic algorithms in their work practices. Rather than algorithms ‘replacing’ or ‘reinventing’ diagnostic work (e.g., Susskind and Susskind 2015), algorithms add to existing practices and reshape them in subtle and specific ways. Many such studies, however, focus on individual physicians’ responses to algorithmic output (Lebovitz et al. 2022; Avnoon and Oliver 2023), raising issues such as insufficient accuracy and opacity that limit physicians’ trust in algorithms (Ananny and Crawford 2018; Avnoon and Eyal 2025). These are serious issues that need to be addressed to make algorithms supportive of diagnostic practices. However, we argue that only focusing on individuals’ responses to algorithms will likely be insufficient. Adhering to the sociology of diagnosis (Brown 1995; Jutel 2009), we illustrated the complex, situated nature of collective, deliberative diagnosis. Rather than individual, linear thought processes (cf. Berg 1997), diagnosing involves many different actors, apparatuses, methods, experiences and perspectives coming together in complex, situated attempts to fit symptoms into pre‐agreed categories (Maslen 2017). Focusing on improving algorithmic systems largely remains within the dominant ‘human versus AI’ binary (Hermann 2023), which—as we have shown—often does not do justice to the complex daily reality of diagnostic practices. With Hajer’s (2005) dramaturgical framework applied to diagnostic deliberations, we provide a concrete way to study algorithms in diagnosis, beyond the dominant human versus AI binary, instead approaching algorithms as relational, material and discursive objects. The approach also contributes to the sociology of diagnosis, as dramaturgy can help analyse in detail how a new element added to diagnosis—in this case an algorithm—interacts with existing practices.

Finally, our perspective can help practitioners in carefully positioning algorithms into diagnostic deliberations. We argue this requires understanding and possibly rewriting existing dramaturgies, addressing questions about the script (Does the algorithm get specific lines? Who speaks the lines?), setting and staging (At what point in the discussion is the algorithmic output added? Is someone present to report it and/or provide an explanation?) and symbolic order (How is the algorithm positioned in relation to dominant tropes and narratives?). Such questions should be addressed collectively, perhaps on an organisational level, rather than by individual engineers or medical practitioners.
