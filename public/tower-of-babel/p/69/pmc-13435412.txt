Association of intraoperative end-tidal CO₂ levels with postoperative pulmonary complications in thoracoscopic lobectomy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Zhenming Zhu, Linjing Sun, Yongtao Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung-protective ventilation strategies (LPVS) are widely used to reduce postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) in patients undergoing one-lung ventilation. The relationship between the partial pressure of carbon dioxide in end-expiratory gas (PetCO₂) and postoperative outcomes remains unclear. This study investigated the association of PetCO₂ with PPCs in patients undergoing thoracoscopic lobectomy with LPVS.MethodsThis retrospective, observational, single-centre study included patients who underwent thoracoscopic lung lobectomy with LPVS between 31 December 2020 and 31 December 2024. The primary outcome was the incidence of PPCs during hospitalisation; the secondary outcome was postoperative length of stay. General and intraoperative characteristics were compared between patients who developed and those who did not develop PPCs. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to determine independent risk factor of PPCs. Subsequently, a predictive nomogram for PPC events was developed in R. The performance of nomogram model was comprehensively evaluated through ROC curve, calibration plot, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.ResultsAmong the 383 patients included, 74 (19.3%) developed PPCs. The PPCs group had significantly lower PetCO₂ levels (t = 3.007, p = 0.003) and longer (t = 2.102, p = 0.036). Receiver operating characteristic analysis identified 40.9 mmHg as the optimal PetCO₂ threshold (sensitivity: 80.0%, specificity: 43.4%). Multivariable regression showed that PetCO₂ ≥ 40.9 mmHg (p = 0.040; odds ratio [OR] = 0.516), age (p < 0.001; OR = 1.032), American Society of Anaesthesiologists Physical Status Classification grade III (p = 0.029; OR = 1.876), forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity (p = 0.015; OR = 0.979), and estimated blood loss (p = 0.030; OR = 1.723 per 100mL) were independent risk factor of PPCs. The predictive nomogram model demonstrated an area under the ROC curve of 0.790 (95% CI:0.711–0.827). Calibration assessment revealed a slope approximating unity, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated an adequate model fit (χ² = 5.668, P = 0.196).ConclusionsThe incidence of PPCs in patients undergoing thoracoscopic lung lobectomy with LPVS was 19.3%. PetCO₂ ≥ 40.9 mmHg was independently associated with a lower risk of PPCs. The constructed nomogram exhibited favorable predictive accuracy for PPCs in patients undergoing thoracoscopic lung lobectomy with LPVS.

Introduction

Globally, more than 310 million major surgical procedures are performed each year, with approximately 8 million patients experiencing severe postoperative morbidity and over 1 million dying during surgery or hospitalisation [1, 2]. The estimated anaesthesia-related mortality rate is 34 per million procedures [2]. An international epidemiological survey across 29 countries reported a 28.4% incidence of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) in adult patients requiring intraoperative ventilation during general anaesthesia [3]. Following thoracoscopic lung resection, 30–50% of patients develop PPCs, which significantly impairs overall survival and worsens prognosis [4]. Reducing the incidence of PPCs can, therefore, have a major impact on postoperative morbidity and mortality worldwide.

Mechanical ventilation itself is a known contributor to lung injury [5], which is why lung-protective ventilation strategies (LPVS) are strongly recommended for patients undergoing thoracic surgery [4, 6]. LPVS typically include appropriate levels of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), low tidal volume (VT), reduced airway pressure, and permissive hypercapnia (PHC). These components help minimise ventilation-perfusion mismatch, preserve oxygenation, and reduce the risk of PPCs [7–9]. Under adequate oxygenation, PHC allows the use of low tidal volumes while tolerating moderate elevations in carbon dioxide (CO₂) levels, thereby reducing ventilation-induced lung injury [10].

One study found that the incidence of hypocapnia during general anaesthesia was as high as 66%, and low partial pressure of carbon dioxide in end-expiratory gas (PetCO2) was associated with increased postoperative morbidity, mortality, and longer hospital stays [11, 12]. While PetCO2 levels generally return to the normal range of 35–45 mmHg after ventilator adjustment, emerging evidence suggests that even within this ‘normal’ range, suboptimal PetCO2 values may contribute to the development and severity of PPCs [13–15]. Some studies have shown that PetCO₂ levels ≥ 40 mmHg may improve patient outcomes and reduce PPCs incidence [14]. In contrast, one report indicated the lowest PPCs incidence occurred when PetCO2 was maintained between 35 and 38 mmHg [15].

Although current evidence supports the potential benefits of higher PetCO₂ levels, the association between PetCO2 and PPCs remains unclear. The optimal PetCO2 threshold for reducing PPCs during LPVS has not yet been defined.

We hypothesised that higher PetCO₂ levels is positively correlated with PPCs in patients undergoing thoracoscopic lung resection under LPVS. To our knowledge, no previous study has evaluated the incidence and associated risk factors of PPCs in this specific surgical context. This study aimed to retrospectively analyse the determinants of PPCs in such patients, with a focus on the impact of PetCO2, in order to inform optimal anaesthesia management and improve patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design overview

All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were conducted in accordance with institutional and national research ethics guidelines and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Rudong Hospital of Xinglin College, Nantong University [IRB no. 2026 Ethics Review No. (10)] and granted exemption from further oversight. All patient data were anonymised prior to analysis to ensure confidentiality and the protection of privacy rights. We reviewed the electronic health records (EHRs) of patients who underwent thoracoscopic lobectomy with LPVS at the Affiliated Rudong Hospital of Xinglin College, Nantong University between 31 December 2020 and 31 December 2024.

Sample size and power analysis

Sample size was determined based on the events-per-variable (EPV) principle [16]. Based on a 22% incidence of PPCs following lung resection at our centre, 7 candidate predictors, and an anticipated 10% dropout rate, we collected EHR data from 383 patients (74 PPC events, EPV = 10.6).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) elective thoracoscopic lung resection surgery; (2) age between 18 and 80 years; (3) duration of one-lung ventilation ≥ 1 h; and (4) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status Classification grade II–III. Patients were excluded if they had acute lung injury, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), a history of previous lung surgery, or were not admitted to the respiratory intensive observation unit for postoperative transition.

Anaesthesia and postoperative pain management

All patients abstained from water for 2 h and fasted for 6–8 h prior to surgery. Routine intraoperative monitoring included non-invasive blood pressure, electrocardiography, heart rate, and blood oxygen saturation. All patients received total intravenous anaesthesia with identical induction and maintenance drugs. Anaesthesia was induced with intravenous midazolam (0.05 mg/kg), etomidate (0.3 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.5 µg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.2 mg/kg). Following induction, tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation were performed. An Aespire 7100 automatic anaesthesia machine (Datex-Ohmeda, Madison, WI, USA) was used to control ventilation and monitor PetCO₂, with the sampling site located at the connection point between the ventilator circuit and the endotracheal tube. PetCO₂ values were automatically recorded by the system every 5 min. To ensure data accuracy, PetCO₂ values below 20 mmHg were excluded as artifacts, as these transient declines are typically caused by airway suctioning, patient positioning changes, or other intraoperative events that may affect the stability of the capnography waveform. Anaesthesia was maintained with continuous intravenous infusion of midazolam, etomidate, sufentanil, and cisatracurium. Ventilator settings were based on a previously published protocol [7]. The lowest recorded PetCO₂ value during OLV period was used for analysis [3, 6, 7]. To ensure reliable measurements, the minimum ventilation time for all patients was 1 h, and PetCO₂ values collected immediately after anaesthesia induction were excluded, as these early readings may be unrepresentative due to excessive ventilation during bag-mask ventilation before airway placement.

Diagnosis of postoperative pulmonary complications

In this study, diagnoses of PPCs were identified based on medical records, radiological reports, and/or discharge summaries. The occurrence of any one of the conditions listed in Table 1 was defined as PPCs. The diagnosis of PPCs was made by two independent clinicians who were blinded to the patients’ intraoperative PetCO₂ data and the study’s hypothesis. Disagreements were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer.

Definitions of postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) used in the study

According to previous literature [19], the severity of PPCs was classified into three levels: mild, intermediate, and severe. Mild: nasal high-flow oxygen therapy with FiO₂ < 0.6. Intermediate: nasal high-flow oxygen therapy with FiO₂ ≥ 0.6 or the use of CPAP ventilation. Severe: unplanned non-invasive mechanical ventilation, CPAP, or invasive mechanical ventilation requiring endotracheal intubation.

Preoperative and intraoperative parameters and postoperative outcomes

Patients who experienced PPCs during hospitalisation were compared with those who did not. Preoperative parameters included age, sex, surgical side (left or right), ASA grade, body mass index (BMI), predicted body weight (PBW), smoking status (current smoker), and comorbidities (including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, COPD, and coronary artery disease). Additional preoperative variables included forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity (FEV₁/FVC), haemoglobin, and albumin levels.

Intraoperative parameters included surgical duration, anaesthesia duration, estimated blood loss, lowest intraoperative PetCO₂ value, tidal volume per PBW, FiO2, respiratory rate, and surgical type (wedge resection, segmentectomy, or lobectomy).

The primary outcome was the incidence of PPCs during hospitalisation. Secondary outcomes included PPC severity grading (mild, intermediate, and severe) and length of hospital stay.

Statistical data analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, medians (interquartile ranges [IQR]) or number with percentage, where appropriate. For univariate analysis, independent t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare variables between the PPC and non-PPC groups, as appropriate. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to evaluate the discriminative ability of PetCO₂ for predicting PPCs. The area under the curve (AUC) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) was reported, and the optimal cutoff value for PetCO₂ was determined using Youden’s index. Variables with a p-value < 0.1 in univariate analysis were identified as potential risk factors for PPCs. Variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values were calculated to assess multicollinearity among the candidate variables. A VIF ≤ 10 or tolerance ≥ 0.1 was considered acceptable. Binary multivariate logistic regression was then used to identify independent risk factors for PPCs. Variables with a p-value < 0.1 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model using a forward stepwise selection method (likelihood ratio test), with entry and removal criteria set at P < 0.05 and P > 0.10, respectively. To visualize the combined effect of the independent risk factors, a nomogram was built in R (version 3.6.3) with the rms package to estimate individual probabilities of PPCs. The points assigned to each variable are directly proportional to its β coefficient in the logistic model. The package automatically scales these coefficients so that the predictor with the largest absolute β value spans the widest range on the “Points” axis. The discriminative ability of the nomogram was assessed through ROC curve analysis, while their calibration and goodness-of-fit were evaluated using calibration plots and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, respectively. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Summary of PPCs and the duration of hospitalisation

Among the 383 patients included in the study, 74 (19.3%) developed PPCs during hospitalisation. The specific complications observed were: suspected pulmonary infection in 22 patients (5.7%), pleural effusion in 27 (7.0%) (diagnosed via chest X-ray), atelectasis in 16 (4.2%)-of which 10 were diagnosed by computed tomography (CT) scan and 6 by chest X-ray as documented in radiology reports—pulmonary oedema in 3 patients (0.8%), and respiratory failure in 5 (1.3%), all diagnosed through arterial blood gas analysis. ARDS was diagnosed in 1 patient (0.3%).

Among the 74 patients with PPCs, 68 (91.9%) were classified as mild, 4 (5.4%) as intermediate, and 2 (2.7%) as severe, based on the severity grading criteria. No in-hospital deaths were recorded in the medical records of any patient.

Patients in the PPC group had a significantly longer length of hospital stay compared to those in the non-PPC group (p = 0.036; see Table 2).

The results of the univariate analysis for the primary outcomes are summarised in Table 2.

ROC analysis on the prediction of PetCO2 level for PPCs

ROC analysis of PetCO₂ showed that the AUC for predicting PPCs was 0.614 (95% CI: 0.549–0.678), with a p-value of 0.002 (Fig. 1). Based on the ROC curve, a PetCO₂ threshold of 40.9 mmHg was identified as the optimal cutoff, yielding a sensitivity of 80.0% and specificity of 43.4%. According to this threshold, 233 patients had PetCO₂ < 40.9 mmHg, and 150 patients had PetCO₂ ≥ 40.9 mmHg, as also summarised in Table 2.

Univariate analysis

The PetCO₂ level in the PPC group was significantly lower than in the non-PPC group (p = 0.003), and the proportion of patients with PetCO₂ ≥ 40.9 mmHg was also significantly lower in the PPC group (p < 0.001). Patients who developed PPCs were more likely to be older adults (p < 0.001), male (p = 0.048), and have COPD (p < 0.001). A lower preoperative FEV₁/FVC ratio was associated with an increased risk of PPCs (p = 0.017). Significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding tidal volume (p = 0.024), respiratory rate (p = 0.030), length of stay (p = 0.030). Additionally, patients in the PPC group had significantly greater estimated intraoperative blood loss compared to those without PPCs (p = 0.013). There were no significant differences between groups in terms of surgical side, FiO2, BMI, current smoking status, other comorbidities, preoperative haemoglobin or albumin levels, type of surgery, surgical duration, or anaesthesia duration (all p > 0.05).

All variables with a p-value < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were assessed for multicollinearity (Table 3). The results showed that the maximum VIF was 1.88, and the minimum tolerance value was 0.532, indicating an acceptable level of multicollinearity among variables.

To use the nomogram, the scores for all predictor variables are summed to calculate a total score. A vertical line is then drawn from the total score axis to the PPCs risk axis to estimate the probability of developing PPCs.

For example, consider a patient undergoing thoracoscopic lung resection with LPVS who is 72 years old, has ASA III, estimated intraoperative blood loss of 145 mL, FEV₁/FVC of 73%, a diagnosis of COPD, and PetCO₂ ≥ 40.9 mmHg. Based on these factors, the total score would be 27.5 points. According to the nomogram in Fig. 3, this corresponds to an estimated PPCs risk of 30%.

Evaluation of nomogram model

The ROC curve was used to evaluate the discriminative ability of the predictive model. The model’s AUC was 0.790 (95% CI:0.711–0.827, Fig. 4A), reflecting a moderate to good level of discrimination. The model reliably distinguished between patients prone to PPCs and those without such risk, demonstrating a balanced trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. The calibration curve assessed the accuracy of the predicted probabilities. The calibration plot showed a slope close to 1, indicating strong agreement between the predicted probabilities and the PPCs event rates (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test suggested that the model fit was acceptable (χ² = 5.668, P-value = 0.196). Overall, these evaluations support the robustness and reliability of the nomogram in predicting the likelihood of PPC events.

Discussion

In this study of patients undergoing thoracoscopic lung lobectomy with LPVS, PetCO₂ was evaluated as a predictor of PPCs during hospitalisation. We retrospectively reviewed the clinical outcomes of all included patients and found that the incidence of PPCs was 19.3%. The most common complications were pulmonary infection, pleural effusion, and atelectasis. Receiver operating characteristic analysis identified 40.9 mmHg as the optimal threshold for PetCO₂ in predicting PPC risk. After adjusting for age, sex, COPD, FEV₁/FVC ratio, and estimated blood loss, PetCO₂ ≥ 40.9 mmHg was found to be independently associated with PPCs PPCs.

Postoperative pulmonary complications after thoracoscopic surgery are associated with increased patient morbidity, prolonged hospital stay, higher healthcare costs, and elevated mortality rates [3, 7]. Early identification of high-risk patients allows for the implementation of preventive measures and timely interventions, potentially reducing the incidence and severity of PPCs. A previously published prospective randomised controlled trial reported a PPC incidence of 29.8% in patients undergoing abdominal surgery with traditional ventilation strategies [7]. Similarly, Piccioni et al. reported a 30.8% in-hospital incidence of PPCs in adult patients undergoing thoracoscopic lobectomy or total lung resection using a conventional ventilation strategy without PEEP or alveolar recruitment manoeuvres (ARM) [20]. In the present study, the incidence of PPCs was 19.3%, suggesting that the use of LPVS with 5 cm H₂O PEEP may contribute to a lower complication rate. Although the majority of PPCs were classified as mild, affected patients experienced longer hospital stays compared to those without PPCs, which is consistent with the findings of Neto et al. [21]. Despite these advancements, the overall burden of PPCs and their associated adverse outcomes remains substantial.

End-tidal carbon dioxide is influenced by a combination of patient factors, cardiac output, surgical procedure, and ventilator settings [22]. Several intraoperative factors specific to one-lung ventilation (OLV) may influence PetCO₂ levels and contribute to the association between low PetCO₂ and PPCs. During OLV, the operative lung is collapsed, leading to increased ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch, which in turn increases alveolar dead space and a subsequent reduction in PetCO₂. Furthermore, ipsilateral lung compression and elevated intrapleural pressure can mechanically reduce venous return and right ventricular preload, thereby decreasing pulmonary arterial blood flow and further lowering PetCO₂. These mechanistic considerations highlight the clinical relevance of PetCO₂ as a non-invasive indicator of cardiopulmonary function during OLV and provide a physiological basis for the observed association between low PetCO₂ and PPCs in patients undergoing thoracoscopic lobectomy. Intraoperative increases in PetCO₂ may enhance cardiac output via sympathetic nervous system activation, mediated by β-adrenergic receptor stimulation, and catecholamine-induced vasoconstriction [14]. Additionally, higher PetCO₂ levels have been shown to improve lung compliance by modulating actin-myosin contraction, and to reduce the incidence of PPCs through its effects on inflammatory and immune pathways [14, 23]. However, the relationship between intraoperative PetCO₂ levels and the risk of PPCs remains poorly defined. In current clinical practice, there is considerable variability in intraoperative PetCO₂ targets, likely reflecting the absence of high-quality evidence supporting specific thresholds. Therefore, this study sought to investigate the potential association between PetCO₂ concentration during anaesthesia maintenance and the occurrence of PPCs in patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery.

Age [24], ASA classification [25, 26], COPD [27, 28], estimated blood loss [27], and the FEV₁/FVC ratio [28] have all been previously identified as risk factors for PPCs.

In the present study, we found that patients with higher intraoperative PetCO₂ concentrations during anaesthesia maintenance had a lower likelihood of developing PPCs. However, in a secondary analysis of a global observational study [29], no significant difference in overall PPC incidence was found between patients with low PetCO₂ (< 35 mmHg) and those with normal to high PetCO₂ (≥ 35 mmHg) (20% vs. 19%). Notably, severe PPCs were more common in the low PetCO₂ group.

The identified PetCO₂ ≥ 40.9 mmHg was an independent risk factor during thoracoscopic lobectomy, which was essentially consistent with the standard value of 40 mmHg recommended by Way and Hill [14]. During one-lung ventilation, maintaining a relatively high PetCO₂ helps ensure adequate alveolar ventilation due to increased dead space, thereby reducing ventilator-induced lung injury. Notably, increased alveolar dead space widens the PetCO₂–PaCO₂ gradient by 5–10 mmHg, reflecting impaired ventilation-perfusion matching. During mechanical ventilation, PetCO₂ is primarily influenced by pulmonary blood flow [30]. However, LPVS may alter pulmonary perfusion by affecting intrathoracic pressure and haemodynamics, which could partly explain the discrepancy between our findings and those of previous studies. Although the exact mechanism underlying the relationship between PetCO₂ and PPCs remains unclear, we hypothesise that higher PetCO₂ levels may decrease infection risk due to its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects. Low PetCO₂ may be a marker of underlying physiological disturbances rather than a direct cause of PPCs. Possible explanations are poor pulmonary perfusion or V/Q mismatch, hemodynamic instability, hyperventilation in response to hypoxemia or acidosis or reduced cardiac output.

While the ROC curve of PetCO₂ for predicting PPCs showed only moderate discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.614, the AUC increased to 0.790 after incorporating other independent risk variables into the nomogram model. This improvement suggests that the selection of independent variables was appropriate and that the performance of the comprehensive model is robust. The predictive performance of the nomogram was evaluated through calibration curve analysis, which showed a slope close to 1, along with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. These assessments indicated that the predictions made by the nomogram were in strong agreement with the observed incidence of PPCs within this patient cohort.

Our study has several limitations. First, due to the retrospective nature of the study, the collected clinical data were not entirely accurate or complete. For example, the classification of “current smoking” status did not distinguish between never-smokers and former smokers, potentially introducing misclassification bias. Even after smoking cessation, former smokers may retain a residual risk due to permanent alterations in lung structure and immune function, which do not fully resolve over time [31]. Additionally, key ventilatory parameters such as driving pressure, plateau pressure, recruitment maneuvers and peak airway pressure could not be analysed, as these values were not consistently or reliably recorded, and their absence represents a significant source of potential residual confounding. Future studies should include comprehensive, time-stamped recordings of all ventilatory parameters to address this limitation. Second, although strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to enhance the internal validity of the study, we cannot completely rule out the possibility that hypercapnia may have occurred more frequently in clinically sicker patients, potentially introducing confounding bias. Third, the relatively small sample size limits the generalisability of our findings to broader patient populations. Fourth, PetCO₂ measurements taken during anaesthesia induction and emergence were excluded from the analysis. These phases may also influence the risk of PPCs, but our study focused specifically on the maintenance phase, during which PetCO₂ levels are more directly influenced by anaesthesiologist-controlled ventilation settings. Fifth, PetCO₂ was not directly correlated with PaCO₂ due to the absence of concurrent arterial blood gas data, limiting the precision of PetCO₂ as a surrogate marker for actual CO₂ levels. Sixth, despite comparing the intraoperative PetCO₂ values using the individual lowest value, we did not consider the duration of exposure and individual dosages during surgery, and may also be susceptible to transient intraoperative events. Seventh, the generalizability of the nomogram to other populations or clinical settings remains uncertain, and external validation in independent cohorts is warranted before clinical implementation. As a result, our findings should be considered exploratory, and the associations observed require confirmation in large-scale, prospective studies.

In future research, additional factors associated with PPCs and their impact on short-term mortality and long-term outcomes should be evaluated. Lastly, while this study identified a potential association between lower PetCO₂ levels and increased PPC risk, a causal relationship cannot be established, and further validation studies are warranted.

Conclusions

In summary, the incidence of PPCs in patients undergoing thoracoscopic lung resection with standardised LPVS was 19.3%. A minimum PetCO₂ concentration ≥ 40.9 mmHg was identified as an independent risk factor for PPCs. The nomogram model may provide convenience for the surgeons to predict the risk of patients developing PPCs.
