The multifaceted roles of ribosomal P complex in cancer
Authors: Bokang Yan, Jiaxing Guo, Weiwei Lai, Yuanbing Chen, Zuli Wang, Yuanhao Peng, Shi Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The ribosomal P complex, a flexible lateral stalk structure of the ribosomal large subunit, consists of the RPLP0, RPLP1, and RPLP2 proteins, and is essential for the elongation phase of protein biosynthesis. Notably, RPLP proteins have been implicated in various pathological conditions, including autoimmune disorders, degenerative diseases, while recent studies have elucidated their biological functions and underlying mechanisms in cancer. This review summarizes the structural and functional aspects of ribosomal stalk proteins, their expression levels across different cancers, and their impact on cancer progression. Additionally, we describe the molecular networks involving these proteins and how their regulatory mechanisms contribute to the hallmark characteristics of cancer phenotypes. Targeting RPLP proteins represents a novel strategy for achieving better treatment outcome of cancer patients in the future.

Background

Cancer continues to be a leading cause of mortality worldwide [1]. Studies have underscored the critical role of ribosomes as important organelles that translate the genetic information encoded in messenger RNA (mRNA) into polypeptide chains, essential for protein synthesis [2]. Abnormal alterations in ribosomal function and composition critically affect the expression of key cancer-associated proteins, and are tightly linked to the initiation and progression of cancer [3, 4].

The ribosomal P complex is a lateral, flexible stalk structure on the large ribosomal subunit, consisting of three core proteins: RPLP0, RPLP1, and RPLP2. It assembles as a pentameric complex, with two RPLP1/RPLP2 heterodimers bound to the C-terminus of RPLP0 [5, 6]. This complex is vital for the elongation phase of protein biosynthesis, as it regulates interactions between soluble translational factors and the ribosome [7]. A defining feature of all RPLP proteins is a conserved C-terminal motif, which is essential for recruiting translation factors and ribosome-inactivating proteins [8–10]. For RPLP0, irreversible binding to the ribosome is required to maintain the structural and functional integrity of the P complex [11]. In contrast, RPLP1 and RPLP2 exist in a free form in the cytoplasm, and the dynamic exchange between ribosome-bound and cytoplasmic RPLP1 and RPLP2 proteins is believed to modulate ribosomal activity [12, 13]. Notably, ribosomal stalk proteins have been implicated in various pathological conditions, including autoimmune diseases [14–16], viral infections [7, 17, 18], and malignancies (Fig. 1) [19–22]. Their dysregulated expression is significantly associated with tumorigenesis, metabolic reprogramming, and poor prognosis, highlighting their potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets [23–27]. However, the precise roles of ribosomal stalk proteins in oncogenesis remain unclear and confused to readers.

Overview of ribosomal P complex: structure and biological functions

RPLP0 gene and biological functions

Ribosomal protein lateral stalk subunit P0 (RPLP0), also known as 60 S acidic ribosomal protein P0, uL10, or P0, was first identified as a polypeptide in chicken ribosomes in the 1980 s [28]. The gene encoding RPLP0 is located on human chromosome 12q24. 23 and spans approximately 4.5 kb. The naming of RPLP0 reflects its functional and structural characteristics: “RPL” stands for “ribosomal protein lateral,” indicating its proximity to the ribosome, while “P0” denotes its specific position among multiple homologous proteins [29].

RPLP0 is an integral component of the 60 S large ribosomal subunit and functions as the equivalent of the E. coli protein L10 [5]. The C-terminal region of RPLP0 is nearly identical to that of two other acidic ribosomal P proteins, which protrudes from the bulk of the protein, in an acidic protein-like conformation, forming a minimal “stalk” structure. At the same time, it acts as an anchor for the P1 and P2 dimers to complete ribosomal stems with GTPase-associated sites essential for the elongation step of protein synthesis (Fig. 2A) [30–34]. RPLP0 is a neutral phosphorylated protein consisting of 317 amino acid residues, with a molecular Weight of approximately 34.3 kDa (Fig. 2B, C). Tissue-specific expression analyses reveal high RPLP0 levels in the pancreas, esophagus, skin, and skeletal muscle, with lower expression in the cerebellum, testis, hypothalamus, and cerebral cortex [29].

Research has demonstrated that abnormal expression of RPLP0 is linked to a variety of diseases, including blood disorders such as Diamond-Blackfan anemia and Fanconi Anemia, Complementation Group a (FANCA) [29], autoimmune diseases including systemic lupus erythematosus [35], degenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [36, 37]. Deciphering the disease-specific molecular networks orchestrated by RPLP0 dysfunction holds significant promise for developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

RPLP1 gene and biological functions

Ribosomal protein lateral stalk subunit P1 (RPLP1), also known as 60 S acidic ribosomal protein P1, P1, or RPP1, is located on chromosome 15q23 with a span of about 3.4 kb [29]. RPLP1 is the functional equivalent of the E. coli L7/L12 ribosomal proteins and belongs to the L12P ribosomal protein family [38]. Unlike most ribosomal proteins, which are typically basic, RPLP1 is acidic and is primarily localized in the cytoplasm. Two alternative splicing variants, which produce distinct protein isoforms, have been identified for this gene [29].

As a component of the 60 S ribosomal subunit, RPLP1 consists of 114 amino acids, with a calculated molecular mass of 11.5 kDa in humans (Fig. 2B, C) [39]. RPLP1 is usually highly expressed in skeletal muscle tissue, skin, ovary, and esophagus, while its expression is low in the retina, testis, cerebellum, and cerebral cortex [29].

RPLP1 plays a significant role in the development of the nervous system. The loss of RPLP1 leads to fetal death, brain atrophy, precursor cell arrest, and apoptosis in the nervous system of mice [38]. In Jurkat and THP-1 cells, overexpression of RPLP1 significantly reduces the susceptibility of these cells to HIV-1 infection, suggesting that RPLP1 may be important in the immune response [40]. Further investigation of RPLP1 is essential to elucidate its role in cell biology and disease mechanisms, which may provide new avenues for the treatment of related diseases.

RPLP2 gene and biological functions

In 1987, Rich and Steitz described the isolation and analysis of three cDNA molecules encoding the human ribosomal proteins RPLP0, RPLP1, and RPLP2 [41]. Ribosomal protein lateral stalk subunit P2 (RPLP2), also known as 60 S acidic ribosomal protein P2, RPP2, and LP2, is encoded on chromosome 11p15.5 within a 2.9 kb genomic region [29].

RPLP2 is an acidic ribosomal protein consisting of 115 amino acids and has a molecular Weight of about 11.7 kDa [29] (Fig. 2B, C). It is typically present in multiple copies on the ribosome and exhibits isoelectric points in the pH range of 3 to 5. The nucleotide sequence of human RPLP2 cDNA and the derived protein amino acid sequence show significant similarity to the corresponding proteins in rat, shrimp, and yeast [41]. The expression of RPLP2 is closely related to cell type. It is predominantly expressed in normal tissues such as the ovary, adipose tissue, pancreas, and skin, whereas its expression levels are lower in the retina, cerebellum, testis, and cerebral tissue [29].

RPLP2 is implicated in the development of various diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus [15], rheumatoid arthritis [16] and AD [42]. Additionally, RPLP2 plays a significant role in the life cycle of certain viruses [7]. Crucially, it has been shown to be essential for the replication of Dengue virus (DENV) by alleviating ribosomal pause during the viral coding sequence, thereby enhancing the stability of viral proteins [17]. Targeting RPLP2-mediated pathways offers dual therapeutic potential, combating chronic inflammatory/neurodegenerative diseases and enabling host-directed antiviral strategies against RNA viruses.

Ribosomal P complex and cancer

RPLP0 and cancer

Expression and prognostic effects of RPLP0 in cancers

RPLP0 is an important ribosomal protein, which plays a key role in protein synthesis [21]. Recent studies have shown that RPLP0 is abnormally expressed in a variety of cancers, such as breast cancer, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), cervical cancer and gastric cancer, and is closely related to the development and prognosis of tumors [23–26, 43, 44] (Table 1).

RPLP0 in cancer: expression and mechanisms

In patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [45], ccRCC [25] and oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) [46], the expression level of RPLP0 is significantly elevated. LUAD progression associates with RPLP0 upregulation and adverse clinical outcomes [24], diametrically opposed to its downregulation in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) [47]. Post-gamma knife radiosurgery (GKRS) proteomic analyses reveal elevated RPLP0 in LUAD brain metastases with the treatment of GKRS [48], mirroring its association with radiation resistance in locally advanced rectal cancer [49]. And in cervical cancer, it correlates with tumor malignancy [26]. Similar associations have been noted in endometrial, ovarian and breast cancers [23, 50, 51], where metastatic lesions exhibit amplified expression compared to primary tumors [52]. Emerging evidence further implicates RPLP0 as a potential diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and colon cancer [22, 53, 54].

In conclusion, RPLP0 shows varying expression patterns in different cancers, highlighting its potential as a prognostic marker.

The interplay between RPLP0 and other molecules

RPLP0 and PLAAT4

Phospholipase A and acyltransferase 4 (PLAAT4), a member of the HREV107 tumor suppressor gene family, exhibits phospholipase A and acyltransferase activity, playing a crucial role in cell membrane biology, influencing cell signaling, proliferation, and apoptosis by regulating the composition and function of the cell membrane [55, 56]. Research indicates a direct interaction between PLAAT4 and RPLP0, as demonstrated through yeast two-hybrid screening, co-immunoprecipitation and co-localization analysis. Notably, RPLP0 levels significantly decrease in PLAAT4-expressing cervical cancer cells, and overexpression of PLAAT4 or silencing of RPLP0 leads to reduced cell survival and proliferation [26].

RPLP0 and NONO

Non-POU Domain Containing Octamer Binding Protein (NONO) is a multifunctional protein from the Drosophila Behavioral/Human splicing (DBHS) family, involved in RNA processing and DNA damage repair [57–59]. Using co-immunoprecipitation and mass spectrum, RPLP0 has been identified as a NONO-binding protein induced by double-strand breaks (DSB), interacting with NONO’s RRM1 and RRM2 domains. The radiation-induced NONO/RPLP0 complex anchors at damaged DNA, promoting DNA-PK autophosphorylation at Thr2609, thereby enhancing DSB repair [49].

RPLP0 and MicroRNAs

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding RNA molecules, about 20–25 nucleotides long, that regulate gene expression by binding to target mRNA, influencing its translation or degradation [60, 61]. Among these, miR-4731-5p is a newly identified miRNA that plays a crucial role in various human cancers [44, 62–64]. It regulates RPLP0 expression by directly targeting it, and increased level of miR-4731-5p inhibits RPLP0, affecting cell proliferation and apoptosis of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [44]. Additionally, silencing of miR-582-3p, which has an inhibitory effect on the growth of ovarian cancer, prostate cancer and liver cancer, could also stimulate NSCLC progression by upregulating RPLP0 and PI3K/AKT cascade activation [65–68]. These mechanisms present a novel therapeutic target for NSCLC. Moreover, as a multifunctional miRNA, miR-450b-5p inhibits LUSC via YAP1 modulation [69], suppresses cervical cancer proliferation by targeting RAB10 [70], and targets gelsolin, enhancing glioblastoma proliferation and invasion [71]. Notably, in HCC, miR-450b-5p downregulates RPLP0 to inhibit tumor progression by suppressing the activation of the JAK/STAT3 pathway [22].

RPLP0 and CTSX

Cathepsin X (CTSX) is a lysosomal cysteine peptidase belonging to the papain superfamily that facilitates cell-cell interactions and signaling, primarily promoting cell adhesion, which impacts migration and aggressiveness in cancer progression [43, 72–74]. The yeast two-hybrid analysis and co-immunoprecipitation assays confirm that CTSX interacts with RPLP0 to regulate multiple signaling pathways. Additionally, laser scanning microscopy reveals that CTSX knockout triggers co-translocation of both CTSX and RPLP0 from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, demonstrating their functional interdependence in intracellular signaling and cancer progression [43].

RPLP0 and FXR1

Fragile X-Related Protein 1 (FXR1) is an RNA-binding protein amplified in several aggressive cancers that belongs to the FMRP family and is closely associated with RNA metabolism, translation regulation, and cell signaling [75]. In leukemia cells, overexpression of FXR1 leads to an increase in RPLP0, resulting in enhanced phosphorylation and activation of GCN2, which in turn phosphorylates eIF2α. Critically, whether in a resting state or undergoing chemotherapy, FXR1 is upregulated, altering ribosomal function and triggering stress signals that facilitate survival during chemotherapy [76].

Expression and prognostic effects of RPLP1 in cancers

RPLP1 in cancer: expression and mechanisms

Studies indicate that RPLP1 is upregulated in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), where high levels correlate with increased recurrence and metastasis risk, as well as poor prognosis, making it an independent prognostic indicator [27]. RPLP1 is also significantly upregulated in breast tumor [21] and gynecological tumors, including endometrial and ovarian cancers, where its high expression is linked to tumorigenesis, cell proliferation, invasion, and migration [23, 79]. In cervical cancer, increased RPLP1 expression promotes cell survival and activity, suggesting its important role in disease progression [78]. Additionally, RPLP1 is among the most upregulated genes linked to osteosarcoma metastasis, showing significant expression in both osteosarcoma cell lines and patient samples, and its overexpression correlates strongly with poor metastasis-free and overall survival (OS) [19]. RPLP1 is overexpressed in LUAD, likely due to abnormal copy number changes and reduced promoter methylation. High RPLP1 expression in LUAD patients is associated with poor OS, first progression (FP), and post-progression survival (PPS), indicating a poor prognosis [24]. Similarly, in HCC, high RPLP1 levels are associated with poor prognosis [77]. Furthermore, RPLP1 mRNA is found to be upregulated in human colon cancer biopsy specimens [39, 80], and downregulated in Kaposi’s sarcoma (KS) [81].

Conclusively, the increased expression of RPLP1 across different cancers suggest its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target.

The interplay between RPLP1 and other molecules

RPLP1 and CNN3

Calponin 3 (CNN3) is a conserved actin-binding protein involved in cytoskeletal rearrangement, originally identified for its role in neural tube morphogenesis during embryonic development [82]. Considered a potential oncogene, CNN3 expression is significantly elevated in various cancers, including cervical [78], stomach [83], and colon cancers [84]. RNA sequencing has indicated that RPLP1 is a downstream target of CNN3, which influences RPLP1 mRNA expression in cervical cancer cells. Gene function experiments demonstrate that RPLP1 exhibits similar biological effects as CNN3, and rescue experiments show that the phenotypes suppressed by CNN3 silencing can be partially or fully reversed by RPLP1 overexpression [78].

RPLP1 and MYL5

Myosin is an ATP-dependent actin-based molecular motor that plays a critical role in various cellular processes, including intracellular transport, cell movement, and invasion [85]. Myosin Light chain 5 (MYL5) encodes one of the light chains of myosin, which is a component of the hexameric ATPase motor protein complex [86]. Notably, MYL5 is significantly downregulated following RPLP1 knockout in tumor cells. Furthermore, a positive correlation between the expression levels of RPLP1 and MYL5 is observed in osteosarcoma samples, suggesting a potential synergistic effect in this cancer type [19].

RPLP1 and FARSB

Phenylalanine tRNA synthetase (PheRS) is a tetrameric protein composed of two α subunits (FARSA) and two β subunits (FARSB), primarily responsible for attaching phenylalanine to its corresponding tRNA, thereby initiating protein synthesis [87]. FARSB has been shown to upregulate the aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathway in gastric cancer and plays a critical role in the progression of liver cancer [88, 89]. Interaction network analyses indicate that FARSB could directly interact with RPLP1 in liver cancer. Study of the secondary structures of FARSB and RPLP1 reveal multiple post-translational modification sites on both proteins. Additionally, a molecular docking model of the FARSB-RPLP1 interaction is constructed based on the FARSB protein interaction network [89].

RPLP1 and GnRH1/2

Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues demonstrate therapeutic efficacy against breast, prostate, and ovarian malignancies through dual mechanisms of action: indirect endocrine modulation via suppression of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, coupled with direct antiproliferative effects observed particularly in human breast cancer cell lines [90–92]. RT-qPCR, western blotting and immunofluorescence analyses demonstrated that both GnRH1 and GnRH2 suppress the expression of RPLP1 and RPLP2. Mechanistically, these proteins interact with elongation factors EF1 and EF2, with their phosphorylation status serving as a regulatory checkpoint for global protein elongation. Functionally, diminished P1/P2 expression reduced translation elongation rates, correlating with attenuated cellular proliferation [93].

Expression and prognostic effects of RPLP2 in cancers

In recent years, RPLP2 has emerged as a significant biomarker, garnering considerable attention within the field of cancer research [94]. RPLP2 in cancer: expression and mechanisms

In breast cancer, while RPLP2 overexpression enhances tumor cell proliferation, suggesting therapeutic targeting potential [21], its paradoxical downregulation in specific cellular contexts correlates with protective effects in patient cohorts [95–97]. Its elevated expression is also noted in colon tumor and gynecological tumors, particularly serous ovarian cancer, and serves as a prognostic marker associated with tumor aggressiveness and metastatic potential [23, 98]. High levels of RPLP2 in HCC promotes cell proliferation by enhancing glycolysis [20], and correlate with advanced clinicopathological features and poor prognosis in HCC patients [94]. In LUAD, RPLP2 is abnormally high and negatively correlates with OS, FP, and PPS [24], particularly in patients with bone metastases [99]. Additionally, RPLP2 expression is significantly increased in colon tumors compared to normal tissues, and its loss in the TP53−/− HCT116 colon cancer cell line severely impacts cell proliferation, leading to cell cycle arrest and autophagy [21]. Furthermore, PCA and LASSO Cox regression analyses have linked RPLP2 to poor prognosis in pediatric AML [100].

Overall, high expression of RPLP2 in cancer is strongly linked to poor prognosis. It serves not only as a crucial indicator for predicting patient survival, but also as a potential target for tumor therapy.

The interplay between RPLP2 and other molecules

RPLP2 and TLR4

Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), a crucial component of the immune system, has garnered significant attention in cancer research in recent years. TLR4 not only plays a vital role in the body’s immune response to pathogens, but also exerts a dual influence on the regulation of the tumor microenvironment [101, 102]. RPLP2 has been shown to bind to TLR4 and activate TLR4 signaling, a correlation confirmed by BLITZ assays. In liver cancer, RPLP2 activates TLR4 in an autocrine manner, meaning that once produced within the cell, RPLP2 can influence the TLR4 activity of neighboring cells through secretion. This binding triggers a cascade of downstream signaling pathways that regulate the expression of various genes associated with cell metabolism and survival [20].

RPLP2 and METTL14

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation, a conserved post-translational modification, drives cancer progression through dynamic methylation changes and reader protein interactions [103, 104]. Methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14), a core m6A methyltransferase partnering with METTL3, dynamically regulates m6A deposition in the nucleus [105]. It exhibits dual oncogenic/tumor-suppressive roles in cancer [106]. It has been reported that METTL14 enhances m6A methylation of RPLP2, leading to reduced stability and suppression of malignant phenotypes in HCC cells. Besides, m6A reader proteins YTHDC2 downregulates RPLP2 expression and antagonizes METTL14-mediated stabilization. Collectively, METTL14 regulates RPLP2 m6A modification through YTHDC2-dependent mechanisms [107].

RPLP2 and GPX4

Glutathione Peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is a crucial antioxidant enzyme primarily responsible for protecting cells from damage caused by lipid peroxidation. It accomplishes this by reducing lipid peroxides, thereby maintaining redox balance within cells [108]. GPX4 is typically expressed at high levels in cancer cells, making it a significant factor in resistance to ferroptosis [109]. Notably, there is a positive correlation between RPLP2 and GPX4. When RPLP2 is inhibited, GPX4 expression is also reduced, leading to an accelerated onset of ferroptosis and subsequently inhibiting tumor progression. This finding underscores the vital role of RPLP2 in regulating ferroptosis, particularly in malignancies such as HCC [94]. While this study has established a significant interaction between RPLP2 and GPX4, the specific molecular mechanisms involved warrant further investigation.

Molecular mechanism of ribosomal P complex involved in cancer development

Ribosomal stalk proteins play a crucial role in the molecular mechanisms underlying cancer development [21] (Fig. 3). These proteins serve as essential components of the ribosomal structure and engage with various oncogenic signaling pathways, thereby influencing protein synthesis and the malignant behavior of cancer cells [23, 110]. It is proposed that disruption of the P complex directly impedes cell proliferation and migration, hinders DNA damage repair, and ultimately induces apoptosis and other forms of programmed cell death (PCD) [21, 43, 44, 49, 79, 94]. Below, we provide a detailed exploration of the interactions between ribosomal stalk proteins and oncogenic signaling pathways.

Cell cycle regulation pathway

RPLP0 is closely associated with cell cycle regulation, particularly during the G1/S transition phase. In cervical cancer, downregulation of RPLP0 results in decreased expression of key factors such as Cyclin D, Cyclin E, Cdk4, and Cdk2, which are vital for cell cycle progression. This downregulation can inhibit cell growth and may lead to increased cell cycle arrest and apoptosis [26]. In gastric cancer, loss of RPLP0 correlates with upregulation of p21, a cell cycle suppressor that halts progression in the G1 phase, and downregulation of CDK2 [43]. In osteosarcoma, RPLP1 knockout is linked to downregulation of MYL5 and other genes, with functional enrichment in cell cycle and cellular interactions [19]. Silencing RPLP proteins in MCF-7 cells leads to a specific accumulation in the G2 phase, with RPLP2 silencing resulting in the highest proportion of cells arrested at this stage and a marked reduction in S phase progression [21]. These findings suggest that RPLP proteins play an important role in the regulation of cell cycle.

Apoptosis signaling pathway

Co-downregulation of RPLP0 and CTSX resulted in significant apoptosis in N87 gastric cancer cells, accompanied by decreased expression of p21 and CDK2, indicating that CTSX and RPLP0 play a crucial role in the apoptosis signaling pathway, thereby influencing gastric cancer growth [43]. Similarly, downregulation of RPLP0 is associated with cervical cancer cell death, mediated by apoptosis-related proteins Bcl-2 and Bax [26]. Furthermore, in NSCLC, the increased apoptosis rate, along with elevated Bax and reduced Bcl-2 expression levels induced by the miR-4731-5p mimic, could be reversed by RPLP0 overexpression [44]. Additionally, inhibition of RPLP1 activates intracellular apoptosis-related signaling pathways, thereby diminishing radioresistance of bladder cancer [111].

Autophagy signaling pathway

Study reveals that the autophagic response to RPLP protein inhibition is closely linked to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the activation of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress/unfolded protein response (UPR). In addition to mTOR, a key regulator of autophagy, the loss of RPLP proteins also activates the MAPK1/ERK2 signaling pathway. Notably, ROS serve as a pivotal signal generated by the disruption of the P complex, initiating a cellular response that unfolds in a sequential manner: first the generation of ROS, followed by ER stress/UPR activation, and ultimately leading to autophagy [21].

DSB repair pathway

NONO, a core component of paraspeckles, interacts with ribosomal protein RPLP0 in the nucleus, and their association is markedly enhanced upon DNA damage induced by irradiation through paraspeckle formation, which recruits RPLP0 to NONO via NONO’s RRM1 and RRM2 domains. This NONO/RPLP0 complex plays a pivotal role in non-homologous end joining (NHEJ)-mediated DNA DSB repair: it binds to damaged DNA sites, promotes autophosphorylation of DNA-PK at Thr2609, and facilitates DSB repair in a ribosome-independent manner [49]. Understanding this mechanism has significant implications for the development of novel cancer treatment strategies.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related signaling pathway

MiR-4731-5p significantly increases e-cadherin protein levels while decreasing vimentin levels in lung cancer. And cotransfection of the miR-4731-5p with RPLP0 lead to a marked reduction in e-cadherin levels, while vimentin levels increased correspondingly. This indicates that RPLP0 may counteract the effects of miR-4731-5p on EMT process [44]. RPLP1 is implicated in cancer metastasis by enhancing cancer cell invasion, likely through its role in the EMT. Overexpression of RPLP1 promotes the mesenchymal marker N-cadherin while reducing the epithelial marker E-cadherin, thereby facilitating cell invasion and influencing EMT [27]. Additionally, downregulation of RPLP1 leads to decreased expression of MMP-2 and MMP-9, increased levels of TIMP-1, and inhibition of EMT-related proteins in Hep3B cells [77].

P53 signaling pathway

A context-dependent association between p53 and ribosomal P proteins has been established in gynecologic malignancies, particularly serous ovarian carcinomas. In vitro evidence shows that mutant p53 upregulates Rplp1 mRNA, and clinically, P protein overexpression correlates significantly with high p53 expression in serous ovarian cancers, an association not seen in other histologic subtypes. This suggests p53 mutations may drive RPLP upregulation in select tumor types, potentially contributing to their malignancy, though the link is restricted to serous ovarian malignancies. This finding is particularly critical, as the increased levels of P protein are associated with lymph node metastasis in serous ovarian cancer [23].

PI3K/AKT signaling pathway

The PI3K/AKT pathway serves as a critical driver of NSCLC tumorigenesis and progression, with oncogenic factors promoting cancer advancement [70, 112]. RPLP0, upregulated through CircATP9A’s competitive sequestration of miR-582-3p, activates the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway to drive malignant progression in NSCLC [68]. Moreover, PI3K/AKT signaling pathway has been found to play an important role in hypoxia-inducing factor-1α (HIF-1α)-mediated aerobic glycolysis and cellular growth [113]. In this context, RPLP2 interacts with TLR4 on the surface of HCC cells, thereby activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. This activation subsequently enhances the translocation of HIF-1α to the nucleus, where it facilitates transcriptional activity and upregulates the expression of glycolytic enzymes and glucose transporters, both of which are integral to HCC progression [20].

JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway

The JAK/STAT3 pathway is essential for cell differentiation and the maintenance of homeostasis [114, 115]. Its extensive role in intercellular signaling renders it a critical factor in various cancers, with dysregulation associated with the progression of breast [116], thyroid [117], medulloblastoma [118], and colon cancers [119]. Meng et al. found that elevated RPLP0 levels activate the JAK/STAT3 pathway, promoting the translocation of phosphorylated STAT3 (P-STAT3) into the nucleus, which subsequently regulates the expression of downstream proteins and influences the malignant progression of HCC [22].

Conclusions and perspectives

The ribosomal P complex, comprising RPLP0, RPLP1, and RPLP2, serves as a critical regulatory nexus in ribosome function, bridging protein synthesis to oncogenic signaling pathways [5–7, 21]. This review synthesizes compelling evidence that dysregulation of these proteins is a hallmark of diverse cancers. Critically, dysregulated expression of these proteins is a recurrent feature in cancers including breast, lung, liver, gastrointestinal, gynecological, and hematological malignancies, strongly correlating with aggressive clinicopathological features, metastatic propensity, and poor patient prognosis [21, 23, 24, 27, 43, 77, 100]. Beyond their canonical roles in ribosomal function, RPLP proteins orchestrate multifaceted pro-tumorigenic networks: they interact dynamically with key regulators such as PLAAT4, NONO, TLR4 and CTSX [20, 26, 43, 49], regulate signaling pathways including PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT3 [20, 22], and influence cellular processes such as cell cycle progression, apoptosis evasion, DNA repair, stress-induced autophagy and EMT [19, 21, 26, 27, 43, 44, 49, 111]. Their expression is further fine-tuned by post-transcriptional regulators such as miR-4731-5p, miR-450b-5p and m6A methylation, adding layers of therapeutic vulnerability [22, 44, 107]. Unlike canonical cancer-associated ribosomal proteins that assemble in the nucleus, RPLP1/RPLP2 undergo Golgi/ER processing before cytoplasmic incorporation during final ribosome maturation [5, 120–122]. This unconventional assembly pathway, coupled with the acidic C-terminal domains of P-proteins that appear particularly important for transcript-selective binding, enables these proteins to serve as critical regulators of transcript-selective translation [8–10]. Cancer-specific alterations in P-protein generate specialized ribosome that preferentially translate key mRNAs such as p21, MYL5, and GPX4 to drive malignant progression [19, 43, 94]. Collectively, the pervasive dysregulation, mechanistic versatility, and clinical associations of RPLP proteins underscore their high potential as novel diagnostic/prognostic biomarkers and promising therapeutic targets.

Despite significant advancements in understanding their role in cancer, the biological functions of ribosomal stalk proteins in cancers of nervous (glioblastoma, neuroblastoma), endocrine (thyroid/adrenal carcinomas), urinary (renal cell/bladder cancers), and male reproductive systems (prostate/testicular cancers) remain largely uncharacterized, demanding urgent investigation [21, 23, 24]. Additionally, a more comprehensive and in-depth understanding of how RPLP genes, along with their upstream and downstream regulatory pathways, contribute to each cancer type could yield new insights into the development of specific tumor biomarkers and innovative therapies targeting critical clinical markers. Moreover, future therapeutic strategies should prioritize designing direct inhibitors including small molecules disrupting P-complex assembly or functional domains like C-terminal motifs, and indirect modulators including miRNA mimics suppressing RPLP expression. These strategies should be synergistically combined with existing targeted therapies and immunotherapies to overcome resistance mechanisms. The pursuit of safe and effective inhibitors for clinical application is therefore a worthy endeavor.
