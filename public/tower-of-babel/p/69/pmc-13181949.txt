New approaches to discovering epigenetic rules of homeostasis in diverse mammal species
Authors: Jorge Gonzalez, Diane P Genereux, Kristin Crouse, Bradley Frishman, Allyson G Hindle, Elinor Karlsson, Carla B Madelaire, Lucas Moreira, Valery Forbes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile the cells of some mammals, such as humans, maintain their internal temperature within tightly controlled ranges, the cells of others, such as dromedary camels, experience wide ranges of temperature variation. In order to understand these differences, it is critical to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and their interactions; however, the data available are often insufficient to obtain statistically significant results.ResultsWe develop an explanatory model to understand the mechanisms of response of mammalian species to environmental perturbation on the basis of empirical gene expression data. Our approach is motivated by the novel idea that approximately preserved or reduced inter-individual variability of expression levels upon environmental change is an indicator that a given gene contributes to a homeostasis-preserving mechanism for the species. To identify such genes, we use a simple non-statistical criterion that is suitable even when the number of replicates is limited. We then identify four extreme subgroups of the DEGs, and from these construct an intuitive neural network architecture that best interpolates the data and describes the principal response rules of the considered species. Finally, we propose measures of the robustness of homeostasis (well-being) from these networks based on perturbation analysis and entropy computations. The data used to develop the model were collected from homogeneous cell cultures of skin fibroblasts.ConclusionsEven with data available for just a few individuals, our model identifies extreme response sets of genes, using inter-individual variability to provide a faithful representation of the response of the species to environmental perturbations. Sets of genes identified as relevant in individual species are useful for comparing responses across species. Background

A central goal of genomics is to identify genes differentially expressed between tissues or treatment conditions. Most existing approaches, such as those implemented by De Anoy et al 2024 [7] and Huang et al. 2019 [10], achieve this by calculating mean fold change—the ratio of gene expression values between two states for each of several biological replicates— and then asking whether the change observed for a given gene is larger than expected under the null assumption that the expression level for that gene does not differ between the two conditions.

While such statistical approaches can be highly effective for identifying genes whose expression levels differ between cell types or are altered in disease states [13], they may be less powerful for identifying genes whose expression changes are essential to a species’ response to environmental change. Consider, for example, a case in which three individuals of the same species have expression levels for a given gene that differ greatly under baseline conditions but are nearly identical under some biological stressor. The change in expression of this gene might well meet the threshold for detecting a statistically significant difference between the two conditions. However, methods focused on the magnitude of change could fail to capture an even more impressive feature of the data: the shift from high to minimal variation among individuals under the altered conditions.

We argue that the extent of change in expression variance among individuals has large—and underutilized—power for identifying genes essential to a species’ response to environmental change. The underlying premise is rooted in evolution: if, for a given species, some level of expression of a given gene is essential for survival under a given environmental condition, then purifying selection should tend to reduce its variation among individuals.

Here, we develop a new mathematical approach that uses changes in expression variance, rather than changes in expression magnitude, to identify genes that are essential to how a species responds to environmental change. Our approach can distinguish, for example, genes for which inter-individual variability is small both before and after the treatment, suggesting essentiality under both conditions, from genes that have relatively large inter-individual variability before treatment but small inter-individual variability after treatment, suggesting that their expression level is essential specifically, and perhaps exclusively, under the altered conditions. We can also identify genes that have small inter-individual variability before the treatment and very large inter-individual variability after treatment, possibly indicating cellular stress and a breakdown in cellular function. Finally, we identify genes that have relatively large inter-individual variability both before and after treatment, but that are also very-well-regulated suggesting that they may be involved in individual-specific responses to the treatment. Another possibility is that these genes are related to the so called house-keeping genes [22].

Our approach was developed from transcriptome data collected from cultured fibroblasts in five mammalian species exposed to increased or decreased temperature. Central to our approach is the biological concept of homeostasis, which describes the state of the internal dynamics of a biological entity changing in such a way that its function remains stable. Our variance-focused approach for classifying DEGs according to their response to environmental perturbations is focused on addressing a fundamental biological question: What are the genome-response mechanisms responsible for maintaining homeostasis in cells exposed to environmental change? We broadly refer to these mechanisms as the rules of homeostasis.

We first develop a robust criterion for identifying differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that does not rely on statistical significance tests. Our DEGs are selected based on a simple inequality in terms of the inter-individual variability at baseline. Then, from all DEGs, we define four groups of extreme response genes taking into account their inter-individual variability after treatment. This second filter ensures that we are modeling the genes responsible for the characteristic response of the species.

The rules of homeostasis are modeled using neural networks that capture the interactions among the most important genes. These networks are required to satisfy conditions to optimize their reliability as biological models. We use the neural networks to model the species response to a change in temperature, however, the method is general and can be applied to any perturbation of a stable system whose dynamics are unknown.

In what follows, we develop a definition of cellular homeostasis that is based on gene transcription dynamics. The underlying assumption is that differential gene expression is the direct result of a regulatory response. Specifically, upon environmental change, the “activity level” of the genes related to a response mechanism will change in a quantitatively measurable manner that can be detected numerically (See [15] for recent work on analyzing gene expression). We assume the cell is at homeostasis if the transcript dynamics are constrained to an attracting set, meaning that a “small” perturbation will cause the system to return to the previous state after some time. This assumption also reflects the stability over long periods of time in the absence of abrupt perturbations.

Methods

Cell culture and experimental treatment

Dermal fibroblasts were established from skin biopsies as previously described (Madelaire et al. 2022) from 13-lined ground squirrel (Ictidomys tridecemlineatus, “squirrel”), Sprague Dawley rat (Rattus norvegicus, “rat”) and little brown bat (Myotis lucifugus, “bat”) under IACUC and biosafety authorizations from the University of Nevada Las Vegas. Cells from dromedary camel (Camelus dromedarius, “camel”) and southern white rhinoceros (Ceratotherium simum simum, “rhino”) were provided under MTA from the San Diego Frozen Zoo. Human fibroblasts were acquired from Lifeline Cell Technology.

Cells were proliferated under baseline conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) in a humidified incubator to establish a common garden framework. We exposed fibroblasts from n=5 individuals per species to either baseline (37°C), a high temperature stressor (41°C) or a low temperature stressor (32°C).

Following 24 h exposure at each culture condition, cells were washed twice in ice-cold PBS, and RNA was extracted using a Trizol-chloroform protocol. Prior to sequencing, RNA was treated to eliminate any genomic DNA contamination and other impurities using a column-based method (Zymo RNA Clean & Concentrator-5). Samples were sent Azenta Biosciences for 150bp paired-end sequencing of polyA-enriched libraries, yielding an average of 20 million reads per sample.

Transcript alignment and quantification

Paired-end raw RNA-seq reads for each sample were inspected for quality using FastQC [18], trimmed for Illumina adapters using Trimmomatic [3], and aligned to their respective reference genomes assembly (NCBI Accessions: Camelus dromedarius: GCF000803125.2; Ceratotherium simum: GCF021442165.1; Ictidomys tridecemlineatus: GCF016881025.1; Rattus norvegicus: GCF015227675.2; Homo sapiens: GCA000001405) using the splicing-aware aligner HiSat2 [11]. We used [14] to identify transcript abundances were quantified for the set of one-to-one orthologs across all study species, identified using TOGA’s 488 placental mammals alignment [12].

RNA-seq preprocessing

Our analysis uses the limma-voom framework, which explicitly models the mean–variance trend in log2-CPM data via LOESS smoothing and assigns precision weights to each observation accordingly (Law et al., 2014). These precision weights correct for mean-expression dependence prior to downstream analyses, yielding weighted residuals that are mean-variance stabilized. Therefore, the variability estimates derived from our voom-weighted analysis are not likely confounded by mean expression level.

Conceptual framework

Our aim is to identify the cellular response of a species to an environmental perturbation from information on a few individuals. To do this, we first identify an equivalence relationship for the individuals’ DEGs. Once an equivalence relationship is established we can construct the mechanism of response of the species as a map on the equivalence classes (the equivalence class containing a denoted by [a] is defined as the collection of all individuals related to individual a, i.e ).

We propose as an adequate notion of equivalence relation for a species, at a given condition, that of membership to the same minimal attractor. Recall that each individual is represented as a list of RNA counts that changes in time. This concept is borrowed from dynamical systems theory. A minimal attractor is an attractor in which every trajectory is dense (any orbit eventually visits any state in the attractor). This is natural notion in this case because any member of the attractor gives an equally good representation of the minimal attractor (the equivalence class); and we precisely want individuals to be indistinguishable in our model.

Previous epigenetic modeling attempts assume that gene regulation follows a stochastic process that converges toward a single value (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck) [15]. Recent work by Yang and collaborators used gene knockout (KO) experiments to infer the function of genes. Their mathematical model uses a neural network with one hidden layer subject to a rectilin-ear activation function (ReLu) [20]. The DEGs were selected using the Scanpy software. These authors found that different methods with varying model assumptions and thresholds could not converge to a consensus set of DEGs. For this reason, as well as the limited amount of data available for individuals in our study, we develop a robust criterion for selecting DEGs that does not rely on statistical methods.

We assume that the gene transcript counts are subject to deterministic processes in the presence of internal and external noise. We do not formally characterize the type of noise in this paper. It is enough for us to note that the presence of noise results in a random deviation from a deterministic behavior. By the level of noise we refer to the magnitude of this deviation. The level of noise expressed in the system as related to the species mechanism can be detected from the genes acting inconsistently among individuals of the same species. One constraint of our study is that we only have data for 5 individuals per species, whereas the gene interactions for each species are complex, involving potentially several thousand genes. Nevertheless, it is feasible to compute networks that interpolate a given data point as it is well known by the universal approximation theorem [6] and due to readily available computational power and software. Following the diagram in Fig. 1, we require that f maps the N-ple of intervals of the available data for each gene before treatment (or best known cover of the attractor containing the data) to the interval containing the data of the considered gene after treatment (or best known cover of the attractor containing the data). Under these assumptions and a few additional technical constraints, we compute a network that interpolates the data points and sends the intervals at baseline to good approximations of the intervals after treatment. We can not ensure that the network map is unique but with a fixed network topology we can at least reduce the search to a finite number of networks. To reiterate, we focus on obtaining coarse rules at the level of intervals for the species.

We are also interested in those genes that do not increase inter-individual variability after treatment, with the rationale that genes (indexed by i) taking part in a mechanism present in the entire species should not have a large inter-individual variability ratio:

An increase in variability for the species is associated with low reliability/low predictability/chaotic behavior. This assumption leads to a proposition that can be tested:

Proposition 2.4

A species is resilient to an environmental perturbation if most DEGs have an inter-individual variability ratio that does not exceed 1 considerably.

Another way to express the proposition is to say that the number of genes for which the inter-individual variability ratio is badly preserved (noisy genes) is inversely related to how well the species as a whole responds to the environmental change. Proposition 2.4 as stated does not take into account the potential influence of noisy genes on the rest of the responding network, meaning that it will not be suitable for comparing the response to treatments on different network topologies. However, it could be useful to compare the response to different treatments in a given species. Specifically, we can validate it with species that are known to be more or less adapted to specific environments.

For example squirrels and bats which hibernate are considered to be better adapted to low temperature than to high temperature. One difficulty of applying Proposition 2.4 is to quantify the notion of “most genes”. It is possible that some genes may have exceptionally high ratios, while the species itself is overall well adapted to the treatment. In other words, we do not know apriori what is a safe ratio for a given species.

Extreme response genes: genes modeling the primary cellular response

An important development of this paper is the identification of the extreme response genes in terms of inter-individual variability. In order to motivate the definition of these subsets of the DEGs we should mention that we are assuming that the distributions of inter-individual variability for all DEGs genes with respect to a treatment at baseline and after-treatment, as well as the distributions of the inter-individual variability ratios are right skewed. This assumption is motivated by the fact that these distributions refer to a single species, meaning that gene regulation for individuals will be similar and so the inter-individual variability will tend to be small rather than large. Also, all the individuals are able to reach homeostasis after treatment, so we expect that the ratio of inter-individual variability is maintained or reduced for most genes with the possible exception of a group of genes for which the response is noisy.

Given the right skewness assumption of the distributions, a suitable notion of small is “before the peak of the distributions”; however, due to the lack of a distinctive universal landmark for “large” in the distributions, we considered the 50th, 60th, 70th, 80th, and 90th percentiles but only show the median below for clarity in the presentation. The rest of the data can be found in the SI. It is also important to highlight that the following gene classes do not attempt to classify all the DEGs; but, as suggested by their names, these gene classes serve to focus the study of the response to stress in an efficient manner.

Definition 2.5

A gene is called very-well-regulated under a given stress if its inter-individual variability after treatment is below the peak in the distribution; or its inter-individual variability ratio is before the peak in the distribution of inter-individual variability ratios for the species; i.e, its ratio is less than for some relatively small number (can be positive or negative) that depends on the species and the stress.

The following classification is in terms of the distributions of inter-individual variability before and after treatment.

Definition 2.6

We will refer to the following sets of genes as the extreme response sets:

Key Response genes (K-R-genes or G1): The set of genes that have small inter-individual variability before the treatment and small inter-individual variability after the treatment. We assume that these are the most important genes for the species’ reaction to the treatment. These genes can be captured by comparing the genes in the lower quantiles before treatments to those also in the lower quantiles after treatment. In terms of our adopted convention of small variability, these are genes that are before the peak at baseline and also before the peak after treatment. These genes allude to the central manifold or central direction in dynamical systems theory. These genes are very-well-regulated.

Key Response genes (K-R-genes or G1): The set of genes that have small inter-individual variability before the treatment and small inter-individual variability after the treatment. We assume that these are the most important genes for the species’ reaction to the treatment. These genes can be captured by comparing the genes in the lower quantiles before treatments to those also in the lower quantiles after treatment. In terms of our adopted convention of small variability, these are genes that are before the peak at baseline and also before the peak after treatment. These genes allude to the central manifold or central direction in dynamical systems theory. These genes are very-well-regulated.

Treatment-specific genes (T-S-genes or G2): The set of genes that have a relatively large inter-individual variability before treatment but have a small inter-individual variability after treatment. These genes are also part of a common response to the treatment and should be included in the model. These genes can be identified by comparing the genes in the upper quantiles before treatment (above 50%, 60%, 70% 80% 90%) to those before the peak after treatment. These genes allude to the stable manifold in dynamical systems theory. These genes are very-well-regulated.

Support genes (S-genes or G3): The set of genes that have high inter-individual variability both before and after treatment; but that are very-well-regulated. These are genes that are in the upper quantiles before and after treatment (from above 50% to above 50%, from above 60% to above 60% etc), but are very-well-regulated according to the definition above. Since neither the baseline nor the after-treatment states are constrained for the species, we claim that these genes carry the least amount of information specific to the response of the species for this treatment. We suspect these are the so called house-keeping genes (see [22]) that are connected to many processes, and so their variability is high but they are still very-well-regulated.

These would be genes that are located before the peak at baseline but have inter-individual variability in the upper quantiles after treatment, indicating that the treatment disrupts their regulation. These genes are constrained at baseline and stressed after treatment, in contrast to the Treatment-specific genes. They carry specific information about how regulation breaks down for a species for the given treatment. These genes allude to the unstable manifold in dynamical systems theory.

Species comparison

As noted above, Proposition 2.4 is only viable for comparing the response after treatments for a given species since it does not account for the effect of genes in the Support and Noisy sets on genes in the Key-Response and Treatment-Specific sets (different network topologies for different species). Support genes are well regulated in a sense, but their response is independent of the treatment (see [19]). We suspect that these are typically ancient genes that are crucial to the survival of the cells and are hence always active. We reason that old genes are more likely to be related to many other genes for all species in which they are still present, which in turn contributes to their high variability at baseline and after treatment. However, since they are important to the survival of the cells of the species their inter-individual variability is conserved. The Noisy genes encode non-coherent responses and are correlated to the noise level associated with the treatment. The second argument above can be understood intuitively as the separation of uncertainty between different groups of genes, meaning that interpreting inter-individual variability in response to a treatment reflects noise in the response. Thus, the presence of a strong connection between genes in the Key-Response and Treatment-Specific sets with genes in the Support and Noisy sets would result in noise (variability) from the latter genes propagating to the former genes, contrary to observations.

We can support the above claims to some extent by computing the quantiles before and after treatment for genes found in the intersections among species. This will allow us to classify the genes in the intersection (or at least determine to which group they are closest to). Other measures of stability of the response

We expect that for a system that is able to maintain homeostasis, small perturbations of the counts at baseline are still mapped to counts within the attractor after treatment. We also expect the system to be resilient to small perturbations of the parameters. We construct yet another measure of stability for the models based on quantifying this ideal property in terms of the parameters.

Discussion & conclusions

In this paper, we identify three subsets of genes that, across mammalian species, capture the primary response of homogeneous cell cultures of skin fibroblasts to changes in temperature. These groups of genes act as a robust set of coordinates that separate the dynamics and permit the study of their interactions separately. In mathematical terms, these three classes of genes can be viewed as a coordinate system that diagonalizes the response of the species, in the same sense that diagonalization simplifies the dynamics of a matrix by isolating independent directions of variation.

The Key-Response set contains genes that appear to be most central to the response to our experimental temperature perturbations, as they maintain low inter-individual variability both before and after treatment. The Treatment-Specific genes are those for which the inter-individual variability contracts, suggesting that their regulation becomes tightly constrained in response to the treatment and that they may only become functionally active under the altered conditions. The third highlighted set, the Noisy genes, consists of those genes for which the inter-individual variability expands significantly following treatment. We hypothesize that this group captures aspects of the vulnerability of the species to the perturbation and reflects components of the response that become destabilized under stress.

The network models constructed on these extreme response sets exhibit predictive power and can be used to identify gene–gene interactions that may play important roles in species survival under changing environmental conditions. A key feature of our framework is that the set of possible models satisfying the architecture and constraints outlined in Methods section is finite. This restriction is critical for model validation, as it allows the incorporation of prior biological knowledge—such as known gene functions and regulatory interactions—to identify the most plausible models among the admissible candidates.

Our analysis further demonstrates that even with transcript count data from a relatively small number of individuals per species, it is feasible to identify differentially expressed genes using a criterion based on activity, rather than relying on fold-change statistics or hypothesis-testing frameworks that require larger sample sizes. Activity, as defined here, is a positive quantity that measures the magnitude of transcriptional change regardless of direction. This approach allows the detection of meaningful biological responses even in datasets where classical statistical methods may lack power.

Comparing the inter-individual variability of DEGs before and after treatment provides insight into the regulatory mechanisms underlying the response of a species to a perturbation. In particular, this comparison allows us to distinguish very-well-regulated genes—those that maintain consistent behavior across individuals—from genes that respond in a less coherent or more heterogeneous manner. The Key-Response, Treatment-Specific, and Noisy groups capture the high-order structure of the species’ response to treatment, while the Support genes, although also very-well-regulated, likely participate in many cellular processes and may represent broadly conserved cellular functions.

One of the most significant contributions of this work is the development of biologically plausible network models that can be constructed from a small number of individual replicates. Identifying the extreme response groups is the first step toward constructing these models. The resulting networks must satisfy the relation depicted in Fig. 1, which ensures that the learned mapping semi-conjugates the homeostasis dynamics under baseline and treatment conditions. This constraint provides a natural dynamical interpretation of the response and limits the set of admissible models. Further validation using known gene functions and biological pathways can help ensure that the selected models are biologically meaningful.

We also introduce a measure of species well-being after treatment based on network entropy. The classical Random Walker Shannon Entropy was adapted to our network architecture in order to quantify the disruption of regulatory interactions induced by a perturbation. Because the entropy measure depends on the network structure itself, it provides a standardized metric that is well suited for comparisons across species and treatments. All measures of well-being introduced in this work consistently rank camels higher than humans in terms of resilience to the 32 °C and 41 °C treatments (Tables 5, 7, 8, 9), in agreement with known physiological differences in temperature tolerance.

Although the present work focuses on extreme response genes, many DEGs were intentionally excluded from the network models. Incorporating additional DEGs represents an important direction for future work, as it may reveal finer regulatory interactions associated with different treatments. With larger numbers of replicates, it will also be possible to refine the network architecture, tune activation functions for each species, and improve the approximation of the attractor sets that characterize homeostasis.

In the current study, extreme response networks were constructed for humans and camels as representative examples of species exhibiting relatively strict versus flexible homeostasis. Future work will extend this comparative analysis to additional species in order to better understand how different regulatory architectures support distinct physiological strategies.

Large-scale computational analyses may also clarify the relationships among the different gene groups identified here. While the extreme response sets capture the dominant structure of the response, weaker interactions between genes in different groups likely exist. Experimental validation will be necessary to determine the extent to which such cross-group interactions contribute to the overall regulatory response. More broadly, the framework developed in this paper was intentionally formulated in general terms with applications beyond the specific biological system studied here. The essential requirement for applying the method is the availability of samples from a stable dynamical system consisting of comparable replicates, together with observations before and after a perturbation. Under these conditions, the method can be used to infer the common response mechanism of the system. In this sense, the approach provides a practical framework for inferring the dynamics of complex interacting systems from sparse observational data, a situation that frequently arises in biological experiments where the number of replicates is limited.

A key conceptual contribution of this work is the observation that the extreme response gene groups act as a low-dimensional coordinate system describing the response of the system to perturbation. Rather than modeling the behavior of thousands of variables simultaneously, the dynamics can be expressed in terms of a small number of coordinated directions corresponding to stable, inducible, and destabilized components of the response. In analogy with diagonalization in linear systems, this representation separates the dominant modes of variation and allows the regulatory dynamics to be studied in a structured and interpretable way. This perspective suggests that many biological responses to environmental changes may admit a similarly reduced description in terms of a small number of coordinated regulatory programs.

In cellular biology, for example, the framework could be applied to study the response of specific cell types to pharmacological treatments, such as cancer cells exposed to chemotherapy agents or immune cells responding to infection. More generally, the approach may be applicable to a wide range of cellular perturbation experiments, including nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, hormonal stimulation, or toxin exposure. The method could also be particularly useful in the analysis of single-cell transcriptomic data, where inter-cell variability is intrinsic to the system and distinguishing coherent regulatory programs from stochastic fluctuations remains a major challenge. Because the method does not rely on large sample sizes, it may also be valuable in experimental contexts where biological material is limited, such as rare cell populations or primary clinical samples.

Beyond transcriptomics, the same principles could be applied to other molecular measurements, including chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation, proteomic abundance, or metabolomic profiles. In these settings, the extreme response groups would identify stable regulatory programs, inducible pathways activated by the perturbation, and components of the system that become destabilized under stress. As a result, the framework may provide a unified way of interpreting multi-omics datasets describing how different regulatory layers coordinate responses to environmental change.

The methodology also naturally extends to comparative and evolutionary studies, where the goal is to understand how different organisms respond to environmental challenges. Because the network models provide quantitative measures of stability and entropy, they offer a principled way to compare the resilience of regulatory systems across species, tissues, or environmental conditions. In this context, the approach may help identify conserved regulatory programs underlying adaptation to stress, as well as species-specific mechanisms that support distinct physiological strategies.

Beyond molecular biology, the approach may also be applicable to ecological and population-level systems. In such contexts, genes could be replaced by species, and transcript counts by population abundances. The resulting framework would then describe how ecological communities reorganize in response to environmental perturbations such as climate change, habitat disturbance, or the introduction of invasive species. In this setting, the extreme response groups could reveal stable components of an ecosystem, species that respond specifically to environmental change, and populations that become destabilized during ecological stress.

More generally, the methodology may be relevant for any complex system in which the goal is to understand how the collective behavior of interacting components changes under shifts in external conditions. Examples include microbial community dynamics, neural population activity, and other large-scale biological networks in which a large number of interacting variables respond to environmental or physiological perturbations. In all such systems, identifying a reduced set of coordinates that captures the dominant response modes may provide a powerful strategy for uncovering the underlying mechanisms governing system-level behavior.
