Differentiating primary angiitis of the central nervous system from paraneoplastic neurological syndrome
Authors: Anastasija Solodjankina, Daina Pastare
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryPrimary angiitis of the central nervous system (PACNS) is a rare, inflammatory condition affecting small- and medium-sized vessels in the brain and spinal cord. Its diagnosis presents challenges due to nonspecific symptoms that mimic various neurological and psychiatric disorders. This case underscores the diagnostic complexity of PACNS and its treatment difficulties. Although a brain biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis, its invasiveness, potential to exacerbate neurological deficits, risk of bleeding and potential damage to surrounding brain tissue make it impractical for all patients. In this case, standard glucocorticoid and immunosuppressive therapies failed to halt disease progression, demonstrating PACNS’s typically poor prognosis. This case highlights the urgent need for improved diagnostic strategies and novel treatments to achieve better clinical outcomes.Keywords: Autoimmunity, Neurology (drugs and medicines), Neuroimaging, Neurooncology

Background

Primary angiitis of the central nervous system (PACNS) is a rare inflammatory disorder affecting small- and medium-sized vessels in the brain and spinal cord without involvement of other organs. Its clinical presentation mimics various neurological and psychiatric disorders, creating significant diagnostic challenges. Thus, clinicians often struggle to differentiate PACNS from more common conditions, leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment.

The pathophysiology of PACNS remains unclear but probably involves an autoimmune mechanism wherein the immune system mistakenly attacks the central nervous system’s blood vessels, causing inflammation and vessel dysfunction. Some infections, particularly viral agents such as varicella-zoster virus, Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus, and bacterial agents such as Mycoplasma, Rickettsia and Treponema, are considered potential triggers. Additionally, systemic inflammatory conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus, Churg-Strauss syndrome, Behçet’s syndrome and Sjögren’s syndrome have been linked to PACNS pathogenesis.1

The disorder manifests with diverse neurological symptoms, including headaches, cognitive impairment, focal deficits, seizures and stroke-like episodes in 30%–50% of cases. These can include hemiparesis, aphasia, numbness, visual disturbances and ataxia.

PACNS is rare, typically affecting individuals aged around 50 years and occurring more often in males, with an incidence of approximately 2.4 cases per 1 million person-years.2

Accurate diagnosis of PACNS requires a multidisciplinary approach, using neuroimaging techniques like MRI, digital subtraction angiography (DSA), magnetic resonance angiography, CT angiography, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis and thorough clinical evaluation to exclude other conditions with similar symptoms. The gold standard remains brain tissue biopsy. Treatment commonly involves immunosuppressive therapy, including corticosteroids (prednisone) and immunomodulatory agents such as cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil and azathioprine, aimed at preventing further neurological damage.3 Emerging treatments like rituximab are being explored, particularly for cases resistant to standard therapies like corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide.4

Prognosis is variable, and early intervention is key to improving outcomes, though the long-term course can be unpredictable, with relapses possible. Continued research seeks to improve the understanding of PACNS and refine diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Case presentation

A man in his 60s presented to the Emergency Department with progressively worsening balance disorders, left-sided hemiparesis, diplopia and blurred vision in the left eye, all of which had been worsening over the previous 6 months. His medical history included coronary artery disease, acute myocardial infarction, chronic heart failure (NYHA Class II), dyslipidaemia and prostate carcinoma treated with radiation and surgery, with ongoing hormone therapy for the past 5 months. Patient family history is unknown based on the information provided by the patient.

Neurological examination revealed a stable Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15. Cranial nerve evaluation showed decreased vision in the left eye, diplopia on left and upward gaze, left-sided corticonuclear insufficiency and horizontal rotatory nystagmus. Mild left-sided hemiparesis was present, with reduced muscle strength (4/5) on the left side but normal muscle tone. Romberg’s test was highly unstable, and the patient’s gait was ataxic. Positive oral automatism reflexes and bilaterally positive Babinski signs were observed. Tendon reflexes were more pronounced on the right side (3+). Pelvic organ function remained intact.

Autoimmune disease and tumour markers were within normal ranges, and bacterial and viral test results were negative. CSF analysis revealed pleocytosis (48/µL) and increased protein (1.13 g/L), Immunoglobulin A (414 mg/L) and albumin (518 mg/L) levels, along with an elevated CSF/serum albumin index (18.81) and positive oligoclonal bands. Nevertheless, no inflammatory changes were detected, and onconeural antibodies (anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate, anti-neuronal antibody type 2, anti-Purkinje cell cytoplasmic antibody type-1, antineuronal nuclear antibody-type 1, anti-Ma2, anti-collapsing response-mediator protein-5) were negative. CSF cytology showed no clinically significant changes. Flow cytometry of CSF revealed that 8.5% of the total cell population were B lymphocytes with cluster of differentiation 5 (CD5) and cluster of differentiation 10 (CD10) disease phenotype, raising suspicion of CNS lymphoma. However, blood flow cytometry showed no evidence of lymphoproliferative disorders.

MRI demonstrated signs of demyelination and inflammation, with bilateral focal changes in both hemispheres, the corpus callosum and along the right corticospinal tract. Inflammatory changes were also observed in the left optic nerve. Given the patient’s malignancy history, paraneoplastic neurological syndrome (PNS) was considered. CT scans of the chest and abdomen showed no enlarged lymph nodes or other signs of oncological or oncohaematological processes. Neck and head ultrasound revealed no lymphadenopathy.

A brain biopsy was initially deferred due to the deep and eloquent location of the lesions, particularly in the corpus callosum and corticospinal tracts, where surgical access posed a high risk of permanent neurological deterioration. Given the patient’s partially responsive visual symptoms and the absence of a rapidly progressive course at that stage, a conservative approach with close monitoring was initially favoured. The patient was evaluated by an ophthalmologist, and methylprednisolone therapy (48 mg/day orally for 10 days) was initiated due to the risk of vision loss. Thereafter, a significant improvement in left eye vision was observed. The patient was discharged with recommendations for follow-up, including a head MRI in 3 months to assess the bilateral lesion progression.

One month later, the patient was readmitted with worsening balance issues and new complaints of headaches in the right temporal and occipital regions radiating to the jaw. Additional diagnostic tests were conducted to refine the diagnosis. DSA (figure 1) revealed no evidence of PACNS or other cerebrovascular diseases. A repeated contrast-enhanced MRI showed persistent vascular and inflammatory focal changes with no improvement compared with the previous scan. Left retrobulbar neuritis remained unchanged, without contrast enhancement.

Cerebral angiography does not detect changes in the vascular lumen. No evidence of vasculitis was found.

To address the patient’s neurological condition, intravenous dexamethasone (8 mg/day) was prescribed. Due to a lack of significant improvement, rituximab therapy (1000 mg, administered in two courses with a 2-week interval) was initiated. The patient’s condition remained stable, with mild improvement in ataxic gait. The patient was discharged with instructions for a follow-up MRI after 2 months and prescribed methylprednisolone (4 mg/day) with a tapering schedule after 5 days.

Two months later, the patient’s neurological condition deteriorated, with vision loss in both eyes, tetraparesis, severe cognitive impairment, aphasia, ataxia and pelvic organ dysfunction. Repeated contrast-enhanced MRI showed chronic progressive focal lesions bilaterally in both hemispheres, the corpus callosum and the right corticospinal tract, with persistent inhomogeneity and mild contrast accumulation, indicating disease progression (figure 2).

Investigations

The patient underwent a series of diagnostic tests to establish a baseline and identify the underlying cause of his neurological symptoms. Blood and urine tests revealed no significant abnormalities (see table 1), and autoimmune markers and tumour markers were within normal ranges. CSF analysis showed elevated white blood cell count (48 /µL), increased protein level (1.13 g/L) and raised IgA and albumin levels alongside positive oligoclonal bands. MRI scans demonstrated signs of demyelinating disease, with bilateral inflammatory changes observed in both hemispheres and the left optic nerve. A decision was made to avoid a brain biopsy due to the high risk of neurological worsening, but the dynamic monitoring continued with additional imaging and CSF analysis.

Differential diagnosis

In this clinical case, the differential diagnosis was extensive due to diverse neurological symptoms. Initial considerations included CNS lymphoma, which was suspected due to elevated B lymphocytes in CSF flow cytometry and the patient’s history of malignancy. However, blood flow cytometry showed no systemic lymphoproliferative disorder. The second consideration was PNS based on the patient’s oncological background, but onconeural antibodies were negative. Multiple sclerosis (MS) was one of the differential diagnoses because of demyelinating changes in MRI but was inconsistent with other findings. Vasculitis and infections were ruled out through serological tests, CSF analysis and lack of systemic inflammatory markers. Brain biopsy ultimately confirmed PACNS.

Imaging in this case helped to rule out the significant differential diagnoses. MS is typically seen with well-defined periventricular lesions oriented perpendicular to the ventricles (Dawson’s fingers), corpus callosum thinning and spinal cord involvement—all of which were absent. CNS lymphoma usually presents as homogeneously enhancing deep periventricular masses or ring-enhancing lesions with restricted diffusion, but the lesions here were ill-defined, non-mass-like and only subtly contrast-enhancing. PNS can mimic demyelinating processes but spare the corpus callosum and optic nerves; moreover, the negative paraneoplastic antibody panel and lack of spinal cord (figure 4) involvement made this diagnosis less likely. Myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD) and Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) were excluded based on negative antibody status and absence of spinal cord lesions or longitudinal optic nerve involvement on MRI. Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy also typically has asymmetric, subcortical T2 hyperintensities without enhancement or mass effect, and JC virus PCR in CSF was not performed as the clinical and radiological presentation was not suggestive. Bilateral, asymmetric, confluent white matter lesions with corpus callosum and corticospinal tract involvement, and ongoing progression on corticosteroids with no clear markers for other aetiologies, supported the diagnosis of PACNS—later confirmed on biopsy.

Treatment

The treatment of PACNS in this case involved immunosuppressive therapy aimed at reducing inflammation and controlling disease progression. The patient was initially treated with oral methylprednisolone (48 mg/day), which temporarily improved vision but failed to halt the progression of other neurological symptoms. Standard treatments were unsuccessful, and disease progression continued, leading to the introduction of rituximab. Rituximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CD20 on B cells, was administered in two courses, but only provided limited stabilisation of symptoms. Despite these therapeutic interventions, the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate, reflecting the poor prognosis often associated with PACNS. Given the increased risk of complications, such as infections, further aggressive immunosuppression was not pursued.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient’s follow-up was marked by a worsening of symptoms despite initial treatments. After the discharge, follow-up MRI scans showed persistent inflammation without improvement. Treatments included methylprednisolone, dexamethasone and rituximab, which provided temporary stability but failed to halt disease progression. Over 2 months, the patient’s condition significantly declined, showing new neurological deficits including vision loss, tetraparesis and cognitive impairment. A brain biopsy confirmed PACNS. Due to the progression and poor prognosis, further immunotherapy was halted. The patient developed complications, such as pneumonia and sepsis, and eventually succumbed to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome. As a result, the patient had a lethal outcome due to comorbidities, complications and PACNS (figure 5).

Discussion

The clinical presentation of PACNS is non-specific and variable, complicating its differentiation from other conditions with overlapping symptoms. Symptoms may be acute or chronic, leading to delayed diagnosis. Headache is the most common symptom, reported in approximately 60% of cases.5 Cognitive impairment and recurrent strokes, including transient ischaemic attacks, occur in 30%–50%. Other symptoms include hemiparesis, ataxia, encephalopathy, seizures, dysarthria and aphasia. In this case, the patient had progressive balance disorders, left-sided hemiplegia, and blurred vision, without headache or stroke history, an atypical presentation.6 Based on Agarwal’s cohort study, visual impairment was observed only in 21.9% and hemiparesis in 54.9% cases, while balance disorders were not typical symptoms.7 8 Initial differential diagnoses included paraneoplastic processes and CNS lymphoma.

The literature identifies four PACNS presentations: recurrent cerebral ischaemia with inflammatory CSF changes, chronic meningitis without infectious or malignant causes, cerebral ischaemia in younger patients without cerebrovascular risk factors and chronic headache with cognitive dysfunction or aseptic meningitis.7

Diagnostic evaluation primarily focuses on excluding conditions like MS, secondary CNS vasculitis, infections, neoplastic disorders, paraneoplastic syndromes, autoimmune encephalitis and cerebral amyloid angiopathy. Given PACNS’s complexity, neuroimaging, CSF analysis and brain biopsy, when necessary, are critical for accurate diagnosis and effective management.9,11

Laboratory tests are generally normal, although CSF abnormalities such as mild pleocytosis and/or elevated protein levels are common but nonspecific. Mayo’s cohort study reported 93% cases of abnormal CSF.12 The main purpose of these tests is to exclude infectious or neoplastic conditions. In this case, laboratory results were normal, but CSF showed signs of aseptic meningitis, including elevated protein, pleocytosis and negative viral/bacterial tests.613,15

MRI is the preferred imaging method, with a sensitivity of 95% for suspected CNS vasculitis. Normal MRI findings, especially alongside normal CSF, reduce the likelihood of PACNS (figure 6). Common MRI findings in PACNS include bilateral small- or medium-vessel involvement, vascular occlusions and leptomeningeal enhancement. MRI typically reveals multifocal, bilateral, asymmetric white matter lesions in areas like the corpus callosum, periventricular regions, basal ganglia, brainstem and spinal cord. These changes include infarcts, haemorrhages and FLAIR hyperintensity, with meningeal/cortical enhancement. Angiography is essential in diagnosing Primary Central Nervous System Vasculitis (PCNSV), offering detailed views of cerebral vasculature and distinguishing it from similar conditions.19 11 16,18 Whereas sulcal and gyral patterns were preserved on MRI in this case, which would otherwise rule out severe mass effect or cerebral oedema, the patient’s left-sided hemiparesis and ataxia are best explained on the basis of lesion localisation of right corticospinal tract and surrounding white matter. MRI shows focal abnormality in the cerebral hemispheres, corpus callosum, along the right corticospinal tract, which could explain the symptoms of the patient. The asymmetric, bilateral T2 FLAIR hyperintensities—more pronounced in the left periventricular region—did not correlate with all clinical presentations, highlighting the diagnostic difficulty of PACNS. Moreover, without radiological indication of increased intracranial pressure (ICP), clinical presentations such as diplopia, visual loss and resulting papilloedema were suggestive of a mild rise in ICP, possibly as a result of diffuse small-vessel inflammation or secondary venous stasis. This disparity underscores the importance of relating imaging with evolving clinical characteristics in inflammatory CNS pathologies.

Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG) does not visualise pathological hypermetabolic foci in the brain. Despite low sensitivity, high complication risk and a false-negative rate of 25%, brain biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing PCNSV. However, sampling errors can cause false negatives.14 15 Therefore, results must be interpreted with clinical context. Agarwal’s cohort study showed positive CNS biopsy results in approximately 90% of cases, with lymphocytic subtype being the most common (54%), similar to this case report.

PACNS treatment aims to suppress the immune response and reduce CNS inflammation. Due to the condition’s rarity and complexity, there is no standardised treatment, and management is individualised. Typically, treatment involves high-dose corticosteroids such as prednisone, which is the first-line treatment, followed by gradual tapering. Severe or refractory cases may require immunomodulatory agents like cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil and azathioprine to prevent further vascular damage.1 9 11 Rituximab is used when conventional immunosuppressants are ineffective, although it may be ineffective for PACNS due to its unique pathophysiology, limited blood-brain barrier penetration and possible immune mechanisms independent of B cells. Additionally, the non-specific presentation of PACNS often delays diagnosis, leading to advanced disease by the time rituximab is initiated.9 In cases with significant vascular damage or irreversible CNS injury, rituximab may not provide benefits. Symptom management, including antiepileptic drugs, pain management and supportive measures, is crucial for improving quality of life. Treatment decisions must consider the risks of worsening neurological conditions.

The prognosis of PACNS varies and is influenced by factors such as timely diagnosis and treatment. However, the long-term course of PACNS is often unpredictable, with some patients experiencing relapses despite treatment. Complications and challenges may further affect prognosis, and disease progression may persist even with aggressive therapies. Balancing the benefits of treatments like steroids and immunosuppressive drugs with their toxicities further complicates PCNSV management.1 9 11

Distinguishing PACNS from PNS is a clinical and diagnostic challenge due to the shared presentation of subacute encephalopathy, multifocal impairment and non-specific imaging. PACNS is characterised by stepwise deterioration of focal neurological impairment and MRI findings of confluent, symmetric white matter abnormalities—often including the corpus callosum, deep periventricular regions and corticospinal tracts—with or without leptomeningeal enhancement. On the other hand, PNS tends to affect the limbic system, cerebellum or dorsal roots depending on the type of antibody-mediated syndrome (eg, anti-Hu, anti-Yo and anti-Ma2) and typically presents as encephalitis, cerebellar ataxia or myelopathy.12 19 Onconeural antibodies confirm the diagnosis of PNS, though lack thereof does not exclude it. CSF in PACNS is typically lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein, whereas CSF in PNS may have subtle inflammatory changes.20 Besides, PACNS also has vasculitic inflammation of vessel walls, as seen on biopsy or angiography—features that do not occur with PNS.21 If imaging and laboratory findings are indeterminate, brain biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing PACNS.22 A methodical, stepwise approach combining clinical phenotype, antibody testing, neuroimaging and, if necessary, histopathology is critical to distinguish these two rare but lethal diseases that require essentially different immunotherapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

This case highlights the complexity of PACNS, presenting significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. The complexity of PACNS requires a comprehensive diagnostic and therapeutic approach. Its management remains challenging, demanding a multidisciplinary team. Early and accurate diagnosis is crucial for timely treatment and better outcomes. A brain biopsy plays a key role in confirming PACNS and excluding other conditions like infections, malignancies or demyelinating disorders. Once confirmed, prompt initiation of high-dose corticosteroid therapy, the cornerstone of treatment, can improve outcomes.

Learning points

Primary angiitis of the central nervous system (PACNS) presents with highly variable and non-specific symptoms, which can often mimic other neurological conditions, making timely diagnosis challenging. When faced with a patient presenting with slowly progressive, multifocal neurological symptoms, non-specific imaging findings and negative autoimmune or paraneoplastic work-up, clinicians should consider PACNS as part of the differential—even in the presence of a known malignancy. In such diagnostically challenging cases, early multidisciplinary discussion and timely consideration of brain biopsy may help avoid delays in diagnosis and treatment escalation, which are critical to preventing irreversible neurological decline.

Early and aggressive treatment with high-dose corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents is key to managing PACNS, although the disease’s unpredictable nature and poor prognosis underscore the need for continued research into more effective therapies. The poor neurological outcome in this case, despite early corticosteroid initiation and rituximab therapy subsequently, likely reflects several factors. First, delayed diagnosis—characteristic of PACNS due to its insidious and heterogeneous presentation—may have allowed irreversible CNS injury to develop before the initiation of effective treatment. Second, the utility of rituximab in PACNS is uncertain, as the disorder is envisioned to involve B cell–dependent and B cell–independent immune mechanisms. Rituximab’s limited ability to cross the blood–brain barrier also reduces its therapeutic promise, especially in those with chronic or late disease. Third, by the time immunotherapy was escalated, there was likely irreversible axonal and white matter damage. Lastly, the presence of comorbidities, infectious complications of immunosuppression and a rapid functional decline all portended a poor prognosis. This highlights the importance of early identification, including brain biopsy, and aggressive treatment before irreversible structural damage occurs.
