Proteins required for stereocilia elongation during mammalian hair cell development ensure precise and steady heights during adult life
Authors: Elli I. Hartig, Matthew Day, Amandine Jarysta, Basile Tarchini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMammalian auditory hair cells (HCs) are not naturally regenerative and must preserve their elaborate structure to ensure lifelong hearing. Stereocilia, the actin-based projections at the HC surface that detect sound vibration, are particularly vulnerable to damage incurred from noise and aging. We show that the tip-localized protein module GPSM2–GNAI required for stereocilia development is also involved in stereocilia maintenance in mature cells. Inactivating Gpsm2 in adult mouse HCs results in low-frequency hearing deficits and stereocilia height reduction proportional to the region reported to turn over actin at the distal tip. Molecular insight into how actin exchange ensures stable height in mature stereocilia will help clarify whether insults during life shift this balance and compromise auditory function.

Hearing and balance are contingent on sensory hair cells (HCs) in the inner ear, which transduce sound waves and other mechanical signals to afferent neurons. Signal detection relies on the highly ordered HC stereocilia, actin-based protrusions of the apical membrane that form rows of graded height and work cohesively as the hair bundle. In mammals, cochlear HCs are postmitotic at birth and do not regenerate when lost to disease or injury. Thus, one lifelong challenge facing HCs is to preserve an intact hair bundle despite stereocilia experiencing a continuous barrage of stimuli. In contrast to highly dynamic actin-based cellular protrusions like filopodia or microvilli, stereocilia are very stable postdevelopment. It is thus imperative to understand how adult stereocilia are normally maintained and to what extent repair is possible following injury—two broad questions that remain largely unanswered (1).

During development, mouse stereocilia are primarily elongating and/or widening by adding new actin filaments, although hair bundle maturation also involves shrinkage and resorption of shorter rows (2, 3). Elongating stereocilia incorporate new actin at the stereocilia tip to grow the shaft (4, 5). In contrast, detailed studies in mature HCs clarified that adult stereocilia only incorporate new actin in a ~0.5 µm region at the distal tip (6–9). Actin in stereocilia shafts is thus remarkably stable during adult life, whereas its actin cross-linkers undergo rapid exchange (10, 11). These results suggest that actin turnover at tips reflects an equilibrium between actin polymerization and dissociation which is required to maintain precise, invariant stereocilia height during life. However, this assumption has yet to be tested functionally.

Constitutive inactivation of either of the two cytoplasmic actins that comprise stereocilia, beta-actin (ACTB) or gamma-actin (ACTG1), produces hair bundles that are indistinguishable from controls at early stages, but later suffer from stereocilia degeneration (12). In humans, mutations in the ACTG1 gene are associated with dominant, progressive hearing loss DFNA20/26 (13, 14). Conditional deletion of mouse Actb or Actg1 in adult HCs shows loss of the corresponding ablated actin protein only at the distal stereocilia tip, unchanged even after a months-long chase period (6, 7). However, previous studies did not report on hair bundle structure or auditory status following adult knock-out of cytoplasmic actins. In addition, we are not aware of studies where actin regulators or other stereocilia proteins were inactivated specifically in adult HCs, leaving the role of the tip compartment unknown.

Stereocilia tips are home to row-specific protein complexes that influence actin behavior. Shorter stereocilia rows carry ion channels responsible for mechanoelectrical transduction (MET) (15). Actin dynamics in these stereocilia is influenced by MET activity (2, 16, 17), possibly via the influx of ions or by way of MET-recruited actin-interacting proteins, for example, the actin severing duo ADF/cofilin (18) or the I-BAR protein BAIAP2L2 (19, 20). Shorter row tips are also subject to tensile force from extracellular tip-links that connect the MET channel to the adjacent taller row (21), with resulting membrane tenting predicted to regulate actin polymerization (5, 22, 23). Interestingly, factors that regulate dimensions of the shorter rows appear generally dispensable for the height of the tallest (row 1) stereocilia, which lack both MET channels and distal membrane tension from tip-links.

In developing HCs, row 1 stereocilia identity and height are established by the MYO15A motor and its cargo: WHRN, EPS8, GPSM2, and GNAI, together referred to as the elongation complex (EC) (5, 24–31). Constitutive loss of any EC member results in stunted row 1 stereocilia and embryonic-like hair bundles where supernumerary rows persist into adulthood. The EC is largely limited to row 1 tips from postnatal (P) day 7 onward and is assumed to boost actin polymerization at barbed ends during development. EC enrichment and restriction to row 1 is dependent on the GPSM2–GNAI module which first occupies the flat bare zone of the apical membrane abutting row 1 stereocilia (29, 31, 32). GPSM2–GNAI presence at the bare zone wanes from P2 while its row-1-exclusive enrichment at stereocilia tips initiated during embryogenesis increases.

EC proteins have been studied strictly in the context of development using constitutive mutant strains, but intriguingly, pioneer members (MYO15A, WHRN, and EPS8) were also sporadically noted at the tip of mature (>P28) stereocilia that no longer elongate and maintain a precise height (5, 28, 33–36). As the EC is essential for actin incorporation during elongation and remains near the site of active actin turnover in adults, the EC may have an adult function related to height maintenance. To address this question, EC proteins need to be abolished after normal hair bundle development is complete because extreme bundle dysmorphology and profound deafness in all EC constitutive mutants obscure any potential adult function.

Here, we inactivate Gpsm2 or Gnai3 in adult HCs using either AAV-Cre surgical delivery or the inducible, HC-specific Gfi1CreERT driver (37). Twelve weeks postinactivation, we observe a ~15% reduction in row 1 stereocilia height in inner HCs (IHCs) which coincides with auditory deficits most severe at low frequencies. We also establish that GPSM2 is required for proper enrichment of the EC complex at large at adult row 1 tips. By inactivating stereocilia proteins in adult HCs after normal development is completed, this work provides evidence of the importance of the EC complex in mature stereocilia.

Results

The EC Persists at the Tip of Mature Stereocilia that no Longer Elongate.

To verify that EC proteins are retained at stereocilia tips well after HC development and elongation are complete, we immunolabeled EPS8, MYO15A, WHRN, GPSM2, and GNAI2/3 in 18 wk (4.5 mo) old mice. Close paralog GNAI2 and GNAI3 are both present at stereocilia tips and most antibodies cannot distinguish them; hereafter, we thus refer to signals as GNAI (38). Each protein shared a similar enrichment at row 1 stereocilia tips in mature cochlear IHCs and GPSM2–GNAI no longer occupied the bare zone (Fig. 1 A–E; arrows). All five proteins are similarly required for stereocilia elongation during development, but their role in mature, fixed-height stereocilia remains unknown. To address this question, we proceeded to inactivate EC proteins after hair bundle development is completed normally, focusing specifically on IHCs where row 1 height can be reliably assessed with confocal microscopy.

Adult-Stage Inactivation of Gpsm2 or Gnai3 Reduces Stereocilia Height.

We hypothesized that the EC could serve to maintain row 1 stereocilia morphology in adults, and specifically to regulate the distal tip compartment where these proteins are close to the spatially restricted actin turnover. To test this idea, we employed mouse strains with flox alleles in Gpsm2 or Gnai3. In the absence of Cre recombination, the respective proteins are present and hair bundles appear to develop normally (31, 38). To limit gene inactivation to mature HCs only, an AAV-Cre-IRES-Egfp viral vector was delivered to the left inner ear of 6-wk-old Gpsm2flox/del or Gnai3flox/neo mice via posterior semicircular canal injection surgery (Fig. 2A, Materials and Methods).

EGFP+ cells indicated successful viral transduction and expression of Cre recombinase (Fig. 2B). EGFP+ cochlear IHCs had largely absent GPSM2 (in Gpsm2flox/del) or GNAI (in Gnai3flox/neo) signals at stereocilia tips 12 wk postsurgery (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). EGFP+ IHCs were compared to a nontransduced, EGFP− neighboring cell, or when not possible due to high transduction efficiency, to a control cell from the unoperated right inner ear of the same animal at a matched position along the cochlea.

We assessed IHC stereocilia height after a 4-, 6-, 8-, or 12-wk chase period (Fig. 2A). To measure row 1 stereocilia height, we acquired confocal Z-stacks at 0.15 µm intervals and used Imaris Microscopy Image Analysis Software to track the position of the same stereocilium in each Z-slice (Materials and Methods).

Stereocilia height is well known to be graded along the length of the cochlea (40, 41), and accordingly, control IHCs (EGFP−) showed increasing row 1 heights from cochlear base to mid to apex positions. Values were consistent across the different chase times, as expected since mature stereocilia maintain a stable height (Fig. 2C). In contrast, row 1 IHC stereocilia experienced a progressive height reduction with time at all cochlear positions (Fig. 2C). Gpsm2 knock-out IHCs had shorter row 1 stereocilia compared to control neighbors by 4 wk postsurgery at the cochlear base, and by 6 wk at the mid and apex (Fig. 2C). Remarkably, by 12 wk posttransduction, the reduction in stereocilia height was relatively uniform at all cochlear positions despite graded heights: The mean reduction (±margin of error; 95% CI) was 568 ± 62 nm (17 ± 2% of height) at the base, 695 ± 166 nm (15 ± 4%) at the mid, and 660 ± 82 nm at the apex (12 ± 1%). In contrast, we did not observe a change in the height of row 2 stereocilia, where GPSM2 is not enriched (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B, mid-cochlear region). These results suggest that GPSM2 is required to maintain the distal region of row 1 stereocilia where it normally resides and that this tip compartment is about similarly sized at all cochlear positions despite large differences in total stereocilia heights: control IHC mean (±SD) height of 3.42 ± 0.09 µm at the base compared to 5.51 ± 0.130 µm at the apex.

Compared to GPSM2, loss of GNAI3 led to a more modest, but still statistically significant reduction in IHC row 1 stereocilia height over time. The stereocilia of Gnai3flox/neo IHCs expressing Cre (EGFP+) were indiscernible from nontransduced (EGFP−) neighboring IHCs at 4 wk postsurgery, but a mean height reduction of 130 ± 51 nm (2.9 ± 1%) was measured at 12 wk at the mid-cochlear region (Fig. 2D). The more modest effect in Gnai3 compared to Gpsm2 mutants could reflect functional redundancy between GNAI3 and GNAI2 resulting in an incomplete loss of total GNAI function, as observed during development in constitutive Gnai3 mutants ((30, 38, 42). We therefore focused on Gpsm2 mutants in further experiments.

GPSM2 Is Long-Lived at Adult Stereocilia Tips.

Although our AAV-Cre strategy was effective to ablate proteins at stereocilia tips (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A), signal quantification revealed that residual GPSM2 protein persisted for weeks after Cre-mediated Gpsm2 deletion. Estimated by percentage of GPSM2 immunostaining in control neighbors (EGFP−), knock-out (EGFP+) IHCs retained 15 ± 9% and 11 ± 9% of protein, respectively, at 4 and 6 wk posttransduction (mean ± SD; Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C and D). While a single Gpsm2 copy needed to be recombined in Gpsm2flox/del animals to achieve a knock-out, GPSM2 signals only reached near-0% values by 8 to 12 wk posttransduction (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C and D). This could arguably reflect delayed Cre expression and recombination following AAV transduction and, if so, would artifactually suggest that GPSM2 is long-lived at tips. To assess this possibility, we delivered the same AAV-Cre-IRES-Egfp virus in R26LSL-tdTomato reporter mice. TdTomato signals could be detected 48 and 72 h postsurgery (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–C), indicating rapid recombination relative to the weeks-long period in which GPSM2 signals were retained.

To confirm long-lived GPSM2 at adult stereocilia tips, we generated a knock-in mouse strain where HaloTag was fused N-terminal to GPSM2. HaloTag is a self-labeling reporter that binds covalently and irreversibly to a versatile set of haloalkane ligands (43, 44). This system can be advantageously used to examine protein turnover with pulse–chase approaches. We used posterior canal surgery in 6-wk-old Gpsm2HaloTag/+ animals and delivered 7-bromo-1-heptanol (7BRO; 100 µM), an unconjugated HaloTag ligand that can act as a blocker (45) (Fig. 3A). Each surgical cohort included one unoperated littermate used as an unblocked control for normalization (Materials and Methods). We collected and fixed inner ears at different time intervals postsurgery (0 to 42 d/6 wk) and permeabilized the tissue before incubating with a fluorescent, TMR-conjugated HaloTag ligand (Fig. 3A). In unoperated animals, TMR signals were highly enriched at row 1 stereocilia tips in IHCs, as expected for the GPSM2 protein (Fig. 3B; compare to Fig. 1D). In contrast, up to 3 d post-7BRO injection, no or minimal TMR signals were recorded in operated ears (Fig. 3 B and C). We concluded that 7BRO effectively bound HaloTag-GPSM2 proteins present in IHCs at the time of surgery, allowing us to monitor newly synthesized HaloTag-GPSM2 by tracking TMR signal emergence at later time points (Fig. 3 A and B). TMR signals steadily increased from 3 to 14 d postsurgery in an exponential pattern but did not reach control levels even after 4 or 6 wk. This suggests that residual 7BRO-bound HaloTag-GPSM2 proteins remained at stereocilia tips until at least 6 wk postinjection (Fig. 3C). Measuring HaloTag-GPSM2 protein recovery thus produced results compatible with measuring GPSM2 protein decay via immunolabeling, where residual GPSM2 was retained at stereocilia tips as late as 6 wk after genetic ablation (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C and D).

We applied a nonlinear regression model to perform a robust comparison of protein turnover estimates obtained with the two methods above. We reverse-transformed HaloTag-GPSM2 TMR signal emergence measurements (Fig. 3C) into 7BRO-blocked HaloTag-GPSM2 decay measurements to facilitate comparison with signal decay measured by immunolabeling (Fig. 3 D and E; Materials and Methods). One-phase association curves were fit to both datasets with equal constraints (Fig. 3 D and E). Despite the different methodologies and time windows studied, GPSM2 half-life estimates (t1/2) and rate constants (k) were remarkably similar between the two approaches (k = 0.068, t1/2 = 10.14 d for antibody signals versus k = 0.077, t1/2 = 9.05 d for HaloTag-GPSM2 signals) (Fig. 3 D and E). We conclude that GPSM2 is long-lived at stereocilia tips, possibly explaining why a height reduction is only observed 4 to 6 wk after Gpsm2 inactivation (Fig. 2C).

Adult-Stage Inactivation of Gpsm2 Using Gfi1CreERT.

Having established a time frame to investigate, we next aimed to assess the impact of row 1 height reduction on auditory function. However, AAV-Cre mediated Gpsm2 knock-out via posterior canal injection surgery (Fig. 2) posed practical limitations because viral transduction efficiency proved highly variable (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). In the course of this study, we thus obtained a tamoxifen (TAM)-inducible, HC-specific Cre driver mouse strain, Gfi1CreERT (Gfi1-GCE) (37) to complement AAV-Cre-based approaches. TAM was administered to Gfi1CreERT/+; Gpsm2flox/del mice via intraperitoneal injection at 6 wk of age (Fig. 4A). Twelve weeks postinjection, Gfi1CreERT-mediated deletion of Gpsm2 resulted in GPSM2 protein depletion in both IHCs and outer HCs (OHCs) as well as shorter row 1 stereocilia in IHCs, comparable to AAV-Cre mediated knock-out (Fig. 4 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–C; compare to Fig. 2 B and E). We did not attempt to quantify height in OHCs, which have shorter, thinner stereocilia that are challenging to measure even with Airyscan microscopy. In TAM-injected Gfi1CreERT; Gpsm2flox/del mice, scanning electron microscopy revealed recombined IHCs with short row 1 stereocilia, distinguishable from occasional IHCs with longer stereocilia that likely represent nonrecombined “escapers” (Fig. 4D). In contrast, control samples from TAM-injected Gfi1+/+ littermates showed stereocilia of uniform height. Together, these data verify that GPSM2 loss and consequent row 1 height reduction can be reproduced without viral transduction.

To ensure high recombination efficiency, we administered three successive doses of TAM over 3 consecutive days (Fig. 5A; Materials and Methods). This strategy yielded successful Gpsm2 deletion in an average of 87% of IHCs 12 wk postinjection as measured by the absence of GPSM2 immunolabeling (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A), an improvement compared to variable AAV-Cre surgical outcomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). We also determined that Gpsm2 inactivation did not result in increased HC death at 12 wk postinjection based on F-Actin “scars” left behind upon cell extrusion (1, 46) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). In general, IHCs were missing in higher proportion at the base (14.6%) compared to the mid and apex (0.18 to 0.6%), with comparable outcomes in controls versus Gpsm2 mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). This degree of HC death is expected in the C57BL/6J background, where mice start to lose an appreciable number of HCs by 3 mo of age in a base-to-apex gradient (47). OHCs suffered higher rates of cell death overall, particularly at the cochlear base, but again there was no significant difference between genotypes (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). In summary, we conclude that inducible Gfi1CreERT-driven Gpsm2 deletion is a valid model to test how a reduction of stereocilia height proportional to the ~0.5 µm tip region undergoing actin turnover may impact auditory function.

Gpsm2 Inactivation in Adult HCs Has Minimal Impact on ABR Thresholds.

We measured ABRs longitudinally in Gfi1CreERT; Gpsm2flox/del mice and control littermates injected for 3 consecutive days with TAM or VEH, beginning at 6 wk of age (Fig. 5A). The control group pooled VEH-injected Gfi1CreERT/+; Gpsm2flox/+ and TAM-injected Gfi1+/+; Gpsm2flox/+or Gpsm2flox/del animals (Materials and Methods). ABRs were recorded immediately before the first TAM or VEH administration (Fig. 5A, time 0), and next at 1, 4, 6, 8, and 12 wk postinjection (8, 12, 16, 24, 32, and 40 KHz pure tones). At 12 wk, we measured additional ABRs for low frequencies (4, 6, 8 KHz pure tones) as well as distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAEs). Inner ears were then harvested for analysis.

Because adult-stage Gpsm2 inactivation did not increase overall HC death (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 B, C and D) and did not produce a loss or gross dysmorphology of stereocilia by 12 wk, we did not expect severe progressive hearing loss. Accordingly, differences in ABR thresholds between Gpsm2 conditional knock-out and control mice were subtle. As expected in the C57BL/6J background, ABR thresholds progressively increased in the higher frequency range (24 to 40 KHz), including in control animals (Fig. 5B) (48). At 40 KHz, ABR measurements yielded poor resolution as even control animals were largely deaf by 4 wk postinjection. ABR measurements at 24 and 32 KHz indicated a nonsignificant trend toward elevated thresholds in the absence of GPSM2 (Fig. 5B). However, ABR thresholds at optimal hearing frequencies (12 and 16 KHz) were not affected by loss of GPSM2 even at 12 wk, nor were thresholds for broadband noise “click” stimuli (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A).

Twelve weeks postinjection, there was no significant change in the magnitude of DPOAEs in response to stimuli at 6, 8, and 12 KHz when Gpsm2 was inactivated (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). There was, in contrast, a modest but significant reduction in DPOAEs at 16 KHz, indicating that OHC function may also be affected by the loss of GPSM2. Since the DPOAE reduction at 16 KHz did not translate to elevated ABR thresholds at the same frequency, however (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), OHCs and IHCs may be most vulnerable to GPSM2 loss at different tonotopic positions along the cochlea.

Gpsm2 Inactivation in Adult HCs Is Detrimental to Low-Frequency Hearing.

Having established that GPSM2 loss has a minimal impact on longitudinal ABR thresholds in the 8 to 40 KHz range, we speculated that a relatively uniform ~600 nm reduction in row 1 stereocilia height along the tonotopic axis (Fig. 2C) might dampen response at the outer ranges of hearing frequencies. We reasoned that hair bundles located at the cochlear apex and harboring the tallest row 1 stereocilia would no longer be represented upon Gpsm2 inactivation, whereas bundles with gradually shorter row 1 would still be present but located at a more basal position along the tonotopic axis. Impairing IHC function at the cochlear apex is expected to affect hearing at low frequencies. We thus collected additional ABR measurements for 4, 6, and 8 KHz tones at the experimental endpoint of 12 wk.

Remarkably, ABR thresholds were elevated at 4 and 6 but not at 8 KHz in conditional mutants compared to controls, significantly so at 6 KHz (Fig. 5C). We next examined low-tone ABR waveforms and noted flatter wave profiles in conditional mutants compared to controls (Fig. 5D; 4, 6, and 8 KHz). Measurements confirmed that ABR wave I amplitudes were significantly reduced, particularly discernable upon high sound pressure level stimuli (Fig. 5E). We next measured wave I amplitudes for 12 and 16 KHz where by contrast ABR thresholds did not trend higher in the absence of GPSM2 (compare Fig. 5C with Fig. 5B at 12 wk). Wave I amplitudes were reduced in response to 12 KHz but not 16 KHz stimuli in Gpsm2 conditional knock-outs, with reductions at 12 KHz less significant than at 4, 6, and 8 KHz (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C and Fig. 5E). We conclude that hearing deficits resulting from adult Gpsm2 deletion in HCs are mild but detectable, and appear biased toward the low-frequency range.

GPSM2 Loss Reduces Enrichment of Other EC Proteins at Stereocilia Tips.

Previous work in developing IHCs showed that MYO15A, EPS8, and WHRN depend on GPSM2 and GNAI for boosted enrichment at row 1 tips, which is in turn required for normal stereocilia elongation (31). We therefore asked how partner proteins in the EC behave when Gpsm2 is inactivated in mature IHCs. We coimmunolabeled GPSM2 and either MYO15A, EPS8, WHRN, or GNAI in Gfi1CreERT; Gpsm2flox/del and control IHCs 12 wk after TAM administration (3-d scheme). The majority of IHCs lacked GPSM2 antibody signal (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A), but we screened the mid-cochlear region for infrequent GPSM2+ escapers and imaged GPMS2+/GPMS2− IHC pairs. We normalized EC protein signals in GPMS2- IHCs to neighboring GPMS2+ IHCs (Materials and Methods). MYO15A, EPS8, and WHRN could still be detected at row 1 tips in GPSM2− IHCs, but in visibly lower amounts than in control GPSM2+ IHCs (Fig. 6 A–C). Enrichment was reduced on average to 60% of control levels for MYO15A, 48% for EPS8, and 33% for WHRN (Fig. 6 A–C). In contrast, GNAI was reduced to near-background levels in GPSM2− IHCs (Fig. 6D). Remarkably, this hierarchy of dependence on GPSM2 mirrors the one described during development where GPSM2 binds WHRN and GNAI to form a module that appears at row 1 stereocilia tips later than the non-row-specific MYO15A-EPS8 module (30, 31). In both developing and mature IHCs, MYO15A, EPS8, and WHRN thus depend on GPSM2 for boosted enrichment at tips. As GNAI and GPSM2 are largely codependent for stable enrichment at the bare zone and at stereocilia tips during development (29–31), we next investigated GPSM2 enrichment in Gfi1CreERT; Gnai3flox/neo IHCs 12 wk post-TAM using the same approach as above. GPSM2 signals were visibly reduced in Gnai3 mutant IHCs (Fig. 6E), but only at ~60% of control levels. GNAI signals were close to background levels in Gnai3 mutant IHCs as expected when using an antibody largely specific to GNAI3 (Fig. 6E) (38). Nonreciprocal GPSM2–GNAI dependence in adult IHCs could be attributed to functional redundancy in the GNAI family (38). Alternatively, GPSM2 may be partially enriched at tips in the absence of GNAI3 via its binding to WHRN in mature IHCs (30). Overall, we conclude that the multifunctional assembly known as the EC is both critical for hair bundle morphogenesis during development and for maintaining the dynamic tip compartment in mature IHCs.

Discussion

In this work, we show that two members of the EC required during development for stereocilia growth and hair bundle maturation, GPSM2 and GNAI3, are also important to maintain the precise and stable height of row 1 stereocilia in adult IHCs. GPSM2 is required for normal tip enrichment of other EC proteins (Fig. 6), as also previously shown during development (31). We thus conclude that the molecularly disparate proteins of the EC work together both in developing and adult HCs, respectively serving a dramatic increase versus steady-state maintenance of stereocilia height. This apparent paradox may be explained by the evolving nature of actin incorporation at tips from developing to mature stereocilia.

Interestingly, the tip compartment defined as the region incorporating new actin molecules in adult HCs was reported to span ~0.5 µm (6–8), a value close to the ~0.6 µm reduction we observe here 12 wk after inactivating Gpsm2 (Fig. 2). Significant stereocilia height reduction is only observed after 4 to 6 wk in Gpsm2 mutants, possibly reflecting the slow turnover of GPSM2 protein at tips (9 to 10 d half-life; Fig. 3). Based on isotope labeling of total proteins, IHC stereocilia signals only plateau-ed after 4 to 5 wk (6), and new actin incorporation at tips was visibly lower at 2 wk compared to 20 wk postactivation (8). GPSM2 slow turnover thus appears to coincide with the pace of actin renewal at the tip compartment, as opposed to more rapid turnover of actin cross-linkers based on FRAP experiments in the zebrafish (11), and robust incorporation of new fascin-2 proteins along the stereocilia shaft by 1 d postactivation in mouse (10). At this point, however, we cannot establish strict causality, as other factors beyond EC protein turnover likely influence height as well.

One salient feature of the height reduction observed in our study is its relatively uniform extent irrespective of original stereocilia height, suggesting that GPSM2-mediated maintenance is agnostic to tonotopic position (schematized in Fig. 7A). One explanation is that the adult EC regulates actin dynamics in the ~0.5 µm tip region, which may be uniform along the cochlea, whereas the stable, cross-linked actin of the stereocilium shaft is resilient to changes in the tip compartment and supports the height gradient.

(B) Model depicting how loss of GPSM2 or global GNAI function may destabilize partner proteins in the EC by compromising their anchoring at the plasma membrane via lipid-modified GNAI. In turn, this may compromise new actin incorporation in the tip compartment, the only zone of actin turnover in mature stereocilia.

Compared to HCs constitutively lacking other EC proteins, loss of Gnai3 results in less stunted stereocilia and a partial, variable phenotype (30, 42). A compound Gnai2; Gnai3 inactivation or pertussis toxin expression that globally inhibits GNAI proteins is required to reproduce the severe hair bundle dysmorphology typical of Gpsm2, Whrn, Eps8, or Myo15a mutants (29, 31, 32, 38, 42). Conditional deletion of Gnai3 in adult IHCs leads to reduced but not absent levels of GPSM2 (Fig. 6E), possibly because GNAI2 remains enriched at stereocilia tips (38). In turn, this could explain why height reduction is modest in Gnai3 compared to Gpsm2 mutants 12 wk post-AAV-Cre transduction (Fig. 2 C and D).

During early development, MYO15A and EPS8 are initially spanning all stereocilia rows, before being largely constrained to row 1 by GPSM2–GNAI after P7 (2, 3, 31). In constitutive Gpsm2 knock-outs, MYO15A and EPS8 still localize to stereocilia tips, but in low amounts and without row specificity (31). GPSM2–GNAI is thus a major driver of row identity by singling out row 1 to adopt a distinct fate from further mechanotransducing rows. Following adult-stage loss of GPSM2, however, we did not observe changes in the row 1-specific localization of EPS8 or WHRN, nor in the row 1 and row 2 distribution of MYO15A as detected by a pan-isoform antibody which also labels the adult row 2-specific isoform (Fig. 1) (39). We conclude that the EC maintains row 1 stereocilia height in mature IHCs, but may not maintain row identity. Future studies are required to define whether row 1 identity is actively maintained via other means in mature HCs.

The mechanism by which GPSM2–GNAI boosts EC enrichment and influences actin dynamics during development and in adult HCs (Fig. 6) remains speculative. Tip-localized myosin motors reportedly regulate actin polymerization by exerting force against membrane tension (49, 50) or by stimulating actin filament nucleation directly, in the case of MYO15A (51, 52). Thus, reduced MYO15A dosage upon loss of GPSM2 may result in height loss from slowed actin incorporation. Mechanistically, GNAI proteins are lipidated (53), which could ensure membrane-anchoring of the rest of the EC (Fig. 7B). Alternatively, GPSM2–GNAI may only depend on MYO15A and WHRN for tip trafficking (30), and have a role distinct from the rest of the EC.

An intriguing hypothesis is that GPSM2–GNAI stabilizes the EC at stereocilia tips by promoting a phase-separated biomolecular condensate (54). Interestingly, an electron-dense region at row 1 stereocilia tips is lost in Myo15a shaker-2 and sporadic in Whrn whirler mutants which respectively show absent or diminished EC protein enrichment (5, 55). This “tip density” may represent a protein-packed condensate that requires the EC for formation. In vitro, MYO15A-EPS8-WHRN is sufficient to create a phase-separated condensate, but this property is vastly enhanced by GPSM2–GNAI (54, 56). Enhanced phase separation of the EC by GPSM2–GNAI also increases the actin-bundling activity of EPS8 and the formation of actin filaments in vitro (54). Thus, GPSM2 may boost enrichment of the EC at tips to enhance its actin nucleation activity via phase condensation.

Stereocilia may continue to shorten past 12 wk following Gpsm2 inactivation, and possibly degenerate in time. Alternatively, however, height reduction and auditory deficits at 12 wk may represent the full extent of the phenotype. Adult-stage depletion of any EC member is not necessarily expected to affect heavily cross-linked, stable actin filaments in the stereocilia shafts (6, 8, 10). Progressive reduction of stereocilia height between 4 and 12 wk upon Gpsm2 inactivation (Fig. 2) suggests that a further decline is possible at the cochlear base and apex, but less likely at the mid-cochlea where reduced heights are relatively comparable at 8 and 12 wk already. A definitive conclusion will require follow-up studies where animal cohorts are analyzed following longer chase times.

Materials and Methods

Animal work was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of The Jackson Laboratory. Procedures below are further detailed in SI Appendix.

Confocal Imaging and Analysis.

Fixed, decalcified samples were immunolabeled, whole-mounted, and imaged using Zeiss LSM 800 with Airyscan. Measurements were conducted in ImageJ and Imaris.

Semicircular Canal Surgery.

AAV2/2-CMV-Cre-IRES-Egfp (VVC-U of Iowa-2600) or 7-bromo-1-heptanol was delivered into the posterior semicircular canal of anesthetized animals with a Nanoliter 2000 injector (WPI).

Scanning Electron Microscopy.

Fixed, dehydrated samples were sputtered with gold-palladium particles and imaged using Hitachi 3000N VP.

ABR and Distortion Product Otoacoustic Emissions.

Anesthetized animals were tested using the RZ6 Multi-I/O Processor System, the BioSig software, and a Medusa4Z amplifier for ABR (TDT). DPOAEs were recorded with a 10 dB+ Microphone (ER).

Data Analysis.

Analyses and visualizations were done in R 3.6.2 with tidyverse, ggpubr, and rstatix packages.
