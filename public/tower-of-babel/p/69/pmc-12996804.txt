Structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging abnormalities and plasma GLP-1 levels in Parkinson's disease with cognitive impairment
Authors: Yunfei Sun, Mengyan Zhang, Xiaoxue Shi, Longyu Wu, Li Wang, Jinhua Zheng, Jianjun Ma, Zonghan She, Xuelin Qi, Yingyun Wang, Qing Xu, Qi Gu, Siyuan Chen, Xue Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate alterations in plasma glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) levels and structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in patients with Parkinson's disease-related cognitive impairment.MethodsA total of 34 PD patients with cognitive impairment and 37 PD patients without cognitive impairment were prospectively recruited at our hospital from September 2022 to July 2024. Data on the cognitive function, demographics and plasma GLP-1 levels of the participants were collected. PD patients underwent 3.0 T MRI, and plasma GLP-1 concentrations were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Voxel-based morphometry (VBM) results were used to examine structural alterations. Resting-state functional MRI results were used to reveal the extent of changes in brain connectivity.ResultsCompared with those in healthy individuals, plasma GLP-1 levels were lower in PD patients and were positively correlated with Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores. We observed volume reductions in the superior temporal gyrus, amygdala, and left frontal operculum in patients with Parkinson's disease-related cognitive impairment (PD-CI). Moreover, these changes were significantly positively correlated with MoCA scores and GLP-1 levels. We observed decreased functional activity in the right cuneal cortex, left occipital lobe and lateral occipital cortex in PD-CI patients.ConclusionsOur findings reveal that more severe Parkinson's disease-related cognitive impairment is associated with abnormalities in the superior temporal gyrus, left frontal operculum and the visual network. These structural and functional abnormalities are associated with plasma GLP-1 levels. Our results may help clarify the pathophysiology of cognitive impairment and suggest potential therapeutic targets.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, GLP-1, Cognitive impairment, Structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease and the most common movement disorder worldwide. The characteristic features of PD are motor dysfunctions such as tremors, rigidity and bradykinesia(Bloem et al., 2021). However, PD is also often associated with a variety of nonmotor symptoms;, As one of the most important nonmotor symptoms of Parkinson's disease, cognitive impairment (CI) occurs approximately six times more frequently in PD patients than in the healthy population.CI occurs in the early stages of PD and increases with age and disease duration, with CI rates of 17% at 5 years after diagnosis, 46% at 10 years after diagnosis, and 83% at 20 years after diagnosis(Aarsland et al., 2021). The main feature of cognitive dysfunction in PD patients is deficits in executive and visuospatial functions(Emre, 2007). CIs can seriously affect the quality of life and functioning of people with PD.

Previous studies have shown that type 2 diabetes is strongly associated with the risk of developing PD(Cheong et al., 2020). Glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) is involved in the enteroglucagon signalling system. GLP-1 is secreted by enteroendocrine cells and released at constant low levels(Viljoen & Bain, 2023); GLP-1 promotes healthy insulin signalling, regulates blood glucose levels and suppresses appetite. In addition, GLP-1 is produced by the brain, as well as other cells and organs in the body(Trapp & Brierley, 2022). GLP-1 is also associated with CI(Monney et al., 2023). Plasma GLP-1 levels reportedly decrease with age in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients, are lower in AD patients than in healthy older adults and are positively correlated with Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores(Liu et al., 2024). However, the clinical relevance of GLP-1 in PD has not been fully elucidated. In this study, we explored the clinical relevance of GLP-1 in PD.

In recent years, structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has been extensively utilized in Parkinson's disease research because of its unique advantages(Ashburner & Friston, 2000; Meijer & Goraj, 2014; Zhu et al., 2023), particularly in the field of PD-related cognitive impairment. Mild midbrain atrophy and reduced frontal cortex thickness are characteristic structural MRI findings in PD patients with MCI(Cicero et al., 2022). One study revealed decreased FC in the bilateral prefrontal cortex within the frontoparietal network (FPN) and increased FC in the thalamus, posterior cingulate cortex, left hippocampus and right cerebellar hemisphere in patients with PD-MCI compared with control(Amboni et al., 2015; Lang et al., 2020). However, these studies are limited to neuroimaging and lack more in-depth investigations.

In our study, we employed voxel-based morphometry (VBM) (Ashburner & Friston, 2000) to investigate structural changes in the brain. To characterize functional alterations, we performed amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) analysis to assess regional spontaneous neural activity. We further analysed plasma GLP-1 levels to gain multidimensional insights into the pathogenesis of Parkinson's disease-related cognitive impairment. This integrated approach aims to provide novel theoretical foundations for disease diagnosis and treatment.

Materials and methods

Participants

Patients with PD were prospectively recruited at our hospital from September 2022 to July 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) clinically established PD according to the Movement Disorder Society Clinical Diagnostic Criteria(Postuma et al., 2015) with a duration of ≥ 1 year; (2) no family history of PD in a first-degree relative; (3) no history of neurological surgery; (4) no history of serious psychiatric disorders as defined by the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 11th Revision, including but not limited to schizophrenia spectrum disorders, bipolar disorders, and major depressive disorder with psychotic features(Organization, 2019); and (5) no other neurodegenerative diseases or metabolic disorders that cause CI. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) MRI showing structural lesions associated with other neurological disorders; (2) head movement artefacts during MRI; (3) iron deficiency anaemia, diabetes mellitus, renal insufficiency, or peripheral neuropathy; and (4) severe cognitive dysfunction, such as dementia, that rendered patients unable to cooperate with the examination. Fifty-seven healthy controls (HCs) were recruited from individuals undergoing routine health check-ups at the same hospital during the study period. All HCs were age- and sex-matched to the PD group. Inclusion criteria were: (1) no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders; (2) no first-degree relatives with PD. Exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome, or other endocrine disorders; (2) chronic gastrointestinal diseases (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease, history of gastrectomy); (3) acute infection or inflammation within 2 weeks prior to enrollment; (4) blood sample quality issues (hemolysis, lipemia, insufficient volume).

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Henan Provincial People's Hospital (approval number 202446). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in this study.

Clinical data

Clinical demographic data, including name, age, sex, duration of illness, and levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD), were collected for each patient(Tomlinson et al., 2010). Disease severity was assessed using the modified Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) staging scale and the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS), and motor symptoms were assessed using the Movement Disorder Society's UPDRS Part III(Goetz et al., 2008). CIs were assessed and analysed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), a widely used and well-validated index for measuring cognitive function in individuals with neurological disorders(Fiorenzato et al., 2024). Although the MoCA is a screening tool, it can also be used to detect CI in PD patients with 90% sensitivity and 75% specificity(Dalrymple-Alford et al., 2010). On the basis of the MoCA scores, we stratified the PD group into two subgroups: PD with normal cognition (PD-NC) (≥26) and PD with cognitive impairment (PD-CI) (≤18).

Consistent clinical diagnoses were given by two experienced senior neurologists. All assessments were completed once the disease onset occurred. History taking, physical examination and scale scoring were performed by trained neurologists.

Blood sample collection and measurement of plasma GLP-1 levels

Peripheral blood was collected in test tubes with anticoagulant from each subject from 07:30–08:30 AM after an overnight fast and before breakfast. The sample was allowed to coagulate at room temperature for 30 min, the blood was centrifuged (1000 ×g, 15 min), and the serum was removed and stored at −80 °C until analysis. All of the samples were collected while the patients were in the “on” state.

The plasma GLP-1 level was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (CUSABIO, China). The detection range of this kit is 2.5–40 ng/mL, the sensitivity is 0.5 ng/mL, and the intra- and interdetection variability ranges are < 15% and < 15%, respectively. Biological replicates were analysed on the same plate according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Blood samples were collected from all 71 PD patients and 57 healthy controls after an overnight fast. Plasma was separated by centrifugation (1000 ×g, 15 min) within 30 min of collection and stored at −80°C until analysis. Plasma GLP-1 concentrations were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (CUSABIO, China; detection range 2.5–40 ng/mL, sensitivity 0.5 ng/mL) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

However, in the initial phase of the study, plasma GLP-1 levels were successfully measured in only 52 of the 71 PD patients. The remaining 19 patients were excluded from the initial analysis due to technical issues: hemolysis (n = 2), insufficient sample volume (n = 10), and a storage deviation (seven sample underwent an unintended freeze-thaw cycle). To obtain complete data for all participants and avoid selection bias, we subsequently retrieved stored backup samples (where available) or re-collected samples during follow-up visits. These supplementary samples were analyzed in a single batch under identical conditions using the same ELISA kit lot number. Standard curves were included on each plate and confirmed to be within the expected range. After this procedure, complete plasma GLP-1 data were obtained for all 71 PD patients and 57 healthy controls, and all subsequent analyses were based on this full dataset.

MRI acquisition

MRI acquisition was performed with a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma 3 T MRI scanner equipped with a 64-channel head coil. A T1-weighted 3D-MPRAGE sequence was used with the following parameters: flip angle: 4°, repetition time (TR): 5000 ms, echo time (TE): 3.43 ms, inversion time (T1): 755 ms, slice thickness: 1.00 mm, slice count: 208, bandwidth: 240 Hz/pixel, voxel dimensions: 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm³ , matrix size: 256 × 256, and field of view: 256 × 256 mm².

Structural MRI data processing and analysis

The MRI scans underwent visual inspection to exclude datasets with significant vascular lesions, space-occupying lesions, or motion artefacts. Image preprocessing and voxel-based morphometry (VBM) analysis were performed using Statistical Parametric Mapping 8 software (SPM8, Wellcome Trust Centre for Neuroimaging, University College London, UK). The processing pipeline consisted of the following steps: structural T1-weighted images were first subjected to unified segmentation. The resulting grey matter and white matter probability maps were then spatially normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template using an advanced nonlinear warping algorithm based on the diffeomorphic anatomical registration through exponentiated Lie algebra (DARTEL) technique(Ashburner, 2007). The modulated images were smoothed with an 8-mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel. Total grey matter volume (GMV), white matter volume (WMV), and cerebrospinal fluid volume (CSFV) were quantified. The total intracranial volume (TIV) was calculated using the formula: TIV = GMV + WMV + CSFV.

We utilized SPM8 to conduct VBM on the individually smoothed GMV to assess differences between Parkinson's disease patients with and without cognitive impairment. TIV, age, sex, education and levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) were included as covariates to eliminate variations in GMV due to differences in TIV, age, sex, education and levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD). The significance threshold was defined as an uncorrected p = 0.001 at the initial voxel level and as a false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p = 0.05 at the cluster level to correct for multiple comparisons. We utilized the brain region exhibiting a significant disparity between the two groups as the region of interest (ROI) and extracted its volume by employing the DPABI toolbox(Yan et al., 2016).

Resting-state fMRI processing and analysis

SPM12b and the Functional Connectivity Toolbox (CONN) (version 22_a)(Whitfield-Gabrieli & Nieto-Castanon, 2012) were used for image preprocessing and analysis of the rs-fMRI data. Flexible pipelines were used for preprocessing functional and structural data; the current functional file was labelled “original data”, and the following steps were performed: realignment with susceptibility distortion interaction correction (subject motion estimation and correction), translation from the functional centre to (0,0,0) coordinates, slice timing correction (STC; acquisition time correction for interslice differences), outlier detection (ART-based scan scrubbing), and simultaneous direct segmentation (gray matter/white matter/cerebrospinal fluid [CSF]) and Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space normalization. These functional files were labelled “MNI-space data”. The structural data were processed using the following steps: translation of the structural centre to (0,0,0) coordinates; simultaneous structural segmentation (gray matter/white matter/CSF) and MNI normalization; and smoothing (spatial convolution with a Gaussian kernel). These files were labelled “smoothed data”. Afterwards, standard denoising pipelines were used to denoise the functional data.

A first-level analysis using the CONN pipeline was performed to generate individual amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation (ALFF) plots to assess regional neural activity. Group-level analyses were performed using a general linear model (GLM) to identify brain regions with significant differences in ALFF values between the two groups. The results were thresholded using a combination of a voxel-level threshold of P < 0.001 and a cluster-level threshold of familywise false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected P value < 0.05. We subsequently extracted the ALFF values of the brain regions that presented significant differences.

Statistical analysis

The normality of distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk (SW) normality test. Continuous data with a normal distribution are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) and were compared between groups via t tests (two groups). Continuous data that did not conform to a normal distribution are presented as medians (25th, 75th percentiles) and were compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test (two groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis (KW) H test (three or more groups, post hoc pairwise comparisons performed using the Bonferroni method). Nominal data are presented as percentages, and comparisons between groups were performed using the χ2 test. Because not all the variables were normally distributed, a Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the correlations between the indicators. Partial correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships among plasma GLP-1 levels, imaging indices, and MoCA scores to eliminate confounding factors. All the statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, United States) and GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Discussion

Our study revealed a significant correlation between plasma GLP-1 levels and MoCA scores in PD patients, with higher plasma GLP-1 levels being associated with higher MoCA scores. These findings suggest that GLP-1 may be a protective factor against the development of cognitive deficits in PD patients, which is consistent with previous findings in animal experiments that GLP-1 can ameliorate motor deficits and delay cognitive deficits in mice with PD(Chen et al., 2018). In recent years, the gut—brain axis theory(Quigley, 2017) has gradually become a popular topic of research as a possible pathogenesis of PD. GLP-1 is widely distributed both centrally and peripherally and can cross the blood—brain barrier(Monney et al., 2023). The secretion of GLP-1 in the periphery, mainly by intestinal L cells, can influence the development of PD through inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, effects on mitochondrial function, and apoptosis(Kopp et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2025). Our study validated the relationship between GLP-1 levels in peripheral blood and PD-related CI.

Our study revealed that PD-CI patients exhibited cortical atrophy and decreased functional activity. The cortical volume decreased mainly in the superior temporal gyrus and left frontal operculum. The superior temporal gyrus serves as a critical hub bridging auditory processing and higher-order cognition, and its impairment constitutes a central mechanism underlying various cognitive disorders. In patients with multiple system atrophy (MSA) accompanied by mild cognitive impairment, neuroimaging analyses have demonstrated reduced cortical thickness in the left temporal lobe regions, including the middle and inferior temporal gyri(Xia et al., 2013). Although this study focused primarily on the middle and inferior temporal gyri, it suggests that widespread structural alterations in the superior temporal gyrus and related regions may be associated with cognitive deterioration. Research on Parkinson's disease patients with cognitive impairment has shown cortical thinning or grey matter volume reduction in the bilateral superior temporal gyrus, with this structural atrophy being more pronounced in patients with severe cognitive impairment(Kuate Defo et al., 2023). Consistent with these findings, our research further revealed a significant positive correlation between superior temporal gyrus atrophy and blood GLP-1 levels. Previous studies have shown that in neurodegenerative diseases, GLP-1 acts through a variety of mechanisms, including improving neuronal energy utilization, repairing cells, reducing chronic inflammatory responses, improving mitochondrial function, and reducing oxidative stress(Kalinderi et al., 2024; Standaert, 2024). In PD patients, GLP-1 analogues can also restore dopamine levels, inhibit dopaminergic neuron loss, attenuate neuronal degeneration and ameliorate CI(Hölscher, 2024; Kalinderi et al., 2024). Decreasing GLP-1 levels or signalling deficiencies may promote atrophy in specific brain regions (e.g., the hippocampus, hypothalamus, substantia nigra, and prefrontal cortex) through multiple pathways, including metabolic dysregulation, inflammation, and oxidative stress. While previous research has focused primarily on the effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists on neurodegenerative diseases, few studies have directly investigated GLP-1 itself—a gap that our study was designed to address. Furthermore, we detected atrophy in the left frontal operculum in Parkinson’s disease patients with cognitive impairment, although no significant correlation with plasma GLP-1 levels was observed. Collectively, our work contributes to the current understanding of the role of peripheral blood GLP-1 levels in modulating cerebral cortex structure.

Compared with those in PD-NC patients, functional activity in PD-NC patients decreased mainly in the right cuneal cortex, left occipital pole, and left lateral occipital cortex. One study revealed that patients with different patterns of CI exhibited thinning of the lateral occipital cortex compared with controls(Sahin et al., 2024). A recent fMRI study of digital memory patterns revealed that the bilateral occipital lobes were highly activated in a short-term memory task, with the activation of the left occipital lobe being the most significant(Nie et al., 2019). These findings suggest that the occipital lobe plays an important role in cognitive activity and that deficits in sensory processing may exacerbate the development of cognitive deficits when occipital cerebral perfusion is decreased. An fMRI study in schizophrenia patients revealed decreased medium/low spatial frequency activation in the left supraoccipital gyrus relative to that in controls, suggesting that these sensory processing deficits may exacerbate cognitive deficits(Martínez et al., 2008). In another study of bidirectional affective disorders, a negative correlation was found between the amount of blood perfusion in the lateral occipital cortex and performance in verbal memory, digit sequencing, and attention and processing speed(Zeng et al., 2020). These findings suggest that the occipital lobe may be involved in a wider range of cognitive deficits. The recently proposed ‘dual syndrome’ hypothesis suggests that the hindbrain system, comprising the temporal, parietal and occipital cortices and the cholinergic system, is involved in PDD(Hirano, 2021), and this hypothesis provides a plausible explanation for our results. Interestingly, while we observed significant associations between GLP-1 levels and both cognitive performance and gray matter volume in the superior temporal gyrus, no such correlation was found with ALFF values in the occipital regions. This dissociation may indicate that the neuroprotective effects of GLP-1 are more directly related to structural preservation rather than to modulation of resting-state neuronal activity. Alternatively, the lack of correlation could be due to the relatively small sample size or the fact that ALFF reflects a different facet of neural function that is less sensitive to peripheral GLP-1 levels. Future studies combining GLP-1 measurements with task-based fMRI may provide further insights.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the absence of neuroimaging data from a healthy control group limits our ability to determine whether the observed gray matter atrophy and functional alterations are specific to PD‑related cognitive impairment or partly reflect age‑related or non‑specific PD‑related changes. Second, the cross‑sectional design precludes any causal inferences regarding the relationship between GLP‑1 levels and cognitive decline. Third, we excluded patients with diabetes mellitus to avoid confounding effects on GLP‑1 levels. While this decision strengthens the internal validity of our findings, it limits the generalizability of our results to the large subset of PD patients with comorbid diabetes—a population that may particularly benefit from GLP‑1‑based therapies. Fourth, our use of a stricter MoCA cutoff (≤18) to define PD‑CI, while maximizing specificity, means that our findings primarily apply to patients with moderate‑to‑severe cognitive impairment and may not extend to those with mild deficits. Future studies incorporating full neuropsychological batteries, longitudinal designs, and well‑matched healthy controls are needed to validate and extend our findings across the entire cognitive and clinical spectrum of Parkinson’s disease.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the associations between plasma GLP-1 levels, structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging measures, and cognitive deficits in Parkinson’s disease by combining plasma markers and regional grey matter volume analyses. We found that higher plasma GLP-1 levels were associated with better cognitive performance, suggesting a potential protective role against PD-related cognitive impairment; we also revealed that in cognitively impaired PD patients, atrophy of the superior temporal gyrus and the left inferior frontal gyrus and decreased functional activity in the right cuneal cortex, left occipital lobe and lateral occipital cortex were detected. Additionally, reduced amygdala volume was observed in PD-CI patients, although it did not correlate significantly with MoCA scores or GLP-1 levels, suggesting a less direct relationship with cognitive impairment in this cohort. These results suggest that plasma GLP-1 may have a protective effect on cognitive function in PD patients and that this effect might be linked to cerebral grey matter integrity. This study highlights possible mechanisms underlying cognitive deficits in PD from multiple perspectives and proposes an association between circulating GLP-1 levels and brain grey matter, providing new insights for understanding the pathogenesis and potential treatment of PD.
