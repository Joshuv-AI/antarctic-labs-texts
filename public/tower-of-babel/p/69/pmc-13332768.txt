Montelukast Prescription Prevalence and Impact on Clinical Outcomes Among Patients with Asthma: A Cross-Sectional Study in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdullah A Alqarni, Abdulelah M Aldhahir, Rayan A Siraj, Jaber S Alqahtani, Rose M Alghamdi, Thuraya A Baqais, Norah A Najrani, Asma Z Albarqi, Ahmed H Alasimi, Hassan Alwafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough montelukast is widely prescribed as an add-on therapy for asthma management, the current rate of montelukast prescription and its association with clinical outcomes among asthmatic patients in Saudi Arabia are unknown. In addition, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration has recently issued a warning regarding potential serious neuropsychiatric adverse effects associated with montelukast use. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of montelukast prescription and its impact on clinical outcomes among asthmatic patients in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among asthmatic patients attending a pulmonary clinic. Clinical and demographic data were obtained from medical records, while anxiety, depression, inhaler adherence, asthma control, and quality of life were assessed using validated questionnaires. Group differences were analyzed using independent t-tests and chi-square tests. Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with montelukast use.ResultsA total of 213 patients with asthma were included in the study; 103 were using montelukast, and 110 were not. The prevalence of montelukast prescription was 48%. Anxiety symptoms were higher among montelukast users compared with non-users (44.6% vs 15.5%), as were depressive symptoms (33.0% vs 11.0%). Montelukast use was significantly associated with increased odds of anxiety symptoms (aOR 4.1, 95% CI 2.01–8.45; P < 0.0001) and depressive symptoms (aOR 4.3, 95% CI 1.92–9.86; P < 0.0001). However, adherence to inhalers, asthma control, and quality of life were not significantly associated with montelukast use.ConclusionMontelukast prescription was common among asthmatic patients in Saudi Arabia and was associated with higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms. Clinicians should consider monitoring psychological symptoms in patients receiving montelukast, and prospective longitudinal studies are needed to further evaluate its psychiatric safety profile.

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease affecting approximately 262 million people worldwide and causing nearly 461,000 deaths in 2019.1 It is characterized by recurrent episodes of wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and cough resulting from airway inflammation and bronchial hyperresponsiveness.2 Despite substantial advances in diagnostic approaches and therapeutic management, asthma continues to impose a considerable burden on healthcare systems and patients’ quality of life globally.3,4 In the Middle East, including Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of asthma has increased markedly over recent decades. Saudi Arabia experienced a 26% increase in age-standardized asthma prevalence between 1990 and 2019, highlighting the urgent need for targeted public health strategies and improved disease management in the region.1

The pathophysiology of asthma involves a complex inflammatory response mediated by eosinophils, mast cells, and T-helper lymphocytes, particularly Th2 and Th17 subsets.5,6 An imbalance between Th17 and regulatory T cells (Treg) has been implicated in asthma pathogenesis, with interleukin-17 (IL-17) contributing to both eosinophilic and neutrophilic airway inflammation and being associated with greater disease severity.7,8 Persistent airway inflammation may lead to structural airway remodeling, including subepithelial fibrosis, smooth muscle hypertrophy, and angiogenesis, resulting in progressive airflow limitation and reduced responsiveness to therapy.9 Clinically, asthma symptoms are often episodic and may worsen at night or during the early morning hours.10 In addition, asthma commonly coexists with several comorbidities, including allergic rhinitis, eczema, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), obesity, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), anxiety, and depression, all of which can adversely affect asthma control, increase exacerbation frequency, and negatively influence treatment outcomes.11,12

Montelukast, a leukotriene receptor antagonist (LTRA), is widely prescribed as an add-on therapy for patients with persistent or uncontrolled asthma.13 The current Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) guidelines recommend montelukast in combination with inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), with or without long-acting beta-agonists (LABA), for selected patients with asthma.14 Although montelukast has demonstrated beneficial effects in improving asthma symptoms and reducing exacerbations, growing concerns have emerged regarding its potential neuropsychiatric adverse effects, including anxiety, depression, sleep disturbances, aggression, and suicidal ideation.15–17 In 2020, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration issued a boxed warning for montelukast because of serious mental health adverse events associated with its use.18 Furthermore, subsequent studies have suggested that montelukast may cross the blood–brain barrier and interact with receptors involved in emotional and behavioral regulation.19,20

Despite increasing safety concerns, montelukast remains commonly prescribed in routine asthma care. However, data regarding the prevalence of montelukast prescription and its potential association with psychological and clinical outcomes among patients with asthma in Saudi Arabia remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of montelukast prescription among patients with asthma in Saudi Arabia and to investigate the association between montelukast use and clinical outcomes, including anxiety, depression, asthma control, inhaler adherence, and quality of life.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross-sectional study aimed to assess the prevalence of montelukast prescription among patients with asthma in Saudi Arabia and investigate the impact of montelukast use on adverse clinical outcomes. The study was conducted at King Abdulaziz University Hospital in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia, between January 2024 and February 2025.

Study Population

Patients were eligible for participation only if they had a confirmed diagnosis of asthma, were aged 18 or above, and visited the pulmonary clinic for routine follow-up. Participants were categorized into the montelukast and non-montelukast groups based on their use of montelukast. Patients were classified into the montelukast group if they were actively prescribed and regularly using montelukast for at least 3 months, as documented in their medical records at the time of study enrolment. Patients with asthma in the studied clinic were generally diagnosed following internationally agreed diagnostic criteria, which include medical history of recurrent episodes of wheezing, chest tightness, coughing and shortness of breath after exposure to one of the risk factors, reliable spirometry results demonstrating reversible airflow obstruction (defined as forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1)/ forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio below 70% during the attack, post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) improve by ≥12% AND ≥200mL).21

Spirometry Parameters

All spirometry data included in the current study were collected from patient medical records obtained within the previous six months. It should be noted that although the tests were performed in accordance with the current American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines22 by a well-trained pulmonary function technician, the tests were also reviewed by a pulmonologist and two consultant respiratory therapists.

Data Collection Procedures and Tools

Data in the current study were collected using a convenience sampling technique by four respiratory therapy interns under the supervision of a respiratory therapy senior specialist. All patients in the study agreed to participate and signed an informed consent form after being informed of the study objectives. Patients who agreed to participate underwent a combination of clinical assessment, interviews, and medical record review, and were asked to complete four assessment tools. The interviews were conducted in a structured one-to-one face-to-face format. Each interview lasted approximately 30–45 minutes, depending on the time required to complete the following questionnaires: the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), Test of Adherence to Inhalers (TAI), Asthma Control Test (ACT), and the St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ). Also, hospital/ICU admissions, the number of comorbidities, current respiratory and non-respiratory medications, and all other demographic and clinical characteristics of all patients included in the current study were collected from their respective medical records, obtained from electronic health systems, and routinely updated and verified by clinical staff. Data confidentiality was strictly maintained through anonymization of all participant information and secure storage of data in password-protected electronic files accessible only to the research team. Questionnaire-based data were checked for completeness during the interview to minimize missing data.

Patient-Reported Outcome Measures

Anxiety and Depression

○The 14-item HADS is a commonly used, reliable tool to assess psychological distress, including symptoms of anxiety and depression, among patients under medical care. The HADS scale serves as a tool for the initial diagnosis and continuous monitoring of the prognosis related to depression and anxiety symptoms. The HADS comprises 14 questions and is evenly divided to address both depression and anxiety aspects. Each item is scored from 0 to 3, with 0 indicating no impairment and 3 severe impairments. Consequently, the potential scores range from 0 to 21 for both anxiety and depression. Scores of 7 or below on either scale indicate a normal level of anxiety or depression, while scores between 8 and 10 suggest borderline, and a score of 11–21 is deemed abnormal.23

Adherence to Inhalers

○TAI consists of two components that identify non-adherent patients and assess adherence to inhaled medication in patients with respiratory diseases, including asthma. The TAI is a validated tool that provides a reliable subjective measure of adherence to inhaler regimens among COPD and asthma patients.24 The questionnaire includes 10 items, each scored from 1 to 5 (1 = worst compliance, 5 = best compliance). The total sum score of the items provides a range between 10 (minimum score) and 50 (maximum score), which further determines the degree of adherence as follows: good adherent (50 points), intermediate adherent (46 to 49 points), and poor adherent (≤45 points).24 Before conducting the study, written permission was obtained from the copyright owners of the TAI questionnaire.

Asthma Control Test (ACT)

○The ACT is a self-administered questionnaire designed to assess asthma control. It consists of 5 questions that focus on how symptoms affect asthma patients’ daily lives and on their impression of their control over the past 4 weeks. Each question was scored from 1 to 5 (1 indicating the poorest control and 5 the best). The total score ranges from 5 (minimum) to 25 (maximum), which further determines the level of asthma control as follows: well controlled (25–20), partially controlled (19–15), not controlled (14–5).25

Quality of Life

○The SGRQ serves as a self-administered tool designed to assess health-related quality of life and symptoms, with a specific focus on symptoms other than dyspnea in patients with obstructive disorders. The questionnaire comprises 53 questions and delineates three distinct domains of illness: symptoms, activity, and the impact of the disease on daily life. The symptom category captures data pertaining to four specific symptoms: cough, sputum production, wheezing, and dyspnea. Scores range from 0 to 100, with lower scores indicating better health-related quality of life and higher scores suggesting more limitations.26,27 Written permission was obtained from the copyright owners of the SGRQ questionnaire.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained before the start of this study from an independent research committee at King Abdulaziz University Hospital, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia (No 492–23).

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism (Version 9). The data normality was assessed graphically using histograms to determine the appropriate statistical test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while arithmetic means ±SDs were used for normally distributed continuous variables. Participants were categorized into two groups based on the use of montelukast: the montelukast group and the non-montelukast group. To explore differences between groups, continuous variables were analyzed using independent t-tests, and categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square tests. Additionally, logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with montelukast use among asthmatic patients, after adjusting for potential confounders, including age, gender, and smoking status, hospital/ICU admission, marital status, and spirometry parameters. A p-value of less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 308 asthmatic patients visited our pulmonary clinic for routine follow-up during data collection. Of these, 21 patients aged 18 years old or below and 74 patients who did not wish to participate in the study were excluded. A total of 103 asthmatic patients on regular asthma therapies with montelukast and 110 patients with regular asthma therapies without montelukast met our inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence of montelukast prescription among patients with asthma in Saudi Arabia and examined its potential impact on various clinical outcomes, including anxiety, depression, adherence to inhaled therapies, asthma control, and quality of life. Our findings revealed that patients prescribed montelukast demonstrated higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms compared to those not receiving montelukast. Interestingly, no significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding asthma control, quality of life, and adherence to inhaled medications.

The higher levels of anxiety and depression observed among montelukast users are consistent with pharmacovigilance reports highlighting potential neuropsychiatric effects of LTRAs, particularly anxiety and depression.16,28,29 Although the exact mechanisms remain unclear, leukotriene pathways may be involved in neuroinflammatory processes and neurotransmitter regulation, which could plausibly contribute to these psychological effects.30–32 In response to these safety concerns, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration issued a boxed warning in 2020 regarding serious neuropsychiatric adverse events associated with montelukast, particularly when alternative treatment options are available.18 These findings underscore the importance of careful risk–benefit assessment when prescribing montelukast, especially in patients with psychiatric comorbidities.33,34

Patients with asthma often have a higher baseline prevalence of anxiety and depression, which may lead to an increased likelihood of receiving medications such as montelukast in more symptomatic or complex cases.35,36 Recent evidence highlighted that pre-existing psychiatric conditions are common among patients exposed to montelukast and may contribute to the risk of subsequent neuropsychiatric outcomes rather than the drug alone being the sole cause.37 Consequently, the association observed in this study may partly reflect underlying patient characteristics and asthma-related burden rather than a direct causal effect of montelukast itself.

Although asthmatic patients using montelukast in the current study had significantly lower BMI than non-users, existing evidence does not support a direct effect of montelukast on body weight.38 More importantly, a double-blind, controlled trial has shown that overweight and obese asthmatic patients may derive greater clinical benefit from montelukast therapy, including fewer asthma control days, compared with normal-weight patients.39 Therefore, the lower BMI observed among montelukast users in our cohort is unlikely to represent a pharmacological effect of the montelukast itself and may instead reflect differences in patient characteristics and asthma phenotype.

In contrast, no significant differences were observed between groups in asthma control and adherence to inhaled therapies. These findings may partly reflect the simplified once-daily dosing schedule of montelukast, which can enhance treatment adherence in routine clinical practice.13,40 However, this improved adherence does not necessarily translate into better symptom control, which may be explained by the relatively lower anti-inflammatory potency of montelukast compared with inhaled corticosteroids in controlling persistent airway inflammation.41,42 Although non-significant, these patterns highlight the ongoing need to optimize asthma management strategies through appropriate combination therapy, particularly the integration of ICS with adjunctive agents such as LTRAs.43,44 Current clinical guidelines support this approach, suggesting that combination therapy may enhance overall disease control by targeting multiple inflammatory pathways and improving both physiological outcomes and treatment adherence.45,46

Additionally, no significant difference in quality of life was observed between the two groups. This suggests that montelukast use alone may not translate into meaningful improvements in patient-perceived well-being. This finding aligns with evidence indicating that montelukast provides modest clinical benefits compared with inhaled corticosteroid–based regimens, particularly in patient-reported outcomes.47,48 Previous literature further supports that montelukast is generally less effective than inhaled corticosteroids in improving overall asthma control and quality-of-life measures, reinforcing its role as an add-on rather than a primary controller therapy.49,50 Moreover, the absence of a clear effect on quality of life may reflect the multifactorial nature of asthma outcomes, which are influenced not only by pharmacological control of airway inflammation but also by psychological status, comorbidities, and environmental factors.51,52 These findings emphasize the importance of a holistic, patient-centered approach to asthma management that integrates pharmacotherapy with education, self-management strategies, and psychosocial support to optimize overall outcomes.53,54

Strengths and Limitations

This study is significant as it offers novel insights into the potential clinical consequences of montelukast use among asthmatic patients in Saudi Arabia. The application of validated assessment instruments enhanced the rigor of patient-reported outcome measures, while data collection under professional supervision minimized interviewer bias and improved the reliability of the dataset. Nonetheless, several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between montelukast use and clinical outcomes, and residual confounding by asthma severity or comorbid conditions cannot be entirely excluded. In addition, baseline anxiety and depression status prior to montelukast initiation were not available. Moreover, the single-center setting at a tertiary hospital in Jeddah may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader Saudi population. Finally, reliance on self-reported measures of adherence and psychological outcomes introduces the potential for recall and response bias. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable evidence on the prevalence and clinical implications of montelukast use among adult asthma patients in the Saudi context. Future longitudinal, multicenter studies with larger, more diverse populations, detailed baseline psychiatric evaluations, and comprehensive assessments of asthma severity are needed to better clarify the long-term safety profile of montelukast and its impact on patient-reported outcomes.

Conclusion

Montelukast prescription was common among asthmatic patients in Saudi Arabia, with nearly half of the cohort receiving the medication. Its use was associated with higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms compared with non-users, while no significant associations were observed with asthma control, adherence to inhaled therapies, or quality of life. Given the cross-sectional design, causality cannot be established, and these associations may partly reflect confounding by indication, baseline group differences, and underlying disease severity rather than a direct effect of montelukast. Overall, clinicians should carefully weigh potential benefits against possible psychiatric risks when prescribing montelukast and consider routine monitoring of mental health symptoms during treatment. Future longitudinal studies with detailed baseline psychiatric and disease severity assessments are needed to better clarify its safety profile.
