Insights into obstetric anesthesia practices: a quantitative survey among physicians across Arab countries
Authors: Nancy Abou Nafeh, Fatima Msheik El Khoury, Amro Khalili, Carine Zeeni, Gloria Al Karaki, Raghad Mroueh, Thuraya HajAli, Sahar Siddik-Sayyid
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObstetric anesthesia guidelines are essential for standardizing obstetric anesthesia practices globally and ensuring high-quality patient care. However, practices may vary across different settings, and there are limited data from Arab countries. This study aims to gain insights into obstetric anesthesia practices in several major hospitals across Arab countries.MethodsA questionnaire was emailed to 85 obstetric anesthesiologists/anesthesia chairpersons in major hospitals, including academic medical institutions and central hospitals, across 11/22 Arab countries. This survey gathered data on key structural and process-related obstetric anesthesia indicators.ResultsOut of 85 contacted, we had 56 responses (65.8%), with 41 being fully completed, providing insights into obstetric anesthesia indicators. Regarding structure: 31 (76%) hospitals had an operating room adjacent to the delivery room, all had intensive care units, and 22 (54%) had a lead obstetric anesthesiologist. For equipment, 19 (46%) had a video laryngoscope in the delivery suite, and 20 (49%) occasionally used ultrasound for epidurals. Regarding process: 33 (81%) held regular meetings, and 21 (51%) conducted research. Before epidural and spinal procedures, 26 (63%) and 28 (68%) required coagulation studies for patients without a history of hemorrhagic complications, while 38 (93%) and 36 (88%) mandated a platelet count, respectively. For labor analgesia, 34 (83%) primarily used epidurals, and 15 (37%) placed preemptive catheters in high-risk pregnancies. For cesarean delivery, 40 (98%) used spinals, with 16 (39%) using intrathecal morphine and 22 (54%) administering aspiration prophylaxis before general anesthesia. Regarding spinal-induced hypotension, 6 (15%) used prophylactic phenylephrine infusion.ConclusionThis survey highlights variations in obstetric anesthesia practices among various major hospitals in several Arab countries, compared to international recommendations. It emphasizes the need for obstetric anesthesia registries in the Arab world for future research. Further studies are required to outline country-specific practices, improve resource allocation, and enhance obstetric population safety and satisfaction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-024-02728-x.Keywords: Anesthesia, Obstetrical; Arab countries; Pregnancy; Surveys and questionnaires; Major hospitals

Background

Different professional associations in several countries have developed guidelines for obstetric anesthesia practice [1–3]. These guidelines aim to harmonize obstetric anesthesia practices on a global scale and ensure alignment with the latest international scientific recommendations [1–3]. The practice of obstetric anesthesia, like other aspects of health services, may exhibit variations across Arab countries. These differences could be attributed to the different obstetric guidelines adopted by each country, the absence of such practice guidelines, or the available resources within their respective healthcare systems. A recent review revealed variations in maternal and neonatal health outcomes across Africa and the Middle East [4]. Despite recent significant improvements, there are persistent inequities in both access to and the quality of obstetric anesthetic care [4].

Over the past two decades, the Arab countries have faced numerous challenges, including devastating wars, refugee crises, revolutions, economic crises, sharp increases in poverty, and most recently the Coronavirus disease of 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. The quality of medical services in these countries may have been adversely affected by burdens such as physician shortages caused by mass migration, as well as shortages in medical equipment and medications [5]. A recent review indicated that, in the context of the global workforce crisis, North American healthcare is experiencing significant challenges, with women’s care being especially affected by these shortages. The COVID-19 pandemic has underscored the heightened vulnerability of women in these situations [6].

To the best of our knowledge, no study has been conducted to date that gathers data and provides insights into obstetric anesthesia practices in hospitals across Arab countries. It is important to address this gap in the literature to better characterize potential variations in practices and to inform key strategies to improve them. Therefore, this study aims to gain insights into obstetric anesthesia practices by surveying obstetric anesthesiologists/anesthesia chairpersons in major hospitals throughout Arab countries.

The collected data will be evaluated against international recommendations, such as those from the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) and the Royal College of Anaesthetists (RCA). The survey data involves examining logistical arrangements, anesthesia procedures for both labor and cesarean deliveries, assessing the availability of equipment and medications, and evaluating the implementation of protocols and training within the obstetric anesthesia framework.

Furthermore, since no previous work has been done in this field within the Arab countries, our goal is to establish a baseline understanding of the logistical challenges and obstacles associated with sampling and data collection.

Methods

Study design

Between February 15, 2023, and June 1, 2023, we conducted a descriptive cross-sectional survey among obstetric anesthesiologists/anesthesia chairpersons in the Arab countries, as illustrated in supplemental digital content 1, Fig. 1. This article follows EQUATOR (Enhancing the Quality and Transparency of Health Research Network) guidelines [7]. The study received Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval from the American University of Beirut (protocol number SBS-2022-0149). All participants signed an electronic informed consent form upon their approval.

Sampling strategy

A list of obstetric anesthesiologists from major hospitals, including academic medical institutions and central hospitals, across the Arab region was not readily available. To facilitate this study, we used convenience sampling through a snowball sampling technique. We initially contacted members of the Pan Arab Federation of Societies of Anesthesia (PAFSA) whose contact information was available on the PAFSA website. We reached out to these members via email and asked them to recommend other obstetric anesthesiologists/anesthesia chairpersons in major hospitals across the Arab region. Through this process, we obtained contacts of obstetric anesthesiologists/anesthesia chairpersons in 85 major hospitals across 11 out of 22 countries in the Arab World. Most of these identified hospitals were listed among the 61 ranked hospitals for 2024 on SCImago, validating that we reached major institutions in the region [8]. These contacts were from Algeria, Egypt, Jordan, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA), Lebanon, Libya, Oman, Qatar, Syria, the United Arab Emirates (UAE), and Yemen.

Data collection

Eighty-five obstetric anesthesiologists/anesthesia chairpersons received an invitation email to participate in this survey, using the online software Lime Survey. The invitation provided the details and objectives of the study, along with an online informed consent to be signed electronically, if they agreed to participate. The informed consent stated that participation was voluntary, responses were anonymous, and participants were free to withdraw at any time. Non-responders received up to 3 follow-up emails every two weeks to encourage survey completion. To ensure the integrity of responses and prevent multiple submissions from the same participant, each survey recipient was provided with a unique token. This measure aimed to maintain data accuracy and reliability throughout the study.

Questionnaire development

We aimed to gather data and gain insights into obstetric anesthesia practices using an 80-item questionnaire that covered various structure and process indicators, organized into six sections: (1) Structure indicators: General information (2) Process indicators: Obstetric anesthesia protocols, educational and scholarly activities, pre-anesthesia screening, analgesia for labor, and anesthesia for cesarean delivery (Supplemental digital content 2, Table 1). These items were generated through a review of the most recent international obstetric anesthesia guidelines to establish evidence-based metrics for assessing obstetric anesthesia practices for healthy parturients. The questions were derived from the practice guidelines for obstetric anesthesia: An updated report by the ASA task force on obstetric anesthesia and the Society for Obstetric Anesthesia and Perinatology (2016) [2], as well as the guidelines for the provision of anesthesia services for an obstetric population by the RCA (2023) [1].

Survey items were developed by reviewing the literature to derive evidence-based metrics underlying successful obstetric anesthesia practices, thus supporting the content validity of this questionnaire [9–12]. Content validity was also assessed by three experienced anesthesiologists in obstetric anesthesia from the American University of Beirut, Medical Center. All questions received a rating of 3 or 4 for relevance, and the value of the content validity index (CVI) was 1. Three additional anesthesiologists from the same institution assessed the clarity and comprehensibility of each question. The survey displayed a positive face validity, thus reflecting an unanimous agreement on its suitability for assessing obstetric anesthesia practice metrics. Two types of answer formats were possible: selecting the appropriate answer(s) and free numerical text.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS version 29.0. Descriptive analysis included frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, as well as medians and ranges for continuous variables. Additionally, 95% confidence intervals were calculated for proportions.

Results

A total of 56/85 (65.8%) anesthesiologists participated in this study from Algeria, Egypt, Jordan, the KSA, Lebanon, Libya, Oman, Qatar, Syria, the UAE, and Yemen. Incomplete responses were deemed invalid due to a high amount of missing data and were consequently excluded from the analysis. This resulted in 41 complete responses. Key findings are outlined below while additional data on other metrics can be found in their corresponding tables.

Structure indicators

Infrastructure, personnel, and equipment/medications

Thirty-one (76%) hospitals had an operating room adjacent to the delivery room. Moreover, 41 (100%) respondents had intensive care units and accepted high-risk pregnancies (Table 1). Regarding personnel, a lead obstetric anesthesiologist with specialized obstetric training was present in 22 (54%) hospitals (Table 1). Among the respondents, 19 (46%) reported having a video laryngoscope specifically allocated for use in the delivery suite. Regarding ultrasound usage, 20 (49%) reported its occasional use for identifying or measuring the depth of the epidural space in specific indications (Table 1).

Anesthesia for cesarean delivery

Eighteen (46%) respondents reported a cesarean delivery rate of 31–50%. The primary technique used was spinal, as reported by 40 (98%) respondents. Additionally, 16 (39%) respondents used intrathecal morphine for post cesarean analgesia, 22 (54%) used aspiration prophylaxis when performing general anesthesia for cesarean delivery, and 8 (20%) administered prophylactic antibiotics only after umbilical cord clamping. Various approaches were reported for managing spinal-induced hypotension, with 6 (15%) respondents using a prophylactic phenylephrine infusion (Table 6).

Discussion

This study aimed to address the lack of data on obstetric anesthesia practices in Arab countries. Our findings characterize obstetric anesthesia practices across diverse major hospitals in different Arab countries, highlighting both adherence to and deviations from ASA and RCA guidelines. Notably, some findings closely aligned (80–100%) with current international ASA and RCA recommendations, while others revealed significant deviations to varying degrees across both structure and process indicators. Additionally, it established a baseline understanding of the logistical challenges and obstacles associated with data accessibility and collection.

The finding on obstetric anesthesia practices indicated that many of the major hospitals surveyed demonstrated a strong commitment to patient safety and optimal care. They showcased the availability of essential resources, and a significant number of respondents emphasized the importance of comprehensive patient assessment and utilized effective anesthesia techniques for obstetric cases. These practices, widely adopted by over 80% of respondents, contribute to improved outcomes. Nevertheless, it is crucial to emphasize the need for 100% compliance with these guidelines.

Structure indicators

In terms of structure indicators, some of the identified hospitals did not comply with RCA guidelines mandating the presence of at least one fully equipped obstetric theater nearby for emergency operative deliveries [1]. This failure can delay urgent interventions for emergency deliveries, raising the risk of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. Many hospitals also lacked designated lead obstetric anesthesiologists as recommended by the RCA guidelines [1]. A recent narrative review highlighted that the availability of competent anesthesiologists in obstetric anesthesia is linked to patient morbidity and mortality outcomes. Among various recommendations, standardizing training and practice could improve global healthcare delivery in obstetric anesthesia in Europe. Fellowship programs run by renowned scientific institutions could facilitate this standardization both within Europe and internationally [13].

Respondents also reported access issues to tools essential for managing critical situations. Less than half had a video laryngoscope or cricothyroidotomy set allocated specifically for the delivery suite, despite ASA and RCA guidelines recommending that this equipment be readily available for managing difficult airways in the delivery suite [1, 2]. Additionally, a substantial proportion lacked a rapid infusion system for quick administration of blood products and intravenous fluids, which may potentially lead to adverse outcomes in hemorrhagic emergencies [1]. The majority also did not have point-of-care testing (TEG or Rotem), which can result in less effective management of coagulation issues, increasing the need for blood product administration, and potentially compromising patient safety during high-risk pregnancies [14, 15, 16]. While financially challenging, it is crucial to upgrade equipment to address these areas of concern and achieve optimal obstetric anesthesia care.

Our findings resonate with the results of a cross-sectional survey of obstetric units in 26 European countries which revealed significant resource variations, with many units lacking critical care equipment and services. Notably, 13.8% lacked point-of-care hemoglobin measurements, 15.2% lacked lactate measurements, and 11% lacked transfusion services. Additionally, 23.8% could not administer hypotensive agent infusions, 45.5% lacked cell savers, and 64.4% lacked thromboelastometry. Invasive ventilation was unavailable in 3.4% of units, 11.7% lacked non-invasive ventilation, and 38.3% lacked extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [17]. These similar deficiencies highlight the potential risks in emergency obstetric care, emphasizing the need for better resource allocation and adherence to guidelines to ensure patient safety and optimal outcomes.

Process indicators

A notable absence of standardized protocols for obstetric anesthesia practice was observed across various institutions. Establishing and ensuring easy accessibility to standardized guidelines and checklists, including aspiration prophylaxis, post-dural puncture headache treatment, local anesthetic systemic toxicity treatment, labor analgesia, cesarean delivery, as well as staffing and supervision protocols, is recommended to standardize treatment modalities and reduce the risk of medical errors [1]. In fact, the use of safety bundles for hemorrhage and hypertension, as part of the widely utilized World Health Organization surgical safety checklist, has been shown to reduce maternal mortality [18]. In addition, fewer respondents engaged in scholarly work such as conducting research in this field, despite encouragement outlined in the RCA guidelines [1]. This might reflect a constraint in available resources such as research funding, and not necessarily a lack of interest or commitment.

Regarding obstetric pre-anesthesia screening, while most respondents routinely ordered a blood type and screen or crossmatch for deliveries, it is important to tailor this approach based on maternal history, anticipated hemorrhagic complications, and institutional policies [2]. Additionally, many respondents required coagulation parameters from parturients without a history of hemorrhagic complications, before administrating neuraxial anesthesia. Such practice can increase healthcare costs and cause delays in administering neuraxial anesthesia [2].

Regarding epidural analgesia and particularly the mode of local anesthetic administration through the epidural catheter, most respondents used continuous infusion via an infusion pump or administered boluses upon patient’s request. However, PCEA might be preferable in such cases as due to its reduced need for anesthetic interventions and lower local anesthetic dosages [2]. The limited use of PCEA may be attributed to the unavailability of PCEA machines and/or the unfamiliarity with their use. In terms of high-risk pregnancies, more than half of respondents didn’t preemptively place an epidural catheter, despite its potential to decrease the need for general anesthesia during emergent procedures [2].

Regarding anesthesia practices for cesarean delivery, the majority reported a high cesarean delivery rate over 30%, exceeding the World Health Organization’s recommended range of 10–15% [19]. This high rate could be due to the nature of the surveyed hospitals as “major”. To address potential overuse and mitigate implications for both mother and child, it is essential to implement quality assurance programs, provide written guidelines, and conduct regular medical audits [20]. The low use of intrathecal morphine for post-cesarean analgesia suggests a higher risk for women to experience moderate to severe postoperative pain, indicating a need for effective pain management strategies [21]. Roughly half of respondents routinely used aspiration prophylaxis as recommended before surgical procedures performed under general anesthesia, which is important to prevent aspiration-related complications [2]. Additionally, many reported inconsistent use of prophylactic antibiotics, which were administered after umbilical cord clamping rather than within 60 min before the start of a cesarean or immediately after skin incision in emergency cases. This practice may increase the risk of surgical site infections [3]. Despite the proven effectiveness of phenylephrine infusion in preventing spinal hypotension, nausea, and vomiting, with fewer clinical interventions [22], its limited use among our respondents suggests an increased risk for maternal hypotension. This could adversely affect uterine perfusion and maternal-fetal well-being.

Recently published papers on obstetric anesthesia from different regions highlight the global challenge of providing optimal care [4, 6, 13, 17, 2, 23–27]. Similar to trends in the Arab countries, significant variability exists in anesthetic standards of care within obstetric departments across many European Union countries. To tackle this issue and improve the safety of obstetric patients, a European minimum standards document for obstetric anesthesia was developed [26]. Additionally, a multicenter international survey revealed disparities in the preparedness for severe maternal morbidity across Europe. The use of the Early Obstetric Warning Score was low, and critical care facilities were often inadequately resourced or not uniformly available [27].

Implications of the study and future work

Key findings reflect practices in a small yet diverse group of major hospitals in several Arab countries. Our study highlights a critical need for reporting and monitoring obstetric anesthesia practices in the region and motivates further research and policy actions on essential aspects of obstetric anesthesia practice. Future studies including qualitative research should aim to better understand the specific challenges major hospitals face in adhering to key guidelines. This understanding could inform targeted interventions and enhance practice adherence.

Our findings also convey important messages to decision-makers in the public health sector of Arab countries. They should act as catalysts to encourage the creation of national workforces in obstetric anesthesia, and the adoption of national clinical practice guidelines based on international recommendations. Regular audit programs should be implemented to ensure adherence to these guidelines. Our future implications align with recent reviews on safe obstetric anesthesia in low- and middle-income countries in Africa which underscored the need for the development of guidelines that are adaptable to specific contexts, while also acknowledging difficulties in achieving both safety and effective care in resource-constrained environments [28].

Additionally, the logistical challenges encountered during this work underscore the necessity for establishing obstetric anesthesia registries across the Arab world, as well as collaborative platforms among researchers to enable the conduct of similar but more comprehensive research work in this field at a regional level.

Limitations of our study

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. Despite our diligent efforts and the use of a snowballing technique to gather contacts, the research team faced notable difficulties, particularly in accessing information for certain Arab countries. Therefore, we collected data from 56 of 85 obstetric anesthesiologists/anesthesia chairpersons from 11 of 22 Arab countries, potentially limiting the sample’s representativeness. Second, due to the anonymization mandated by the IRB, we cannot identify which of the 11 countries responded and we could not categorize the results into broader groups such as low, middle, and high-income countries. Hence, aggregating countries with diverse economic statuses into a unified set of results may have overlooked specific areas that might be lacking in resources. Third, our survey results cannot be generalized to entire countries. The emphasis on major hospitals prevents an accurate assessment of the situation in regional and district hospitals, where circumstances may significantly differ.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study offers insights into obstetric anesthesia practices in diverse major hospitals across Arab countries, revealing variations in access to different anesthesia services. While some aspects align with international guidelines, significant divergences are evident in other areas. These non-compliant practices may increase the risk of morbidity and mortality among the obstetric population. Furthermore, it emphasizes the significance of establishing registries for obstetric anesthesia in the Arab world to address the logistical challenges associated with future research in the field. Additional research is warranted to thoroughly describe obstetric anesthesia practices in each Arab country, facilitating better allocation of resources to enhance the safety and satisfaction of the obstetric population in the region.
