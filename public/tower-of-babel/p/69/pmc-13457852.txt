Discovery and quantification of novel diazepam metabolites BP-246 and BP-271 in freshwater fish using HRMS and UPLC-MS/MS
Authors: Ping Ouyang, Junlin Wang, Nianhua Zhang, Zhengyan Hu, Changren Ye, Dingnan Wang, Yang Wang, Pinggu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiazepam, commonly used in freshwater fish transport and aquaculture, poses potential food safety and public health risks due to its residues and metabolites. However, current monitoring efforts primarily focus on the parent drug and its major metabolites, leaving potential toxic transformation products uncharacterized. This study reports two novel metabolites (BP-246, 5-chloro-2-(methylamino)phenyl)(phenyl)methanone, and BP-271, 6-chloro-1-methyl-4-phenylquinazolin-2(1 H)-one) in freshwater fish muscle, a matrix in which these compounds have not been previously reported, using liquid chromatography-Orbitrap high-resolution mass spectrometry (LC-Orbitrap HRMS). Using ProTox3.0 and ADMETlab3.0 for toxicity prediction revealed that BP-246 and BP-271 exhibit high blood-brain barrier permeability, neurotoxicity and ecotoxicity. A sensitive and validated ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) method was developed for the simultaneous quantification of diazepam, three established metabolites (nordiazepam, oxazepam, temazepam), and the two novel compounds in fish tissue. Sample preparation involved acetonitrile ultrasonication extraction followed by solid-phase extraction (SPE) purification. Matrix-matched calibration curves demonstrated excellent linearity ( R2 > 0.999) over the range of 0.2–10 µg/kg. Method recovery ranged from 78.5% to 119.9%, with intra-day and inter-day relative standard deviations (RSDs) below 9.9% and 9.1%, respectively. Limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were 0.05–0.06 µg/kg and 0.15–0.20 µg/kg, respectively. The method showed robust selectivity and consistent retention times. When applied to 10 market-sourced, diazepam-positive fish samples, the method successfully revealed differential metabolite profiles, enabling rapid, high-throughput monitoring of diazepam and transformation products in commercial fish products. These findings provide a basis for comprehensive risk assessment and regulatory control of diazepam residues in the food supply chain.Keywords: Diazepam, Novel metabolite, Fish, HRMS, UPLC-MS/MSSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Chemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Diazepam (DZP), a long-acting benzodiazepine, first synthesized by Sternbach at Hoffmann-La Roche in 1959 and introduced as Valium in 1963, possesses well-established sedative-hypnotic and anxiolytic properties and is widely used in clinical practice. However, its widespread application, combined with metabolic excretion and anthropogenic activities, has led to diazepam contamination in aquatic ecosystems1. Additionally, diazepam has been illicitly used in aquaculture, transportation, and bait fishing since the 1960–1970 s. Multiple studies have reported the presence of diazepam in both aquatic environments and aquatic organisms2–4. This contamination ultimately results in the accumulation of diazepam and its metabolites in aquatic organisms, which can enter the human body through the food chain or the water cycle, potentially inducing neurotoxicity and other adverse health effects5. Although concerns about the environmental persistence of benzodiazepines emerged in the 1980 s, detailed investigations into their biotransformation and impacts on aquatic ecosystems only began in the early 2000 s, which partly explains why analytical methods for detecting complex metabolites have lagged. In response to these public health risks, China’s Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs stipulates, in GB 31650 − 2019 (National Food Safety Standard: Maximum Residue Limits of Veterinary Drugs in Foods), that while diazepam may be administered therapeutically, it must not be detectable in animal-derived food products.

Current research regarding diazepam contamination in aquatic environments primarily focuses on the parent compound and its three active metabolites: nordiazepam, temazepam, and oxazepam6–9. These metabolites, produced via hepatic biotransformation, exhibit toxicity and bioactivity comparable to the parent compound. For instance, Saaristo et al. observed that wild fish exposed to trace concentrations of oxazepam displayed anxiety-like behaviors within short exposure durations10. Similarly, a study reported oxazepam concentrations in perch muscle were six times higher than those in Fyris River water, indicating bioaccumulation potential11. Furthermore, Shan et al. demonstrated that orally administered diazepam in carp is primarily metabolized into nordiazepam, with minor formation of temazepam. Their study identified nordiazepam as a key residual marker and found that frequent consumption of contaminated carp may pose health risks to humans12.

However, current research on fish muscle matrix has primarily focused on the three metabolites mentioned above, while the presence of other diazepam-related products, such as the chlorinated disinfection by-products BP-246 and BP-271, remains largely unexplored. Based on this, we aim to employ a non-targeted high-resolution method to further screen for other diazepam-related compounds in fish muscle. These known and unknown metabolites may interact synergistically, forming complex transformation networks. Braun et al. highlighted that cumulative neurotoxic effects can arise from exposure to multiple compounds at sub-threshold concentrations, even when individual substances are below toxicological effect level13. Therefore, investigating additional diazepam metabolites in aquatic organisms is critical for safeguarding human health, particularly considering the complex nature of environmental transformation processes.

High-Resolution Mass Spectrometry (HRMS) is a powerful analytical tool for the non-targeted screening of unknown compounds in environmental and food safety applications. By providing precise ion fragmentation patterns and exact mass values, HRMS enables accurate compound identification, while its integration with spectral databases facilitates structural elucidation. This approach has been widely applied in non-targeted analyses across food safety, environmental health, and drug metabolism studies14–16. For instance, Yu et al. developed a non-targeted strategy to detect antimicrobial transformation products (ATPs) in wastewater, successfully identifying 52 antimicrobials and 49 ATPs9. Similarly, Zhang et al. established a qualitative pathway-structure relationship (QPSR) model by combining concentration-dependent transcriptomics with LC-HRMS to prioritize organic pollutants in tap and raw water17.

Although analytical techniques such as GC-MS, HPLC-MS/MS, and UPLC-MS/MS are widely used for targeted residue analysis of diazepam and its metabolites, these methods generally employ low-resolution mass analyzers (e.g., triple quadrupoles), which offer superior quantification capabilities but limited scope for identifying unknowns18. For example, Zhou et al. optimized a QuEChERS UPLC-MS/MS method for the efficient quantification of diazepam and three active metabolites in aquatic products and aquaculture environments with high extraction efficiency19. Hybrid analytical strategies that combine HRMS with low-resolution MS have demonstrated significant potential. Alvarez-Mora et al. utilized both HPLC and UHPLC-q-Orbitrap systems for targeted and non-targeted analyses to investigate bioaccumulation and biotransformation of diazepam in glass eels20.

Following a similar integrated strategy, the present study utilizes HRMS for the non-targeted screening and structural identification of diazepam metabolites, supplemented by UPLC-MS/MS for quantitative analysis of diazepam and metabolites—a methodologically robust framework. Distinctively, our workflow identifies novel metabolites using secondary MS/MS without relying solely on spectral databases or omics driven techniques.

Using this approach, two metabolites - designated BP-246 and BP-271 - previously unreported in fish muscle, were identified. Their structures were confirmed to be similar to those of known diazepam-related compounds based on comparative analysis with literature data21,22. Based on these findings, a UPLC-MS/MS method was developed for the simultaneous quantification of diazepam and five metabolites-including the two novel compounds-in fish tissue. This method was successfully applied to analyze market-sourced fish samples, demonstrating its practical applicability in food safety monitoring.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Diazepam, Nordiazepam, Oxazepam, Temazepam, Diazepam-D5, Nordiazepam-D5, Oxazepam-D5, Temazepam-D5standard solutions (all 100 µg/mL) were purchased from Tianjin Alta Scientific Co., Ltd. (China). BP-246 and BP-271 standard solutions (100 µg/mL each) were kindly provided by Dr. Shao Bing (Beijing Center for Disease Prevention and Control.),and detailed synthesis procedures and purity data were available in Zhang et al22. All solvents and reagents were analytical grade: acetonitrile and methanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, China); sodium chloride (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China). The main solid phase extraction column was performed using C18/PSA column (500 mg/6 mL; Kangyuan Technology Co., China). Fish samples were collected from local markets in Hangzhou, China. The water used for the preparation of the mobile phase was obtained from an ultrapure water preparator (Merck Millipore, USA).

Instrumentation

Qualitative analyses were conducted using a Thermo Scientific™ Orbitrap Exploris™ 240 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Quantitative analyses were performed on a Waters ACQUITY™ UPLC-MS/MS system (Waters Corporation, USA), comprising an I-Class UPLC and an Xevo™ TQ-S triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with electrospray ionization (ESI).

Orbitrap HRMS analysis for metabolite identification

The high-resolution mass spectrometer was calibrated using Pierce™ FlexMix™ solution for non-targeted screening of products in full scan mode and product ion mode, and product ion mode for confirmation. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY™ UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm×100 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters) maintained at 40 ℃, with binary mobile phases consisting of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B). Gradient elution was as follows: 0–0.5 min, 40% B; 0.5–4 min, 40% B − 50% B; 4–9 min, 50% B − 100% B; 9–10 min, 100% B; 10–10.1 min, 100% B − 40% B; 10.1–12 min, 40% B. The flow rate and injection volume were 0.4 mL/min and 5 µL. Key MS/MS parameters included: ESI capillary voltage, 3.0 kV; sheath gas flow, 50 arb; auxiliary gas flow, 10 arb; purge gas flow, 1 arb; ion transfer tube temperature, 325 ℃; and vaporizer temperature, 350 ℃. The resolution of the orbit trap in full scan mode was 120,000, the scanning range was m/z 150–400 using nitrogen as the collision gas. Compound identification was executed via Xcalibur™ software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

UPLC-MS/MS method development for quantitative analysis

Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY™ UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm×2.1 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters) at 35 ℃ with a gradient of 0.1%(v/v) formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B) as mobile phases. Gradient elution was as follows: 0–2 min, 30% B; 2–7.00 min, 30% B − 70% B; 7.00–9.00 min, 70% B − 100% B; 9 min, 100% B; 10.00–10.10 min, 100% B − 30% B; 10.10–12.00 min, 30% B. The flow rate and injection volume were 0.3 mL/min and 2 µL, respectively.

The mass spectra were performed using an electrospray ionization source (ESI) in positive ion mode under Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) with the following parameters: capillary voltage, 0.5 kV; ion source temperature, 150 ℃; desolvation temperature, 500 ℃; desolvation gas flow, 800 L/h; and cone gas flow, 150 L/h. Quantitative analyses of diazepam, BP-246, BP-271, nordiazepam, oxazepam, and temazepam was performed using MassLynx™ software (Waters).

Preparation of standard solutions

100 ng/mL mixed standard solution: accurately pipette 10 µL each of diazepam, BP-246, BP-271, nordiazepam, oxazepam, and temazepam standard solutions into a volumetric flask, add acetonitrile to 10 mL, homogenize, and store in a 10 mL amber glass vial.

400 ng/mL internal standard (IS) mixed standard solution: Pipette 40 µL each of diazepam-D5, nordiazepam-D5, oxazepam-D5, and temazepam-D5 into a 10 mL volumetric flask, add acetonitrile to 10 mL, homogenize, and store in a 10 mL amber glass vial. All standard and IS stock solutions were stored at −20 °C. Chemical structures of the six target compounds are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Sample preparation and extraction procedures

Fish muscle tissues were homogenized, and minced fish samples were transferred into 50 mL centrifuge tubes and stored at − 20 °C.

Sample preparation followed established protocols23 for diazepam metabolite extraction. Briefly, 2.00 ± 0.01 g of thawed fish tissue was spiked with 20 µL of a mixed internal standard solution (400 ng/mL), incubated for 5 min, and extracted with 10 mL of acetonitrile via 20 min vertexing followed by 30 min ultrasonication. Subsequently, 2 g of NaCl was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 5 min, then centrifuged at 9000 ×g for 4 min at 4 ℃ The supernatant was diluted to 5 mL with 90% (v/v) acetonitrile aqueous solution. The entire solution was loaded onto a C18/PSA solid-phase extraction (SPE) column pre-conditioned with 90% acetonitrile. After elution, 4 mL of the purified extract was collected, evaporated to near-dryness under a nitrogen stream at 35 ℃, reconstituted in 1.0 mL of initial mobile phase, filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE membrane, and analyzed by UPLC-MS/MS.

Method validation

The analytical performance of the UPLC-MS/MS method was evaluated for matrix effects, linearity, regression equations, correlation coefficients (R2), limits of detection (LOD), limits of quantification (LOQ), recovery, and precision.

To assess matrix effects, matrix-matched calibration curves were prepared using blank fish tissue at concentrations of 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, and 10 ng/mL (2 ng/mL for mixed internal standards). These curves were compared to solvent-based calibration curves by analyzing the slopes to quantify matrix interference. Specifically, linear equations were derived via the external standard method, where the slope of the matrix-matched curve (B) and the solvent-based curve (A) were used to calculate matrix effect (ME) as follows: ME (%) = B/A × 10024. Method accuracy and precision were evaluated through six replicate analyses at three spiked concentration levels (low, medium, high), quantified as mean recovery (%) and relative standard deviation (RSD). Intra-day precision was assessed by analyzing all replicates within a 24-hour period, while inter-day precision involved repeating the procedure over three consecutive days. Method sensitivity was defined by LOD and LOQ, corresponding to signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios of 3 and 10, respectively.

In routine practice, for a fully validated method, single measurements are generally considered acceptable when appropriate quality controls are included in each analytical batch25,26. Each batch included spiked samples for quality control (recoveries within the acceptable range of 80–120%), along with solvent and matrix blanks to check for carryover and contamination. To further enhance data reliability, all samples were analyzed in duplicate (n = 2). Additionally, two randomly selected samples were analyzed in triplicate (n = 3) to strengthen the reliability of the detected data. The mass spectrometer was calibrated weekly following the manufacturer’s instructions, and system suitability was verified before and during each batch analysis by injecting standards to ensure consistent sensitivity and retention time stability.

Toxicity prediction databases

Given that diazepam, nordiazepam, oxazepam, and temazepam are relatively common benzodiazepines, this study focused solely on BP-246 and BP-271, conducting toxicity predictions using ProTox3.0 and ADMETlab3.0.

ProTox3.0 predicts 61 toxicological endpoints based on machine learning algorithms and molecular similarity principles. Leveraging established toxicity databases, it enables the prediction of potential toxic effects of compounds without the need for in vivo or in vitro experiments—facilitating the screening of compounds requiring further analysis, reducing reliance on animal testing, supporting early-stage risk assessment, and lowering the costs associated with toxicity prediction27. In the present study, ProTox3.0 was primarily utilized to predict endpoints including LD50, toxicity classification, drug-induced liver injury(DILI), neurotoxicity, carcinogenicity, and ecotoxicity.

ADMETlab 3.0 employs a multi-task graph attention framework integrated with high-quality datasets to comprehensively evaluate the absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) of chemicals, analyze physicochemical properties, and predict toxicity endpoints. Building on the reliability of its predecessor (ADMETlab 2.0), the 3.0 version expands endpoint coverage, enhances model performance, and incorporates uncertainty assessment28,29. Previous studies have highlighted its utility as a preliminary screening tool for assessing the toxicity of novel psychoactive substances30. In this study, ADMETlab 3.0 was primarily used to predict endpoints including ADME properties (e.g., plasma protein binding [PPB], blood-brain barrier penetration [BBB]), multiorgan toxicities (e.g., hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity), and genotoxicity alerts.

Results

Structural characterization of novel metabolites (BP-246 and BP-271) in fish muscle

To identify potential diazepam metabolites in fish samples, a non-targeted screening strategy using LC-Orbitrap HRMS was employed. Our research group has previously established an in-house MS/MS database for psychiatric drugs, which includes benzodiazepines, with diazepam being one of the benzodiazepines covered. Subsequently, when expanding the database, we added diazepam-related compounds, including diazepam metabolites and chlorinated disinfection by-products of diazepam reported in the literature. Fish sample extracts were analyzed in full-scan mode, and the acquired data were matched against this existing database based on accurate mass and fragmentation patterns. Candidate compounds were then confirmed by comparison with reference standards. The overall discovery workflow is illustrated in Supplementary Material Figure S1. Non-targeted screening of diazepam-positive fish samples via high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Full-scan HRMS profiles of diazepam-positive fish samples.

During chromatographic peak extraction for nordiazepam, characteristic fragment ions at m/z 271.0632 were observed at retention times (RT) of 2.71 and 3.19 min. These ions were further characterized through MS/MS spectral analysis. The nordiazepam standard exhibited a protonated molecular ion ([M + H]⁺) at m/z 271.0632 in positive ion mode, with prominent product ions at m/z 140.0262, 208.0996, and 165.0214. The fragment ion profile and relative abundances in the nordiazepam standard aligned with the MS/MS spectrum of the compound eluting at RT 2.71 min. In contrast, the peak at RT 3.19 min displayed distinct secondary fragments, including m/z 221.0709 and 193.0163. Notably, under identical collision energy conditions (70 eV), the m/z 271.0632 precursor ion at RT 2.71 min underwent complete fragmentation, whereas the same precursor ion at RT 3.19 min remained stable with high abundance. These findings suggest that this substance is a novel compound with similar ionic structure to nordiazepam.

The MS/MS spectrum was acquired in positive ion mode using product ion scans. As shown in Fig. 3, the peak at 3.19 min exhibited a protonated molecular ion ([M + H]⁺) at m/z 271.0632. Key diagnostic ions were observed at m/z 193.0163, 221.0708, and 165.0214, which were consistent with the fragmentation pattern of the BP-271 reference standard. Detailed fragmentation pathways are provided in Supplementary Material Text S1. Comparative analysis with literature data revealed alignment with the reported structure of BP-271. Structural confirmation was achieved using a reference standard, demonstrating identical MS/MS properties. Consequently, this compound was designated as BP-271.

MS/MS fragmentation spectrum of BP-271 in diazepam-positive fish samples.

Further non-targeted screening revealed a precursor ion at m/z 246.0681, which fragmented into characteristic product ions at m/z 193.0887 and 228.0575 (Fig. 4). These fragmentation patterns resemble those of diazepam, suggesting potential diazepam metabolites.

Optimization of UPLC-MS/MS chromatographic conditions

Mobile phase selection

Nordiazepam and BP-271 are structural isomers that co-elute in the mass spectrum, necessitating chromatographic separation to ensure quantitative accuracy. In ESI⁺ mode, the inclusion of formic acid (0.1%, v/v) in the aqueous mobile phase component enhanced ionization efficiency and signal intensity, thus optimizing detection sensitivity. For organic phase selection, methanol and acetonitrile were evaluated for chromatographic resolution. As shown in Fig. 5, methanol-based mobile phases resulted in co-elution of nordiazepam and BP-271 in mixed standards, with overlapping peaks at identical retention times. Despite systematic optimization of elution gradients, column temperatures (25–40 °C), and flow rates (0.2–0.4 mL/min), adequate separation was unachievable. In contrast, acetonitrile achieved baseline separation for both compounds. Consequently, a mobile phase system comprising 0.1% (v/v) formic acid aqueous solution and acetonitrile was adopted (Fig. 5).

Optimization of purification conditions

Solid phase extraction column selection

Based on preliminary optimization studies, five solid-phase extraction (SPE) columns—C18/PSA, SLC, C18/SAX, EMR, and Silica/PSA—were evaluated for cleanup efficiency. Blank fish tissue was extracted with acetonitrile, spiked with 2 ng/mL of mixed standards, and processed through the SPE columns to compare recovery rates (Table 2). The C18/PSA column demonstrated superior recovery performance (70.9%–115.3%) and was selected for subsequent analyses. Notably, BP-246 exhibited relatively lower recovery (70.9%), likely attributable to matrix suppression effects (ME = 86.2%). To mitigate this, future studies should employ matrix-matched standards to enhance quantitative accuracy.

Method validation

Matrix effect evaluation

In ESI mode, the fish matrix affects the ionization efficiency of the target compounds, leading to inaccurate quantification. Matrix effects (ME) for the six target compounds were quantified using the Equation in 4.7. The measured ME values were as follows: diazepam (94.7%), BP-246 (86.2%), BP-271 (91.6%), nordiazepam (98.0%), oxazepam (100.3%), and temazepam (98.8%). Results demonstrated mild matrix suppression in all compounds except oxazepam, with BP-246 exhibiting the most pronounced suppression effect (ME = 86.2%). While these effects were within acceptable thresholds for bioanalytical methods (80% < ME < 120%), employing matrix-matched standards with isotopically labeled internal standards (IS) further enhanced quantification accuracy.

Linearity, sensitivity (LOD/LOQ), accuracy, and precision

Regression equations were established via least-squares linear regression using the peak area ratios of target compounds to corresponding isotopically labeled internal standards. All six compounds exhibited excellent linearity (R2 > 0.999) across the concentration range of 0.2–10.0 ng/mL (Table 3).

Preliminary in silico toxicity prediction for BP-246 and BP-271

Since no experimental toxicity data are available for BP-246 and BP-271 in the fish muscle matrix, this study employed ProTox3.0 and ADMETlab3.0 for preliminary in silico predictions to provide an initial screening of potential toxicological endpoints. It should be emphasized that these predictions are exploratory in nature and intended solely to flag potential concerns for future investigation; they do not constitute definitive toxicity conclusions, and all predictions require experimental verification.

Toxicity prediction results using ProTox3.0

As shown in Table 6, using the multi-dimensional toxicity endpoint predictions from ProTox 3.0, BP-246 was identified as “Active” across drug-induced liver injury (DILI, 0.52), neurotoxicity (0.86), carcinogenicity (0.54), mutagenicity (0.55), and ecotoxicity (0.81), thereby suggesting potential toxicological concerns for these toxicity endpoints. Discussion

Previous research on diazepam in fish has predominantly focused on three well-established metabolites: nordiazepam, oxazepam, and temazepam. In contrast, the present study reports two previously uncharacterized diazepam metabolites (BP-246 and BP-271) in fish muscle through non-targeted LC-Orbitrap HRMS screening. Notably, prior research by Zhang et al. identified five diazepam-derived disinfection by-products (DBPs) under simulated chlorination conditions, among which four, including BP-246 (an oxidation product) and BP-271 (an endocyclic demethylation product), were detected in drinking water22. In contrast, this study identified novel metabolites structurally identical to BP-246 and BP-271 in fish tissue, demonstrating that these compounds can be biotransformed and retained in living aquatic organisms.

Computational toxicology studies have suggested that certain transformation products of diazepam may exhibit higher toxicity to humans than their parent compounds31. Comparative analysis with reference standards of chlorination by-products BP-246 and BP-271 revealed identical structural features in novel metabolites detected in fish tissues. This finding leads to the inference that BP-246 and BP-271 in fish also likely possess higher toxicity than diazepam. Analysis of ten diazepam-positive fish samples sourced from markets revealed BP-246 in 80% of samples (concentration range: 0.13–3.95 µg/kg) and BP-271 in 30% (concentration range: 0.15–2.61 µg/kg). BP-246 was detectable even in samples with relatively low diazepam concentrations (mean: 11.03 ± 0.81 µg/kg), suggesting its potential use as a sensitive biomarker of diazepam exposure in aquatic organisms. It should be noted that the fish samples analyzed in this study were collected from local markets, and information regarding diazepam exposure history and environmental conditions was not available. The wide concentration range of diazepam observed (1.53–362.94 µg/kg) likely reflects complex and variable sources of contamination. However, accurate source tracing remains challenging due to multiple influencing factors. Future controlled exposure experiments are needed to systematically investigate the relationship between exposure conditions and metabolite formation.

Given the lack of toxicity data for these novel metabolites, ProTox3.0 and ADMETlab3.0 were used to predict their potential risks, yielding preliminary toxicological insights. Notably, the two novel metabolites (BP-246 and BP-271) identified in fish muscle in this study have not been previously reported in aquatic products, with no available toxicity data for reference. Thus, characterizing their potential risks to human health is of critical importance. In this context, network toxicology—grounded in compound structural information and leveraging algorithmic models from databases such as ProTox3.0, ADMETlab3.0, and TEST—enables rapid prediction of compounds’ potential hazards, including multi-organ toxicity, genotoxicity, and carcinogenicity32–34. This approach complements traditional chemical toxicity screening methods, which are labor-intensive and costly35. By facilitating multi-dimensional prediction and assessment of toxicity data, it provides a foundation for subsequent research and significantly enhances the efficiency of toxicity evaluation.

To further interpret the in silico prediction results presented in Sect. 3.6, BP-246 exhibits multidimensional coordinated toxicity in both ProTox3.0 and ADMETlab3.0 models. Specifically, the risk of central neurotoxicity shows an extremely high correlation: the combination of neurotoxicity (ProTox3.0: 0.86; ADMETlab3.0: 0.75) and high blood-brain barrier penetration (ProTox3.0: 0.91; ADMETlab3.0: 0.999) enables BP-246 to readily access the central nervous system, thereby increasing the risk of neurological disorders and pathological changes. Genetic and long-term risks show a high correlation, as the carcinogenicity (0.54) and mutagenicity (0.55) predicted by ProTox3.0 synergize with the five genotoxicity alerts from ADMETlab3.0, collectively suggesting potential long-term carcinogenic hazards. Ecological and multi-organ risks demonstrate a moderate correlation: the ecotoxicity (ProTox3.0: 0.81) indicates that upon entering the environment, this compound may exert significant toxicity on aquatic ecosystems (e.g., fish). Furthermore, drug-induced liver injury (DILI) represents an extremely prominent toxic risk (ProTox3.0: 0.52; ADMETlab3.0: 0.947), accompanied by nephrotoxicity (ProTox3.0: 0.77; ADMETlab3.0: 0.929), implying a potential “liver-kidney metabolic crosstalk damage”.

BP-271 exhibits multi-system synergistic toxicity, with core toxicities including neurotoxicity, respiratory toxicity, blood-brain barrier penetration, and ecotoxicity. It shows an extremely high correlation in terms of central nervous system risk: high neurotoxicity (ProTox3.0: 0.912; ADMETlab3.0: 0.95) combined with strong blood-brain barrier penetration (ProTox3.0: 0.984; ADMETlab3.0: 0.92), similar to BP-246, directly endangering the safety of the central nervous system. There is a high risk of liver toxicity and exposure: the value of drug-induced liver injury (DILI) and the high plasma protein binding rate (98.9%) imply a risk of hepatic accumulation, necessitating attention to the effects of long-term low-dose exposure. Ecological and food chain risks are also present: the ecotoxicity predicted by ProTox3.0 is 0.71, which is consistent with the aquatic toxicity alert from ADMETlab3.0. Combined with the detection of this compound in fish meat in the present study, it is speculated that BP-271 may accumulate through the food chain, ultimately affecting human health. In summary, the toxicity predictions of BP-246 and BP-271 exhibit common characteristics of “central neurotoxicity + ecotoxicity” in terms of mechanism consistency and ecological relevance. It is worth noting that there are some differences (such as DILI) between the prediction results of ProTox3.0 and ADMETlab3.0. These differences may stem from the different training datasets and algorithms used by the two platforms, reflecting their complementary application domains.

In China, diazepam is not permitted to be detected in any animal-derived food products. However, diazepam in freshwater fish is not present solely as the parent compound; it undergoes metabolic conversion into secondary metabolites, including BP-246 and BP-271. Current regulatory monitoring and detection protocols typically target diazepam and its three primary metabolites, omitting these potentially toxic transformation products. Therefore, incorporating the newly identified diazepam metabolites (BP-246 and BP-271) into quantitative methods is of significant importance for regulating diazepam residues in animal-derived foods.

To address this gap, we expanded upon our previous analytical methodology by developing a UPLC-MS/MS assay capable of simultaneously quantifying diazepam and five metabolites—including the newly identified BP-246 and BP-271—in fish tissue. Target analytes were extracted using acetonitrile and purified via a pass-through solid-phase extraction (SPE) column. This streamlined SPE approach improved sample preparation efficiency while maintaining high cleanup standards suitable for complex biological matrices.

The resulting UPLC-MS/MS method demonstrated excellent performance characteristics. Calibration curves showed strong linearity (r > 0.999) across the concentration range of 0.2–10.0 µg/kg. Mean recoveries ranged from 78.5% to 119.9%, with in-tra-day relative standard deviations (RSD) between 1.5% and 9.9%. the method also exhibited high sensitivity, with limits of detection (LOD) between 0.05 and 0.06 µg/kg and limits of quantification (LOQ) ranging from 0.15 to 0.2 µg/kg.

Conclusions

This study identified two novel metabolites, BP-246 and BP-271, in fish muscle using non-targeted LC-Orbitrap HRMS. These findings confirm that diazepam undergoes in vivo transformation into secondary metabolites that can persist in aquatic organisms. A validated UPLC-MS/MS method was subsequently established for simultaneous quantification of diazepam and five metabolites - including two novel metabolites in fish tissues. This method was successfully applied to real-world market samples, addressing the urgent need for sensitive and comprehensive monitoring of emerging pharmaceutical contaminants in commercial fish the supply chain. Larger-scale surveys are warranted to further characterize the occurrence and distribution of these compounds. The analytical method developed in this study provides essential data that may inform future monitoring programs for diazepam metabolites in food safety.

Notably, toxicity predictions using ProTox3.0 and ADMETlab3.0 suggested that BP-246 and BP-271 may pose multi-organ toxicity risks, characterized by potential neurotoxicity and high blood-brain barrier penetration, as well as ecotoxicity and food chain bioaccumulation concerns. However, these in silico predictions are preliminary and require further experimental validation. BP-246 and BP-271 can be ingested via drinking water or food sources (e.g., fish). Given the preliminary nature of these predictions, definitive conclusions on human health risks cannot be drawn at this stage. Nevertheless, under chronic low-dose exposure, cumulative toxicity, risks to vulnerable populations, and potential synergistic interactions with other unknown diazepam metabolites or environmental pollutants may collectively lead to heightened health hazards.

The integrated analytical approach combines the high-resolution capabilities of LC-Orbitrap for non-targeted metabolite discovery and UPLC-MS/MS for sensitive quantification, ensuring both structural accuracy and analytical sensitivity. The method exhibited high precision, accuracy, and sensitivity for diazepam and its metabolites, supported by a rapid and efficient pretreatment process, making it suitable for high-throughput analysis of fish products.
