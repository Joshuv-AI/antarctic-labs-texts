Human serum albumin and biological fluids reduce cefiderocol activity in carbapenem-resistant gram-negative bacteria
Authors: Azadeh Varnado, Samyar Moheb, Shayra D. Sanchez, Cecilia Rodriguez, Mase Hamza, Jenny Escalante, Marisel R. Tuttobene, German M. Traglia, Fernando Pasteran, Marcelo E. Tolmasky, Robert A. Bonomo, Luis A. Actis, Gauri Rao, Maria S. Ramirez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human serum albumin (HSA), the predominant plasma protein, influences bacterial physiology and antibiotic susceptibility. We previously showed that HSA exposure alters Acinetobacter baumannii transcriptional profiles, reducing biofilm formation and modulating the expression of β-lactam resistance genes, while decreasing cefiderocol (FDC) activity through downregulation of TonB-dependent receptors (TBDRs). Since FDC uptake relies on siderophore-mediated transport, we hypothesised that albumin’s iron content could affect bacterial susceptibility to FDC. However, its effect on FDC activity across carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative pathogens remains underexplored. Here, we evaluated FDC bactericidal efficacy in the presence of physiological HSA concentrations and various human biological fluids against five different species of carbapenem-resistant strains. Time-kill assays revealed that HSA markedly attenuates FDC activity, permitting bacterial regrowth in species including A. baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, and Achromobacter xylosoxidans. Human fluids with high protein content similarly reduced FDC efficacy. Transcriptional analyses indicated downregulation of iron uptake genes, suggesting that albumin-mediated iron availability modulates siderophore-dependent drug uptake. These findings highlight the significant role of host proteins and fluid environments in modulating FDC activity, underscoring the need to consider such factors when interpreting susceptibility information and optimising treatment strategies for infections caused by carbapenem-resistant pathogens.

Introduction

The emergence of multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, particularly carbapenem-resistant strains, represents a major clinical challenge worldwide [1–3]. Cefiderocol (FDC) is a novel siderophore-cephalosporin conjugate that exploits bacterial iron uptake pathways to overcome conventional β-lactam resistance [4,5]. This “Trojan horse” mechanism has demonstrated potent in vitro and in vivo activity against highly resistant Gram-negative pathogens, making FDC a promising option for difficult-to-treat infections [6].

Recent evidence suggests that host factors may modulate FDC efficacy, but these influences are often overlooked in standard sus ceptibility testing. Human serum albumin (HSA), the most abundant plasma protein, not only maintains osmotic regulation, metal ion transport, and drug binding, but also influences bacterial physiology and antibiotic susceptibility [7]. In Acinetobacter baumannii, exposure to HSA has been linked with reduced expression of the global repressor H–NS, altered motility, biofilm formation, natural competition, and changes in the expression of antibiotic resistance genes [7–10]. Furthermore, HSA or human serum has been reported to downregulate iron uptake genes while upregulating β-lactamases, correlating with an increase in minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of FDC [11].

Although most studies have focused on A. baumannii, the impact of HSA on FDC activity in other carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative pathogens remains poorly understood. Since FDC uptake relies primarily on the siderophore-mediated TonB-dependent receptor (TBDR), HSA-mediated modulation of bacterial iron acquisition could hinder drug entry and reduce antibacterial activity [12,13].

In this study, we investigated whether physiologically relevant concentrations of HSA modulate the bactericidal activity of FDC against diverse carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative pathogens. We also evaluated FDC activity in the model CRAB strain M27835 using six clinically relevant human fluids and examined the expression of key iron-uptake genes by RT-qPCR to elucidate albumin-mediated effects on FDC activity.

Methods

Bacterial strains, culture conditions, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The bacterial strains used in this study are listed in Table 1. The panel consisted of 10 carbapenem-resistant strains, including model (US CDC AR-Isolate Bank) and clinical isolates of A. baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, and Achromobacter xylosoxidans.

Strains were stored at −80 °C in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth supplemented with 20% glycerol. For experiments, frozen stocks were streaked onto cystine-lactose-electrolyte-deficient (CLED) agar (Beckton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ) and incubated at 37 °C for 16–18 h. Fresh colonies were used for each assay.

The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of cefiderocol (FDC; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) were determined by broth microdilution using cation-adjusted iron-depleted Mueller-Hinton broth (ID-CAMHB; Thermo Fisher Scientific), following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [14].

Time-kill assays

Time-kill assays were performed for all strains listed in Table 1 to assess the bactericidal activity of FDC in the presence or absence of human serum albumin (HSA, 3.5%, physiological concentration), which has been previously reported to contain approximately 72 μM total iron at this concentration [12,15]. Exponential-phase cultures (~106 CFU/mL) were incubated at 37 °C with shaking in the corresponding test media containing FDC at 4x MIC (two double dilutions of the MIC value) for each strain. For A. baumannii M27835, an additional condition was evaluated in which exogenous iron (FeCl3, 25 μM or 50 μM) was added to ID-CAMHB supplemented with 3.5% HSA and FDC at 4x MIC. Samples were collected at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 24 h, serially diluted, and 50 μL of the appropriate bacterial dilution was spirally plated on Mueller-Hinton agar using an automated spiral plater (Don Whitley WASP Touch, Microbiology International, Frederick, MD) and incubated at 37 °C. To determine viable counts, expressed as log10 CFU/mL, bacteria were quantified using a ProtoCOL automated colony counter (Symbiosis, Cambridge, UK). Assays were repeated at least two times using independent biological samples each time. The log fold change was calculated as the difference between the log1 n CFU/mL obtained with FDC plus HSA and that observed with FDC alone. The limit of detection was 1.30 log1 n CFU/mL (20 CFU/mL).

Time-kill assays in the presence of human biological fluids

The effect of human biological fluids with different protein contents on FDC activity was evaluated using the model strain A. baumannii M27835. Time-kill assays were performed as described above, with FDC at 4x and 8x the MIC, alone or in combination with 100% human pleural fluid (HPF), human serum (HS), human alveolar fluid (HAF), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), human urine (HU), and nasal fluid (NSF). Human fluids from healthy individuals were obtained from a certified vendor (Innovative Research, Novi, MI, certified vendor approved by ISO, FDA, USDA, and EPA). In addition, the model strain M27872 was used to perform time-kill assays (4x FDC MIC) comparing high-cellular, high-HSA fluid (HPF) with low-cellular, low-HSA fluid (CSF) conditions.

Bacterial suspensions (~106 CFU/mL) were incubated at 37 °C with shaking, and samples were collected at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 24 h, serially diluted, and plated on Mueller-Hinton agar to determine viable counts, expressed as log1 n CFU/mL. Assays were repeated at least two times using independent biological samples each time. The log fold change was calculated as the difference between the log1 n CFU/mL obtained with FDC plus fluid and that observed with FDC alone. The detection limit was 1.30 log10 CFU/mL (20 CFU/mL).

RNA-extraction and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from A. baumannii M27835 cultures exposed for 24 h to 4 μg/mL (1x MIC) of FDC in ID-CAMHB, in the presence or absence of 3.5% HSA. RNA extraction was performed from three independent biological replicates using the Directzol RNA Kit (ZymoResearch), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNase-treated RNA was used for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis with the iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). cDNA was adjusted to 50 ng/μL and used as template for qRT-PCR with qPCRBIO SyGreen Blue Mix Lo-ROX (PCR Biosystems, Wayne, PA, USA) on a CFX96 Touch™ Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Expression levels were normalised to the rpoB gene (reference control) using the Ct method [16]. qRT-PCR was carried out to evaluate the expression levels of 15 target genes (Supplementary Table 1). Data represent mean values from three independent biological replicates; each measured in technical triplicate. Statistically significant differences were determined by two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test (P < 0.05) using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software).

Results

HS albumin reduces the bactericidal activity of FDC in carbapenem-resistant strains

Time-kill assays performed with 4x MIC FDC concentrations in the presence of 3.5% HSA revealed a marked reduction in its bactericidal activity in the panel of carbapenem-resistant strains (Fig. 1). In most isolates, the addition of HSA resulted in only a transient reduction in viable counts during the early time points (2 h), followed by regrowth at 24 h, as indicated by positive log10 fold change values (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 2). In addition, the growth of FDC-treated cultures showed negative log1 n fold-change values at 24 h across species when compared with the cognate controls, a response consistent with the sustained antibacterial activity of FDC alone. Reductions ranged from −0.84 to −8.28 log1 n CFU/mL, with the most pronounced decreases observed in E. coli and K. pneumoniae, whereas A. xylosoxidans exhibited the lowest reduction; the remaining species displayed intermediate and comparable responses (Fig. 2).

Both A. baumannii strains (M27835 and M27872) reached bacterial densities close to 109 CFU/mL at 24 h when cultured in ID-CAMHB and ID-CAMHB supplemented with 3.5% HSA (Fig. 1A). The treatment with FDC showed a clear bactericidal effect against both model strains (Fig. 1A). In strain M27835, exposure to 16 μg/mL (4x MIC) of FDC resulted in an initial decrease of approximately 1–2 log1 n CFU/mL during the first few hours, followed by sustained suppression of growth. Similarly, in strain M27872, 8 μg/mL of FDC (4x MIC) reduced the bacterial load by approximately 1–2 log1 n CFU/mL and maintained the population below 106 CFU/mL over time (Fig. 1A). However, when the strains were grown in ID-CAMHB supplemented with 3.5% HSA, both reached bacterial densities close to 108–109 CFU/mL within the first hours of the killing kinetics (Fig. 1A), showing that HSA significantly reduced the activity of FDC. In M27835, the presence of HSA prevented FDC from maintaining its initial bactericidal effect, and a progressive recovery of growth was observed, reaching 108–109 CFU/mL at 24 h (Fig. 1A). This loss of activity was also reflected in the log1 n fold-change values shown in Fig. 2, which increased progressively up to 24 h. Addition of exogenous iron to FDC plus HSA (FDC + HSA + FeCl3) was associated with enhanced bacterial regrowth in M27835 compared with FDC + HSA alone, at both 25 and 50 μM FeCl3. These findings are consistent with further attenuation of FDC activity in the presence of increased iron availability (Supplementary Fig. 1). In M27872, FDC in the presence of HSA also reduced the initial decline observed with FDC alone and allowed a marked increase in bacterial growth, with a log1 n fold-change of 4.48 at 24 h, demonstrating the significant loss of antibiotic efficacy under these conditions (Fig. 1A and Fig. 2).

Both K. pneumoniae strains (KPNMA216 and Kp-1) exhibited sustained growth in the control condition (ID-CAMHB) (108–109 CFU/mL at 24 h), showing similar growth in 3.5% HSA (Fig. 1B). Treatment with FDC showed a marked bactericidal effect in both strains. In KPNMA216, 32 μg/mL of FDC (4x MIC) reduced the bacterial load to approximately 102 CFU/mL over 24 h (Fig. 1B). However, when FDC was combined with HSA, its bactericidal activity was limited during the first hours, and the strain recovering growth to 108–109 CFU/mL at 24 h, with a log1 n fold-change of 7.67 (Figs. 1B and 2). Similarly, in Kp-1, 8 μg/mL FDC (4x MIC) reduced the bacterial population to 102–103 CFU/mL at 24 h. In the presence of HSA, an initial decline in CFU was observed, followed by progressive recovery to 108 CFU/mL at 24 h, with a log1 n fold-change of 6.18, indicating a significant reduction in FDC bactericidal efficacy (Figs. 1B and 2).

When the P. aeruginosa strains PAEMA232 and PAE319 were cultured in the presence of 3.5% HSA, values like those seen in the control condition were reached (Fig. 1C). In PAEMA232, 2 μg/mL of FDC (4x MIC) reduced growth during the first hours, remaining around 104 CFU/mL until 24 h. When FDC was combined with HSA, slow growth was observed in the first hours, followed by a more rapid increase, reaching 108 CFU/mL at 24 h, with a log1 n fold-change of 4.47 (Figs. 1C and 2). In PAE319, FDC at 2 μg/mL (4x MIC) produced an initial decrease in the population from 107 to 105 CFU/mL at 2 h, remaining at those values until 24 h. In the presence of HSA, growth was sustained from the beginning, reaching 109 CFU/mL at 24 h, with a log1 n fold-change of 4.85, indicating a significant reduction in the FDC bactericidal activity under these conditions (Figs. 1C and 2).

For both E. coli strains Ec7499 and Ec1705, densities close to 109 CFU/mL at 24 h were obtained for ID-CAMHB and ID-CAMHB supplemented with 3.5% HSA (Fig. 1D). In Ec7499, 2 μg/mL of FDC (4x MIC) produced a marked bactericidal effect, reducing CFU to below the detection limit at 24 h. When FDC was combined with HSA, a slight increase in CFU was observed at 2 h, followed by a decrease of approximately 1–2 log10 CFU/mL until 6 h, and then sustained growth that reached over 109 CFU/mL at 24 h, with a log1 n fold-change of 8.63 (Figs. 1D and 2). In Ec1705, treatment with 2 μg/mL of FDC (4x MIC) resulted in an initial decrease of approximately 3 log10 CFU/mL during the first 6 h, followed by limited growth up to approximately 105 CFU/mL at 24 h. In the presence of HSA, the population also decreased by 3 log10 CFU/mL during the first 6 h, but then grew steadily, reaching nearly 109 CFU/mL at 24 h, with a log1 n fold-change of 5.57 (Fig.1D and Fig. 2).

Lastly, when A. xylosoxidans strains Ax67 and Ax130 were tested, as observed previously, the growth control conditions, as well as supplementation with 3.5% HSA, achieved densities of approximately 109 CFU/mL at 24 h (Fig. 1E). However, in contrast to the previously analysed species, FDC showed limited bactericidal activity in both strains. In Ax67, 64 μg/mL of FDC (4x MIC) caused an initial increase of approximately 1 log1 n CFU/mL during the first 2 h, remaining largely unchanged until 6 h and then increasing by an additional 1 log1 n to reach 107–08 CFU/mL at 24 h. In the presence of HSA, the population increased by approximately 2 log1 n CFU/mL during the first 6 h and remained close to that level until 24 h, with a log1 n fold-change of 5.38 (Figs. 1E and 2). In Ax130, 4 μg/mL of FDC (4x MIC) caused only a slight initial decrease of 1 log1 n CFU/mL during the first 4 h, followed by growth up to 106 CFU/mL at 24 h. In the presence of HSA, a similar pattern was observed, with an initial 1 log1 n CFU/mL decrease during the first 4 h and subsequent growth reaching nearly 109 CFU/mL at 24 h, with a log1 n fold-change of 2.82 (Figs. 1E and 2).

Taken together, these results indicate that physiological levels of HSA significantly reduce the bactericidal efficacy of FDC against multiple carbapenem-resistant pathogens.

Human biological fluids differentially reduce the bactericidal activity of FDC

To broaden the analysis, we evaluated FDC bactericidal activity in six human fluids that differ in albumin content (high vs low HSA) using the A. baumannii model strain M27835. Time-kill assays showed loss of FDC bactericidal activity when the strain was grown in the presence of all tested biological fluids (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 3).

As a reference condition, FDC alone at 16 μg/mL (4x MIC) and 32 μg/mL (8x MIC) produced the expected bactericidal effect in ID-CAMHB, characterised by an initial ~1–2 log1 n reduction in viable counts during the first 6 h, followed by regrowth of approximately 1 log1 n by 24 h (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2). This profile served as the baseline against which all fluid-associated effects were compared.

HPF and HS, both containing high HSA levels, produced the most pronounced reduction in FDC activity. In these fluids, the M27835 strain displayed a growth similar to the ID-CAMHB control, reaching approximately 109 CFU/ml at 24 h. When FDC was tested in 100% HPF and 100% HS, both 4x and 8x MIC exhibited sustained bacterial growth throughout the 24-h period, with log1 n fold change of 2.40 and 4.07 in HPF and 2.00 and 3.69 in HS, respectively (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Compared with purified 3.5% HSA, whole HPF and HS induced greater loss of FDC activity, suggesting that other fluid components contribute to this effect.

Ascitic fluid (HAF), which contains moderate-to-high levels of HSA, exhibited a distinct baseline growth profile yet ultimately caused comparable impairment of FDC activity. In 100% HAF alone, M27835 showed limited growth, increasing slightly (>1 log1 n) during the first 6 h and stabilising around 107 CFU/mL at 24 h (substantially lower than the 109 CFU/mL reached in HPF and HS). This reduced baseline growth suggests that additional components of ascitic fluid may limit bacterial growth. When FDC was combined with HAF, its activity was markedly attenuated: at 16 μg/mL (4x MIC), cultures increased from ~106 to 107 CFU/mL and remained stable (log1 n fold change = 0.223), while at 8x MIC, growth became exponential after 6 h, reaching ~108 CFU/mL at 24 h (log1 n fold change = 3 (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

In 100% CSF, which contains low levels of HSA, strain M27835 reached slightly above 107 CFU/mL at 24 h. In the presence of CSF, FDC efficacy decreased substantially: both concentrations allowed sustained bacterial growth, reaching nearly 108 CFU/mL at 24 h, with log1 n fold changes of 1.45 (4x MIC) and 2.89 (8x MIC), indicating that CSF also diminishes FDC bactericidal activity (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Human urine (HU), characterised by very low protein content, had the least impact on FDC, consistent with previous observations [15]. In 100% HU alone, M27835 grew similarly to the ID-CAMHB control, reaching ~109 CFU/mL at 24 h. When FDC was combined with HU, 4x MIC allowed exponential growth during the first 6 h (from 106 to 108 CFU/mL), stabilising thereafter and yielding a log1 n fold change of 1.62 at 24 h. At 8x MIC, an initial 1-log1 n reduction was followed by regrowth to >109 CFU/mL (log1 n fold change = 2) (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2). To further evaluate the contribution of HSA to bacterial regrowth in urine, HU was supplemented with 3.5% HSA in the presence of FDC at 16 μg/mL (4x MIC) (Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Fig. 3A and B). Supplemented urine produced a stronger initial bactericidal effect (approximately a 1-log1 n reduction at 6 h) but ultimately supported regrowth to ~a109 CFU/mL, like HU alone. This yielded a log1 n fold change of 3.29 relative to FDC in isolation, indicating that urine containing HSA markedly impairs FDC efficacy. The comparison with urine containing only FDC (log1 n fold change = 1.66) demonstrated that HSA interferes with FDC activity even in the urinary environment. Relative to FDC with HSA in the absence of urine, the supplemented urinary environment produced a log1 n fold change of 1.086, highlighting the specific contribution of urine constituents to bacterial recovery.

Finally, in 100% NSF, strain M27835 displayed enhanced growth compared with the ID-CAMHB control, reaching bacterial densities exceeding 109 CFU/mL. NSF also reduced FDC efficacy: at 16 μg/mL (4x MIC), only a mild early antimicrobial effect occurred, followed by robust regrowth (log1 n fold change = 2.71). At 8x MIC, a stronger initial reduction (~1 log1 n) was observed, but regrowth still occurred, reaching ~108 CFU/mL at 24 h (log1 n fold change = 3.29) (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

To further test this observation, we challenged the CRAB model strain M27872 with 8 μg/mL FDC (4x MIC) in the presence of two representative fluids: one with high HSA and cellular content (HPF) and one with low content of these host products (CSF). In ID-CAMHB without drug, M27872 increased from ~106 to ~109 CFU/mL by 24 h, whereas 8 μg/mL FDC (4x MIC) alone produced sustained bactericidal activity, reducing counts by ≈2 log1 n and maintaining them around 104–105 CFU/mL at 24 h. In the presence of HPF, FDC killing activity was almost completely abolished, and cultures regrew to ~109 CFU/mL, overlapping with the control. CSF also attenuated FDC efficacy, allowing regrowth to ~108 CFU/mL at 24 h (Supplementary Fig. 4A and Supplementary Table 5). Bacterial survival increased progressively over time in both fluids, with larger log1 n fold increases in HPF (4.30) than in CSF (3.00), suggesting that host fluids, particularly those rich in HSA and cells, markedly diminish FDC bactericidal activity against M27872 (Supplementary Fig. 4B and Supplementary Table 4).

Taken together, these results indicate that fluids with higher HSA content significantly reduce FDC bactericidal activity, whereas fluids with low HSA content exert a milder effect.

FDC-induced transcriptional response in A. baumannii is differentially modified by HSA

To investigate the transcriptional response of A. baumannii M27835 to FDC, we quantified the expression of 15 genes related to iron acquisition, surface structures, regulatory pathways, and β-lactamases under exposure to 1x MIC FDC (4 μg/mL), either alone or with the addition of 3.5% HSA (Fig. 4A–D). Exposure to FDC alone triggered a strong upregulation of most siderophore-associated genes. The transcription of basD, basE, piuA, cirA, pirA, exbD, and ppiA was significantly induced (P < 0.05–P < 0.0 0 01) (Fig. 4A). The effect of adding HSA was gene-dependent: for basD, basE, piuA, and pirA, transcript levels returned to values similar to the untreated control, counteracting the induction observed with FDC alone. In contrast, cirA and exbD remained upregulated despite the presence of HSA, higher than the control but significantly lower than under FDC alone, while ppiA showed an additional increase under FDC with HSA compared with FDC alone (Fig. 4A).

Genes from the BaeSR regulatory system exhibited distinct transcriptional patterns under the tested conditions. baeS showed no significant differences between the control, FDC, or FDC in the presence of HSA, although expression was higher when HSA was added compared with the untreated condition. In contrast, baeR differed across all comparisons, with a progressive increase in expression from the control to FDC, and from FDC to FDC with HSA (Fig. 4B).

All surface-associated genes were upregulated upon exposure to FDC (Fig. 4C). csuA/B, csuE, ompA, and oprD were significantly upregulated (P < 0.05–P < 0.0001). When HSA was added, csuA/B, csuE, and oprD exhibited even stronger expression (P < 0.0001), while ompA remained significantly induced but with a more moderate fold change (Fig. 4C).

Finally, β-lactamase genes showed distinct transcriptional responses to the tested conditions (Fig. 4D). blaNDM-1 exhibited a non-significant decrease under FDC alone but was significantly upregulated when FDC was combined with HSA, relative to both control and FDC alone. blaADC was significantly induced by FDC, and its expression increased even further upon addition of HSA, with all pairwise comparisons showing significant differences.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that HSA and physiologically relevant biological fluids reduce the bactericidal activity of FDC across a diverse panel of carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative pathogens [11,12,17]. These findings expand upon previous observations in A. baumannii and place them in a broader and clinically relevant context.

In prior work from our group, we showed that host proteins and serum components alter the physiology of A. baumannii and its responses to antibiotics. Specifically, HSA was shown to modulate global regulators (including H–NS), affect motility and biofilm formation, and influence competence and the expression of resistance genes [8,9,18–21]. These phenotypic changes were linked to modifications in antibiotic susceptibility, including the modulation of β-lactam resistance determinants [22,23]. The present study further supports and extends previous observations by demonstrating that HSA not only modulates transcriptional programs in A. baumannii but also compromises the bactericidal activity of FDC across multiple species [11,12]. We show that physiologically relevant concentrations of HSA markedly attenuate FDC activity in all species tested, including K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, E. coli, and A. xylosoxidans. In most isolates, FDC produced an early reduction in bacterial burden when tested in standard ID-CAMHB, but this effect was greatly diminished or completely abolished in the presence of HSA, resulting in robust growth at 24 h. This consistent loss of bactericidal activity suggests that HSA-mediated interference with FDC uptake is a broad phenomenon rather than species- or isolate-specific.

In addition, transcriptional analyses revealed that several siderophore-associated genes were strongly induced by FDC in the absence of HSA, but this response was attenuated when HSA was present. This pattern is consistent with our previous studies showing that HSA modulates TonB-dependent receptor and iron-uptake pathways and reduces FDC activity in A. baumannii, including the contribution of albumin-associated iron and the emergence of FDC-adaptive subpopulations in host-like fluids [11–13,17]. Importantly, the present findings with the CDC AR-Isolate Bank model strain provide an independent and standardised confirmation of these HSA-driven transcriptional effects, strengthening our earlier observations. As FDC relies on siderophore-mediated active transport, reduced expression of these iron uptake systems likely limits FDC internalisation and, consequently, its bactericidal effect. The HSA used in this study, which was not iron-depleted, has been previously shown to contain physiologically relevant iron levels, supporting the proposed link between albumin-associated iron and repression of iron uptake pathways [12,15]. The present findings are consistent with this evidence and support the idea that HSA, together with the iron it carries, acts as a signal of an iron-replete environment, suppressing siderophore uptake pathways and thereby restricting the “Trojan Horse” entry route that FDC uses to access the bacterial cell. However, while the 24-h time point was selected to align with the conditions under which bactericidal activity was performed, we acknowledge that earlier intermediate time points could provide additional insight into the initial transcriptional dynamics underlying HSA-mediated modulation of FDC activity, and this remains an important direction for future investigation.

FDC exhibits moderate plasma protein binding of approximately 40%–60%, primarily to HSA [24]. Accordingly, the HSA content of a given fluid could directly reduce the free FDC fraction, shifting the exposure-response relationship and contributing to diminished bactericidal activity. However, in our in vitro system, the FDC concentrations tested were deliberately chosen to largely compensate for and exceed the estimated HSA-bound fraction, making it unlikely that protein binding alone accounts for the observed loss of activity.

Importantly, beyond purified HSA, our study shows that human biological fluids also reduce FDC activity, though to varying degrees depending on their biochemical composition. Fluids with high HSA content (pleural fluid and serum) produced the greatest loss of bactericidal activity, suggesting that additional components, such as lipoproteins, complement proteins, or cellular debris, may interact with FDC or alter bacterial physiology. Ascitic fluid also attenuated FDC activity, though its intrinsic growth-limiting properties produced a distinct basal profile. Le et al. [18,23] previously reported that human serum proteins alter MIC values and transcriptional responses, implicating iron transport modulation as a key factor. Our findings align with these studies and underscore the possibility that clinically relevant matrices modulate antimicrobial activity through multifactorial mechanisms. In contrast, low-protein fluids such as cerebrospinal fluid and urine reduced FDC activity to a lesser extent, indicating that although albumin concentration contributes substantially, it is not the primary determinant of reduced FDC efficacy. The observation that urine produced the smallest reduction in FDC activity aligns with clinical evidence supporting the favourable outcomes of FDC in urinary tract infections [25,26]. Urine supplementation experiments further illustrate the context-dependent contribution of albumin to FDC attenuation. While urine alone, naturally low in protein and iron, only modestly interfered with FDC activity, the addition of physiological levels of HSA markedly reduced its efficacy. This suggests that specific urinary constituents interact with albumin to create a chemical environment that further restricts the expression of siderophore-mediated uptake systems. These findings reinforce the idea that albumin is a key driver of diminished FDC activity, although its impact varies depending on the biochemical properties of each fluid. Similarly, nasal fluid, despite its relatively low protein concentration, substantially reduced FDC activity while supporting enhanced bacterial growth, suggesting that non-albumin components may interfere with FDC either by altering bacterial physiology or by directly sequestering or competing with it.

Moreover, the pronounced loss of efficacy in pleural fluid and serum may help explain the poor clinical outcomes observed in non-urinary infections, including ventilator-associated pneumonia [27–29]. In these microenvironments, characterised by elevated HSA concentrations, siderophore-mediated transport of FDC may be impaired, potentially contributing to the variable therapeutic responses reported in severe respiratory and bloodstream infections.

An additional observation of clinical relevance is the diminished activity of FDC against A. xylosoxidans, a species frequently associated with cystic fibrosis and known for its high metabolic adaptability [30]. Although baseline susceptibility to FDC was limited in the isolates tested, the presence of HSA further reduced its activity. This aligns with evidence showing that baseline resistance to FDC in A. xylosoxidans is higher than previously reported, with multiple isolates exhibiting non-susceptibility even before drug exposure [31]. Given that the airways of individuals with cystic fibrosis are enriched in HSA, extracellular DNA, and inflammation-derived proteins, these combined findings raise concerns regarding the pharmacodynamic reliability of FDC in this setting. Taken together with the clinical variability previously described, these results further underscore the need for a more individualised and microenvironment-specific evaluation of FDC activity under conditions characteristic of cystic fibrosis.

Despite these considerations, extrapolating in vitro fluid-based models to in vivo infections has limitations. Although commercially sourced human fluids replicate several biochemical features, they do not fully capture the complexity of infected tissues or the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic dynamics of the drug. Nevertheless, the agreement between our findings and previous work supports the biological relevance of HSA-mediated modulation of iron transport and its impact on FDC activity [11–13,17].

Taken together, the information presented in this report indicates that host proteins and fluid composition substantially influence the activity of FDC against different Gram-negative bacterial pathogens. Standard in vitro susceptibility testing does not account for these interactions and may therefore overestimate the efficacy of FDC in protein-rich environments. Incorporating physiologically informed conditions, such as albumin supplementation or alternative growth matrices, could improve the predictive value of susceptibility testing and better anticipate clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that HSA and diverse human biological fluids significantly reduce the bactericidal activity of FDC across multiple carbapenem-resistant species. This attenuation appears to be mediated, at least in part, by downregulation of iron uptake pathways essential for FDC internalisation. In addition, we observed that FDC also modulates genes associated with surface structures, including pili/biofilm-associated determinants and outer membrane functions, and that HSA can differentially shape these responses. Collectively, these findings highlight the need to consider the biochemical characteristics of the infection microenvironment when evaluating antibiotic activity and suggest that optimising FDC therapy may require site-specific, physiologically informed approaches.
