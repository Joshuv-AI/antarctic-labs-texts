Pneumothorax following rigid bronchoscopy for foreign body removal in a child: A case report

Introduction

Rigid tube bronchoscopy was developed by an ENT specialist, Gustav Killian, the late 19th century to remove foreign bodies and has since become standard practice, particularly in children [1,2]. Bronchoscopy is not exempt from complications that can be minor, such as bleeding, or more severe, such as a fatal tension pneumothorax. However, this complication is rare [3]. We report a case of Pneumothorax following rigid bronchoscopy for foreign body removal in a child. This case report was carried out according to recent SCARE criteria [4].

Case presentation

A 20-month-old child was admitted to our department after inhaling a foreign body (peanut). The initial symptomatology was a typical foreign body aspiration regressing into intermittent coughing fits; the admission examination showed a respiratory-stable child with respiration at 24c/min and saturation at 98 %; on pulmonary auscultation, there was a decrease in vesicular murmurs on the right side. The decision was to perform a standing chest X-ray before taking her to the operating room, which showed a thoracic distension on the right side (Fig. 1). The approach was to proceed with bronchoscopy after respiratory preparation. General anesthesia was induced intravenously by sevoflurane with 100 % oxygen, propofol, sufentanil and atracurium. Then it was maintained with IV propofol. After removal of the foreign body blocking the right bronchus during bronchoscopy, the child presented with a pre-arrest state (SpO2 begins to fall up to 50 % with cyanosis and the heart pulse has dropped to 50 beats per minute). Manual ventilation was attempted with a marked airway resistance and a slight improvement in SpO2 up to 70 %. External cardiac compression was performed and 10 μg epinephrine was injected intravenously three times. A right auscultatory silence was noted. Pneumothorax was suspected and blindly aspirated; a 19 Gauge IV needle was inserted in the second right intercostal space along the mid clavicular line. SpO2 reached 100 % in a few seconds and the pulse increased to 130 beats per minute. An intercostal drain was placed in the right chest at the fifth intercostal space along the anterior axillary line. We decided to order a chest CT scan rather than a chest X-ray because it is more specific in detecting pneumothorax, even if small or anterior. The thoracic CT scan confirmed an anterior right pneumothorax with a posterior opacity suggestive of atelectasis (Fig. 2). The child was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for close monitoring under mechanical assist-control ventilation mode. He remained stable in the ICU and awoke without any complications on the first postoperative day. After a 3-day stay, follow-up Chest X-ray revealed re-expansion of the right lung. Therefore, the chest tube was removed, with good clinical and radiological progress.

Discussion

Inhalation of foreign bodies is a common condition that can be life-threatening for children, accounting for 7 % of deaths in those under 3 years old [[5], [6], [7]]. This condition is more frequent during the first 3 years of life, attributable to several factors: a high laryngeal position, a dysfunctional epiglottis, underdeveloped chewing and swallowing reflexes, and uncoordinated swallowing and breathing. Additionally, children's restlessness during mealtimes, with frequent crying and smiling, lack of coordination between hands and mouth, and incomplete development of their teeth further increase the likelihood of foreign body aspiration [5,6]. It is more common among boys, likely due to their higher activity levels and increased tendency to place objects in their mouths [7,8].

Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on identifying foreign body aspiration syndrome, which is present in 95 % of cases, with food being the most common foreign body [7]. Foreign bodies are more frequently found in the right bronchus because it is more vertical than the left one [8]. Rigid bronchoscopy is the technique of choice for extracting bronchial foreign bodies in pediatric population. Although straightforward, the procedure is particularly challenging when performed on children and require no improvisation; the ENT and anesthetic team must include experienced practitioners well trained in this procedure [9]. The primary goals of the anesthesiologist are to ensure adequate oxygenation and ventilation at all times, to prevent laryngospasm and bronchospasm, and to prevent cardiac rhythm disturbances and changes in blood pressure [9,10]. Overall, rigid bronchoscopy is associated with a low complication rate, but this rate is notably higher in children than in adults. According to the study by Hasdiraz L and al, the complication rate in children is around 14 % [11]. Serious complication are organ damage due to low oxygen and high carbon dioxide levels, spasm, cardiac arrest [5] and barotrauma (pneumothorax and pneumomediastinum) [9,10]. Barotrauma is rare, with a prevalence of approximately less than 1 % [1].

During bronchoscopy, early detection of a pneumothorax can be challenging as it may be masked by anesthesia. However, the presence of ventilation worsening or any deterioration in gas exchange during the procedure should alert the team to the possibility of a pneumothorax, necessitating preliminary auscultation to check for asymmetry or auscultatory silence [3,12]. The exact pathogenesis of pneumothorax occurring during bronchoscopy remains unclear and is based on controversial hypotheses discussed in the literature. It may be triggered by a pleural breach during the extraction of a foreign body, or by barotrauma, an air leak caused by alveolar rupture during overpressure induced by mechanical ventilation, which could be the main cause in our case, as we did not have any laceration [13].

Prolonged use of the bronchoscope can elevate a minor pneumothorax to a life-threatening bilateral pneumothorax in a child. When airway obstruction occurs due to the instrument for an extended period, air can enter the pleural cavity during positive pressure ventilation but cannot be expelled during exhalation. This ongoing pressure can compress the ipsilateral lung and lead to a mediastinal shift, severely endangering the child's condition. The first measure to be taken in the event of a suffocating pneumothorax is needle decompression, followed by intercostal drainage, and the subsequent transfer to an ICU [14].

Further research is needed to explore advanced diagnostic tools and innovations in bronchoscopy equipment. Among the recent techniques utilized to reduce bronchoscopy complications is cryotherapy, which has been successfully employed to facilitate the removal of foreign bodies via flexible and rigid bronchoscopy in children younger than 40 months of age, offering a promising adjunctive approach [15,16].

Although rigid bronchoscopy is the gold standard, flexible bronchoscopy has a wider visual field and is better suited to cases of friable or distal foreign bodies and for residual foreign bodies after rigid bronchoscopy [17]. And rigid bronchoscopy gives better results in cases of asphyxiating foreign bodies, and therefore is a safe and effective method to handle such potentially life-threatening emergencies [18].

For children undergoing rigid bronchoscopy, a proactive approach to accident prevention is essential, avoiding overpressure during mechanical ventilation and bronchial lacerations, just as long-term follow-up is important to detect delayed complications and ensure full recovery. Educating parents and caregivers about the possible risks and taking safety precautions during mealtimes are key strategies to significantly reduce the number of accidents [16].

Conclusion

Although pneumothorax secondary to bronchoscopy is mainly benign, some cases can be fatal. Due to the rarity of this clinical entity, appropriate assessment and management have been little studied to date. Effective communication between ENT and anesthesiologist is essential for early diagnosis and rapid management of such rare complication.
