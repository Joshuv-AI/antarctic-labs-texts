Integrating multi-omics and mendelian randomization identifies therapeutic targets for Lichen Sclerosus: A druggable genome-wide study
Authors: Shuai Wang, Pengfei Wang, Haiping Li, Xinyi Guan, Shuling Ji, Xian Zhang, Yuanhong Chen, Nan Li, Hu Li, Lujiadai Xue, Chunmei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLichen sclerosus (LS) is a chronic inflammatory skin disease characterized by limited treatment options and an unclear pathogenesis. This study aimed to identify potential therapeutic targets for LS by integrating druggable genome (DG) data with multi-omics and Mendelian randomization (MR).MethodsBlood expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) were obtained from the eQTLGen consortium, and protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) were sourced from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP) and the Decode cohorts. Outcome data for LS were derived from the FinnGen R12 database. We performed two-sample MR analyses to estimate the causal effects of druggable genes on LS, followed by sensitivity analyses, Steiger directionality tests, and multi-omics integration. For multi-omics integration, we cross-referenced eQTL- and pQTL-derived causal genes to identify overlapping genes supported by both transcriptomic and proteomic evidence. Functional concordance was further assessed by comparing effect directions and magnitudes between the two molecular layers. Potential drugs were predicted using the DGIdb database, and molecular docking was performed using CB-Dock2.ResultsAfter rigorous screening and Holm-Bonferroni correction, we identified 50 druggable genes (eQTL-derived) and 35 druggable proteins (pQTL-derived) with causal effects on LS. Integrative multi-omics analysis revealed integrin subunit beta 2 (ITGB2) and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) as the most robust therapeutic targets. Phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) analysis indicated no significant pleiotropic effects for these genes. Protein-protein interaction and pathway enrichment analyses showed that these genes are involved in integrin-mediated cell adhesion and ADP-ribosylation processes. The DGIdb database predicted 24 potential drugs targeting ITGB2 and PARP1, and molecular docking confirmed strong binding affinities (Vina score < −5).ConclusionThis integrative MR study identifies ITGB2 and PARP1 as promising therapeutic targets for LS, providing a foundation for future drug development and personalized treatment strategies.

Introduction

Lichen sclerosus (LS) is a chronic, progressive skin condition that primarily affecting the vulvar and genital regions, and imposes a substantial burden in the general population [1], [2]. It is characterized by skin atrophy, white patches, pruritus, and pain, which may lead to functional impairment and complications [3]. Although its exact etiology remains unclear, research indicates involvement of immune responses, genetic susceptibility, and environmental factors [4], [5]. LS is more common in women, particularly postmenopausal women, but it also occurs in children and men [6]. Due to symptomatic overlap with other dermatoses, the diagnosis of LS is often delayed, resulting in prolonged distress [7], [8]. Current treatment mainly involves topical corticosteroids, along with immunomodulators and laser therapy [9], [10]. While these treatments can alleviate symptoms, some patients respond poorly to conventional therapy because of individual variability [1]. Therefore, developing targeted, precise therapeutic strategies is clinically important for improving outcomes and reducing disease burden.

Recent advances in multi-omics have enabled integration of transcriptomics, proteomics, and other data domains, facilitating cross-validation and identification of biomarkers and therapeutic targets [11]. Integrating eQTL and pQTL with genome-wide association study (GWAS) datasets help identify genetic variants associated with LS at both gene expression and protein levels [12], [13], [14]. This multi-omics approach offers greater statistical power and biological credibility than using either resource alone, as it enables cross-validation of causal signals across independent molecular layers and reduces false positives arising from tissue-specific or post-transcriptional confounding. Notably, cis-acting variants are more likely to identify functional variants linked to phenotypes, directly revealing genetic mechanisms of gene regulation [15], [16], [17]. Target identification and validation are critical steps in drug development, and the druggable genome (DG) has greatly advanced screening of drug targets for human diseases [18], [19]. As a subset of the genome encoding potentially druggable proteins, the DG provides valuable guidance for drug design. Notable examples include GPCR-targeted drugs (e.g., dupilumab for atopic dermatitis) and kinase inhibitors (e.g., tofacitinib for rheumatoid arthritis), underscoring the success of DG-guided strategies in inflammatory diseases and supporting their application in LS therapeutic discovery [20], [21]. However, existing observational or cross-sectional studies are limited by confounding factors, restricting their ability to systematically infer causal relationships between the DG and LS. Thus, more effective methods are needed to explore these causal links.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a widely used causal inference method employing genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to avoid reverse causation and reduce confounding [22], [23], [24]. Combining the DG with cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL in an MR framework has identified potential drug targets for numerous diseases [19], [25].

Currently, research integrating multi-omics with the DG to explore drug targets for LS is lacking. This study aims to integrate DG data, transcriptomics, proteomics, and LS GWAS summary data to identify effective drug targets for this disease.

Methods

Identification of druggable genes

Druggable genes in this study were derived from the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb v4.2.0, https://www.dgidb.org/downloads) [26] and a study by Finan C et al. [18]. DGIdb is an online resource that provides drug-gene interaction information from publications, databases, and other web-based sources. We downloaded the interaction data (February 2022 release), which included all druggable genes with documented drug-gene interactions. Additionally, druggable genes reported by Finan C et al. were incorporated into our study, as their work links GWAS-identified loci associated with complex diseases to druggable genes, thereby facilitating drug target identification and validation.

Genome-wide association study data sources and analysis workflow

Blood expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) for 2532 druggable genes were obtained from the eQTLGen Consortium (https://www.eqtlgen.org/phase1.html), comprising 31,684 individuals. Protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) were derived from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP, https://registry.opendata.aws/ukbppp/), which included 54,219 UK Biobank participants, and a GWAS of plasma protein levels measured using 4907 aptamers in 35,559 Icelandic individuals by Egil Ferkingstad et al. [27]. The outcome dataset for LS et atrophicus (L12_LICHENSCLERATROPH) was obtained from the FinnGen R12 database (https://www.finngen.fi/fi), accessed on August 13, 2025.

The data obtained in this study were derived from populations of European ancestry, with summary information presented in Table 1. Assumptions for instrumental variable selection

IVs were defined as single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) located near druggable genes with significant effects on biomarkers (eQTLs and pQTLs). The four core assumptions for drug-target MR were applied as follows:

Relevance assumption: IVs were strongly associated with the exposure, using a significance threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻⁸. F-statistics > 10 were used to exclude weak IVs.

Exclusion restriction assumption: IVs were not directly associated with the outcome but influenced it only through the exposure (i.e., no horizontal pleiotropy). Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the MR-Egger regression intercept (P > 0.05 indicating no pleiotropy) and the MR-PRESSO global test (P > 0.05 indicating no significant pleiotropy).

Independence assumption: IVs were independent of confounders. Since MR utilizes SNPs that follow the principle of random assortment of parental alleles, they are minimally influenced by environmental and lifestyle factors, theoretically ensuring independence from socioeconomic and environmental confounders. Target relevance: IVs were located within ±1 Mb of the target gene (cis-acting region).

Instrumental variable selection

IVs were selected from eQTLs and pQTLs of druggable genes using a significance threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻⁸. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) was pruned with an r² threshold of 0.3 within a 500 kb window, and a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.01 was applied to ensure SNP independence and mitigate LD effects. SNPs associated with confounders [1], [28] or the outcome were excluded based on the GWAS Catalog [29]. SNPs were extracted within ±1000 kb of the cis-acting region of druggable genes. Corresponding IVs were extracted from eQTL and pQTL datasets. For the outcome (LS), corresponding SNPs were extracted from GWAS summary statistics; palindromic SNPs were excluded, as were SNPs directly associated with the outcome (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸). MR-PRESSO was used to identify and remove outlier SNPs.

Mendelian randomization analysis for druggable gene-disease therapeutic targets

A total of five regression models—MR-Egger regression, inverse-variance weighted (IVW), weighted median estimator (WME), weighted mode, and simple mode—were employed in two-sample MR analyses to evaluate the potential causal relationship between exposures (druggable genes) and the outcome (LS). IVW was used as the primary causal estimation method, as it is the most commonly applied approach. The Wald ratio was used for single SNP effects, while IVW was applied for multiple SNPs. IVW estimates causal effects by weighting each SNP’s effect estimate by the inverse of its variance (R²). MR-Egger, which relaxes the assumptions of IVW, was used to detect and correct for pleiotropy by incorporating an intercept term; results from MR-Egger were referenced when horizontal pleiotropy was present. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Holm-Bonferroni method [30]. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test (P < 0.05 indicating significant heterogeneity). I² was calculated to quantify the proportion of total variation attributable to heterogeneity, with I² > 50% indicating moderate heterogeneity. Pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-Egger intercept term (P > 0.05 indicating no pleiotropy). Leave-one-out analysis was conducted to assess the influence of individual SNPs on the overall causal estimate.

Causal direction validation using Steiger filtering

Steiger filtering was employed to verify the consistency of causal direction between genotype, intermediate traits, and the outcome. This method estimates the variance explained by IVs for both the exposure (druggable genes) and the outcome (LS), testing whether the variance explained for the outcome is less than that for the exposure. A result of "TRUE" in the Steiger test indicates that the causal direction aligns with the expected hypothesis, whereas "FALSE" suggests the opposite direction.

Phenome-wide association study analysis of druggable genes

Phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) is a reverse genetic approach used to investigate which phenotypes are associated with a given genetic variant. This method enables simultaneous exploration of statistical associations between an exposure and multiple disease phenotypes, facilitating the identification of pleiotropic causal relationships and shared pathogenic mechanisms. To further investigate whether druggable genes influence other traits, we performed gene-level PheWAS using 17,361 binary phenotypes and 1419 quantitative phenotypes from the AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal (https://azphewas.com) [31], [32], [33]. PheWAS results were interpreted as associations between genetically determined protein expression and specific diseases or traits.

Protein-protein interaction network analysis of druggable genes

Druggable genes were imported into the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) to construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, with a confidence score threshold of ≥ 0.7 [34], [35]. The resulting network was exported and visualized using Cytoscape.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

To explore the biological functions and signaling pathways involving druggable genes, GO enrichment analysis was performed using the "clusterProfiler" R package, with a significance threshold of p.adjust < 0.05 [36]. GO analysis encompassed biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) categories, with the top five most significantly enriched terms in each category visualized using circle plots. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis [37] was conducted using the KOBAS tool with a threshold of p.adjust < 0.05, and the top 10 pathways were visualized using a bar plot.

GeneMANIA analysis

To further investigate interactions between druggable genes and other genes, a co-expression network was constructed using GeneMANIA (https://genemania.org/) [38], which searches large, publicly available biological datasets to identify related genes, including those involved in protein-protein interactions, protein-DNA interactions, genetic interactions, pathways, reactions, gene and protein expression data, protein domains, and phenotypic screening profiles. This analysis facilitates hypothesis generation, gene list analysis, and prioritization of genes for functional studies.

Drug prediction for druggable genes

Drug prediction can reveal genes associated with drug response, thereby identifying potential novel therapeutics. To explore candidate drugs for LS, we searched the DGIdb database (https://dgidb.org) [39] for small molecules potentially targeting the identified biomarkers. The resulting drug-biomarker interaction data were imported into Cytoscape for network visualization.

Molecular docking of druggable genes and drugs

To evaluate the binding affinity between the predicted drugs and druggable genes, molecular docking was performed using CB-Dock2 (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php). Three-dimensional structures of drugs were obtained from the PubChem database. Receptor structures for key genes were retrieved from the UniProt and Protein Data Bank (PDB), with the highest resolution structures selected. Docking was performed and visualized using CB-Dock2. The Vina score, calculated by AutoDock Vina, represents the binding affinity between the ligand (drug) and receptor (protein); lower Vina scores indicate stronger binding. Compounds with Vina scores > −5 were excluded [40], [41], [42] to rapidly identify molecules with potential drug activity for further experimental validation and optimization.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.0). For MR analyses, the IVW method served as the primary causal estimator, with MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode used as complementary methods. Causal estimates were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for binary outcomes. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test, with P < 0.05 indicating significant heterogeneity. Horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-Egger intercept test and MR-PRESSO global test, with P > 0.05 indicating no significant pleiotropy. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using leave-one-out analysis. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Holm-Bonferroni method. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Causal effects of druggable genes on LS

We systematically evaluated the causal effects of druggable genes on LS using IVs in an MR framework. In the eQTL dataset, 362 druggable genes showed associations with LS at a nominal significance level (P < 0.05), while in the pQTL dataset, 297 druggable gene proteins (238 unique proteins) showed such associations (P < 0.05). After Holm-Bonferroni correction, 60 druggable genes in the eQTL dataset and 43 druggable gene proteins (38 unique proteins) in the pQTL dataset remained significantly associated with LS (Holm-Bonferroni corrected P < 0.05; results shown in Fig. 1A and 1B). Discussion

LS is a chronic, progressive inflammatory skin condition that significantly impacts patients' quality of life, yet effective targeted therapies remain limited. Current treatment options are largely empirical and symptom-based, such as ultrapotent topical corticosteroids, which lack long-term efficacy data and carry risks of atrophy and relapse. These gaps highlight the urgent need to elucidate LS pathogenesis and identify novel therapeutic targets. In this study, we employed a comprehensive multi-omics approach integrating DG data with cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL within an MR framework to systematically identify potential therapeutic targets for LS. Through this strategy, we identified two druggable genes, ITGB2 and PARP1, with robust multi-omics evidence supporting their causal roles in disease pathogenesis.

ITGB2, also known as CD18, encodes a beta subunit of integrin proteins that mediate leukocyte adhesion and migration. Integrins play critical roles in immune cell trafficking, inflammation, and tissue homeostasis [43], [44]. The significant enrichment of ITGB2-associated genes in integrin-mediated cell adhesion and leukocyte migration pathways aligns with the established pathophysiology of LS, which is characterized by chronic inflammation and lymphocyte infiltration [3].While previous studies have predominantly described these histological features at the cellular level, direct genetic and molecular evidence linking specific genes to LS pathogenesis has remained scarce. Our PheWAS analysis revealed that ITGB2 did not show significant associations with other traits beyond its own protein expression, suggesting that targeting ITGB2 may have a favorable safety profile with minimal off-target effects. Molecular docking further identified eight candidate drugs with binding affinity to ITGB2, including lifitegrast, prednisone, methylprednisolone. Notably, corticosteroids such as prednisone, methylprednisolone are already established as first-line treatments for LS [9], [45], [46], providing external validation for our analytical approach. The identification of additional drugs such as lifitegrast and colchicine suggests potential alternative or adjunctive therapeutic options that warrant further investigation.

PARP1 is a nuclear enzyme involved in DNA damage repair, transcriptional regulation, and inflammatory responses [47], [48]. PARP1 has been implicated in various inflammatory and autoimmune conditions through its role in modulating immune cell function and cytokine production. However, evidence specifically linking PARP1 to cutaneous inflammatory diseases such as LS remains limited, underscoring the rationale for investigating this target in our study [49]. Our GO enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of PARP1 in protein auto-ADP-ribosylation and NAD + ADP-ribosyltransferase activity, consistent with its known molecular functions. The KEGG pathway analysis identified base excision repair as a significantly enriched pathway, further supporting the role of PARP1 in DNA repair mechanisms. Interestingly, PARP1 was also enriched in pathways related to cell adhesion and ECM-receptor interaction, suggesting potential crosstalk between DNA repair machinery and inflammatory processes in LS pathogenesis. Molecular docking identified six PARP inhibitors with binding affinity to PARP1, including olaparib, niraparib, and rucaparib, which are currently approved for cancer therapy. The potential repurposing of PARP inhibitors for LS represents a novel therapeutic avenue, particularly given the emerging evidence linking PARP activation to inflammatory skin diseases.

The integration of multi-omics evidence in our study strengthens the causal inference for ITGB2 and PARP1 as therapeutic targets. By leveraging cis-eQTL and cis-pQTL data, we were able to assess genetic effects at both transcriptional and translational levels, reducing the likelihood of false-positive findings due to pleiotropy or confounding. The consistency of causal direction across both levels of evidence further supports the robustness of our findings. Additionally, the use of MR methodology minimizes reverse causation and confounding, which are inherent limitations of observational studies [22].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the GWAS data were derived exclusively from European populations, limiting generalizability to other ancestries. Second, while MR supports causality, it does not replace experimental validation; functional studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms of ITGB2 and PARP1 in LS. Third, the molecular docking results are computational predictions requiring experimental confirmation, and further safety assessments are necessary before clinical translation.

Despite these limitations, our study has several strengths. The integration of multi-omics data with the DG represents a systematic and efficient approach to prioritize therapeutic targets. The use of cis-acting variants within ±1 Mb of target genes enhances the biological relevance of the identified IVs. The application of rigorous sensitivity analyses, including heterogeneity and pleiotropy assessment, ensures the reliability of the causal estimates. Furthermore, the convergence of evidence across multiple analytical layers—MR, PheWAS, PPI network, functional enrichment, and molecular docking—provides a comprehensive foundation for prioritizing ITGB2 and PARP1 as promising drug targets for LS. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified ITGB2 and PARP1 as potential therapeutic targets for LS through an integrative multi-omics MR approach. The molecular docking results identified several candidate drugs with binding affinity to these targets, including existing corticosteroids for ITGB2 and PARP inhibitors for PARP1. These findings provide a rationale for further experimental validation and may ultimately contribute to the development of targeted therapies for patients with LS, particularly those who are refractory to conventional treatment.
