RHOT1/2-driven mitochondrial membrane vesicles confer tumor-homing selectivity and enable in situ cancer vaccination
Authors: Wanrong Meng, Bo Li, Chang Cao, Jialu He, Bingzhi Li, Yuhang Xie, Jiajing Gong, Wenxuan Wang, Rong He, Xuelei Ma, Guiquan Zhu, Longjiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of in situ tumor vaccines has been limited by the lack of delivery systems with precise tumor selectivity. Here, we show that mitochondria from cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) exhibit an efficient tumor-homing property. We find that enrichment of the RHOT1/2 complex on the mitochondria mediates selective uptake by cancer cells through ITSN1-dependent clathrin endocytosis. Using this insight, we engineer RHOT1/2-enriched mitochondrial outer membranes into nanoscale vesicles (RMNPs) that preserve this intrinsic targeting capacity. In multiple mouse cancer models, RMNPs loaded with an immune-stimulating agent (TLR7/8 agonist) and gold nanoparticles enable photothermal therapy, which releases tumor antigens and activates antigen-presenting cells. This generates strong CD8⁺ T cell responses and long-lasting protection against tumor rechallenge. These findings reveal a fundamental organelle-based mechanism of selective intercellular targeting and establish a mechanism-driven platform with translational potential for cancer immunotherapy, highlighting how natural cellular and molecular biology can be harnessed for precision medicine.

Introduction

The development of highly specific and low-toxicity therapeutic strategies remains a central goal in cancer research, aiming to eradicate tumors while minimizing recurrence1. In recent years, immunotherapy has emerged as a powerful therapeutic approach by harnessing the intrinsic capacity of the immune system to recognize and eliminate malignant cells2–5. Among these modalities, cancer vaccines offer distinct advantages by eliciting broad antitumor responses6,7. Whole cancer cell vaccines, such as those derived from lysates of dissected tumor tissues, can elicit immune responses against diverse tumor-associated antigens, theoretically rendering them applicable to diverse solid tumor types8,9. However, the complicated manufacturing process, uncertain composition and dosages, as well as limited clinical efficacy, have restricted translation10.

To this end, in situ cancer vaccines have been developed as a highly specific and effective strategy that leverages tumor-derived antigens directly within the tumor microenvironment11. In situ cancer vaccines encompass any approach that leverages tumor antigens at the tumor site to induce tumor-specific adaptive immune responses12. These vaccines hold considerable promise for treating various solid tumors13. As is known, successful cancer immunotherapy requires the activation of the cancer-immunity cycle14,15. However, this cycle is often impaired in most tumors16,17, allowing tumor cells to escape immune surveillance and leading to immunotherapy failure18. In situ cancer vaccination aims to restore the cancer immunity cycle19 by employing agents that promote tumor antigen release, primarily through inducing tumor cell death, enhancing antigen processing and presentation by dendritic cells (DCs), and ultimately generating tumor-specific T cell responses. These immunostimulatory agents are administered directly into the tumor or its microenvironment20. Successful reactivation of the cancer-immunity cycle can also elicit an abscopal effect21, wherein tumor regression occurs in distant, untreated lesions concurrent with tumor shrinkage at the primary treatment site.

To enhance treatment outcomes, ablative cancer therapies such as photothermal therapy22, photodynamic therapy23, and radiation therapy24 are frequently combined with Toll-like receptor agonists21 to improve the antigenicity and adjuvanticity of the vaccine25. A research group developed a laser-activatable in situ vaccine that amplifies the cancer-immunity cycle by incorporating the immune adjuvant CpG26. Similarly, another group developed an in situ vaccine termed AMOPs (Au@MnO2 nanoparticles), which effectively elicited long-lasting antitumor immunity, considerably inhibiting primary, recurrent, and metastatic tumors following laser irradiation27. These findings underscore the potential of directly transforming a tumor into a vaccine and represent a promising strategy for personalized cancer therapy.

Despite this progress, systemic toxicity and the low efficiency of artificial delivery technologies remain significant challenges12. These obstacles restrict the broader application of in situ cancer vaccines, underscoring the urgent need for delivery strategies that improve tumor-targeting efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity28. Exploring an endogenous pathway for targeted in situ vaccine delivery would be an important breakthrough in advancing personalized immunotherapy and precision medicine. Our group has been focused on intracellular mitochondrial transfer29,30, a phenomenon implicated in various physiological and pathological processes, including ischemic stroke31, inflammatory pain32, cancer progression33–35, and acute lung injury repair36. Notably, aberrant activation of mitochondrial transfer within the tumor microenvironment has been observed37–39. Our preliminary findings reveal that mitochondrial transfer occurs not only between mouse tumor cells but also between human tumor cells29. Consistently, research evidence suggests that cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) transfer functional mitochondria to prostate cancer cells, thereby boosting their malignant progression40. Encouragingly, in this work, we found that mitochondria derived from CAFs, termed cancer-associated mitochondria (CAM), actively home to tumor cells both in vivo and in vitro. Next, we isolated the CAM outer membrane and fabricated 100 nm nanoparticles, termed mitochondrial membrane nanoparticles (MMNPs). Similarly, intravenous injection of MMNPs in tumor-bearing mice exhibited tumor-homing properties. Acting as a biomimetic carrier enveloped in a mitochondrial coat, MMNPs efficiently penetrate tumor cells. Importantly, unlike whole mitochondria, such vesicles lack oncogenic nucleic acids or tumor-promoting proteins, presenting a favorable safety profile. Harnessing the physiological process of spontaneous mitochondrial transfer thus provides a promising avenue for developing next-generation in situ tumor vaccine delivery technology.

In this study, we demonstrate that MMNPs exhibit extraordinary tumor-targeting affinities. MMNP co-loaded with R848—a Toll-like receptor 7/8 (TLR7/8) agonist—and gold nanoparticles (GNPs) exhibited prolonged retention at the tumor site, enhanced tumor antigen release and uptake by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), and elicited robust tumor-specific immune responses. Proteomics profiling revealed that the tumor cell-targeting mechanism of MMNP involves ITSN1-mediated clathrin-dependent endocytosis through interactions with the RHOT1/2 complex. This targeted uptake enables MMNP to exert significant biological activity within the tumor microenvironment without affecting normal tissues. In a tumor-transplanted mouse model, tumor-associated antigens were generated in situ following photothermal tumor ablation, validating the in situ vaccine functions of MMNP. This process strongly activated APCs within the draining lymph nodes (DLNs) and increased immune cell infiltration. Ultimately, MMNP-mediated immunomodulation induced a potent antitumor response, reinforcing antitumor immunity and providing long-term protection against repeated and highly aggressive tumor rechallenges.

Results

Mitochondrial transfer from CAFs to tumor cells in vitro

To confirm the transfer of mitochondria from stromal cells to tumor cells, mitochondria derived from cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), referred to as CAM, were labeled with green fluorescent protein (GFP) and co-cultured with Cherry Red-labeled tumor cells (CAL27, FD-LSC-1) and normal epithelial cells (HLMECs, HOK) for 48 h, respectively. Confocal microscopy revealed that tumor cells became positive for CAM transfer (Fig. 1a, right), while normal cells showed minimal transfer (Fig. 1a, left), suggesting that tumor cells acquire mitochondria from CAFs, while normal cells do not (Fig. 1b). Interestingly, field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) image analysis revealed that CAFs and tumor cells physically connect via tunneling nanotube (TNT), but normal epithelial cells do not (Fig. 1c, d). Four days after cell passage, CAM remained in observation in tumor cells compared with normal epithelial cells. (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Flow cytometry and confocal microscopy supported that CAM transfer increased in a time-dependent manner in CAL27 and FD-LSC-1 tumor cells, but not in HLMECs and HOK normal cells (Fig. 1e, f and Supplementary Fig. 1c). These observations indicated a selective property in mitochondrial transfer for CAFs-to-tumor. We next studied this selective property of purified CAMs on tumor cells. CAMs were isolated by multistep density gradient ultracentrifugation, and ultrathin sections of mitochondria revealed the typical mitochondrial bilayer membrane structure and inner membrane ridges (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Purified CAMs were successfully labeled with GFP (Supplementary Fig. 2b). The nanoparticle tracking analyzer measured the average CAMs size as 584.9 ± 120.9 (Supplementary Fig. 2c), and an average zeta potential of −47.1 ± 3.5 (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Continuous time-lapse image recordings showed that tumor cells (CAL27 and FD-LSC-1) completely internalized GFP-labeled CAM within 6 h, whereas normal epithelial cells (HLMECs and HOK) rarely internalized CAM. This effect was not observed in normal fibroblast-derived mitochondria (NM) (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed that tumor cells (CAL27 and FD-LSC-1) internalized CAM at significantly higher rates compared with normal cells, and this effect was not observed in NM treated tumor cells or normal cells. Extraordinary homing ability of CAM to tumor sites in vivo

To investigate the distribution of CAM in vivo, we constructed two tumor-bearing mouse models: one with tumors subcutaneously implanted in the neck and the other in the left back. Purified CAM were intravenously administered into these mouse models, with 500 nm liposomes matching the same size distribution of CAM serving as controls (Supplementary Fig. 2g). In the neck-transplanted tumor-bearing mouse model (Supplementary Fig. 3a), substantial fluorescence signals were observed at the tumor site from 1 to 24 h following CAM injection, while minimal signals were detected in the liposome and free dye DiR (a lipophilic tracer) groups (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The fluorescence intensity at the tumor site peaked at 4 h post-injection (Supplementary Fig. 3b). At the time point of 4, 12, and 24 h, major organs were harvested for fluorescence measurement. Tumor accumulation of CAM was significantly greater than that of liposomes and free dye at 4, 12 and 24 h post-injection (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d and Fig. 2a). Further, cryosections of tumor tissues were analyzed under fluorescence microscopy, confirming the superior enrichment of CAM in tumors compared to liposomes and free dye (Fig. 2b). Consistently, in the left back transplanted tumor model (Supplementary Fig. 4a), CAM exhibited increased fluorescence at the tumor site over the 0.5 to 24 h period post-injection (Supplementary Fig. 4b). The signal accumulation in tumors was significantly higher for CAM than for liposomes and free dye at 4, 12, and 24 h post-injection (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d, e). These findings suggest that CAM exhibits notable tumor-homing abilities, independent of the transplantation site. CAM outer membrane-derived nanoparticles home to the tumor in vivo

We hypothesized that the observed homing properties of CAM are attributable to its mitochondrial outer membrane and sought to explore the underlying mechanisms. In this regard, we isolated the outer membrane of CAM (OMM) through gradient centrifugation. We analyzed the protein compositions of the cell lysates and OMM via sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). Notably, significant differences in protein banding patterns were observed between the OMM and cell lysates (Fig. 2c). The OMM showed high levels of VDAC1, a key component of the outer mitochondrial membrane, and negligible levels of UQCRC2, a component of the inner mitochondrial membrane’s respiratory chain (Fig. 2d), suggesting successful isolation of OMM.

Next, we fabricated CAM-derived mitochondrial membrane nanoparticles (MMNP) through an extrusion procedure using a 100 nm polycarbonate filter (Fig. 2e). The MMNP were negatively stained with uranyl acetate and visualized under transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The TEM images revealed a shell-like structure, consistent with the expected lipid bilayer configuration (Fig. 2f). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed an average particle size of approximately 98.9 ± 1.1 nm (Fig. 2g). The surface zeta potential of MMNP was −10.3 ± 1.6 mV (Fig. 2h). Further, we delineated proteomics profiles of MMNP using mass spectrometry and identified 156 proteins in the MMNP (Supplementary Dataset 1). GO Cellular Component Enrichment was performed to confirm that the identified proteins are predominantly associated with the outer mitochondrial membrane (Fig. 2i). Next, to detect the orientation of the outer mitochondrial membrane in MMNP, immunoelectron microscopy and immunoprecipitation were performed. The outer mitochondrial membrane protein VDAC1 was identified on the outside by immuno-gold (Fig. 2j). Similarly, anti-VDAC1 conjugated beads successfully captured MMNP, and co-IP pellets detected outer mitochondrial membrane protein VDAC1 (Fig. 2k). These results confirm that we have successfully prepared these organelle membrane vesicles with the right-side-out.

To assess the tumor-homing ability of MMNP in vivo, we injected these nanoparticles intravenously into tumor-bearing mice via the tail vein (Supplementary Fig. 5a), with 100 nm LNP matching the same size distribution of MMNP serving as controls (Supplementary Fig. 2h). Real-time fluorescence imaging revealed that, compared to 100 nm LNP, MMNP preferentially accumulated at tumor sites (Supplementary Fig. 5b). At the time point of 4, 12, and 24 h, major organs were harvested for fluorescence measurement. The signal accumulation in tumors was significantly higher than LNP and free dye at 4, 12 and 24 h post-injection (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d and Fig. 2l). Fluorescence microscopy of cryosectioned tumor tissues further confirmed the enhanced accumulation of MMNP in the tumors compared to LNP and free dye (Fig. 2m). Likewise, in the left-back transplanted tumor model (Supplementary Fig. 6a), MMNP exhibited increased tumor infiltration from 1 to 24 h post-injection compared to LNP (Supplementary Fig. 6b). The accumulated signal from MMNP in tumors was significantly higher than that from LNP and free dye at 4, 12, and 24 h post-injection (Supplementary Fig. 6c–e). These in vivo results suggest that the tumor-homing property inherent to MMNP, derived from OMM, can be leveraged for targeted therapy in combating cancer.

MMNP targeting tumor cells depends on RHOT1/2-mediated endocytosis

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the selective uptake of MMNP, we analyzed the proteomic profiles of cells with the highest (FD-LSC-1) and lowest (HOK) MMNP uptake rates. Principal component analysis revealed significant differences in the membrane protein compositions between these tumor and normal cell lines (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 7a). A total of 2369 proteins were found to be significantly upregulated, while 1626 proteins were significantly downregulated in tumor cells compared to normal cells (Fig. 3b). KEGG pathway analysis of differentially expressed proteins highlighted significant enrichment in the endocytosis, lysosome, and autophagy pathways in tumor cells (Fig. 3c). Given the central role of transmembrane proteins in cellular endocytosis, we further focused on the 250 identified membrane proteins (Supplementary Fig. 7d). First, we cross-referenced these proteins with 156 endocytosis-related proteins from the Uniprot Database (ID: KW-0254), which resulted in 35 overlapping proteins (22 proteins with fold-change (FC) ≥ 1.5 and 13 proteins with FC < 1.5) (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 7e). Moreover, tumor cells were significantly enriched in proteins associated with receptor-mediated endocytosis and the clathrin-coated pit pathway (Supplementary Fig. 7f, g). Specifically, the abundance of clathrin-dependent endocytosis proteins, including AP2A2, AP2M1, EPN1, ITSN1, ITSN2, PIK3C2A, and FNBP1, was significantly upregulated in tumor cells compared to normal cells (Supplementary Fig. 7h).

As is known, AP2A2 and ITSN1 are crucial for the initial steps of endocytosis, facilitating the internalization of clathrin-coated vesicles at clathrin-coated pits41,42. To further investigate their role in MMNP uptake, we selectively blocked AP2A2 and ITSN1 in tumor cells and assessed the impact on MMNP endocytosis efficiency. Blocking AP2A2 only slightly reduced the endocytosis rate of MMNP at 3 μg/ml (Supplementary Fig. 7i), while blocking ITSN1 drastically decreased the endocytosis rate in a dose-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 7j). To investigate whether the MMNP endocytosis rate correlates with protein expression levels of ITSN1 and AP2A2, we analyzed protein levels in both normal cell and tumor cell lines, including HOK, bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs), and various tumor cell lines (CAL27, FD-LSC-1, MOC1, and B16-F10). Results showed the endocytosis rate was significantly higher in tumor cells compared to normal cells, with varying uptake rates observed among different tumor cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 8a). ITSN1 protein levels were significantly higher in tumor cells compared to normal cells, while AP2A2 was upregulated only in MOC1 and FD-LSC-1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Correlation analysis demonstrated a strong positive correlation between ITSN1 expression levels and MMNP endocytosis capacity (Supplementary Fig. 8c). In contrast, AP2A2 expression did not show a significant correlation with the endocytosis rate (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Collectively, these findings suggest that the high MMNP endocytosis rate in tumor cells is strongly correlated with ITSN1 expression.

To elucidate the direct regulation between ITSN1 and MMNP uptake, we first analyzed the reciprocal interactions of ITSN1 with 156 membrane proteins derived from MMNP (Supplementary Dataset 1) using the AlphaFold 3 database. This analysis revealed that ITSN1 has potential interactions with both RHOT1 and RHOT2 (Fig. 3e). We next tested the binding affinity of ITSN1 to RHOT1 and RHOT2, respectively. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) results show that ITSN1 directly interacts with RHOT1 or RHOT2 protein in a positive dose-dependent manner. The determined equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) for ITSN1 binding to the RHOT1 and RHOT2 proteins were 188 and 477 nM, respectively (Fig. 3f). Microscale thermophoresis (MST), a method that allows the determination of Kd values in solution and requires ITSN1 to be fluorescently labeled, was performed with increasing concentrations of RHOT1 or RHOT2 proteins. The results show that increasing amounts of RHOT1 and RHOT2 clearly affected the thermophoretic motion of ITSN1, and the ITSN1 binding to RHOT1 and RHOT2 proteins yielded Kd values of 119 nM and 337 nM, respectively (Fig. 3g). Subsequently, we performed co-immunoprecipitation to validate the direct interaction between overexpressed ITSN1 and RHOT1/2 in MMNP. Our results demonstrated that ITSN1 physically interacts with both RHOT1 and RHOT2 in tumor cells (Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 9a). However, knocking down RHOT1/2 in MMNP abolished the interaction with ITSN1 (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 9b). The endocytosis rate of MMNP in FD-LSC-1 cells, following RHOT1/2 knockdown, was significantly reduced compared to the control group, as analyzed by confocal microscopy (Fig. 3j) and flow cytometry (Fig. 3k). Taken together, these results indicate that MMNP targeting tumor cells depends on RHOT1/2 complex-mediated clathrin-dependent endocytosis.

RHOT1/2 overexpression enhances MMNP tumor-targeting ability

To explore the role of RHOT1/2 in tumor-targeting ability, we engineered RHOT1/2-overexpressing MMNP (RMNP). Owing to RHOT1/2 overexpression, the endocytosis rate of RMNP by tumor cells was significantly increased compared to MMNP (Supplementary Fig. 9c). The RMNPs efficiently entered tumor cells and accumulated in the cytoplasm within 2 h (Supplementary Fig. 9d, bottom panel). In contrast, MMNP predominantly accumulated around the cell membrane and remained engaged in the endocytic process at 6 h (Supplementary Fig. 9d, top panel). To assess the effect of RHOT1/2 on tumor-homing ability in vivo, tumor-bearing mice were treated with RHOT1/2 knockdown (KD) or overexpression of MMNPs. The results showed that RHOT1/2 KD MMNP significantly reduced tumor accumulation, while RHOT1/2 overexpressing RMNP showed significantly enhanced tumor-targeting ability, confirming the importance of RHOT1/2 in tumor targeting (Supplementary Fig. 9e). These findings suggest that RHOT1/2 overexpression enhances the intracellular uptake of MMNPs in tumor cells, thereby improving their delivery efficiency.

Major concerns regarding the application of RMNP are its immunogenicity and safety. As shown in (Supplementary Fig. 10a–c), the RMNP exhibited significantly lower levels of organ distribution and uptake by the spleen and lymph nodes, comparable with LNP. To determine the long-term toxicity, RMNP was injected into C57BL/6 mice every 2 days. Peripheral blood was harvested after 7 days and subsequently analyzed. The proportions of white blood cells, red blood cells, platelets, lymphocytes, granulocytes, and monocytes in the RMNP-treated group showed no significant difference from those in the PBS-treated group (Supplementary Fig. 10 d–g), indicating that the immune system was not activated by treatment with RMNP. Serum levels of aspartate transaminase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were not significantly different from those of the groups (Supplementary Fig. 10h, i), indicating that RMNP did not cause damage to liver function. H&E staining of the major organs revealed no obvious pathological changes in these groups (Supplementary Fig. 10j). These results suggest that RMNP has limited immunogenicity and high safety, making it an ideal drug delivery vehicle.

Given that RMNPs exhibited the highest internalization efficiency and safety, we sought to leverage their potential as a drug delivery system for in situ tumor vaccines. To evaluate the tumor-targeting capabilities, we co-loaded the Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonist R848 and photothermal gold nanoparticles (GNPs) into RMNPs, establishing R848/GNP@RMNP (Fig. 4a). TEM images revealed that R848/GNP@RMNP nanoparticles exhibited a well-defined spherical morphology with uniform size distribution (Fig. 4b). The average particle size and zeta potential of R848/GNP@RMNP were 99.1 ± 1.2 nm and −9.2 ± 0.3 mV, respectively, as measured by NTA (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). To evaluate the photothermal performance of R848/GNP@RMNP, both GNPs and R848/GNP@RMNP were exposed to an 808 nm near-infrared (NIR) laser at a power density of 1 W/cm2. When irradiation was alternately switched on and off, the photothermal effect of R848/GNP@RMNP was reversible, demonstrating outstanding photothermal stability and photocontrolled performance for effective tumor ablation (Fig. 4c). Moreover, as the temperature increased, the concentration of R848 remained unchanged, indicating excellent thermostability (Supplementary Fig. 11e). To further investigate the tumor-homing ability and photothermal effects of R848/GNP@RMNP, mice with neck-transplanted tumors received tail vein injection of R848/GNP@RMNP, followed by NIR irradiation for 10 min. The results demonstrated that R848/GNP@RMNP exhibited an extraordinary targeted photothermal effect at the tumor site compared to free GNPs (Fig. 4d, e). Similarly, in a left-back tumor model, the tumor site temperature significantly increased in the R848/GNP@RMNP-treated group, whereas no substantial temperature rise was observed in the free GNP-treated group (Supplementary Fig. 11f, g), consistent with findings from the neck-transplanted tumor model. Furthermore, in vivo pharmacokinetic assessment indicated that the circulation profiles of R848@RMNP and R848/GNP@RMNP were significantly altered compared to free R848, with RMNPs effectively prolonging the retention time of R848 (Supplementary Fig. 11h). More importantly, compared with the free R848-treated group, both R848@RMNP- and R848/GNP@RMNP-treated groups showed a significantly enhanced accumulation of R848 at the tumor site 24 h post-injection (Supplementary Fig. 11j).

Next, we evaluated the internalization efficiency of R848/GNP@RMNP in tumor cells. FD-LSC-1 and CAL27 cells successfully internalized R848/GNP@RMNP after 12 h of co-culture (Fig. 4f, g). No significant difference in intracellular fluorescence intensity was observed between the two cell lines (Fig. 4h). Flow cytometry further confirmed the internalization of R848/GNP@RMNP by FD-LSC-1 and CAL27 cells after 12 h of co-culture (Fig. 4i, j). To assess the cytotoxicity caused by photothermal therapy, FD-LSC-1 and CAL27 cells were irradiated with an 808 nm laser at a power density of 1 W/cm2 for 10 min after 12 h of GNP and R848/GNP@RMNP treatment. As a result, cell viability was significantly decreased in the R848/GNP@RMNP group compared to the free GNP group in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 4k, l). In contrast, no significant difference in cell viability was observed without laser irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 11j, k). TEM analysis of FD-LSC-1 cells revealed distinct structural changes following R848/GNP@RMNP treatment. In R848/GNP@RMNP-treated cells without laser irradiation, the RMNPs and cell structures remained intact (Fig. 4m, right). However, with laser irradiation, R848/GNP@RMNP ruptured and released their contents, leading to nuclear membrane wrinkling and vacuolization of the endoplasmic reticulum cisternae. (Fig. 4n). In addition, with laser irradiation, cells exhibited enhanced HMGB1 secretion (Supplementary Fig. 11l) and extracellular ATP release (Supplementary Fig. 11m) while decreased intercellular ATP (Supplementary Fig. 11n), confirming an immunogenic cell death induction. Altogether, our findings demonstrate that RMNPs work as an effective nanocarrier for tumor-targeted photothermal therapy.

Photothermal tumor ablation induces DCs' activation in vitro

DCs, as pivotal antigen-presenting cells, play an essential role in initiating and regulating innate and adaptive immunity. Upon encountering antigens, immature DCs engulf and process them into peptides while migrating to nearby lymph nodes. During this process, immature DCs differentiate into mature DCs, presenting major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-peptide complexes to T-cell receptors upon reaching the lymph nodes.

To evaluate the immunological effects of R848/GNP@RMNP on DCs' activation, we isolated bone marrow-derived DCs (BMDCs) from C57BL/6 mice and examined their response to photothermal tumor ablation. Tumor cells were treated with 808 nm NIR laser therapy and subsequently co-cultured with BMDCs for 48 h (Fig. 5a). Flow cytometry was performed to assess the upregulation of the co-stimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86, which are well-established markers of DCs' maturation. Next, we investigated the in vivo interaction between mature DCs and T cell populations within the DLNs following photothermal tumor ablation. Discussion

In summary, the physiological phenomenon of tunneling nanotube (TNT)-mediated mitochondrial transfer from cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to tumor cells relies on the RHOT1/2 complex on the mitochondrial outer membrane. In the presence of TNTs, intact mitochondria are transported directly into the recipient tumor cell cytoplasm via microtubule-based motors, bypassing the endocytic pathway. However, once the cancer-associated mitochondrial (CAM) outer membrane is isolated and formulated into RMNPs, TNT connections are no longer available. Importantly, the same RHOT1/2 proteins on the isolated membrane retain their ability to selectively recognize tumor cells through direct interaction with ITSN1, a scaffold protein highly expressed on tumor cells. In the absence of TNTs, this recognition triggers clathrin-dependent endocytosis, allowing RMNPs to be internalized and deliver their payload. Thus, the presence or absence of TNT connections dictates the route of internalization—direct cytoplasmic delivery versus endocytic uptake. This mechanistic distinction does not represent a contradiction but rather a functional adaptation that enables the translation of a natural intercellular communication process into an engineered targeted delivery platform.

In this study, we have developed an in situ vaccine delivery platform leveraging CAM, offering a promising strategy for personalized immunotherapy with enhanced safety and minimal systemic toxicity. Mitochondrial transfer within the tumor microenvironment is a naturally occurring physiological process43. The engineered R848/GNP@RMNP mimics mitochondrial characteristics, disguising itself as an energy supplier to infiltrate tumor cells efficiently. Upon entering tumor cells, it generates localized heat upon noninvasive NIR light irradiation, inducing immunogenic cell death and promoting the release of tumor-associated antigens. In the presence of an immune adjuvant, this platform enhances DCs' antigen presentation and processing, thereby amplifying T-cell responses in DLNs. This process establishes long-term antitumor immunity, effectively preventing tumor recurrence.

This study presents a clinically translatable approach, requiring only a tiny sample of pathological CAFs from patients44. These CAFs can be expanded in vitro under good manufacturing practice (GMP)-compliant conditions to produce large quantities of CAMs. Through large-scale membrane extrusion, substantial yields of mitochondrial vesicles can be obtained. MMNP with an average size of 100 nm demonstrates an enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect compared with CAM with an average size of 500 nm, which substantially prolongs its retention time at the tumor site. This size-dependent superiority represents a crucial advantage for achieving optimal drug delivery efficacy, a fundamental requirement for clinically viable therapeutic carriers. When integrated with photothermal nanoparticles and immune adjuvants, the resulting formulation can be reinfused into patients, enabling the development of personalized cancer vaccines. Importantly, MMNP does not carry any carcinogenic nucleic acids or tumor-promoting proteins, ensuring a favorable safety profile for normal tissues. Preclinical animal studies demonstrate that only four consecutive injections are sufficient to achieve durable recurrence prevention, underscoring the potential of this in situ vaccine platform to elicit long-lasting, tumor-specific immunity. Future research will prioritize accelerating the clinical translation of this in situ cancer vaccine platform.

As a noninvasive therapeutic modality, photothermal therapy holds significant promise for cancer treatment45. The development of next-generation photothermal nanomaterials with high heat conversion efficiency and excellent safety profiles will significantly advance this field46. GNPs exhibit maximal absorption of longitudinal localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) in the near-infrared (NIR) spectrum, making them highly effective for the thermal ablation of cancer cells47. Notably, GNPs have already progressed into early-phase (NCT00436410) and phase I (NCT00356980, NCT00436410) clinical trials. A phase I clinical trial conducted in patients with breast, colon, and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma demonstrated no dose-limiting toxicity47. The GNPs utilized in this study rank among the most effective materials currently available for photothermal therapy. Moving forward, investigating the safety, biodistribution, and metabolic pathways of GNPs in the human body will be crucial to facilitating their full clinical translation.

Beyond direct photothermal effects, this localized photothermal therapy, when combined with TLR agonists, significantly enhanced the activation of the cancer-immunity cycle, amplifying the efficacy of the in situ vaccine. In this study, R848-loaded MMNPs extended biological half-life, thereby increasing their retention and bioavailability at the tumor site. TLRs are primarily expressed on immune cells and play a pivotal role in tumor immunology. Among them, TLR7, an endosomal single-stranded RNA receptor, is highly expressed by macrophages, DCs, natural killer cells, and B cells48–51. Thus, prolonging R848 retention at the tumor site further amplifies the antitumor immune cycle, reinforcing its therapeutic potential52. Importantly, leveraging TLR engagement offers a promising strategy to further enhance the efficacy of whole-cancer cell vaccines derived from ablated tumor cells. By activating TLR-mediated immune signaling, this approach can potentiate antitumor immunity and improve long-term protection against cancer recurrence.

In conclusion, the R848/GNP@RMNP developed in this study represents one of the most advanced in situ vaccines based on the current technological frameworks. This work lays a critical and solid foundation for developing nano-vaccines derived from tumor-associated mitochondria, providing a transformative approach for personalized in situ cancer vaccination.

Cell separation and cell culture

Human head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cell lines (CAL27, #CRL-2095, ATCC; FD-LSC-1, #BFN60810341, BLUEFBIO), normal oral epithelial keratinocytes (HOK, #2610, ScienCell), human laryngeal mucosal epithelial cells (HLMECs, #PDHX-C166, Hycyte), bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs, #PCS-500-012, ATCC), and murine cell lines (mouse oral squamous cell carcinoma cells, MOC1, #BFN6021632, BLUEFBIO, and melanoma cells, B16-F10, #CRL-6475, ATCC) were obtained. Human and murine primary cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) were isolated from surgical explants of an HNSCC patient (male) and a MOC1 xenograft mouse (male) model following previously established protocols for homologous experiments to avoid species cross-reactivity in compliance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of West China Hospital, Sichuan University.

All cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Cell transfection

For mitochondrial-specific GFP labeling, CAFs were seeded into 6-well plates and allowed to reach 40% confluency before infection with the prepared lentivirus. The mitochondrial-targeted GFP lentivirus, Ubi-MCS-mito & AcGFP-SV40-Puro, COX8A signal peptide, referred to as mito, was purchased from Genechem (Shanghai, China). For cell-specific Cherry Red labeling, human HNSCC cells (CAL27, FD-LSC-1) and normal epithelial cells (HOK, HLMECs) were seeded into 6-well plates and infected with lentivirus upon reaching 40% confluency. The Cherry Red-labeled lentivirus, U6-MCS- mCherry-IRES-Puro, was purchased from Genechem (Shanghai, China). After 48 h of infection, 8 μg/mL puromycin was used for selection, and cells were maintained for at least one week to ensure the enrichment of stably transfected positive cells. For ITSN1-Flag expression, FD-LSC-1 and HEK-293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates and infected with adenovirus upon reaching 40% confluency. The adenovirus-mediated ITSN1-Flag-expressing vector, CMV-MCS-3FLAG-ITSN1-Puro, was purchased from Genechem (Shanghai, China). Cells were treated with siRNA to achieve RHOT1 and RHOT2 knockdown. The sequences were as follows: hRHOT1: shRNA#GTGGACGCTCACGACTTATTT;hRHOT2:shRNA#GCGTCTACAAGCACCATTACA;mRHOT1:shRNA#GATGATCATAAGTCCTATTAT;mRHOT2:shRNA#TTCGGCCTGTTGGAGTAATAT.

For RHOT1 and RHOT2 overexpression, CAFs were infected with lentiviral vectors, Ubi-MCS-hRHOT1-puro and Ubi-MCS-hRHOT2-puro, Ubi-MCS-mRHOT1-puro and Ubi-MCS-mRHOT2-puro, both of which were purchased from Genechem (Shanghai, China).

Co-culture of CAFs with HNSCC cells and mitochondrial transfer quantification

CAFs labeled with mito-GFP were seeded into 12-well plates and allowed to adhere. HNSCC cells (CAL27, FD-LSC-1) or normal epithelial cells (HOK, HLMECs), labeled with Cherry Red, were then added to the plates containing pre-seeded CAFs for co-culture. After 48 h of co-culture, fluorescent images were captured using a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan). When cell density reached 90% confluency, cells were passaged, and fluorescence microscopy was performed daily at fixed observation points. Mitochondrial transfer was quantified by calculating the proportion of cells exhibiting red (recipient cell) and green (mitochondria) co-localization per field using ImageJ 2.14.0. Fifteen randomly chosen microscope fields from three biologically independent replicates were analyzed per group. After cells were passaged, the percentage of CAM-retaining cells was quantified by calculating the proportion of cells exhibiting red (recipient cell) and green (mitochondria) co-localization per field by ImageJ 2.14.0 using the same procedure as above.

To quantify mitochondrial transfer, flow cytometry analysis was conducted at 0, 24, 72, and 120 h post-co-culture. The culture medium was removed, and cells were washed with PBS, detached from the plates, and analyzed using flow cytometry (BD FACSAria Fusion, USA).

Scanning electron microscopy of a tunneling nanotube

CAFs and recipient cells (normal or tumor) were co-cultured on 12-mm glass coverslips for 24 h. Cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer, washed, and post-fixed in 0.1% OsO₄ for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, samples were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (35%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), critical-point dried, and sputter-coated with 5 nm Au/Pt using a Denton Desk V TSC. Imaging was performed on a Zeiss Ultra55 FESEM equipped with a Gemini column and SE2 detector. TNT frequency was quantified by calculating the number per cell based on randomly chosen 15 microscope fields using ImageJ 2.14.0 software, applying morphological criteria consistent with those described for human mitochondrial transfer nanotubes: with average length ≥10 µm and width ≥0.25 µm, extending at least one cell diameter, and without substrate contact.

Mitochondrial isolation and morphological observation

CAF-derived mitochondria were isolated using the mitochondrial extraction kit (Solarbio, SM0020, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cultured CAFs were digested and washed with PBS. Lysis buffer was added to 5 × 10⁷ ice-precooled resuspended CAFs in a final volume of 1.0 mL. The cell suspension was then transferred to a small-capacity glass homogenizer and homogenized in an ice bath (0 °C) for 30 to 40 strokes. The resulting homogenate was subjected to centrifuging at 1000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was removed and transferred to a new centrifuge tube, and centrifuged again at 1000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was then transferred to another new centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The mitochondrial pellet was washed with buffer and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the sample was centrifuged again at 12,000 × g for 10 min. After discarding the supernatant, high-purity mitochondria were deposited at the bottom of the tube. All centrifugation steps were performed at 4 °C to maintain mitochondrial integrity.

To observe mitochondrial morphology, samples were prepared for TEM (JEM-2100F, JEOL Ltd., Japan). Isolated mitochondria were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and subjected to a series of gradient dehydration steps. Following embedding and curing, 70 nm ultrathin sections were prepared. These sections were then double-stained with 3% uranyl acetate and lead citrate and observed under TEM.

Uptake of CAM by tumor cells and normal cells in vitro

To investigate mitochondrial uptake dynamics, HNSCC cells (CAL27, FD-LSC-1) or normal epithelial cells (HOK, HLMECs) were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to adhere. GFP-labeled cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) mitochondria (CAM) or normal fibroblast-derived mitochondria (NM) were then added to these cells at a protein concentration of 20 µg/mL. Live-cell imaging was conducted hourly from 0 to 6 h using the IncuCyte Zoom Live-Cell Analysis System (Essen Bioscience). For each group, fifteen randomly selected microscope fields from three biologically independent replicates were analyzed. Co-localization areas of GFP CAM with cells were quantified using ImageJ 2.14.0, and the co-localized area per field was normalized to that of HOK cells. Given the nonparametric distribution of the data, statistical significance was evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc analysis.

Flow cytometry was used to quantitatively analyze mitochondrial uptake by cells. HNSCC cells (CAL27, FD-LSC-1) or normal epithelial cells (HOK, HLMECs) were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and allowed to adhere. Before adding CAM (20 µg/mL), the culture medium was replaced with fresh FBS-free medium. After 12 h of incubation, free mitochondria were removed by washing with PBS, and cells were detached from the plates for flow cytometric analysis using flow cytometry (BD FACSAria Fusion, USA).

OMM preparation and characterization

The OMM was collected following the above protocol. Specifically, purified mitochondria were first pelleted by centrifugation, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in ultrapure water to achieve a final protein concentration of 5 mg/mL. The sample was gently stirred on ice for 20 min. Next, an equal volume of 1.4 M sucrose solution was added, and the mixture was stirred on ice for an additional 5 min. The sample was then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min, and the resulting supernatant was collected. To further isolate the OMM fraction, the collected supernatant was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 30 min, yielding the OMM as an off-white pellet. The membrane protein concentration was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, and the isolated OMM was stored at −80 °C for subsequent experiments. Under our experimental conditions, we obtained 38.6 ± 0.2 μg of OMM from 224.1 ± 0.4 μg of purified mitochondria (total protein weight), corresponding to an isolation yield of 17.23%.

Preparation of MMNP and RMNP

The collected OMM was sonicated in a capped glass vial for 5 min using a bath sonicator (FS30D, Fisher Scientific) at 42 kHz frequency and 100 W power. The resulting vesicles, referred to as MMNP, were then extruded through 100-nm polycarbonate porous membranes using an Avanti Mini-Extruder (Avanti Polar Lipids). The preparation steps for RMNPs followed the same procedure, except that RHOT1/2-overexpressing OMM was used instead of wild-type OMM.

To prepare R848- and GNP-loaded RMNPs (R848/GNP@RMNP), 1 mg/mL of RHOT1/2-overexpressing OMM was mixed with 2.5 mg/mL of R848 (Selleck, S28463) and 1 mg/mL of the photothermal agent GNPs (10 nm, Abace, Beijing, China). These components were incorporated during the vesicle preparation process.

Membrane orientation validation

To verify the right-side-out membrane orientation of MMNPs derived from isolated OMM, we employed two independent and complementary methods: immunogold electron microscopy and immunoprecipitation. Immunogold electron microscopy: Freshly prepared MMNPs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, then dropped onto carbon-coated copper grids and allowed to stand at room temperature for 5 min. After blocking with PBS containing 1% BSA for 30 min, the grids were incubated with rabbit anti-VDAC1 antibody (EPR27552-6, 1:1000, Abcam, #ab306581) at 4 °C overnight. Following three washes with PBS, the grids were incubated with 6 nm Colloidal Gold-AffiniPure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (1:20, Jackson ImmunoResearch, #111-195-144) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After washing with deionized water, the grids were negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 1 min, dried, and observed under a transmission electron microscope. Immunoprecipitation: 500 µL of freshly prepared MMNPs (100 μg/mL in PBS) were mixed with anti-VDAC1 antibody pre-conjugated magnetic beads (Protein A/G Magnetic Beads, ab286842) and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h with rotation. The beads were separated using a magnetic stand, and the supernatant was collected. The beads were washed three times with PBS and then resuspended in RIPA lysis buffer. Western blot was performed to detect VDAC1 expression in the precipitated complex and the supernatant.

Uptake of MMNP and RMNP by tumor cells in vitro

To investigate MMNP and RMNP uptake by tumor cells, FD-LSC-1 pre-stained with cell membrane PKH26 (Red) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was seeded on 24-well plates with glass slides (0.5 × 105 cells/ well). The MMNP (Ctrl, RHOT1/2KD) or RMNP (100 μg/mL) labeled with PKH67 (Green) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were then added for co-culture. After 12 h of incubation, the tumor cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min, and stained for the nuclei with spectral DAPI (Akoya Biosciences, USA). Fluorescent images of the tumor cells were captured using a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan) to observe the uptake of MMNP and RMNP by tumor cells. For each group, the intracellular fluorescence intensity of MMNPs (green) was quantified using ImageJ 2.14.0. The data are based on three biologically independent replicates, with 10 randomly chosen microscope fields analyzed per group in each replicate. The normal distribution of continuous variables was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the statistical significance of pairwise comparisons was assessed by the Student’s t test.

Additionally, flow cytometry analysis was used to quantitatively assess the cellular uptake of MMNP and RMNP. MMNP and RMNP were stained with PKH67 (Green). FD-LSC-1 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and allowed to adhere. After 12 h of incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove unbound nanoparticles. Then, FD-LSC-1 cells were detached from 6-well plates and analyzed using flow cytometry (BD FACSAria Fusion, USA). PBS was used as a blank control to assess the background fluorescence and ensure accurate measurement of nanoparticle uptake by the tumor cells.

Mass spectrometry analysis

Protein extraction and digestion

In all, 1.0 × 106 FD-LSC-1 and HOK cells were treated with MMNP (20 μg/mL) for 24 h (n = 3), then washed with PBS three times. Each sample was lysed in an appropriate volume of SDT buffer (4% SDS, 100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6) for protein extraction. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA method. For each sample, 15 µg of protein was mixed with 5× loading buffer, boiled for 5 min, and separated by SDS-PAGE on 4–20% precast gradient gels (180 V, 45 min), followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining. All samples were digested with trypsin. The resulting peptides were desalted on C18 Cartridges, lyophilized, and reconstituted in 40 µL of 0.1% formic acid. Peptide concentration was measured by OD280. An appropriate amount of iRT standard peptides was spiked into each digested sample before DIA analysis.

Mass spectrometry analysis

For DIA (data-independent acquisition) analysis, chromatographic separation was performed on a nanoflow Vanquish Neo system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After separation by nano-flow high-performance liquid chromatography, the samples were analyzed by DIA on an Astral high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). The analysis was carried out in positive ion mode. The precursor ion scan range was 380–980 m/z, with a full-MS resolution of 240,000 at 200 m/z. The normalized AGC target was set to “Maximum” and the injection time (IT) to 5 ms. For MS2, DIA acquisition mode was used with 299 scanning windows, an isolation window of 2 m/z, HCD collision energy of 25 eV, normalized AGC target “Maximum”, and IT of 3 ms. DIA data were processed using DIA-NN software. Proteins identified by database searching were filtered to a false discovery rate (FDR) < 1%.

AP2A2 and ITSN1 blocked

HNSCC cells (FD-LSC-1) were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and allowed to adhere. A titration experiment to determine the optimal concentration was performed; cellular fluorescence intensity increased with antibody concentration and reached a plateau at 3 μg/mL; higher concentrations (5 μg/mL) did not further increase signal. Thus, 3 μg/mL was considered the saturation concentration, and we used concentrations up to this value. Blocked antibodies anti-AP2A2 (Invitrogen, PA5-143990) and anti-ITSN1 (Invitrogen, H00006453-M02) at concentrations of 0, 0.6, 1.5, and 3 μg/ml were added for 12 h, respectively. After incubation, unbound antibodies were removed and washed with PBS. Following antibody blocking, a fresh FBS-free medium containing MMNP (20 µg/mL) was added to the cells. After 12 h of incubation, uninternalized MMNP were removed and washed with PBS, and HNSCC cells were detached from the plates for flow cytometry analysis (BD FACSAria Fusion, USA).

Characterization of MMNP and R848/GNP@RMNP

The morphology and size of MMNP were analyzed using TEM (JEOL-2100F, Japan) and NTA (NanoSight LM10 system, UK) following the preparation steps described above. For TEM imaging, MMNP and R848/GNP@RMNP samples were deposited onto a 200-mesh carbon film-coated grid, negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate for 1 min, and washed with deionized water.

Western blot analysis was performed to evaluate the expression of mitochondrial outer membrane marker proteins in the prepared MMNP. Total cellular protein was extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer. Protein samples were subjected to 8% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Merck Millipore, Germany). Membranes were blocked and incubated with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-VDAC1 antibody (EPR27552-6, 1:1000, Abcam, #ab306581), rabbit anti-UQCRC2 antibody (EPR13051, 1:1000, Abcam, #ab203832), and rabbit anti-β-actin antibody (EPR21241, 1:1000, Abcam, #ab213262). Then, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated rabbit anti-goat IgG antibody (1:10,000, Abcam, # ab205718) for 1 h. Membranes were treated with Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Merck Millipore, Germany). Chemiluminescence signals were detected using the LAS 3000 imaging system (Fujifilm, Japan).

Immunoprecipitation

As described above, FD-LSC-1 and MOC1 cells were transfected with Flag-hITSN1 lentivirus and Flag-hITSN1, respectively. Cell membrane proteins were extracted using a membrane protein extraction kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), followed by vortexing and centrifugation at 18,000 × g at 4 °C for 25 min. The supernatant was collected and quantified for protein concentration. Anti-Flag magnetic beads (20 μL) were added to every 500 μL of protein sample. The mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C on a side-to-side shaker or a rotator mixer. Mouse IgG magnetic beads were used as a negative control. The next day, magnetic separation was performed. The prepared hMMNP and mMMNP/hMMNP(RHOT1/2KD) and mMMNP(RHOT1/2KD) (derived from human or murine primary CAFs) were diluted to a final concentration of 100 µg/mL in lysis buffer and then added to the washed anti-Flag magnetic bead complexes. The mixture was incubated for 2 h at 4 °C with rotation. The beads were separated using a magnetic stand and then washed three times with 500 μL of ice-cold lysis buffer. The sample was boiled for 5 min at 95 °C in 100 μL of SDS-PAGE sample loading buffer. Source data, including uncropped blots, are provided as a Source Data file.

ITSN1-RHOT1/2 interaction predictions using AlphaFold3

The protein sequences of the candidate genes (ITSN1, Entry: Q15811; RHOT1, Entry: Q8IXI2; RHOT2, Entry: Q8IXI1) were obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Predictions of the interactions between human ITSN1 and RHOT1/2 were generated using AlphaFold3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/). The predicted monomer structure of the ITSN1-RHOT1/2 interaction was downloaded as a CIF file from the AlphaFold3 database. PyMOL v2.4.0 was used to annotate the models and color different structural regions of the ITSN1-RHOT1/2 interaction predictions for better visualization.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)

SPR measurements were conducted on a Biacore 8K instrument. Purified ITSN1 protein was immobilized on a Series S Sensor Chip using a standard amine-coupling protocol, achieving a density of approximately 10,000 response units (RU). PBS containing 5% DMSO served as the running buffer. Serially diluted solutions of RHOT1 and RHOT2 (each at six concentrations) were injected separately over the chip surface at a flow rate of 65 μL/min for a 60-s association phase at 25 °C. Sensorgram data were collected and analyzed using the Biacore 8K Evaluation Software. The equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) was derived by fitting the data to a 1:1 binding model.

Microscale thermophoresis (MST)

The ITSN1 protein was fluorescently labeled following the manufacturer’s instructions (Genechem, Shanghai, China). RHOT1 and RHOT2 proteins were diluted to the indicated concentrations and incubated with 0.8 mg/mL of the purified, labeled ITSN1 protein for 15 min in running buffer (50 mmol/L Tris-HCl, 100 mmol/L NaCl, pH 7.5). The samples were then loaded into NanoTemper premium-coated capillaries. Microscale thermophoresis (MST) measurements were performed using instrument settings of 80% LED power and 80% MST power. The equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) was calculated by fitting the data to the mass action equation using the NanoTemper Analysis software, based on duplicate measurements.

Living imaging and CAM biodistribution analysis in vivo

All mice were maintained on a standard normal chow diet and provided food and water ad libitum. Environmental conditions were maintained in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Mice were housed under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle (lights on from 7:00 AM to 7:00 PM), at an ambient temperature of 21–23 °C and relative humidity maintained between 30–70%. The in vivo distribution of CAM was measured in male BALB/c nude mice (5–6 weeks) with FD-LSC-1 xenografts. FD-LSC-1 cells were subcutaneously injected into the back or neck region of the mice. Once tumors reached a volume of approximately 200 mm³, the mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 8 per group): free dye DiR, liposomes (500 nm), and CAM. To avoid significant interference from tissue autofluorescence during in vivo imaging, we selected the near-infrared lipophilic dye DiR (excitation/emission maxima ~748/780 nm). DiR emits outside the range of predominant tissue autofluorescence, thereby reducing background noise and improving signal-to-noise ratio, while its lipophilic nature ensures stable incorporation into the lipid bilayer of the vesicles, enabling accurate tracking of exogenously administered CAM in vivo. Both liposomes and CAM were labeled with DiR dye before administration. Mice received intravenous injections (100 µL) of free dye DiR, liposomes, or CAM, with each injection containing 1 × 10⁶ particles per 100 µL. Live images at multiple time points were acquired using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) (Caliper, USA). At 4, 12, and 24 h post-injection, mice were euthanized, and the fluorescence intensity in various organs was quantitatively analyzed to determine the systemic distribution of CAM. Tumor tissues were harvested, sectioned, and fixed, then incubated with 100 µL of DAPI (5 mg/mL) for 10 min in the dark to stain the nuclei. Samples were observed using confocal microscopy to confirm CAM localization within the tumor microenvironment. To investigate the biodistribution of MMNP in vivo, FD-LSC-1 xenograft-bearing mice were intravenously administered 100 µL of either LNP (100 nm) or MMNP, each containing 1 × 10⁶ particles per 100 µL. Live images were acquired at multiple time points using the IVIS imaging system (Caliper, USA), following the same procedure described above.

Photothermal effect of R848/GNP@MMNP and thermal stability of R848

To detect the photothermal effect of R848/GNP@MMNP, 1 mL of the R848/GNP@MMNP solution, with a GNP equivalent concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, was irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1 W/cm²) for 10 min. An infrared camera was used to record the temperature every 30 s to monitor the heating profile of the solution. The thermal stability of R848 was assessed using a reverse-phase ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) method. R848/GNP@MMNP solutions were first exposed to an 808 nm laser (1 W/cm²) until reaching final temperatures of 25 °C, 37 °C, 40 °C, 45 °C, 50 °C, or 60 °C. The samples were initially diluted in acetonitrile and subsequently in a solution composed of 10% acetonitrile and 90% H₂O. The prepared samples were then injected into a Waters C18 column, along with a series of standard injections prepared by diluting R848 in the same solution of 10% acetonitrile and 90% H₂O.

Drug loading efficiency and quantification of R848

Separation and quantitative analysis of R848 were conducted using a 3.5 µm Waters C18 column, with a mobile phase flow rate of 1.0 mL/min and a detection wavelength set at 227 nm. For sample preparation, a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube containing 30 µL of R848/GNP@MMNP was placed in a 70 °C oven to allow complete solvent evaporation. Subsequently, 300 µL of acetonitrile was added, and the sample was subjected to vortexing and ultrasonic dissolution. A 10 µL aliquot of the dissolved sample was diluted with 990 µL of acetonitrile. Then, 100 µL of the prepared sample was mixed with 100 µL of the internal standard working solution (50 ng/mL). Afterward, 1 µL aliquot of this final mixture was injected into the UPLC-MS/MS instrument for analysis.

The encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of R848/GNP@MMNP was calculated using the following equation: EE (%) = (R848 encapsulated in MMNP)/(Input R848) × 100% The loading capacity (LC%) of R848/GNP@MMNP was calculated using the following equation: LC% = (R848 encapsulated in MMNP)/(Total MMNP mass) × 100%

Optical imaging and quantification of R848 in vivo

Male BALB/c nude mice bearing FD-LSC-1 tumors (∼200 mm³) were intravenously injected with 100 μL of R848/GNP@RMNP or free GNP solution, both at a GNP-equivalent concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. Two hours post-injection, tumor sites were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1 W/cm²) for 10 min and imaged using an infrared camera to monitor the photothermal effect.

For pharmacokinetic analysis, male BALB/c nude mice bearing FD-LSC-1 tumors (∼200 mm³) were randomized into three groups and received intravenous injections of free R848, R848@RMNP, or R848/GNP@RMNP. Each group received an R848 dose of 1 mg/kg. Blood samples were collected via the jugular vein at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post-treatment. Subsequently, the blood samples were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 2 min to separate the plasma, which was then analyzed for R848 concentration using UHPLC, as described above. To assess the organ retention of R848, major organs were harvested 24 h post-treatment, followed by tissue homogenization and R848 extraction. The extracted samples were analyzed using UHPLC to determine the biodistribution and systemic clearance of R848.

Cellular targeting of R848/GNP@RMNP in vitro

To assess the cellular uptake of R848/GNP@RMNP by HNSCC tumor cells, FD-LSC-1 and CAL27 cell lines, 1 × 10⁵ tumor cells were seeded on glass coverslips and allowed to adhere. The cell membranes were stained with PKH26 (Red). The R848/GNP@RMNP (100 μg/mL), labeled with PKH67 (Green), was then added to the tumor cells for co-culture. After 12 h of incubation, the tumor cells were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min, and stained for the nuclei with spectral DAPI (Akoya Biosciences, USA). Fluorescent images of the tumor cells were captured using a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan) to observe the co-localization of the red membrane and green nanoparticle signals.

Additionally, flow cytometry analysis was used to quantitatively assess the cellular uptake of R848/GNP@RMNP. R848/GNP@RMNP were stained with PKH67 (Green). FD-LSC-1 and CAL27 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and allowed to adhere. After 12 h of incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove unbound nanoparticles. Then, FD-LSC-1 and CAL27 cells were detached from 6-well plates and analyzed using flow cytometry (BD FACSAria Fusion, USA). PBS was used as a blank control to assess the background fluorescence and ensure accurate measurement of nanoparticle uptake by the tumor cells.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity assay was performed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Beijing 4 A Biotech Co., Ltd., China). Briefly, FD-LSC-1 and CAL27 cells were seeded at a density of 1.0 × 10⁴ cells per well in 96-well plates. After a 12-h incubation with PBS, GNP, or R848/GNP@RMNP, the cells were exposed to 808 nm laser (1 W/cm²) for 10 min to activate the photothermal effect of the nanoparticles. After 24 h, the culture medium was discarded, and 100 μL of reagent (composed of 90 μL DMEM and 10 μL CCK-8 solution) was added to each well. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow for the conversion of the CCK-8 reagent, which produces a colorimetric change proportional to cell viability. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a POCH2 microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc., USA) to determine the relative cell viability.

R848/GNP@RMNPs activity on BMDCs

To isolate BMDCs, intact tibias were harvested from male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old). The tibias were first dipped in 70% ethanol for 5 min, followed by storage in RPMI cell culture medium (Gibco, USA) on ice. Both ends of each tibia were cut, and the bone marrow was flushed with 10 mL of RPMI medium using a syringe with a 23-gauge needle. Bone marrow cells were collected and centrifuged at 320 × g for 9 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended and filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer.

To assess cytokine release, BMDCs were treated with various concentrations of PBS, R848, GNP, R848/GNP, R848/GNP@RMNP without NIR, and R848/GNP@RMNP and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Following incubation, the culture supernatant was collected and then analyzed using ELISA kits for IL-6, TNFα, and IL-12p40 (Wuhan Fine Biotech Co., Ltd., Cat# EM0121, Cat# EM0183, Cat# EM1470).

For co-stimulatory marker characterization, BMDCs and tumor cells were assessed using a transwell co-culture system with 8-μm pore size membranes (BioVision, K906). Tumor cells (mouse oral squamous cell carcinoma, MOC1) were seeded in the bottom wells of the transwell plate and allowed to adhere. After incubation with PBS, R848, GNP, R848/GNP, and R848/GNP@RMNP for 12 h, cells were subjected to 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W/cm²) for 10 min, while R848/GNP@RMNP without NIR as control. BMDCs were subsequently counted, and 5 × 10⁵ cells per pore were seeded into the top wells for co-culture with the irradiated tumor cells. The co-culture was maintained for 48 h, after which BMDCs were collected for flow cytometry analysis. Briefly, BMDCs were carefully washed, scraped from the plates, and stained with Fixable Viability Zombie NIR (BioLegend, San Diego, CA; #423105) and various antibodies, including BV510-conjugated anti-mouse CD45 (30-F11, BioLegend, #103138), BB700-conjugated anti-mouse CD11b (M1/70, BD Bioscience; 1:400 dilution), BV650-conjugated anti-mouse CD11c (N418, BioLegend, #117339), PE-conjugated anti-mouse MHC II (M5/114.15.2, BioLegend, #107607), APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 (GL-1, BioLegend, #105011), and BV421-conjugated anti-mouse CD80 (16-10A1, BioLegend, #104725). Flow cytometry was performed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSAria Fusion, USA), and data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.9 software (FlowJo™, USA). For all primary antibodies, validation was either deduced from the manufacturer’s website or from cited publications.

DLN activation and tumor rechallenge in vivo

To evaluate the immunostimulatory effects of R848/GNP@RMNP in vivo, a tumor rechallenge model was established using mouse oral squamous cell carcinoma (MOC1) cells. In brief, 5 × 10⁴ MOC1 cells were subcutaneously injected into the shaved left dorsal region of C57BL/6 male mice. The mice were randomly divided into six groups. Once the tumors reached approximately 100 mm³, mice were intravenously administered the following treatments: PBS, R848, GNP, R848/GNP, R848/GNP@RMNP without NIR, and R848/GNP@RMNP, with equivalent GNP doses of 4 mg/kg and R848 doses of 1 mg/kg, respectively. At 6 h post-injection, mice were subjected to 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W/cm²). Each group received a total of three treatment sessions.

Following treatment, draining lymph nodes (DLNs) from the tumor-bearing side were harvested and processed into single-cell suspensions by shearing the tissues through a 70-µm cell strainer. The prepared DLN suspensions were analyzed using flow cytometry. Additionally, tumor tissues were collected for immunofluorescence detection of immune cells and serum inflammatory factor analysis.

In the rechallenge experiment, the remaining mice from each treatment group were subcutaneously rechallenged with 5 × 10⁴ MOC1 cells on day 14. The injections were made into the shaved right dorsal region of each mouse. Tumor sizes were measured using a micrometer caliper. At the treatment endpoint, tumor tissues from each group were collected and subjected to multiplex immunohistochemistry staining to analyze the infiltration of inflammatory cells.

Flow cytometry of DLNs

For flow cytometry analysis, freshly isolated DLNs were processed into a single-cell suspension and passed through a 70-µm cell strainer. Antitumor effect assessments in vivo

To evaluate the antitumor function of R848/GNP@RMNP in tumor-bearing mice, luc-B16-F10 cells (1 × 10⁶) or OVA-B16-F10 cells (1 × 10⁶) were subcutaneously injected into the shaved left dorsal region of C57BL/6 male mice. The mice were randomly divided into six groups. When the tumors reached approximately 100 mm³, the mice were intravenously administered one of the following treatments: PBS, R848, GNP, R848/GNP, R848/GNP@RMNP without NIR, and R848/GNP@RMNP at an equivalent GNP dose of 4 mg/kg and R848 doses of 1 mg/kg.

At 6 h post-injection, mice were subjected to 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W/cm²). Mice in each group were monitored to assess survival rates and tumor growth. Tumor sizes were measured using a micrometer caliper, and tumor volumes were calculated using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{Tumor\; Volume}}}=({{{\rm{Long\; Length}}}\times {{\rm{Short\; Length}}}}^{2})/2\,({{{\rm{mm}}}}^{3}).\,$$\end{document}Tumor Volume=(Long Length×Short Length2)/2(mm3).

For luciferase imaging, the IVIS200 instrument (Caliper Life Sciences, USA) was used. If any signs of morbidity were observed, the mice were euthanized immediately according to IACUC guidelines.

Immune fluorescence

Tumor samples from in vivo experiments were frozen in OCT and stored at −80 °C. Tumor sections of approximately 8 µm were then prepared and fixed with cold acetone for 15 min, followed by air drying for 30 min. The slides were incubated using 20% FBS in PBS for 1 h to block non-specific binding. Primary antibody staining was performed overnight at 4 °C in a humidified staining container using mouse anti-CD3 (GT0013, Invitrogen, MA5-31463) and rabbit anti-CD8 (EPR21769, Abcam, #ab217344) antibodies. On the following day, the slides were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h using goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 647, Abcam, #ab150079) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488, Abcam, #ab150113). Finally, nuclear staining was performed using Spectral DAPI (Akoya Biosciences, USA). Fluorescent images were obtained using a confocal microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Safety analysis

To determine the biodistribution in immune organs, free DiR dye, DiR-labeled LNP, and DiR-labeled RMNP (containing 1 × 10⁶ particles per 100 µL) were intravenously injected. Live images at multiple time points were acquired using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) (Caliper, USA). At 24 h post-injection, mice were euthanized, and the fluorescence intensity in the spleen and lymph nodes (inguinal and axillary) was quantitatively analyzed. Flow cytometry was used to analyze the proportion of cells in the spleen and lymph nodes that had phagocytosed LNP and RMNP. Briefly, spleens and lymph nodes were homogenized and passed through a 70-μm cell strainer. After antibody staining, the samples were analyzed on a flow cytometer. The following antibodies were used: Fixable Viability Zombie Aqua (BioLegend, #423101) (UV395 channel), BV510-conjugated anti-mouse CD45 (30-F11, BioLegend, #103138), BB700-conjugated anti-mouse CD11b (M1/70; BD Bioscience; 1:400 dilution), and BV650-conjugated anti-mouse CD11c (N418, BioLegend, #117339). The DiR signal was detected using the APC-Cy7 channel.

To determine the immunogenicity of RMNPs, PBS, LNP, and RMNP were injected into C57BL/6 mice every 2 days. Blood samples were collected after 7 days and subsequently analyzed using hematology and blood chemistry tests. The safety of RMNPs was further evaluated by examining the main organs via H&E staining.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using Graphpad Prism v9.0.2 and Excel 2019. The normal distribution of continuous variables was assessed by Student’s t test. Comparisons of three or more groups were performed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test or two-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. Where data showed a nonparametric distribution, a Kruskal–Wallis test was used, followed by post-hoc Dunn’s test. Descriptive statistics, statistical tests, and post-hoc tests for multiple comparisons are reported in each figure legend. The number of independent experiments is described in the figure legend.
