Gender differences in a causal model of risk factors underlying early alcohol sipping
Authors: Andrea Maxwell, Kathryn Cullen, Erich Kummerfeld, Rajita Sinha, Anna Zilverstand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly alcohol sipping with permission, here defined as tasting alcohol without necessarily taking a full drink by late childhood with parental permission, is an early marker of future problematic alcohol use. This study aimed to generate an initial data-driven causal model of the potential risk factors underlying early alcohol sipping and probe gender differences in this model.MethodsWe used deidentified data from the baseline visit of Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study (N = 9,253; 47.2% girls, ages 9–10 years; 76% White, 20% African-American, 8% Other, 7% Asian, 4% Native; 21% Hispanic); 1,266 (13.7%) in this sample endorsed early alcohol sipping by age 9–10 (40.9% girls). We included the top-ranked environmental, health, and psychological factors underlying early alcohol sipping identified among 2000 + variables in a previous study in the same data set. We applied Causal Discovery Analysis to generate combined (boys and girls) and gender-specific causal models to investigate potential causal links with early alcohol sipping. We evaluated model stability by bootstrapping.ResultsFirst, our causal model of early alcohol sipping in both boys and girls suggested that family-level factors directly linked to substance use (i.e., norms around alcohol use and maternal prenatal alcohol use) were causally linked to early alcohol sipping. Second, our causal model of sipping specific to girls suggested that suicidal ideation was causally linked to early alcohol sipping in girls only. Third, our causal model of sipping specific to boys suggested that early alcohol sipping was causally linked to sensation seeking and disliking school in boys only.ConclusionsThe current findings provide an initial model of important risk factors underlying the early onset of alcohol use. More broadly, the results suggest that the mechanisms previously linked to substance use in adolescence and adulthood can be traced back to childhood, and that gender differences are already present at this young age.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13034-026-01077-6.

Introduction

A growing body of work suggests that earlier age of alcohol use initiation predicts problematic alcohol use behavior later in life. Although the majority of this work studies the relationship between consuming full drinks of alcohol in adolescence and later alcohol-related problems, early alcohol sipping (defined in the present study as tasting alcohol with parental permission in childhood, without necessarily taking a full drink) also seems to be a developmentally informative marker of vulnerability to problematic drinking in adulthood [8, 16, 22, 47]. This is concerning, given the high prevalence of alcohol sipping by late childhood (i.e., by about age 10), with reports ranging from 17% − 39% prevalence [9, 10, 16, 49]. Jointly, these emerging findings suggest that late childhood may be an important timepoint of early intervention to reduce the risk of future problematic alcohol drinking.

A better understanding of the risk factors driving early onset of alcohol use in childhood is urgently needed to inform the development of early interventions. The identification of relevant risk factors, however, is challenging without an understanding of the complex interplay between the many factors underlying the early onset of alcohol use. Existing literature has primarily focused on the importance of environmental or family-level factors, demonstrating that access to alcohol within the home and parentally-approved alcohol sipping play a key role [7, 9, 10]. Additionally, we previously demonstrated that a wide array of health and psychological factors are also linked to early alcohol sipping [33]. Among other factors, our and others’ previous work support that early mental health challenges, such as suicidal ideation and other self-harm behaviors, are related to the early onset of alcohol use [1, 33]. This is particularly alarming, given that approximately 10% of children 12 years and younger endorse suicidal ideation [13]. Other individual-level psychological factors, such as sensation-seeking, may also predict alcohol use at a very young age [5, 16]. Importantly, these factors likely do not work independently from each other; meaningful clinical applications, therefore, necessitate an integrative approach to understanding risk [17]. A better understanding of both which factors predict risk and how they influence each other is therefore necessary to inform early intervention efforts.

Furthermore, little is known about gender differences in the risk factors underlying the early onset of alcohol use in childhood. There is evidence supporting the presence of gender differences in the onset of adolescent alcohol use [29]; whether these patterns emerge in childhood or later remains unknown. Indeed, prior research reports that although adolescent girls are more likely to report greater perceived parental monitoring [31] and greater disapproval of alcohol use than boys [32], girls are also more likely to be served alcohol at home than boys [46]. This pattern suggests that, at least in adolescence, there may be gender differences in the role of the home environment in early alcohol use. There also seem to be gender differences in the role of health and psychological factors in early alcohol use, although current work in childhood is, again, limited. Furthermore, a more comprehensive understanding of whether gender differences exist for other risk factors, and the interplay between these factors, is also lacking. In sum, although evidence suggests that there are gender differences in several risk factors for adolescent alcohol use, very little work has focused on whether these patterns present in childhood.

Here, we apply the machine learning approach Causal Discovery Analysis (CDA) to data from the Adolescent Brain and Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, which is a comprehensive, longitudinal dataset of 11,878 children [4], to generate a data-driven causal model of risk factors for early alcohol sipping with permission and evaluate potential gender differences. Specifically, we used CDA to generate a sparse causal model of the top-ranked environmental, health, and psychological factors that were identified as linked to early alcohol sipping with permission among 2000 + variables through a machine learning analysis in the same data set [33]. We employed CDA as a modelling approach, since this approach allows us to model the complex interplay between different potential risk factors. Through the generated causal models, we further explored whether gender differences in the potential factors underlying alcohol sipping emerge even in childhood. By doing so, we aim to generate testable hypotheses that may inform experimental designs in future work regarding interventions/prevention of childhood alcohol sipping.

Methods

Participants

We used deidentified data obtained from the ABCD Study, which is an ongoing, longitudinal study of 11,878 children enrolled at ages 9–11 years old across 21 sites in the United States (see Table 1 for details) [4]. The study collects a wide array of measures, including psychological, health, social, and environmental factors, brain function, and genetics, facilitating the generation of a multi-domain model of early alcohol sipping. The present analysis evaluates cross-sectional data from the baseline visit (Year 0; starting in 2017) from the Annual Release 4.0 (October 2021; 10.15154/1523041). Cross-sectional data was employed to identify potential risk factors underlying alcohol sipping in late childhood, since comprehensive research at this age is currently lacking. A centralized Institutional Review Board located at the University of California San Diego approved the ABCD study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants and their parents/guardians provided written assent/consent. For the present study, after data use permission was obtained, a protocol filed with the University of Minnesota Institutional Review Board met criteria for exemption.

Alcohol sipping measure

At the baseline study visit, children were asked whether they had ever heard of alcohol and other substances [23]. If they endorsed ever hearing of alcohol, they were then administered the iSay Sipping Inventory, an 8-item measure asking a variety of alcohol use initiation questions (i.e., whether they had ever sipped alcohol and the type of alcohol it was, to whom the drink belonged to, whether it was offered or taken without permission [i.e., furtively], whether they had ever sipped alcohol outside of a religious context, and, if so, at what age) [16]. We excluded children whose first sips were within a religious context (n = 639), accidental (n = 446), or furtive (n = 83) by age 9 to 10 years (n = 1,168) or if their self-reported age at first sip was inaccurate (i.e. they reported alcohol sip at age 11 but were age 10 at the interview (n = 6; 3 of these participants were accidental or furtive sippers). We therefore focused exclusively on children who sipped with permission given that parentally-approved early alcohol use is both a common yet separate behavioral phenotype than alcohol use without parental permission [7, 12, 40]. Participants were not required to be substance naïve and were included even if they reported continuing on to drink a full drink after taking initial sips (n = 24). Sipping was modelled as a binary outcome (had they ever sipped by the baseline visit), to assess which factors were linked to early onset of alcohol use (rather than its severity). The initial sample consisted of 10,707 children (42.08% girls), of whom 14% endorsed ever sipping alcohol by the baseline visit at ages 9 or 10 years (n = 1,498, 42.06% girls).

Factor selection and optimization

We included environmental, health, and psychological variables (e.g., features) that were previously identified as top-ranked contributing features to accurately classify children with early alcohol sipping among 2000 + initially included features [33]. The previous study used eXtreme Gradient Boosting [6] (XGBoost), a fast and scalable nonparametric machine learning approach to classification, in combination with the feature ranking tool Shapley Additive ExPlanations (SHAP) [25] to evaluate the unique and additive importance of each of the 2000 + included environmental, health, psychological, or brain structure and function features for classifying early alcohol sipping. We initially included the top-ranked (most important) 20 environmental, 20 health, and 20 psychological features from these previous analysis [33]. Shared family membership was controlled for in the previous analysis [33] and was therefore not included as an explicit variable. We further excluded all brain measures since our previously tested functional task fMRI, resting-state, and structural brain classification models performed only marginally better than chance [33]. Importantly, these previous analyses included both sum scores and item-level indices because XGBoost builds factor dependencies into the tree structure, therefore enabling the use of correlated factors. In the current analyses, both item-level and sum scores were therefore included.

In the current analysis, we optimized feature selection to reduce missingness by excluding features with > 25% missing data (i.e., ‘Deepening Voice’, ‘Transgender Identity’, ‘Bothered by Prodromal Psychosis’, ‘Vancouver Index of Acculturation’) (Supplemental Table S1). Next, to reduce collinearity and improve the stability of the causal modelling, we selected the highest-ranked features within each previously identified correlation cluster (Supplemental Figures S1-S3). To further optimize feature selection, we made the following adjustments: although ‘Weekend Screentime’ was not identified within a discrete cluster of the Environmental model, we included this feature in the present analysis to provide a comprehensive set of features (Supplemental Figure S1). Although ‘Acne’ was higher ranked within the ‘Early pubertal onset’ cluster of the Health model, we replaced this feature with ‘Height’ to reduce missingness (Supplemental Figure S2, Supplemental Table S1). ‘Past Self-injurious Behavior’ was ranked highest in the ‘Self-harm cluster’ of the Health model, but we retained both ‘Past Self-injurious Behavior’ and ‘Past Passive Suicidal Ideation’ given their clinical implications (Supplemental Figure S2). We included both ‘Easily Feels Tense’ and ‘Negative Urgency’ from the ‘Negative Urgency’ cluster of the Psychological model, as ‘Easily Feels Tense’ probes trait anxiety, which is separable from negative urgency (Supplemental Figure S3). Finally, although ‘Would like to ski fast’ was higher ranked within the ‘Sensation-Seeking’ cluster of the Psychological model, we replaced this feature with the ‘Sensation-Seeking Subscale’ score from the Urgency, Premeditation, Perseverance, Sensation-Seeking (UPPS) Scale for generalizability (Supplemental Figure S3). Therefore, we included 19 features in the present analysis, in addition to the endorsement (or not) of sipping (i.e., 20 total features). More feature details are available in Supplemental Table S2. A flow chart of the feature selection and optimization process is presented in Supplemental Figure S4. After adjusting for missingness and feature optimization, we conducted Little’s Test of Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) and found that the data was not missing at random (p < 0.05). We therefore conducted a listwise deletion of participants to retain only participants with complete data of the selected features. Our final analytic sample consists of 9,253 children (48.34% girls), 13.68% of whom endorsed early sipping (40.92% girls). The retained sample did not differ from the excluded sample (N = 1,454) regarding average age, proportion of girls, average total family income, number of participants who endorsed self-injurious behavior, proportion of Asian participants, and proportion of Hispanic participants. There were, however, significantly fewer participants who endorsed sipping, fewer who endorsed past passive suicidal ideation, more white participants, fewer Black participants, and fewer Native participants in the retained sample (Supplemental Table S3).

Causal discovery analysis

We applied the machine learning approach CDA to data from the ABCD Study to generate a data-driven causal model of the potential risk factors for early alcohol sipping and evaluate potential gender differences. CDA is a class of machine learning algorithms that estimate potential causal relations between features based on statistical relationships. Similar to traditional modeling approaches like structural equation modeling (SEM), CDA estimates directed relationships between multiple features. SEM, however, is restricted to testing a researcher-generated hypothesized model and therefore is limited to evaluating the relationships that have been pre-defined in the hypothesized model. In contrast, CDA harnesses a machine learning approach to generate a data-driven model of estimated causal relationships, evaluating all possible relationships between all included features without needing any priors with regards to the assumed model structure (although it is possible to restrict the model using prior knowledge, if appropriate) [45]. In CDA, the generated data-driven model is optimized based on the given data using a sequence of conditional independence tests. The term ‘cause’ here therefore refers to how the model predicts that intervening on certain features would change the probability distribution of other features [36]. To distinguish this from other forms of causality, such as temporal causality, we generally refer to causal relationships in the model as “causal links”, which can be direct or indirect.

In the current study, we applied CDA to generate a sparse causal model of the top-ranked environmental, health, and psychological factors that were identified as linked to early alcohol sipping among 2000 + variables through a machine learning analysis in the same dataset [33]. We used a specific form of CDA here, Greedy Fast Causal Inference (GFCI), which does not assume that all relevant causal features are included in the model (i.e., causal sufficiency) but can instead model unidentified latent features [34], making it particularly suited for sparse models. This approach is particularly advantageous when a relatively sparse model (e.g., only 20 features) is a good fit for the given data. Here, we used GFCI to generate a model of causal relationships between the 20 selected features, including whether the participant endorsed early alcohol sipping. In short, GFCI first estimates a model (consisting of features that are linked by ‘edges’) that optimizes goodness-of-fit while assuming causal sufficiency (that is, it assumes that all possible common causes of two features are measured in the data and therefore there are no unmeasured or latent confounders). If unmeasured confounders do exist, this step will add extra edges erroneously (to account for the additional dependencies). To correct for these errors, GFCI’s second step (a) performs a series of conditional independence tests to check for such erroneously added edges, and (b) modifies the edge marks (e.g. arrowheads and tails) on existing edges, to account for the possibility of latent confounding (i.e. possible violations of causal sufficiency). For example, unmeasured confounding could result in the first algorithm connecting feature X to feature Y, but the conditional independence tests might find that X and Y are actually independent of each other and remove the incorrectly added edge. Similarly, the model learned in the first step might indicate that X causes Y, but the second step might find that it can’t be ruled out whether some unmeasured feature L actually causes both X and Y instead, and so it replaces the edge from X to Y with a different type of edge that indicates possible latent confounding. The final model is the estimated causal structure that best fits the sampled data (see Supplemental Figure S5 for more detail). The characteristics of each edge (i.e., direction, weight, color) in the final model indicate the relationship between the features it connects (Fig. 1), including the presence (or possible presence) of unmeasured confounders for this edge.

The present analysis employed default parameters from Tetrad version 6.7.0. Specifically, Fisher Z tests were used for conditional independence testing, and the statistical p-value threshold parameter (alpha) was set to the default value of 0.01. A penalty discount value of 1 was used to generate the Bayesian Information Criterion score, which was used to iteratively test model fit while edges were removed based on conditional independence testing. This penalty discount value was chosen to capture the breadth of possible causal links between features, especially given the exploratory component of this study. The following prior knowledge additionally informed ‘forbidden’ edges (edges that the model was not allowed to generate): First, we defined that ‘Maximum drinks while pregnant’ and ‘Parental education’ could not be caused by any of the other features, as these two features preceded all other features in time. Second, we defined that ‘Height’ could only potentially be caused by ‘Maximum drinks while pregnant’ and ‘Parental education.’ Third, we defined that ‘Past passive suicidal ideation’ and ‘Past self-injurious behavior’ could only be caused by ‘Maximum drinks while pregnant’, ‘Parental education’, or and/or ‘Height’, as only these features preceded past (not current) suicidal ideation/behavior in time. Fourth, all remaining features could be caused by any other feature, as these were current reports. Notably, we did not specify that any features had to necessarily cause alcohol sipping; all edges toward alcohol sipping were discovered by the algorithm (Supplemental Figure S6).

Model stability and model fit

We interrogated model stability by using a bootstrapping procedure that consisted of 100 bootstraps using 100% of the sample size [20]. Supplemental Tables S4-S6 list the proportion of bootstraps that included each edge. Bootstrapped models show only stable edges, defined as directed edges that are in at least 50% of the bootstraps. The quality of the edge (e.g., weight, color) in the bootstrapped model represents the most frequently discovered edge quality. In this way, we present the most stable causal model following subsampling (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Using the lavaan package (version 0.6–15) in R (version 4.3.0), we estimated the effect sizes of the edges in the initial causal model by fitting a linear model to the generated causal model after scaling the raw data. In this SEM, edges connecting to possible latent features were modelled as indirect covariations. We assessed model fit by using the indices Comparative Fit Index (CFI) and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). Correlation coefficients between all features are available in Supplemental Tables S7-S12.

Gender analysis

Gender was identified here using parent-report and binarized into boy or not-boy. N = 13 participants (0.014% of the entire sample) were identified as neither a boy or girl by parent-report and, because of the binarization, were grouped together with girls. To investigate gender differences in the causal model leading to sipping, we first generated the causal model with the full sample including both boys and girls as above. We then generated two separate causal models, for boys and girls respectively, using identical parameters and subsampling methods. Just as we did for the combined model, we estimated the effect sizes of the edges by fitting SEMs to both these gender-specific models. Gender differences were quantitatively evaluated by performing 2-sample test for equality of proportions with continuity correction, correcting for multiple comparisons, on the resampling frequency in boys compared to girls. In the discussion, we focused on edges that differed significantly between girls versus boys (specifically, that were present in one gender but absent in the other).

Sensitivity analyses

Finally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of the applied model constraints. Specifically, we regenerated all three causal models (combined, boys only, and girls only), eliminating all model constraints except for the following two constraints: (1) no feature in the model could cause ‘Maximum drinks while pregnant’ and (2) no feature in the model could cause ‘Parental education’. In this ‘low constraint model’ all other features could cause each other (see Supplemental Figure S7 for a visual summary).

Results

Modeled causal links to and from early alcohol sipping in both boys and girls

The initial causal model generated using the combined sample of both boys and girls modeled several direct or indirect causal links to early alcohol sipping with good fit (RMSEA = 0.027, CFI = 0.90) (Supplemental Figure S8). First, in the most stable model (which includes only edges that survive bootstrapping), easier access for the child to alcohol was directly and unconfounded causally linked to early alcohol sipping (β = 0.16, 95% CI [0.10, 0.18]) (Fig. 2). Second, the model estimated a causal link from higher parental education to easier access for the child to alcohol (β = 0.23, 95% CI [0.20, 0.25), with potential unmeasured confounders for this link. Third, higher number of maternal maximum drinks while pregnant was causally linked to easier child access to alcohol (β = 0.15, 95% CI [0.10, 0.17), with potential unmeasured confounders. Fourth, a higher number of maternal maximum drinks while pregnant was causally linked to past passive child suicidal ideation (β = 0.04, 95% CI [0.02, 0.06), with potential unmeasured confounders. Fifth, past passive child suicidal ideation itself was causally linked to early alcohol sipping without confounding (β = 0.10, 95% CI [0.08, 0.12).. Sixth, in the most stable model, early alcohol sipping itself had an unconfounded causal link to increased sensation-seeking (β = 0.09, 95% CI [0.07, 0.11] (Fig. 2).

Modeled causal links to and from early alcohol sipping in boys

The generated causal model for the subsample of boys demonstrated a fair to good fit (RMSEA = 0.030, CFI = 0.86) (Supplemental Figure S9) and was very similar to the model using the combined sample. First, like that of the combined model, this most stable (bootstrapped) model estimated that easier access to alcohol for the child was directly and unconfounded causally linked to early alcohol sipping (β = 0.16, 95% CI [0.013, 0.19] (Fig. 3). Second, as in the combined model, higher parental education was causally linked to easier access of the child to alcohol (β = 0.22, 95% CI [0.019, 0.25], with potential unmeasured confounders. Third, the boys only causal model estimated an additional direct causal link between higher parental education and early alcohol sipping (β = 0.11, 95% CI [0.08, 0.14]), with potential unmeasured confounders. Fourth, as in the combined model, higher number of maternal maximum drinks while pregnant was estimated to be causally linked to increased child access to alcohol (β = 0.15, 95% CI [0.12, 0.18]), with potential unmeasured confounders. Fifth and sixth, this most stable model in boys only estimated that sipping itself was directly and unconfounded causally linked to both increased sensation-seeking (β = 0.09, 95% CI [0.06, 0.12]), and less liking of school (β = -0.08, 95% CI [-0.11,- 0.05]) (Fig. 3). The bootstrapped proportions for both of these boy-specific edges were statistically different compared to the girls (p < 0.001) (Supplemental Table S13).

Modeled causal links to and from early alcohol sipping in girls

The causal model generated from only girls was again similar to the combined boys and girls model (Supplemental Figure S10) with fair to good fit (RMSEA = 0.031, CFI = 0.85). The most stable model for the girls included the following causal links (Fig. 4). First, the girls only model retained the direct and unconfounded causal link from easier access to early alcohol sipping (β = 0.16, 95% CI [0.13, 0.19]). Second, as in the previous models, the girls only model estimated a causal link from higher parental education to easier access for the child to alcohol (β = 0.20, 95% CI [0.17, 0.23]), with potential unmeasured confounders. Third, as in previous models, higher number of maternal maximum drinks while pregnant was estimated to be causally linked to easier child access to alcohol (β = 0.16, 95% CI [0.13, 0.18]), with potential confounders. Fourth, different from the previous models, there was an additional estimated causal link between higher parental education and higher maternal maximum drinks while pregnant (β = 0.11, 95% CI [0.08, 0.14]) for girls only, with potential confounders. Fifth, as in the boys only causal model, the girls only causal model estimated a direct causal link between higher parental education and early alcohol sipping (β = 0.09, 95% CI [0.06, 0.12), with potential unmeasured confounders. Sixth, and most important, the girls only model estimated a causal link from past passive child suicidal ideation to earlier alcohol sipping (β = 0.09, 95% CI [0.06, 0.12]), potentially confounded by unmeasured features. The bootstrapped proportion for this girl-specific link was significantly different from the boys model (p < 0.001) (Supplemental Table S13). Finally, again unlike the boys only model, the girls only model did not include any causal links from sipping to any other feature (p < 0.001) (Supplemental Table S13). A summary of the findings from all three causal models is provided in Fig. 5.

Sensitivity analyses

The sensitivity analyses with fewer model constraints confirmed the previous results (Supplemental Table 14). The modeled causal link from access to alcohol to sipping was retained in the combined, boys only, and girls only samples. Similarly, the estimated causal link from past passive suicidal ideation to sipping remained in girls, and the estimated links from sipping to higher sensation-seeking and more disliking of school remained in boys. No other causal links survived bootstrapping.

Discussion

Here, we used Causal Discovery Analysis to generate a data-driven causal model of empirically selected potential risk factors of early alcohol use. Our study has several strengths, including (1) the use of a very large, rich dataset of childhood factors, (2) application of causal discovery analysis to generate, for the first time, a data-driven (rather than researcher-generated) model of early alcohol use in childhood that also explores gender differences, and (3) the use of already empirically-selected features to include in the models. By harnessing these strengths, we have started to develop a causal model of the most important early environmental, health, and psychological risk factors of early onset of alcohol use. Additionally, we evaluated if and how gender differences were present during childhood. We found that for both boys and girls, easy access to alcohol, higher parental education, and higher levels of maternal prenatal alcohol use were causally linked to increased likelihood of early alcohol sipping. Second, we found a causal link between past passive suicidal ideation and early alcohol sipping in girls only. Third, in boys but not girls, early alcohol sipping was causally linked to higher levels of sensation seeking and disliking school, suggesting a causal link between early alcohol use and a need for stimulation.

Our findings provide preliminary evidence for a causal link between family-level environmental factors and early onset of alcohol use for both genders, specifically implicating a causal role of easy access to alcohol, higher parental education, and higher maternal prenatal alcohol use on early alcohol sipping. Broadly, these results confirm the importance of family-level factors directly linked to substance use (i.e., norms around alcohol use and maternal alcohol use), while demonstrating that other family-related factors (i.e., parental sensitivity, caregiver somatic symptoms) were not causally linked to early alcohol use. These findings add to the robust body of literature evidencing the importance of parenting practices, family norms, and parental substance use on early alcohol use initiation [7, 9, 10, 33] and extend previous findings by providing evidence that these substance use related family-level factors are causally linked to early onset of alcohol use. The current findings are also in line with work suggesting that parental alcohol-related modeling, especially provision of alcohol within the home and lenient alcohol-related family rules, are some of the most robust predictors of adolescent alcohol use, with growing research suggesting that these parental effects exert an influence on childhood sipping as well [7, 15, 18, 43, 44, 48]. It is important to note, however, that because we restricted the sample to sipping with parental permission, access to alcohol is somewhat inherent to the outcome. In addition, we found that higher parental education was causally linked to permissive rules around alcohol use. This is in line with previous work positing that affluent youth may be particularly vulnerable to some maladaptive behaviors, namely substance use, depression, and anxiety, relative to low- and middle-income youth due to elevated parental pressure to achieve and isolation [26]. In summary, our current findings highlight the importance of parent behavior around alcohol use in scaffolding their children’s future behaviors, including in shaping risk for early onset of alcohol use.

In addition to these family-level environmental factors, we also found causal influences of individual-level health and psychological risk factors on early onset of alcohol use, with important gender differences even at this early age. Specifically, we found that passive suicidal ideation evidenced a robust causal link to early alcohol sipping in girls, but not boys. It is well-established that girls and women have higher past and lifetime prevalence of suicidal ideation between ages of 12–24 years [41]. These patterns have generally been examined exclusively in adolescents and adults, although there is a growing interest in childhood suicidal behavior [13, 19, 24]. Our findings suggest that gendered patterns causally linking suicidal ideation to alcohol use are already present in childhood and posit that suicidal ideation may be a risk factor for early onset of alcohol use in childhood in girls, which is a novel finding. A previous preliminary report using cross-sectional ABCD baseline data found that lifetime alcohol use increased the odds of classification of suicidality (versus not) and that this effect was stronger in boys [1]. The underlying hypothesis of this prior study was that higher levels of low-level alcohol use would cause suicidality. Our data-driven causal modeling approach, however, demonstrates that the directionality of this effect may also be the reverse. Indeed, our model suggests a phenotype characterized by higher levels of suicidality potentially causing early onset of alcohol use in girls. Overall, our findings strikingly parallel both the adolescent and the adult literature, which has established that high levels of negative emotionality can drive alcohol use [11, 29, 37]. Previous research has also shown that negative emotionality plays a larger role in driving substance use in women versus men [14, 37], linked to underlying neurobiological differences [28]. Here, we establish for the first time that these mechanisms are already present in childhood.

In addition to the identification of risk factors of early onset of alcohol use, we also found gendered causal links between early alcohol use and other behaviors, specifically a causal link between early onset of alcohol use and disliking school and higher sensation seeking in boys only. Substantial work has found that positive school engagement is protective against future alcohol use in adolescence, with more limited work also reporting the reverse effect, that is, that higher alcohol use also predicts less positive school engagement and lower academic performance [2, 35, 42]. Similarly, the relationship between sensation-seeking behaviors and substance use has been extensively investigated, with some literature describing sensation-seeking as an antecedent rather than a consequence of alcohol use [27, 30, 38]. However, there are also sparse reports on a reverse relationship in young adults. A prior study in a sample of college students found that heavy drinking predicted increases in impulsivity and sensation seeking, even after accounting for trait effects and social behavior [39]. In addition, other studies have suggested that higher disinhibition may have a reciprocal effect on alcohol quantity in young adults [21]. Here, we found evidence that onset of alcohol use in childhood may be causally linked to increased sensation-seeking. Importantly, our current finding demonstrates that these mechanisms are already at play in childhood, and hence at a much younger age than previously considered, consistent with the literature on early sipping as a marker of vulnerability for later substance use [7, 16, 47]. In addition, we observed this effect only in boys, suggesting that gendered mechanisms underlying substance use exist as early as in childhood. Previous studies have reported gender differences in adolescence [3] and adulthood [37], but not childhood. Our findings therefore evidence for the first time that these gender differences are already present in childhood.

The interpretation of our findings should account for the following limitations. First, we selectively investigated children that sipped alcohol with parental permission, excluding the potentially most vulnerable group of children who used alcohol without parental permission. Second, as we were interested in the binary outcome of whether a child had sipped or not, we did not account for sipping frequency. The causal models presented here are therefore specific to risk factors of early onset of alcohol use, not of early alcohol use severity. Third, our findings are model-specific. The analyzed sample demonstrated demographic differences from the sample excluded for missing data, including fewer individuals who endorsed sipping and suicidal ideation as well as racial differences. Future work would need to model the same (or similar) features in different samples to better understand the generalizability of these findings to the larger population. Fourth, the current modeling analysis was by no means expansive since many important factors were not explicitly modelled. Fifth, the current analyses used cross-sectional data from the baseline collection of the ABCD Study. Temporal directionality of the causal links is therefore estimated from these data; the absence of measured temporality for many of the included features is a limitation of this study. Again, because of these constraints, these estimated causal structures are model-dependent. That is, causal structures generated from other samples or using a different array of features (e.g., sipping severity) could potentially estimate different causal links. We do, however, believe that this analysis can nonetheless demonstrate two important general findings. First, we demonstrate that the mechanisms underlying onset of alcohol use seem to mirror the mechanisms underlying substance use in adolescence and adulthood. Second, we demonstrate that gender differences in these mechanisms are present even at this early age. In addition to these general findings, the presented causal models generate testable hypotheses for future studies probing causes and consequences of early alcohol sipping. For example, our model estimates that passive suicidal ideation may have a causal effect on sipping in girls. Accordingly, future experimental work could test whether specific intervention methods for suicidal ideation reduces early alcohol sipping in girls. We further found that alcohol sipping itself may exert a causal effect on disliking school in boys. Future research may test whether interventions that delay the onset of sipping in boys leads to an improvement in their affinity with school. These are just two examples of the way in which the presented causal models can provide empirical rationales for testable hypotheses in future work.

Conclusion

In conclusion, a growing body of work suggests that early alcohol sipping in childhood is an important developmental precursor to problematic alcohol use in both adolescence and adulthood. Our current findings confirm an important role of family-level factors directly linked to substance use as a risk factor for early onset of alcohol use in both genders, underscoring the importance of developing early interventions at the family level. For example, psychoeducation for parents of young children on the importance of parental modelling may be a targetable, family-oriented prevention strategy. We further found that passive suicidal ideation may be a risk factor of early onset of alcohol use in girls, which could motivate regular mental health screenings even at this young age. Finally, we found an estimated causal link from early onset of alcohol use to increased sensation seeking and disliking school in boys only. Taken together, our findings suggest two important general conclusions. First, mechanisms underlying substance use in adolescence and adulthood may already be at play during childhood, and second, gender-specific pathways to later problematic substance use seem to be present even at this very young age. Importantly, these findings also provide empirical rationale for a wide array of testable hypotheses regarding both the causes and consequences of alcohol sipping in boys and girls.
