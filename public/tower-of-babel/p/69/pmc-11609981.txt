Impact of early detection and steroid treatment on fetal ventricular heart rate and pacemaker implantation in anti‐Ro/SSA positive congenital heart block
Authors: Sven‐Erik Sonesson, Aurelie Ambrosi, Felicia Nordenstam, Håkan Eliasson, Marie Wahren‐Herlenius
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe investigated the effects of timing of detection and transplacental fluorinated steroid treatment on ventricular heart rate (HR) and age at pacemaker implantation in fetal third‐degree atrioventricular block (AVB).Material and MethodsTwenty‐five of 31 fetuses diagnosed with Ro/SSA autoantibody‐positive AVB II‐III at our tertiary fetal cardiology center (2000–2020) and AVB III as final feto‐neonatal outcome were reviewed.ResultsAVB was detected approximately 5 weeks earlier in pregnancy if followed in a surveillance program compared to cases referred from primary care for bradycardia (20.6 [2.3] [mean (SD)] vs. 25.4 [3.2] weeks, p = 0.001). AVB detected before 24 weeks had higher HR than those detected later in gestation (63.3 [6.9] vs. 57.2 [6.9] bpm, p = 0.042), with a larger proportion having HR >60 bpm (80% vs. 33%, p = 0.041). The 17/25 cases that received treatment with fluorinated steroid were diagnosed earlier in gestation, with higher HR at diagnosis (61.7 [7.1] vs. 54.7 [6.3] bpm, p = 0.026), 1–2 weeks after diagnosis/treatment start, and before birth (65.4 [12.4] vs. 54.9 [5.7] bpm, p = 0.030) than untreated cases. Overall, 11 cases were commenced on betamimetics: three at diagnosis and eight at or after the examination made 1–2 weeks after diagnosis/treatment start, without any HR improvement. Two of 24 surviving babies were born preterm, and 4/24 received a neonatal pacemaker. Age at pacemaker implantation correlated significantly with HR before birth (Spearman R 0.57, p = 0.004), and fetuses with HR >60 bpm had a higher rate of pacemaker‐free survival at three (90% vs. 40%, p = 0.018) and 12 months of age (80% vs. 13%, p = 0.002). The same trend was observed in pacemaker‐free survival at 3 months of age in fluorinated steroid‐treated compared to untreated cases (71% vs. 38%, ns).ConclusionsOur data confirm that AVB III detected earlier in gestation have a higher HR, and suggest that this higher HR can be successfully maintained to the end of gestation in cases treated with fluorinated steroids. Fetuses with HR >60 bpm before birth had a lower rate of pacemaker implantation at 3 and 12 months of age.Keywords: anti‐Ro/SSA, congenital heart block, fluorinated steroid, heart rate, pacemaker, treatment

Key message

Early detection of fetal third‐degree atrioventricular block and early initiation of transplacental treatment with fluorinated steroids may maintain a higher heart rate to the end of gestation and delay pacemaker implantation.

INTRODUCTION

Immune‐mediated congenital heart block (CHB) is a rare, life‐threatening disease that may develop in fetuses exposed to maternal anti‐Ro/SSA antibodies. 1 , 2 , 3 The women are commonly diagnosed with SLE or Sjögren's disease. 4 CHB is most frequently diagnosed at 20–24 weeks of gestation by the severe cardiac manifestation, a complete third‐degree atrioventricular block (AVB), reported to have a 10%–25% feto‐neonatal mortality, 1 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 a need of neonatal pacemaker (PM) implantation in 35%–50% of the survivors, 5 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 12 and a 14%–19% risk of postnatal cardiomyopathy. 6 , 8 Although the pathogenesis of CHB is not fully elucidated, it is known to involve an inflammatory process in and beyond the atrioventricular node, 13 , 14 , 15 making it rational to use transplacental steroid treatment to try to reduce the antibody‐ and inflammation‐mediated cardiac damage. A majority of studies have however failed to detect any protective treatment effect of fluorinated steroids (FS) on either survival or the need for PM treatment. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Nevertheless, two more recent studies have reported a better one‐year survival rate without cardiac transplantation in dexamethasone‐treated (89%) compared to untreated cases (68%), 10 and a better neonatal survival rate (93% vs. 77%–84%) and lower incidence of postnatal cardiomyopathy (3% vs. 19%–29%) when compared with inconsistently treated patient cohorts with similar length of postnatal follow‐up. 12 Of note, given the rarity of CHB, almost all these studies were retrospective and multicenter, and the vast majority of CHB cases were referred for bradycardia with established AVB III, with an unknown time delay from AVB development to initiation of FS treatment.

Assuming that CHB develops progressively from a lower degree of AVB to AVB III, 16 prospective surveillance studies based on weekly fetal monitoring by Doppler echocardiography have been conducted to assess whether early detection of fetal AVB and FS treatment could restore normal conduction and/or prevent progression to complete AVB. These studies revealed that AVB III can develop within days of a normal AV conduction time and mostly remain refractory to FS treatment, making timely treatment initiation challenging. 17 , 18 , 19 The rapid transition to AVB III probably explains why AVB II is a rare diagnosis. Interestingly, we and other investigators have still observed restoration of 1:1 AV conduction during FS treatment of AVB II. 5 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 In addition, our own previous experience of three fetuses in whom FS treatment was started within 1 week after AVB III development suggests that early detection and treatment may still offer some benefit in cases with AVB III. 19 In that study, the three cases starting FS treatment within 1 week of an observed normal conduction responded with a higher ventricular heart rate (HR). They did not require PM implantation until a later age compared to the other two AVB III cases in the same surveillance study in whom FS treatment was started later. 19

Here, we present observations from a larger cohort of 25 cases with a final fetal diagnosis of AVB III, comparing fetal and postnatal outcomes in fetuses treated with FS or not, soon after AVB development or not, to add to the limited experience and continued discussion on how to best manage this rare condition.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Participants and data collection

Cases of CHB were prospectively followed in a single tertiary fetal cardiology referral center (Stockholm, Sweden), either as part of a surveillance program of anti‐Ro52 antibody‐positive pregnancies, including weekly scans between 18 and 24 weeks, 19 or following detection of bradycardia during routine prenatal care. The time of detection in pregnancies not under surveillance was accordingly due to timing of midwifery surveillance of low‐risk pregnancies, with an unknown delay from AVB development to its detection. From 2000 to 2020, 32 fetuses were diagnosed with isolated AVB II or AVB III at our center. All women were positive for anti‐Ro52 antibodies except for one woman who was negative for anti‐Ro52, ‐Ro60 and ‐La antibodies and excluded from the study. The fetus of this woman was diagnosed with bradycardia and normal AV conduction at 22 weeks, indirect signs of a long AVB I at 31 weeks, AVB II at 35 weeks, and finally AVB III at birth.

Since 2000, treatment with FS was initiated with the intention to prevent progression from AVB II to complete AVB, or to reverse AVB III in surveillance cases with a known observation of 1:1 AV conduction no more than 1 week earlier. The indication for FS treatment was extended during the study period to also include AVB III cases with a HR <50–55 bpm and/or endocardial fibroelastosis (EFE), suggesting a poor prognosis, and later also AVB III cases detected ≤25 weeks, with the intention to preserve a higher ventricular HR. We routinely used betamethasone 4 mg/day for 4–6 weeks, subsequently reduced to 2 mg/day at 30 weeks' gestation and withdrawn completely at or shortly after birth. Three cases were, however, started on 6 mg/day for the first 3–7 days. Betamimetics were considered in case of a HR below 50–55 bpm. Based on our experience that PM implantation during the first months of life carried a significantly increased risk of a new intervention, our clinical practice was to delay PM implantation beyond 3 months of age, as long as the infant stayed asymptomatic and had a HR >50–55 bpm. 26

To study the effects of fetal surveillance and steroid treatment on HR and age at PM implantation in cases with a final fetal diagnosis of AVB III, we re‐analyzed medical records and digitally stored echocardiographic examinations (Syngo US Workplace, Siemens Medical Solutions, Mountain View, CA, USA). Heart rate was calculated from 3 to 5 consecutive systolic arterial flow profiles on stable Doppler tracings without signs of fetal movement or breathing. The effect of FS treatment was analyzed by comparing fetal examinations made at AVB III diagnosis, 1–2 weeks later, and before birth. This last intrauterine examination was preferred to postnatal electrocardiogram (ECG) as we had observed HR to be temporarily decreased during the first 2–3 days after birth with a subsequent improvement during the following days. A second reason was that the ECG procedure was considered more prone to affect the activity and HR of the newborn baby compared to the fetus during echocardiography.

The study was approved by the regional ethics committee in Stockholm and the participating women gave informed consent. Five of the seven AVB III cases detected within our surveillance program have been previously reported in detail and the outcome of the majority of cases has been briefly presented in a short correspondence. 19 , 20

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistica 13.0 (Dell Inc., Tulsa, OK, USA) and Sigmaplot 13.0 (Systat Software, San Jose, CA, USA). Groups with skewness and kurtosis within ±1.0 and homoscedasticity (Levene's test) were compared using t‐test and repeated measures ANOVA. Groups with departures from the assumption of normality or uniformity of variances were compared using Mann–Whitney U‐test, Wilcoxon matched pairs test, and Spearman rank order correlations. Survival and freedom from PM were assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Categorical data were analyzed using Fisher exact test. A p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics at diagnosis

Twenty‐five of 31 anti‐Ro52 antibody‐exposed fetuses diagnosed with CHB in our fetal cardiology center had AVB III as final fetal outcome and were included in the present analysis (Figure 1). The remaining six cases were born with 1:1 AV conduction following transplacental FS treatment. These six cases are thus not included in the present study and have not progressed to AVB III during 4–21 years of postnatal follow‐up.

Cases of third‐degree atrioventricular block and treatment course. Of the 25 cases with AVB III, seven were diagnosed within our surveillance program, approximately 5 weeks earlier than the 18 cases referred for bradycardia (Table 1). HR at AVB III diagnosis was numerically higher in surveillance than referral cases, and the proportion of fetuses with a HR >60 bpm tended to be higher in cases detected during surveillance, 86% vs 39%. However, neither of these observations reached statistical significance (Table 1). Overall, 10 cases were diagnosed with AVB III before 24 weeks of gestation and had a higher HR than those detected later in pregnancy, with a larger proportion of cases having a HR >60 bpm, 80% vs 33% (Table 1). Six fetuses had EFE at diagnosis, whereof two in combination with pericardial effusion (Figure 1). In all, five fetuses had pericardial effusion of minor degree, and in combination with a minor degree of pleural effusion in one case. Ascites was not observed in any case. The nine fetuses with EFE and/or pericardial effusion were detected at a gestational age and with a HR that did not deviate from the other fetuses (Table 1).

Transplacental treatment with steroids and betamimetics and fetal heart rate

Seventeen of the 25 cases with AVB III were commenced on FS with the intention to improve AV conduction and/or limit cardiac inflammatory damage to maintain ventricular HR and avoid fetal demise (Figure 1). Treatment was started at diagnosis, which meant before or within 1 week after AVB III development in seven fetuses (treatment group “FSD≤1W”): two had 2:1 AVB II, one AVB I and EFE, and four had documented 1:1 AV conduction at the previous weekly surveillance visit. The delay between AVB III development and treatment initiation was considered unknown in 10 cases (treatment group “FSDU”): eight were referred with already established AVB III, one surveillance case had AVB III at the first examination, and another surveillance case had missed weekly visits and had last been examined 3 weeks before AVB III was diagnosed. The remaining eight fetuses did not receive treatment with FS (treatment group “FSNO”): one of them developed cardiomyopathy with a dilated hypertrophic heart and died in utero at gestational week 36 (Figure 2). Individual details are presented in a supplementary table (Table S1).

Fetuses in group FSD≤1W were diagnosed 5 weeks earlier and with higher HR than those in the FSNO group (Table 1, Figure 3A). HR increased during FS treatment at a dose of 4–6 mg/day in the FSD≤1W group, and 1–2 weeks after the AVB III diagnosis/treatment start, the HR remained significantly higher than in the FSNO group. Shortly before birth, at a time when the dose of FS had been decreased to 0–2 mg/day for several weeks, mean HR in the FSD≤1W group had not changed much from the previous examination. However, there was a considerable individual variation and no longer any statistically significant difference from cases in the FSNO group. Fetuses in the FSDU group were diagnosed 4 weeks earlier than FSNO fetuses (Table 1), with a mean HR between that of the other two groups at all three examinations (Figure 3A). HR was maintained throughout the rest of the pregnancy, with an increased individual variation and a small increase in HR at the examination shortly before birth. The only two fetuses who were exposed to hydroxychloroquine belonged to the FSD≤1W group and had a HR of 50, 55, 54 and 62, 66 and 65 bpm at diagnosis, 1–2 weeks later and before birth, respectively.

Overall, 11 cases were commenced on betamimetics: three at AVB III diagnosis and eight at or after the examination 1–2 weeks later (Figure 3B). HR remained unchanged, and lower than in untreated fetuses, following betamimetics initiation. Only one of 10 fetuses with HR >60 bpm before birth was found among the treated. A combination of betamimetics and FS was given in eight cases: 3/7 in the FSD≤1W group, 5/10 in the FSDU group (Figure 3A). Only one case was started on both drugs at the time of diagnosis and had a HR of 53 and 56 bpm at the first and follow‐up exam, respectively. The other seven were started on betamimetics after a poor response to FS treatment was observed at follow‐up. This clinical approach most probably explains why cases not receiving betamimetics seem to have a better outcome (Figure 3B). The lower HR in cases given betamimetics most likely also explains the increased significance of FS treatment on HR when including both drugs in a multivariable analysis. As we could not see any improvement of the lower HR in cases following betamimetics initiation, this treatment was disregarded in our downstream analyses, to improve the power of our analyses regarding FS treatment.

Overall, the 17 cases treated with FS (FSYES) were diagnosed 4 weeks earlier and had a higher HR at AVB III diagnosis than fetuses who were not treated (Table 1, Figure 4A). Ventricular HR remained unchanged and higher than in the FSNO group throughout pregnancy, despite a reduction of the FS dosage from 4.4 (0.8) (mean [SD]) mg/day, at treatment start to 1.1 (0.7) mg/day before delivery. There was also a trend for a higher proportion of cases with HR >60 bpm before birth among the FS treated compared to untreated cases, 9/17 (53%) vs 1/8 (13%), that did not reach statistical significance. ECG recorded during one of the first postnatal days did not demonstrate any HR difference between the FS treated and untreated infants (61.1 [10.2] vs. 59.0 [6.9] bpm). Three infants had a HR <50 bpm, whereof two in the FSYES group.

Incidence and timing of pacemaker treatment

Four infants required PM in the first month of life, another five between 1 and 3 months, and an additional five between 3 and 12 months. Age at PM implantation correlated with HR 1–2 weeks after diagnosis, before birth (Spearman R = 0.57, p = 0.004) and on ECG (Spearman R = 0.67, p < 0.001), but not with HR at diagnosis of AVB III (Table 2). Cases with HR >60 bpm before birth had a higher rate of PM‐free survival at 3 months, 90% vs 40% (p = 0.018), and 12 months, 80% vs 13% (p = 0.002) of age (Table 2).

The median age at PM implantation was 4 months higher in FSYES than FSNO cases, and the rate of infants alive without PM at one and 3 months of age tended to be higher in those exposed to FS, 88% vs 63% and 71% vs 38%, respectively. However, neither of these observations reached statistical significance (Table 2). Survival analysis illustrates the same trend with less PM surgery during the first months of life in FS‐treated and no significant overall difference compared to untreated cases (Figure 4B).

Gestational age and weight at birth and childhood outcome

In addition to the case of fetal death, we observed only two preterm deliveries, at 34 and 35 weeks, whereof one was a twin pregnancy. Excluding the twin with AVB III, there were no significant differences in gestational age (median 38.0 vs. 38.0 weeks) or weight at birth (2.76 (0.46) vs. 3.09 (0.31) kg, p = 0.105) between FS‐treated and untreated cases. When correcting for gestational age, a difference in weight was however found (median −1.21 vs. 0.38 SD, p = 0.035), and five of five newborn babies with a weight below −2.0 SD had been treated with FS in utero. Notably though, at the latest outpatient PM control with documented growth data, at 2.3–18.5 (median 7.6) years, neither a difference in weight (−0.39 [1.26] vs. −0.33 [1.08] SD, p = 0.906) nor height (0.06 [1.16] vs. −0.21 [0.59] SD, p = 0.560) between FS‐treated and untreated were any longer present. During a follow‐up period of 3.5–23.3 (median 10.6) years, no cases needed cardiac transplantation, but one child developed aplastic anemia and died at 4.2 years.

DISCUSSION

We found that surveillance of pregnancies at risk of CHB detected fetuses with AVB III 5 weeks earlier than those referred for bradycardia, and that cases detected before 24 weeks of gestation had a higher ventricular HR than those detected later in gestation. Fetuses with AVB III are commonly reported to have an average HR of 60–65 bpm at detection, a nadir of 45–50 bpm later in gestation, 5 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 12 , 23 and recently ventricular rate in AVB III has also been shown to decrease as a function of gestation. 9 The majority of AVB cases are diagnosed at 20–24 weeks of gestation. 1 More specifically, across four prospective surveillance studies, all 15 AVB III cases diagnosed were detected before 24 weeks of gestation. 17 , 18 , 19 , 25 Together, these observations suggest that a delay in detection after the occurrence of the AVB, resulting in a more disseminated atrioventricular node damage, is the most likely explanation for the lower HR seen at diagnosis in our untreated cases, who were detected at a later gestational age compared to those that were started on FS treatment.

We also observed that HR remained unchanged throughout pregnancy in our group of treated cases and that the difference in HR between treated and untreated cases was preserved to the end of gestation. Our previous experience with CHB is that FS treatment starting within days of occurrence can temporarily reverse AVB III and might result in a better long‐term outcome in incomplete AVB. 19 This concept is supported by the more recent observation that dexamethasone treatment at a dose of 8 mg/day can reverse AVB III, if started ≤24 weeks of gestation in fetuses with a ventricular HR >60–70 bpm, and results in a lower rate of postnatal pacing even when the complete block reappears. 27 In accordance with these observations, we find it reasonable to suggest that our anti‐inflammatory treatment limited the antibody‐mediated cardiac damage, prevented the expected HR decrease during gestation and contributed to the higher HR seen in treated cases at the end of gestation. Notably, only one of 17 (6%) FS‐treated fetuses had a HR ≤50 bpm at follow‐up during the rest of gestation. This number is of interest as it can roughly be compared to the 25%–35% observed at nadir in other studies. 6 , 7 , 12

In the present study, the rate of PM implantations before 3 and 12 months of age was significantly lower in fetuses with HR >60 bpm before birth. The FS‐treated cases had higher HR before birth than the untreated cases, with a trend for a higher age at PM implantation (median 6.0 vs. 2.0 months) and a higher percentage of infants alive without PM at 3 months of age (71% vs. 38%) in those prenatally exposed to FS vs those not treated. However, the material is too limited to gain statistical significance. In a recent single‐center retrospective study focusing on immune‐mediated AVB III, the rate of pacing in the first week of life was 38%–39% regardless of FS treatment or not. 9 That this proportion is significantly higher than the 8% (2/17) observed at 1 month of age in our cases commenced on FS treatment, is probably to some extent due to differences in the clinical condition of the baby at birth, as the indications for PM treatment were practically the same at both institutions. Support for this idea are the observations that babies in the present study were born 1.5 weeks later with a higher weight, a lower occurrence of cases with HR <50 bpm, 3/24 (13%) vs 15/43 (35%), and without any neonatal mortality compared to 4/43 deaths prior to PM implantation in the other study.

Another two retrospective multicenter studies did not observe any significant effect of transplacental FS treatment on neonatal pacing. When restricted to babies born with AVB III, the need for neonatal PM treatment in FS treated vs untreated was 4/9 (44%) vs 14/27 (52%) in the study by Van den Berg et al. 8 Notably, FS‐treated cases were born at an earlier gestational age, 36 (3) vs 39 (2) weeks, with a higher degree of growth restriction, 38% vs 11%, than the untreated. After excluding five terminations, the feto‐neonatal mortality was 8/51 (16%), including seven cases without AVB III at birth. In the study by Sunderji et al. that included 19/113 liveborn cases with other types of cardiac NLE than AVB III, 26/91 (28%) of the treated and 9/22 (41%) of the untreated had a PM implanted before 1 month of age. 10 Excluding six terminations, the feto‐neonatal mortality was 16/121 (13%), without considering if the cases had or developed AVB III or not. Fifty percent (56/113) were delivered <37 weeks of gestation without any difference between treated and untreated. This proportion is significantly higher than the 2/24 (8%) observed in the present study, but at the same time similar to the 44%–45% reported in other earlier studies. 6 , 23 Noticing these differences in the degree of preterm deliveries, neonatal PM treatment and the lower feto‐neonatal mortality (4%) in our cohort of AVB III cases, we do not believe that the results from the single‐center 9 and the two multicenter studies 8 , 10 are directly comparable to those obtained in this present study.

In a previous study, we had observed that CHB was a more important predictor of growth restriction than maternal rheumatic disease and prenatal exposure to autoantibodies, and that this might be aggravated by transplacental FS treatment. 28 Growth restriction was diagnosed in 14/130 (11%) babies of anti‐Ro antibody‐positive mothers with SLE or Sjögren's syndrome, compared to 5/18 (28%) in cases with AVB II‐III, whereof 16 were exposed to transplacental treatment with FS. In the present study, 31% of the fetuses exposed to FS were small for gestational age and we observed a significant difference in weight between treated and untreated when correcting for gestational age, indicating that FS treatment contributes to fetal growth restriction. This will be more thoroughly investigated in our ongoing studies, and at present, we can only add that this difference was no longer observed when analyzing data from the latest outpatient PM control.

Our experience presented in this report suggests that early detection and initiation of FS treatment may offer benefits in fetal cases of AVB III, such as maintaining a higher ventricular HR to the end of gestation, delaying PM implantation, and in turn decreasing the risk of PM complications. We have previously, in a very limited number of surveillance cases, observed that treatment started within 1 week after the development of first, second, and third‐degree AVB might result in a better long‐term outcome. 19 Our present observations do not negate this idea as we have increasingly offered FS treatment to cases referred for bradycardia before 24 weeks of gestation. As a result, our two treatment groups FSD≤1W and FSDU were similar regarding both gestational age and HR at detection (Table 1), suggesting that the groups might not be significantly different in the delay from occurrence of the heart block to treatment start. The FSD≤1W group did however show an earlier HR increase after treatment start (Figure 3A), and a detailed analysis of the cases with a HR increase of ≥5 bpm during fetal follow‐up showed that such an increase was observed already at the examination made 1–2 weeks after treatment start in 3/3 cases in the FSD≤1W compared to 1/4 cases in the FSDU group. However, in two of the three cases in whom treatment was started before the occurrence of AVB III, HR decreased ≥10 bpm and ended up around 55 bpm before birth, suggesting that the individual treatment response is sometimes more dependent on other factors than delay in treatment start (Table S1).

Another observation we made when taking care of the newborn babies in the present study was that there was a transient HR decrease during the first 2–3 days after birth, making us accept a HR around 50 bpm shortly after birth in a few cases and wait for an improvement during the following days, as long as the baby stayed hemodynamically stable. Unfortunately, the neonatal follow‐up procedure was not structured to allow retrospective confirmation of the exact day during the first week after birth that the ECG was recorded, as a significant number of cases were born before the introduction of our present electronic ECG storage system. These systematic postnatal HR changes, from day to day, probably had an impact on why the effect of FS treatment on HR before birth could not be confirmed by ECG. This concept is supported by another research group demonstrating that fetal HR was a slightly stronger predictor of neonatal PM treatment, with a greater predictive power, than neonatal HR. 29

The incidence of preterm deliveries and the rate of neonatal PM implantations in the present study were significantly lower than in most previous studies, which often were performed without Doppler surveillance and/or no transplacental FS treatment in confirmed close temporal proximity to block development. Our approach resulted in a 1‐year survival of more than 95%. No case has needed cardiac transplantation.

This observational study is limited by the fact that our FS treatment policy resulted in a lower GA and a higher HR in the treated group than those untreated. Betamimetics was mainly used in cases with HR still in the lower range after FS treatment started and did not increase HR. Still, it cannot be excluded that betamimetic participated in preventing a further HR decrease in these cases. While we acknowledge that the interpretation of our findings is limited by the low incidence of CHB and the small number of cases, we believe that further evaluation of the benefits of early detection and FS treatment initiation in CHB in larger cohorts is warranted. Twice‐daily home fetal heart rhythm monitoring performed by the pregnant woman is probably the most promising method for early detection of second and third‐degree AVB and could accordingly be expected to define a better window of time for successful therapy. 25

CONCLUSION

Our study suggests that early detection of AVB III and early initiation of FS treatment may maintain a higher HR to the end of gestation and delay PM implantation.
