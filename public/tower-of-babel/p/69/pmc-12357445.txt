Palliative care models in primary health care system of India: a scoping review
Authors: Shalu R. Chaudhary, Ankith Thomas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPalliative care is essential in alleviating the suffering of patients with chronic or terminal illnesses and improving their quality of life. Despite progress, in India it reaches only 1% of the population, highlighting significant gaps in access and awareness that can be mitigated by integration into primary health care. States like Kerala have demonstrated community-based palliative care through Local Self-Government Institutions (LSGIs). This scoping review aimed to examine models and interventions for integrating palliative care into India’s Primary Health Care (PHC) system, focusing on their strengths, limitations, and policy implications.MethodsFollowing Arksey and O’Malley’s scoping review framework, a systematic search was conducted across PubMed, JSTOR, and Google Scholar for studies published between 2000 and 2024. Of 2,262 screened records, 19 articles met the inclusion criteria. Data were charted and synthesized into thematic categories: community-based care, structured existing models, and hospital-linked initiatives.ResultsThe review identified three main model types: community-based care, structured models, and hospital-linked initiatives. Community-driven approaches in Kerala showed high reach, cost-effectiveness, and cultural acceptability. Models like Pallium India showcased scalable volunteer-supported care, embedded within primary care. Structured models led by NGOs and public-private partnerships revealed strengths in continuity, training, and patient satisfaction, though often limited by funding fragility. Hospital-linked interventions highlighted critical gaps in implementation, while also piloting innovations such as telemedicine and digital monitoring. Across models, a common thread was the importance of community engagement, governance support, and specialist linkages. However, most studies lacked long-term outcome data, cost-effectiveness analysis, and evidence of successful replication, pointing to significant research and policy gaps.ConclusionIntegration of palliative care into PHC in India is both feasible and essential. Evidence-informed, community-anchored, and governance-supported models must guide future policy, with attention to quality, sustainability, and sociocultural fit.

Introduction

Palliative care is an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families who are facing problems associated with life-threatening illnesses. It prevents and relieves suffering through the early identification, correct assessment and treatment of pain and other problems, whether physical, psychosocial or spiritual [1]. The Lancet Commission on Global Access to Palliative Care and Pain Relief evaluated that more than 61 million people worldwide experience health conditions associated with suffering that could be significantly alleviated through palliative care each year. However, almost 80% of these individuals do not have access to even the most basic palliative care interventions, such as pain medication [2].

On October 9th, 2021, a day observed as World Palliative Care Day, the WHO issued a report which asserted that although more than 56.8 million people globally need this care—25.7 million of whom are in the last year of life—only 1 in 10 people are receiving it. They also estimated that the need for palliative care will double by 2060 [3].

In India, there has been significant progress in the field of palliative care over the past decades, but the challenge remains that even today it reaches only 1% of the population. Although the National Programme in Palliative Care began in 2012, only a small portion of it has been implemented due to a lack of budget al.location. State governments must ensure that the program is properly planned and implemented [4].

Primary palliative care is the palliative care provided by primary health care (PHC) workers, who are responsible for caring for people in their communities throughout their lives. This approach facilitates early identification and comprehensive chronic disease management [5].

A significant proportion of people who require palliative care prefer to be at home, making it a medical and ethical necessity to provide palliative care within the community as part of primary health care. It is not always possible for palliative care specialists to be available, especially in resource-limited settings. In such cases, training primary health care providers in palliative care and symptomatic relief can effectively address most palliative care needs and facilitate referrals to higher levels of care when necessary [6].

Studies have shown that even though there have been advances in recent years, palliative care practices in PHC are still in the nascent stage. The reasons for this include gaps in continuity of care, challenges in delivering palliative care at home, a lack of sufficient visits by healthcare providers, limited interdisciplinary approaches, inadequate training, meagre research output in the field, the need for modifications in PHCs to better accommodate palliative care, the presence of overly broad care protocols, and obstacles in accessing psychological support [7].

Further, delivering palliative care in the primary health care setting is challenged by the fact that there is an ever-increasing number of people requiring palliative care, because of which there is an increasing need for a capable workforce. There is also a need to support those who actively provide palliative care with training, evidence to inform practice and tools [8].

Over 5.8 million Indians lose their lives each year to NCDs like diabetes, cancer, heart disease, and stroke [9]. This means that there will be many people who will endure pain and suffering. To combat this as well as many other health issues, the Government of India has renewed its focus, directed by its National Health Policy 2017, based on the recommendations of the Task Force for Comprehensive Primary Health Care (CPHC). It endorsed the establishment of Ayushmaan Arogya Mandir (AAMs) and committed that two-thirds of the budget be allocated towards primary healthcare. The main aim of the AAM under the Ayushmaan Bharat scheme is to provide a package of 12 services and one of them is palliative care [10].

To the best of our knowledge, we did not find any existing reviews on palliative care models within the PHC system in India. However, such reviews have been undertaken in other countries. A review undertaken by Van Gaans et al. in Australia, provides insight into the roles of individual health professionals and the multidisciplinary team approach to actively providing palliative care within the Primary Health Care setting [8]. In Brazil, a scoping review done by Justin et al. revealed that palliative care in primary health care has been gradually developed, but it is necessary to consider the organization of primary health care and the social policies that support or weaken it, as this is a complex challenge [7]. A systematic review conducted by Peeler et al. in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) showed that evidence supporting primary palliative care in these countries is limited, with much of the published literature coming from Asia and Southern Africa [11].

This scoping review is vital for informing policy decisions and shaping the future of end-of-life care delivered through PHC, in India. With India facing a significant and growing need for palliative care due to an ageing population and a rise in non-communicable diseases like cancer, understanding the current landscape is paramount. The South Asian region homes one-quarter of the world’s population and gets 1.3 million new cases every year. There is also a burden of other chronic diseases end-stage heart failure, cerebrovascular accidents, renal and respiratory diseases, which require specialized palliative care, yet palliative care provision is dismal in these countries [12]. Experts estimate that 80% of people dying from cancer and 50–80% of those dying from AIDS will require relief from moderate to severe pain, typically for an average duration of 90 days during the course of their illness [13]. The review will explore various models of care, including home-based services, integration with primary healthcare centres, and the role of community volunteers. It will also examine the impact of these models on patient outcomes, caregiver support, and healthcare resource utilization. Ultimately, this scoping review will serve as a valuable resource for policymakers seeking to expand and strengthen palliative care services within India’s primary healthcare system.

Hence, with this background, we conducted this scoping review with an objective to explore various models and interventions for palliative care in the primary health care system in India. By critically analyzing the existing body of evidence, the review aimed to elucidate the challenges as well as strengths of these models, uncover policy implications of sustainable scaling up of these models and expanding the scope.

Establishing a scoping review question

This scoping review was initiated by identifying the broader idea of examining the existing models, packages, interventions and evidence on the implementation of palliative care in the primary health care system in India. To further broaden the scope of the review, considering palliative care to be a recent addition to PHC in India under Ayushmaan Bharat, literature on associated gaps, challenges, opportunities and best practices was also included. Identifying relevant studies

The search for published literature was conducted using a strategy developed by testing keywords, incorporating relevant medical subject headings (MeSH) terms, on various databases. This search utilized the National Library of Medicine (NLM) research databases, particularly PubMed. Additionally, a keyword search using Google Scholar was undertaken using search terms related to the ‘concepts of interest’ (palliative care in primary health care sector) ‘context’ (India), and ‘population’ (individuals with chronic conditions requiring palliative care). Detailed Search Strategy (As of December 2024)

Two researchers specializing in scoping reviews and primary health care screened the articles. First, the titles were assessed, followed by the abstract if seemed suitable. Articles that met the selection criteria were then fully reviewed. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion among both the authors.

The article selection process began with the identification of 4544 records from PubMed, JSToR and Google Scholar (first 10 pages of the search history), and were uploaded in Rayyan software for further screening. After removing 2282 duplicate entries, 2,263 unique records remained available for screening. Of these, 2,238 records were discarded after evaluating their titles and abstracts, leaving 25 articles for further eligibility assessment. All 25 articles were then carefully reviewed based on the defined selection criteria, whereby 5 articles were removed for not meeting the criteria. 20 articles were analyzed and summarized for the review process (Fig. 1). The inter-reviewer agreement rate was 96%.

Synthesizing and reporting of findings

The findings were summarized descriptively and arranged using an Excel spreadsheet. They were conveyed in a narrative format centered around key three themes, which are community-based or comprehensive care models, existing programme-based models of palliative care, and hospital-based models, providing a comprehensive description and graphical representation of the data. Moreover, the results were also organized in tables for additional synthesis. The scoping review adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) Checklist [34].

Results

Characteristics of studies

Our review involved 20 articles extracted from the systematic search. The studies included were published between 2006 and 2023. The extracted articles included various study designs, with 6 being qualitative [18, 20, 23, 26, 28, 34], 7 quantitative [12, 14, 15, 20, 24, 27, 32], and 7 mixed method studies [13, 17, 19, 20, 22, 33, 35]. These studies employed diverse methodologies, ranging from 11 being descriptive [12–15, 17, 18, 20, 22, 24, 27, 32], 5 being analytical [23, 26, 28, 33, 34], and one experimental study [19]. The study-specific characteristics and key findings are presented in Table 4.

The included studies reported various models of palliative care. The findings of which are synthesized narratively under three themes: community-based or comprehensive care models, existing programme-based models of palliative care, and hospital-based models. Each study provided insights into different aspects of palliative care services, including implementation, effectiveness, sustainability, and challenges. The models varied in terms of their design, setting, target populations, level of integration with primary care, community involvement, and institutional backing.

The included studies covered 10 Indian states, with Kerala being the most frequently reported. Other states included Tamil Nadu, Assam, Maharashtra, Delhi, West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Jharkhand, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, and Tripura. Models were implemented in both rural and urban settings, though most emphasized rural or semi-urban reach through community-based strategies or linkage with primary healthcare centers (PHCs).

Community-based or comprehensive models

Community-based palliative care services focus on delivering care outside hospital settings, often involving community health workers, local self-government institutions, or home-based care models. Several studies highlighted the implementation and evaluation of such services across India. Vinayagamoorthy et al., 2017 explored different stakeholder perspectives on community-based palliative care services [14], while Vijay et al., documented the adaptation and implementation of a palliative care model in West Bengal [15]. The feasibility of integrating community health workers into home-based palliative care was assessed by Cartmell et al. [23] Munday et al. used a mixed-method approach to evaluate a rural palliative care model [26]. Additionally, Potts et al., investigated the role of community health workers in delivering home-based palliative care [18], while Ajjarapu and Broderick, explored the effectiveness of home-based chronic pain relief through patient and family experiences [19]., while Krishnan et al., 2018 evaluated the role of NGOs in delivering palliative care in low-resource settings [22]. The role of family physicians in integrating palliative care into primary healthcare was explored by Atreya et al., 2019 [35]. Dhiliwal et al., 2021 examined five years of data on implementing a home-based cancer palliative care model in Mumbai [20]. Yeager et al., 2016 described CanSupport’s approach offers a reliable, cost-effective model for palliative care in low- and middle-income countries [17]. Philip et al., 2019 highlighted that Kerala’s community-based palliative care model offers a scalable, volunteer-led approach that is both cost-effective and culturally adaptable for low- and middle-income countries [32].

Existing program-based models

Several studies assess structured and ongoing models of palliative care under national or state health programs in India, focusing on their impact, sustainability, and patient satisfaction. Azeez and Anbuselvi, 2021 assessed the sustainability of Kerala’s widely recognized palliative care model [29]. Meanwhile, Jayalakshmi and Suhita, analyzed how Kerala’s home-based palliative care services align with policy and regulatory frameworks [27]. Nair and Augustine evaluated the long-term impact of community-driven palliative care services in Kerala [24]. Quality improvement initiatives are highlighted by Sunilkumar et al., 2021, which focuses on implementing standardized protocols for a homecare team [28]. Sharma et al., 2023 identified gaps in the implementation of the National Program for Palliative Care (NPPC) in Puducherry [16]. Patient satisfaction was assessed by Krishnan et al., 2024, offering insights into service quality [31]. Meanwhile, Kochuvilayil et al., 2023 described a collaborative governance framework for managing palliative care services within the primary healthcare system [30].

Hospital-based models

Hospital-based palliative care models focus on providing outreach services predominantly to patients attached with the tertiary care centers, or cancer hospitals. Viswanath et al., 2021 described a Vishakhapatnam based hospital-driven home-based palliative care initiative [25]. Additionally, Balasubramanian et al., 2021 evaluated the impact of telemedicine (e-Palliative Care) on patient satisfaction in a tertiary cancer center [21].

Discussion

We systematically reviewed the qualities, drawbacks, and policy implications of palliative care approaches incorporated into India’s primary healthcare system in this scoping review. The results show significant variation among models in terms of governing frameworks, methods of service delivery, and sustainability approaches. Even though India has made significant strides in integrating palliative care into its healthcare system, enduring inequalities underscore the necessity of a more unified yet adaptable strategy for successfully scaling these models.

Governance and public health integration

Kerala has pioneered a governance-led approach to palliative care, with its Local Self-Government Institution (LSGI)-driven model providing sustainable financing, policy continuity, and community engagement. Studies by Nair et al. Kochuvilayil et al., and Jayalakshmi & Chopra illustrate how community-based care is strategically integrated with primary healthcare services [24, 27, 30]. Critical success factors include decentralized governance, structured resource allocation, and workforce capacity-building. However, inter-regional disparities in LSGI implementation underscore the importance of administrative leadership and coordination of policies in ensuring equitable service delivery.

Community-based and home care models

Initiatives in community-led palliative care, such as the Kerala homecare program studied by Azeez & Anbuselvi and the Emmanuel Hospitals Association (EHA) model in Uttar Pradesh, studied by Munday et al., have shown improved patient-centred results and cost-effectiveness [26, 29]. In order to provide care outside of institutional settings while maintaining accessibility and affordability, these models make use of nurse-led mobile teams under physician supervision. As demonstrated in the Malappuram and Palakkad regions of Kerala, sustainability depends on a variety of financial sources, such as community donations and collaborations with non-governmental organizations (NGOs) [29]. The community-based palliative care model implemented by CanSupport in Delhi has demonstrated effectiveness in delivering high-quality, home-based care through trained multidisciplinary teams, improving access and patient-centered outcomes in urban and semi-urban settings [17].

The community-based and home care model implemented in Kerala studied by Philip et al. has demonstrated significant effectiveness in enhancing healthcare accessibility and patient outcomes [32]. However, its applicability remains context-dependent, necessitating further investigation into its effectiveness across diverse sociocultural and health system settings. A comprehensive evaluation is required to assess its scalability, adaptability, and potential integration into varying healthcare frameworks, ensuring its relevance and sustainability in different regional contexts.

Notwithstanding their benefits, these models have operational drawbacks such as staffing shortages, logistical constraints, and the requirement for ongoing training. In tackling these issues, the Trivandrum Institute of Palliative Sciences (TIPS) model demonstrates the value of structured quality improvement approaches [28]. However, substantial institutional backing, regulatory consistency, and context-specific adjustments are necessary for the effective replication of such systems.

Hospital-based and specialist-integrated models

The importance of tertiary care institutions in bolstering palliative care services is highlighted by hospital-integrated palliative care models, such as those at the Homi Bhabha Cancer Hospital and [25]Center’s PCP-FP liaison model in Kolkata (Shrikant et al., 2019) [25, 35]. By connecting primary care physicians with specialist expertise, these approaches improve care continuity. Effective home-based follow-up is still very difficult to ensure, though, especially in underserved and rural areas.

States differ in how they provide home care services, which emphasizes the necessity for flexible frameworks that take into account local healthcare conditions. According to Viswanath et al. (2022), maximizing home-based palliative care necessitates striking a balance between localized service flexibility and standardized protocols [25]. Maintaining these connections and enhancing service continuity require training family practitioners (FPs) and primary care physicians (PCPs) in palliative care.

The evolution of palliative care in India reflects both remarkable progress and persistent challenges. From its early humanitarian roots in Kerala to its inclusion in national health policy, the growing recognition of palliative care as a public health need has expanded its scope. However, this evolution has been uneven, often shaped by fragmented policy ownership, variable state-level engagement, and inconsistent funding streams. While Kerala’s LSGI-led approach demonstrates the potential of embedding palliative care within decentralized health governance, its replication has faced hurdles due to differences in administrative capacity, political will, and community participation across regions. Moreover, the diversity in service delivery models from community-based care to hospital-linked pathways raises essential questions about what constitutes a scalable yet contextually responsive model in India’s complex health landscape. The current ecosystem reveals a tension between innovation and standardization: while locally adapted models are necessary, there is a growing need for a coordinated policy framework that provides guidance on workforce development, quality assurance, financing, and inter-sectoral collaboration. Addressing these structural gaps is essential for sustaining and scaling palliative care integration in primary health systems across the country.

Challenges and policy considerations

The scalability of palliative care in India is nevertheless hampered by staff shortages, financial constraints, and infrastructure deficiencies, notwithstanding policy achievements. The long-term viability of many programs is impacted by their reliance on different financing sources. Furthermore, initiatives to improve care delivery and responsiveness are weakened by the lack of established quality assessment frameworks (Kochuvilayil et al. [30]

Since differences in administrative commitment and governance structures affect the efficacy of services, it is crucial to strengthen community engagement. Wider adaption and implementation might be made easier by a national policy framework that integrates Kerala’s best practices and those of other successful state models. Additionally, including palliative care within national health initiatives like Ayushman Bharat would improve workforce capacity, financial stability, and accessibility in long run.

Limitations

While this scoping review aimed to provide a broad and detailed overview of models integrating services within primary care in India, a few limitations should be acknowledged. Despite a comprehensive search strategy across multiple databases, it is possible that some relevant sources, particularly grey literature such as government documents, NGO reports, or unpublished evaluations were missed. This is especially important in the Indian context, where many innovative models are implemented locally and may not always find their way into indexed journals.

We also limited our search to publications in English, which may have excluded studies or reports published in regional languages. Given India’s linguistic diversity, this may have led to the omission of some locally significant initiatives or findings.

Another limitation lies in the variation among the included studies. The differences in study design, scope, and reporting made it challenging to compare models directly or to synthesize findings in a consistent way. Many studies lacked detailed descriptions of how integration was implemented or evaluated, which in turn limited our ability to assess their effectiveness or sustainability.

Finally, given the dynamic nature of India’s health system and ongoing policy changes, some recent or emerging models may not yet be reflected in the published literature. Future updates of this review may benefit from incorporating newer initiatives as more evidence becomes available.

Conclusion

Several studies in India have documented palliative care models operating within the primary health care context, with the majority originating from Kerala. These models were broadly categorized into community-based or comprehensive models, hospital-based models, and those based on national or state health programs. Each model presented its own set of strengths and challenges.

While the palliative care models from Kerala have demonstrated notable effectiveness, their direct replication in other regions may not be feasible due to variations in sociocultural, economic, and health system contexts. Nevertheless, the findings from this review offer a comprehensive overview of existing practices and can serve as a foundational reference for policymakers, researchers, and practitioners. They highlight key areas for adaptation and innovation, positioning this review as a valuable resource for informing future research, program development, and the implementation of context-specific palliative care strategies across diverse settings in India and other low- and middle-income countries.
