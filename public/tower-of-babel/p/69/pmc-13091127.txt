Comprehensive chemical profiling of a Botanical-Infused Chinese Fortified Wine (Guogong) via UHPLC-Q-exactive orbitrap MS and HS-GC-IMS
Authors: Hanxiu Deng, Shuteng Huang, Ruyue Zhang, Shengqian Sun, Xia Wei, Zijian Wang, Zhibin Wang, Jiayu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A traditional Botanical-Infused Chinese Fortified Wine-Guogong (BCFW) has a complex formula and diverse chemical composition. In this study, chemical profiling of BCFW was performed using UHPLC-Q-Exactive Orbitrap MS in both full-scan and complementary modes. A chemical database of BCFW was constructed from 30 botanical ingredients, with compounds identified through diagnostic ions and fragmentation patterns and further verified using literature sources. Headspace gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS) was employed to profile volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in both BCFW and base wine, and chemometric analysis revealed batch-to-batch variations. A total of 658 compounds were identified, including 590 non-volatile and 68 volatile constituents. HS-GC-IMS fingerprints revealed generally consistent VOC profiles across batches, with notable differences observed in S1, S7, and S10. This comprehensive chemical characterization of BCFW provides a valuable reference for quality assessment and control.

Introduction

For centuries, wine has had a fundamental role in the culture and habits of different civilizations. Among numerous wine types that involve specific winemaking processes, Chinese botanical-infused wines possess added value and have attracted growing interest internationally. Botanical-infused wines exemplify the combination of alcoholic beverages and medicinal plants, offering a unique source of nutraceutical compounds. Ethanol in wine acts as an efficient solvent that facilitates the extraction and dissolution of bioactive constituents from botanical materials, thereby enhancing their bioavailability and biological efficacy (Bezerra, Ribeiro, Cosme, & Nunes, 2024; Xia, 2013). Botanical-infused wines provide nutraceutical benefits and may protect against pathogenic microorganisms and inflammation owing to their richness in antioxidants (Morya et al., 2024). In China, such wines have historically been developed for health promotion and widely used for disease prevention and general wellness maintenance. However, to achieve their potential benefits, they should be consumed in moderation and preferably under professional guidance.

Among these, Botanical-Infused Chinese Fortified Wine-Guogong (BCFW) is particularly valued for its distinctive flavor, nutritional value, and notable health-promoting properties. Guogong Wine is a representative traditional BCFW, with origins dating back to the Ming Dynasty. It has long been used to alleviate symptoms associated with wind-cold-dampness syndromes, including joint discomfort and impaired mobility (Ascione et al., 2023). Formulated from 30 botanical ingredients, including Angelica sinensis (Oliv.) Diels, Saposhnikovia divaricata (Turcz.) Schischk., and Citrus reticulata Blanco, Guogong Wine exhibits a highly complex phytochemical profile. The formulation adheres to the traditional Jun-Chen-Zuo-Shi compatibility principle, in which multiple botanical ingredients are combined in specific ratios to achieve synergistic therapeutic effects while minimizing potential adverse effects.

The biological efficacy of BCFW is closely linked to its chemical composition (Martínez-Francés, Rivera, Obon, Alcaraz, & Ríos, 2021). Accurate characterization of these constituents is therefore critical for elucidating the material basis of their bioactivity; optimizing production processes; and establishing robust quality control standards (Fernanda & Luís, 2024). However; the complex multi-herbal formulation of BCFW results in a highly complicated chemical profile; making comprehensive characterization of its constituents particularly challenging. Recent advances in analytical technologies have provided powerful tools for investigating the chemical basis of complex herbal formulations (Cosme et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). In particular, liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) has been widely employed for the characterization of bioactive compounds due to its high resolution, sensitivity, and accurate mass measurement (Simpson, Simpson, & Gerber, 2024). Moreover; headspace gas chromatography-ion mobility spectrometry (HS-GC-IMS) has emerged as an effective technique for the rapid profiling of volatile organic compounds (VOCs). By combining the high separation capability of gas chromatography with the ppm-level sensitivity of ion mobility spectrometry; HS-GC-IMS enables efficient profiling of VOCs (Giménez-Campillo et al., 2025; Kh et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the massive datasets generated by these techniques require advanced data-processing strategies for efficient compound identification. Component-mining approaches, including database searching, diagnostic ion filtering, and neutral loss analysis, have therefore become important tools for interpreting complex mass spectrometric data (Lei et al., 2023).

To address these challenges, a comprehensive strategy was developed to systematically characterize the chemical composition of BCFW in terms of volatile and non-volatile components. UHPLC-Q-Exactive Orbitrap MS combined with component-mining strategies was used to characterize the non-volatile constituents, while HS-GC-IMS was employed to profile volatile components across different batches. Multivariate statistical analyses, including hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), principal component analysis (PCA), and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), were applied to identify and differentiate key volatile compounds. In total, 658 compounds were identified, comprising 590 non-volatile and 68 volatile compounds, providing a comprehensive chemical profile of BCFW. These findings elucidate the chemical basis of BCFW's bioactivity and support its quality evaluation, standardization, and functional stability.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

One BCFW sample batch (No. 22180027) and ten additional batches (S1–S10, batch Nos. 22,180,001–22,180,010) were supplied by Beijing Tongrentang Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). A total of 32 reference compounds were utilized for the qualitative analysis of compounds in BCFW. These reference compounds, including hesperidin, naringin, neohesperidin, narirutin, ferulic acid, synephrine, osthole, imperatorin, magnolol, honokiol, notopterol, prim-O-glucosylcimifugin, paeonol, hydroxysafflor yellow A, psoralen, angelicin, nobiletin, 5-O-methylvisammioside, nodakenin, atractylodin, oleanic acid, ursolic acid, paeoniflorin, isoimperatorin, gardenoside, naringenin, neobavaisoflavone, bavachinin, rutin, scopoletin, and scoparone, were purchased from Chengdu Must Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Sichuan, China). Chlorogenic acid was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The purity of all reference compounds was ≥98%, with batch number information provided in Table S1.

The 30 botanical ingredients used in the preparation of BCFW were provided by Beijing Tongrentang Co., Ltd., with details summarized in Table S2. UHPLC-grade ethanol, acetonitrile, and formic acid were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd. (MA, USA). Ultrapure water was obtained using a Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, USA).

A series of C4–C9 n-alkanones, including 2-butanone, 2-pentanone, 2-hexanone, 2-heptanone, 2-octanone, and 2-nonanone (all ≥99.5%), were purchased from Aladdin Scientific Corp. (Shanghai, China). Headspace vials (20 mL) were obtained from Shandong Haineng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China).

Sample preparation

Sample preparation of UPLC-MS detection

Fifty milliliters of BCFW (batch No. 22180027) were placed in a 100 mL rotary evaporation flask and evaporated under reduced pressure at 45 °C until a concentrated residue was obtained. The residue was then dissolved in 10 mL of 70% ethanol and ultrasonicated for 1 min to facilitate dissolution. During ultrasonication, the container was sealed with plastic film to minimize solvent evaporation. The solution was stored at 4 °C overnight and subsequently centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was collected as the test solution. Appropriate amounts of the 32 reference compounds were weighed and dissolved in methanol using ultrasonic treatment. Each of the 30 constituent herbs was extracted using the same procedure as BCFW to prepare individual herbal samples for analysis. Both the standard and sample solutions were stored at 4 °C and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane prior to analysis by UHPLC-Q-Exactive Orbitrap MS/MS.

Sample preparation of GC- IMS detection

Two milliliters of BCFW from each batch (Nos. 22,180,001–22,180,010) were accurately transferred into 20 mL headspace vials and sealed with PTFE/silicone septa to prevent analyte loss. The vials were equilibrated in a thermostatically controlled incubation chamber at 60 °C for 15 min to ensure sufficient volatilization of aroma compounds. After incubation, the headspace gas was automatically sampled and injected into the analytical instrument to profile volatile compounds under the specified conditions.

UHPLC-Q-Exactive orbitrap MS conditions

UHPLC-MS analysis was performed on a Thermo Fisher UHPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive Orbitrap HRMS (Waltham, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Waters Acquity UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water containing 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile) and was run according to the following gradient program: 0–1 min, 5% B; 1–9 min, 5–15% B; 9–22 min, 15–35% B; 22–29 min, 35–55% B; 29–35 min, 55–80% B; 35–38 min, 80–100% B; 38–41 min, 100% B; 41–42 min, 100–5% B; 42–45 min, 5% B. The sample injection volume was 2 μL, and the flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min.

The mass spectrometer was equipped with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source, and data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes with the following parameters: spray voltage, ±3.5/2.0 kV; sheath gas flow rate, 50 arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 10 arb; capillary temperature, 350 °C; source heater temperature, 350 °C. Full Scan/dd-MS2 was used to acquire untargeted data. The resolution settings for MS and MS2 acquisitions were 70,000 and 15,000, respectively. The scan range was set from m/z 100 to 1500, and HCD collision energies were applied at 25, 40, and 55 eV.

HS-GC-IMS analysis conditions

Measurements were conducted using a HS-GC-IMS instrument (FlavourSpec®; G.A.S., Shandong HaiNeng Science Instrument Co., Ltd., Shandong, China). Two milliliters of each sample were transferred into a headspace vial and incubated at 60 °C for 15 min with agitation at 500 rpm. Subsequently, 500 μL of the sample headspace gas was automatically injected using a heated syringe (110 °C, splitless mode) and separated on an MXT-WAX capillary column (15 m × 0.53 mm, 1.0 μm, Restek Corporation, USA) maintained at 60 °C. High-purity nitrogen (≥99.999%) was used as both the carrier and drift gas, with the IMS drift tube maintained at 45 °C. The GC flow program was as follows: E1, constant flow at 150 mL/min; E2, 0–2 min at 2 mL/min, ramped linearly to 10 mL/min from 2 to 10 min, maintained at 100 mL/min from 10 to 20 min, and held at 100 mL/min from 20 to 40 min. Molecules were ionized using a tritium source and transported through a 53 mm drift tube under an electric field of 500 V/cm, with a drift tube temperature of 45 °C and a drift gas flow rate of 150 mL/min. All data were acquired in positive ion mode (Chen et al., 2025).

Data processing and statistical analysis

The raw UHPLC-MS data were automatically processed using the Xcalibur workstation (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The processed data were then imported into Compound Discoverer 3.0 for peak extraction and normalization. The chemical formulas of the selected peaks were determined using the formula predictor, with parameters set as follows: C [2–60], H [5–100], O [0−30], N [0−10], and a mass accuracy tolerance within 5 × 10−6. ChemDraw 18.0 was used to analyze the chemical structures of BCFW compounds through fragmentation patterns. A database of individual monopharmaceutical compounds in BCFW was established by searching the literature and online databases such as bioactive compoundsID, bioactive compoundsSP, MZcloud, PubMed, and Web of Science. This chemical composition database includes compound names, chemical structures, molecular formulas, retention times, fragment ions, and sources, providing reference information for analyzing the chemical composition of BCFW.

For HS–GC–IMS analysis, the instrument-specific GC × IMS Library Search software was employed for compound identification. A calibration curve for retention time and retention index was established by analyzing a mixed standard of six ketones. The retention indices of the target compounds were then calculated based on their retention times. Qualitative analysis was performed by retrieving and comparing data from the built-in GC retention index (NIST 2020) and IMS drift time databases using VOCal software. The Reporter and Gallery Plot plug-ins in VOCal were used to generate 3D, 2D spectra, difference maps, and fingerprints of the volatile components of BCFW. HCA, PCA, and OPLS-DA were conducted across different batches of BCFW using SPSS and SIMCA 14.1 (Umetrics, Sweden) to compare their VOC profiles.

Results and discussion

Establishment of compound databases and analysis of fragmentation rules

A database of the chemical composition of 30 bioactive ingredients in BCFW was established by searching publicly accessible resources, including DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com), ChEMBL (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/chembl/), Web of Science (https://www.webofscience.com/), and CNKI (http://www.cnki.net/).

The standards of flavonoids, including hesperidin, neohesperidin, narirutin, naringin, hydroxysafflor yellow A, naringenin, neobavaisoflavone, bavachinin, nobiletin, and rutin, were analyzed using a UHPLC-Q-Exactive Orbitrap MS system operating in full-scan acquisition mode. The fragmentation patterns of each standard were summarized using the assigned fragment ions, and the prominent product ions were selected as diagnostic ions. Key diagnostic ions or neutral loss fragments were confirmed through MS/MS spectral analysis of 10 standards and by comparison with the self-constructed compound database. Because flavonoids exhibit characteristic fragmentation via a retro-Diels-Alder (RDA) reaction, the significant product ions generated from the RDA reaction at m/z 119.04969, 137.02387, 153.01878, 121.06534, and 151.03952 were set as diagnostic ions. The standards of coumarins including osthole, isoimperatorin, imperatorin, psoralen, isopsoralen, scoparone, and nodakenin, were analyzed in full-scan acquisition mode. The fragmentation patterns of each standard were summarized based on fragment ion data and relevant literature. Coumarins are prone to cleavage of the lactone ring in the parent structure, followed by decarbonylation or deoxygenation, generating characteristic fragment ions such as m/z 131.04969, 147.04406, 151.03952, and 159.04460, which can serve as diagnostic ions for coumarins. UHPLC-MS analysis and identification of the main constituents in BCFW

A total of 590 compounds were identified by comparing the constructed database with information from fragment ions of reference standards and cleavage pattern analyses. These compounds include 162 flavonoids, 122 coumarins, 92 organic acids, 68 terpenoids, 34 alkaloids, 17 phthalides and 95 other compounds. Among them, 32 components were identified by comparing their retention times and fragment ions with those of reference standards. The remaining components were tentatively characterized based on precise molecular weights, diagnostic ions, retention times, and secondary fragmentation patterns reported in the literature. Additionally, some component data were inferred by searching the database using Compound Discoverer (CD) software. The source of each compound was confirmed by comparing the chromatographic retention times and MS/MS fragmentation patterns of BCFW with those of the individual herbal extracts under identical UHPLC–MS conditions, in combination with literature information. Identification of flavonoids in BCFW

A total of 162 flavonoids were identified in BCFW, including 73 flavonoid glycosides, 44 polymethoxyflavonoids, 13 dihydroflavonoids, 10 isoflavonoids, 8 chalcones, and 14 other flavonoids. Among the 73 flavonoid glycosides, 55 were flavonoid oxysides and 18 were flavonoid carbon glycosides.

Compounds 152, 183, 198, 217, 231, and 522 were identified by comparing their retention times and fragment ions with those of reference compounds. The fragmentation of flavonoid oxyglycosides is exemplified by compound 217 (tR = 16.97 min). In positive ion mode, the quasimolecular ion peak appeared at m/z 611.19705 [M + H]+, corresponding to the molecular formula C28H34O15. In the ESI-MS2 chromatogram, a characteristic fragment ion at m/z 303.08548 [M + H-Rha-Glc]+ resulted from sequential loss of rhamnose and glucose. Based on this fragment ion, an RDA cleavage reaction at the 1,3 bond of the C-ring generated ions at m/z 151.07504 [M + H-Rha-Glc-C7H4O4]+ and m/z 153.01784 [M + H-Rha-Glc-C9H10O2]+. A further fragment at m/z 177.05417 [M + H-Rha-Glc-C6H5O3]+ corresponded to the loss of C6H5O3 (125 Da) via RDA cleavage at the 1,4 bond of the C-ring. This fragmentation pattern matched that of the reference substance, and compound 217 was identified as hesperidin. The fragmentation pathway is shown in Fig. 3A and Fig. S1A. The ion at m/z 187.03854 arose from loss of C3H6 (42 Da) from the precursor ion, whereas the ion at m/z 175.03864 resulted from the loss of C4H6 (54 Da). Subsequent fragmentation involved loss of CO (28 Da) from m/z 187.03854 to yield m/z 159.04373 [M + H-C3H6-CO]+, and loss of CO2 (44 Da) from m/z 175.03864 to produce m/z 131.04878 [M + H-C4H6-CO2]+. The fragment ion at m/z 159.04373 further lost ·C2O (40 Da), generating m/z 119.04890 [M + H-C3H6-CO-C2O]+. Based on database searches and related literature (Mukandiwa, Ahmed, Eloff, & Naidoo, 2013; Okot et al., 2023), compound 409 was identified as seselin, and its proposed fragmentation pathway is shown in Fig. 3J and Fig. S1J.

Identification of organic acids in BCFW

A total of 92 organic acids were identified in BCFW. Compound 55 (tR = 7.74 min) was used as an example to illustrate the mass spectral fragmentation pattern of organic acids. In negative ion mode, the precursor ion was observed at m/z 353.08781 [M-H]−, and the molecular formula was inferred to be C16H18O9. In the ESI-MS2 spectrum, a prominent fragment at m/z 191.05580 [M-H-Caf]−, corresponding to the quinic acid moiety, was detected and was attributed to the loss of a caffeoyl group (C9H6O3, 162 Da) from the precursor ion. Another fragment ion at m/z 161.02406 [M-H-C7H12O6]− arose from the loss of quinic acid group (C7H12O6, 192 Da) and subsequently underwent CO loss (28 Da) to generate m/z 133.02937 [M-H-C7H12O6-CO]−. This fragmentation pattern matched that of the reference standard, and compound 55 was identified as chlorogenic acid. Its proposed fragmentation pathway is shown in Fig. 3K and Fig. S1K. Compound 136 was identified by comparing its retention time and fragment ions with those of the reference standard. Compounds 6, 64, 103, 109, 188, 189, 223, 234, 293, 314, 573 were identified using diagnostic ions at m/z 191 and 149 as the diagnostic ions and were further identified by retention times and the MS/MSn data reported in the literature. Other organic acids were tentatively assigned using CD software and then confirmed by comparison with the self-constructed compound database.

Compounds 75, 101, 574 and 578 were identified by comparing their retention times and fragment ions with reference standards. Further fragmentation of m/z 175.04041 produced m/z 123.04504 [M-H-Glc-CH4O-C3O]− by loss of C3O (52 Da), and m/z 101.02431 [M-H-Glc-CH4O-C6H2]− by loss of C6H2 (74 Da). In positive ion mode, It exhibited an [M + H]+ ion at m/z 168.10191, with the molecular formula proposed as C9H13NO2. Significant fragment ions at m/z 150.09091 [M + H-H2O]+, m/z 135.06145 [M + H-H2O-CH3]+ and m/z 119.04884 [M + H-H2O-CH3-NH2]+ were observed. Qualitative analysis of VOCs in different batches of BCFW and base wine

Fig. 4D presents the qualitative analysis results of 10 BCFW batches, with each signal peak corresponding to a volatile compound. The detailed VOCs shown in the figure are listed in Table S4. A total of 68 VOCs were identified in BCFW using HS-GC-IMS, including 16 esters, 10 ketones, 8 terpenes, 6 alcohols, 4 aldehydes, 4 furans and 4 other components. However, 16 peaks remained uncharacterized due to limitations in the current database. In comparison, 40 volatile compounds were identified in BCFW, including 18 esters, 5 alcohols, 2 ketones, 2 furans and 2 other components. Eleven peaks in BCFW also remained uncharacterized due to database limitations. Additionally, 8 VOCs were identified in both monomer and dime forms. For example, γ-pinene (listed in Table S4) exhibited similar retention times in two forms: its monomer (serial number 24) and dimer (serial number 25). The formation of dimers or multimers was found to be related to the concentration of the relevant compounds and their high proton affinity. Specifically, high substance concentrations promote dimer formation through the association of protonated and neutral molecules in the ionized region. Furthermore, the proton affinity of certain compounds is higher than that of water, leading to the transfer of protons from the reactants to these compounds, resulting in the generation of dimers or polymers. It is now widely accepted that simultaneous presence of monomers and dimers enables more accurate compound characterization. A comparison of the VOCs in BCFW and its base wine was also performed. As shown in Fig. 4D, there were 33 common VOCs between BCFW and base wine, and 35 unique VOCs in BCFW. It was found that the ester components in BCFW decreased, while ketone components increased. The peaks observed peaks in BCFW included (+)-limonene, γ-terpinene, α-pinene, β-pinene, 3-carene, among others (Fig. 4E). The highest concentrations of (+)-limonene and γ-terpinene-M indicated that these components originated from the botanical-infused materials.

PCA

The feasibility of HS-GC-IMS for assessing quality differences in BCFW was further evaluated by examining the discriminatory power of characteristic VOCs screened based on fingerprint differences. The peak areas of 68 shared volatile components from 10 batches of BCFW were used as variables and imported into SIMCA 14.1 for unsupervised PCA. As shown in Table S5, the first two principal components explained 65.40% and 16.30% of the variance, respectively. The cumulative explained variance of the first five principal components (R2X(cum)) was 0.942, and the cumulative predictive ability of the model (Q2(cum)) was 0.945, indicating excellent model performance. In the PCA score plot (Fig. 5B), the 10 batches were clearly separated into two groups, with samples S7 and S10 clustering apart from the remaining batches. This result is consistent with the HCA findings and further supports the classification obtained from cluster analysis.

OPLS-DA

The peak areas of 68 shared volatile components from 10 batches of BCFW samples were used as variables and imported into SIMCA 14.1 software for supervised OPLS-DA analysis, as shown in Fig. 5C. In this model, R2X was 0.940, R2Y was 0.996, and Q2 was 0.887, all of which are greater than 0.5, indicating that the model is stable and reliable. To assess potential overfitting, a permutation test (n = 200) was conducted on the OPLS-DA model to evaluate its predictive performance, as shown in Fig. 5D (Serrano-García et al., 2025). The results indicate that the Q2 and R2 values on the left side of the figure were lower than the original values on the right. The slopes of the regression lines for both R2 and Q2 were greater than 0; with intercepts of 0.776 and − 0.972; respectively. These results suggest that the model's replacement validation was satisfactory and that no overfitting occurred (Chen et al., 2025). The OPLS-DA score plot classified the 10 batches of samples into two groups, consistent with the results from HCA and PCA. The variable importance projection (VIP) values of the OPLS-DA model for different VOC peak areas are shown in Fig. 5E. Larger VIP values indicate a higher contribution of the VOCs to the classification of the 10 BCFW batches. Using VIP > 1.0 as the screening criterion, 10 components with VIP values greater than 1 were screened: ethyl D-lactate (monomer and dimer), acetic acid, isoamyl alcohol, γ-pinene (monomer), (Z)-4-heptenal, α-pinene, benzaldehyde (monomer), ethyl butyrate, and p-toluene anisole. These components may be the primary contributors to the VOC differences across the 10 BCFW batches and are key components for quality control of BCFW.

Analysis of the chemical component and function of BCFW

Non-volatile components

First, by reviewing the chemical components of 30 individual herbs in BCFW through literature and database analysis, we established a compound database to identify its potential chemical components. Next, simple impurity removal and enrichment pretreatment were applied to BCFW to ensure the acquisition of more compound information within the detection limits of the LC-MS instrument. Third, the chemical components in the database were classified, and the fragmentation patterns of the major components were summarized based on the structure of each compound type. The mass spectrometry data were simultaneously analyzed using diagnostic ion filtering, neutral loss analysis, and CD software inference methods. This approach enables a rapid and comprehensive analysis of the chemical composition of BCFW. Mass spectrometry detection has inherent limitations, including difficulty detecting components like polysaccharides (from Polygonatum odoratum (Mill.) Druce and Lycium barbarum L.) and steroidal saponins (from Achyranthes bidentata Bl. and Ophiopogon japonicus (L. f.) Ker-Gawl.) using C18/ESI methods. Furthermore, it cannot distinguish isomers. For example, BCFW contains 23 compounds with the molecular formula C10H8O3. Although these compounds can be identified as coumarins through diagnostic ions, their specific structures cannot be distinguished. IMS offers considerable potential for distinguishing isomers, providing a foundation for further research on differentiating chemical components in BCFW (Dodds, Solosky, Disselkoen, Joseph, & Baker, 2025; Nielsen et al., 2025). These results indicate that BCFW contains a diverse range of chemical components, primarily flavonoids, coumarins, organic acids, terpenes, alkaloids, and phthalates.

In recent years, numerous studies have consistently reported the pharmacological effects of these compounds, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties. For example, rutin is a valuable compound known for its anti-inflammatory and anticancer effects. Research has shown that rutin inhibits acute pancreatitis induced by AP and oncogenic Kras-driven tumorigenesis by suppressing ADM (Dadgar, Maleki Dizaj, Alizadeh Oskoee, Pournaghi Azar, & Jafari Navimipour, 2025). Additionally; naringin has been reported to exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects in both acute and chronic animal models of rheumatoid arthritis (RA). Studies indicate that naringin treatment inhibits the phosphorylation of Akt and ERK in RA-FLS (Aihaiti et al., 2021). Hesperidin; a prominent active component in Citrus aurantium L. (CAL); also demonstrated anti-inflammatory properties. Research has shown that hesperidin significantly inhibits the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway; alleviating ulcerative colitis in mice (Liu et al., 2025). Psoralen has been found to play a protective role in the treatment of osteoarthritis by modulating the Nrf2 pathway; thereby reshaping the microenvironment of articular cartilage (Yang et al., 2025). In conclusion, the synergistic effects of various compounds make BCFW highly effective in treating arthralgia caused by wind-cold-dampness obstruction.

Volatile components

According to relevant literature, herbal plants such as Notopterygii Rhizoma et Radix, Saposhnikoviae Radix, and Angelicae Pubescentis Radix in BCFW contain volatile components, although some of these components are present in trace amounts and are difficult to detect (Hu, Na, Xue, Gao, & Yang, 2024). As a trace detection technique for VOCs; HS-GC-IMS combines the high separation efficiency of gas chromatography with the enhanced sensitivity of ion mobility spectrometry. This method allows for not only the qualitative and quantitative analysis of volatile components but also the identification of various aroma compound types (Losch, Liedtke, Vautz, & Weigend, 2023). For instance; esters have fruity aromas; ketones exhibit green and medicinal scents; terpenes have woody and fresh sweet aromas; aldehydes present bitter-almond and green notes; furans impart fruity and ether-like aromas; and alcohols are associated with wine and light scents (Garrido-Delgado, Dobao-Prieto, Arce, & Valcárcel, 2015; Procida, Cichelli, Lagazio, & Conte, 2016). This study is the first to apply HS-GC-IMS technology for identifying volatile components in BCFW. The method enables the rapid and accurate detection of significant differences among 10 batches of BCFW. Since limonene, α-Pinene, β-Pinene, and γ-Terpinene are major components, they may serve as key active ingredients in BCFW.

Limonene, a monoterpene found in citrus fruits, has demonstrated a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities. Studies have highlighted limonene's biological versatility, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antitumor, antidiabetic, neuroprotective, and gastroprotective effects (Rakoczy et al., 2025). Research has also shown that α-pinene exhibits anti-inflammatory activity in the Berry Essential Oil of Juniperus phoenicea L.var. Turbinata (Osmani et al., 2025). Chemical analysis identified β-pinene (38.73%) and α-pinene (11.79%) as the major components of Algrizea minor essential oil; which displayed antibacterial activity (Amanda et al., 2025). The cellulase-HD extract was found to be predominantly composed of γ-terpinene (14.981%); limonene (13.352%); and α-terpineol (8.085%) and studies have shown that it possesses antioxidant properties (Gu, Lv, Nian, Xie, & Yang, 2025). These volatile components work synergistically to enhance the therapeutic effect of BCFW in treating rheumatoid arthritis. Additionally; HS-GC-IMS offers several advantages over GC–MS; including its cost-effectiveness; low maintenance requirements; and fewer environmental constraints. HS-GC-IMS can serve as a new; effective; and complementary analytical technique for discrimination purposes (Tucci et al., 2025). Furthermore; this technology has applications in food classification (Zhou et al., 2025); and can identify dynamic changes in the flavor and metabolic conditions of food under varying processing conditions (Mazzucotelli et al., 2025). This study demonstrated the use of HS-GC-IMS to establish a fingerprint evaluation method for BCFW, which can further enhance quality control and help ensure the stability of its therapeutic effects.

Conclusion

In this study, a combined approach using UHPLC-Q-Exactive Orbitrap MS and HS-GC-IMS techniques was employed to comprehensively characterize the chemical components of BCFW. The compound database, CD software, and diagnostic ion filtering strategy were utilized for mass spectral analysis. A total of 590 compounds were rapidly identified in BCFW using UHPLC-Q-Exactive Orbitrap MS, of which 32 compounds were unequivocally identified by comparing retention time and MS/MS data with those of reference compounds. Additionally, 68 VOCs were detected from BCFW using HS-GC-IMS, and the HS-GC-IMS fingerprints of BCFW were established. These fingerprints allow for a visual analysis of the VOC differences across BCFW batches, providing valuable methods and references for its quality control. Furthermore, this analytical approach can be applied to the comprehensive characterization and identification of chemical components in other bioactive compound-based formulations.
