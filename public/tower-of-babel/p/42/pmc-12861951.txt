The MAZE procedure in atrial fibrillation: An evolution of rhythm surgery
Authors: Antoine HG Driessen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Cox-Maze procedure is the cornerstone of surgical rhythm-control therapy for atrial fibrillation (AF). Developed to interrupt macro-reentrant conduction while preserving physiological atrial activation. It has evolved from the original cut-and-sew Maze I–III Operations, based on earlier strategies like a corridor surgery to today's energy-based Cox-Maze IV (CM-IV) and minimal invasive operations. Contemporary surgical ablation can be performed concomitantly with valve or coronary surgery, as a stand-alone operation, or via minimally invasive or hybrid strategies integrating electrophysiologic mapping.Numerous works is done by Cox, providing the understanding, development and acceptance for this surgery. Later, early work was done by Ad and Damiano. The minimal invasive approach, originally developed by Wolff and later performed through VATS by van Boven was based on combining the work of Cox, with the landmark paper of Haissageurre in 1998, showing that the origin of AF is mostly situated in the PVI's. This was further developed in a Hybrid approach. Providing evidence, in diverse ways, from the groups of Maastricht and Amsterdam.In concomitant operation, performing a Maze or a PVI beside another cardiac operation, current evidence shows durable sinus-rhythm restoration and survival benefit without added operative risk. Future directions center on advanced imaging, patient tailored lesion design and new energy sources like non-thermal pulsed-field ablation (PFA) next to combining surgical and catheter-based therapies in hybrid operating rooms. This review traces the evolution, evidence, technical refinements, and forward trajectory of Maze surgery.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia encountered in clinical practice and a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide. It affects nearly 2 % of the global population and accounts for one in four strokes after age 40 (1). Its prevalence doubles with each decade of life and is strongly linked to hypertension, obesity, and valvular disease. AF increases all-cause mortality by 30–50 % and imposes major economic and societal costs through recurrent hospitalizations and loss of productivity [2]. In Asia, the burden is increasing faster than in Western regions because of rapid urbanization, hypertension, and obesity [1].

AF confers a five-fold increased risk of stroke, three-fold risk of heart failure, and two-fold increase in overall mortality [3]. These outcomes stem from electrical and structural remodeling. Progressive atrial fibrosis, myocyte hypertrophy, and gap-junction, create conduction heterogeneity that allows multiple simultaneous re-entrant wavelets. The clinical trajectory—from paroxysmal to persistent and long-standing persistent (LSPAF)—reflects this substrate evolution. Pharmacologic therapy remains limited: antiarrhythmic drugs maintain sinus rhythm in fewer than half of patients at one year and carry proarrhythmic risks [4]. Rate control alone does not eliminate stroke risk and fails to prevent atrial remodeling. Consequently, procedural rhythm-control strategies have gained prominence. Catheter ablation, introduced in the late 1990s, established pulmonary-vein isolation (PVI) as the cornerstone of interventional AF therapy. However, for patients with persistent or long-standing persistent AF (PersAF/LSPAF), durability remains suboptimal: 12-month arrhythmia-free survival averages 45–55 % despite advanced mapping systems [5]. Extensive posterior-wall and left-atrial disease often precludes complete transmural lesions via the endocardial approach. Surgical ablation offers direct epicardial access, controlled lesion transmurality, and simultaneous management of structural pathology. It remains the definitive rhythm-control option when catheter ablation fails or when concomitant cardiac surgery is required [6].

The Maze procedure, conceived by James L. Cox in the late 1980s, transformed the surgical landscape by introducing an anatomic solution to an electrophysiologic problem. Its lesion set channels sinus impulses through a predefined path (“maze”) to the atrioventricular node while blocking macro-reentry circuits [7]. Over three decades, the operation has transitioned from extensive incisions to elegant, reproducible energy-based lines and, more recently, to thoracoscopic and hybrid forms. In patients already undergoing valve or coronary surgery, concomitant ablation can restore durable sinus rhythm without significant incremental risk.

Guidelines reinforce its importance: the 2023 Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) and 2024 ESC/EACTS guidelines both assign a Class I recommendation for performing surgical ablation of AF during any indicated open cardiac operation [8,9] They further advocate left-atrial-appendage (LAA) management for stroke prevention and continu. ous rhythm monitoring. Despite this, adoption remains inconsistent. Registry analyses reveal that only 20–25 % of eligible patients undergoing mitral-valve or coronary surgery receive a Maze ablation [10]. Barriers include perceived procedural complexity, variable training, and limited awareness of outcome data. These gaps persist even though multiple studies show that maintaining sinus rhythm after cardiac surgery improves long-term survival and reduces stroke rates [10].

Recent randomized and large observational trials have re-emphasized the prognostic importance of rhythm control. The EAST-AFNET 4 trial demonstrated that early rhythm-control therapy, reduced cardiovascular death and hospitalization compared with usual care [11]. Similarly, the CASTLE-AF trial confirmed survival benefit of ablation in heart-failure patients [12]. These findings have renewed interest in durable rhythm-control strategies, including surgical and hybrid Maze approaches, particularly for patients with concomitant structural disease or failed catheter ablation.

Historical evolution of the maze procedure

Early mapping and the pathophysiologic basis

The Maze procedure emerged from an era of intense experimental electrophysiology during the late 1970s and 1980s. Using high-resolution epicardial mapping in canine and human atria, Allessie and Schuessler independently demonstrated that AF is maintained by multiple functional re-entrant wavelets traveling through regions of non-uniform conduction and refractory dispersion [13,14].

Building on these insights, James L. Cox at Washington University (St. Louis) began exploring surgical solutions, to interrupt all potential Macro-reentrant circuits. Earlier techniques, such as left atrial isolation—used primarily in patients with rheumatic mitral disease—isolated the fibrillating atrium from the rest of the heart but sacrificed atrial transport and predisposed to thrombus formation [15]. Cox reasoned that complete electrical compartmentalization of the atria could terminate re-entry while maintaining contractile function if the incisions were properly configured.

In 1987 Cox and colleagues performed the first clinical Maze I operation following detailed intraoperative mapping that identified AF as a macro-reentrant arrhythmia sustained by multiple functional circuits [7]. The cut-and-sew Maze I employed a complex network of right- and left-atrial incisions designed to block re-entrant pathways while preserving sinus conduction to the atrioventricular node. Early success confirmed the mechanistic concept but revealed postoperative atrial transport dysfunction due to excessive atriotomies [15,16].

Subsequent refinements produced the Maze III (1991), now considered the “classic” Cox-Maze. This iteration restored mechanical atrial contraction by optimizing incision geometry and eliminating unnecessary atriotomies. Cox's 10-year follow-up reported >90 % freedom from AF, low stroke incidence, and preserved atrial function [16]. However, the operation's technical demands and long cross-clamp times limited widespread adoption outside specialized centers [16].

During the 1990s, numerous centers sought simplified Maze procedures. The “left-atrial Maze” (LAMA) targeted only the pulmonary veins and posterior wall, reducing operative time but providing inferior rhythm outcomes, especially in long-standing persistent AF (15). Others combined the Maze with valve repair (“biatrial Maze”) or developed mini-Maze variations emphasizing pulmonary-vein isolation alone [17]. These experiences confirmed that complete lesion sets—particularly the posterior-wall “box” and mitral-isthmus lines—were essential for durable success (Fig. 1).

Transition to energy-based maze (Cox-Maze IV)

In 2002 Damiano introduced the Cox-Maze IV (CM-IV), replacing incisions with linear lesions created by bipolar RF clamps and cryoprobes. This dramatically simplified the operation and reduced ischemic time from >90 to ≈45 min. The bipolar clamp ensures simultaneous heating from both sides of the tissue, guaranteeing transmurality in ≤10 s, while cryoablation provides safe lesion creation near valve tissue or the conduction system [18].

CM-IV replicates the Maze III geometry: bilateral PVI, a posterior-left-atrial “box,” connecting lines to the mitral and tricuspid annuli, and right-atrial lesions preventing flutter. Validation studies confirmed equivalent rhythm outcomes and superior safety compared with cut-and-sew techniques [19] (Fig. 2).

Classic CM-IV lesion set – PV isolation, posterior box, and connecting mitral/tricuspid isthmus lines [36].

Minimally invasive evolution and continuing influence

As the CM-IV proved durable, international adoption grew. European and American centers adapted and developed the technique to smaller incisions, creating right mini-thoracotomy and port-access variations suitable for stand-alone or hybrid procedures [20]. These approaches preserved full lesion geometry while avoiding sternotomy. Concomitantly, robotic assistance and three-dimensional endoscopy enhanced visualization and precision.

Long-term multicenter registries confirm that outcomes are consistent. In a meta-analysis of >40 000 patients, concomitant CM-IV was associated with a decreases risk of long term mortality (HR 0.78), Stroke (HR 0.60) heart failure rehospitalization (HR 0.92) and more freedom from AF during follow-up, (relative risk 1.93) [21]These results match or surpass those from catheter ablation in persistent AF populations.

The transition from the Maze III to the efficient CM-IV transformed surgical ablation from a niche procedure into a universally applicable therapy. Beyond direct patient benefit, the Maze established foundational principles that guided catheter ablation development—especially the importance of posterior-wall containment on top of PVI. Most modern endocardial lesion sets are, in effect, endocardial reinterpretations of Cox's surgical blueprint.

The work of Cox and his Maze procedures provided the conceptual framework for subsequent innovations: minimally invasive thoracoscopic ablation, hybrid approaches integrating electrophysiologic mapping. Each new technology continues to trace its lineage to Cox's original insight: rhythm restoration through anatomical interruption of atrial macro-reentry while preserving physiologic conduction.

Stand-alone maze procedures

Indications

The stand-alone Cox-Maze IV (CM-IV) is indicated for symptomatic patients with drug-refractory atrial fibrillation, typically after failed catheter ablation or in those with markedly dilated atria where endocardial therapy is unlikely to achieve durable transmural lesions [18]. The operation also benefits younger patients seeking durable rhythm control without long-term antiarrhythmic medication.

Surgical approach

Access can be obtained through median sternotomy, right mini-thoracotomy, or totally thoracoscopic port approach. Bipolar RF clamps isolate all pulmonary veins and the posterior wall; cryoprobes complete the mitral- and tricuspid-isthmus lines near valve tissue. Intraoperative pacing manoeuvres can confirm conduction block. When performed through a mini-thoracotomy, the operation may be combined with robotic assistance to enhance visualization. Left-atrial-appendage (LAA) exclusion through stapler or clip occlusion is performed under direct visualization and is routine to minimize stroke risk [22].

Video 1: Mini-thoracotomy CM-IV with bipolar RF box isolation and cryo-mitral line [23].

Long-term data from Damiano's series show 89 % freedom from atrial arrhythmia at 1 year and 77 % at 5 years, with 70 % off antiarrhythmic drugs [24]. Operative mortality is typically <1 %, and major morbidity ≈ 3–4 %.

In the multicenter CURE-AF registry, which included 807 stand-alone CM-IV operations, the overall 5-year freedom from atrial arrhythmia was 76 %, validating reproducibility across institutions [25]. Predictors of late recurrence were long AF duration, left-atrial diameter >6 cm, and incomplete posterior-wall isolation [26].

When compared with repeat catheter ablation for long-standing persistent AF, stand-alone Maze provides superior rhythm durability (5-year arrhythmia-free survival >70 % vs 43 %) and markedly lower need for re-intervention [24]. Quality-of-life indices, particularly the SF-36 physical-component score, improve significantly within 6 months after surgery. In experienced hands, stand-alone CM-IV rivals catheter ablation in persistent AF while providing improved rhythm control.

Minimally invasive stand-alone and hybrid

The transition from median sternotomy to minimally invasive techniques reflect the broader surgical trend toward reducing morbidity without compromising efficacy. Traditional Maze operations, though effective, required cardiopulmonary bypass and large incisions. As energy-based devices matured, it became feasible to reproduce the Cox-Maze IV lesion set through limited thoracic access without cardiac arrest or atriotomies.

After the development of the Wolff Minimaze through bilateral Mini thoracotomy's, van Boven demonstrated the feasibility to perform a Minimaze, totally thoracoscopic. Shortly after, this was adapted in more Dutch center's also incorporating several Hybrid approaches. In a 2012 prospective cohort of 81 patients with persistent or long-standing AF, freedom from arrhythmia at 2 years was 84 %, with no peri-operative mortality [27]. The group of LaMeir and Pison emphasized rigorous lesion validation partnering with electrophysiologists for staged endocardial confirmation and early rhythm follow-up, forming the basis for subsequent thoracoscopic programs across Europe and Asia.

The Amsterdam Group, integrated thoracoscopic epicardial ablation with real-time high-density mapping in the electrophysiology laboratory, demonstrating >90 % acute pulmonary-vein isolation and ≈75 % 2- year sinus-rhythm maintenance in patients with long-standing persistent AF [28].

These collaborative programs have been pivotal in redefining stand-alone and hybrid AF surgery as a continuous spectrum rather than competing modalities. The shared surgical-electrophysiologic expertise pioneered in Maastricht and Amsterdam underpins today's “AF Heart-Team” philosophy recommended by ESC/EACTS.

Hybrid integration

The minimally invasive Maze serves as a natural platform for hybrid ablation, wherein surgical epicardial lines are supplemented by endocardial mapping and gap closure. Programs lean Amsterdam and Maastricht demonstrated that combining these skill sets yields superior lesion completeness and long-term efficacy for long-standing AF.

Between 2008 and 2015, La Meir and Pison (Leuven, Belgium) performed more than 400 thoracoscopic bi-atrial hybrid procedures, reporting 84 % freedom from AF at 2 years and no operative mortality in the first -patient cohort of 78 patients {Pison, 2014 #44. Extended follow-up confirmed 73 % sinus-rhythm maintenance at 5 years, comparable to sternotomy CM-IV results. His meticulous lesion standardization and emphasis on LAA exclusion established a procedural template adopted globally.

The Amsterdam Program integrated thoracoscopic ablation within the electrophysiology laboratory, combining endoscopic PVI with immediate high-density mapping to verify bidirectional block. Their hybrid registry reported >90 % acute PVI and ≈75 % 2-year arrhythmia-free survival in long-standing persistent AF{Krul, 2011 #70}. Robotic and port-access variants

Robotic and endoscopic platforms extend the minimally invasive concept. Using five 8-mm robotic ports, surgeons can complete a full bi-atrial lesion set on the arrested or beating heart. The system's dexterity facilitates precise cryo-application at the mitral annulus and reduces bleeding risk.

The port-access approach (small right thoracotomy with peripheral cannulation) permits cardiopulmonary bypass if needed for combined procedures such as LAA resection or septal myxoma removal. This hybrid access balances exposure with cosmetic and recovery advantages without a difference in rhythm success. While minimally invasive cases enjoyed reduced transfusion and shorter length of stay. Procedure-related mortality remain low.

The existing registries confirm earlier findings with improved QoL in 6 months. Importantly, recurrent atrial tachycardias were readily treatable by catheter touch-up, reflecting complementary synergy between surgical and electrophysiologic follow-up.

Technical challenges and learning curve

Although minimally invasive Maze procedures are safe, they demand a steep learning curve. Studies suggest 15–25 cases are required for consistent lesion completion and efficient cardiopulmonary management. Challenges include limited reach to the posterior mitral isthmus, difficulty in left-sided cryo-application through small ports, and reliance on single-lung ventilation tolerance.

To mitigate these issues, centers adopt a team-based workflow where one surgeon focuses on right-sided lesions and another on left-sided ablation. Incorporating electrophysiologic testing intraoperatively shortens the curve and reduces incomplete lines. Simulation training and dry-lab practice with bipolar clamps have been known to accelerate proficiency.

Summary

Restoration of sinus rhythm after stand-alone Maze correlates with reverse atrial remodeling and improvement in left-ventricular ejection fraction. MRI studies reveal a 20–30 % reduction in left-atrial volume at 1 year and decreased fibrosis progression. These structural benefits translate into lower long-term incidence of heart-failure hospitalization and stroke compared with medically managed persistent AF [33].

Stand-alone Maze surgery provides the most durable rhythm-control strategy for patients with non-paroxysmal, drug-refractory AF. The evolution from full sternotomy to thoracoscopic and robotic platforms has broadened applicability while maintaining excellent outcomes. With low mortality, high rhythm success, and consistent functional improvement, CM-IV remains the reference standard against which all other AF ablation strategies are measured. Concomitant Maze procedures

Rationale and indications

Atrial fibrillation commonly coexists with structural heart disease: 60 % of mitral-valve, 30 % of aortic-valve, and 10–20 % of CABG patients. AF in this setting portends worse outcomes — increased operative mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and long-term risks of stroke and heart-failure progression [34]. The concomitant Maze aims to restore sinus rhythm, reduce stroke risk, and improve survival during corrective surgery without significant incremental risk [10]. Historically, concerns regarding added cross-clamp time or pacemaker need limited adoption. However, successive studies have demonstrated that when performed by experienced teams, the incremental risk is minimal while long-term benefit is substantial [34].

Guidelines from both STS (2023) and ESC/EACTS (2024) classify concomitant surgical ablation as Class I (Level A) when performed by experienced teams [8,9]. Contraindications include emergency surgery, significant hemodynamic instability, or inability to prolong cross-clamp time safely.

Resource use and cost-effectiveness

Although concomitant ablation adds 15–25 min of cross-clamp time and modest disposable costs. Beyond clinical outcomes, economic analyses consistently show favorable cost-effectiveness. The incremental procedure cost is offset within several years by lower AF-related hospitalizations, reduced anticoagulation monitoring, and fewer stroke-related disabilities. Quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) modeling yields cost-utility ratios well below commonly accepted thresholds for cardiovascular interventions. The benefits are can be expected to be particularly pronounced in healthcare systems where stroke and heart-failure admissions impose major societal costs.

From the beginning a focus on the success of the maze procedure is in reducing these health related costs. Largely based on reducing the stroke risk and improving cardiac function. It is expected that adding the closure of the left atrial appendage and restoring sinus rhythm will decrease the thromboembolic risk but it is shown that this is not true for an enlarged atrium or an atrium without a mechanical contraction at baseline [37].

Clinical outcomes

The cumulative evidence supporting concomitant Maze is robust. The CURE-AF and PRAGUE-12 trials showed significantly greater sinus-rhythm maintenance at 1 year with concomitant ablation (60–80 %) compared with surgery alone (20–40 %), without higher mortality or morbidity [25,34]. Multiple randomized and observational studies confirm durable rhythm control without increased mortality or morbidity [19,25,38]. In a meta-analysis of >11 000 patients, concomitant Maze increased 1-year freedom from AF from 33 % to 77 %, with no rise in early mortality or pacemaker implantation [10]. Stroke reduction is expected and the addition of LAA excision or occlusion further lowers thromboembolic risk [22].

Long-term registry data from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Adult Cardiac Surgery Database show higher 10-year survival in patients who received concomitant ablation during mitral surgery compared with controls (68 % vs 57 %, p < 0.01) [22].

Restoration of sinus rhythm and LAA exclusion together yield stroke rates towards 1 %/year Restoration of atrial transport improves cardiac output by, translating into higher exercise capacity and better quality-of-life scores. Early concerns about sinus-node dysfunction have proven unfounded; permanent pacemaker requirement is consistently <5 % in modern series [39].

Predictors of success and failure

Predictors of durable sinus rhythm include smaller LA diameter (<6 cm), early restoration of sinus rhythm, and biatrial lesion completion [27]. Incomplete mitral-isthmus lines and lack of posterior-wall isolation predict recurrence [28]. Advanced age and renal dysfunction correlate with lower success but do not preclude benefit [40,41].

Summary

Concomitant Maze surgery is now supported by over three decades of evidence demonstrating durable rhythm control, stroke reduction, and survival benefit without excess risk. Advances in bipolar RF and cryo technology have made the operation efficient and reproducible, while guideline endorsement has solidified its role as standard of care. The contemporary consensus views concomitant Maze ablation not as an optional add-on but as a standard component of surgical management in AF patients undergoing open procedures. Both STS 2023 and ESC/EACTS 2024 emphasize institutional program development, multidisciplinary decision-making, and mandatory rhythm follow-up [8,9] (Table 3).

Convergent approaches

Rationale and conceptual foundation

Despite the success of both catheter and surgical ablation, neither alone achieves optimal results in long-standing persistent atrial fibrillation (LSPAF), where extensive atrial remodeling leads to complex arrhythmogenic circuits. Catheter ablation provides precise mapping but limited lesion depth and durability on the posterior wall, whereas surgical ablation ensures transmurality but lacks real-time electrophysiologic feedback. The hybrid approach merges these strengths: surgical epicardial ablation creates durable lesions, while endocardial mapping and touch-up confirm conduction block and address residual gaps.

Historical development

The first hybrid AF ablations emerged in the late 2000s. La Meir and colleagues proposed a staged workflow: thoracoscopic epicardial pulmonary-vein isolation (PVI) followed by catheter confirmation several weeks later. This early experience proved the feasibility of complementary lesion delivery and mapping verification [27,44].

Building upon the Amsterdam Hybrid Program, formally integrating surgeons and electrophysiologists in a single procedural setting [41,45]. Their approach emphasized thoracoscopic bilateral PVI, posterior-wall “box” isolation, ganglionated-plexus ablation, and ligament-of-Marshall dissection, followed immediately by epicardial high-density mapping using a 3-D navigation system (BARD). This method allowed bidirectional block testing in real time and correction of incomplete lines before concluding the case.

The ESC/EACTS 2024 and STS 2023 guidelines recognize hybrid ablation as a reasonable (Class IIa) strategy for patients with persistent or long-standing AF after failed catheter ablation or with markedly enlarged atria [8,9]. Dedicated hybrid centers, equipped with combined surgical-electrophysiologic teams and advanced imaging, are expected to drive future growth.

The Convergent procedure and randomized evidence

The Convergent approach accesses the posterior left-atrial surface through a small subxiphoid incision with a pericardioscopic catheter to deliver unipolar RF lesions, followed by endocardial testing and catheter ablation of PVs and isthmus lines [46]. In the CONVERGE trial (2020), hybrid therapy achieved 67.7 % freedom from atrial arrhythmia vs 50 % for catheter ablation alone (p = 0.036) with similar major-complication rates (46. A larger meta-analysis confirmed sustained benefit and improved symptom scores but at a higher complication rate {Eranki, 2022 #52).

Summary

Hybrid AF ablation represent the most sophisticated evolution of the Maze concept. By uniting surgical epicardial access with endocardial mapping verification, this techniques achieve the completeness and durability that elude either modality alone. The pioneering work in different centers around the world has defined procedural standards and proven that multidisciplinary cooperation is the cornerstone of durable rhythm control in complex AF. The newer CONVERGE platform is promising but at this point still shows a higher complication rate in larger meta-analysis.

Hybrid platforms increasingly incorporate the work of Cardiologist and surgeons as the historical division between surgery and electrophysiology will continue to dissolve.

Post-operative management, rhythm monitoring

Early rhythm outcomes and the “blanking period”

Comprehensive peri-operative rhythm management maximizes Maze durability and patient safety. Post-operative atrial tachyarrhythmias occur in up to 40 % of cases and should be viewed as part of the remodeling process rather than procedure failure [38]. In the so-called Blanking period with takes up to 3 months. multiple series, including Damiano's CM-IV cohort, confirm that >80 % of patients in sinus rhythm at three months rem ain arrhythmia-free at five years [24].

Predictors of early success include Immediate sinus rhythm at weaning from bypass, Restoration of atrial contraction on intraoperative TEE, Small left-atrial (LA) diameter (<60 mm), Short AF duration (<5 yers) [26].

Rhythm control strategy

Short-term antiarrhythmic therapy (amiodarone or sotalol for 6–12 weeks) helps suppress early atrial arrhythmias and allows electrical remodeling [35]. Early cardioversion is recommended if AF or atrial tachycardia persists be yond 48 h. Maintaining sinus rhythm in the first 3 months predicts long-term success.

Rhythm surveillance

Structured rhythm follow-up ensures exact success assessment. The 2024 ESC/EACTS guidelines recommend at least 7-day Holter or patch monitoring at 3, 6, and 12 months, with annual surveillance thereafter [9]. Implantable loop recorders offer continuous monitoring and better detection of asymptomatic recurrence [47].

Anticoagulation and stroke prevention

All patients receive systemic anticoagulation for ≥3 months after ablation, according to CHA2DS2-VASc score [9]. Although it is a common assumption that if the left-atrial appendage (LAA) is excised or occluded and sinus rhythm is maintained, anticoagulation can be discontinued, this is not supported by current evidence (LAAOS III and EXCLUDE), [22,48]. As no evidence is available, it should be continued according to the CHA2DS2-VASc score.

Ten-year follow-up from the STS database reveals superior survival (68 % vs 57 %) and 40 % lower stroke incidence when concomitant Maze is performed [10]. These benefits persist after risk adjustment for age and comorbidity [10] (Chart 1).

Hybrid approach

The AFACT trials and the studies performed in the group of La Meir and Pison document 65–70 % arrhythmia-free survival at 1 year, improving to >75 % at 2 years in recent single-center series employing high-density mapping [41].

Convergent procedures

The CONVERGE trials document 65–70 % arrhythmia-free survival at 1 year, improving to >75 % at 2 years in recent single-center series employing high-density mapping [46,49]. Combining surgical posterior-wall isolation is a promising technique although the complication rate at this moment is higher than in a hybrid setting or catheter based ablation [49].

Predictors of durable rhythm control, recurrence and management strategies

Incomplete lesions, extensive fibrosis, or untreated isthmus gaps are still leading causes of recurrence. Incorporating intraoperative mapping and impedance feedback has reduced late reconnection rates from 18 % to below 8 % in recent hybrid cohorts [50].

Late recurrence typically arises from reconnection of the posterior box or mitral-isthmus line, or from new atrial tachycardia circuits.

Most recurrences occur within two years and are amenable to catheter touch-up. Incorporating mapping feedback during the index surgery reduces this risk.

Risk-factor modification is equally critical: weight reduction, treatment of sleep apnea, hypertension control, and avoidance of excessive endurance exercise all improve maintenance of sinus rhythm. In the ARREST-AF surgical substudy, patients achieving >10 % weight loss maintained 87 % sinus rhythm at 3 years, versus 53 % in those without lifestyle intervention [33].

Quality-of-life and functional recovery

Patients report significant improvements in the SF-36 physical and mental-component scores after Maze surgery. The AFACT and CONVERGE trials demonstrated parallel enhancements in symptom burden, exercise tolerance, and psychological well-being [41,46,51].

Cost-effectiveness and health-system success

Sustained rhythm success translates into substantial economic benefit. Analyses from U.S. and European registries reveal net cost savings within three years due to fewer hospitalizations, reduced anticoagulation, and lower stroke incidence. Cost-utility ratios for Maze ablation fall below USD 25000 per QALY—well under conventional thresholds.

In developing economies, where stroke rehabilitation resources are scarce, preventing thromboembolic events through effective rhythm control has an even greater societal impact.

Summary

Post-operative management plays a decisive role in consolidating the procedural success of Maze surgery. With structured rhythm surveillance, timely cardioversion, temporary antiarrhythmic support, and rigorous risk-factor control, modern Maze and hybrid procedures achieve 75–90 % long-term freedom from atrial arrhythmia, accompanied by striking reductions in stroke, heart-failure hospitalization, and mortality. Early sinus rhythm and complete lesion sets remain the strongest predictors of sustained success, while integrated follow-up ensures that minor recurrences are detected and addressed promptly. These outcomes underscore the Maze procedure's position as the most durable rhythm-control strategy in the contemporary management of atrial fibrillation.

Future perspectives

The convergence of surgery and catheter-based therapies

In the century after the first surgical incision for atrial fibrillation, the distinction between “surgical” and “catheter-based” ablation is dissolving. The Maze procedure, once a technically formidable open-heart operation, has evolved into a platform of approaches — sternotomy, minimally invasive, thoracoscopic, and hybrid. — united by a single electrophysiologic principle: durable anatomical interruption of re-entry circuits.

In the contemporary landscape, the most exciting transformation is the fusion of surgical and catheter techniques. Hybrid programs has proven that multidisciplinary cooperation yields superior rhythm durability and safety. This convergence heralds a future in which the Maze concept becomes not an isolated operation, but a part of thecontinuum of rhythm-restoration strategies tailored to individual patient profile.

Real-time mapping, imaging, and verification

A critical frontier for the next decade is real-time lesion assessment. As surgeons we have to cooperate with the world of the electrophysiologist. The aim is “closure certainty”: complete, gap-free posterior-wall and mitral-isthmus isolation confirmed before leaving the operating room. When combined such feedback it can reduce incomplete lesions, historically, the primary cause of late recurrence.

This integration will make the Maze not just an operation, but a measurable electrophysiologic achievement, with the surgical team empowered by objective intraoperative data together with the electrophysiologist.

Training and teamwork

The “AF Heart-Team” model—formal collaboration between surgeons, electrophysiologists, anesthesiologists, and imaging specialists—has become essential. Structured hybrid fellowships and simulation curricula are expanding, aiming to standardize competence across disciplines. The ESC/EACTS 2024 guidelines explicitly endorse multidisciplinary planning for complex AF [9].

The future Maze will be accessible worldwide, not restricted to high-volume centers. Advances in device design, reduced operative time, and simplified lesion sets are democratizing the procedure. Minimally invasive and hybrid workflows no longer require full cardiopulmonary bypass, expanding feasibility across smaller cardiac programs.

Patient-centered outcomes and global impact

Ultimately, the Maze procedure's success must be measured not only by freedom from atrial fibrillation but by improved longevity, function, and quality of life. Future research should therefore emphasize patient-centered metrics — functional recovery, cognitive preservation, and emotional well-being — alongside rhythm outcomes.

In the upcoming world, where cardiovascular disease is a leading burden and healthcare equity remains a challenge, Maze surgery offers a curative and durable intervention that aligns with the goals of sustainable global cardiology: fewer chronic drugs, fewer reinterventions, and fewer strokes. With minimally invasive technology, tele-mentoring, and follow-up, the barriers to widespread adoption are rapidly dissolving.

Conclusions

The Maze procedure has progressed from a technically demanding cut-and-sew operation to a versatile, energy-based cornerstone of rhythm control. Concomitant Maze during valve or coronary surgery provides clear survival and stroke benefits without excess risk. Stand-alone and minimally invasive CM-IV offer durable rhythm restoration for refractory AF, while hybrid approaches exemplify synergy between surgical durability and electrophysiologic precision.
