Implementation of a digital obstructive sleep apnoea pathway in a tertiary respiratory service: a prospective service evaluation
Authors: John Murray, Feargal Helly, Peter Coss, Alanna Brennan, Barry Kennedy, Brian Kent, Laura Piggott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is highly prevalent, affecting a substantial proportion of the adult population, but remains frequently underdiagnosed. Traditional face-to-face assessment pathways are slow and resource-intensive, contributing to long waits for diagnosis and treatment. Digitally supported models that combine home testing with virtual review offer a potential solution, yet real-world implementation data from tertiary respiratory services remain limited.MethodsThis prospective observational service evaluation examined a digital OSA pathway in a tertiary respiratory service at St. James's Hospital, Dublin, Ireland. During a 17-month period, adults newly referred for suspected OSA were enrolled in the digital pathway, with the option to opt out in favour of a traditional face-to-face clinic review.Enrolled patients completed app-based questionnaires, underwent WatchPAT home sleep testing, and received virtual sleep physician review via a digital platform. Primary outcomes were pathway activity and completion, diagnostic yield, referral-to-review interval, and 6-month continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) adherence; secondary outcomes included CPAP referral rates and patient satisfaction, and comparison with a retrospective face-to-face clinic cohort.ResultsOver 17 months, 23 digital clinics delivered 160 appointments to 118 patients, of whom 104 (88.1%) completed WatchPAT testing. Diagnostic yield was high, with 81/104 tests (77.9%) positive and a mean apnoea hypopnea index (AHI) in the severe range. Mean referral-to-review interval was 5.5 months. Of those with a positive test, 52 (64.2%) commenced CPAP, of whom 29 (55.8%) met standard 6-month adherence criteria.By comparison, 69 patients treated via the standard pathway commenced CPAP and 29 (42.0%) were adherent, corresponding to an odds ratio for adherence of 1.74 (95% confidence interval 0.84–3.60; p = .13). Overall, 76% of 53 respondents reported satisfaction with the digital pathway.ConclusionsA digitally integrated OSA pathway combining app-based assessment, home WatchPAT testing and virtual review was feasible to implement in a tertiary respiratory service, with high diagnostic yield and acceptable CPAP adherence in a selected cohort. These findings support further evaluation of digitally enabled OSA pathways as a potential model to expand access and reduce reliance on repeated in-person visits, including rigorous assessment of long-term outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and whether all patient groups benefit equally.Keywords: CPAP adherence, Digital health, Home sleep apnoea testing, Obstructive sleep apnoea, Telemedicine, WatchPAT

What was known

OSA is highly prevalent yet frequently underdiagnosed, and traditional face-to-face diagnostic pathways are slow and resource-intensive, contributing to prolonged waits for assessment and treatment. Home sleep apnoea testing and individual telemedicine components, such as telemonitoring and virtual review, had each shown promise, but most prior evidence focused on isolated interventions rather than fully integrated, end-to-end care pathways. Real-world implementation data from respiratory services, where operational, staffing and workflow constraints differ from controlled research settings, remained limited.

What this study adds

This prospective service evaluation shows that a fully integrated digital OSA pathway, combining app-based assessment, home WatchPAT testing and virtual sleep physician review, can be implemented within a tertiary respiratory service, achieving a high diagnostic yield and acceptable six-month CPAP adherence in a selected cohort. Adherence in the digital pathway was numerically higher than in a contemporaneous face-to-face comparator, although this difference was not statistically significant. These findings support further evaluation of digitally enabled OSA pathways to expand access and reduce reliance on repeated in-person visits, alongside rigorous assessment of long-term outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and whether all patient groups benefit equally.

Introduction

OSA is a common chronic respiratory disorder associated with significant cardiovascular, metabolic and neurocognitive morbidity. Global prevalence estimates suggest that nearly one billion adults are affected [1]. Despite this, OSA remains underdiagnosed worldwide [[2], [3], [4]]. The burden of disease continues to increase, driven in part by rising obesity rates, ageing populations and increasing clinical recognition of sleep-disordered breathing [[5], [6], [7]]. Growing pressure on diagnostic pathways risks prolonged waiting times for specialist review and sleep testing, with resultant delays in diagnosis and treatment initiation [1,[8], [9], [10]]. Beyond the direct health consequences of untreated disease, the excessive daytime sleepiness characteristic of OSA is associated with an increased risk of road traffic and occupational accidents [11,12], extending the impact of delayed diagnosis beyond the individual patient.

Traditional OSA pathways are resource-intensive, typically requiring multiple hospital attendances. A patient referred with suspected OSA has historically been placed on a waiting list for in-person assessment, followed by a separately scheduled sleep study and a further appointment to review results and discuss treatment. Even where home sleep testing is used, pathways still depend on physiologist (referred to as sleep scientists in some jurisdictions) appointments for suitability assessment, device setup and patient education, alongside separate specialist visits for consultation, results review and treatment initiation. These staff-intensive steps, together with in-laboratory polysomnography, place substantial demands on physiologist time and sleep laboratory capacity, particularly in publicly funded healthcare systems [9,13,14].

A recent internal review at our institution found that sleep-related cases account for 40% of respiratory referrals to the department, yet the next available appointment for a face-to-face sleep clinic review is 18–24 months away. Such delays between referral, diagnostic testing and clinical review may prolong time to treatment initiation, and at review diagnostics are often incomplete or limited to basic testing such as overnight pulse oximetry. Demand is also rising, greater clinical awareness across secondary and tertiary care and growing public engagement with sleep health are bringing increasing numbers of patients into already stretched pathways. Together, these factors raise concerns about the scalability and sustainability of service models and have prompted growing interest in ambulatory and digitally supported approaches to care.

Sleep medicine is particularly well-suited to telehealth, as diagnosis can be supported by remote devices, management through remote monitoring, and assessment by subjective symptom reporting (PROMs), with comparatively little reliance on physical examination relative to other respiratory diseases. Home sleep apnoea testing (HSAT) is increasingly utilised in selected patients with uncomplicated suspected OSA and allows the development of ambulatory diagnostic pathways [8,13,15]. Peripheral arterial tonometry (PAT)-based devices, such as WatchPAT, have been validated against polysomnography in selected patient groups and, like many other level 3 HSAT options, are increasingly incorporated into clinical practice [[16], [17], [18]].

Alongside advances in ambulatory diagnostics, telemedicine has become increasingly integrated into sleep medicine care pathways. This trend accelerated following the COVID-19 pandemic, during which remote models of assessment and follow-up were widely adopted to maintain service provision [19,20]. Remote CPAP monitoring, telemonitoring and virtual review pathways have since been evaluated across numerous randomised controlled trials and pooled in several systematic reviews and meta-analyses [21,22]. These studies span individual stages of the care pathway, from telemetry-supported home initiation of therapy to longer-term remote follow-up. Meta-analyses of telemonitoring interventions have consistently reported improvements in CPAP adherence relative to usual care, while studies of virtual follow-up have demonstrated adherence and patient outcomes broadly comparable to conventional face-to-face care [14,23,24].

Despite this evidence, the existing literature remains heterogeneous, with considerable variation in patient selection, technologies utilised and clinical pathway design [13,23,25,26]. Many published studies have focused on isolated interventions such as HSAT validation, telemonitoring, or remote CPAP adherence support rather than on the evaluation of fully integrated end-to-end care pathways. In addition, relatively limited real-world implementation data are available from publicly funded tertiary respiratory services, where operational, staffing, and workflow challenges may differ substantially from those in controlled research settings. This supports a need for pragmatic evaluations of digitally integrated OSA pathways within routine clinical services, including their impact on waiting times and treatment adherence. We therefore prospectively evaluated the implementation of a digitally integrated OSA pathway within a tertiary respiratory service, focusing on pathway activity, diagnostic yield, referral-to-review intervals, CPAP uptake and adherence, and comparing these with a retrospective standard-pathway cohort.

Methods

Setting

This was a prospective observational service evaluation conducted in the tertiary respiratory sleep service at St James's Hospital, Dublin, Ireland, between January 2024 and May 2025 inclusive.

Digital pathway

Adults newly referred with suspected OSA were enrolled in the digital pathway on an opt-out basis, with the option of face-to-face review. Enrolment was determined by age: patients aged 70 years or younger were eligible for the digital pathway, whereas those aged 71 years or older were allocated to the standard face-to-face pathway. The digital cohort was therefore “selected” only in comprising age-eligible patients suitable for, and willing to use, a remote pathway; no formal triage by disease severity or pre-test probability was applied. Enrolled patients were sent written information and subsequently received a text message inviting them to download the myPatientSpace (MPS) application. MPS maintains an Information Security Management System (ISMS) certified to ISO 27001 standards to secure patient data. Through the application, patients accessed educational material and completed symptom questionnaires, including the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [27], STOP-BANG questionnaire [28,29] and Epworth Sleepiness Scale [30]. Following questionnaire completion and submission, patients were posted a WatchPAT One home sleep testing device (Itamar Medical Ltd., Caesarea, Israel; a ZOLL Medical company). Sleep study data were uploaded to a secure cloud-based platform for review by the sleep service, after which patients underwent virtual video review with a sleep physician through the MPS application. Comparator cohort

Comparator data were obtained from a retrospective, face-to-face cohort comprising all patients who commenced positive airway pressure therapy through standard outpatient clinics between June 2025 and November 2025, the most recent consecutive period for which complete six-month CPAP adherence data were available at the time of analysis (May 2026). This yielded 69 consecutive patients; the cohort size was determined by the defined time window rather than being a fixed or arbitrarily selected number. Most patients (61%) in this cohort underwent oximetry-based assessment for suspected OSA, while the remaining patients underwent WatchPAT assessment.

Outcomes

A study was deemed positive for obstructive sleep apnoea when the apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) was ≥5 events per hour. Because WatchPAT derives respiratory events from peripheral arterial tonometry rather than airflow, the AHI reported here is the PAT-derived AHI (pAHI). Disease severity was graded using standard American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) criteria [31,32]: mild (AHI 5 to <15), moderate (AHI 15 to <30), and severe (AHI ≥30 events per hour).

Primary outcomes were pathway completion, diagnostic yield of WatchPAT testing, referral-to-review interval, and CPAP adherence at six months. CPAP adherence was defined as usage ≥4 h/night on ≥70% of nights at six-month review. Secondary outcomes included CPAP referral rates and patient satisfaction, assessed using a brief questionnaire after pathway completion.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as number and percentage. Comparisons between the digital and retrospective face-to-face cohorts are primarily descriptive because of the observational design and differences in baseline characteristics and diagnostic methods. Baseline continuous variables (for example, age and BMI) were compared between cohorts using the independent-samples t-test where the distribution was approximately normal (assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test) and the Mann–Whitney U test otherwise. Where indicated, comparisons of proportions (for example, CPAP adherence between cohorts) were explored using chi-square or Fisher's exact tests, with P < .05 considered statistically significant. Data were collated and descriptive statistics calculated in Microsoft Excel. The odds ratio and 95% confidence interval were derived in IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.

Ethics statement

This project was conducted as a service evaluation and reviewed locally by the Research and Innovation team and Data Protection Officer; a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) was completed and approved (ID 8861), confirming that formal research ethics committee approval was not required and that data handling complied with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the Data Protection Act 2018.

Results

Pathway activity

Twenty-three digital OSA clinics were delivered over a 17-month period (January 2024–May 2025). A total of 187 appointments were offered (123 new, 64 follow-up), of which 160 were completed - 118 new-patient appointments and 42 follow-up appointments arranged as appropriate after initial review. Twenty-seven appointments (14.4% of those offered) were recorded as did not attend (DNA).

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the 118 patients assessed are summarised in Table 1. Most referrals originated from primary care (78.8%). Patients were predominantly middle-aged (mean age 42.1 years) with a high mean BMI, and around one quarter were female. Common comorbidities included hypertension, depression and hyperlipidaemia. Baseline questionnaire scores indicated, on average, elevated daytime sleepiness and impaired sleep quality. The most frequent reasons for referral were snoring (63.6%), high BMI (41.5%), fatigue (39.8%) and witnessed apnoea (39.0%), with many patients referred for more than one indication as detailed in Table 2.

Patient flow & pathway completion

Of the 118 patients assessed, 104 (88.1%) completed WatchPAT home sleep testing and 14 did not (Fig. 2); pathway completion, the first primary outcome, was therefore 88.1%. Of the 14 patients who did not complete testing, the recorded reasons were not interested or no reason specified (n = 7), smartphone or device incompatibility (n = 3), and no available assistance with device set-up (n = 4); all 14 were redirected to standard face-to-face assessment. Of those tested, 81 (77.9%) had a positive study and 23 (22.1%) a negative study. All 81 patients with positive studies were reviewed, 52 commenced CPAP therapy, and 29 met six-month adherence criteria.

Diagnostic yield, a primary outcome, is shown in Table 3. The majority of patients who completed testing (81/104, 77.9%) had a positive study. The mean pAHI among positive studies fell within the severe range (32.5 ± 27.7 events/hour), consistent with a cohort at a relatively high pre-test probability of clinically significant OSA. The mean referral-to-review interval was 5.5 ± 1.8 months.

Treatment outcomes

Treatment outcomes for the digital and comparator cohorts are summarised in Table 4. All 81 patients with a positive study were offered CPAP, and 52 (64.2%) commenced treatment (CPAP referral and uptake, a secondary outcome). The primary outcome of six-month CPAP adherence was met by 29 of 52 patients (55.8%). The comparator comprised 69 consecutive patients commenced on CPAP via the standard face-to-face pathway with complete six-month adherence data, of whom 29 (42.0%) were adherent. Adherence was numerically higher in the digital cohort (55.7% vs 42.0%; OR 1.74, 95% CI 0.84–3.60; p = .13), although this did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted cautiously given the unmatched, observational comparison and the baseline differences in age and sex between cohorts.

Patient satisfaction

Patient satisfaction, a secondary outcome, was assessed by a survey sent to every participant. Among the 53 respondents, satisfaction was high: 76% reported being satisfied, 8% were neutral, and 14% dissatisfied, while 2% did not complete the satisfaction item - highlighting scope for further refinement of the pathway.

Discussion

This prospective service evaluation shows that a digitally integrated OSA pathway—combining app-based assessment, home WatchPAT testing and virtual physician review—can be delivered within a tertiary respiratory service, and it allows each of the study aims to be assessed against conventional care. With respect to pathway activity and completion, most enrolled patients progressed to home testing (88.1%), demonstrating feasibility within routine practice. Diagnostic yield was high (77.9% positive), and the referral-to-review interval reflected time to a single, decision-ready consultation (mean 5.5 months) rather than to a first appointment alone, in contrast to the sequential multi-visit standard pathway, for which local first-review waits currently extend to 18–24 months. Six-month CPAP adherence was acceptable and numerically higher than in a contemporaneous face-to-face comparator (55.7% vs 42.0%), although this difference was not statistically significant and is subject to important baseline differences between cohorts. Taken together, these findings indicate that the pathway met its intended aims of maintaining diagnostic performance while compressing the diagnostic-to-treatment interval and reducing in-person attendances relative to standard care, with the caveats detailed below.

Successful implementation of the pathway was demonstrated across multiple stages of care delivery, including digital patient enrolment, completion of app-based symptom questionnaires, postal distribution of HSAT devices and virtual clinical review. A total of 160 appointments were completed across 23 digital clinics during the study period, while 104 patients successfully completed WatchPAT testing. A deliberate design feature was the sequence of patient contact; enrolled patients first received written information by post and were subsequently sent a text message containing the app download link. Pre-notifying patients by post was intended to confirm the legitimacy of the pathway and reduce the risk that the invitation would be mistaken for a phishing attempt. Building trust in this way is likely to become increasingly important as services adopt app-based, message-driven pathways. Most patients successfully progressed through the pathway to completion of home sleep testing, although non-attendance and incomplete study completion remained important implementation challenges, with 27 DNAs and 14 patients not completing HSAT. The high rate of home sleep study completion supports the feasibility of integrating remote assessment, HSAT and specialist video review within routine clinical care. This end-to-end approach is consistent with the growing role for telemedicine across respiratory sleep services outlined in the recent ERS statement [15].

The diagnostic yield observed in this study was high, consistent with a cohort with a relatively high pre-test probability of clinically significant OSA. Rather than reflecting any formal triage within the service, this is likely explained by the characteristics of the referral population: the predominant reasons for referral (snoring, witnessed apnoea, obesity and daytime fatigue) corresponded to the classic clinical features of OSA, indicating that referrals carried a high clinical suspicion of the condition. The relatively young age of the cohort is largely explained by the pathway's age-based eligibility criterion, which enrolled only patients aged 70 years or younger, with older patients managed through the standard pathway. Acceptance of virtual OSA care may additionally be lower among some older patients, so this age restriction means the findings may not generalise to the wider, older sleep clinic population [26].

The comorbidity profile of the cohort further underscores the importance of expediting diagnosis and treatment. Depression was among the most frequently recorded comorbidities in a condition already associated with significant neurocognitive morbidity. The relationship between OSA and depression is recognised to be bidirectional, and untreated OSA may contribute, at least in part, to depressive symptoms and impaired cognition through mechanisms including sleep fragmentation, intermittent hypoxia and inflammation [33,34]. As treatment of OSA has been shown to improve depressive symptoms [35], prolonged waits risk leaving a potentially modifiable contributor to low mood. The cohort also carried a substantial burden of cardiovascular risk factors, with hypertension and hyperlipidaemia among the most common comorbidities, alongside a high mean BMI, and untreated OSA is independently associated with adverse cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes. Taken together, this patient profile suggests that timely diagnosis and treatment within an expedited pathway may carry benefits extending beyond the relief of sleep-related symptoms.

CPAP adherence within the digital pathway was acceptable at six-month follow-up. Mean nightly usage was broadly comparable to adherence outcomes reported within telemonitoring and remote CPAP support literature [14,36]. Adherence was numerically higher in the digital pathway cohort than in the face-to-face cohort, although interpretation is limited by differences in patient characteristics, diagnostic methodology and the non-randomised nature of the comparison. In particular, the standard-pathway cohort was older, included a higher proportion of women, and was assessed predominantly using overnight oximetry rather than WatchPAT, limiting the validity of direct inference regarding the superiority of the digital model.

Adherence to CPAP therapy is recognised to be challenging in routine clinical practice, with substantial variability reported across studies and healthcare systems [36,37]. Digital follow-up may be particularly valuable after CPAP initiation, when remotely transmitted usage, mask leak and residual event data can identify patients requiring additional support. This could further allow follow-up to be tailored according to clinical need, reducing routine hospital attendance for patients established on therapy while facilitating earlier intervention for those experiencing treatment difficulties.

Physician review within the pathway was conducted entirely by video, and the high level of patient satisfaction observed (76% of respondents) suggests that virtual review was well received despite the absence of face-to-face contact, which may reflect the convenience of reduced travel and remote access to review. This further supports evidence that video consultation supports rapport and effective communication, providing visual cues that telephone contact cannot [38,39]. Although telephone review is more widely used and less technologically demanding, comparative evidence suggests video is generally preferable where visual information contributes to assessment [40], supporting its use as the default modality within this pathway, with telephone contact reserved as a fallback. Nonetheless, virtual review cannot replicate a complete clinical examination. In sleep medicine, assessment of the upper airway and oral cavity may be clinically important. Where patients are assessed solely by video, it is therefore essential that pathways incorporate a robust governance structure ensuring that the need for face-to-face examination has been actively considered by the referring clinician, for example, through a mandatory referral checklist confirming suitability for remote assessment, to mitigate the risk that clinically relevant findings are missed.

The present findings also have implications for sleep service delivery. A particular strength of the model lies in what the referral-to-review interval represents. In the digital pathway, virtual review took place only once home sleep testing and patient-reported outcome measures were complete, so that the consultation was a single, decision-ready encounter at which the diagnosis could be confirmed and treatment initiated. This contrasts with the traditional sequential model, in which an initial clinic appointment typically precedes diagnostic testing and a further review, with delay accruing at each step. The 5.5-month interval, therefore, reflects time to a complete diagnostic and management decision rather than to a first appointment alone, and is not directly comparable with a conventional first-review waiting time. For the same reason, and because this interval was not recorded for the standard-pathway comparator, no formal between-cohort comparison of waiting time was undertaken. The pathway was designed to address several recognised pressures within the service - reducing clinic waiting lists; expediting diagnosis, review and treatment initiation; lowering costs; reducing patient travel and hospital footfall; and improving the experience of patients and staff. These aims appear to have been met in principle - the referral-to-review interval compared favourably with estimated routine waiting intervals, in-person attendances were reduced, and patient feedback was positive - although formal comparative was not performed, the reduction in face-to-face visits and use of postal HSAT suggests potential for improved clinic capacity and shorter waiting times, but a dedicated health-economic analysis incorporating staff time, device and platform costs and downstream resource use is required before efficiency or cost-effectiveness can be claimed. Although the cost savings of this study remain unquantified, the ERS statement on e-Sleep suggests that telemedicine OSA care cost savings are ‘probable’ [15].

Delivering the pathway on an opt-out basis raises important considerations regarding digital literacy and equity of access. Smartphone ownership and digital engagement are now near-universal in the general population, with recent national data indicating that approximately 95% of adults in Ireland own a smartphone [41], supporting the feasibility of an app-based, opt-out model for most patients. Nonetheless, a minority remain at risk of digital exclusion, including older patients and those with limited technological access or confidence, and the opt-out design must ensure that these patients are not disadvantaged and retain a clear route to conventional face-to-face care. From a governance perspective, the digital platform used was approved for clinical use in accordance with local patient data safety guidelines, information governance, and data protection standards, which is essential for the safe deployment of any digital pathway. Virtual pathways are, in any case, unlikely to be appropriate for all patients, particularly those with complex comorbidity, diagnostic uncertainty, limited digital literacy or a preference for face-to-face assessment [26,42].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. This was a single-centre observational service evaluation rather than a randomised comparative study, and the patients included represented a selected cohort considered suitable for virtual assessment, creating potential for selection bias and limiting generalisability to broader sleep clinic populations. In particular, the pathway's age-based eligibility (≤70 years) excluded older patients, in whom OSA is common and who may derive substantial benefit from expedited diagnosis, so the results may not generalise to this group. Related to this, the opt-out, app-based design carries a risk of digital exclusion among patients with limited technological access or confidence. The face-to-face comparator cohort was, in addition, unmatched and differed substantially in both demographic characteristics and diagnostic methodology, with most patients having undergone oximetry-based assessment rather than WatchPAT testing, which limits the validity of any direct comparison between cohorts.

Adherence, moreover, was assessed at a single six-month time point, so the longer-term durability of CPAP use and clinical outcomes beyond this period remain unknown. Data completeness was a further limitation: self-entered baseline information was affected by missingness, with BMI recorded for only 31 of 118 patients despite in-app prompting. The evaluation also did not capture the reasons for non-attendance or non-completion of home testing, limiting understanding of where in the pathway patients were lost and how engagement might be improved. Finally, the satisfaction findings warrant particular caution: responses were obtained from only the 53 patients who completed the survey (fewer than half of those enrolled), and because more satisfied patients may have been more likely to respond, the reported rate may overstate satisfaction across the whole cohort (non-response bias); the survey was, in addition, a simple service-feedback process rather than a validated patient-reported experience measure.

Conclusion

This service evaluation demonstrates that app-based assessment, postal home sleep apnoea testing and virtual specialist review can be integrated into routine OSA care for selected patients. Most patients completed home testing, and more than half of those who commenced CPAP met the prespecified adherence criterion at six months. However, the single-centre design, incomplete baseline data, low satisfaction-survey response rate and unmatched comparator limit conclusions regarding superiority, waiting-time reduction or cost-effectiveness. Prospective comparative evaluation should examine clinical outcomes, service capacity, costs, long-term CPAP use and equitable access across a broader sleep clinic population.
