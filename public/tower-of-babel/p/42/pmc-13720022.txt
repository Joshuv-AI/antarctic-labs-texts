Volatile flavoromics and metabolomics reveal quality evolution mechanism of Zhenghe white peony tea during storage
Authors: Wenlong Li, Yashu Wei, Wenmiao Wu, Yaqi Zhao, Feng Yang, Jian Chen, Juan Zhang, Zheng Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Long-term storage (aging) is considered a crucial process for improving white tea quality, yet insights into the quality formation of Zhenghe white peony tea (WPT) during storage remain insufficient. This study systematically investigates the metabolite-driven quality evolution of WPT stored for 0–12 years. Newly produced WPT was characterized by high levels of amino acids, catechins, and volatile aldehydes, ketones and alcohols contributing to umami-sweet taste and grassy-fruity aroma, while cedrol, 3-methylbutanoic acid, and total flavonoids increased with storage, potentially promoting mellow taste and aged flavor. Metabolomics identified 2522 metabolites, predominantly flavonoids, amino acids, and peptides. Storage years 4, 8, and 10 were identified as critical periods for WPT quality transformation. Notably, while proanthocyanidins decreased during storage, functional flavonoids (e.g., astilbin, hesperetin glucuronides) and specific peptides, particularly those rich in glutamate, histidine, proline, and tyrosine residues, were enriched. Furthermore, metabolic pathway enrichment analysis highlighted glycerophospholipid metabolism and phenylalanine biosynthesis as key pathways potentially involved in the flavor development and quality formation of WPT during long-term storage. Finally, the transformation networks of key flavor-associated and non-volatile metabolites during storage were integrated to provide scientific insights into the quality evolution mechanism of Zhenghe WPT.

Introduction

Tea has been regarded as one of the most widely consumed natural healthy beverages globally1. White tea, one of the six traditional categories of Chinese tea, is highly favored for its mild taste characterized by a fresh and slightly sweet flavor with low bitterness and astringency, as well as abundant bioactive components2,3. Unlike the complex manufacturing processes of other categories of tea, the processing of white tea only includes picking, withering, and drying. Such simple and mild processing conditions may have preserved a richer content of the natural nutrients in fresh tea leaves for white tea, such as tea polyphenols, amino acids, catechins, flavonoids, and so on3. These components have been reported to be closely associated with various potential health benefits such as anti-cancer, anti-diabetic, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant properties4. In addition to the withering and drying processes, long-term storage (also known as aging) is also regarded as a crucial process for further enhancing the quality and economic value of white tea5. Exploring the metabolite changes of white tea during long-term storage can provide insights into the quality evolution of white tea during aging.

The quality evolution of white tea during long-term storage involves multiple dimensions. In terms of sensory characteristics, the aroma of younger white tea is typically more grassy and green, while the woody aroma of aged white tea is more prominent3. Meanwhile, bitterness and astringency decrease with prolonged storage, accompanied by increased richness, acidity, sweetness, and smoothness6. At the metabolite level, volatile esters, linalool, and its oxides gradually decline over time, whereas dihydroactinolide, 2-methylnaphthalene, benzyl alcohol and cedrene accumulate, which may contribute to the formation of aged flavor7–9. Additionally, long-term storage leads to a reduction in the antioxidant capacity of white tea, primarily attributed to the depletion of phenolic compounds such as catechins10–12. Notably, N-ethyl-2-pyrrolidinone-substituted flavan-3-ols have been reported to increase during the long-term storage of Fuding white tea and are regarded as potential aging markers13,14. However, current studies are usually limited to differential analysis of a single type of metabolite (e.g., volatile flavor compounds, amino acids, or flavonoids) across different storage periods, which is insufficient to explain the overall quality changes of white tea during storage. Meanwhile, insights into the transformation mechanisms of key metabolites at the metabolic pathway level remain inadequate. Furthermore, previous research has mainly focused on Fuding white tea, and the metabolite-driven quality evolution mechanism of Zhenghe white tea, another core production area, during storage remains unclear.

In this study, Zhenghe WPTs stored for 0 to 12 years were collected from a single manufacturer, all produced and stored in accordance with unified standards. The overall quality differences of WPT during storage were systematically investigated at multiple levels, including sensory quantitative description, main chemical components, volatile flavor compounds, and non-volatile metabolites. On this basis, the aging characteristic metabolites of WPT were identified through multivariate statistical analysis, and their potential transformation pathways were integrated to reveal the metabolite-driven quality evolution mechanism of Zhenghe white tea during long-term storage.

Results and discussion

Sensory features of WPTs

The changes of appearance, tea preparation, taste and aroma of WPT during 0 to 12 years of storage were analyzed to preliminarily understand the quality changes of Zhenghe WPT during storage (Fig. 1A–C). The newly produced WPT (LH_0) leaves exhibited a neat appearance with distinct silvery-white trichomes, presenting a lustrous blend of silvery-white and verdant hues. By contrast, WPTs stored for over 4 years displayed overlapping silver, yellow and reddish-brown tones, with a more brittle and fragile state (Fig. 1A). The tea infusions progressively deepened in reddish-brown color with extended storage duration, similar to the changes of leaves. In terms of flavor attributes, the newly produced WPT (LH_0) recorded the highest scores in sweet (5.0) and umami (5.2) taste, accompanied by prominent floral (4.8) and fruity (5.6) aromas, indicative of a vibrant and fresh flavor profile (Fig. 1B, C). WPTs stored for 4 to 8 years exhibited a mild floral aroma (3.2 to 5.2) and sweet taste (4.4 to 6.6), with aged (3.2 to 7.2) and herbal (5.6 to 6.2) aroma starting to emerge during this period. Notably, WPT stored for 12 years (LH_12) showed muted bitter (3.2) and astringency (2.0), characterized by dominant mellow (7.1) and smooth (7.2) textures, along with intense aged (7.8) and herbal aromas (5.2). Main chemical components analysis of WPTs

The contents changes of main chemical components in WPTs were analyzed (Fig. 1D–G and Supplementary Table S1). The amino acid content accounts for ~6–9% of the dry weight of white tea and is crucial to the taste characteristics of tea18. 16 amino acids were detected in WPTs (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Table S1). Among them, theanine (Thea) is the most abundant amino acid in WPT (15.24 to 42.44 mg/g), accounting for more than 70% of the total content in all samples (Supplementary Table S1). Two umami amino acids (Asp and Thea) increased before stored for 6 years, while two sweet amino acids (Gly and Ala) rose within 8 years of storage (Fig. 1D). Notably, the content of most amino acids decreased with the increase of storage time, except for Gly, Thea, Ala, and Glu, which showed an increasing trend at the 6th year of storage. However, almost all amino acids decreased to varying degrees after long-term storage for >10 years. For instance, the concentrations of Asp, Ser, His, Met, Phe, and Leu decreased by more than 50% after 12 years of storage (Supplementary Table S1). Most amino acids contribute to umami, bitterness and sweetness, and their reduction may be an important factor for the weakening of WPT umami and sweetness with storage19.

EGCG was the most abundant catechin (38.76–66.64 mg/g), showing a trend of first increasing, then decreasing, and then increasing again (Supplementary Table S1). The contents of EGC and EC decreased obviously with storage time, while GC increased to some extent during long-term storage. This decrease in catechins may be attributed to oxidation and polymerization, which may help reduce the astringency and bitterness of the tea infusion, as well as the deepening of the tea infusion color3. The concentration of caffeine (CAF) was slightly lower at the 6th and 8th years of storage than at other periods, but remained relatively stable throughout the storage process (17.29–19.86 mg/g) (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Table S1). The contents of total polyphenols and soluble sugar decreased overall with the increase of storage time (Fig. 1E, F). Their decrease may contribute to the reduced bitterness and astringency of aged WPTs, as well as the tendency of its overall taste style to become more mellow and smooth. Flavonoids have many biological activities and provide health benefits20, and the increase in total flavonoids content during storage may help enhance the efficacy of aged white tea (Fig. 1G).

Dynamics of volatile flavor components during WPTs storage

A total of 70 volatile flavor components were detected in WPTs by HS-SPME-GC-MS, including 20 aldehydes, 19 alcohols, 11 ketones, 9 heterocyclics, 5 esters, 3 acids and 3 others (Supplementary Table S2). The variations patterns of flavor components were analyzed through concentration heat maps and K-means clustering (Fig. 2A, B). The heat maps showed that the vast majority of aldehydes and ketones were abundant in newly produced WPT, far exceeding those in WPTs stored for 4 years or more. Additionally, WPT samples stored for 4–8 years exhibited higher contents of heterocycles and esters (Fig. 2A), including [F53] 1-ethyl-1H-pyrrole-2-carboxaldehyde, [F6] 2-methylfuran, [F13] pyrazine, [F23] 2-methylpyrazine, [F36] 2,3-dimethylpyrazine, [F33] 2-ethylpyridine, [F2] methyl acetate, [F34] butyrolactone, [F54] γ-caprolactone, [F37] methyl hexoate, which may contribute to the formation of roasted, nutty, and floral-fruity aromas in white tea21. Furthermore, the content variations patterns of 70 volatile flavor components were clustered into 5 classes through K-means clustering analysis (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table S2). The contents of 40 compounds in sub classes 2 and 4 continuously decreased during storage, while 22 compounds in sub classes 1 and 5 showed a trend of first increasing then decreasing. Notably, 8 compounds in sub class 3 exhibited an overall increasing trend throughout storage, which might be signature aroma components in the storage process, including [F9] 2-methylbutanal, [F14] 2-methyl-1-butanol, [F20] 2,3-butanediol, [F25] 3-furaldehyde, [F27] 3-methylbutanoic acid, [F55] acetophenone, [F63] linalool oxide IV, and [F70] cedrol (Supplementary Table S2).Fig. 2Analysis of volatile flavor components of WPTs.A Concentration heat map of 70 volatile flavor components. PCA was used to evaluate the differences in the overall characteristics of the volatile flavor components of WPTs at different storage times (Fig. 2C). The PCA score plot illustrated distinct separations in the volatile component profiles of WPTs during the storage intervals of 0 to 4 years and 8 to 10 years, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 52.4% and 27.4% of the total variance, respectively. In contrast, the separation degrees were notably reduced between 4 to 6 years and 10 to 12 years, suggesting minimal variations in their characteristics. This observation was further corroborated by hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) based on compositional similarities (Fig. 2D), which divided the volatile flavor profiles of WPTs stored for different years into four groups, namely group 1 (LH_0), group 2 (LH_10 and LH_12), group 3 (LH_08), and Group 4 (LH_04 and LH_06). Furthermore, a PLS-DA model was constructed to identify key flavor component features that distinguish WPTs stored for different years (Q² = 0.87, R² = 0.92, p < 0.01 by 100-fold permutation tests) (Fig. S2). The PLS-DA score plot clearly demonstrated distinct separation of flavor profiles in WPTs, indicating the presence of discriminative flavor component features (Fig. 2E). Subsequently, 17 key volatile flavor compounds with VIP (Variable important in projection) more than 1 were identified (Fig. 2F). Among these, [F39] heptenal, [F67] β-cyclocitral, [F56] (3E,5E)-3,5-octadien-2-one, [F41] heptanol, [F15] 3-penten-2-one, [F18] 1-pentanol, [F30] 1-hexanol were predominantly concentrated in WPTs stored for 0 to 4 years, while [F2] methyl acetate, [F6] 2-methylfuran, [F47] o-cymene were more abundant in WPTs stored for 6 to 8 years. Notably, [F27] 3-methylbutanoic acid, [F20] 2,3-butanediol, [F9] 2-methylbutanal, [F25] 3-furaldehyde, [F70] cedrol, [F55] acetophenone and [F14] 2-methyl-1-butanol showed remarkable abundance in WPTs stored for longer times (10 and 12 years).

rOAV calculation of volatile flavor components and identification of aging characteristic flavor components in WPTs

The aroma characteristics of tea are determined by the combined effects of volatile compound concentrations and their proportions, with the contribution of individual aroma compounds to overall tea fragrance largely dependent on their rOAV22. Generally, volatile flavor compounds with rOAV ≥ 1 are considered to have a potential contribution to aroma characteristics23. A total of 37 volatile flavor compounds with rOAV ≥ 1 were identified in WPTs, including 14 aldehydes, 8 ketones, 7 alcohols, 4 heterocycles, 2 esters, 1 acid, and 1 others (Table 1 & Supplementary Table S2). In newly produced WPT (LH_0), [F58] linalool (rOAV = 116.99), [F40] benzaldehyde (rOAV = 300.90), [F56] (3E,5E)-3,5-octadien-2-one (rOAV = 401.99), [F8] 3-methylbutanal (rOAV = 135.34), [F22] hexanal (rOAV = 326.68), [F59] nonanal (rOAV = 140.14) and [F45] octanal (rOAV = 150.87) exhibited high rOAV values due to their high concentrations and low odor thresholds, potentially dominating the tea aroma at this stage (Table 1). Specifically, [F58] linalool contributed floral, citrus, sweet, and rose notes, while [F56] (3E,5E)-3,5-Octadien-2-one imparted green and fruity aromas21. Additionally, abundant aldehydes such as [F8] 3-methylbutanal, [F45] octanal, [F59] nonanal, and [F11] pentanal showed high rOAV values in newly produced WPT (Table 1). Storage altered the composition and concentration of volatiles, thereby modulating tea flavor characteristics. Most aldehydes, such as [F11] pentanal and [F22] hexanal, showed a continuous decline in rOAV during storage (Table 1), possibly leading to the gradual weakening of green and grassy notes. Meanwhile, flavor components like [F70] cedrol (rOAV reaching 70.43 in the 12th year) and [F9] 2-methylbutanal (rOAV reaching 77.30 in the 12th year) experienced increased rOAV, becoming characteristic flavor compounds in long-term stored WPTs (Table 1). [F70] Cedrol exhibited woody aromas, while [F9] 2-methylbutanal imparted distinctive fruity and fusel notes, potentially shifting the tea aroma toward a more mellow and deep character25. Notably, the rOAV of heterocyclic compounds such as [F23] 2-methylpyrazine, [F6] 2-methylfuran and [F35] 2-ethylpyrazine increase during the storage of 4 to 8 years (Table 1), which might enhance the nutty and roasted aromas of WPT. The Maillard reaction of carbohydrates and amino acids serves as the main source of heterocyclics in tea. Additionally, esters like [F2] methyl acetate reached concentration peaks during this period, enhancing sweet and floral-fruity notes and further enriching the aroma hierarchy. Finally, based on the criteria of rOAV ≥ 1, VIP ≥ 1, and increased with storage (in subclass 3 of K-means clustering), three volatile compounds, namely [F70] cedrol, [F9] 2-methylbutanal, and [F27] 3-methylbutanoic acid, were identified as aging characteristic flavor components of WPT.

Overall, the continuous reconstruction of volatile flavor compound concentrations and proportions resulted in the disparate flavor profiles of WPTs with different storage times. The newly produced WPT are dominated by green, grassy, and fruity components, presenting a fresh aroma profile. As storage time increases, the great decrease in rOAV of aldehydes with green, grassy, and fatty notes weakens the grassy odor of WPTs. Meanwhile, the accumulation of some flavor components with woody, roasted, and distinctive floral and fruity aromas promotes the evolution of WPTs’ odor toward a aged, herbal style with a profound aged character. This flavor transformation may result from the synergistic effects of multiple biological and chemical reactions, including Strecker degradation6, Maillard reactions3, and potential microbial activities5,26, ultimately forming the unique aged flavor quality of Zhenghe white peony tea.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis of WPT during storage

The metabolite profiles of WPTs stored for different times were explored by untargeted metabolomics, aiming to more comprehensively reveal the formation process of the aged quality of WPTs during storage. Following quality control and peak identification, a total of 2522 metabolites were identified under electrospray ionization positive (ESI + ) and negative (ESI−) detection modes for downstream analysis (Supplementary Table S3). Metabolites category annotation showed that flavonoids (446) were the most abundant compounds in WPTs, predominantly existing as flavonoid glycosides (206), along with isoflavonoids (66), biflavonoids and polyflavonoids (65), flavans (53) and O-methylflavonoids (32) (Fig. 3A). They were followed by carboxylic acids and their derivatives (289), among which amino acids, peptides, and analogs (265) were dominated (Fig. 3A). Analysis of flavonoids during WPTs storage

Flavonoids are the most diverse metabolite category in WPTs, and most of them possess biological activities, possibly involving potential health benefits27. PLS-DA model was established to identify the main differences inflavonoid profiles among WPTs stored for different years, with Q² = 0.88, R² = 0.94, and p = 0.03 by 100-fold permutation tests (Fig. S3). The score plot showed that the overall characteristics of flavonoids in WPTs could be well separated (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, 80 flavonoids with VIP ≥ 1 were identified as the main differential flavonoids in WPT with different storage years, 76 of which showing significant changes (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Table S4). Among them, k-means clustering analysis showed that the 23 flavonoids in subclass 3 were considered aging signature flavonoids that continuously accumulated with storage (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Table S4). Specifically, flavonoids with complex glycosylation, acylation, and methylation modifications obviously accumulated, implied that flavonoids might undergo continuous complex modification reactions during long-term storage3, such as two hesperidin derivatives (hesperetin 7-O-glucuronide, hesperetin 3’-O-glucuronide), eriodictyol and its derivative riodictyol 7-(6-galloylglucoside), and three rhamnosides (galangin 7-rhamnoside, myricetin 3-(3”,4”-diacetylrhamnoside), and astilbin (also known as taxifolin 3-O-rhamnoside) (Fig. 4B). Analysis of other metabolites (excluding flavonoids and amino acids) during WPT storage

PLS-DA was also performed on 1811 other metabolites excluding flavonoids, amino acids, peptides and analogues (Q² = 0.87, R² = 0.92, and p = 0.02 by 100-fold permutation tests) (Figs. 6A and S5). The metabolic profiles of WPT samples showed regular separation with storage years. Notably, samples from 0 to 4 years and 10 to 12 years exhibited obvious separation in material characteristics, while samples from 4 to 6 years and 10 to 12 years showed high similarity. Subsequently, 230 key metabolites with VIP score ≥1 were screened, which showed differential distribution in WPTs of different years. These mainly included organooxygen compounds (46), fatty acyls (21), glycerophospholipids (16), benzene and substituted derivatives (13), prenol lipids (12), steroids and steroid derivatives (10) (Fig. 6B). In this study, most of glycerophospholipids showed decreasing abundance with storage time, potentially participating in flavor formation during WPT storage through degradation. Integration of potential metabolic mechanisms of key metabolites during WPT storage

Finally, by referring to the KEGG pathway database and previous related studies35–37, the potential metabolic pathways of key volatile flavor components, flavonoids, amino acids, and some other metabolites during storage were integrated to provide insights into metabolite-driven quality evolution mechanisms of Zhenghe white peony tea during storage (Fig. 7). The aging signature aroma compounds 2-methylbutanal and 3-methylbutanoic acid are mainly derived from the further conversion of α-keto acids, which are produced either by the degradation of branched-chain amino acids (L-leucine and L-isoleucine) or by the stepwise synthesis from pyruvate. They also served as precursors and derivatives for higher alcohols such as 2-methyl-1-butanol and isoamyl alcohol, which are important flavor compounds commonly present in food, especially alcoholic beverages38. Given that L-leucine and L-isoleucine decline during storage, it is speculated that the degradation pathway of branched-chain amino acids may be dominant. Cedrol is a sesquiterpene alcohol consisting of three isoprene units, synthesized from farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) derived from the mevalonic acid (MVA) or methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathway39.Fig. 7The integrated network of potential metabolic pathways for key aging metabolites of WPTs during storage.It mainly includes flavonoids metabolism, amino acids metabolism, key volatile flavor components metabolism, caffeine metabolism and glycerophospholipids metabolism.

The integrated network of potential metabolic pathways for key aging metabolites of WPTs during storage.

It mainly includes flavonoids metabolism, amino acids metabolism, key volatile flavor components metabolism, caffeine metabolism and glycerophospholipids metabolism.

Flavonoids are synthesized from phenylalanine via the shikimate and phenylpropanoid pathways20. We noticed that naringenin chalcone and naringenin, the starting points of flavonoid synthesis, showed an overall increasing trend with storage (Fig. 7), which may indicate that the biosynthetic activity of flavonoids gradually increases during WPT storage, confirming the previous result of the increase in total flavonoid content. The slight decrease of hesperetin during storage may be used for the formation of two hesperidin derivatives, i.e., hesperetin 3’-O-glucuronide and hesperetin 7-O-glucuronide (Fig. 7). Three main flavonols, quercetin, myricetin, and kaempferol, may undergo complex methylation, glycosylation, and acylation modifications to form derivatives such as myricetin 3-(3”,4”-diacetylrhamnoside), isorhamnetin 3-(6”-malonylglucoside), and pinoquercetin (Fig. 7). It is worth noting that astilbin is the flavonoid with the highest fold increase during storage, which is mainly formed by the rhamnosylation modification of its precursor, dihydroquercetin. In addition, most catechins continuously decrease during storage (Fig. 7), which may contribute to the formation of specific catechin polymers and tea pigments40.

Aromatic amino acids (e.g., L-phenylalanine, L-tyrosine) are synthesized from phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) and erythrose-4-phosphate (E4P) via the shikimate pathway36. They can be further converted into coumaric acid, p-coumaric acid and their coenzyme A forms, and then enter flavonoid biosynthesis. In this study, shikimic acid and p-coumaric acid were observed to accumulate during WPT storage (Fig. 7), which can well explain the phenomenon that the total flavonoid content continuously increases with storage. During storage, L-glutamic acid and L-glutamine can be converted into 4-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and succinic acid into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, or to form 5’-phosphoribosylglycinamide (GAR) into the purine metabolism. Proline may undergo complex substitution reactions during storage, leading to the accumulation of derivatives such as 4-hydroproline, L-1-pyrroline 3-hydroxy-5-carboxylate, and L-4-hydroxyglutamate semialdehyde.

Glycerophospholipids are important lipid components in tea24. They can release fatty acids (such as linolenic acid and linoleic acid) through degradation, and these fatty acids can produce various saturated and unsaturated aldehydes and alcohols through oxidative cracking, thereby affecting the aroma quality of WPT24,32. Notably, glycerophospholipids of the Pc and LysoPc types, such as Pc(18:3/0:0), Pc(17:2(9z,12z)/0:0), LysoPC(20:5(5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z,17Z)/0:0), LysoPC(18:4(6Z,9Z,12Z,15Z)/0:0), LysoPC(18:1(11Z)/0:0), LysoPC(18:2), LysoPC(18:1), and LysoPC(17:0), consistently accumulated during WPT storage, while Ps, Pg, and Pa-related lipids such as Ps(18:2(9z,12z)/18:0), Ps(18:0/18:3(9z,12z,15z)), and Ps(14:1(9z)/22:0) decreased with storage (Fig. 7). Caffeine is one of the main anti-fatigue components in tea and coffee41, and its abundance remained relatively stable during WPT storage. However, its synthetic precursors and derivatives such as theophylline, 1,3,7-trimethyluric acid, 3-methylxanthine, and 7-methylxanthosine showed obvious accumulation, indicating that caffeine synthesis and transformation may be in a dynamic equilibrium state (Fig. 7).

Methods

Chemical reagents

All chemicals used in this study were of analytical or HPLC grade. Ethyl decanoate (≥99%, No. 148970) and C7-C30 alkanes (No. 49451-U) were from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Anthrone (≥98%, No. A46323) was from Beijing Wokai (Beijing, China). Glucose, KH₂PO₄, TEA, THF, TCA, H₃PO₄, NaAc, NaCl, and H₂SO₄ were supplied by Sinopharm (Shanghai, China). Acetonitrile (ACN, ≥99.9%, No. A119010) and methanol (MeOH, ≥99.9%, No. M116118) were from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). A mixed catechin standard (≥99%, No. CDAA-M-299008-AD) containing EGC, C, EC, EGCG, ECG, and GA was obtained from ANPEL (Shanghai, China). A mixed amino acid standard (≥99%, No. 5061-3330) containing 17 amino acids (Gly, Asp, Glu, Ser, Thr, Arg, Pro, Ala, Tyr, Cys, Val, Met, Phe, Ile, Leu, Lys, His) and o-phthalaldehyde (OPA, No. 5061-3335) were purchased from Agilent (California, USA).

Samples information

According to the Chinese national standard (GB/T 22291-2017), white tea is categorized into four varieties based on the tenderness and freshness of raw tea leaves, including silver needle white tea, white peony tea (WPT), Gongmei tea and Shoumei tea4,42. Among them, WPT is currently the most popular type, boasting the highest production and consumption levels6. In this study, six batches of WPT samples (grade I) were collected in August 2024 from Fujian Longhe Tea Industry Co., Ltd., located in Donggan Village, Zhenghe County, Nanping City, Fujian Province, China. These samples were produced in six different years, namely 2024 (LH_0, newly produced), 2020 (LH_04, stored for 4 years), 2018 (LH_06, stored for 6 years), 2016 (LH_08, stored for 8 years), 2014 (LH_10, stored for 10 years), and 2012 (LH_12, stored for 12 years). For each storage year, three independent biological replicates were prepared. All WPT samples were processed under consistent manufacturing protocols: fresh tea leaves were naturally withered at room temperature for 48–60 h, followed by drying at 70 °C for 30 min to maintain a moisture content ≤7%. During long-term storage, all samples were kept in a cool, dark, and dry room environment with temperature of 20 ± 5 °C and humidity <40%. The packaging was multi-layer sealed. The innermost layer was food-grade aluminum foil bag to isolate air, the middle layer was plastic bag for further sealing, and the outermost layer was thick carton for anti-collision and light shielding. In addition, odorless dehumidifying agents (mainly activated carbon) were used in the storage environment to keep dry and prevent mildew.

Determination of catechins and caffeine contents

Catechins (EGC, C, EC, EGCG, ECG), gallic acid (GA), and caffeine (CAF) were quantified using an HPLC system. Tea powder (0.2 g) was extracted with 5 mL of 70% (v/v) aqueous methanol in a 70 °C water bath for 10 min. After cooling to room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected and diluted to 10 mL prior to analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ODS-2 HypersilTM column (5 µm, 4.6 × 250 mm) maintained at 28 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.2% glacial acetic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B) at a flow rate of 1 mL/min, with the following gradient: 0-4 min, 8% B; 4–32 min, 8–30% B; 32–35 min, 30–8% B; 35–40 min, 8% B. The injection volume was 10 µL, and detection was carried out at 278 nm. Analyte concentrations were calculated using external standard calibration curves and expressed as mg per gram of tea (mg/g).

Determination of amino acids contents

Free amino acids were extracted following Chinese national standard (GB/T 8314-2013). Tea powder (0.6 g) was infused with 30 mL of boiling water (100 °C, 45 min). After cooling, the mixture was centrifuged at 8000 rpm (1307 × g) for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected. Protein residues were precipitated by adding an equal volume of trichloroacetic acid (TCA), followed by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was subjected to amino acid analysis. 17 amino acids (including Gly, Asp, Glu, Ser, Thr, Arg, Pro, Ala, Tyr, Cys, Val, Met, Phe, Ile, Leu, Lys, His) were quantified using a HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with an ODS-2 HypersilTM (5 µm, 4.6 × 250 mm) chromatographic column. Mobile phases A (pH 7.2) consisted of 0.4 M sodium acetate buffer containing 0.02% (v/v) triethylamine and 0.5% (v/v) tetrahydrofuran. Mobile phases B (pH 7.2) consisted of 0.4 M sodium acetate buffer mixed with 40% (v/v) acetonitrile and 40% (v/v) methanol. Gradient elution was performed at a flow rate of 1 mL/min as follows: 0–27.5 min, 8% B; 27.5–31.5 min, 8–100% B; 31.5–35.5 min, 100% B; 35.5–40 min, 8% B. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and detection was carried out at 338 nm with an injection volume of 10 µL. Prior to analysis, both standards and samples were derivatized using OPA solution. Amino acid concentrations were calculated based on external standard calibration curves and expressed as mg per gram of tea (mg/g).

Determination of soluble sugar, total polyphenols and total flavonoids contents

The test of total flavonoids content refers to the previous method5. Tea powder (1.0 g) was mixed with 30 mL of 70% ethanol and allowed to stand for 24 h, followed by ultrasonic extraction for 30 min. The mixture was then filtered and diluted to 100 mL with 70% ethanol to obtain the flavonoid extract. An aliquot (1.0 mL) of the extract was sequentially mixed with 0.4 mL of 5% NaNO3, allowed to react for 6 min, then 0.4 mL of 10% Al (NO3)3 was added and mixed. After another 6 min, 4.0 mL of 5% NaOH was added, and the volume was adjusted to 10 mL with water. The mixture was vortexed and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 500 nm against a reagent blank. Rutin standard solutions prepared in 70% ethanol were used for calibration, and total flavonoid content was calculated based on the standard curve. Total polyphenol content was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method according to the Chinese national standard GB/T 8313-2018. Soluble sugar content was measured by anthrone colorimetry as described previously43.

Analysis of volatile flavor compounds

Volatile compounds were analyzed using headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) coupled with gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) following our previous report44. Briefly, tea powder (2.0 g) was placed into a 20 mL headspace vial for volatiles extraction using a 50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 60 °C for 30 min. After extraction, the fiber was immediately desorbed in the GC-MS (Trace1310-TSQ8000, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) injector at 250 °C for 5 min. Chromatographic separation was performed on a TG-5 column (60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. The oven temperature was programmed as follows: held at 40 °C for 2 min, increased to 250 °C at 10 °C/min, and maintained at 250 °C for 5 min. The transfer line and ion source temperatures were set at 280 °C and 300 °C, respectively. Mass spectra were acquired in electron impact (EI) mode at 70 eV, scanning over a range of m/z 33-400. Volatile compounds were identified by comparing their mass spectra with the NIST library and retention indices (RI) calculated from C7-C30 n-alkane standards (Fig. S1 and Supplementary Table 2). For quantification, 10 μL of ethyl decanoate (0.1 mg/mL) was added as an internal standard prior to extraction, following the method described previously45. The dor descriptors of flavor compounds were obtained from online databases (http://www.perflavory.com and http://www.thegoodscentscompany.com). Sensory quantitative description of WPTs

Sensory evaluation of WPTs was conducted in accordance with Chinese national standard GB/T 23776-2018. The evaluation team consisted of 5 professional tea taster (3 males and 2 females) from Fujian Longhe Tea Co., Ltd. All panelists held professional tea taster certificates in accordance with the National Vocational Skill Standard for Tea Tasters of China, and had more than 15 years of continuous experience in tea sensory evaluation, ensuring that their comprehensive evaluations could well reflect the sensory quality of WPTs. Before formal evaluation, all panelists received unified training and consistency calibration for each sensory attribute to ensure the stability and consistency of scoring. The final intensity value was calculated as the mean score from the five calibrated panelists, which ensures the reliability of the sensory results. Based on previous studies and the tea taster’ experience, 6 odor attributes and 6 taste attributes were finally selected for sensory quantitative evaluation of WPTs. Odor attributes included floral aroma (flower-like scents similar to rose and violet), fruity aroma (fruit-like scents such as pineapple, green jujube, apple, banana, citrus, and plum), aged aroma (a complex, mellow, time-imparted blend of woody, nutty, Chinese medicinal herb, and jujube-like scents), sweet aroma (like caramel, honey, and brown sugar scents), grassy note (a fresh grass-like fragrance), and herbal aroma (scents of Chinese medicinal materials). Taste attributes included bitterness (one of the basic tastes), astringency (a feeling of convergence on the surface of the mouth and tongue after tasting, including dryness, roughness, tension, drag), sweetness (one of the basic tastes, the sensation from sugary foods), umami (one of the basic tastes, associated with glutamic acid and nucleotides), mellow taste (a harmonious, mild mouthfeel without any heterogeneity), and smoothness (a silky, butter-like smooth mouthfeel with no roughness after swallowing). Each attribute was rated on a 0–9 scale (0 = barely detectable, 3 = weak, 5 = moderate, 7 = strong, 9 = very strong). The final intensity value was calculated as the mean score from the five panelists and visualized using radar charts.

The sensory evaluation of tea samples was exempt from formal ethical approval under Chinese regulatory guidelines, as it constituted non-invasive food product assessment. All panelists were fully informed of the study objectives, procedures, and potential risks, and provided oral consent prior to participation. Participation was voluntary, and panelists could withdraw at any time without consequence. Strict anonymization was maintained throughout data collection to protect participant identity and privacy, and all sensory data were used with the consent of the participants.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Tea powder (50 mg) was extracted with 400 μL of methanol/water (4:1, v/v) by cryogenic grinding (6 min) followed by ultrasonication (5 °C, 40 kHz, 30 min). After incubation at −20 °C for 30 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was subjected to UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X analysis (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters, USA). Mobile phases were water/acetonitrile (95:5, v/v) and acetonitrile/isopropanol/water (47.5:47.5:5, v/v), both containing 0.1% formic acid, and the column temperature was set at 40 °C. Electrospray ionization was operated in both positive (3500 V) and negative (−3500 V) modes. Full MS scans (m/z 70–1050) were acquired at a resolution of 60,000, and MS² fragmentation spectra were collected at a resolution of 7500 with normalized collision energies of 20%, 40%, and 60%. Quality control (QC) samples, prepared by pooling equal volumes of all sample extracts, were injected every 5–15 runs to monitor analytical stability. Raw data were processed using Progenesis QI v3.0 (Waters, USA), with metabolites annotated by matching MS and MS² spectra against the HMDB database and an in-house library, and mass error was limited to <10 ppm.

Statistical analysis

K-means clustering and fold change analysis were conducted on the Metware Cloud platform. Principal component analysis (PCA), hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA), and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were performed using SIMCA 14.1 and MetaboAnalyst 6.0 to visualize metabolite distribution across WPT samples. Heatmaps for volatile compounds were generated with the heatmap package in R software, with color gradients reflecting Z score normalized abundances. For non-volatile metabolites, significant differences among WPTs were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
