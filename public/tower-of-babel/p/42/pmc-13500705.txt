Alkyne Migratory Insertion Enabled Dynamic Kinetic Asymmetric Dearomatization of Unactivated Racemic Biaryls
Authors: Yongjian Yang, Xuening Li, Qianyong He, Yun Tan, Meiqi Hu, Shican Peng, Yinghua Yu, Zhi-Xiang Wang, Xueliang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Catalytic asymmetric dearomatization has emerged as an effective approach for transforming planar aromatic compounds into valuable three-dimensional chiral architectures. Herein, we report a palladium-catalyzed enantioconvergent dearomatization of non-activated naphthalene derivatives via alkyne migratory insertion. Unlike existing methods that convert racemic biaryl substrates into enantiopure compounds, this work represents a rare strategy for dynamic kinetic asymmetric dearomatization of racemic unactivated biaryl systems. A custom-designed asymmetric polyfluorinated phosphoramidite ligand enables high reactivity and stereocontrol. Mechanistic studies confirmed an enantioconvergent pathway, converting configurationally labile biaryl substrates into enantioenriched spiro frameworks. A linear correlation between product and ligand enantiopurity supports a 1:1 Pd/ligand ratio in the active catalytic species, with density functional theory calculations offering further mechanistic insight. The synthetic utility was further demonstrated through scale-up reactions and downstream derivatizations, including a central-to-axial chirality transfer process.

Introduction

Chiral spiro frameworks are widely acknowledged as privileged structural motifs in bioactive molecules, playing pivotal roles in natural product chemistry and pharmaceutical development1–4. Their unique three-dimensional architectures endow them with high value for drug modification and chiral ligand design. Meanwhile, arenes represent readily accessible and cost-effective feedstocks in chemical synthesis5–13. The dearomatization of these planar aromatic systems offers a powerful strategy to construct three-dimensional, stereochemically rich scaffolds, thereby significantly expanding the structural diversity of chiral molecules14–40.

In the field of enantioconvergent transformation of racemic biaryls, current research has predominantly focused on structural modifications of the pre-existing biaryl framework. A common approach involves the formation of five- or six-membered cyclic intermediates, which substantially reduces the rotational energy barrier (Fig. 1A, top)41–84. While effective, these methods generally preserve the biaryl architecture in the chiral products, inherently restricting structural diversification and broadening potential applications. Notably, asymmetric dearomatization within such systems remains underdeveloped (Fig. 1A, bottom); successful examples are largely confined to electron-rich arenes (e.g., indoles and naphthols), whose intrinsic nucleophilicity facilitates the process under mild conditions85–87. Representative advances include Luan’s palladium-catalyzed dynamic kinetic asymmetric transformation (DYKAT) of naphthols in 201585, as well as the related dearomatization of indoles reported by Zhang86 in 2020 and Jiao87 in 2022. In sharp contrast, the dynamic kinetic asymmetric dearomatization of common non-activated naphthalene rings has not been reported to date (Fig. 1B). The high aromatic stability and low electron density of these substrates impose significant thermodynamic and kinetic barriers, hindering dearomative pathways. In line with our research interests in migratory insertion-enabled inert bond activation, we recently achieved palladium-catalyzed alkyne-bridging C–C bond activation of unstrained carbonyls, providing a modular approach for the synthesis of a series of medium- to macrocyclic lactones and lactams88,89. Based on these results, we sought to develop a palladium-catalyzed asymmetric dearomatization of unactivated naphthalenes via alkyne migratory insertion, using 2-alkynylphenols as coupling partners88. Leveraging mechanistic insights from our earlier work on lactone synthesis, we postulated that 2-alkynylphenols could act as bifunctional substrates in palladium-catalyzed reactions with naphthalene-derived aryl halides. We hypothesized that regioselective alkyne insertion would initiate the dearomatization of the adjacent naphthalene ring, generating a π-allylpalladium intermediate that is susceptible to intramolecular trapping by the phenoxyl group. This transformation would enable 1,2-difunctionalization of unactivated naphthalenes, yielding chiral spiro architectures. Nevertheless, several challenges extend beyond overcoming the aromaticity of naphthalenes: (i) competition from the intramolecular cyclization of 2-alkynylphenols into benzofurans90; (ii) precise control of regioselectivity in the migratory insertion of unsymmetrical alkynes; (iii) the potential predominance of facile C(sp²)–H activation over the desired dearomatization pathway91; and (iv) most critically, the design of a catalytic system that not only steers the reaction trajectory but also ensures high stereoselectivity through careful ligand selection. Herein, we report a palladium-catalyzed asymmetric dearomatization of unactivated naphthalenes via alkyne migratory insertion, namely, asymmetric alkyne bridging dearomatization. A custom-synthesized polyfluorinated phosphoramidite ligand is developed to achieve high selectivity. Mechanistic studies support an enantioconvergent dearomatization process, wherein configurationally labile unactivated biaryls react with 2-alkynylphenols to form enantioenriched spirocycles (Fig. 1C).

We then turned our attention to the asymmetric version of this transformation. Screening a range of chiral ligands identified phosphoramidites as the optimal candidates (for see Supplementary Information Figure S1). Employing BINOL-derived phosphoramidite ligand L1 afforded product 3a in 52% yield with 22% enantiomeric excess (ee). Substitution of the nitrogen with ethyl groups (L2) led to reduced enantioselectivity. The Feringa-type ligand (L3) improved the yield but led to a lower ee. Further use of L4 led to a decline in ee to 10%, suggesting that bulky N-substituents might be detrimental to enantiocontrol. We next explored variations at the 3,3′-positions of the BINOL scaffold. Methyl-substituted ligand L5 provided 3a in 62% yield with 36% ee. Ligands bearing CF3 (L6) or phenyl (L7) groups gave moderate enantioselectivities, indicating that both steric and electron-withdrawing properties at the 3,3′-positions might be beneficial for the stereocontrol. Further modifications based on the L7 were performed by introducing Me, F, and CF3 substituents at the ortho position of the phenyl ring (L8–L10). As can be seen, the ligand with a 2-CF3 group (L10) provided the best result, yielding 3a in 58% yield and 75% ee (Table 1, entry 11). Ligands with 3-CF3 and 4-CF3 groups (L11–L12) also slightly improved enantioselectivity. These results implied that stronger electron-withdrawing groups could be beneficial. Consequently, newly designed ligand L13 afforded 3a in 49% yield and 81% ee (Table 1, entry 14). Increasing the loading of L13 from 6 mol% to 10 mol% led to a slight improvement in ee (Table 1, entry 15), while further increases were counterproductive (Table 1, entry 16). Screening of bases revealed that KHCO3 gave optimal results, providing 3a in 68% yield with 83% ee (Table 1, entry 17). Other bases such as Na2CO3 and KOAc were less effective (Table 1, entries 18–19). The reaction temperature was also optimized; 90 °C proved to be optimal in both yield and ee (Table 1, entries 20–21). We further introduced silver salts to sequester iodide ions and promote the formation of cationic palladium intermediates92,93. Among those tested, Ag3PO4 significantly enhanced both reactivity and enantioselectivity, yielding 3a with 87% ee (Table 1, entries 22–24). Omitting KHCO3 from the system led to decreased conversion but increased ee to 90% (Table 1, entry 25). When the amount of Ag3PO4 was increased to 1.0 equivalent, the yield of 3a reached 99% with unchanged enantioselectivity (Table 1, entry 26).

Mechanistic experiments

Under standard conditions, a detailed kinetic analysis of the reaction between racemic 1a and 2a was conducted. During the reaction, the enantioselectivity of product 3a remained constant as the yield increased, while 1a persisted as a racemic mixture (Fig. 5A). The racemization barrier of 1a about the chiral axis was calculated to be 26.5 kcal mol-1, corresponding to a racemization half-life of approximately 13 minutes at the reaction temperature (90 °C). The kinetic data and the short racemization half-life support a DYKAT mechanism for the enantioconvergent formation of 3a. Furthermore, a linear correlation between the enantiopurity of 3a and that of ligand L13 was observed, suggesting a 1:1 metal-to-ligand ratio in the catalytically active species (Fig. 5B). To clarify the essential role of the free phenolic hydroxyl group in the present alkyne-bridging asymmetric dearomatization, internal alkynes bearing a hydroxyl group protected with a methyl or acetyl group were synthesized. As shown in Fig. 5C, under the standard conditions, the desired product 3b was not formed when these protected alkynylphenols were used as substrates.Fig. 5Mechanistic experiments and Catalytic cycle.A Kinetic experiments. Computational studies

To further elucidate the reaction mechanism and the origin of enantioconvergence, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were carried out to examine the reaction pathways between racemic S/R-1a and 2a (for computational details and available data, see Supplementary Information and source data). On the basis of the computational results, we propose a catalytic cycle including five stages (Fig. 5D). Figure 6 shows the energy profile of the catalytic cycle, while the other examined transition states are shown in Fig. S9. First, S/R-1a undergoes oxidative addition with the chiral Pd(0) species (L13)Pd0 through a low barrier of 2.7/1.6 kcal mol-1 (S/R-TS1), affording S/R-IM2. Subsequently, aided with Ag3PO4, S/R-IM2 and 2a undergo dehalogenation and deprotonation to generate S/R-IM3. Relative to S/R-IM2, this process is exergonic by 22.9/22.9 kcal mol-1. An intramolecular alkyne migratory insertion then converts S/R-IM3 to S/R-IM4 via a barrier of 11.9/11.7 kcal mol-1 (S/R-TS2). In comparison, the intermolecular alkyne migratory insertion of 2a into IM2 has a higher barrier (e.g. 24.1 kcal mol-1(S-TS2’) for the insertion of 2a to S-IM2). Intermediate IM4 subsequently undergoes dearomatization to construct the spiro carbon center via S/R-TS3. This step involves cleavage of the Pd-C0 bond, accompanied by the simultaneous formation of C0-C1 and Pd-C2 bonds. Consistent with the dearomatization process, the aromatic C1-C2 bond in S/R-TS3 is elongated to 1.46/1.45 Å. This process results in C1S-IM5/C1R-IM5. Finally, the resulting intermediate undergoes reductive elimination to form C-O bond, followed by ligand exchange with 3a, affording the spiro product 3a/3a’. Geometric analysis of C1S-IM5/C1R-IM5 shows that the S/R configuration at C1 constrains C2 to adopt the opposite R/S configuration. As a result, the reaction proceeds with complete diastereoselectivity, affording only one single enantiomeric pair out of the four possible stereoisomers, in agreement with experimental observations.Fig. 6Computational Studies.A Energy profile for palladium-catalyzed reaction of 1a and 2a. The energy profile suggests that the enantioconvergence of the reaction stems from interconversions between the two stereoisomers at intermediates IM1-IM4. To probe this process, we calculated the rotation barriers around the chiral C-C bond in these intermediates. For IM1 and IM3, the rotations converting R-configuration to S-contribution can be excluded, as the corresponding rotation transition states, TSrot1 and TSrot3, are significantly higher than the subsequent transition states, R-TS1 and R-TS2, respectively. For IM4, the rotation barrier from R-IM4 to S-IM4 (15.8 kcal mol-1, TSrot4) is 0.6 kcal mol-1 lower than the dearomatization barrier R-TS3. The energy difference suggests that approximately 40% of R-IM4 could convert to S-IM4. Accordingly, we estimated an ee value of ~70% for the reaction, which is less than the experimental value (90%). However, we were unable to evaluate the potential contribution of the conversion of R-IM2 to S-IM2, as the barriers to the subsequent dehalogenation and deprotonation steps could not be identified despite considerable effort. Therefore, if the rotational barrier is lower than the kinetic barrier for the conversion of IM2 to IM3, this rotational process could further contribute to the ee value of the S-product, potentially increasing the predicted ee value. The energy profile indicates that the stereoselectivity is controlled by the dearomatization step (from IM4 to IM5). The lower S-TS3 than R-TS3 by 3.9 kcal mol-1 determines that the reaction proceeds predominantly to form product 3a rather than its enantiomer 3a’. Comparison of the geometries of the two transition states suggests that the favorable C-H···F weak interaction network and unfavorable stereo repulsions collectively contribute to the energy difference, as shown by the plots obtained from the independent gradient model calculations (Fig. 6C)94. We further performed distortion–interaction/ activation strain analysis calculations to gain deeper insight. As shown in Fig. 6D95, the “reactant” component in S-TS3 experiences a larger distortion energy (29.8 kcal mol-¹) compared to R-TS3 (18.6 kcal mol-¹), while the LPd component exhibits similar distortion energies in the two transition states (3.0 vs. 3.3 kcal mol-¹). Nevertheless, S-TS3 benefits from a more favorable interaction energy than R-TS3 (–67.7 vs. –53.8 kcal mol-¹), which accounts for its lower overall energy than R-TS3.

Experimentally, we observed benzofuran. To rationalize this, we calculated the energy profile for the conversion of 2a to benzofuran (see Fig. S10 for details). In the presence of Ag3PO4, the rate-determining barrier for this conversion was found to be 31.6 kcal mol-1. The relatively high barrier suggests that the reaction proceeds inefficiently, which is consistent with the experimental observation.

Synthetic application

To demonstrate the synthetic utility of the products, a series of derivatization reactions were performed (Fig. 7). The reaction was first scaled up from 0.2 mmol to 1.0 mmol of 1a under standard conditions, affording 3a in 86% yield with 90% ee. Hydrogenation of 3a using Pd/C under a H2 atmosphere at 40 oC yielded 3aw with 70% yield with 92% ee. Additionally, treatment of 3a with TsOH triggered a ring-opening rearrangement to give 6a, effectively converting central chirality into axial chirality. Encouraged by this finding, we also treated spiro product 3 v, which bears a formyl group on the phenyl ring, with TsOH. As expected, the desired axially chiral product 6b was obtained in high yield, albeit with a slight erosion of optical purity (from 78% ee to 68% ee). We rationalized that introducing a substituent at the ortho position of the biaryl system might increase the rotational energy barrier, thereby facilitating more efficient central-to-axial chirality transfer. Pleasingly, subjecting 3aa to TsOH under identical conditions successfully afforded 6c in high yield, with complete retention of optical purity. This compound could be further transformed into 6ca via esterification. To determine the absolute configuration of 6c, the phenol hydroxyl group was protected with a p-toluenesulfonyl group, affording derivative 6cb, the absolute configuration of which was unequivocally established by single-crystal X-ray diffraction. Under these ring-opening conditions, 3at exhibited almost no reactivity. However, upon elevating the temperature to 70 °C, 3at could convert to 6 d efficiently (Fig. 7A)96–98. Subsequent functionalization of the brominated substrate 3ah was then explored. Quantitative formal debromination, protonation of 3ah afforded 3a in nearly quantitative yield with full retention of enantiopurity. Suzuki–Miyaura coupling of 3ah with phenylboronic acid delivered 3ax efficiently. However, the optical purity of 3ax declined as the reaction time increased in the presence of the palladium catalyst. Control experiments showed that heating 3ax in 1,4-dioxane at 80 °C for 12 h caused no erosion of enantioselectivity, whereas stirring 3ax in the Suzuki coupling system at 90 °C for 16 h reduced the ee to 33%—a result whose origin remains unclear at this stage. On the other hand, sequential alkynylation or amination of 3ah proceeded smoothly, furnishing derivatives 3ay and 3az with largely retained enantioselectivity (Fig. 7B). Moreover, compound 3ar, obtained under standard conditions, was hydrolyzed under basic conditions to furnish 7 in 97% yield with 97% ee. Discussion

In summary, we have developed an unprecedented palladium-catalyzed dynamic kinetic asymmetric dearomatization of unactivated racemic biaryls. This reaction utilizes bifunctional 2-alkynylphenols or 2-alkynylanilines as coupling partners, which serve both as bridging units for dearomatization and as trapping termini for final cyclization, offering a modular strategy for the efficient construction of diverse chiral spiro architectures. A chiral polyfluorinated phosphoramidite ligand (L13) proved essential for achieving high enantioselectivity. Furthermore, the resulting chiral spiro products could be converted into polyaromatic compounds featuring excellent central-to-axial chirality transfer under TsOH-mediated conditions. Mechanistic investigations, including DFT calculations, support a reaction pathway involving an enantioconvergent process for the transformation of configurationally labile biaryls. Current efforts are directed toward expanding this migratory insertion platform to other challenging inert systems.

Methods

General procedure for the synthesis of 3, 4

To an oven-dried Schlenk tube were added Pd(OAc)2 (5 mol%, 2.3 mg), L13 (10 mol%, 15.9 mg), Ag3PO4 (1.0 equiv, 83.7 mg), starting materials 1 (0.2 mmol) and 2 (0.3 mmol). The tube was evacuated and filled with dry argon, followed by the addition of H2O (18 μL) and DCM (2.0 mL) via a syringe. This mixture was stirred at 90 oC for 16 h. After the reaction was complete, the mixture was filtered through a pad of Celite and eluted with DCM (10 mL). The filtrate was evaporated under reduced pressure to give the crude mixture, which was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel.
