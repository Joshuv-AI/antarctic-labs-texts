Integrative taxonomy of Leptotrombidium species collected from murine hosts in Thailand with emphasis on L. deliense
Authors: Kamonchanok Bunmee, Kittipong Chaisiri, Urusa Thaenkham, Patchara Sriwichai, Jiraporn Ruangsittichai, Yudthana Samung, Jean-Pierre Dujardin, Suchada Sumruayphol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeptotrombidium chiggers (larval trombiculid mites) are medically important vectors of scrub typhus caused by Orientia tsutsugamushi. We performed comparative genetic and morphometric analyses of four Leptotrombidium taxa (L. deliense, L. imphalum, L. turdicola, and Leptotrombidium cf. guzhangense), isolated from murine rodents from various localities across Thailand. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using mitochondrial and nuclear gene sequences using specimens of L. deliense from different geographical localities. Quantitative morphological variation was examined using linear measurements of the larval body (20 measurements), together with two geometric morphometric approaches applied to the scutum: an outline-based analysis of scutal contour and a landmark-based analysis of the seven scutal sensillar positions. Remarkably, geometric morphometric analyses of the scutum provided taxonomic resolution equivalent to traditional morphometry based on 20 linear measurements of the entire body. Morphometric analyses showed some concordance with genetic data but could not help definitively identify an unexpected genetic variation within L. deliense, as revealed by phylogenetic reconstruction. By integrating molecular phylogenetics with traditional and geometric morphometrics, we raised the working hypothesis of a cryptic species within the L. deliense taxon. Our approach offers an effective new framework for identification of Leptotrombidium spp. Our findings improve the identification of medically important chigger species and clarify genetic structuring within Leptotrombidium, and also enhance scrub typhus vector surveillance and control programmes.Keywords: Leptotrombidium deliense, Integrative taxonomy, Phylogenetics, cox1, ITS2, Linear morphometrics, Geometric morphometrics, Chiggers, Trombiculidae, Thailand

Introduction

Chiggers are the larval stage of trombiculid mites that belong to the families Trombiculidae and Leeuwenhoekiidae (Phylum Arthropoda, Class Arachnida, Order Trombidiformes, Superfamily Trombidioidea) (Alexander et al., 2019; Ashani et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022). Over 3000 species of chiggers parasitize various hosts, including mammals, poultry, lizards, amphibians, domestic animals, and humans (Nielsen et al., 2021). Several species of chiggers, particularly those in the genus Leptotrombidium Nagayo, Miyagawa, Mitamura & Imamura, 1916, are important in medical and veterinary practice because they are vectors of scrub typhus, an acute mite-borne febrile illness caused by the bacterium Orientia tsutsugamushi (Kelly et al., 2009). Of these species, L. deliense is the most prominent vector (Lv et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2023).

In Thailand, more than 20 species of Leptotrombidium have been identified, reflecting the remarkable diversity of this genus in the region. Furthermore, this diversity highlights the complexity of the chigger population in the country. Besides L. deliense, other species also contribute significantly in certain geographical areas. For example, L. imphalum has been identified as a key vector in northern Thailand, where scrub typhus incidence is particularly high. Chaisiri et al. (2019) reported the prevalence of Leptotrombidium spp. parasitizing small mammals in Thailand, with L. deliense (5.22%), L. imphalum (0.52%), L. turdicola (0.46%), and Leptotrombidium cf. guzhangense (0.33%) being the most prevalent.

Within the genus Leptotrombidium, several species are widely distributed in Thailand. Among them, L. deliense is particularly noteworthy due to its broad geographical range and medical importance as a principal vector of scrub typhus. It has been reported from northern, northeastern, central, western, and southern Thailand (Chaisiri et al., 2016, 2019), and occurs in diverse habitats including agricultural landscapes, mixed vegetation, and both rural and urban environments (Chaisiri et al., 2019; Elliott et al., 2021). Accordingly, the present study examines multiple Leptotrombidium species collected from murine hosts in Thailand, with particular emphasis on assessing interspecific differences and geographical variation within L. deliense. This would restrict accurate species identification across different geographical regions (Zajkowska et al., 2023). Morphological identification of chiggers can be challenging due to their small size and the subtle differences among closely related species. Nevertheless, a variety of identification keys are available to facilitate species-level determination. Many of these keys were developed from geographically limited material, and their application to specimens from other regions may require careful interpretation. Consequently, accurate and reliable identification depends on the experience and expertise of trained taxonomists who are familiar with both morphological variation and regional species diversity.

Classical chigger taxonomy is commonly based on discrete morphological characters and meristic features, supported by individual measurements and ratios of various body structures such as the scutum, sensilla, idiosomal setae, and legs. In this study, we used these linear measurements only – excluding meristic features – to perform the linear (or traditional) morphometric approach and compare it with the geometric morphometrics. Unlike the linear approach, which measures absolute distances, the geometric approach analyzes the relative positions of anatomical landmarks. Having been used for over two decades to identify a wide range of organisms, the geometric morphometric approach has become well established and favored by morphometricians. Geometric morphometrics provides a quantitative framework that separates size and shape variation, enables statistical and visual assessment of intra- and interspecific morphological differences, and improves the reproducibility and objectivity of species discrimination, particularly in morphologically similar taxa. The advancement of geometric morphometrics provides powerful tools for quantifying subtle morphological differences between specimens that traditional morphometric methods cannot accurately capture (Zúniga-Reinoso and Benítez, 2015). The technique typically analyzes a single, non-articulated part of the arthropod, such as wings for insects, or, more recently, the scutum for chiggers (Sungvornyothin et al., 2019; Wulandhari et al., 2024).

In the present study, two geometric techniques were applied to the scutum, namely, the landmark- and outline-based techniques. We explored the scutum as both an inter- and intraspecific character to assess possible geographical influences on scutum shape variation. We employed three morphometric approaches to classify chiggers: the classical linear method and the two modern landmark- and outline-based geometric morphometric techniques. For all three methods, we tentatively separated size and shape using specific statistics. Importantly, while the shape described by the geometric approach refers exclusively to the scutum, the shape computed from the linear measurements represents an abstraction quantity not clearly localized on the mite’s body.

Together, these advances underscore the need for integrative approaches that combine molecular and morphological evidence to achieve robust species identification and to better understand patterns of diversity in ecologically and medically important mite taxa. DNA markers are a cornerstone of modern arthropod research, providing accurate tools for organism identification and clarifying genetic structuring. In Acari studies, which focus primarily on specimen identification and biodiversity assessment, mitochondrial gene sequences (mtDNA) are commonly used for studying the phylogeny of mites (Zhou et al., 2020). For example, the identification of feather mite species (Astigmata: Analgoidea and Pterolichoidea) and elucidation of intraspecific variation among cryptic species with broad ecological niches, such as water mites (Hydrachnidae), were achieved using the mitochondrial gene cox1 (Doña et al., 2015; Pešić et al., 2017). Kumlert et al. (2018) proposed that combining morphological and molecular methods for mite identification and classification would enhance the development of a comprehensive reference database. In particular, the compilation of morphometric and molecular data from chigger populations across diverse geographical regions would support vector surveillance by enabling analyses of species distributions in relation to environmental variables such as topography, habitat characteristics, host associations, and local biodiversity. Such a chigger reference database would ensure consistent species identification and facilitate comparative assessments of vector distribution, thereby enhancing the accuracy of vector detection, monitoring, and risk assessment within surveillance frameworks. Thus, chigger species identification using molecular methods is critical. The use of DNA markers offers a more detailed understanding of genetic diversity, thereby significantly expanding our knowledge of biological systems and improving the accuracy of environmental management strategies (Antonovskaia, 2018; Chen et al., 2022).

Here, we present an integrative taxonomic investigation of Leptotrombidium chigger species infesting murine rodents in Thailand. Using a combination of classical morphological identification, linear morphometrics, landmark- and outline-based geometric morphometrics, and molecular analyses, we evaluated taxonomic hypotheses and quantified both intra- and interspecific variation among medically important chigger taxa. This approach enabled reliable discrimination among closely related Leptotrombidium species and revealed patterns of geographical variation, particularly within L. deliense. To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply multiple complementary morphometric frameworks together with molecular data to Leptotrombidium chiggers in Thailand. Our findings demonstrate the value of an integrative taxonomic framework for improving species identification and provide a foundation for future studies on population structure, vector surveillance, and biodiversity of scrub typhus vectors.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and subsampling

In total, 118 Leptotrombidium chigger specimens were collected from various murine rodent hosts from eight different localities across northern, northeastern, central, eastern, western, and southern Thailand (Table 1). Chiggers were preserved in microcentrifuge tubes filled with 95% ethanol until species identification. Subsampling (∼10% per infested host) was targeted rather than randomized sampling, with individuals selected to represent variation in external appearance (body size, general body shape, and coloration) from the same attachment site on each host, to capture potential species-level diversity and estimate intraspecific variation within hosts. The chiggers were preliminarily sorted under a stereomicroscope (Olympus SZX7 Stereomicroscope, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) based on gross external features. No taxonomic identification was performed at this stage. Each selected individual was subsequently mounted in Berlese’s fluid and examined in detail under a compound microscope for formal morphological identification and morphometric analysis. Detailed examination and measurements were conducted using a Zeiss Axio Observer Z1 motorized Inverted Fluorescence Microscope (Carl ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany). Measurements were made using the Zeiss ZEN Pro Microscopy Software (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Initial identification of individual chiggers was performed up to the subgenus level, using the pictorial key developed by Nadchatram and Dohany (1974), followed by species-level identification using published morphological keys for trombiculid mites (Traub and Morrow, 1955; Traub and Evans, 1957; Vercammen-Grandjean and Langston, 1976; Goff et al., 1982; Fernandes and Kulkarni, 2003; Stekolnikov, 2013).

Non-destructive DNA extraction, amplification, and sequencing

Non-destructive DNA extraction was performed only on specimens preserved in 95% ethanol that remained after subsampling from infested rodent hosts. These specimens represented a subset of the total of 118 collected chiggers and were selected based on preservation quality and the availability of ethanol-preserved material. The molecular dataset included Leptotrombidium deliense from Bangkok (n = 4), Kanchanaburi (n = 10), and Chanthaburi (n = 5); L. imphalum from Narathiwat (n = 4); and L. turdicola from Nan (n = 2). Specimens available only as permanent microscope slides were excluded from molecular analyses and used exclusively for morphometric analyses. DNA extraction and sequencing were conducted using a non-destructive protocol, after which specimens were permanently mounted for morphometric examination. Only sequences of sufficient quality were retained for molecular analyses. Whenever possible, molecular and morphometric analyses were conducted on the same individual specimens. Individual chiggers underwent DNA extraction using a puncture technique with filter paper, followed by DNA extraction from the entire body (non-destructive) along with the material on the filter paper. The entire procedure was performed under a stereomicroscope to ensure careful and optimal extraction using the Qiagen Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). All steps followed the manufacturer’s protocol except for the following modifications: (i) a puncture technique with filter paper was used prior to lysis to facilitate DNA release; and (ii) after the lysis step, the intact exoskeleton was carefully removed from the lysis buffer under a stereomicroscope before the supernatant was transferred to the DNeasy spin column. The exoskeleton was then removed and placed on a glass slide, then mounted in Berlese’s fluid. Following DNA extraction, the exoskeleton was preserved on a permanent mounting slide. Key morphological characteristics were recorded for geometric morphometric analyses and species identification (see Sections 2.4 and 2.5.1).

The extracted DNA was analyzed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to amplify approximately 710-bp fragment of the mitochondrial gene cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) (Chaisiri, 2016) and 200-bp fragment of ITS2 (Zhou et al., 2020). The PCR products were analyzed to assess their quantity and molecular weight and subsequently visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis. The approximate sizes of the amplicons were determined by referring to a DNA ladder. Then, representative PCR amplicons were sent for sequencing to a commercial sequencing services provider (Macrogen, Inc., Seoul, Korea). Based on these previously identified specimens, the amplicons included 8 sequences for L. deliense (4 from Bangkok, 2 from Chanthaburi, and 2 from Kanchanaburi), four sequences for L. imphalum, two sequences for L. turdicola, and a sequence for Helenicula naresuani, which was used as the outgroup. These specimens used for molecular analyses were selected from previously published collections (Chaisiri et al., 2016, 2019; Wulandhari et al., 2024). Specimens were originally collected from murine rodents, mounted on permatent slides, and morphologically identified to species level following standard taxonomic keys. Primary identifications were performed by K. Chaisiri, R. Kumlert and K. Bunmee; and were subsequently verified by an independent expert (A. A. Stekolnikov).

Phylogenetic analysis

The resulting sequences were aligned using ClustalX 2.1 (Thompson et al., 2003), and manual nucleotide verification was performed using BioEdit 7.0 (Hall, 1999). Species identification was performed by comparing the newly generated sequences to those deposited in the NCBI database using the BLASTn algorithm and assigning the sequence to the species with the highest identity score match.

Phylogenetic analysis was then performed using the assigned sequences; cox1 and ITS2 sequences were concatenated only when obtained from the same individual specimen to ensure direct correspondence between mitochondrial and nuclear data and associated morphological observations. Helenicula naresuani was designated as the outgroup, as it belongs to the same family (Trombiculidae) but a different tribe (Schoengastiini) from Leptotrombidium (Tribe Trombiculini), allowing appropriate rooting of the phylogenetic tree. Phylogenetic reconstruction was performed using the maximum likelihood method. Codon position saturation revealed saturation at the third codon position of cox1. Sequence divergence was evaluated by estimating the proportion of absolute nucleotide sites (uncorrected p-distance) to compare intra- and interspecific genetic distances. The p-distance matrix was generated using MEGA X 10.2.6 (Kumar et al., 2018) and by incorporating variance estimation and 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Morphological identification

Chiggers were identified based on their exoskeletons, which were permanently mounted on microscope slides using Berlese’s fluid, at the subfamily, tribe, and genus levels following the identification key of Nadchatram and Dohany (1974). Leptotrombidium specimens were further identified to the species level using the identification key developed by Stekolnikov (2013). Similar to the subsampling approach, species-level identification was refined through detailed measurements of the idiosome and its associated legs. These measurements were conducted following the methods outlined by Chaisiri (2016) and Nadchatram and Dohany (1974), with all values reported in micrometres (Fig. 1). Subsequently, a hierarchical clustering tree was constructed using the Euclidean distances between the mean principal component values of each group in order to generate a global view of the morphological similarities between taxa. Principal components analysis (PCA) was performed using XYOM software. Linear measurements were log-transformed and converted into log-shape ratios (LSR) in order to remove isometric size effects. Principal components analysis (PCA) was then performed on the LSR matrix using a covariance matrix, producing size-free shape variables. The last principal component had zero variance due to row-centering of the data.

Morphometrics

In addition to the morphological examination described above, we performed a triple morphometric approach consisting of linear (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2), landmark-based, and outline-based morphometrics. In total, 118 photomicrographs of the chigger scutum were captured individually using a stereomicroscope at 100× magnification with the ZEISS ZEN Pro Microscopy software. The photographs were subsequently used for landmark- and outline-based geometric morphometrics analysis (Fig. 2).

Linear measurements data

The linear morphometric approach was based on standardized measurements defined as distances between homologous anatomical landmarks of the idiosome and associated legs, following established terminology. Meristic characters (DS, VS, NDV) were excluded from the analysis. In total, 20 linear characters were measured (Fig. 1), including: AW, distance between anterolateral scutal setae; PW, distance between posterolateral scutal setae; SB, distance between sensilla; ASB, distance from the level of sensillary bases to extreme anterior margin of scutum; PSB, distance from the level of sensillary bases to extreme posterior margin of scutum; SD, length of scutum (ASB + PSB); AP, distance from anterolateral to posterolateral scutal setae on one side; AM, length of anteromedian setae of scutum; AL, length of anterolateral setae of scutum; PL, length of posterolateral setae of scutum; S, length of sensilla; H, length of humeral setae; IP, total length of legs I-III (pa + pm + pp); pa, length of leg I (including coxa); pm, length of leg II (including coxa); pp, length of leg III (including coxa); TaIII, length of leg tarsus III; TaW, width of leg tarsus III; SI, length of leg tarsala I; SII, length of leg tarsala II (Goff et al., 1982; Kudryashova, 1998; Stekolnikov and Daniel, 2012; Ashani et al., 2022; Nielsen et al., 2021).

Landmarks

The landmark-based approach digitized the insertion points of the 7 sensilla covering the scutum (Fig. 2A). These positions were captured by clicking on them and were converted into x- and y-coordinates. A micrometric scale on each image enabled the conversion of pixels into micrometres.

Outlines

The outline-based technique analyzed the scutum contour by digitizing a series of points along the entire scutum edge to capture their x- and y coordinates, using the same computer screen coordinate system as the landmark-based approach (Fig. 2B).

Statistical analyses

Our general statistical treatment of the morphometric data separated size from shape, allowing two separate examinations of the same data. The tentative separation of size and shape followed very different statistical techniques according to the kind of data at hand for the four study species, L. deliense, L. imphalum, L. turdicola, and Leptotrombidium cf. guzhangense. Differences in size and shape variables among groups were assessed using a non-parametric permutation ANOVA (1000 permutations). The success of the two separate analyses was evaluated by measuring the allometric residue in the shape variables (see Section 2.8).

Linear measurements taken as described by Darroch and Mosimann (1985) were log-transformed, and for each individual, a global size variable called the “log-size” was calculated as the mean of these log-measurements. “Shape” was defined as log-shape ratios, obtained by centering the log-measurements using the mean. Due to the loss of one degree of freedom, the principal components of the log-shape ratios, except the last component, were used as the final shape variables.

The landmark-based raw coordinates were analyzed using generalized Procrustes analysis. This method determines shape as the difference between homologous landmarks after their optimal superposition, which includes the translation to the same centroid, the removal of size variations between individuals, and the optimal orientation of each configuration of landmarks (Rohlf, 1990; Bookstein et al., 1991). The removal of size variation is obtained by dividing each coordinate by the centroid size (Bookstein et al., 1991), which is the square root of the sum of squared distances between each landmark and the configuration’s centroid. Because the shape variables obtained by GPA lost 4 degrees of freedom, we used their principal components except for the last four.

The outline-based data (also called pseudo-landmarks) were analyzed using elliptic Fourier coefficients analysis (Kuhl and Giardina, 1982). This less intuitive analysis decomposes the original contour considered as a wave into simpler waves of decreasing amplitudes. The sum of these simpler waves can accurately reconstruct the original shape, provided that the number of simpler waves is sufficient. The derived coefficients were then normalized to eliminate size variation (estimated using the perimeter) and orientation, resulting in shape variables called normalized elliptic Fourier coefficients (NEFs) (Lestrel, 1997). Since NEFs can be very numerous, we used a representative subset of their first principal components for subsequent analysis.

Shape variation

Due to the low sample sizes within some localities or for some species, PCA was used for multivariate analyses. The covariance-based PCA computed from the grand total of individuals rather than to each sample separately. Thus, shape variations between groups were estimated using the Euclidean distances between the principal components of shape, enabling the selection of a number of first principal components lower than the sample sizes.

Allometry

Since the elimination of size variations can be very unequal depending on the groups being compared and the techniques used, we measured the importance of size effects potentially present in the different shape variables. This estimation was conducted based on a simple linear regression between shape, represented by the first principal components of shape and size. The contribution of size variations to shape variables, i.e. the residual allometry, was estimated using the coefficient of determination within each of the four species, as well as that in the combined dataset of the four species.

Software

All analyses and graphical morphometric outputs were performed using the online XYOM software (Dujardin and Dujardin, 2019) and the CLIC software (Dujardin and Slice, 2006). For phylogenetic analysis, nucleotide sequence alignment was conducted using ClustalX v.2.1 (Thompson et al., 2003), followed by manual verification of nucleotide sequences using BioEdit v.7.0 (Hall, 1999). Phylogenetic tree reconstruction and pairwise genetic distance calculations (p-distance matrix) were performed using MEGA X v.10.2.6 (Kumar et al., 2018).

Results

In total, 118 chigger specimens were collected across multiple geographical locations in Thailand, representing populations of L. deliense, L. imphalum, L. turdicola, and Leptotrombidium cf. guzhangense (Table 1). The spatial distribution of these samples shows collection sites mapped across the northern, northeastern, central, western, and southern regions of Thailand (Fig. 3). Leptotrombidium deliense was the most widely distributed species, occurring in almost all sampled provinces, including Kanchanaburi, Bangkok, Chanthaburi, Nan, and Narathiwat. Interspecific variation was geographically structured. Leptotrombidium imphalum was detected in Chiang Rai, Phayao, Nan, and Narathiwat provinces, while L. turdicola was confined to Nan Province. A distinct population of Leptotrombidium cf. guzhangense was found exclusively in Kalasin Province, with no sympatry observed with the other three species (Fig. 3).

Molecular analysis

The molecular results highlight patterns of multi-locus genetic structuring that can be compared with morphological and morphometric variation at the individual level. The phylogenetic tree illustrates patterns of genetic relatedness among three Leptotrombidium species (L. deliense, L. imphalum, and L. turdicola), with Helenicula naresuani as the designated outgroup. The concatenated mitochondrial (cox1) and nuclear (ITS2) DNA sequences (approximately 825 bp in length) were constructed using maximum likelihood analysis. The GenBank accession numbers for cox1 and ITS2 sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Leptotrombidium deliense samples were collected from Kanchanaburi (KRI), Chanthaburi (CTI), and Bangkok (BKK). Each of the representative L. deliense populations formed a monophyletic group with varying bootstrap support. The four BKK samples (BKK 1–4) formed a monophyletic group with 100% bootstrap support. The two CTI samples (CTI 1 and CTI 2) were also strongly supported (99%). The two KRI samples (KRI 1 and KRI 2) formed a well-supported group with 100% bootstrap support. Furthermore, four L. imphalum samples collected from Narathiwat (L. imphalum 1–4) showed a hierarchical structure with well-supported internal relationships (bootstrap values of 96–97%). In contrast, L. turdicola from Nan (L. turdicola 1 and 2) formed a distinct lineage with 100% bootstrap support. The ML tree suggested a grouping of L. imphalum and L. turdicola; however, this relationship was poorly supported (bootstrap value of 41%). In addition, the relative arrangement of some lineages, particularly among geographically distinct L. deliense populations, did not strictly mirror the rankings inferred from pairwise genetic distances (see Fig. 4 and Table 2). This reflects the fact that genetic distance matrices and phylogenetic topology capture related but not identical aspects of genetic divergence. A positive correlation was obtained (r = 0.99, 10,000 permutations) between the genetic distance and patristic distance. This high correlation is expected because patristic distances are derived from the same sequence data used to compute genetic distances. Therefore, the Mantel test primarily reflects internal consistency between distance-based and tree-based representations of genetic divergence, rather than providing independent evidence of evolutionary processes.

Morphological identification

Morphological identification of the four Leptotrombidium species was based on a combination of scutal dimensions, meristic characters, and leg measurements (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Among the four taxa, L. turdicola was the most readily distinguishable species, characterized by consistently larger scutum dimensions, including AW, PW, ASB, and SD, as well as greater overall length of legs, such as IP, pa, pm, and pp, allowing reliable morphological separation of L. turdicola. Leptotrombidium deliense and L. imphalum showed partial overlap in some linear measurements; however, they could be differentiated by combinations of scutal proportions and leg characters; L. imphalum exhibited, on average, larger values of AP, AM, AL, PL, IP, and TaIII compared with L. deliense. Leptotrombidium cf. guzhangense was morphologically distinct from both L. deliense and L. imphalum by its comparatively narrower measurement ranges, smaller scutum dimensions, and lower values for IP and leg measurements; this allowed consistent separation of this taxon.

These morphological characters provide reliable criteria for distinguishing the four Leptotrombidium spp. examined in this study and are consistent with traditional diagnostic approaches used in trombiculid taxonomy. We performed simple principal components analysis (without applying the Daroch and Mosimann algorithm) to construct an unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) tree based on the Euclidean distances between the principal components (Fig. 5). The tree illustrated the hierarchical clustering of Leptotrombidium spp. based on all morphological characteristics used in the classical identification of the species, and provided a structured visualization of species and inter-population relationships, emphasizing hierarchical morphological similarities. Leptotrombidium turdicola appeared as the most external group, while Leptotrombidium cf. guzhangense was closely connected to L. imphalum and L. deliense. The five geographically distinct L. deliense samples (Bangkok, Narathiwat, Chanthaburi, Kanchanaburi, and Nan) showed structured relationships, with Bangkok and Narathiwat forming the closest pair, followed by Chanthaburi and Kanchanaburi, while the Nan sample appeared slightly more distinct. The overall clustering pattern highlights clear morphological differentiation between species and geographical variations within L. deliense (Fig. 5).

Morphometric identification

Size variations

The values of the morphometric measurements are detailed in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Shape variations

Shape was computed very differently for each data type, i.e. coordinates data, scutum landmarks or perimeter, and linear measurements. Since the latter covered various parts of the body, including the scutum, it was referred to as the global body shape, whereas those from coordinates were referred to as the scutum shape(s).

Statistical differentiation of the landmark-based shape variation between samples of L. deliense from different geographical locations was explicitly tested. Permutation-based non-parametric ANOVA performed on NEF-based shape variables revealed moderate but statistically significant differentiation, with partial overlap among localities (F(4,63) = 6.643, P < 0.001), further confirmed by significant pairwise differences (P ≤ 0.01–0.001) (see Supplementary Table S4). There were two exceptions: Bangkok vs Narathiwat samples and Chanthaburi vs Kanchanaburi samples showed the greatest similarity; these geographical samples clustered closely together in both the landmark-based morphospace (Fig. 8A) and the corresponding UPGMA dendrogram (Supplementary Fig. S1A). The superposition of outline-based shapes yielded a congruent pattern (Fig. 8B), as also reflected in the outline-based UPGMA tree (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Furthermore, the log-shape ratios did not indicate any alternative arrangement of the hierarchical tree (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Notably, the topology of the log-shape tree based on 20 linear measurements was the same as the one derived from the scutum architecture of landmarks (compare Supplementary Fig. S1A and C).

Correlation coefficients between size estimators

Since each of the three morphometric approaches estimated size in different ways, we examined the linear correlation coefficients between them, both within and across the Leptotrombidium species (Table 5), with varying results. Within species, the geometric size estimators using centroid size and perimeter consistently showed very strong positive correlations, ranging from r = 0.929 in L. imphalum to r = 0.957 in L. turdicola, indicating high consistency between these two geometric approaches. In contrast, the correlations between geometric size estimators (centroid size and perimeter) and the log-transformed linear size measurements were consistently weaker. These results indicate that size scaling can produce very divergent shape estimates between geometric and linear methods, with only the former allowing a visual assessment of relevance. The higher correlation coefficients found in the interspecies category are attributed to interspecies differences in relative size, which were indeed more or less the same regardless of the method (Fig. 7).

Size and shape (allometry)

The effect of size variation on shape, i.e., the residual allometry of shape variables, differed across Leptotrombidium species and measurement approaches (Table 6). Both within and among species, both the landmark- and outline-based geometric morphometric approaches generally showed a low to moderate influence of size variation on shape changes. Globally, the geometric shape variation was relatively independent of size variation, indicating that shape differences can be interpreted without the confounding influence of size. The shape variables derived from linear measurement data also showed a low level of residual allometry, except between species (45%). It should be noted that interspecific morphometric patterns were derived from sample sizes that varied among taxa. Consequently, results for species represented by limited numbers of specimens, particularly L. turdicola and Leptotrombidium cf. guzhangense, should be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

Morphological taxonomy has been the foundation of chigger systematics and remains crucial for species recognition and description. However, chigger identification is inherently demanding, requiring extensive experience and familiarity with a large body of specific literature. For several taxa, available identification keys are based on limited material or geographically restricted samples, and intraspecific morphological variation remains incompletely documented for many species. Integrative approaches that complement classical morphology with molecular and quantitative morphometric data do not replace traditional taxonomy, but rather provide additional, independent lines of evidence that may assist taxonomic experts in resolving difficult species complexes and assessing intraspecific variation (Pearce and O’shea, 2007; Alghamdi, 2019; Chen et al., 2022). This approach was adopted to ensure representation of potential co-occurring species, particularly within the genus Leptotrombidium, which was the primary focus of this study. Species of Leptotrombidium may co-occur on the same host individual and attachment site and can exhibit notable interspecific and intraspecific morphological variation, making targeted subsampling a practical means of capturing within-host taxonomic diversity under sampling constraints. All specimens were analyzed using standardized landmark placement and measurement procedures, and size-shape separation and residual allometry were considered in the geometric morphometric analyses. Integrative taxonomy has been proposed as an effective strategy for accurately delineating species boundaries and refining our understanding of biodiversity (Dayrat, 2005; Bakkes et al., 2018). Our study developed an integrative taxonomic approach that combined genetic and morphometric analyses to evaluate evolutionary and morphological divergence among four species of Leptotrombidium and to explore intraspecific variations in five L. deliense geographical samples.

Three morphometric approaches

In this integrative framework, traditional diagnostic characters were applied qualitatively for species identification, and meristic characters were treated as quantitative descriptive traits consistent with classical taxonomy. Morphometric analyses were used as complementary tools to explore patterns of size and shape variation based on continuous characters, rather than to replace traditional taxonomic criteria. The three morphometric approaches allowed us to examine size and shape variations separately at the intraspecific (Fig. 6) and interspecific (Fig. 7) levels. Within species, both geometric morphometric analyses presented very similar patterns of variation, globally similar to the linear method. An acceptable parallelism of relative size differences was also observed between species, as indicated by all three methods; however, only the geometric estimation of the scutum size sufficiently distinguished almost all L. turdicola specimens (Fig. 7). Nevertheless, our main comparisons and resulting classification trees analyzed only shape, not size, variation. Expectedly, the quality of shape described by these variables would differ depending on the morphometric technique. Furthermore, visual verification was possible only for the geometric approaches (landmarks, contours) (Fig. 8, Fig. 9). Measuring residual allometry is another means of assessing the quality of shape variables that is also applicable to the linear approach. The residual contribution of size to shape variation according to the different approaches is detailed in Table 6. Overall, regardless of the method of deriving shape variables, intraspecific shape variations were relatively independent of size variations, indicating that shape differences could be interpreted without the confounding influence of size. The within-species residual allometry of Leptotrombidium cf. guzhangense was not discussed here because specimens were collected from one site and from a unique host; thus, the sample size (n = 8) was too low. However, the sample was included in the interspecific residual allometry analysis to measure the effect of interspecific size differences on shape variations. Since shape, as a metric variable, is also influenced by the environment, within-species shape variations were expected between localities and/or animal hosts, especially for L. deliense and L. imphalum (Table 1 and Fig. 3). For the L. turdicola sample, which was collected from only one site, the sole external parameter potentially affecting size was the host type (see Table 1). Thus, higher effects of within-species size variation (host and geography) were expected in L. deliense and L. imphalum than in L. turdicola. This expectation was confirmed for the geometric shape, but not for the log-shape ratios. Thus, the three morphometric methods produced relatively comparable results for within-species variations but produced a convergent classification of the geographical populations of L. deliense (Supplementary Fig. S1). The interspecies residual allometry of shape (including Leptotrombidium cf. guzhangense) remained very low when considering geometric shape (0.5% and 2.1%), but very high (45%) for the linear measurement (Table 6). The high residual allometry observed in interspecific log-shape ratios derived from linear measurements (45%) highlights an important methodological limitation of linear morphometrics when applied across species. Linear ratios inherently confound size and shape, particularly when species differ markedly in overall body size, leading to inflated allometric effects. The relatively low residual allometry detected using landmark- and outline-based geometric morphometrics reflects their ability to separate shape variation from size-related scaling effects. Nevertheless, this result should be interpreted as supporting the relative robustness of geometric morphometrics, rather than as sole evidence of their superiority. This study is subject to limitations related to sample size, particularly for certain species and localities. Limited specimen availability constrained the number of individuals available for some taxa, e.g. L. turdicola and Leptotrombidium cf. guzhangense. As a result, the multivariate analyses were restricted to covariance-based PCA, and estimates of intraspecific variation and multivariate morphometric patterns for these species should be regarded as preliminary. Future studies incorporating larger and more geographically balanced sample sizes will be necessary to confirm and refine these patterns.

Comparisons between genetic and morphometric trees

Mitochondrial cox1 and nuclear ITS2 markers were combined to exploit their complementary evolutionary properties. With its relatively high substitution rate, cox1 is well suited for detecting recent divergence and population-level genetic structuring, whereas ITS2 evolves more conservatively and provides a biparentally inherited nuclear perspective that is less sensitive to demographic processes such as sex-biased dispersal. In species of the Trombiculidae, where morphological differentiation may be subtle, the integration of mitochondrial and nuclear markers allows a more robust assessment of multi-locus genetic patterns while reducing the risk of marker-specific bias. In the present study, this approach was used to evaluate congruence among loci and morphology rather than to delimit species formally. Two small samples (Leptotrombidium cf. guzhangense and L. deliense from Narathiwat) that were not documented for the genetic analyses were excluded from direct comparison between the genetic and morphometric classifications. The final dataset included five samples: L. deliense BKK, L. deliense KRI, L. deliense CTI, L. imphalum, and L. turdicola, which exhibited some consistency between genetic and morphometric data (Fig. 10). The phylogenetic tree showed a clear clustering pattern among L. deliense, L. imphalum and L. turdicola; however, the geographical samples of L. deliense were not recovered as a single conspecific cluster. The Kanchanaburi samples L. deliense KRI 1-2 were separated from the samples from other L. deliense localities (Chanthaburi, CTI and Bangkok, BKK) and emerged as the most external group of the four Leptotrombidium spp., indicating it may represent an undetected species (Fig. 4, Fig. 10). The splitting of the L. deliense morphological cluster observed in the genetic tree (Fig. 10A) did not appear in the landmark-based UPGMA tree (Fig. 10B). In this tree, the geographical samples of L. deliense constituted a single group, as expected from the morphological classification. However, in the trees derived from the other two morphometric approaches (Fig. 10C and D), L. deliense BKK appeared to be attracted to L. imphalum (see also Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S2), a relationship that also divided the L. deliense group. Thus, the UPGMA morphometric trees from two morphometric approaches (Fig. 10C and D), except for the landmark-based one (Fig. 10C), also indicated heterogeneity in the L. deliense morphological cluster, although not exactly as described by the genetic tree (Fig. 10A). Notably, the UPGMA tree derived from the outline-based approach also identified L. deliense KRI as a completely external group but grouped it with L. deliense CTI, an association not confirmed by the genetic tree (Fig. 10D). However, the UPGMA dendrograms derived from morphometric data should be interpreted as phenetic clustering reflecting overall similarity in shape, rather than as hypotheses of evolutionary relationships. Their use here is exploratory and intended to evaluate the consistency of clustering patterns across different morphometric approaches within an integrative taxonomic framework.

The hypothesis of cryptic speciation

Unless all samples represent a single, panmictic species, which appears unlikely, and assuming that the observed genetic structuring reflects biologically meaningful divergence, our working hypothesis is that L. deliense may comprise cryptically differentiated lineages. Meanwhile, we acknowledge that the phylogenetic analysis does not aim to reconstruct the full evolutionary history of Leptotrombidium, and that inclusion of additional reference sequences may further improve taxonomic context. Nevertheless, the current approach is appropriate for evaluating congruence between morphology and multi-locus genetic data at the individual and clustering patterns of genetic differentiation among specimens. The observed multi-locus genetic structuring within L. deliense, together with consistent morphological and morphometric differentiation, raises the hypothesis that substantial genetic differentiation among geographically distinct L. deliense populations (Tamura-Nei distances up to 0.14–0.21; Table 2) was not reflected in traditional morphological identification (Supplementary Fig. S1). Although genetic distance thresholds alone cannot delimit species boundaries, the observed overlap between intra- and interspecific divergence highlights a genetic-morphological discordance consistent with cryptic genetic structuring. However, these results are interpreted here as preliminary evidence, and additional sampling and integrative analyses would be required to formally assess cryptic species status. Further, given the limited geographical sampling, uneven sample sizes among populations, and the use of only two genetic markers, these results should be interpreted as evidence supporting a working hypothesis rather than a definitive taxonomic conclusion.

A discrepancy between genetic and morphological or even morphometric differentiation is not new in mites. For instance, a study by Zajkowska et al. (2023) compared L. europaeum and Leptotrombidium sp. A and found no clear morphological differences between the two species, but found 10.1–11.5% of genetic distance. This indicates that species considered morphologically similar may, in fact, be genetically distinct, and their classification cannot be accurately reassessed without incorporating molecular techniques. Rajasegaran et al. (2024) in an AMOVA analysis revealed that 96.41% of the total genetic variation was partitioned among geographical groups of populations of Neoschoengastia gallinarum (Hatori, 1920) from three localities in Malaysia and Thailand. Despite exhibiting similar morphological characteristics based on morpho-taxonomic identification, the genetic data demonstrated that the populations from Peninsular Malaysia and Thailand were genetically distinct, indicating cryptic species (Rajasegaran et al., 2024). As defined by Bickford et al. (2007) and Struck et al. (2018), cryptic species are characterized by minimal or undetectable phenotypic differences alongside considerable genetic differentiation. Cryptic speciation has been documented across a wide range of organisms. A review of cryptic species in Acari reported such cases in 24 out of 142 acarine superfamilies. Notably, the likelihood of discovering cryptic species was most strongly associated with the level of research effort devoted to specific taxa (Skoracka et al., 2015). Taken together, our genetic and morphometric results support the presence of putative cryptic lineages within Leptotrombidium deliense. However, broader geographical sampling and multi-locus genomic data will be required to determine whether these lineages represent distinct genetically divergent species-level entities.

Lack of morphometric resolution

The lack of resolution of the classical morphological approach compared with the genetic approach is unsurprising; however, that of the geometric morphometric approach is notable. Indeed, geometric morphometrics has been able to distinguish cryptic species in many organisms, such as insects (Charlesworth et al., 1982; Kirkpatrick, 1982; Estes and Arnold, 2007; Dujardin et al., 2014; Zúniga-Reinoso and Benítez, 2015; Dujardin, 2025). Our geometric approach focused on the scutum because the only two geometric morphometric studies on mites, as of this writing, recommended the scutum as the distinguishing taxonomic trait (Sungvornyothin et al., 2019; Wulandhari et al., 2024). However, the inability of scutum-based geometric morphometrics to fully resolve the putative cryptic lineage in the present study likely reflects a trait-specific limitation rather than a methodological one. The scutum is a relatively rigid and conservative structure, and subtle shape variation associated with recent divergence or cryptic speciation may be expressed more strongly in other anatomical regions. Structures such as legs or idiosoma contours may capture different aspects of morphological differentiation. Further exploration of alternative morphological traits is needed to evaluate their suitability for detecting fine-scale evolutionary divergence. Accordingly, while scutum-based morphometrics remain appropriate for species-level discrimination in Leptotrombidium, our results suggest that its effectiveness for detecting fine-scale lineage differentiation may warrant re-evaluation in this genus or closely related taxa, rather than across Trombiculidae more broadly. For example, a geometric morphometric study in soft ticks highlighted the importance of body contour in the Ornithodoros capensis (sensu lato) complex (Dupraz et al., 2016), while another highlighted the importance of tarsus I in distinguishing among Afrotropical Ornithodoros species.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the value of an integrative taxonomic framework for improving Leptotrombidium species identification by combining qualitative and quantitative morphological data, including linear measurements and landmark- and outline-based geometric morphometrics, with molecular analyses based on concatenated mitochondrial (cox1) and nuclear (ITS2) markers. This combined approach offers a clearer and more reliable framework for evaluating taxonomic hypotheses than single-method analyses alone, particularly in groups characterized by limited morphological variation. Our results highlight patterns of genetic and morphometric discordance that support the presence of genetically structured lineages within Leptotrombidium, warranting further investigation. To robustly test species hypotheses and to better understand the evolutionary processes underlying phenotypic and genetic divergence, the incorporation of ecological and host-associated variables should be considered an essential next step. Factors such as geographical distribution, host specificity, and habitat conditions are likely to play a central role in shaping diversification within this genus. Integrating morphological, molecular, ecological, and host-related data will be critical for developing a more comprehensive and biologically meaningful understanding of Leptotrombidium diversity and evolution, as well as for strengthening future taxonomic and epidemiological studies involving medically important chigger mites.
