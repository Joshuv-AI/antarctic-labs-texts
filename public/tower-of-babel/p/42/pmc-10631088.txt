Systolic blood pressure levels and mortality in Australian medical inpatients
Authors: Jin Nuo Joan Tsang, Stephen Bacchi, Joshua G Kovoor, Aashray K Gupta, Brandon Stretton, Samuel Gluck, Toby Gilbert, Yogesh Sharma, Richard Woodman, Arduino A Mangoni
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe epidemiology of elevations in blood pressure is incompletely characterized, particularly in Australia. Given the lack of evidence regarding the frequency and the optimal management of in‐hospital hypertension, the authors performed a multicenter retrospective cohort study of consecutive medical admissions in South Australia over a 2‐year period to investigate systolic blood pressure levels and their association with in‐hospital mortality. Among 16 896 inpatients, 76% had at least one systolic blood pressure reading of ≥140 mmHg and 11.7% of ≥180 mmHg during hospitalization. A statistically significant negative relationship was observed between having at least one reading ≥140 mmHg and a likelihood of in‐hospital mortality (odds ratio 0.41, 95% CI: 0.35 to 0.49, P < .001). Our results suggest that elevations in systolic blood pressure are common in Australian medical inpatients. However, the inverse association observed between systolic blood pressure values ≥140 mmHg and in‐hospital mortality warrants further research to determine the clinical significance and optimal management of blood pressure elevations in this group.Keywords: general medicine, hospital medicine, hypertension, mortality, patient monitoring

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of elevations in blood pressure in Australian hospitals is currently unknown, in contrast to international studies. 1 , 2 The awareness of elevated blood pressure among inpatients has not been addressed in manner comparable to that of hypertension within the community. 3 There is also limited evidence to guide management of such elevations, particularly in the absence of signs and symptoms of target organ damage. 4 Although hypertensive urgencies describe a systolic pressure ≥180 mmHg without end‐organ damage, there are conflicting evidence regarding patient benefits with different treatments and significant variation in practice. 5 In addition, in‐hospital systolic blood pressure elevations are commonly characterized by systolic blood pressure values between 140 and 180 mmHg, where there is even less evidence in terms of clinical significance and the effect of management. We sought to address these uncertainties by investigating the systolic blood pressure elevations, using a threshold conventionally used in hypertension guidelines for community patients (≥140 mmHg), 6 in Australian medical inpatients and its association with in‐hospital mortality.

METHODS

Study design and setting

Data of consecutive admissions to the medical wards of two tertiary hospitals in South Australia were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records (EMR) over a 2‐year period (March 2020 to March 2022) at the Royal Adelaide Hospital, an 800‐bed hospital within the Central Adelaide Local Health Network, and over a 1‐year period (August 2021 to August 2022) at Flinders Medical Centre, a 600‐bed hospital within the Southern Adelaide Local Health Network. The difference in the period of data collection was due to the different timing of EMR implementation for inpatient services in the two hospitals. Only the first admission was included for individuals with multiple admissions during the study period. Admissions to non‐general medical wards (e.g., intensive care unit) and those during which no blood pressure measurements were recorded were excluded from further analysis. The study received ethics approvals from the Central Adelaide Local Health Network (reference number: 11643) and the Southern Adelaide Local Health Network (reference number: 131.22) Research Ethics Committees.

Blood pressure measurement

Blood pressure at the participating hospitals was typically measured on the ward by nursing staff using either automatic or manual blood pressure cuffs. While the study focused on non‐invasive blood pressure measurements, staff may at times enter readings from arterial lines in this field of EMR, and the frequency of this occurrence is unable to be determined retrospectively. There was no fixed measurement schedule of blood pressure, which primarily reflected the individual patient's medical condition as well as nursing staff availability. In all patients, all systolic blood pressures were captured after the time of ward admission and throughout the hospitalization, while those in the emergency department prior to ward admission were not evaluated. Demographic data and the Charlson comorbidity index, a commonly used index of comorbidity, 7 were also collected from administrative records.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to assess patient characteristics and patterns of systolic blood pressure in the cohort. Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate possible associations between having at least one systolic blood pressure reading ≥140 mmHg during hospitalization and in‐hospital mortality after adjusting for age, gender, and the Charlson comorbidity index. The variance inflation factor was used to evaluate for multicollinearity, with variables removed for scores > 10. Alpha was set at 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using Python (Delaware, USA).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

We identified 16 896 medical inpatients during the study period. The mean age was 69.0 ± 19.1 years and there were 8519 females (50.2%). The median length of stay was 4 days (interquartile range, IQR 2 to 7 days). A total of 667 patients died in hospital (4.0%). The median number of blood pressure recordings was 25 (IQR 12 to 52) and the median systolic blood pressure was 126 mmHg (IQR 112 to 141 mmHg). There were 12 842 inpatients with at least one systolic blood pressure reading of ≥140 mmHg during admission (76.0%; Table 1). A total of 1974 patients had at least one systolic blood pressure reading of ≥180 mmHg (11.7%).

Participant characteristics.

Systolic blood pressure at time of admission

The median first systolic blood pressure recorded upon ward admission was 127 mmHg (IQR 113 to 143 mmHg). A total of 5105 (30.2%) and 285 (1.7%) patients had an initial systolic blood pressure reading of ≥140 mmHg and ≥180 mmHg, respectively.

Maximum systolic blood pressure during admission

The median maximum systolic blood pressure was 155 mmHg (IQR 140 to 168 mmHg). There was a statistically significant relationship between having at least one maximum systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and a lower likelihood of in‐hospital mortality (odds ratio 0.41, 95% confidence interval 0.35 to 0.49, P < .001).

Number and proportion of systolic blood pressures in hypertensive range

The median number of systolic blood pressure readings ≥140 mmHg was 4 (IQR 1 to 14). These recordings corresponded to a median proportion of systolic blood pressure measurements ≥140 mmHg of 18.8% (IQR 1.4% to 47.6%). A total of 4087 patients (24.2%) had systolic blood pressure recordings ≥140 mmHg for at least half of their readings. In the entire cohort, the median and IQR number of systolic blood pressure readings ≥180 mmHg was 0 (i.e., the number of systolic blood pressure readings ≥180 mmHg at the 25th, 50th, and 75th centiles, was 0). Only 40 patients (0.2%) had a systolic pressure ≥180 mmHg for at least half of their readings.

DISCUSSION

Our multicenter retrospective cohort study of consecutive medical admissions has shown that the majority (75.6%) of Australian general medical inpatients have at least one systolic blood pressure reading of ≥140 mmHg during hospitalization. As expected, systolic blood pressure readings of ≥180 mmHg were observed in a much smaller group. Notably, having at least one systolic blood pressure reading of ≥140 mmHg was associated with a significantly lower likelihood of in‐hospital mortality (odds ratio 0.41). It is not an expected finding as hypertension is a well‐established risk factor for cardiovascular disease, along with diabetes, smoking and dyslipidaemia, while cardiovascular death is a major cause of mortality in the Australian population. 8 There are several reasons potentially accounting for this finding. One possible reason is related to the type of presenting illness seen in the general medicine population and its relation with blood pressure, for example, sepsis. In Australia, the incidence of sepsis has been reported to be 1.1%. 9 Hypotension in the setting of sepsis portends septic shock, which is associated with a high risk of poor outcome, for example, a reported mortality rate of 30%−50%. 10 Alternatively, the lower mortality rate associated with systolic blood pressure readings of ≥140 mmHg may relate to the natural progression of existing disease states. For example, patients with advanced heart failure or cirrhosis may develop hypotension. Clinical trials have shown that low blood pressure occurs in 10%−15% of patients with heart failure, 11 while hypotension is also a complication of cirrhosis stemming from portal hypertension which results in splanchnic and systemic vasodilation. 12 Therefore, multiple factors may account for the observed association between systolic blood pressure reading ≥140 mmHg and in‐hospital mortality. This finding highlights the need for further research investigating the clinical significance on systolic blood pressure elevations and optimal monitoring and management strategies.

This study has not evaluated the aetiology, investigation, and treatment of systolic blood pressure elevations in this cohort. Further studies could be done to investigate management approaches and interventions as well as the pathophysiological and clinical significance of different thresholds to define hypertension in this group. Additionally, due to its retrospective nature, assertions regarding causality in this study are not possible and the missing data may limit the comprehensiveness of the analysis. It is also worth noting that the blood pressures measurements used in our study cohort were entered manually by healthcare providers into the EMR during routine clinical practice, which may have resulted in some errors or omissions. Measurement bias may also arise from the different blood pressure measurement technique among healthcare providers. 13 Despite the adjustment for age, gender and comorbidity index, which is an established predictor of adverse clinical outcome in a wide range of medical and surgical patient groups, 7 possible unaccounted confounding factors such as the use of drugs, underlying medical conditions and lifestyle factors may affect the validity of the observed association between inpatient hypertension and mortality. 14 While we employed an accepted threshold of hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg) for community patients, additional research is warranted to ascertain a more accurate threshold for inpatient hypertension.

To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the clinical significance of blood pressure elevations among inpatients, future research in this area should also seek to a) characterize other blood pressure components, such as diastolic blood pressure and mean arterial blood pressure, the effect of specific comorbid conditions, and the number of blood pressure measurements b) evaluate the effectiveness of antihypertensive treatment and patient responses to these interventions, as recent studies have identified an increased risk of adverse outcomes, 2 and c) expand the scope of research to include other inpatient populations, such as those in surgical specialties, as well as in other demographic regions such as the Aboriginal population as they have a different risk profile for cardiovascular disease which may show different outcomes with inpatient elevation of blood pressure. 15
