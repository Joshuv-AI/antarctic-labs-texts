Impacts of external airfoil flaps on the S809 airfoil
Authors: Rui Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The trailing edge flap is an effective airfoil flow control technique having many forms, including the external airfoil flap (EAF) which lacks deep studies. In this paper, the calculated results of computational fluid dynamics are first verified through experiments, and the impact of the EAF parameters on the aerodynamic performance of the S809 airfoil is then studied. The obtained results show that the EAF can significantly improve the lift coefficient and stall angle of attack (AOA). Furthermore, the standard L9(34) orthogonal range analysis is conducted, demonstrating the parameter impact ranking. More precisely, for the improvement of the lift coefficient, the order is normal distance > flap angle > tangential distance. For that of the lift-drag ratio, the order is flap angle > tangential distance > normal distance. For improving the stall and optimal AOA, the order is flap angle > normal distance > tangential distance. The results obtained in this study provide a basis for the blade design of rotating mechanism with EAF.

Introduction

Wind energy plays a crucial role in green energy due to its cleanliness and renewability. The aerodynamic characteristics of the blade are key components of wind turbines, as they significantly affect their energy harvesting efficiency. The design of the blade is based on the airfoil, and therefore it is essential to increase its aerodynamic performance.

The flow control technology of the airfoil allows to adjust its aerodynamic performance by changing its shape1. Among them, the trailing edge flap (TEF) is considered as the most feasible2. In fact, the TEFs can increase the aerodynamic performance by increasing the camber of the airfoil3,4. The study presented in5 shows that the TEFs have the forms of plain, split, slotted, double-slotted, and external airfoil flaps (EAFs). In general, the plain flaps can be divided into hinged and morphed ones. Although the hinged TEFs have a simple structure, the gap at the joint has an adverse effect on the efficiency of the airfoil, which reduces its lift coefficient (Cl)6,7. Many studies have been conducted on the performance of dynamic hinged TEFs8–11. Although the morphed TEFs need higher material requirement, they have a high performance12,13. Nevertheless, the large pressure fluctuation generated by the morphed TEFs results in greater radiation far-field noise14.

Reiswich et al.15 experimentally studied the impact of flexible split flaps on the airfoil stall characteristics at low Reynolds number (Re). Their results showed that the flexible split flaps can increase the aerodynamic performance of the airfoil stall, and they exhibit the non-harmonic mean oscillation characteristics of slow upward and fast downward. Tian et al.16 experimentally analyzed the impacts of three different types of split flaps on the energy capture efficiency of the airfoil. Their results showed that the split flaps set on the pressure surface have the best effect. In addition, they studied the influence laws of the opening amplitude, flap length, and position.

Wang et al.17 numerically analyzed the impact of slotted flaps on the airfoil aerodynamic performance. Their results showed that the airfoil can obtain better comprehensive aerodynamic performance by simultaneously deflecting the two flaps and spoilers. Marouf et al.18 numerically analyzed the impact of a new slotted flap on the aerodynamic performance of the airfoil. Their results showed that the optimized cambering shape increases the aerodynamic comprehensive performance of the airfoil. Aziz et al.19 studied a novel multi-slot airfoil through numerical simulation. Their results showed that, at high angle of attack (AOA, α) and Re of 2.74 × 105, the lift coefficient and stall AOA (αs) of a multi-slot airfoil are larger than those of a single-slot and clean airfoil.

Gurney flaps (GFs) have also been widely studied. For instance, Chandrasekhara et al.20 experimentally studied the problem of optimal flap height of the GFs. Their results showed that the optimum height is 1% of the chord length of the airfoil, and the canonical pressures can be used as a new parameter to determine the height of the GF in order to reduce the computational load. Amini et al.21 used the adjoint shape optimization process for optimizing the height of GFs. Their results showed that the flap height designed by this method significantly reduces the drag coefficient (Cd) of the airfoil, while its lift coefficient is almost unaffected. Xie et al.22 numerically studied the impact of the GFs of different heights on the energy capture efficiency of the airfoil. Their results showed that they significantly improve the maximum output power, efficiency, and lift of the airfoil. The maximum output power coefficient increases with the increase of the flap height, while it remains stable in the later stage. Frederick et al.23 studied how the dynamic GFs can reduce the unsteady loads on wind turbine blades through experiments and numerical simulations. Their results showed that the blade load measurement and dynamic GF feedback control can effectively remove the turbulence fluctuations. Min et al.24 studied the aerodynamic performance of dynamic GFs using the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) method. Their results showed that the GFs can reduce the rotor vibration of wind turbines. Zhu et al.25 numerically compared the adaptive GFs and fixed GFs in terms of improving the energy capture efficiency of an oscillating airfoil. Their results showed that the adaptive GFs are able to increase the energy efficiency of the oscillating airfoil by increasing the lift force and adapt to a larger flap length and operate in a wider range of oscillation frequency. Moreover, the best position for the GFs is as close to the trailing edge of the airfoil as possible. Li et al.26 experimentally studied the impact of GFs on the divergent trailing edge airfoils. Their results showed that they significantly improve the lift coefficient, maximum lift coefficient, and maximum lift-drag ratio (Cl/Cd) of the airfoils. Ye et al.27 numerically analyzed the law of improving the airfoil aerodynamic performance with serrated GFs. Their results showed that it can significantly improve the lift coefficient of the airfoils, delay the stall, and significantly reduce the noise.

Wenzinger et al.28 experimentally studied the pressure distribution over a NACA 23012 airfoil with an EAF. Their results showed that the EAF reduces adverse pressure gradients and the tendency of the main airfoil (MA) to stall. It also allows the MA to carry more load without stalling. Sun et al.29 numerically evaluated the vibration-fatigue load reduction potential of an active EAF for wind turbine blades. Their results showed that the EAF can reduce the bending moment fluctuation and the damage equivalent load of the blade root. Thakur et al.30 numerically evaluated the load mitigation impacts on foundations of the wind turbine using EAF. Their results showed that the EAF can result in response reduction for pile foundations. Aguilar et al.31 studied the impact of the EAF on the energy harvesting efficiency of horizontal-axis hydrokinetic turbine through numerical simulations combined with experiments. Their results showed that EAF can increase the power coefficient of the turbine.

Studies on the TEFs control technology.

These studies greatly promoted the development of TEFs. It is important to mention that, the TEF, which is a form of TEFs can increase the power coefficient of rotating machinery (e.g. wind turbines, unmanned aerial vehicles, and hydrokinetic turbines) and reduce the cross-sectional area without changing the chord length, allowing to reduce the cost. To the best of our knowledge, a deep study on the impacts of the EAF parameters on the aerodynamic performance of the airfoil does not exist. In this paper, the S809 airfoil, which is commonly used in wind turbine blades, is considered as the object of the study. The impacts of the EAF parameters on the aerodynamic performance of this airfoil are studied through numerical simulation. In addition, the change laws of pressure, streamline, noise characteristics, and vortex shedding are analyzed. Finally, the magnitude of the promotion efficiency of the EAF parameters on the airfoil performance are studied by conducting the standard L9(34) orthogonal range analysis.

EAF configuration

In this paper, the S809 airfoil, which is the blade profile of the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) Phase II wind turbine32, is studied. The constant chord length (c) of the S809 airfoil is equal to 600 mm33. With the same chord length, the S809 airfoil is scaled into two parts: one with a chord length of 0.75c (i.e., the MA) and the other with a chord length of 0.25c (i.e., the EAF). The new airfoil is referred to as the airfoil with EAF. Its profile is shown in Fig. 1, where x and y are the horizontal and vertical positions from the leading edge, respectively. It can be seen that a gap exists between MA and EAF. The tangential and normal distances from the trailing edge of the MA to the leading edge of the EAF are respectively denoted by Δx and Δy, and the corner of the EAF around its leading edge is denoted by ψ (flap angle). The area of the airfoil with EAF is reduced by 37.53%, compared with the S809 airfoil.Fig. 1Airfoil profile.

Numerical simulation

Solving method

The viscous incompressible flow is calculated by CFD in this study. The continuity and Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) equations are considered as the governing equations. The two-equation k − ω shear stress transport (SST) model34 is considered as the turbulence model. The COUPLED algorithm is adopted to couple the velocity and pressure, the convergence criterion is set to 1.0 × 10−6 for the residual value of continuity equation. The aforementioned results are based on steady-state calculations. However, for the S809 airfoil, the lift coefficient exhibits fluctuations at α = 19.18° and 20.16°, which indicates that the steady-state calculations cannot accurately capture the real aerodynamic behavior. Therefore, an unsteady-state simulation approach is adopted in this study. The time-averaged results are taken after the solution reaches a periodic steady state. To determine a suitable time step, values of 0.002, 0.004, and 0.008 s are tested. The lift coefficient at α = 19.18° is calculated for each case and compared with the experimental value. The obtained results are shown in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Lift coefficient of the S809 airfoil with time steps of 0.002, 0.004, and 0.008 s.

The lift coefficient calculated with a time step of 0.004 s is equal to 0.939, which is the closest to the experimental value of 0.929. Therefore, a time step of 0.004 s is adopted.

Figure 5 shows a comparison between the calculated lift and drag coefficients and those obtained by experiments36. For α = 19.18° and 20.16°, the steady-state calculation results of Cl are lower than the experimental values, while those of the unsteady-state are consistent with them. Results and discussion

Effect of the tangential distance

Figure 6 shows the variation of the lift and drag coefficients of the airfoils function of the tangential distance. It can be seen that the stall AOA increases with the increase of the tangential distance. For α < 3.07°, when the tangential distance increases, the lift and drag coefficients decrease and increase, respectively. For 3.07° < α < 13.22°, the tangential distance has a slight impact on the lift and drag coefficients. For α > 13.22°, when the tangential distance increases, the lift and drag coefficients increase and decrease, respectively. For α > 15.23°, the drag coefficient increases with the increase of the tangential distance. In general, the tangential distance increases the stall AOA and the lift coefficient after the stall, which is consistent with the conclusion drawn in28.Fig. 6Variation of the lift and drag coefficients of the airfoils function of the tangential distance.

Variation of the lift and drag coefficients of the airfoils function of the tangential distance.

For α = − 1.04°, when the tangential distance increases, the Cp curve areas of the MA and EAF decrease and increase, respectively. However, the Cp curve area of the MA is more significantly changed. Conclusion

This paper studies the impacts of different EAF parameters on the aerodynamic performance of the S809 airfoil through numerical simulation.

The main results can be summarized as follows:At equal chord length, the EAF can reduce the airfoil area by 37.53%, increase the lift coefficient and stall AOA, decrease the streamline separation near its suction surface, reduce the far-field SPL at large AOA values, and decrease the amplitude of the lift coefficient. In general, the appropriate increase of the gap between the MA and EAF allows to increase the ability of the airflow to resist the adverse pressure gradient, which increases the lift coefficient. However, a very large gap often causes streamline separation, which decreases the lift-drag ratio and stall AOA, and destabilizing the gap vortex at large AOA values, leading to the instability of the aerodynamic performance and the increase of the far-field SPL.In general, the tangential distance increases the stall AOA and lift coefficient after the stall, while the normal distance increases the latter before and after the stall. The flap angle decreases the stall AOA and increases the lift coefficient before the stall. The tangential and normal distances have a slight impact on the period, while the latter increases with the increase of the flap angle.MA provides greater lift than EAF, especially at large AOA values. Therefore, in design optimization, MA should be given priority.The order of the impact of the EAF parameters on the lift coefficient is Δy > ψ > Δx, that on the lift-drag ratio is ψ > Δx > Δy, and that on the stall AOA is ψ > Δy > Δx.
