Effects of adding antibiotics to an inactivated oil-adjuvant avian influenza vaccine on vaccine characteristics and chick health
Authors: Xuehuai Shen, Anyun Zhang, Ruihong Zhao, Lei Yin, Dongdong Yin, Yin Dai, Hongyan Hou, Jieru Wang, Xiaomiao Hu, Xiaocheng Pan, Danjun Zhang, Wei Liu, Yongjie Liu, Kai zhan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring poultry immunization, antibiotics are typically added to inactivated oil-adjuvant avian influenza (AI) vaccines. Here, we evaluated the effects of adding ceftiofur, a third-generation cephalosporin, to an AI vaccine on vaccine stability and structure and on chick growth, immune efficacy, blood concentrations, biochemical and immunological indices, and gut microbiota. The results demonstrated that neither aqueous ceftiofur sodium nor ceftiofur hydrochloride oil emulsion formed a stable mixture with the vaccine. Adding ceftiofur formulations, particularly ceftiofur hydrochloride, at >4% significantly destabilized the vaccine's water-in-oil structures. Adding ceftiofur also increased vaccine malabsorption at the injection site; specifically, adding ceftiofur hydrochloride reduced H5N8 and H7N9 antibody titers after the first immunization (P < 0.05) and H7N9 antibody titers after the second immunization (P < 0.01). Serum drug concentrations did not differ significantly between the groups with ceftiofur sodium and hydrochloride addition. Ceftiofur addition increased postvaccination chick weight loss; compared with the vaccine alone, ceftiofur sodium–vaccine mixture increased chick weight significantly (P < 0.05). Ceftiofur addition also increased stress indices and reduced antioxidant capacity significantly (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). Vaccination-related immune stress reduced gut microbiota diversity in chicks; ceftiofur addition reversed this change. AI vaccine immunization significantly reduced the relative abundance of Lactobacillus and Muribaculaceae but significantly increased that of Bacteroides and Eubacterium coprostanoligenes group. Ceftiofur addition restored the gut microbiota structure; in particular, ceftiofur hydrochloride addition significantly increased the abundance of the harmful gut microbes Escherichia-Shigella and Enterococcus, whereas ceftiofur sodium addition significantly reduced it. The changes in gut microbiota led to alterations in metabolic pathways related to membrane transport, amino acids, and carbohydrates. In conclusion, adding ceftiofur to the AI vaccine had positive effects on chick growth and gut microbiota modulation; however, different antibiotic concentrations and formulations may disrupt vaccine structure, possibly affecting vaccine safety and immunization efficacy. Thus, the addition of antibiotics to oil-adjuvant vaccines is associated with a risk of immunization failure and should be applied to poultry with caution.Key words: avian influenza vaccines, antibiotic, immunization effect, drug concentration, chick health

INTRODUCTION

Avian influenza (AI) viruses (AIV) can infect different avian species, including chickens, waterfowl, and wild birds; these viruses are transmitted to humans through birds (Tian et al., 2021). In particular, highly pathogenic AIVs, such as H5N1 and H7N9, cause acute influenza syndromes in humans; these syndromes can cause a pandemic (Chen, et al., 2006; Zhu, et al., 2022). Many countries, including China, have effectively controlled epidemics due to highly pathogenic AIVs by using the “culling + immunization” approach (Shi, et al., 2023). Therefore, immunization with AI vaccines is essential for poultry health maintenance at poultry farms. Of these vaccines, inactivated oil-adjuvant AI vaccines are the most widely used because of their efficacy, safety, and ease of mass production (Shin, et al., 2015; Xu, et al., 2023). However, during the immunization process, chickens may experience stress and inflammation at the injection site due to exogenous antigens, vaccine adjuvants, and handling (Yang, et al., 2011; Wu, et al., 2021).

Cephalosporins, a major class of antibacterial agents currently used for both humans and animals, are active against many Gram-positive and some Gram-negative bacteria (Lin and Kück, 2022). Ceftiofur, a third-generation cephalosporin developed only for veterinary use, is popular in veterinary medicine because of its activity against a broad spectrum of bacteria and pharmacodynamic properties (Hornish and Kotarski, 2002). In poultry farming, antibiotics are often combined with vaccine immunization to prevent local inflammation possibly caused by vaccine immunization or inhibit infections by some respiratory pathogens. A survey found that over 80% of chicken farms administer antibiotics concurrently with poultry vaccines (including the Marek's disease vaccine and inactivated AI vaccines). Among these, ceftiofur is the most commonly used antibiotic, followed by lincomycin–spectinomycin (Shen, unpublished data). A study evaluated the effects of ceftiofur sodium and gentamycin sulfate on a commercial herpesvirus of turkey vaccine for Marek's disease. The results demonstrated that adding ceftiofur sodium to the vaccine did not affect the viral titer after 1 h of treatment, but it significantly reduced viral titers after 2 h of treatment; moreover, the in vivo experiments demonstrated that the addition of ceftiofur did not affect postimmunization viremia (Buscaglia, 2013).

Studies have also found that cephalosporin antibiotics can affect immune function in poultry. For instance, Klaudia and Alina (2015) reported that ceftiofur administration reduced the percentage of splenic B-cell and T-cell subsets in chicks; in addition, ceftiofur–lipopolysaccharide (LPS) cotreatment significantly reduced the percentages of CD4+CD8− and CD3+TCRγδ+ cells but increased those of CD4−CD8+ cells. Another study demonstrated similar results: in 2-day-old chicks, administration of ceftiofur at recommended therapeutic doses significantly reduced the number of Bu-1+ cells in the medulla of the bursa fascicularis, as well as maturation of B cells (Chrzastek, et al., 2011). Thus, in organisms, cephalosporin antibiotics may affect lymphocyte differentiation and alter the proportions of lymphocyte subpopulations, in turn affecting the immune status. However, most of the aforementioned studies have focused on lymphocytes, and research on whether cephalosporin antibiotic application affects antigen-specific antibody levels in poultry remains limited. Some studies have found that ceftiofur treatment combined with vaccine immunization can significantly attenuate mucosal immune response to the vaccine and specific antibody production in pigs (Pomorska-Mól, et al., 2015; Pomorska-Mól, et al., 2016). Moreover, commercial ceftiofur is mostly available as a sodium salt or hydrochloride, and almost all inactivated vaccines against highly pathogenic AI (HPAI) available in China are the oil-adjuvant type; as such, the addition of ceftiofur may destabilize vaccine structure and stability (Mo, et al., 2023).

In this study, we investigated the effects of practical application of ceftiofur combined with an AI vaccine in poultry farming. We added 2 types of ceftiofur to the AI vaccine and compared their effects on the vaccine's stability and structure. We also evaluated the effects of ceftiofur combined with an AI vaccine on growth performances, antibody titers, blood drug concentrations, biochemical indices, cytokines, and gut microbiota in chicks.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Vaccines and Antibiotics Preparation

We purchased reassortant AIV (H5+H7) trivalent oil-emulsion inactivated vaccine (H5N6 strain H5-RE13 + H5N8 strain H5-RE14 + H7N9 strain H7-RE4), product batch number: 2022060, from Harbin Harvac Biotechnology (Harbin, China), ceftiofur sodium for injection (1 g, product batch number: 20220201) from Sichuan Jixin Animal Pharmaceutical (Sichuan, China), and ceftiofur hydrochloride injection oil emulsion (50 mg/mL, product batch number: 20220402) from Chongqing Fangtong Animal Pharmaceutical (Chongqing, China).

Effect of Adding Different Ceftiofur on AI Vaccine Quality

Ceftiofur sodium was dissolved in saline to a concentration of 50 mg/mL. Next, the prepared ceftiofur sodium solution or the ceftiofur hydrochloride oil emulsion was added directly to the AI oil-adjuvant vaccine at 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20% of the vaccine's volume. The mixtures were shaken at 3,000 rpm for 5 min, with the AI vaccine used in the control group.

Assessment of vaccine mixture appearance and stability. We observed all vaccine–ceftiofur mixtures for changes in color, texture, and other external characteristics. The stability of the vaccine–ceftiofur mixtures was evaluated using the centrifugal emulsion breaking method for stability testing of oil-adjuvanted vaccines in the Chinese Veterinary Pharmacopoeia. In brief, the mixtures were added to a 10-mL centrifuge tube, centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min, and observed for the presence of delamination. An absence of delamination was considered to indicate a well-stabilized water-in-oil (W/O) structure of the oil-adjuvanted vaccine, whereas its presence was considered to demonstrate poor stability.

Microscopic assessment of vaccine particle appearance. We also placed 10 µL of each vaccine–ceftiofur sodium mixture on a slide and covered it with a coverslip. Next, the morphology of the oil-emulsion particles was observed under a 200× optical microscope, and images were recorded.

Animal Grouping and Immunization

We purchased 240 specific-pathogen-free (SPF) chicks (age = 2 days) from Beijing Merial Vital Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China). These chicks were reared until 20 d of age; then, they were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 60 chicks per group): vaccine-only, vaccine + ceftiofur sodium aqueous [vaccine + ceftiofur (A)], vaccine + ceftiofur hydrochloride oil group [vaccine + ceftiofur (O)], and control. Each group contained 6 replicates with 10 chicks each. All groups were immunized at the ages of 20 d (subcutaneous injection in the neck) and 35 days (intramuscular injection in the chest). At both timepoints, the control, vaccine-only, vaccine + ceftiofur (A), and vaccine + ceftiofur (O) groups received sterile saline (0.5 mL), AI vaccine (0.5 mL), the vaccine (0.5 mL) + ceftiofur sodium (2 mg in 0.02 mL), and the vaccine (0.5 mL) + ceftiofur hydrochloride (2 mg in 0.02 mL), respectively.

The feeding, management, inoculation, and euthanasia procedures used for animal experiments in this study were carried out in compliance with the Institutional Animal Care Regulations and Guidelines of the Anhui Academy of Agricultural Sciences and strictly followed the requirements outlined in the Laboratory Animal Guideline for Ethical Review of Animal Welfare (National Standards of the People's Republic of China, GB/T35892-2018).

Chick Growth Performance Determination

The chicks in all 4 groups were weighed at 20, 35, and 50 d of age; their body weights were averaged, and their total weight gain during the experimental period was calculated.

Assessment of vaccine absorption at the vaccination site

At 35 and 50 d of age (i.e., at 15 d after each vaccination timepoints), 10 chicks from each group were selected for autopsy, and vaccine absorption at their vaccination site was assessed. We observed the vaccine injection sites for vaccine malabsorption and encapsulation according to the evaluation method reported by Imamura et al. (2010); malabsorption and encapsulation were graded as normal (−), mild (+), moderate (++), and severe (+++), which corresponded to the scores of 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Mean scores were calculated for each group.

Serum Ceftiofur Content Determination

We selected 35-day-old SPF chicks (n = 60) and divided them into 4 groups (n = 15 chicks per group): ceftiofur sodium control, ceftiofur hydrochloride control, vaccine + ceftiofur (A), and vaccine + ceftiofur (O). The ceftiofur sodium control, ceftiofur hydrochloride control, vaccine + ceftiofur (A), and vaccine + ceftiofur (O) groups were intramuscularly administered with ceftiofur sodium (2 mg in 0.02 mL), ceftiofur hydrochloride (2 mg in 0.02 mL), the vaccine (0.5 mL) + ceftiofur sodium (2 mg in 0.02 mL), and the vaccine (0.5 mL) + ceftiofur hydrochloride (2 mg in 0.02 mL), respectively. At 2 and 6 h after injection, we collected the chicks’ blood samples. Next, the serum was separated from the samples through centrifugation at 5,000 rpm for 5 min and stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Next, the serum samples were analyzed for ceftiofur concentration at Shanghai Fuda Analytical Testing Group (Shanghai, China). In brief, 0.1 mL of serum was added in a 10-mL centrifuge tube and mixed with 5 mL of the extraction solution (methanol:water, 10:90) on a vortex mixer for 1 min, followed by ultrasonic oscillation for 30 min. The tube was incubated at −40°C for 60 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected and subjected to solid-phase extraction in a solid-phase extraction column. The eluent from the extraction was concentrated in a concentrator until dryness. Then, 0.5 mL of 10% methanol–water solution was added, followed by mixing on a vortex mixer for 1 min, centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was aspirated for liquid chromatography (LC)–tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) analysis. Integration was performed using MultiQuant, and serum ceftiofur concentrations were calculated using a prepared standard curve.

Serum Antibody Titer Determination

Blood samples were collected from chicks 15 d after each immunization (i.e., at 35 and 50 d of age). These samples were subjected to centrifugation at 5,000 rpm for 5 min to separate serum from the blood. Next, we used hemagglutination inhibition (HI) and hemagglutination (HA) test kits from Harbin Guosheng Biotechnology (Harbin, China) to determine the titers of AI H5N8 (Re-14) and H7N9 (Re-4) subtype antibodies in the serum. The AI antibody detection method and result interpretation were performed in accordance with the testing standards in Diagnostic Techniques for Highly Pathogenic Avian Influenza, GB/T 18936-2020.

Serum Biochemical Index Determination

Blood samples were collected from chicks at 40 d of age (i.e., 5 d after the second immunization) in negative-pressure blood collection tubes. The blood samples were allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 5,000 rpm for 5 min to collect serum, which was stored at −40°C until further use. Malondialdehyde (MDA), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) concentrations and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) in the serum samples were determined using commercialized kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Serum Immune Index Determination

Serum immunoglobulin (Ig) Y, interleukin (IL) 2, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) α, and interferon (INF) γ contents were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits from Wuhan Colorful Gene Biological Technology (Wuhan, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Gut microbiota analysis

Fresh feces were collected from all groups at 40 days of age (i.e., 5 days after the second immunization) and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Total genomic DNA was extracted using a DNA extraction kit from Guangzhou Magen Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Next, DNA concentration was verified using NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. Genomic DNA was then used as a template for 2 rounds of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification with the 16S rRNA genes (V3–V4 variable regions). The PCR products were then analyzed through electrophoresis and purified using AMPure XP beads (Agencourt). The purified PCR products were quantified using a Qubit dsDNA Assay Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA). Raw sequencing data were presented in the FASTQ format. Paired-end reads were then preprocessed using cutadapt to detect and cut off the adapter. After trimming, paired-end reads were processed to filter out low-quality sequences. By using DADA2 (Callahan, et al., 2016) set to the default parameters of QIIME2 (Bolyen et al., 2019) (2020.11), we denoised, merged, detected, and cut off the chimera reads. Finally, the representative reads and the amplicon sequence variant (ASV) abundance table were obtained as the software output. The representative read of each ASV was selected using the QIIME 2 package. All representative reads were annotated and blasted against the Silva database (version 138) by using a q2-feature-classifier with the default parameters. The 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech (Shanghai, China). The microbial sequencing data were deposited in GenBank (accession no. PRJNA PRJNA1073046).

Data and Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard errors of the means. Significant differences between different groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) on SPSS (version 16.0; IBM Corp., New York, NY). The parameters were using the least significant difference (with equal variances assumed) and Dunnett's T3 (with equal variances not assumed) methods in post hoc multiple comparisons one-way ANOVA. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant difference between the 2 groups. All graphs were created in GraphPad (version 8; GraphPad Software, CA).

RESULTS

Effects of Ceftiofur Addition on AI Oil-Adjuvant Vaccine Quality

The color of all AI vaccine–ceftiofur formulations appeared milky white after they were mixed well. As the antibiotic concentrations increased, the color of the mixed vaccine darkened gradually (Supplementary Figure S1), correlating with the gradual dilution of the antibiotic in the vaccine. In the stability test, centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 15 min led to delamination in all vaccine–ceftiofur mixtures, with aqueous ceftiofur delaminating at the bottom and oil-emulsion ceftiofur delaminating at the top; in contrast, no delamination occurred in the vaccine-only sample (Supplementary Figure S2). Next, we microscopically observed the W/O particle structure of the vaccine in all mixtures. The results (Figure 1) revealed that the addition of aqueous ceftiofur led to the formation of heterogeneous vesicular particles; as the ceftiofur concentrations increased, it increasingly affected the stability of the vaccine particles but without causing any significant structural damage. The addition of ceftiofur hydrochloride oil emulsion led to the formation of emulsion-like structures at lower concentrations (2% and 4%) but resulted in the disruption of the W/O particle structure, forming obvious aggregated block-like structures at higher concentrations (≥8%).

Effects of adding ceftiofur at different concentrations (v/v) on the water-in-oil structure of inactivated oil-adjuvant avian influenza vaccine. (A) Effects of aqueous ceftiofur sodium. (B) Effects of ceftiofur hydrochloride oil emulsion.

Taken together, these results indicated that despite thorough shaking and mixing, the addition of ceftiofur to the oil-adjuvant vaccine—whether in the aqueous or oil-emulsion form—does not fuse with the vaccine's W/O particles, compromising the vaccine–ceftiofur formulation stability. Moreover, the addition of the antibiotic at concentrations >4% (v/v) of the vaccine volume may damage the oil-adjuvant vaccine structure; the intensity of this effect is also closely related to the form in which the antibiotic is added.

Effects of Ceftiofur Addition on AI Oil-Adjuvant Vaccine on Vaccine Absorption

During the experimental period, none of the chicks demonstrated abnormalities in the appearance of the immunized sites; moreover, no deaths were noted. Figure 2 illustrates the appearance of vaccine absorption at the vaccination site. In the vaccine-only group, vaccine absorption was better after subcutaneous injection in the neck (overall malabsorption score = 0.3) than after intramuscular injection in the chest (overall malabsorption score = 0.8). Moreover, for both subcutaneous and intramuscular injections, the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) group (overall malabsorption scores = 0.9 and 1.6, respectively) demonstrated significantly higher scores than the vaccine-only group (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). Among all groups, the vaccine + ceftiofur (O) group demonstrated the highest malabsorption score for vaccine absorption (2.4 and 2.6 for subcutaneous and intramuscular injections, respectively), with evident oil-emulsion lumps observed (all P < 0.01). The anatomical observations demonstrated that the unabsorbed vaccine particles and aggregates formed after administration of mixtures of the AI vaccine and ceftiofur sodium or ceftiofur hydrochloride. When the aggregates were cut open, unabsorbed oil emulsion could be observed (Figure 2C). These results indicated that the addition of different ceftiofur formulations to AI vaccines significantly worsens vaccine absorption at the injection site.

Effects of Ceftiofur Addition on AI Oil-Adjuvant Vaccine on Antibody Titer

We assessed the chick serum titer of antibodies against AI H5N8 and H7N9 subgroups 15 days after each immunization timepoints. The results demonstrated that after the first immunization, the H5N8 and H7N9 antibody titers were 24.15 and 24.64 in the vaccine-only group, respectively. Compared with the vaccine-only group, the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) group demonstrated no significant differences in the H5N8 (24.00) and H7N9 (24.57) antibody titers (P > 0.05); in contrast, compared with the vaccine-only and the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) groups, the vaccine + ceftiofur (O) group demonstrated significantly lower H5N8 (22.36) and H7N9 (23.36) antibody titers (P < 0.05; Figures 3A and 3B).

(C, D) Serum avian influenza H5N8 and H7N9 antibody titers after the second immunization. Values are presented as means ± standard errors of the means. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, compared with the control group. NS: no significance between the indicated groups.

After the second immunization, the H5N8 and H7N9 antibody titers were 29.46 and 29.38 in the vaccine-only group, respectively. Compared with the vaccine-only group, the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) group demonstrated no significant differences in H5N8 (29.62) and H7N9 (29.15) antibody titers (P > 0.05). Moreover, in the vaccine + ceftiofur (O) group, H5N8 and H7N9 antibody titers were 29.16 and 27.85, respectively; here, H7N9 antibody titers were significantly lower than those in the vaccine-only and the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) groups (P < 0.01; Figures 3C and 3D).

Effects of Ceftiofur Addition on AI Oil-Adjuvant Vaccine on Serum Drug Concentration

All chicks were intramuscularly injected with 2 mg of ceftiofur, and their serum drug concentrations were measured at 2 and 6 h after injection based on the peak area of the curve (Figure 4A). The results demonstrated that at 2 h after injection, the blood drug concentration was 649.91 ng/mL in the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) group and 656.38 ng/mL in the ceftiofur (A) control group but 491.65 ng/mL in the vaccine + ceftiofur (O) group and 480.77 ng/mL in the ceftiofur (O) control group. At 6 h after injection, the blood drug concentration was 17.85 ng/mL in the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) group and 17.62 ng/mL in the ceftiofur (A) control groups but 18.99 ng/mL in the vaccine + ceftiofur (O) group and 15.53 ng/mL in the ceftiofur (O) control group. The results demonstrated no significant differences in serum drug concentrations between the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) and vaccine + ceftiofur (O) groups compared with their respective control groups (all P > 0.05; Figures 4B–4E). However, the average serum concentration of ceftiofur sodium was higher than that of ceftiofur hydrochloride at 2 h after injection; nevertheless, serum ceftiofur sodium and ceftiofur hydrochloride concentrations became similar at 6 h after injection.

Effects of Ceftiofur Addition on AI Oil-Adjuvant Vaccine on Serum Immune Indices

Next, we measured the serum immune indices of our chicks (Figure 5). The results demonstrated that serum IgY levels were higher in the vaccine-only, vaccine + ceftiofur (A), and vaccine + ceftiofur (O) groups than in the control group; moreover, these levels were significantly higher in the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) group than in the control and vaccine-only groups (all P < 0.05).

Serum IL-2 levels were significantly higher in the vaccine-only, vaccine + ceftiofur (A), and vaccine + ceftiofur (O) groups than in the control group (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). In contrast, serum IL-6 levels were lower in the vaccine-only, vaccine + ceftiofur (A), and vaccine + ceftiofur (O) groups than in the control group; however, this difference was only significant between the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) and control groups (P < 0.01). Effects of Ceftiofur Addition on AI Oil-Adjuvant Vaccine on Gut Microbiota

We used high-throughput sequencing of 16S rRNA to evaluate the effects of vaccination and the addition of ceftiofur to the vaccine on the gut microbiota of our chicks. The results demonstrated that the number of ASVs was lower in the vaccine-only group but higher in the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) group than in the control group; moreover, this number was significantly higher in the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) group than in the vaccine-only group (P < 0.05; Figures 6A and 6B). Moreover, alpha diversity (based on Ace and Shannon indices) was significantly lower in the vaccine-only, vaccine + ceftiofur (A), and vaccine + ceftiofur (A) groups than in the control group (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01; Figure 6, Figure 6D). Furthermore, the results of our beta diversity analysis indicated significant intrasample group clustering in all groups, indicating that both vaccination alone and the addition of ceftiofur to the vaccine significantly affected the gut microbiota of our chicks (Figure 6E).

DISCUSSION

HPAI vaccines are essential for protecting poultry and human health by preventing and controlling HPAI epidemics in many countries, including China (Park, et al., 2016; Villanueva-Cabezas, et al., 2017). Inactivated oil-adjuvant vaccines are the most widely used types of HPAI vaccines (Mo, et al., 2023). In commercially available vaccines, an oil adjuvant is mixed with the vaccine antigen in a W/O dosage form; the integrity and stability of the resulting W/O structure significantly improve the immunization effect of the vaccines. However, improper handling of commercial AI vaccines may disrupt their oil-in-water structure. For instance, in many poultry farms, antibiotics are added to AI vaccines, possibly compromising the vaccines’ structural stability (Del Giudice and Rappuoli, 2015). In the current study, we added 2 commonly used ceftiofur formulations to an AI vaccine and evaluated their effects on the W/O particle structure of the vaccine; neither of the 2 ceftiofur–vaccine mixtures demonstrated antibiotic–vaccine fusion. The centrifugal emulsion breaking method is now commonly used to evaluate oil-emulsion stability; in this method, centrifugation accelerates delamination, facilitating the observation of stability (Yu, et al., 2017). Our antibiotic–vaccine mixtures produced significant delamination after centrifugation, suggesting that the addition of ceftiofur can compromise oil-adjuvant vaccine stability. W/O emulsions, used in many avian vaccines (Jansen, et al., 2007; Aljumaili, et al., 2020), not only activate antibody production strongly but also act as a storage depot from which antigens are released gradually; this prolongs antigen retention time and improves vaccine stability (Jansen, et al., 2005; Lee and Suresh, 2022). In oil-adjuvant vaccines, intact W/O structures are the basis for their action. In the present study, microscopic observations from oil-emulsion particles in the vaccine revealed that adding aqueous ceftiofur sodium did not disrupt the vaccine's W/O particle structure; however, it could not be fused with the W/O particles. In contrast, adding ceftiofur hydrochloride oil emulsion, only at relatively large doses, led to significant disruptions in the W/O particle structure. These results suggested that different doses and formulations of ceftiofur have varied effects on the W/O structures of AI vaccines.

Safety and efficacy are 2 major indicators of vaccine quality (Yan, et al., 2018; Crouch, et al., 2020). We comprehensively evaluated the safety and efficacy of our ceftiofur–AI vaccine mixtures. During the experimental period, no significant difference was noted between the immunization groups. However, an anatomical examination of the vaccination site revealed that the addition of either of 2 ceftiofur formulations to the vaccine significantly increased lump occurrence and vaccine malabsorption at the injection site; in particular, the addition of ceftiofur hydrochloride oil emulsion led to more severe abnormalities at the injection sites. In practice, the absence of any apparent abnormalities at the injection site after coadministration of ceftiofur and AI vaccines leads poultry farmers to overlook the potential for vaccine malabsorption (Song, et al., 2016). A poultry vaccine's effectiveness is mainly based on the levels of specific antibodies produced after vaccination (Ravikumar, et al., 2022; Kapczynski, et al., 2023). To assess the effectiveness of the AI vaccine after the addition of ceftiofur, we measured the AI antibody titers at both immunization timepoints; the AI (H5N8 and H7N9) antibody titers did not differ significantly between the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) and vaccine-only groups, whereas these titers (H5N8 and H7N9 after first immunization, and only H7N9 after second immunization) were significantly lower in the vaccine + ceftiofur (O) group than in the other immunization groups. Studies on the effect of cephalosporin antibiotics on vaccine immunization efficacy in poultry are scant. Buscaglia (2013) found that treatment with ceftiofur sodium significantly reduced Marek´s disease titer; however, the authors’ in vivo results demonstrated that ceftiofur sodium did not affect postvaccine immunization viremia. Several studies have evaluated the effects of ceftiofur, as a bactericide, on the immunizing effect of live bacterial vaccines (Fernandes Filho, et al., 2013; Hu, et al., 2021). In the present study, our results revealed that the addition of different ceftiofur formulations to the vaccine reduced the safety and efficacy of the vaccine, and this effect may be closely related to the disruption of the W/O structure caused by the addition of different ceftiofur formulations.

Ceftiofur, a third-generation cephalosporin, is widely used for treating bacterial infections in animals; ceftiofur products are commercially available, mainly in the form of sodium salt or hydrochloride (Hornish and Kotarski, 2002; Pomorska-Mól, et al., 2015; Li, et al., 2019). The effects of ceftiofur depend on its blood and organ concentrations (Hornish and Kotarski, 2002; Meyer, et al., 2009). In the present study, we determined the effects of adding different ceftiofur formulations to the AI vaccine on blood drug concentrations through LC-MS/MS. The results demonstrated that at 2 and 6 h after intramuscular injection, the ceftiofur blood concentrations did not differ significantly from those in the corresponding control group. After intramuscular injection, the changes in blood concentrations of ceftiofur exhibit a typical pharmacokinetic profile (Hornish and Kotarski, 2002). Although we did not completely assess ceftiofur pharmacokinetics in this study, our 2- and 6-h assay results in the vaccine + ceftiofur group did not differ significantly from those in the ceftiofur control group; indicating that the vaccine + ceftiofur and ceftiofur control groups had similar ceftiofur pharmacokinetics. Knafo et al. (2019) reported that a plasma ceftiofur concentration of ≥1 µg/mL can exert antimicrobial effects against many pathogenic bacteria. However, in the present study, ceftiofur demonstrated a blood concentration of <1 µg/mL in all treatment groups and thus potentially exerted a limited antimicrobial effect. The bactericidal efficacy of ceftiofur is correlated with the duration for which it is maintained in the target tissue above the minimum inhibitory concentration of pathogenic bacteria, and a single dose of ceftiofur administered concomitantly with the AI vaccine is insufficient to maintain effective bactericidal efficacy over the treatment duration (Hornish and Kotarski, 2002; Knafo, et al., 2019). Moreover, in healthy animals, antibiotic use can lead to resource and financial losses (Davies and Davies, 2010), and it may result in environmental pollution and bacterial resistance development (Paulson and Zaoutis, 2015; Saraiva, et al., 2018).

During the vaccine immunization process, stimulation by exogenous antigens, as well as inflammatory reactions and scratching at the injection site, can trigger stress in chicks (Scanes, 2016). This stress can increase mortality rates and reduce growth performance (Ye, et al., 2023). A study reported that LPS inoculation–induced immune stress was noted to significantly reduce growth performance, increase stress-related hormones and inflammatory factors, and alter liver transcript expression profiles in chickens (Bi, et al., 2022). The current results are in agreement with previous results: AI vaccine immunization can cause a significant reduction in chick weight gain, and adding ceftiofur to vaccines can somewhat reverse reductions in weight gain, with aqueous ceftiofur sodium having the most significant effect.

Despite considerable debate regarding their effectiveness, antibiotics have been widely employed as growth promoters at subtherapeutic concentrations in animal production and agricultural practices over the last century (Broom, 2017). In the current study, despite being administered concurrently with the AI vaccine only twice, a growth-promoting effect on chicken weight gain was noted in the ceftiofur-supplemented groups compared with the vaccine-only group. Hypotheses on the mechanisms underlying the growth-promoting effects of antibiotics have focused on the beneficial modulation of the gut microbiota (Brüssow, 2015). Moreover, the direct anti-inflammatory effects of antibiotics, reducing the nutritional cost of (intestinal) inflammation, can explain their growth-promoting effect (Galarza, et al., 2021). Ceftiofur is primarily used as a clinical therapeutic agent, and comprehensive research on its long-term effects on growth promotion and health in agricultural animals is lacking. Furthermore, in animals, ceftiofur is rapidly metabolized, leading to its blood concentrations decreasing (Hornish and Kotarski, 2002); however, the irrational use of ceftiofur is associated with food safety and ecological health risks.

In the current study, serum biochemical indices were also assessed to determine the stress and antioxidant status of the chicks in different immunization groups (Amevor, et al., 2022). The levels of stress indices (i.e., MDA and LDH) were significantly higher in all immunization groups (with or without antibiotic addition); moreover, compared with the vaccine alone, adding ceftiofur to the vaccine led to higher MDA and LDH levels, indicating the presence of cytotoxicity and tissue damage (Ghonimi, et al., 2021; Yang, et al., 2022). Similarly, compared with the vaccine alone or the control, adding ceftiofur to the vaccine led to significantly lower levels of serum antioxidant indices (i.e., SOD, CAT, and T-AOC). Thus, the addition of ceftiofur may increase organismal and oxidative stress caused by AI vaccine immunization.

Bactericidal antibiotics such as quinolones, aminoglycosides, and β-lactams can induce reactive oxygen species formation in mammalian cells through mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to damaged DNA, protein, and lipid accumulation. These effects may have long-term consequences on the organismal systems (Kalghatgi, et al., 2013; Kohanski, et al., 2016). Cephaloridine (Tune, et al., 1989) and ceftriaxone calcium (Yifan, et al., 2020) can induce oxidative stress in kidney cells, which leads to kidney damage. In the present study, the addition of ceftiofur to the AI vaccine further increased serum MDA and LDH levels, indicating that ceftiofur treatment may cause oxidative damage in chicks—consistent with the previous findings. However, because we only investigated the short-term effects of ceftiofur on organismal stress and antioxidants, further research exploring the long-term impacts on chicken health and production is warranted.

Next, we evaluated serum levels of IgY and cytokines (i.e., IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α, and INF-γ), which correspond to an organism's humoral and cellular immune status (Simon, et al., 2014; Stambas, et al., 2020). A study demonstrated that in immunized chicks, oral ampicillin and neomycin treatment enhanced antibody (IgM, IgA, and IgY) responses compared with the antibiotics-free control; this enhancement was potentially associated with altered levels of Lactobacillus in the chick feces (Murai, et al., 2016). We obtained similar experimental results in the current study: AI vaccine immunization (with or without ceftiofur) increased IgY levels; these levels were higher in the group receiving AI vaccine immunization with ceftiofur. Moreover, IL-2 levels were significantly elevated in the sera of all immunized chicks (with or without ceftiofur), whereas those of IL-6 and TNF-α demonstrated a decreasing trend of varying degrees. Moreover, INF-γ level decreased only in the groups with ceftiofur addition. Many studies have demonstrated that antibiotic treatment can significantly affect lymphocyte differentiation and maturation in chicks. Chrzastek et al. (2011) reported that early administration of antibiotics (including ceftiofur) to chicks did not disturb the microstructure of the bursa of Fasciola but resulted in a significant decrease in the Bu-1 + B-cell percentage in the organ. Ci et al. (2008) demonstrated that ceftiofur inhibited TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 secretion from LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells and in C57BL/6 mice. Klaudia and Alina (2015) reported that ceftiofur–LPS cotreatment significantly reduced CD4+CD8− and CD3+TCRγδ+ cell percentages and increased CD4−CD8+ cell percentages. In an experimental porcine model, a study reported that ceftiofur hydrochloride treatment significantly delayed the development of humoral response against pseudorabies virus and considerably suppressed the production of antibodies against swine influenza virus; moreover, after restimulation with pseudorabies virus, the porcine model demonstrated significantly lower INF-γ concentrations after treatment with ceftiofur (Pomorska-Mól, et al., 2015). Ceftiofur can inhibit inflammatory cytokine production by blocking NF-κB and MAPK signaling; this may be the main reason that ceftiofur treatment led to similar results in poultry, murine, and porcine experimental models (Ci, et al., 2008). IL-2, an important proinflammatory cytokine, affects lymphocyte proliferation and natural killer cell activation (Malek, 2008). In the present study, IL-2 concentrations were high, particularly in the groups with ceftiofur addition; this change corresponded to the local inflammation induced by vaccination at the injection site, particularly vaccine malabsorption caused by ceftiofur addition. As such, the current results corroborate those of previous studies: the addition of ceftiofur suppresses cellular immunity in chicks, and antibacterial agents may have multiple modulatory effects on phagocyte and lymphocyte functions (Labro, 2000; Jankowski, et al., 2022); however, these results warrant further confirmation.

Gut microbiota plays a crucial role in determining host health (Clavijo and Flórez, 2018; Fan and Pedersen, 2021). In chicks, LPS-induced immune stress can significantly alter the gut microbiota, thereby affecting the growth performance of the chicks (Yang, et al., 2011; Ye, et al., 2023). Ceftiofur is a broad-spectrum antibiotic, often used for prophylactic administration in newly hatched chicks to mitigate early gastrointestinal infections caused by Enterobacteriaceae bacteria in the gut. Ceftiofur can significantly affect the gut microbiota structure of chicks, altering many metabolic pathways associated with digestion (Lemos, et al., 2020). The ASV number is used to represent the number of microbial taxa in the microbial samples in the sequenced samples, and similar to alpha diversity, this value indicates microbial diversity in the samples (Chang, et al., 2022). In the present study, the vaccine-only group demonstrated significant reductions in the ASV number and alpha diversity (Ace and Shannon indices); however, the addition of ceftiofur reversed this reduction in gut microbial diversity. In our beta diversity analysis, significant clustering of samples was noted between the different immunization groups. Therefore, immunization with the AI vaccine (with or without ceftiofur) can affect gut microbial diversity significantly in chicks.

In the chick gut, the abundance of Lactobacillus and Muribaculaceae was relatively low in the vaccine-only group, but the addition of ceftiofur increased their abundance. Many studies have shown that in chickens, Lactobacillus aids in increasing growth performance, improving intestinal health, and resisting infection by pathogenic microorganisms (Xu, et al., 2019; Shen, et al., 2022; Yin, et al., 2023). Muribaculaceae, a genus that has been clearly classified only recently, may have multiple functions in complex carbohydrate degradation (Lagkouvardos, et al., 2019). Li and Hao demonstrated that in a model of DSS-induced enterocolitis, Muribaculaceae plays an important anti-inflammatory role (Hao, et al., 2021; Li, et al., 2022); moreover, this genus may be critical for gut–brain axis regulation (Zhang, et al., 2022). Bacteroides and Eu. coprostanoligenes group are microbial genera involved in intestinal fatty acid and cholesterol metabolism pathways, respectively (Wei, et al., 2021; Fan, et al., 2023). Their abundance was significantly higher in the vaccine-only group; however, the addition of ceftiofur reversed this abundance to normal levels (compared with the control group). Escherichia-Shigella, a genus of harmful intestinal bacteria, is strongly associated with intestinal inflammation and systemic infection development (Liu, et al., 2020; Schreier, et al., 2022). In the current study, Escherichia-Shigella abundance was significantly higher after vaccine immunization alone or combined with ceftiofur hydrochloride; in contrast, its abundance decreased after coadministration of ceftiofur sodium with the vaccine. Finally, Enterococcus—initially believed to be a harmless commensal organism in chicken gut—is now considered a major pathogen causing morbidity and mortality in chickens (Jung, et al., 2018). In the current study, Enterococcus abundance increased only in the vaccine + ceftiofur (O) group.

Gut microbiota plays a vital role in maintaining gut health and influences the overall performance of chickens (Khan, et al., 2020). In the present study, immunization with the AI vaccine alone appeared to have a detrimental effect on the intestinal microflora structure in our chickens (compared with the control group); for instance, the abundance of the beneficial intestinal bacteria Lactobacillus and Muribaculaceae decreased significantly but that of Escherichia-Shigella was increased significantly. Ceftiofur can directly affect the gut microflora structure; thus, its beneficial effects include the control of harmful pathogenic microorganisms and a reduction in intestinal inflammation (Galarza, et al., 2021). In the present study, the addition of ceftiofur to the AI vaccine reversed the trend of decreasing gut Lactobacillus and Muribaculaceae abundance in the AI vaccine-only group but reduced gut Escherichia-Shigella and Enterococcus abundance in the vaccine + ceftiofur (A) group; these beneficial changes in gut microflora potentially improved weight gain in the chicks further.

In our functional prediction analysis of gut microbiota, the vaccine-only group demonstrated low levels of metabolism with respect to major functions involving gut microbes such as membrane transport as well as cofactor, vitamin, amino acid, and carbohydrate metabolism. Altered gut microbiota can modify metabolism, intestinal inflammation, and immunity, increasing energy harvest from the diet (Rogawski, et al., 2017). The addition of ceftiofur reversed this reduction. Notably, these changes in gut microbiota metabolism were consistent with the variations in the mean body weight observed in each immunization group; this may have been due to alterations in the gut microbiota. The differences between the 2 ceftiofur–vaccine coadministration groups may be attributable to the differences in intestinal drug concentrations and blood pharmacokinetics of the different ceftiofur formulations used (Hornish and Kotarski, 2002; Foster, et al., 2019).

In this study, we comprehensively evaluated only 2 formulations of ceftiofur and the AI vaccine for their oil-adjuvanted vaccine stability and efficacy and their effects on chick health. Despite the encouraging results, the study has some limitations. Variations in antibiotic types and formulations, oil-adjuvant vaccine production processes, and factors related to immunized animals may have diversely affected the immunization effects of our antibiotic–vaccine mixtures. Therefore, further research exploring the beneficial effects of antibiotics while preserving the immunological efficacy of vaccines is required. Nevertheless, to reduce bacterial resistance and ensure food safety and ecological health, the safe and rational use of antibiotics in animal production remains a primary consideration.

CONCLUSIONS

The current results indicated different ceftiofur formulations and commercial inactivated AI oil-adjuvant vaccines cannot form stable mixtures; moreover, the antibiotic may even disrupt the W/O particle structure of the vaccine. Vaccine structure disruption by ceftiofur hydrochloride may be a major factor contributing to poor vaccine absorption and impacting AI antibody levels. Furthermore, the addition of ceftiofur may induce stress in the body, reducing antioxidant capacity and cytokine immunity levels. Nevertheless, ceftiofur–vaccine coinjection does not affect the pharmacokinetics of the antibiotic (aqueous or oil emulsion), but it influences chick weight gain positively, possibly through gut microbiota modulation. In conclusion, to prevent immunization failure, antibiotics and oil-adjuvant vaccines should be coadministered to poultry with caution. However, considering the beneficial effects of antibiotics in immunization using oil-adjuvanted vaccines, the development of antibiotic formulations suitable for addition to these vaccines is warranted.
