Reply to Strømland et al.: Confusion should be minimized rather than used to explain cooperation in public goods game experiments
Authors: Guangrong Wang, Jianbiao Li, Wenhua Wang, Xiaofei Niu, Yue Wang
Published: 2024

Wang et al. (1) introduce a question that assesses participants’ confusion in the public goods game (hereinafter “PGG”) and suggest that social preferences, rather than confusion, play a crucial role in determining cooperation in PGG. Strømland et al. (2) argue that the conclusion of Wang et al. is misleading.

Comments of Strømland et al. result from misunderstanding the nature of Wang et al.’s and Burton-Chellew, Mouden and West’s (hereinafter “BEW”) test questions (1, 3). In Wang et al.’s test question, participants report their supposed contributions if they only consider their own interests. Answering the test question correctly or incorrectly reflects the traditional definition of confusion, i.e., misunderstanding the game. Maximizing payoff is achieved by contributing zero, regardless of what others contribute. In Wang et al.’s experiment 1, 97.5% of the participants answer the test question correctly pre-experiment. In experiment 2, 96% of the participants answer the question correctly post-experiment, while only 26% of the participants answer BEW’s test question correctly. This confirms the robustness of Wang et al.’s test question, as it concerns only the understanding of the game, but not the actual decisions.

In contrast, answering “yes” to BEW’s test question does not necessarily mean that decisions are based on a misunderstanding of the game. There may be other factors involved, such as normative considerations, image concerns, and some other behavioral motives (Table 1; (1, 4, 5)). Thus, participants’ incorrect belief that their choices can influence the choices of others does not mean that they misunderstand the game. That is, Wang et al.’s question about the payoff-maximizing action is much more relevant to whether there is confusion in PGG. In fact, only the informed and self-interested players could answer both the Wang et al. and BEW questions correctly, but the confused players or the informed and non-self-interested players could not answer both questions correctly.

Besides, Strømland et al. argue that Wang et al.’s finding of increased contributions in the computer condition is consistent with confusion. Wang et al.’s evidence from Experiment 1 shows that there are many factors that influence contributions in the computer condition beyond confusion, such as social norm and image concern (Table 1). Regarding the sample size issue, Wang et al.’s Experiment 2 is fully comparable to BEW’s. And there are significant differences between Fosgard, Hansen, and Wengström’s (6) and Wang et al.’s comprehension question. Fosgard et al. ask participants to answer the payoff-maximizing action from the third-party perspective, as their prompt is “imagine a person who only cares about their own earnings”, and participants answer these questions in an incentivized way. They also do not explicitly state: “Of course, your actual contribution may be different,” as Wang et al. do.

In summary, confusion (i.e., misunderstanding the experiment) cannot explain cooperative behavior in PGG. We are heartened to see the field engaging with our work.
