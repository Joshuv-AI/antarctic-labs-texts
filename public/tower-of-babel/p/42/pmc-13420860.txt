Association between hepatitis C virus and overactive bladder: a cross-sectional study based on the 2013–2018 NHANES
Authors: Yiming Ding, Tonglei Zhao, Jie Ji, Zejun Wang, Dakun Zhang, Zhicheng Ye, Ming Chen, Weipu Mao, Jianping Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundsOveractive bladder (OAB) is a common urological disorder with an incompletely understood pathogenesis that markedly impacts patients’ quality of life, and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection is associated with systemic inflammation and extrahepatic complications, with their potential association remaining understudied. This study thus aims to investigate the association between HCV and OAB in adults. This study aims to investigate the association between hepatitis C virus (HCV) and overactive bladder (OAB) in adults.MethodsThis study analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted between 2013 and 2018. Logistic regression analysis, subgroup analysis, and interaction tests were employed to assess the association between HCV and OAB. Additionally, propensity score matching (PSM), inverse probability weighting (IPTW), and overlap weighting (OW) were employed to control for confounding factors. E-value analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of results against unmeasured confounders, while sensitivity and specificity analyses evaluated the predictive performance of HCV for OAB.ResultsThis study included 14,012 patients aged ≥ 20 years. Logistic regression analysis demonstrated an association between HCV and OAB (OR = 2.06, 95% CI 1.46–2.90, p < 0.001). Subgroup analyses and interaction tests confirmed that the relationship between HCV and OAB remained consistent across all subgroups (all interaction P values > 0.05). After PSM, IPTW, and OW analyses, the association between the two remained significant; E-value analysis demonstrated strong robustness to unmeasured confounders; Sensitivity and specificity results indicate that HCV has predictive value for OAB.ConclusionsThe findings of this study indicate that there is a significant correlation between HCV and OAB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-026-02170-w.

Introduction

Overactive bladder (OAB) is a condition characterized primarily by urinary urgency, often accompanied by urinary frequency and nocturia, with or without urge urinary incontinence (UUI), occurring in the absence of urinary tract infection or other apparent pathology [1]. Studies indicate that in the American population, the prevalence of OAB is 16.0% among men and 16.9% among women, with rates increasing with age [2]. In 2020, the total number of people affected by overactive bladder (OAB) across eight major countries—the United States, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the United Kingdom, Japan, and China—was approximately 363 million. This figure is projected to rise to 401.6 million by 2030 [3]. Furthermore, OAB exerts clinically significant impacts on quality of life, sleep quality, and mental health, while also affecting work productivity to varying degrees, thereby increasing the healthcare burden on affected populations [2, 4, 5].Recent studies have confirmed that OAB in women is independently associated with increased all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality, and that metabolic markers related to the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG) are closely linked to the risk of developing OAB [6].

The pathogenesis of OAB is complex, involving multiple aspects such as neurological, muscular, inflammatory, and metabolic factors. Several risk factors have been identified, including age, gender, diabetes, obesity, and smoking; however, some causes remain unclear [7].

HCV is a blood-borne viral disease causing substantial morbidity and mortality worldwide [8–10]. In the United States, over 4 million individuals are infected with hepatitis C virus (HCV), with 85% progressing to chronic active hepatitis. Some patients develop liver fibrosis, while others may deteriorate to cirrhosis and ultimately hepatocellular carcinoma [11–13].

In recent years, increasing research has focused on the impact of HCV infection on the urinary system, particularly its effects on bladder function. Chuang et al. included 158 patients with chronic viral hepatitis-related cirrhosis and found that the prevalence of OAB reached 31%, closely correlated with the severity of liver function impairment. The proportion of OAB among cirrhosis patients was higher than that in the general population [14]. Epidemiological studies have shown that HCV infected individuals have a higher prevalence of chronic kidney disease, proteinuria, and lower urinary tract symptoms compared to the general population, potentially mediated by viral-induced vascular damage, autoimmune responses, and pro-inflammatory cytokines cascades. However, large-scale population-based evidence linking HCV infection to OAB remains limited, and the underlying physiopathologic link remains unclear [15–18]. This study aimed to investigate the association between HCV and OAB. Publicly available data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) were utilized to examine the relationship between HCV and OAB among survey participants.

Method

Data sources and study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a study conducted by the National Centre for Health Statistics to assess the health and nutritional status of the US population. All NHANES data are accessible to the public via its official website: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/. This study utilized data from the 2013–2018 NHANES cohort, encompassing 29,400 participants. Individuals with missing data pertaining to OAB assessment (n = 14,526) or HCV related information (n = 862) were excluded. Participants aged 80 years or older and under 20 years were also excluded. Additionally, participants with conditions that could affect bladder function—such as urinary tract infections, urinary tract stones, or prostate diseases—were excluded, resulting in a final cohort of 14,012 participants, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Assessment of OAB

Overactive bladder (OAB) is defined as an overactive voiding reflex characterized by urinary urgency and incontinence (UUI) and nocturia [1]. Face-to-face interviews were conducted by trained interviewers using standardized questionnaires. The presence and severity of UUI are determined by two questions in the questionnaire: “In the past 12 months, have you experienced leakage or loss of control over small amounts of urine due to movement or pressure, and were you unable to reach the toilet in time? Severity is assessed via the question: How often does this occur? Nocturia severity is evaluated using: In the past 30 days, how many times per night did you typically get up to urinate between going to bed at night and waking in the morning?” (Based on questionnaires KIQ044, KIQ450, KIQ480) As shown in Fig. 2, OAB is defined by the Overactive Bladder Symptom Score (OABSS). An OABSS score of 3 or higher indicates the presence of OAB [19].

Definition of HCV infection

HCV information was derived from NHANES. NHANES uses an in vitro nucleic acid amplification test (COBAS AMPLICOR HCV Surveillance Test, Version 2.0) to quantitatively measure HCV RNA in human serum or plasma.Participants were considered HCV infected if they tested positive for hepatitis C virus ribonucleic acid (HCV-RNA) or answered “yes” to the questionnaire question: “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you have hepatitis C?” [20].

Statistical analysis

For all analyses, the R package (https://www.r-project.org/) and EmpowerStats (http://www.empowerstats.com/) were employed, with P < 0.05 deemed statistically significant. For continuous variables, survey-weighted means were calculated using survey-weighted linear regression with P-values. For categorical variables, survey-weighted percentages and P-values were calculated using survey-weighted chi-square tests. Three regression models were constructed using multivariate logistic regression, adjusting for different covariates. Model 1 was unadjusted for covariates; Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, and race; Model 3 adjusted for age, gender, race, PIR, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, and diabetes. Covariate selection was based on previously reported risk factors for OAB in the literature. Variables were screened using univariate logistic regression (those with P < 0.2 were included in the multivariate model), while simultaneously examining the variance inflation factor (VIF < 5) to exclude multicollinearity. Variables that were significant in univariate analysis and showed no severe colinearity were incorporated into the final model. Furthermore, to further analyse this association, subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted for age, gender, race, PIR, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, and diabetes. In the PSM analysis, OAB patients were matched 1:1 with healthy controls, sensitivity analysis was performed using both IPTW and OW methods to further eliminate the influence of confounding factors.Additionally, the predictive value of HCV for OAB was assessed using sensitivity and specificity, and E-value analysis was employed to evaluate the potential impact of unmeasured confounding factors on the study conclusions.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

This study enrolled 14,012 participants aged between 20 and 80 years, comprising 3,119 OAB patients and 10,893 non-OAB participants. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of OAB patients compared with non-OAB participants, continuous variables were described using mean (95% CI) and analyzed using parallel t-tests. Categorical variables were described using n (%) and analyzed using parallel χ² tests. Significant differences existed between the OAB and non-OAB groups regarding age, gender, race, PIR, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, alcohol consumption, and HCV status. Among all participants, the mean age was 57.98 years in the OAB group and 46.14 years in the non-OAB group, with the OAB group exhibiting a higher mean age. Regarding gender, a higher proportion of OAB patients were female (62.61% vs. 48.98%). Furthermore, non-Hispanic whites constituted the largest proportion in both OAB and non-OAB groups. Compared to non-OAB patients, OAB patients were more likely to have comorbid diabetes (22.15% vs. 8.79%), hypertension (52.36% vs. 29.65%), and smoking (49.61% vs. 42.11%), and were more likely to be infected with HCV (1.80% vs. 0.77%).

Correlation between OAB and HCV

To further investigate the relationship between HCV and OAB, this study employed multivariate logistic regression, adjusting for various covariates to construct three regression models, as shown in Table 2. Model 1 was the unadjusted model. Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, and race. Model 3 adjusted for age, gender, race, PIR, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, and diabetes. In the unadjusted Model 1, HCV was found to be associated with OAB (OR = 2.92, 95% CI 2.12–4.02, p < 0.001), substantially increasing the prevalence of OAB. Similarly, the same relationship was observed in Model 2 (OR = 2.60, 95% CI 1.85–3.63, p < 0.001) and Model 3 (OR = 2.06, 95% CI 1.46–2.90, p < 0.001).

This study used E-value analysis to assess the impact of unmeasured co-founders on the aforementioned association results. The results showed that the E-value for the multivariate logistic regression model (Model 3) was 2.91 (95% CI: 2.03–4.05). This suggests that unmeasured confounding factors strongly associated with both HCV infection and OAB would be required to reverse the association findings reported in this study, indicating that the association between HCV and OAB identified in this section is robust to unmeasured confounding factors (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate whether the association between HCV and OAB remained consistent across different demographic characteristics. Figure 3 results indicate a higher prevalence of OAB in the 59 years and older age group (OR = 10.24, 95% CI 5.85–17.94). Furthermore, females were more susceptible to OAB (OR = 6.96, 95% CI 3.48–13.94), those with a PIR below 1.3 (OR = 2.87, 95% CI 1.81–4.55) and non-Hispanic Blacks (OR = 8.91, 95% CI 3.71–21.36) were more likely to have OAB. Among HCV patients, smoking (OR = 3.77, 95% CI 2.08–6.83), alcohol consumption (OR = 2.14, 95% CI 1.18–3.86), hypertension (OR = 3.39, 95% CI 1.77–6.51), and diabetes (OR = 6.75, 95% CI 2.33–19.60) were associated with a higher likelihood of OAB. Interaction tests revealed no significant differences between any stratification factors (P > 0.05 for all), indicating that the relationship between HCV and OAB remained consistent across all subgroups.

Propensity score matching analysis

To further substantiate the association between HCV and OAB risk, this study employed propensity score matching (PSM) for participants. A 1:1 PSM analysis was conducted, adjusting for age, gender, race, poverty income ratio (PIR), alcohol consumption, smoking status, history of diabetes, and hypertension (Fig. 4). Table 3 displays baseline characteristics post-PSM, revealing no statistically significant differences between groups except for the poverty income ratio (PIR) (P = 0.009). Using post-PSM data, multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted, with results presented in Table 4. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, and race; Model 3 adjusted for age, gender, race, PIR, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, and diabetes. Findings from all three models demonstrated an association between HCV and OAB.

This study conducted an E-value analysis of the logistic regression results following propensity score matching (PSM). As shown in Table 4, the E-value for Model 3 was 2.63 (95% CI: 1.66–3.91), indicating that even after adjusting for unmeasured co-founders via PSM, the association between HCV and OAB remained robust, reflecting the strong stability of this association following matching.

Sensitivity analyses using inverse probability weighting (IPTW) and overlapping weighting (OW) were conducted to validate the robustness of propensity score matching (PSM) results. Weighted logistic regression results showed: in the IPTW analysis, the OR for the association between HCV and OAB was 2.02 (95% CI: 1.42–2.86, P < 0.001), while the OW analysis yielded an OR = 1.99 (95% CI: 1.40–2.83, P < 0.001). These results were highly consistent with the PSM findings (OR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.24–2.79), and the standardized mean differences (SMDs) for all covariates after weighting were < 0.1, indicating strong robustness of the study conclusions to confounding factors.

Using HCV infection as a positive diagnostic criterion, we analyzed its predictive value for OAB. The results showed that HCV had a sensitivity of 40.3% and a specificity of 77.9% for predicting OAB, suggesting that HCV infection has a certain predictive value for the development of OAB.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study involving 14,012 participants, a significant correlation was observed between HCV and OAB. Furthermore, results from subgroup analyses and interaction tests further demonstrated that this association remained consistently stable across various demographic characteristics, including age, gender, race, PIR, smoking, alcohol consumption, and the presence of hypertension or diabetes. To mitigate confounding effects, propensity score matching was employed in this study. Results from the propensity score-matched analysis also confirmed the positive correlation between HCV and OAB.

The findings of this study are consistent with previous relevant research. Past studies have examined the relationship between viral hepatitis-related cirrhosis and overactive bladder (OAB) or the association between HCV and other urological disorders. Chuang et al. conducted an observational study involving 164 patients with chronic viral hepatitis-related cirrhosis, of whom 158 completed the OABSS questionnaire. The study found a significantly elevated prevalence of OAB among patients with HCV-related cirrhosis. Cirrhosis indicators such as low serum albumin and low platelet count were predictive of OAB occurrence [14]. Furthermore, studies by Wei et al. confirm a strong association between systemic inflammatory markers and OAB. Given that chronic systemic inflammation is a core pathological feature of HCV infection, this provides significant pathophysiological evidence supporting the link between the two conditions [21–24]. Compared with previous studies, this research leverages a nationwide large-sample dataset, yielding results with superior population representativeness. Employing multiple methods for adjusting confounding factors and conducting sensitivity analyses, the findings demonstrate enhanced robustness. The study clearly establishes the predictive value of HCV infection for OAB and identifies high-risk populations, providing concrete evidence for clinical screening.

At present, the underlying mechanisms linking HCV and OAB remain unclear, potentially involving multiple pathways. We hypothesise that the significant correlation between HCV and OAB may be attributable to the following factors. Firstly, prior literature indicates that HCV patients exhibit systemic inflammatory responses with elevated levels of inflammatory biomarkers [23, 25]. According to existing research reports, the platelet-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) is closely associated with virological response in HCV patients, while the systemic immune inflammation index (SII) and neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR) may also serve as significant prognostic factors for HCV [26–28]. Kim et al. observed that female patients with OAB exhibited higher NLR values, with SII also showing a strong association with OAB [29, 30]. These studies indicate a close relationship between OAB and inflammation, suggesting that molecular imbalances within inflammatory proteins may contribute to OAB pathogenesis [31]. This may occur because HCV patients develop systemic inflammatory responses, triggering inflammatory reactions in bladder tissue and thereby inducing OAB symptoms. Secondly, the liver is intrinsically linked to the human immune system. HCV infection causes chronic hepatic dysfunction, thereby compromising the body’s immune barrier function and creating a favourable environment for bacterial infection around the bladder [32, 33]. Mitigating immune-mediated inflammatory responses holds significant importance in OAB treatment, particularly for refractory detrusor overactivity (DO), a subtype of OAB [34]. Thirdly, studies indicate that hepatitis viruses can invade the nervous system. By compromising spinal nerves, they may disrupt bladder sphincter function, leading to urinary incontinence symptoms [35–37]. Finally, insulin resistance may represent another potential mechanism linking HCV and OAB, as studies have confirmed an association between HCV infection and insulin resistance, while substantial evidence indicates a correlation between OAB and metabolic syndrome (METS) [38, 39]. METS induces ischemic damage to the bladder wall and reduced perfusion of the bladder and bladder neck, thereby precipitating OAB. Insulin resistance induced by HCV is one of the primary causes of METS [40–42]. These hypotheses may potentially elucidate the underlying mechanisms linking HCV and OAB; however, supporting evidence remains insufficient, necessitating further research for validation.

The findings of this study have significant practical implications: (1) Public health implications: It is recommended that OAB be included in routine screening protocols for individuals with HCV infection to mitigate the impact of extrahepatic manifestations of HCV on patients’ quality of life; (2) Clinical implications: It is recommended that multidisciplinary treatment plans be developed for patients with HCV infection and OAB, integrating antiviral therapy with management of urinary symptoms; (3)Future research directions: Conduct prospective cohort studies to validate the causal relationship between HCV and OAB, and undertake mechanistic studies to explore the molecular biological pathways underlying this association.

This study represents the first investigation into the correlation between HCV and OAB, including the following limitations. Firstly, as a cross-sectional study design, it cannot accurately investigate the causal relationship between HCV and OAB. Secondly, despite accounting for multiple covariates, it remains impossible to exclude the influence of all potential confounding factors. Thirdly, OAB diagnosis and certain covariate data (such as lifestyle factors) rely on self-administered questionnaires, which may introduce recall bias and information bias.

Despite these limitations, the study possesses certain strengths. Firstly, the nationally representative sample drawn from the NHANES database ensures broad applicability. Secondly, adjustment for confounding covariates enhances the reliability of the findings. Finally, subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the HCV and OAB association across different populations. Based on a large-scale national dataset, this study confirms the association between HCV and OAB, identifies high-risk populations, and establishes the predictive value of this association. It provides an epidemiological basis for clinical screening and intervention and points the way for future research into the underlying mechanisms linking the two conditions.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that HCV is associated with an increased risk of developing OAB. Public health strategies should be developed to screen for OAB among individuals with HCV infection and integrate urinary symptom management into the HCV clinical pathway. Future prospective cohort and mechanistic studies are needed to validate causal associations and explore intervention targets.
