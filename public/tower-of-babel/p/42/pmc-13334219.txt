Investigating the Gut Microbiota-Inflammatory Cytokine-Skin Axis in Inflammatory Skin Diseases: Evidence from Mendelian Randomization
Authors: Yuting Hu, Jing Chen, Qinghai Zeng, Ling Jiang, Yibo Hu, Shijie Zhu, Chuhan Fu, Li Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have reported causal associations between gut microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Inflammatory cytokines may mediate the gut-skin axis. This study aimed to investigate the mediating roles of inflammatory cytokines in the associations between gut microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases.MethodsGenome-wide association study (GWAS) summary data for 473 gut microbiota taxa, 91 inflammatory cytokines, and 11 inflammatory skin diseases were analyzed. Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses were performed using the inverse variance weighted method as the primary approach. Two-step MR analysis was further conducted to evaluate the mediating effects of inflammatory cytokines.ResultsTen inflammatory cytokines were identified as potential mediators linking gut microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases. Interleukin (IL)-10 and IL-18 mediated associations with eczema, while IL-18R1 mediated associations with eczema and atopic dermatitis. Fibroblast growth factor (FGF)-19 and IL-6 mediated associations with allergic contact dermatitis. In addition, C-C motif chemokine ligand 23 (CCL23), vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A), artemin (ARTN), IL-8, and Delta and Notch-like epidermal growth factor-related receptor (DNER) mediated associations involving alopecia areata, vitiligo, seborrheic dermatitis, and rosacea. All identified inflammatory cytokines showed mediation proportions greater than 5%.ConclusionOur findings suggest that specific inflammatory cytokines may mediate the associations between gut microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases. These results provide new insights into the gut microbiota-cytokine-skin disease axis and may contribute to the identification of potential therapeutic targets for inflammatory skin diseases.

Introduction

Inflammatory skin diseases are a heterogeneous group of disorders with multifactorial etiologies, involving environmental triggers, immune system dysregulation, sebaceous metabolism disorders, and gut microbiota imbalances. These diseases are characterized by excessive activation of innate and adaptive immune responses accompanied by the release of large amounts of pro-inflammatory cytokines, ultimately leading to skin barrier dysfunction and persistent chronic inflammation.1,2 In recent years, the concept of the “gut-skin axis” has provided a new perspective for understanding the pathogenesis of inflammatory skin diseases. Increasing evidence suggests that gut microbiota dysbiosis is commonly observed in patients with various inflammatory skin diseases, and alterations in the gut microbiota may participate in cutaneous inflammatory responses by regulating cytokine expression and immune cell activation.3 In this study, a total of 11 common inflammatory skin diseases were included and broadly classified into three categories according to their major pathophysiological and immunological characteristics: allergic diseases (eczema, atopic dermatitis (AD), allergic contact dermatitis (ACD), and urticaria), autoimmune-related diseases (psoriasis, alopecia areata (AA), lichen planus, and vitiligo), and sebaceous gland-related diseases (seborrheic dermatitis (SD), acne, and rosacea).

Gut microbiota dysbiosis in patients with inflammatory skin diseases is commonly characterized by a reduction in beneficial bacteria and an increase in potentially pathogenic bacteria. For instance, Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus exhibit a decreasing trend in patients with eczema, AD, urticaria, psoriasis, acne, and rosacea.4–9 This reduction may weaken the gut barrier, allowing microbial components and toxins to enter systemic circulation, thereby triggering immune dysregulation, overactivating inflammatory pathways, and ultimately disrupting skin homeostasis.10 Moreover, alterations in the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio have been observed in psoriasis, AA, vitiligo, acne, and rosacea.8,9,11–13 As Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes collectively constitute 90% of the gut microbiota and are major producers of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), changes in their ratio lead to a reduction in SCFAs levels.14 SCFAs exert immunomodulatory effects by regulating the NF-κB signaling pathway and inhibiting histone deacetylases, thereby influencing immune cell activation and subsequently modulating skin inflammatory responses.15,16 Notably, an increase in Ruminococcaceae has been observed in AD and psoriasis, whereas a decrease has been reported in AA, acne, and rosacea.8,9,11,13,17 This suggests that the same microbial taxa may exert distinct effects in different inflammatory skin diseases, underscoring the need to elucidate the specific alterations in gut microbiota associated with each disease.

Although clinical and animal studies have provided some evidence that gut dysbiosis contributes to inflammatory skin diseases by altering cytokine levels, the mechanisms through which specific gut microbiota influence specific inflammatory cytokines remain unknown. Clarifying the causal relationships among gut microbiota, inflammatory cytokines, and inflammatory skin diseases is crucial. Furthermore, previous clinical studies have often overlooked patient heterogeneity, including genetic, dietary, and environmental factors that may impact the gut-skin axis.3 In recent years, GWAS have provided a new foundation for gut microbiome research. Nevertheless, microbiome GWAS data still face several methodological challenges, including low heritability of gut microbial traits, the compositional nature of microbial abundance data, and substantial environmental influences, all of which complicate causal inference.

Despite these limitations, MR analysis is a powerful method that utilizes genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships. The design of MR studies is analogous to randomized controlled trials and has been widely applied to investigate the causal effects of various clinical variables on disease etiology. This approach helps overcome common limitations of observational studies, such as confounding factors and reverse causation, thereby providing more robust causal inferences.18 In this study, we performed MR analyses based on GWAS summary statistics to investigate the causal relationships among gut microbiota, inflammatory cytokines, and 11 common inflammatory skin diseases. Furthermore, we evaluated the potential mediating role of inflammatory cytokines in the association between gut microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases, aiming to elucidate the potential “gut microbiota-inflammatory cytokine-inflammatory skin disease” axis and provide theoretical insights into the pathogenesis and potential intervention strategies for these diseases.

Methods

Study Design

The overall research workflow is shown in Figure 1. Detailed procedures for instrumental variable selection, harmonization, exclusion criteria, mediation screening, and sensitivity analyses are shown in Figure S1. First, a bidirectional two-sample MR analysis was conducted to assess the bidirectional causal relationships among gut microbiota, inflammatory cytokines, and inflammatory skin diseases. Next, a two-step analysis was performed to evaluate whether inflammatory cytokines mediate the causal relationship between gut microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases. This MR study is based on three core assumptions: 1) Relevance assumption: IVs are strongly associated with the exposure; 2) Independence assumption: IVs are not confounded by factors associated with either exposure or outcome; 3) Exclusion restriction assumption: IVs influence the outcome only through the exposure. This MR study was conducted following the STROBE-MR guidelines (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomization).19 The completed STROBE-MR checklist is provided in Table S20. Data Sources

The gut microbiota data for 473 species and 91 inflammatory cytokines analyzed in this study were obtained from the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/), with GWAS IDs ranging from GCST90032172 to GCST90032644 and GCST90274758 to GCST90274848, respectively.20,21 We also obtained data on 11 common inflammatory skin diseases from the FinnGen biobank, with details provided in Table S1.22

Selection of Instrumental Variables

Considering the limited number of SNPs reaching the conventional genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5×10−8) for gut microbiota and inflammatory cytokines, a significance threshold of P < 5×10−6 was used to select IVs for univariate MR (UVMR) analyses. For reverse MR analyses using inflammatory skin diseases as exposures, a genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5×10−8 was applied. However, due to the insufficient number of SNPs available for ACD and lichen planus at this threshold, the significance threshold was relaxed to P < 5×10−6. All selected SNPs underwent linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping (r2 < 0.001, distance = 10,000 kb) to reduce the impact of correlated variants. The strength of IVs was evaluated using the F-statistic, calculated as F = r2 (N-2) / (1-r2), where r2 represents the proportion of variance explained by genetic instruments, and N is the effective sample size of the exposure. SNPs with an F-statistic < 10 or those exhibiting a palindromic structure (which prevents clear determination of effect alleles) were excluded from further analysis.

MR Causal Effect Analysis

To assess the causal effects of gut microbiota and inflammatory cytokines on inflammatory skin diseases, Inverse variance weighted (IVW) was used as the primary method for evaluating causal relationships between exposures and outcomes. MR-Egger regression, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode were used as auxiliary analyses. Results with inconsistent directions between IVW, MR Egger, and weighted median were excluded from further analysis. The IVW fixed-effect model was initially used for MR analysis, and if heterogeneity was found in sensitivity analysis, the IVW random-effect model was applied. In the first step of the two-step MR analysis, the same methods were used to verify the causal relationship between gut microbiota and inflammatory cytokines. Reverse MR analysis was used to determine whether there was reverse causality. To avoid bias caused by reverse causality, results indicating reverse causality were excluded from further analysis.

Sensitivity Analysis

Cochran’s Q test was used to assess heterogeneity in SNP effect estimates. If significant heterogeneity was indicated (P < 0.05), the IVW random-effect model was applied. MR-Egger regression was used to detect horizontal pleiotropy, with P-value for the intercept < 0.05 indicating the presence of horizontal pleiotropy; Leave-one-out analysis was performed by sequentially excluding each SNP and re-estimating the MR effect to assess whether any single SNP had an excessive influence on causal estimates.

Mediation Analysis

We used two-step MR approach for mediation analysis to determine whether inflammatory cytokines mediate the causal pathway between gut microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases. In the first step, UVMR was used to evaluate the causal relationship between gut microbiota and inflammatory cytokines, estimating the MR effect (α) while excluding gut microbiota - inflammatory cytokines pairs with reverse causality. In the second step, multivariate MR (MVMR) was used to analyze the relationship between gut microbiota, inflammatory cytokines, and inflammatory skin diseases, determining the direct MR effect values (β1, β2) and P-values for gut microbiota and inflammatory cytokines on inflammatory skin diseases. Statistical Analysis

All data analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2) and the “MR-PRESSO” and “TwoSampleMR” R packages (https://mrcieu.github.io). In both MR and reverse MR analyses, P-values < 0.05 were considered nominally statistically significant. To account for multiple testing, P values were further adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method separately within each analysis step, including gut microbiota-skin disease associations, gut microbiota-inflammatory cytokine associations, and inflammatory cytokine-skin disease associations. Associations with FDR-adjusted P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

According to their major pathophysiological and immunological characteristics, the 11 inflammatory skin diseases included in this study were classified into three categories: allergic skin diseases (eczema, AD, ACD, and urticaria), which are primarily characterized by Th2-mediated inflammation and skin barrier dysfunction; autoimmune-related skin diseases (psoriasis, AA, lichen planus, and vitiligo), which involve Th1/Th17-mediated chronic inflammation and autoimmune responses; and sebaceous-related skin diseases (SD, acne, and rosacea), which are closely associated with abnormal sebaceous secretion and alterations in the follicular microenvironment. Subsequent analyses were performed according to these classifications.

Causal Relationship Between Gut Microbiota and Inflammatory Skin Diseases

For 473 gut microbiota, we identified a total of 4288 SNPs associated with these microbiota at a significance level of p < 5×10−6. The number of IVs per microbiota ranged from 1 to 70 (median = 8), with F-statistic values ranging from 20 to 157 (median = 22.5) (Table S2). All IVs had F-statistics greater than 10, indicating no weak instrument bias, thus supporting the robustness of instrument selection. The IVW results and total effects of 473 gut microbiota on allergic skin diseases, autoimmune skin diseases, and sebaceous-related skin diseases are presented in Tables S3–S5, respectively. Although several nominally significant associations were identified (pIVW < 0.05), none remained statistically significant after FDR correction, suggesting that these associations between gut microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases are exploratory and require further validation.

Causal Relationship Between Inflammatory Cytokines and Inflammatory Skin Diseases

We identified 1696 SNPs associated with 91 inflammatory cytokines at a significance level of p<5×10−6, with the number of each IVs ranging from 6 to 35 (median = 18) and F-statistic values ranging from 20 to 3550 (median = 22.9) (Table S8). The detailed MR results of inflammatory cytokines on allergic skin diseases, autoimmune skin diseases, and sebaceous-related skin diseases are presented in Tables S9–S11, respectively.

Sensitivity Analysis

MR-Egger regression analysis indicated that gut microbiota were not affected by pleiotropy (Table S13). However, in psoriasis, CCL19 exhibited pleiotropy and was excluded from further analysis (Table S7). No pleiotropy was observed for other inflammatory cytokines (Table S14). For gut microbiota and inflammatory cytokines exhibiting heterogeneity, we further applied the IVW random-effects model for validation (Table S15). The results demonstrated that all heterogeneous gut microbiota and inflammatory cytokines passed the validation and were therefore eligible for inclusion in subsequent analyses.

Funnel plots of IVs for gut microbiota and inflammatory cytokines (Figures S15–S27, S52–S62) demonstrated symmetric distributions around the IVW model line, indicating no significant bias. Leave-One-Out analysis (Figures S28–S40, S63–S73) showed that effect estimates remained highly consistent with the reference range after excluding individual SNPs, supporting result reliability.

Mediating Inflammatory Cytokines Between Gut Microbiota and Inflammatory Skin Diseases

Initially, we conducted UVMR analysis to identify significant causal associations between gut microbiota and inflammatory cytokines that demonstrated causal effects on inflammatory skin diseases, while excluding gut microbiota-inflammatory cytokine axes exhibiting reverse causality (Tables S16 and S17). For the identified gut microbiota and inflammatory cytokines, we subsequently conducted MVMR analyses to examine their associations with common inflammatory skin diseases (Table S18). Finally, for inflammatory cytokines with MVMR p-values <0.05, we calculated βM for the gut microbiota-inflammatory cytokine axis and determined whether its direction was consistent with the total effect value β (Table S19).

Autoimmune Skin Diseases

The causal relationship between Species Clostridium E sporosphaeroides and AA was mediated by CCL23 (5.3%). No potential inflammatory cytokines were identified as mediating the causal relationship between gut microbiota and psoriasis or lichen planus (Table 4).

Sebaceous-Related Skin Diseases

The causal relationship between Species UBA7182 sp002491115 and Species Bacteroides A plebeius A with seborrheic dermatitis was mediated by ARTN (19.1%) and IL-8 (11.0%), respectively. The causal relationship between Species Bacteroides stercoris and rosacea was mediated by DNER (11.7%) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study first employed bidirectional two-sample MR analysis to assess the causal relationships between gut microbiota, inflammatory cytokines, and common inflammatory skin diseases. Subsequently, a two-step MR analysis identified 10 inflammatory cytokines that partially mediate the causal relationship between gut microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases. Overall, our findings suggest that gut microbiota may participate in the development and progression of multiple inflammatory skin diseases through immune-inflammatory pathways, thereby further supporting the important role of the “gut-skin axis” in inflammatory skin diseases.

In this study, multiple gut microbial taxa were found to be potentially causally associated with several common inflammatory skin diseases and were further linked to distinct inflammatory cytokine-mediated pathways involved in immune-inflammatory regulation, including IL-10, IL-18, IL-18R1, IL-6, VEGF-A, IL-8, ARTN, DNER, and other cytokines. Some of the microbial taxa identified in this study have previously been reported to be associated with inflammatory regulation, immune homeostasis imbalance, or host immune responses. For example, our results showed that Genus Turicibacter was associated with increased eczema risk and elevated IL-18 levels, while Xue et al23 have also reported a correlation between Genus Turicibacter and increased IL-18 levels in inflammatory bowel disease, suggesting its potential involvement in pro-inflammatory immune responses. In contrast, Phylum Cyanobacteria exhibited a potential protective effect against eczema and showed a negative causal association with IL-18 in our study. Previous studies have demonstrated that Phylum Cyanobacteria possesses anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties through suppression of the NF-κB signaling pathway and reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokine levels.24–26 These findings suggest that different gut microbial taxa may exert pro-inflammatory or protective effects on eczema through modulation of IL-18-related inflammatory pathways, while also indicating that distinct gut microbial taxa may differentially regulate inflammatory cytokines, thereby contributing to diverse biological effects in inflammatory skin diseases. Moreover, several microbial taxa involved in the gut microbiota-inflammatory cytokine axes identified in this study, particularly Family Turicibacteraceae, Species Absiella dolichum, Species UBA7182 sp002491115, and Species Clostridium E sporosphaeroides, have been rarely investigated previously, suggesting that they may represent novel candidate microbial taxa and potential immune-regulatory targets worthy of further investigation.

Notably, several inflammatory cytokines identified in this study as mediators of the potential causal associations between gut microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases are involved in immune activation, Th1/Th2 imbalance, and inflammatory regulation, suggesting that different inflammatory skin diseases may share common immune-inflammatory mechanisms. For example, IL-18 can promote Th1 immune responses and T-cell activation, thereby aggravating eczema-related inflammation.27–29 IL-6 may contribute to the inflammatory response in contact dermatitis by promoting adaptive immune activation,30,31 whereas VEGF-A may participate in the pathogenesis of vitiligo through angiogenesis and Th1 polarization.32,33 In addition, Genus Faecalicoccus exhibited potential protective effects against both eczema and atopic dermatitis and showed a negative causal association with IL-18R1, suggesting that eczema and atopic dermatitis may share a common “gut microbiota-inflammatory cytokine” interaction pattern. Collectively, these findings indicate that inflammatory skin diseases may not be entirely independent entities, but instead may share overlapping inflammatory and immune-regulatory mechanisms.

Importantly, some of our findings were inconsistent with previous inflammation-related studies. For instance, we found that IL-8 may exert a potential protective effect against seborrheic dermatitis, whereas previous studies have primarily emphasized its pro-inflammatory role in local inflammatory responses.34 Similarly, IL-6 and CCL2 in psoriasis,35,36 CCL19 in lichen planus,37 and TNF in vitiligo38 have previously been reported as risk factors for these diseases, whereas our MR results suggested potential protective effects. These discrepancies may reflect fundamental differences between MR analyses and conventional observational or experimental studies. Previous studies often evaluate cytokine expression under local inflammatory or acute disease conditions, which may represent downstream consequences of disease activity rather than causal drivers.18,39 In contrast, MR analyses estimate the lifelong systemic effects of genetically predicted cytokine levels, thereby reflecting long-term biological influences rather than transient inflammatory responses.18,40 Moreover, inflammatory cytokines may exert context-dependent effects across different disease stages, tissues, and immune microenvironments. For example, cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF have been shown to display both pathogenic and regulatory functions depending on the timing, intensity, and cellular context of immune activation.41–44 Therefore, the apparent inconsistencies between our findings and previous studies may indicate the complex and dynamic roles of inflammatory cytokines in inflammatory skin diseases, warranting further mechanistic and longitudinal investigations.

Taken together, our findings provide genetic evidence supporting the involvement of gut microbiota-inflammatory cytokine interactions in inflammatory skin diseases and further highlight the complexity of immune-inflammatory regulation within the gut-skin axis. This study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate the potential mediating role of inflammatory cytokines between gut microbiota and common inflammatory skin diseases from a mediation-analysis perspective, providing novel genetic evidence for understanding the gut-skin axis. Second, MR analysis can reduce confounding factors and reverse causality bias commonly encountered in traditional observational studies. In addition, we utilized microbiota data at the species or strain level, which improved the specificity and resolution of the analyses. Furthermore, multiple sensitivity analyses were performed to enhance the robustness of our findings.

However, several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, this study involved a large number of association analyses among gut microbiota, inflammatory cytokines, and diseases. Although multiple sensitivity analyses were conducted, false-positive findings due to multiple testing cannot be completely excluded. Second, because of the limited number of available genetic instrumental variables for certain microbial taxa, together with the relatively low heritability of microbiome GWAS data, weak instrument bias may exist. In addition, the GWAS datasets used in this study were primarily derived from European populations, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups. Finally, MR analyses provide evidence of potential causal inference at the genetic level rather than direct clinical or biological mechanistic evidence; therefore, the identified associations still require further validation through basic experiments, multi-omics analyses, and prospective studies. Cross-sectional studies of gut microbiota composition are also susceptible to environmental influences and cannot establish causality.45

Overall, our study suggests that gut microbiota may participate in the development and progression of common inflammatory skin diseases through inflammatory cytokine-related pathways. These findings provide novel genetic evidence supporting the role of the gut-skin axis and may offer new directions for future mechanistic studies and potential intervention strategies for inflammatory skin diseases.

Conclusion

In summary, this study systematically investigated the potential mediating roles of inflammatory cytokines in the associations between gut microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases using MR analyses. Our findings suggest that distinct inflammatory cytokines may mediate disease-specific associations between gut microbiota and inflammatory skin diseases, providing further insight into the gut-skin axis. These results improve our understanding of the potential immunological mechanisms underlying inflammatory skin diseases and may contribute to the identification of potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. However, given the observational nature of MR and the lack of experimental validation, the biological mechanisms underlying these associations require further investigation in future functional and clinical studies.
