The Eastern Association of Electroencephalographers: A Canadian/USA success story
Authors: Michael H. Goodman, Paul Hwang, Carole D. Burnham, Solomon L. Moshé, Jeremy M. Barry, Gregory L. Holmes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The Eastern Association of Electroencephalographers (EAEEG) is the oldest EEG society in North America.•The EAEEG has advanced research, education, and collaborative efforts in clinical neurophysiology in Canada and the US.•This review of the EAEEG’s history notes the influential role of the Kershman and Milner lectureships in shaping the field,

Introduction

The Eastern Association of Electroencephalographers (EAEEG) held its 78th annual meeting at the Harvard Club of Boston on April 25, 2025. Founded through a collaborative spirit rooted in both Canada and the United States, this organization boasts a rich history of fostering research and education through collegiality and partnership. The EAEEG has featured the participation and leadership of leading clinical neurophysiologists, neuropsychologists, and neuroscientists, not only from Canada and the U.S., but globally. This organization exemplifies the value of a regional medical and research society that bridges two nations. However, recently the tradition of collaboration and cooperation between these neighboring countries faces significant challenges. In this discussion, we explore the distinguished history of the EAEEG and highlight why it has been a successful Canadian-U.S. venture, emphasizing its importance in the landscape of neurological research and education. Review board approval for this historical review was waived.

The Hartford Connection

There was a major growth in EEG following World War II as clinicians and researchers returned from military duty to civilian positions in hospitals. The development of the four-channel Model II EEG by Albert Grass, and the emergency wartime usage of many machines, was a testament to the value of EEG in epilepsy and traumatic brain injury (Stone and Hughes, 2013). The comprehensive Gibbs’ Atlas of EEG in 1941 was then the standard publication used by most electroencephalographers throughout the world (Gibbs, 1941). After the war Albert Grass and his wife, Ellen R. Grass, developed an innovative commercial EEG instrument company and became unwavering supporters of the EEG community and associated research for the next 60 years (Zottoli, 2001).

The increasing accessibility of electroencephalographs (EEGs) and a growing recognition of their diagnostic value contributed to the formation of early interest groups in the United States. By late 1934 or early 1935, the New England EEG group comprised approximately 8 to 10 individuals who met informally every one to two months to discuss EEG-related topics (Stone and Hughes, 2013). Hallowell Davis (1986–1992), who established EEG services at Harvard Medical School and was the first person in the United States to have an EEG recorded, served as the group's host (Stone and Hughes, 2013).

The Eastern Association of Electroencephalographers (EAEEG) originated from this regional network of electroencephalographers in New England. On March 1, 1946, this group expanded to include 27 civilian and military electroencephalographers from several states, who convened at the Graduate Club of the Institute of Living in Hartford, Connecticut, to organize the EAEEG (Table 1) (Organization of Eastern Association of Electroencephalographers, 1964). The primary objectives were to promote research collaboration in neurophysiology and to explore the clinical applications of electroencephalography (1946). Lieutenant Commander Robert S. Schwab, U.S.N., of the United States Naval Hospital in Boston, was elected chairman of the organization (Fig. 1). It was decided that the society would hold bimonthly meetings in a seminar format. The subsequent meeting was scheduled for April 12, 1946, featuring a presentation of the society’s constitution and by-laws, as well as discussions of EEG use in the military. One of the immediate projects of the EAEEG was to approach the American Physiological Society, the American Neurological Society, the American Psychiatric Association and the Council on Physical Medicine of the American Medical Association on the matter of establishing a joint committee among the several organizations for the purpose of considering the desirability and means of establishing minimum standards for approved electroencephalographic laboratories. The chairman of the committee appointed to pursue this project was Dr. Hallowell Davis.

The first constitution and by-laws available of the EAEEG were from May 24, 1947. The purpose of the Association was to promote research and to further the understanding of knowledge in electroencephalography and closely related fields of interest; to exchange scientific and professional information; to facilitate personal contacts between the investigators in these fields; and to maintain high scientific standards of clinical electroencephalographic laboratories. In the initial by-laws, an ordinary meeting shall be held at least five times a year with one business meeting per year. The EAEEG was incorporated in the state of Connecticut as a corporation in 1947. The EAEEG formed months before the American Electroencephalographic Society’s first organizational meeting in 1947.

The Montreal Connection

Paralleling the rise of EEG in the US was the remarkable growth in EEG at the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) under the direction of neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield and neurologist and electrophysiologist Herbert Jasper. The collaboration of Wilder Penfield, a neurosurgeon who founded the MNI, and Herbert Jasper, an electroencephalographer, pushed the boundaries of electroencephalography to spectacular new levels.

Wilder Penfield was born in the United States and received his training in neurosurgery in Boston. After taking a surgical apprenticeship under Harvey Cushing, Penfield obtained a position at the Neurological Institute of New York where he performed his first operations on patients with epilepsy. While in New York, he met David Rockefeller, who wished to endow an institute where Penfield could further study the surgical treatment of epilepsy. Academic politics amongst the New York neurologists, however, prevented its establishment in New York, so, in 1928, Penfield accepted an invitation from Sir Vincent Meredith to move to Montreal. There, Penfield taught at McGill University and the Royal Victoria Hospital, becoming the city's first neurosurgeon. In 1934, Penfield founded and became the first director of the MNI at McGill University, established with the Rockefeller funding. The same year he became a British subject (as part of the British Empire, there was no distinct Canadian citizenship until 1947). While at MNI Penfield continued his work on patients undergoing surgical therapy for medically intractable epilepsy using cortical stimulation in awake patients to identify eloquent cortex that should not be resected (Penfield, 1938, Penfield and Flanigin, 1950a, Penfield and Flanigin, 1950b, Penfield and Kristiansen, 1948, Penfield and Perot, 1963, Penfield and Rasmussen, 1949, Penfield and Steelman, 1947, Penfield and Welch, 1951, Rasmussen and Penfield, 1947, Rasmussen and Penfield, 1948).

From 1932 to 1938 while Penfield was building the epilepsy surgery program at MNI Jasper was working at Brown University in Providence, Rhode Island developing an EEG laboratory. In 1935 with Leonard Carmichael, he published a paper in Science, describing how they had confirmed and amplified Hans Berger's findings in EEG, noting “spontaneous” fluctuations in amplitude of alpha rhythm (Jasper, 1948). Shortly thereafter Jasper and his team showed that EEG was valuable in detecting seizures (Jasper et al., 1950, Jasper et al., 1951, Jasper, 1949, Jasper and Carmichael, 1935, Jasper et al., 1938, Jasper and Kershman, 1941b).

Jasper’s first encounter with Penfield was at Penfield's Harvey Lecture on “The Cerebral Cortex and Consciousness” in 1936. Jasper invited Penfield to give a lecture at one of the weekly seminars in the Psychology Department at Brown where he learned about Penfield’s cortical mapping of eloquent cortex using electrical stimulation of the exposed human brain during surgery for treatment of focal epilepsy. While Penfield was skeptical about recording EEG from the exposed cortex, Jasper convinced him to operate on several of his patients. Jasper brought both the patients and the equipment necessary for electrocorticograms to the operating theater. Jasper began commuting between Providence and Montreal in 1937 and eventually accepted a position at the MNI in 1938. Thus, a long-standing fruitful collaboration with Penfield commenced, culminating in the renowned work Epilepsy and the Functional Anatomy of the Human Brain (Penfield and Jasper, 1954) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Penfield (seated) and Jasper with the manuscript of their book “Epilepsy and the Functional Anatomy of the Human Brain.” From Jasper (History of the Early Development of Electroencephalography and Clinical Neurophysiology at the Montreal Neurological Institute: The first 25 years 1939–1964)) (Jasper, 1991).

Jasper’s EEG laboratory was officially opened in 1939. Invited guests included Robert S. Schwab and Hallowell Davis, both founding members of the EAEEG. Japer noted in his history of the EEG laboratory at the MNI “Following the opening ceremonies, and a tour of the Institute, we all went to Domaine D'Esterel in the Laurentian mountains for a symposium and skiing, including a race among all participants who wished to enter (Jasper, 1991).” This was the first of the legendary annual ski meets of the EAEEG, which have continued ever since (Feindel, 1999, Jasper, 1991).

Format of the meetings

For many years two meetings were held, one a ski meeting in the Laurentians and the other in the US northeast. The ski meeting was usually held in February and the US meeting in December.

The ski meetings

The ski meetings conventionally commenced with a guided tour of the MNI followed by an afternoon epilepsy conference, held on the day prior to the EAEEG meeting. Owing to the legacies of Jasper and Penfield, the MNI was widely regarded as a venerable institution for electrophysiologists, epileptologists, and neuroscientists. This reverence was reinforced by the building’s architectural and artistic elements, which contributed to its reputation as a sanctuary for neurological research.

Upon entering the MNI, visitors are greeted by an elegant marble statue of a woman in the entrance foyer, inscribed with the phrase “La Nature se dévoilant devant la Science” (“Nature unveiling herself before science”)(Fig. 3). This sculpture is a replica of Ernest Barrias’s renowned 1899 work housed in the Musée d'Orsay in Paris. Within one of the institute’s conference rooms, titled “The Advance of Neurology”, a prominent mural by Mary Harris Filer is displayed. Completed in 1949, the mural spans an entire wall and vividly chronicles the progress of neurology over the decades. It depicts various figures—including scientists, physicians, and patients—symbolizing the collaborative spirit of medical research and patient care (Fig. 4). The figures are often portrayed in motion, embodying the energy and dedication characteristic of the field. Additionally, the mural incorporates symbolic representations of the brain, neurons, and other anatomical structures, underscoring the scientific foundations of neurological advancements. This mural has become an iconic feature of the MNI, inspiring visitors and professionals alike, and serves as a testament to the achievements and ongoing pursuit of knowledge within neuroscience.Fig. 3The main feature of the entrance foyer of the Montreal Neurological Institute is the elegant marble statue of a woman identified by the legend La Nature se dévoilant devant la Science (Nature unveiling herself before science). The annual symposium held in the Laurentians served as a platform for scientific exchange and social engagement among members of the electrophysiology community. The event typically incorporated morning sessions dedicated to scientific presentations, including symposiums on contemporary topics delivered by field experts, along with contributed talks aimed at encouraging participation from early- career investigators and trainees. Afternoons were reserved for recreational activities such as skiing or touring the area. Scientific discussions of presented papers were characterized by vigorous debate conducted in a collegial atmosphere, thereby fostering constructive dialogue. For many junior investigators, this meeting represented their inaugural opportunity to present research findings to an audience of knowledgeable peers. The overall quality of scientific content was consistently high. The format of the meeting facilitated interactions among trainees and young scientists interested in clinical and basic electrophysiology within a small, congenial setting, offering an alternative to large-scale conferences. Abstracts from the meetings were published in Electroencephalography and Clinical Neurophysiology until 1998, when the journal transitioned to Clinical Neurophysiology, which continues to publish association abstracts.

A distinctive aspect of these gatherings was the alpine ski race. In its initial iterations, the event was informal and primarily served as a social occasion rather than a competitive sport. Over time, the race evolved into a more challenging giant slalom course, featuring 15–20 gates. In 1950, the race received official recognition, with the winner being awarded the Savoy Trophy, named in honor of George A. Savoy. An American immigrant to Canada in 1921, Savoy managed the Canadian branch of a prominent manufacturer of ledgers and looseleaf registers (Rémillard et al., 1996). This company supplied Jasper’s laboratory with rolls of plain, unlined paper, and Savoy later developed fan folded, lined EEG paper, which was first employed at the MNI. Through his collaborations with Jasper and Penfield, Savoy developed an interest in epilepsy and became a generous supporter of programs dedicated to assisting individuals affected by the condition.

Jasper originally envisioned the race as a walk-up contest, where participants would hike up the mountain before skiing down—an idea that didn’t gain much traction among the older members. He also restricted trainees from participating, fearing injuries and missing work. The first race winner in 1950 was the legendary neurosurgeon from MNI, William Feindel. While all meeting attendees could enter, only members could have their names inscribed on the Savoy Trophy. A controversy arose in 1973 when the son of a member was awarded the trophy; recognizing the mistake, Dr. Jasper expressed his concern in a letter to Mayor Huntington, a neurologist at the University of Vermont and Secretary Treasurer of the EAEEG in 1974. Subsequently, only members’ names were listed on the trophy. Later, a Nordic race was organized, and the Gloor Trophy was engraved with the winner’s name. Around 2010, as younger members showed less interest in downhill or cross-country skiing, a challenge shield donated by Paul Hwang was introduced for the snowboard race.

Two travel awards funded by a donation from the Savoy Foundation of Quebec to the EAEEG were awarded annually. The Stephenson Award recognized the best paper by a junior member, while the Strauss Award honored a deserving EEG technologist. After George Savoy’s passing, Mac Burnham continued to support these awards through donations from various corporations. To mark the passing of past President, Kershman lecturer and long-standing secretary Mac Burnham, the EAEEG created a fellowship in his name at the 2025 meeting.

The 25th (1971) and 50th (1990) meetings were held at Mont Gabriel Lodge in the Laurentians. During the 25th anniversary gathering, an informal evening session featured a workshop titled “Control One’s Own Alpha – Do Your Own Thing,” which included a 3-channel Grass polygraph (two EEG channels and one DC channel). The Tuesday afternoon symposium on “The Past, Present, and Future of Electroencephalography” included talks by Alberta Dawson and Albert Grass on “The Evolution of Electrophysiological Instrumentation,” and by Hallowell Davis and Howard Simpson on “Oscilloscope and Undulator Revisited: Re-enactment by Original Participants of Early EEG Sessions.” R.S. Schwab, the association’s first president, delivered a talk titled “The Queen, the Analyzer, and Grey Walter: Dockside Trials, Tribulations, and Red Tape—December 1946.”

The USA meetings

The USA meetings took place at various locations across the Northeast, most commonly in New York City. In the early years, the meetings were often scheduled back-to-back with the Association for Research in Nervous and Mental Diseases (ARNMD) at the Roosevelt or Biltmore hotels. These gatherings lasted one to two days and featured symposiums as well as informal presentations. Like the ski meetings, the US meeting fostered a supportive environment, encouraging trainees and junior faculty to share their research in a relaxed and friendly setting. Canadian attendance was high as many members from Canada liked to do holiday shopping in New York.

Frank Morrell had a profound impact on the USA meetings in the 1980s and 1990s, frequently organizing high-quality symposiums centered on innovative topics. Just as EEG evolved over the years, expanding into specialized testing methods such as evoked potentials, magnetoencephalography, EEG/video monitoring, and intracranial EEG to improve diagnostic capabilities, the Morrell courses also grew in scope. Beyond exploring the technical aspects of these tests, the courses emphasized understanding the neurophysiological foundations underlying each modality. Morrell was unafraid to push the boundaries, often introducing ideas that challenged conventional thinking. This encouraged lively and vigorous discussions, energizing the meetings. His courses attracted a diverse audience, including experienced clinicians and neuroscientists, as well as trainees just starting their careers in the field.

Presidents of the EAEEG.

Robert S. Schwab (1903–1972) was the inaugural president of the EAEEG. An American neurologist, Schwab established an EEG laboratory at Massachusetts General Hospital in 1937, utilizing a two-channel EEG system (Stone and Hughes, 2013). He was one of the founders of the EEG Journal (Electroencephalography, and Clinical Neurophysiology) and was its first Managing Editor. His seminal textbook, Electroencephalography in Clinical Practice, published in 1951, remains a classic (Schwab, 1951). Schwab was a pioneer in applying EEG to epilepsy (Abbott and Schwab, 1948) and brain death (Hockaday et al., 1965). His earliest EEG recording, made in 1940, was preserved on microfilm, which he introduced as a storage method.

Arthur Earl Walker (1902–1995) was a Canadian-born American neurosurgeon, neuroscientist, and epileptologist. Known for describing the syndromes now bearing his name—Dandy-Walker syndrome and Walker-Warburg syndrome—his research focused on post-traumatic epilepsy (Kaufman and Walker, 1949, Marshall and Walker, 1961, Walker, 1956, Walker et al., 1948) and seizure propagation in an experimental monkey model (Udvarhelyi and Walker, 1965, Walker and Rodriguez, 1963, Walker and Udvarhelyi, 1965).

Gilbert H. Glaser (1920–2012) was an American neurologist and neuroscientist who was Chair, Department of Neurology at Yale University for 34 years. His research interests in epilepsy expanded treatment through surgical and non-surgical techniques (Glaser, 1980b). He was a leader in the neuropharmacology of antiseizure medications (Glaser, 1980a, Travers et al., 1971) and in delineating the semiology and EEG features of seizures (Glaser, 1967). He served as president of the American Academy of Neurology and the American Epilepsy Society.

Pierre Gloor (1923–2003) was a Swiss-born Canadian neurologist who trained at the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) with Penfield and Jasper in the early 1950s. He was considered a leading figure in clinical neurophysiology and epilepsy research following Jasper’s legacy. Gloor’s notable contributions include his work on the temporal lobe and limbic system (Gloor, 1986, Gloor, 1990, Gloor et al., 1982) culminating in his magnum opus, The Temporal Lobe and the Limbic System (Gloor, 1997). He received numerous honors, including the Epilepsy Research Award from the American Epilepsy Society, the Michael Prize (Germany), the Robert Bing Prize (Swiss Academy of Medical Sciences), the William Lennox Award (American Epilepsy Society), and the Penfield Award (Canadian League Against Epilepsy). Gloor also served as president of both the Canadian and American EEG societies, as well as the American Epilepsy Society.

William F. Caveness (1909–1981) was an American neurologist who earned his MD at McGill University. He served as chief of the Laboratory of Experimental Neurology at the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke, NIH. Caveness is renowned for his extensive work on the evaluation and outcomes of head trauma (Caveness et al., 1979, Rish and Caveness, 1973), as well as his studies on radiation-induced pathology in monkeys (Caveness, 1977, Caveness et al., 1975, Kemper et al., 1977).

Cesare T. Lombroso (1917–2013) was an Italian-born American pediatric neurologist who succeeded William Lennox as chief of the Seizure Unit at Boston’s Children’s Hospital, serving from 1960 to 1988. Lombroso’s research encompassed various facets of childhood epilepsy, with a primary focus on EEG features in pediatric populations (Lombroso, 1967, Lombroso, 1990), particularly neonatal seizures (Lombroso, 1982, Lombroso, 1985, Rose and Lombroso, 1970).

Dominick P. Purpura (1927–2019) was an American neuroscientist whose research centered on developmental neuroanatomy, electrophysiology (Noback and Purpura, 1961, Purpura, 1961, Purpura and Shofer, 1965) and epilepsy (Goldensohn and Purpura, 1963). He received the inaugural National Medical Research Award from the National Health Council in 1988 and was a member of both the National Academy of Sciences and the Institute of Medicine. Dr. Purpura served as dean of the Albert Einstein College of Medicine for 22 years and held leadership roles as president of the American Epilepsy Society, the Society for Neuroscience, and the International Brain Research Organization.

Theodore Rasmussen (1910–2002) was an American-born Canadian neurosurgeon, neurologist, and neuropathologist specializing in epilepsy treatment and pathology. Collaborating with Wilder Penfield and émigré neuroscientists such as Jerzy Olszewski and Fred Andermann, Rasmussen advanced surgical and neurological interventions for epilepsy (Rasmussen et al., 1958, Smith et al., 1991, Tinuper et al., 1988). In addition to his eponymous designation, “Rasmussen syndrome,” a chronic encephalitis associated with epilepsy, his contributions encompassed the refinement of neurosurgical techniques, comprehensive documentation of surgical outcomes in epilepsy treatment, and cortical mapping studies conducted in collaboration with Wilder Penfield. (Penfield and Rasmussen, 1949, Penfield and Rasmussen, 1950, Rasmussen and Penfield, 1947, Rasmussen and Penfield, 1948).

Ernst Niedermeyer (1920–2012) was a German-born American neurologist and electroencephalographer who spent the majority of his career at Johns Hopkins University. During his tenure, he collaborated with Earl Walker, a prominent epilepsy surgeon and former president of the EAEEG. Niedermeyer authored extensively on various aspects of EEG (Niedermeyer, 2003, Niedermeyer and Lopes da Silva, 1987a, Niedermeyer et al., 1987b, Niedermeyer and Naidu, 1990, Niedermeyer and Walker, 1971), and his seminal textbook, Electroencephalography: Basic Principles, Clinical Applications, and Related Fields (Niedermeyer and Lopes da Silva, 1983), remains a foundational reference in the field.

Willets McIntyre (Mac) Burnham (1939–2024) was an American-born Canadian neuroscientist and neuropsychopharmacologist renowned for his extensive research on the kindling model of epilepsy (Frantseva et al., 2000, Kalichman et al., 1981, Niznik et al., 1983). His work utilizing this model significantly advanced the understanding of epileptogenesis and provided a valuable platform for evaluating the efficacy and mechanisms of various therapeutic interventions, including antiseizure medications (Burnham et al., 1989), cannabinoids (Fallah et al., 2021) and the ketogenic diet (Likhodii et al., 2003). Burham’s contributions extended beyond his research; he was a central figure within the EAEEG, delivering the Kershman Lecture in 2002, and fulfilling the role of Secretary-Treasurer for over two decades. His dedication and leadership were instrumental in shaping the direction of epilepsy research and fostering international collaboration within the field. Burnham’s influence and mentorship left a legacy, inspiring generations of neuroscientists and clinicians dedicated to advancing epilepsy treatment and understanding. In his honor the EAEEG has established the Burnham basic science symposium.

Lectureships

The EAEEG has two renowned lectureships – the Kershman and Milner Lectureships − that are delivered at the annual meeting. Both lectureships are highly prestigious and highly esteemed.

Kershman lectureship

John Kershman was among Dr. Jasper’s earliest research fellows. Kershman, was born in England in 1908. He came to Canada in 1922 and received academic and clinical training at McGill. In 1937 he started a formal service at the MNI and was soon promoted to Assistant to the Director and Assistant Professor of Neurology (Feinsod, 2005). Kershman’s main expertise was in EEG (Jasper and Kershman, 1941a, Jasper and Kershman, 1941b, Kershman et al., 1949, Kershman et al., 1951). He was soon recognized as a leader in his field and was one of the founders of the Journal of Electroencephalography and Clinical Neurophysiology. He tragically passed away on June 27, 1951, in Atlantic City during his tenure as Vice President of the EAEEG.

In a tribute delivered by Dr. Jasper before the American and Southern EEG Societies on October 19, 1951, he reflected on Kershman’s life and contributions: “It was in the fall of 1938 that I first met Dr. Kershman, shortly after my arrival at the Montreal Neurological Institute to establish their first laboratories of electroencephalography. He soon became one of the inaugural Research Fellows in the new EEG Department and immediately began making significant contributions to its development. In those days, we had no technician; we not only recorded the EEGs ourselves but also applied the electrodes. Dr. Kershman tirelessly operated our homemade EEG machine day in and day out, studying the recordings and interpreting the results during the evenings, often working far into the night.” Jasper continued, “You are all familiar with the circumstances of Dr. Kershman's death in Atlantic City, June 27, 1951. He had just presented an important contribution on photo-Metrazol activation of the EEG in both normal and psychiatric patients before the American EEG Society when he was suddenly stricken with a coronary occlusion. His passing cast a pall over the remainder of the meeting and the subsequent American Neurological Association gathering.” He concluded by emphasizing Kershman's legacy: “His scientific work, his contributions to humanity, the remarkable achievements of his life from humble beginnings, and the memory held by friends worldwide will endure.”

Following his death, a motion was passed at the 1951 Annual Meeting to establish a permanent lectureship in Kershman's honor. In 1957, the John Kershman Memorial Lecture was inaugurated with the following stipulations: “The John Kershman Memorial Lecture shall be delivered annually at the EAEEG’s Annual Meeting. The lecturer shall be an individual of outstanding merit, whose interests and activities are outside the immediate field of electroencephalography. The honorarium shall not exceed two hundred dollars, with up to three hundred dollars allocated for expenses. The lecturer shall be selected by the President” (Fig. 5). Brenda Milner lectureship

The Brenda Milner lectureship was established in 2016 to honor a pioneering neuropsychologist whose groundbreaking research has profoundly influenced the fields of neuropsychology and cognitive neuroscience (Fig. 7). She is particularly renowned for her work on memory and the role of the hippocampus in memory formation. Milner's relationship with Penfield and Jasper was instrumental in shaping her career.Fig. 7Brenda Milner. Dr. Milner is regarded as a pioneering figure in neuropsychology, often referred to as one of the founding mothers of the field.

Brenda Milner. Dr. Milner is regarded as a pioneering figure in neuropsychology, often referred to as one of the founding mothers of the field.

One of Milner's most significant contributions came from her work with patient H.M. (Henry Molaison). In 1953 Scoville offered H.M. a chance to cure his epilepsy through a pioneered experimental procedure. Scoville thought that the hippocampi were responsible for the seizures and removed both of H.M.’s hippocampi. Following the surgery, H.M. was cured of his epilepsy but had profound anterograde amnesia, unable to form new explicit memories while retaining his short-term memory and prior knowledge, Scoville consulted with Penfield and Milner, who had previously reported on two other patients who had bilateral hippocampal lesions and memory deficits (Penfield and Milner, 1958). Milner conducted a series of detailed assessments of H.M.'s cognitive abilities, which led to the discovery of the critical role of the hippocampus in forming new memories. Her findings demonstrated that while the hippocampus is essential for the consolidation of long-term memories, other brain regions are involved in different types of memory processing. For example, H.M. could still learn new motor skills, even when he had no recollection of having learned them before. This was demonstrated through a mirror-tracing task, where H.M. improved his ability to trace a star while only seeing its reflection, a task that relies on procedural memory.

Milner's work with H.M. not only advanced the understanding of memory systems but also had broad implications for the study of amnesia, learning, and cognition. Her research highlighted the distinction between declarative memory (facts and events) and non-declarative memory (skills and habits), paving the way for further investigations into the neural mechanisms underlying these processes (Milner and Klein, 2016, Penfield and Milner, 1958, Scoville and Milner, 1957a, Scoville and Milner, 2000).

Recent developments and future outlook

As with many other organizations during the Covid epidemic and for several years thereafter virtual meetings were held. Starting in 2023 the annual in-person meetings resumed in Burlington, Vermont. The 2024 meeting was in Quebec City and the 2025 meeting in Boston. The Boston meeting featured the inaugural Burnham Basic Science Symposium titled “Systems Neuroscience Approaches Applied to Epilepsy and Neuropsychiatric Disease” a Clinical/Translational Science Symposium titled “Clinical Approaches in Epilepsy: Sleep and Cognitive Outcomes.” Keeping with the excellence of prior meetings and the historical foundation of the society, the EAEEG continues to feature speakers from both Canada and the U.S. and provides updates on contemporary topics in neuroscience, epilepsy and EEG. The association retains its focus on rigorous science in a collegial atmosphere and still fosters and maintains collaborations between clinicians and basic scientists at all career stages.

Conclusion

The EAEEG was founded in 1946 as a collaborative venture between Canada and the United States, and it has since flourished as a premier society dedicated to the advancement of electroencephalography and neuroscience. By integrating the expertise of both Canadian and American neurophysiologists and clinicians, the society has established a reputation for promoting high-quality research, education, and clinical practice. One of its core missions has been to nurture the next generation of neuroscientists, electroencephalographers and epileptologists, offering a platform where young researchers can engage directly with leading experts in a supportive and intimate setting. These regional meetings provide a unique opportunity for emerging scientists to interact with world-renowned figures in electrophysiology, fostering mentorship and collaboration in a welcoming environment.

In an era dominated by large international conferences attended by thousands, regional societies like the EAEEG continue to play an outsized and vital role in maintaining a close-knit community focused on specialized topics. Despite recent efforts by some administrative entities to create divisions between the US and Canada, it is crucial to recognize that scientific and clinical collaboration should transcend such barriers. The success of the EAEEG exemplifies the enduring strength of North American cooperation in neuroscience, demonstrating that regional societies can effectively promote excellence and unity in the field of electrophysiology and neurophysiology across borders.
