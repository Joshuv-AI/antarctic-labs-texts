Comparing contact dermatitis due to tissue adhesives in port-site closure
Authors: Julie L Holihan, Abigail G Wrather, Claire Lancaster, Kulvinder S Bajwa, Melissa M Felinski, Shinil K Shah, Jonah J Stulberg, Todd D Wilson, Erik B Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile tissue adhesives are widely used in the closure of surgical incisions, contact dermatitis remains a poorly characterized complication. Dermabond™ (Ethicon; DB) and LiquiBand® (Advanced Medical Solutions; LB) are two commonly used tissue adhesives; however, there are no direct data comparing their risk of contact dermatitis. In this randomized controlled trial, we compare the rate of contact dermatitis associated with DB versus LB in patients undergoing laparoscopic abdominal surgery.MethodsThis was a single-center, intra-patient randomized controlled trial which included patients undergoing elective laparoscopic or robotic abdominal surgery. For each patient, DB was applied to incisions on one side of the abdomen and LB was applied to the contralateral side. The side for each glue was randomized by date of the month. The primary outcome was the proportion of patients experiencing contact dermatitis within 6 weeks postoperative period. ResultsOne hundred eighty patients were enrolled, and 1 was lost to follow up. Contact dermatitis occurred in 21 of 180 patients (11.7%). DB alone caused reactions in 3 patients (1.7%), LB alone in 1 patient (0.6%), and both adhesives in 17 patients (9.5%). The matched-pairs odds ratio for LB versus DB was 0.33 (95% CI 0.01–4.15, p = 0.63). A sensitivity analysis for patients lost to follow up did not change the results. 7 (33%) patients required treatment for contact dermatitis, all of whom had bilateral reactions.ConclusionContact dermatitis due to tissue adhesive was seen in 11.7% of patients; however, there was no statistically significant difference between the rates seen with DB and LB. The risks and benefits of tissue adhesive should be carefully considered when choosing to use these products in the closure of surgical incisions, and surgeons need to have a clear management algorithm for dermatitis should it occur. This risk does not seem to be mitigated by manufacturer, so adhesive choice may reasonably be guided by factors such as cost, availability, or institutional preference.Keywords: Contact dermatitis, Tissue adhesive, Dermabond

The use of tissue adhesives has become increasingly common in the closure of surgical incisions. Initially used for small skin lacerations, it has become common practice to use adhesives to reinforce skin closure in the operating room. Advantages include the convenience of rapid application, eliminating the need for suture removal, and improved cosmetic outcomes. Despite the widespread use of tissue adhesives in clinical practice, a paucity of data exists evaluating their adverse effects. One of the more significant such complications is the development of contact dermatitis. [1] The reported rate of contact dermatitis associated with tissue adhesive use varies widely with ranges from 0 to 14% in the literature.[2–5] Such reactions can range broadly from minor skin irritation to permanent discoloration and scarring.

Dermabond™ (Ethicon) was the first cyanoacrylate-based tissue adhesive to receive approval from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for external use in 1998 and has been widely adopted as the market standard [6]. Over time, other brand competitors, such as LiquiBand® (Covidien), have entered the market and may offer a significant difference in product cost depending on hospital contracts.

Dermabond Advanced™ (DB) and LiquiBand® Exceed™ (LB) share the same primary monomer (2-octyl cyanoacrylate); however, they differ in their proprietary formulation chemistry. [6, 7] Both systems incorporate stabilizers to prevent premature polymerization in the applicator, but the balance of inhibitors and initiators is different, resulting in variations in curing kinetics. LB is formulated to promote more rapid polymerization and consistent film formation, whereas DB is designed for more controlled curing and formation of a slightly thicker, more durable polymer layer. In addition, each product includes proprietary plasticizers and rheology modifiers that influence viscosity, spreadability, and final film characteristics, leading to differences in flexibility, handling, and barrier properties despite similar underlying polymer chemistry. Many surgeons report anecdotal evidence leading them to favor one tissue adhesive over another; however, there are no studies on the rate of contact dermatitis using LB, and no trials have been conducted offering direct comparison between DB and LB.

Given the prevalence of tissue adhesive use, we sought to clarify whether specific products are associated with higher rates of contact dermatitis. To address this gap, we conducted a randomized controlled trial evaluating two commonly used tissue adhesives, DB and LB, in patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery. We hypothesized that use of LB to reinforce closure of port-site incisions would be associated with a higher rate of contact dermatitis compared to DB at 6 weeks postoperative period.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the IRB (HSC-MS-22-0143) and registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05492721).

This was a single-center, intra-patient, randomized controlled trial conducted by the Department of Surgery, Division of Minimally Invasive Surgery at UTHealth from June 2022 to June 2025.

Inclusion criteria

Adult patients (≥ 18 years) undergoing elective laparoscopic or robotic abdominal surgery were eligible.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with a known allergy to skin glue, inability to complete postoperative follow-up due to living out of state, or being unreachable by phone or email were excluded.

Study design

Eligible patients were approached for enrollment either in clinic or in preoperative holding. All participants provided informed consent.

Each participant served as their own control to reduce inter-patient variability. DB was applied to surgical incisions on one half of the abdomen, and LB was applied to the contralateral side. The side for each glue was randomly assigned by day of the month to control for differences in incision size and level of contamination. For example, in sleeve gastrectomy, the stomach is always removed through a larger port-site incision on the patient’s right side. On odd days, DB was applied to the patient’s left abdomen. On even days, DB was applied to the patient’s right abdomen. In the case of an odd number of incisions, the extra incision was included on the patient’s left. All incisions were closed per surgeon’s usual practice, with subcutaneous Monocryl suture prior to application of skin adhesive. Each adhesive was applied to a dry wound at room temperature in one continuous layer, per manufacturer recommendations.[7].

Of note, the study was initially designed using SwiftSetTM (Medtronic) instead of LB, but the hospital involved in the study changed its supply prior to the start of the study. Therefore, the study was carried out as designed comparing DB to LB.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this trial was the proportion of patients with contact dermatitis within 6 weeks postoperative period. Contact dermatitis was defined as pruritic, red, irritated skin around an incision. The secondary outcomes included wound dehiscence or surgical site infection. Wound dehiscence was defined as spontaneous separation of wound edges. Surgical site infection was defined by redness, pain, warmth, and/or purulent drainage from the wound. All outcomes were assessed by history and physical examination was taken by a clinician blinded to the treatment arms at all follow-up clinic visits in the first 6 weeks of postoperative period.

Sample size

The true rate of contact dermatitis is unknown but ranges from 2 to 14% in the literature. Using pooled values from the literature, we used expected dermatitis rates of 2.2% versus 6.8%. Using McNemar’s tests comparing two correlated proportions (alpha = 0.05, beta = 0.20, correlation coefficient = 0.5), a sample size of 186 was needed.

Statistical analysis

Intention to treat analysis was performed. Univariate analysis was performed comparing patients with and without any skin adhesive reaction using chi-square and Wilcoxon rank sum test as appropriate. The primary outcome was analyzed using an exact McNemar’s test. A post hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the result for patients who were lost follow-up by counting them as no dermatitis and as having a reaction to each glue.

The overall rate of contact dermatitis to either glue was 11.7% (Table 2). The majority of patients with contact dermatitis reacted to both products (9.5%). Only 1.7 and 0.6% reacted to DB or LB only, respectively. Due to the small numbers of discordant pairs [1, 3], an exact McNemar’s test was run to examine whether LB caused more contact dermatitis than DB. The matched-pairs odds ratio comparing LB to DB is 0.33 (95% CI 0.01–4.15, p = 0.63). There is no evidence supporting that LB resulted in a higher likelihood of contact dermatitis compared to DB.

On sensitivity analysis for patient lost to follow-up, assuming the patient had a reaction to DB but not to LB, the discordant pairs would be 4 (reacted to DB only) and 1 (reacted to LB only); the matched-pairs odds ratio comparing LB to DB would be 0.25 (95% CI 0.01–2.53, p = 0.37). Assuming the patient had a reaction to LB but not to DB, the discordant pairs would be 3 and 2; the matched-pairs odds ratio comparing LB to DB would be 0.67 (95% CI 0.06–5.82, p = 1). The conclusion would not change under these two assumptions. Of the 21 patients experiencing contact dermatitis at 6 weeks postoperatively, 7 required treatment for their reactions. Three patients were treated with over-the-counter antihistamines, while 4 patients required topical triamcinolone. All patients requiring treatment for contact dermatitis demonstrated bilateral reactions to both adhesives.

Discussion

In this randomized controlled trial, we aimed to compare the rates of contact dermatitis between DB and LB when used for laparoscopic port-site closure. Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference between the rates of contact dermatitis associated with the two adhesives at 6 weeks postoperatively (p = 0.63; 95% CI 0.01–4.15). The high rate of bilateral skin reactions within the same patient suggests that postoperative skin reactions are unlikely to be driven by inherent differences in adhesive formulation alone. Rather, patient-specific factors, such as prior sensitization or a history of atopic conditions, may play a more significant role.

Though it is impossible to predict with certainty preoperatively who will develop an allergic response, in this study, females were more likely to develop contact dermatitis than males. This is consistent with the literature, which has consistently shown that contact dermatitis is more prevalent in women than men. [8] For patients at higher risk of allergic reaction, such as those with pre-existing atopic conditions, surgeons may consider avoiding tissue adhesive altogether in favor of suture-only closure. In patients without significant perceived higher risk of contact dermatitis, adhesive choice may reasonably be guided by factors such as cost, availability, or institutional preference.

The overall incidence of contact dermatitis is consistent with the upper range of reported literature values, which vary from 0 to 14%. [1–4] The relatively high rate of contact dermatitis observed in this study highlights the importance of preoperative counseling and postoperative monitoring for skin reactions. Surgeons using tissue adhesives should be familiar with this potential complication and have an algorithm for treatment of dermatitis if it develops. The authors favor immediate removal of all tissue adhesive and topical triamcinolone 0.5%. In rare cases, oral steroids are needed when patients do not respond to topical treatment. Additionally, antihistamines can be used for symptom control.

Despite their risk, tissue adhesives still offer several practical advantages in surgical wound closure. They provide protection against surgical site infection by limiting microbial entry. They can increase the overall strength of wound closure compared to suture alone, helping reduce wound dehiscence. Cosmetically, adhesives distribute tension evenly along the epidermis and avoid cross-hatching, potentially resulting in an improved aesthetic outcome. In addition, they simplify postoperative care, eliminating the need for return visits for suture or staple removal, which can improve patient convenience and satisfaction. Alternatives to tissue adhesives include suture-only closure and wound closure strips; however, wound closure strips also carry a risk of allergic reaction [9].

Strengths of this study include the intra-patient randomized design, which minimizes confounding variables at the level of the patient and allows for direct comparison of adhesives. Additionally, outcome assessors were blinded to reduce the risk of detection bias, and the wide range of laparoscopic and robotic procedures included serves to enhance the generalizability of findings to routine minimally invasive surgery practice.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. This study was conducted at a single-center, which may limit the generalizability to other patient populations. Additionally, while contact dermatitis was diagnosed clinically by trained assessors, patch testing was not performed to further distinguish between irritant and allergic contact dermatitis. Although the sample size was powered based on expected rates of contact dermatitis from literature review, the low rate of discordant reactions resulted in wide confidence intervals around the odds ratio, limiting precision. Furthermore, there are many other tissue adhesives available that were not tested in this study. Finally, long-term outcomes such as scarring or persistent pigmentation were not evaluated beyond 6 weeks postoperative period.

Further studies are needed to identify if any other tissue adhesives are less prone to dermatitis. In addition, future studies can focus on patient-specific risk factors that could predispose patients to adhesive-associated reactions and explore strategies to mitigate reactions in individuals deemed to be at a higher risk for contact dermatitis.

Conclusions

This randomized controlled trial demonstrates a high rate of contact dermatitis to tissue adhesive (11.7%), with no significant difference between DB and LB. Surgeons should be familiar with this potential outcome if they choose to use tissue adhesive and have a clear management algorithm should it occur. When it is used, preoperative counseling and postoperative surveillance are necessary. Given no significant difference in contact dermatitis risk, product choice can be guided by cost, availability, or institutional preference.
