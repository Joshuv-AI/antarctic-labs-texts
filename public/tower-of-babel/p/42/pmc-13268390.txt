Planetary Health Diet, Socioeconomic Inequalities, and CKD and Mortality Risk
Authors: Shaohui Liu, Qiyini Ma, Li'e Zhang, Quanhong Chen, Yubo Zhang, Jindi Li, Jinling Guo, Yunan Xu, Hao Chen, Yunfeng Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual Abstract

Introduction

Global mortality due to CKD increased from 14.9 to 18.5 per 100,000 population from 1990 to 2021.1 The Global Burden of Disease Consortium predicted that CKD will be the fifth greatest cause of death worldwide by 2040.2 CKD onset and progression are intimately linked to dietary pattern.3,4 For instance, adherence to Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension mode is associated with lower CKD incidence among Iranian population.5 Having Mediterranean diet is correlated with better-preserved kidney function.6 However, research evidence regarding the health benefits of adhering to planetary health diet (PHD) against CKD remains sparse.

PHD is a dietary mode launched by the EAT-Lancet Commission in 2019 to promote a healthy dietary pattern with environmental benefits.7 High scores of PHD, or adherence to a PHD pattern, entails a marked reduction of consumption in food items such as meat and milk, primarily in the middle—and high—income regions, in favor of vegetables, fruits, nuts, and legumes.7 Researches suggest that vegetables, fruits, and legumes are characterized by their antioxidants, anti-inflammation, and gut microbiota-regulating properties,8,9 which may play a role in reducing chronic disease. Adherence to PHD is linked to lower risks of respiratory diseases,10,11 cardiovascular diseases,12,13 cancer,14 and all-cause and cause-specific mortality.14–16 However, evidence linking PHD adherence to kidney health remains limited. Given that oxidative stress, inflammation, and gut dysbiosis are the key mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of CKD,17,18 adherence to PHD may confer protective effects against its onset and prognosis. Furthermore, a global shift to PHD was estimated to reduce 50% greenhouse gas (GHG) emission and increase 39% carbon dioxide removal by 2050 versus a no-shift scenario.19 Therefore, it is crucial to characterize both the renoprotective effects and environmental sustainability of adopting a PHD in a large population.

Socioeconomic status (SES) is a key determinant of dietary habits because individuals of lower SES less likely to consume healthy foods (such as fruits, vegetables, and fibers) and more likely to make unhealthy dietary choices.20,21 While a healthy dietary pattern like PHD may yield health benefits, socioeconomic inequities may hinder its adoption and maintenance by disadvantaged groups, potentially undermining the associated health gains. To quantify these socioeconomic disparities, we used the index of multiple deprivation (IMD), a composite metric aggregating weighted data from distinct domains (e.g., income, employment, education, health, housing access, living environment, and crime). We hypothesize that socioeconomic deprivation, as measured by IMD scores, modulates the associations between PHD adherence and kidney health indicators.

In this study, by using data from UK Biobank and the National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys (NHANES), we aimed to assess the associations between PHD scores and kidney function, CKD, mortality, and environmental impacts, as well as the moderating relationships of SES on these associations.

Methods

Study Population

The UK Biobank is a large-scale biomedical database containing deidentified genetic, lifestyle, and health information from approximately 500,000 UK participants aged 40–69 years. Participants were recruited between 2006 and 2010 and completed dietary intake assessments using a 24-hour dietary recall questionnaire administered on five occasions. In this study, we included participants with two to five dietary assessments (n=126,769), using the mean food consumption for the final analysis. Participants with a baseline diagnosis of CKD International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10; code of N18, n=1188), were excluded, leaving 125,581 participants for analyses of both cross-sectional outcomes (eGFR, cystatin C, GHG emissions, land use, and water use) and longitudinal outcomes (incident CKD and all-cause mortality; Supplemental Figure 1). The UK Biobank received ethical approval from the Northwest Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (21/NW/0157). All participants had provided their informed consent.

NHANES is an ongoing program conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics to evaluate the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. In this study, 80,312 participants from NHANES 2003–2018 were included. Individuals were excluded if they were younger than 18 years (n=32,549), lacked 24-hour dietary assessment (n=10,409), missed outcome data for eGFR (n=1900), urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR; n=386), or death status (n=47). Finally, 35,021 NHANES participants were included in the analyses (Supplemental Figure 1). The protocols of NHANES have been approved by National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board (Protocol #2005-06; Protocol #2011-17; Protocol #2018-01).

Calculation of PHD Scores

The calculation of PHD scores was based on the food consumption records in 24-hour dietary recall questionnaire. Briefly, scoring criteria were developed for 14 food groups, as detailed in Supplemental Tables 1 and 2.16 All 14 food groups were then categorized into adequacy components (e.g., vegetables, fruits, and fish), optimum components (e.g., dairy foods, chicken and poultry, and eggs), and moderation components (total grains, red meat, saturated oils, and added sugar) on the basis of their assumed health effects.16,22 Each dietary component was scored on a scale from 0 to 10. However, the score does not necessarily increase with the increasing amount of the consumed component. Please refer to the Supplemental Methods for detailed scoring system. The total PHD score, calculated as the sum of all 14 dietary components, ranged from 0 to 140.16 Higher scores indicated greater adherence to PHD.

Assessment of Environmental Impacts

GHG emissions, land use, and water use were used for indicating environmental impacts.23 These parameters incorporate life cycle of food products from the earliest stages of production to the retail distribution center. The detailed methodology to assess environmental effects of PHD were explained in the Supplemental Methods. Briefly, dietary data were grouped into 65 distinct food group aggregates in UK Biobank and 96 in NHANES (Supplemental Tables 3 and 4). Among them, nine food group aggregates in each dataset can be directly obtained from Poore's study.23 For the remaining food group aggregates, we constructed the environmental effects of each food product using the following four techniques, which we describe as similar items, average value, adjusting for density, and estimating for recipes. The estimates for the food group aggregates were presented in the Supplemental Methods. We did not account for the cooking process (either at the industrial stage or at home) for any of the food product.

Assessment of Socioeconomic Inequities

IMD was used to assess the SES of UK Biobank participants at baseline. While each constituent nation of the United Kingdom applies slightly different IMD metrics, common domains include income deprivation, employment deprivation, education, skills and training deprivation, health deprivation and disability, crime, barriers to housing and services, and living environment deprivation. Higher IMD scores indicating greater socioeconomic deprivation. The detailed description of IMD and its domains were attached in the Supplemental Methods. Owing to the limitations of NHANES, annual family income and education were selected as socioeconomic factors for further analyses.

Outcome Definitions

In UK Biobank, to evaluate the direct relationships between PHD and CKD, we only focus on primary CKD. Primary incident CKD were identified using ICD-10 code N18, with onset dates derived from primary care, hospital admissions, and self-reported conditions. Mortality data were obtained through linkage of UK Biobank with national death registries from National Health Service Digital (for England and Wales), National Records of Scotland, and the Northern Ireland Statistics and Research Agency. This linkage provided the date and the underlying cause of death, coded using ICD-10. All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause during the follow-up period. For both CKD and mortality, the latest date of complete information was censored on December 31, 2021. Baseline eGFR was calculated from serum creatinine using the 2021 CKD Epidemiology Collaboration equation.24 Baseline blood cystatin C concentrations were measured through an immunoturbidimetric method on the Siemens Advia 1800 platform as a renal function indicator. In NHANES, CKD was defined as eGFR <60 ml/min per 1.73 m2 or a UACR ≥30 mg/g. Mortality data up to December 31, 2019, were obtained from the National Death Index database.

Covariates

Information on age, sex, body mass index, ethnicity, physical activity, smoking status, drinking status, IMD, marital status, annual family income, education, total energy intake, personal history of hypertension and diabetes was collected using online questionnaires at baseline. Body mass index was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. Moderate and vigorous physical activity intensity recommendation was defined as undertaking 150 minutes of moderate intensity activity (equivalent to a brisk walk) and 75 minutes of vigorous intensity activity (such as running) every week for adults, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

Participant characteristics were demonstrated as mean (SD) or median (interquartile ranges) for continuous variables and count (percentage) for categorical variables by quartiles of PHD score. In UK Biobank, follow-up time was calculated from the date of recruitment to the earliest of CKD diagnosis, death, loss to follow-up, or December 31, 2021. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HR) for CKD and all-cause mortality. In NHANES, person-months of follow-up were calculated from the examination date at Mobile Examination Center to December 31, 2019. The associations between PHD scores and CKD prevalence were evaluated using logistic regression. P trend across quartiles were tested by modeling the median value of each quartile as a continuous variable. Multiple linear regression was applied to evaluate the associations between PHD score and eGFR, cystatin C, UACR, and environment impact indicators. The assumption of linearity for the associations between PHD score and each outcome were tested using restricted cubic splines with four knots placed at the fifth, 27.5th, 72.5th, and 95th percentiles in both UK Biobank and NHANES. Multiplicative interaction terms were introduced to assess the moderating associations of socioeconomic factors on the associations between PHD scores and health outcomes. In sensitivity analyses, we fitted linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts only (lme4 package, R) to examine eGFR changes over time. Random slopes were omitted because they cannot be reliably estimated with only two measurements per participant. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.1, and a two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant Characteristics

The distribution of the PHD score differed markedly between the two cohorts: UK Biobank participants had a median of 62.34 (53.74, 70.79), whereas NHANES had a median of only 40.45 (31.38, 50.21). Participants with higher adherence to PHD were generally older, more likely to be female, nonsmokers, physically active, and nonobese. In UK Biobank, those in the highest quartile (Q4) of PHD score were less likely than those in the lowest quartile (Q1) to have a history of hypertension (22% versus 24%) or diabetes (3% versus 4%) at baseline. By contrast, NHANES participants with higher PHD scores were more often diagnosed with hypertension (37% versus 30%) and diabetes (14% versus 8%), which may be attribute to the discrepancy of participant age (52.8±18.2 versus 42.8±18 years; Table 1).

Associations between PHD Score and Kidney Function Parameters

We first assessed whether PHD scores were associated with early functional parameters of kidney. In UK Biobank, higher adherence to PHD was associated with higher eGFR levels (βcontinuous [95% confidence interval [CI]], 0.05 [0.04 to 0.05]) and lower cystatin C levels (−0.0006 [−0.0007 to −0.0006]) after adjusting for potential confounders. PHD score showed a linear association with eGFR (P for nonlinearity = 0.77), but a nonlinear association with cystatin C (P for nonlinearity < 0.001; Supplemental Figure 2). Similar associations between PHD score and eGFR were observed in NHANES participants (0.06 [0.05–0,08]; Table 2), with evidence of a nonlinear dose response (P for nonlinearity = 0.01; Supplemental Figure 3). Among the food groups contributing to PHD score, higher scores of vegetables, fruits, red meat, legumes, peanuts or tree nuts, saturated oils, and added sugar were significantly associated with higher eGFR levels in both cohorts. Higher starchy vegetables, vegetables, fruits, chicken and other poultry, fish, peanuts or tree nuts, and added sugar scores were linked to lower cystatin C levels. No significant association was observed between PHD score and UACR (Supplemental Figures 4 and 5).

Associations between PHD Score and CKD

We further assessed whether higher PHD scores were linked with lower CKD incidence or prevalence. In UK Biobank, a total of 4651 incident CKD cases (2.74 per 1000 person-years) were documented over a median follow-up of 13.56 (12.96–14.34) years. After adjusting for potential confounders, higher PHD score was significantly and linearly associated with lower CKD incidence (HR] quartile 4(Q4) versus quartile 1 (Q1) [95% CI], 0.75 [0.69 to 0.81], P for nonlinearity = 0.60). Similarly, greater adherence to PHD was associated with lower CKD prevalence (OR Q4 versus Q1 [95% CI], 0.80 [0.73 to 0.88]) in NHANES, with evidence of a nonlinear dose response (P for nonlinearity = 0.01; Table 3 and Supplemental Figures 2 and 3). UK Biobank participants with higher scores of vegetables, fruits, red meat, fish, peanuts or tree nuts, and added sugar were significantly associated with lower CKD incidence (Supplemental Figure 2). In NHANES, higher component scores of vegetables, red meat, legumes, peanuts or tree nuts, and saturated oils were related to lower CKD prevalence (Supplemental Figures 4 and 5).

Associations between PHD Score and All-Cause Mortality

We also examined whether adhering to PHD was associated with lower mortality. Given the very low number of CKD-related deaths in UK Biobank (n=2) and the absence of detailed cause-of-death information for CKD in NHANES, analyses were restricted to all-cause mortality. During follow-up, 6844 deaths (4.04 per 1000 person-years) occurred in UK Biobank and 4008 (13.40 per person-years) in NHANES. After adjusting for potential confounders, higher PHD scores were significantly associated with lower all-cause mortality in both cohorts (UK Biobank, HR Q4 versus Q1 [95% CI], 0.83 [0.78 to 0.89]); NHANES, HR Q4 versus Q1 [95% CI], 0.85 [0.77 to 0.93]; Table 3). The associations between PHD score and all-cause mortality is linear in UK Biobank (P for nonlinearity = 0.69), but nonlinear in NHANES (P for nonlinearity = 0.03; Supplemental Figures 2 and 3). In UK Biobank, higher component scores for vegetables, fruits, red meat, fish, peanuts or tree nuts, added sugar, and unsaturated oils were associated with lower mortality (Supplemental Figure 2). In NHANES, higher scores for vegetables, fruits, fish, legumes, and peanuts or tree nuts showed similar associations (Supplemental Figures 4 and 5).

PHD Score and Environment Effects

We further assessed the environment effects of adhering to PHD. Restricted cubic spline and multiple linear regression analyses revealed nonlinear and negative associations between PHD score and both GHG emissions (β [95% CI], −0.02 [−0.02 to −0.02]; −0.01 [−0.01 to −0.01], P for nonlinearity < 0.001) and land use (−0.10 [−0.11 to −0.10]; −0.05 [−0.06 to 0.05], P for nonlinearity < 0.001) in both UK Biobank and NHANES, respectively. The positive association between PHD score and water use in UK Biobank was nonlinear (β [95% CI], 0.06 [0.05 to 0.06], P for nonlinearity < 0.001), but linear in NHANES (β [95% CI], 0.12 [0.11 to 0.12], P for nonlinearity = 0.27; Table 4 and Supplemental Figures 2 and 3). Red meat was the largest contributor to both GHG emissions and land use, followed by dairy food. For water use, the main contributors were dairy products, red meat, and unsaturated fats (Supplemental Figure 6). The inverse associations between PHD score and both GHG emissions and land use were largely attributable to lower red meat consumption (Supplemental Figure 7).

Associations between IMD, PHD Score, and CKD and All-Cause Mortality

UK Biobank participants were categorized into low, medium, and high socioeconomic deprivation groups on the basis of IMD scores. Compared with low-IMD group, participants in high-IMD group were less likely to follow a PHD pattern (median [interquartile range], tertile (T) 3 versus T1: 61.82 [52.92–70.53], 62.68 [54.30–71.03], P < 0.001). IMD score domains including income deprivation, employment deprivation, health deprivation and disability, education, skills and training deprivation, and crime showed the same negative relationships with PHD adherence (Supplemental Table 6).

We further assessed whether IMD score and its domains moderated the associations between PHD scores and CKD or all-cause mortality. No interactions between IMD scores and PHD scores were observed for eGFR or CKD incidence (all P for interaction > 0.05; Supplemental Figures 8 and 9). Significant interactions emerged between specific IMD domains—such as education, skills and training deprivation; health deprivation and disability; and employment deprivation—and PHD scores on cystatin C levels. The inverse association between PHD adherence and cystatin C levels was more pronounced among participants experiencing higher deprivation in these domains (all P for interaction < 0.05; Figure 1). Similarly, the favorable associations of PHD adherence on all-cause mortality were stronger among participants with higher deprivation scores for income, employment, and living environment (all P for interaction < 0.05; Figure 2). In NHANES, a comparable moderating association of annual family income was observed on the association between PHD score and all-cause mortality (Supplemental Figure 10).

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed to check the robustness of our findings. After accounting for the competing risk of death, the association between higher PHD adherence and lower CKD incidence remained essentially identical to the primary Cox model (HR Q4 versus Q1 [95% CI], 0.75 [0.69 to 0.81] versus 0.75 [0.69 to 0.81]; Supplemental Table 7). We further conducted a linear mixed-effects analysis to model the trajectory of eGFR over time. In this augmented cohort of 8562 participants, higher PHD scores were significantly associated with better preserved eGFR (β [95% CI], 0.06 [0.04 to 0.08]), which strongly supports our primary results (β [95% CI], 0.05 [0.04 to 0.05]).

Discussion

In this study, we found that higher PHD score was associated with higher eGFR level, lower cystatin C level, and lower CKD prevalence and incidence and all-cause mortality. Moreover, adherence to PHD was linked to lower GHG emission and land use, primarily driven by lower consumption of red meats and diary food. Individuals experiencing greater socioeconomic deprivation were less likely to adhere to PHD, and socioeconomic inequalities significantly moderated the associations of PHD score with cystatin C and all-cause mortality.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the associations between PHD and CKD. The favorable association of the PHD against CKD aligns with its previously reported benefits in reducing the risk of other noncommunicable diseases.16,25,26 A global systematic review and meta-analysis of 28 publications involving more than 2.21 million participants demonstrated inverse associations between PHD adherence and all-cause mortality.25 A prospective cohort study of middle-aged and older adults conducted in China come to the same conclusion.26 In line with these findings, we documented inverse associations between PHD score and all-cause mortality among UK Biobank and NHANES participants. Although the associations of PHD scores with CKD and mortality were modest, such effect estimates are typical in nutritional epidemiology, where dietary patterns represent complex exposures with inherently small-to-moderate impacts. However, the public health implications should not be underestimated. Given the high prevalence of CKD and the universal nature of diet, even a modest risk reduction at individual level can translate into a substantial effect on disease burden at population level. Our novel findings on the nephrological benefits of PHD further underscore the comprehensive health advantages of adopting this emerging dietary framework.

The mechanisms underlying the health benefits of PHD on CKD and mortality remain unclear; however, several hypotheses have been proposed. One key explanation involves the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of PHD. The PHD emphasizes plant-based foods while substantially limiting the intake of animal-derived products. Vegetables, fruits, and legumes are well-known for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties.8,27 A systematic literature review supported a positive association between meat-based diets and low-grade chronic inflammation, whereas diets rich in vegetables and fruits show an inverse relationship.28 Oxidative stress contributed to kidney disease development and progress by directly damaging kidney cells, impairing function, and triggering inflammatory and fibrotic responses.17 Our results indicated that higher PHD scores—reflecting optimal intake of peanuts and tree nuts, added sugar, fruits and vegetables—were the key dietary components driving the inverse associations between PHD score and CKD. Meanwhile, higher PHD scores of food groups including unsaturated fat, peanuts and tree nuts, vegetables, and fruits contributed the most to the inverse associations between PHD score and all-cause mortality. In addition, consuming a diverse range of fiber-rich and polyphenol-rich foods, particularly fresh fruits and vegetables, promotes gut microbial diversity and improved health status overall.9 Evidence suggests that gut microbial diversity may influence kidney health through a gut-kidney axis.18,29,30 Therefore, it is rational to believe that PHD may protect individuals from CKD through regulating gut microbial diversity.

In addition to the health benefits of adherence to PHD, our findings indicated that higher PHD scores were linked to lower GHG emission and land use, which was consistent with previous studies conducted in another European population.31,32 Results from a Singapore Chinese health study found that higher adherence to PHD was associated with lower GHG emission, but higher land use.16 We observed that reduced consumption of red meats and diary food contributed most significantly to the environmental benefits associated with PHD adherence. These findings align with existing data indicating that livestock accounts for approximately 70% of agricultural emission and 15% of total national GHG emission.33 A recent review concluded that plant-based diets generally linked to lower GHG emissions and land use, but their effect on water use varies depending on the types of plant-based foods consumed.34 This nuance may explain the positive association between PHD score and water use observed in this study.

However, socioeconomic disparities may limit some individuals' ability to achieve a high PHD score. Our findings demonstrated that higher socioeconomic deprivation groups were less likely to follow a PHD pattern. Notably, the association between PHD adherence and lower cystatin C levels was more pronounced among those experiencing greater deprivation in education, skills and training, health, and employment. Moreover, the favorable associations of PHD on all-cause mortality were stronger among those experiencing higher levels of income, employment, and living environment deprivation. Consistent with our findings, previous research indicated that individuals with higher socioeconomic deprivation were more likely to adhere to an unhealthy diet (more red and processed meat, less vegetables and fruits).35,36 Low education, low income, and unemployment were associated with increased risk of CKD and poor outcomes in people with CKD.37,38 Cardio-kidney-metabolic syndrome-related mortality was negatively associated with median annual household income, high school completion rate, and primary health care access rate among US participants.39 Measures aimed at promoting PHD may yield more health benefits among people with lower SES, while improving socioeconomic inequalities may also be beneficial to promote PHD adherence.

Notably, absolute PHD scores differed significantly between UK Biobank and NHANES, likely because of distinct dietary assessment methods and national food environments, so direct numerical comparison is invalid. However, our analyses focused on the relative ranking of adherence within each cohort. The observation that a higher PHD adherence was consistently associated with outcomes in both distinct populations strengthens the validity of our findings, suggesting the beneficial trend of PHD is robust and generalizable across different settings.

While prior work has linked PHD to several noncommunicable diseases, ours is the first study to examine its association with CKD. Specifically, this study simultaneously evaluated dietary patterns, kidney health, and environmental sustainability across two large-scale cohorts. Furthermore, we have uniquely contextualized these findings by examining the effect modification by SES. This move beyond a simple association to a more nuanced understanding of who benefits and how is a significant contribution. The finding that the favorable associations of PHD are not uniform across SES groups is a clinically significant insight. It identifies a high-risk population (low-SES group) were less likely to adhere to PHD, thus may not reap the same benefits from general dietary advice, highlighting a critical area for future clinical and public health focus.

Key strengths of this study include its long-term follow-up, repeated dietary assessments at baseline, and a large sample size incorporating data from both UK Biobank and NHANES. The consistency of findings across these distinct cohorts enhances the robustness and generalizability of our results to diverse populations. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the 24-hour dietary questionnaires were administered multiple times, they were only collected at baseline, limiting assessment of dietary changes during follow-up. This could induce nondifferential misclassification and biased estimates. Second, the dietary data, collected approximately 15 years ago, may not fully represent current trends. Future studies with more recent data are needed to confirm the applicability of our findings. Third, SES in NHANES was limited to family income and education. The findings may not be directly comparable with or verifiable against those from UK Biobank. Fourth, both cohort in this study reflect Western dietary patterns, limiting the generalizability of these findings to non-Western populations. Finally, as with all observed studies, residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out.

In conclusion, greater PHD adherence is associated with better kidney function, reduced CKD prevalence and incidence and all-cause mortality, as well as lower GHG emission and land use. Importantly, PHD adherence was associated with lower cystatin C and lower all-cause mortality, with stronger associations observed among participants with greater deprivation in education, skills and training, health, employment, income, and living environment. Measures aiming at promoting a PHD style diet may yield more health benefits particularly considering people with lower SES.
