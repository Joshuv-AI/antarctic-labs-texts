Bone stability after regenerative peri‐implantitis treatment: A 10‐year follow‐up case study
Authors: Stefano Parma‐Benfenati, Marisa Roncati, Lucrezia Parma‐Benfenati, Paolo Nava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeri‐implantitis poses a significant challenge to implant longevity, leading to progressive bone loss and potential implant failure. While reconstructive surgery has been proven effective in regenerating peri‐implant tissues, the long‐term stability of the regenerated tissues and the impact of supportive peri‐implant care (SPIC) on bone levels remains underexplored. This case study presents a 10‐year follow‐up of a patient treated with reconstructive surgery for peri‐implantitis and enrolled in a structured maintenance program.MethodsA 68‐year‐old male with a failing implant in the mandibular right first molar was treated using a reconstructive approach involving surface decontamination, particulate bone grafts, and an acellular dermal matrix as a barrier. Three re‐entry procedures were performed over a 10‐year period: at 9 months for the second stage of the two‐step GBR procedure and at 8 and 10 years for implant placement to replace fractured teeth. These interventions allowed for direct clinical and radiographic assessment of bone‐level changes.ResultsAt 9 months, bone regeneration was observed with minor residual dehiscences. After 8 years, bone remodeling and crestal bone gain on the lingual aspect were noted in the absence of further surgical interventions. At 10 years, radiographic evaluations confirmed minimal changes in marginal bone levels.ConclusionsThe presented two‐stage regenerative approach successfully restored peri‐implant health and function over time while minimizing the patient's financial burden. The three re‐entry procedures provided direct clinical evidence of bone regeneration and demonstrated the positive impact of effective maintenance therapy on marginal bone levels and their long‐term stability.Key points The described regenerative treatment for peri‐implantitis may ensure long‐term satisfactory outcomes.Supportive peri‐implant care (SPIC) is effective in preventing disease recurrence and may improve crestal bone levels over time. Plain language summaryThis study presents the case of a 68‐year‐old patient with a failing dental implant due to peri‐implantitis. The implant was treated by thoroughly cleaning its surface, placing bone grafts, and using a protective membrane to regenerate bone around the fixture and obtain disease resolution. The patient returned for three follow‐up surgeries over 10 years, providing a unique opportunity to directly examine bone stability around the implant. Direct observations revealed that the bone graft healed well within the first 9 months, and the regenerated peri‐implant bone remained stable with minimal changes for a decade. Regular check‐ups and cleanings also helped prevent disease recurrence and further bone loss. By saving the existing implant, the patient avoided the extra expenses of removing and replacing it. Overall, these results suggest that a regenerative surgical approach, combined with structured maintenance care, can help obtain disease resolution and maintain stable long‐term outcomes. This can improve comfort and function while reducing costs for patients.Keywords: peri‐implantitis, dental implant, osseointegration, regenerative therapy, bone regeneration, supportive periodontal therapy, treatment outcome, follow‐up studies, case study

INTRODUCTION

Dental implants have become an integral component of contemporary restorative dentistry, offering predictable solutions for rehabilitating edentulous spaces. 1 Nevertheless, the increasing prevalence of peri‐implant diseases, such as peri‐implantitis, poses a significant challenge to their long‐term success. 2 This inflammatory condition, associated with progressive bone loss and biofilm accumulation, if left unmanaged, can lead to implant failure, compromising patients’ function and esthetics. 3

Among the various treatment modalities, reconstructive surgical therapy has gained prominence as a targeted approach in moderate and severe cases, not only to resolve inflammation but also to regenerate the peri‐implant tissues lost during disease progression. 4 , 5 This modality typically involves the use of bone grafting materials, barrier membranes and biologically active agents aimed at re‐establishing the structural integrity of the peri‐implant environment. 6 , 7

Despite its clinical potential, reconstructive therapy is not without challenges. Achieving complete decontamination of the implant surface remains a critical yet unpredictable task, especially given the complexity of implant micro and macro‐structure. Furthermore, several factors influence surgical outcomes, including defect morphology, soft tissue management, and patient‐specific variables such as smoking habits and adherence to supportive care and oral hygiene measures. 5 , 7

Supportive peri‐implant care (SPIC) plays a pivotal role in maintaining the stability of peri‐implant tissues following surgical treatment for peri‐implantitis. The importance of a structured, individualized maintenance program is essential for preventing disease recurrence or progression and ensuring the long‐term success of therapeutic interventions. 3 , 8 , 9

Although literature provides insights into the outcomes of regenerative therapy and the subsequent supportive care, accurately assessing long‐term results, particularly at the bone level, remains challenging. Periapical radiographs provide only two‐dimensional views, while the radiation exposure associated with CBCT imaging makes it impractical for most long‐term studies to provide comprehensive assessments of bone level changes around diseased implants treated with reconstructive approaches and maintained over time. Consequently, there is a paucity of long‐term cases that offer precise, circumferential evaluations of bone‐level changes following regenerative treatment.

The aim of this case study is to present long‐term outcomes of the reconstructive surgical therapy for the treatment of peri‐implantitis through three re‐entry procedures. This approach provides a comprehensive evaluation of bone levels around the treated implant and shows the positive impact of supportive non‐surgical therapy on marginal bone levels.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This case study conforms to the CARE guidelines. 10

A 68‐year‐old, otherwise healthy, male patient was referred to a specialist periodontal practice in April 2013 with a failing implant replacing the mandibular right first molar (Figure 1). He complained of persistent tenderness and bleeding gums on the lingual aspect. The implant had been placed 2 years earlier by a different provider. Upon evaluation, the final implant‐supported crown was found to be provisionally cemented, likely due to peri‐implant mucositis during the early healing stages.

Periapical radiograph taken at the first visit (C) and periapical radiograph the day of the surgery (D).

The case was diagnosed as stage III, grade B generalized periodontitis. The diagnosis of peri‐implantitis was made based on clinical and radiographic examination. 11 Periapical radiographs, taken in 2013 using the paralleling cone technique, revealed marked radiolucency involving approximately two‐thirds of the implant surface. Periodontal probing on the buccal side ranged from 7 to 11 mm, while probing depths (PDs) on the lingual side exceeded 10 mm, with bleeding noted on both the mesial and distal aspects and absence of mobility. Keratinized mucosa width was sufficient on both the buccal and lingual surfaces at the implant site.

Following initial non‐surgical periodontal and peri‐implant instrumentation and three additional sessions of non‐surgical therapy performed at 3‐month intervals, the patient continued to report discomfort at the implant site during brushing and chewing. A clinical and radiographic reassessment conducted 9 months from baseline revealed increased PDs and peri‐implant bone loss, indicating the need for surgical intervention (Figure 1).

A two‐stage guided bone regeneration (GBR) technique was selected, allowing for the temporary removal of the provisionally cemented crown. The patient was thoroughly informed that, due to the chosen submerged approach, masticatory function of the first molar would be temporarily unavailable.

In January 2014, the first regenerative surgical phase was performed following an already published regenerative protocol for treating vertical and horizontal defects around implants. 12 , 13 , 14

On the day of the surgical procedure, after the removal of prosthetic components, peri‐implant PDs exceeded 12 mm on the buccal side and greater than 10 mm on the lingual side both associated with bleeding on the mesial and distal aspects (Figure 1A,B).

A full‐thickness flap was elevated with a crestal incision, effectively detaching the band of keratinized tissue. On the buccal aspect, the marginal incision extended mesially, involving the second premolar, including the base of the papilla between the second and first premolars. A hockey stick‐shaped vertical releasing incision was made on the mesial aspect of the mandibular first premolar, starting 5 mm apical to the mucogingival junction. On the lingual side, the crestal marginal incision extended mesially to involve three teeth at the base of the corresponding papillae, without vertical releasing incisions.

As described in a previous article, implant surface decontamination and detoxification were carried out through a series of sequential steps to ensure a thorough mechanical and chemical cleansing of the implant site. 15 First, the implant surfaces were decontaminated using ultrasonic instrumentation equipped with a plastic‐fused‐to‐metal insert.* A titanium curette† was then employed to remove any remaining deposits, and final mechanical debridement was performed with a titanium brush.‡

Once decontamination was achieved, the implant surfaces underwent detoxification. An air‐powder abrasive system§ was used in two stages: the first stage involved the application of amino acid glycine powder for 1 min, followed by sodium bicarbonate powder for another minute. Subsequently, the photodynamic therapy** was applied for 1 min to further reduce the bacterial load. Next, a 50 mg/mL tetracycline solution was placed on the implant surface for 3 min and then rinsed with a saline solution for 30 s. No implantoplasty was performed in this case.

Based on its morphology, the defect was classified as Class 3C Grade A according to Monje et al. 16 (Figure 3A–C). Due to a lack of detailed knowledge about the prosthetic and implant components, gutta‐percha was used to seal the coronal part of the implant in place of a cover screw.

A resorbable acellular dermal matrix membrane†† was placed in conjunction with a combination of cancellous and cortical freeze‐dried mineralized human allograft‡‡ and autogenous bone chips. Finally, the membrane was secured using fixation pins.

Following the surgical procedure, amoxicillin with clavulanic acid (1 g) was prescribed to be taken twice daily for 6 days, along with nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) as needed for pain management. Comprehensive oral hygiene instructions were provided, including the daily application of a medicated gauze§§ soaked in 0.12% chlorhexidine especially during the first three weeks post‐surgery. The patient was advised to wrap the gauze around the index finger and gently perform a sweeping motion in an apico‐coronal direction—from the oral mucosa toward the implant or tooth surfaces—emulating the roll brushing technique.

After an uneventful 9 months healing period, the second surgical phase was performed in October 2014 to expose the submerged implant and recement the existing prosthetic crown.

The definitive crown was cemented prior to flap closure to prevent residual cement from being left beneath the soft tissues.

Maintenance care was conducted at 3‐month intervals for the first year following surgical management of peri‐implantitis, transitioning to 4‐month intervals over the subsequent 10 years of follow‐up. 17 , 18 (Figure 2).

Timeline of interventions.

During each supportive therapy visit, oral hygiene instructions were reinforced as needed and calcified deposits were meticulously removed in a site‐specific manner using both manual and mechanical instruments. This included the use of a titanium‐coated stainless steel curette† and ultrasonic devices with dedicated inserts*.

The fractured right mandibular second premolar, with a previous failing restoration, was extracted in February 2021 due to restorative and occlusal factors. 6 months later, an implant was placed using a flap approach to address the residual post‐extraction defect with regenerative material.

The right mandibular first premolar fractured in September 2022. In October 2024, another implant was placed using a flap technique with regenerative material.

The prosthetic restoration was temporarily delayed to allow for the regenerative surgical procedure using a submerged technique. During the second surgical phase, normal masticatory function was successfully restored.

During each surgery, marginal bone levels around the implant were assessed and documented both clinically and radiographically. Clinical parameters such as PD, bleeding on probing (BoP), and recession (REC) were recorded at each appointment to assess the stability of peri‐implant tissues.

RESULTS

Nine months

At the first re‐entry procedure, regeneration was obtained within the infrabony component of the defect. On the buccal side only the micro‐threaded coronal portion of the implant was still exposed, while on the lingual side six threads remained exposed and a residual dehiscence was clearly visible after flap reflection.

Radiographically, however, no threads appeared to be exposed interproximally (Figure 3D–F).

Eight years

During a follow‐up visit 8 years after the regenerative procedure, the periapical radiograph showed remarkable changes in interproximal bone levels under loading conditions, with three threads exposed on the distal aspect and one on the mesial. Before flap elevation, no signs of inflammation or PDs greater than 3 mm were detected around the implant. During the surgical re‐entry procedure, a reduction in the lingual bony dehiscence was observed, which was now limited to the first thread, while no changes were noted on the buccal side (Figure 3G–I).

Ten years

At the 10‐year follow‐up, PDs of 2–3 mm were observed circumferentially around the implant, with no signs of inflammation. No changes in interproximal bone levels were detected radiographically, except for the exposure of one additional thread on the mesial aspect. However, direct clinical examination revealed the exposure of two additional threads circumferentially around the implant (Figures 3J–L and 4).

Clinical and radiographic assessment at the 10‐year follow‐up. Probing depths of 2–3 mm are detected circumferentially around the implant, with no signs of inflammation (A, B). The periapical radiograph confirms the long‐term stability of the regenerated bone (C).

DISCUSSION

The findings of this case study demonstrate that the presented regenerative technique, combined with an appropriate maintenance program, can ensure long‐term satisfactory outcomes.

The decision to adopt a reconstructive treatment was based on the predominantly intraosseous nature of the defect, where only the most coronal portions of the buccal and lingual walls were absent. 3 , 16 , 19

In this case, implant removal would have represented a viable treatment alternative to surface decontamination, given the increased risk of failure associated with the regenerative treatment for cases with bone loss exceeding 50% of the endosseous component. 20 , 21 Thus, the decision to retain the already placed implant was primarily based on patient's financial considerations.

The decision to adopt a two‐step regenerative protocol was primarily dictated by the extent of the peri‐implant bone defect. Although recent studies have shown that less invasive techniques involving transmucosal healing can yield favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes, a submerged approach was selected in this case to minimize the risk of bacterial contamination and to maximize the regenerative potential of the procedure. 22 , 23 , 24

We believe that the outcomes achieved after the regenerative treatment are closely linked to the characteristics of the bony defect. At the 9‐month re‐entry, near‐complete coverage of the exposed implant threads was observed interproximally, while residual bony dehiscences proportional to the supracrestal components of the defect remained visible on the buccal and lingual aspects.

Although no histological evaluations were conducted to assess the quality of the regenerated bone, clinically there is no discernible difference between the newly formed tissue covering almost all implant threads and the native basal bone surrounding the treated implant. The optimal integration and the absence of visible residual graft particles can be attributed to the choice of a freeze‐dried mineralized human allograft as the grafting material. 25

An additional important aspect to consider following regenerative peri‐implantitis treatment is the potential for re‐osseointegration on previously contaminated implant surfaces. Historically, achieving re‐osseointegration was considered unpredictable and challenging; however, recent experimental and clinical research has provided encouraging evidence supporting its feasibility. 26 , 27 , 28 Animal studies have demonstrated that the extent of re‐osseointegration can vary significantly depending on implant surface characteristics and the specific decontamination techniques used. 29 , 30 Despite the absence of histological verification in the current case, the clinical and radiographic stability documented throughout the re‐entry procedures indirectly suggests successful re‐osseointegration. The long‐term functional stability reinforces the hypothesis that the regenerated bone is functionally integrated. This is particularly relevant considering the extensive bone loss initially observed, which could have otherwise significantly compromised implant longevity. 31 , 32

Effective biofilm removal from contaminated implant surfaces is crucial for facilitating tissue healing and regeneration, especially considering the complex topography of rough implant surfaces. Although the current evidence does not allow definitive recommendations on the most effective methods, recent systematic reviews suggest that combining mechanical instrumentation with adjunctive chemical methods significantly improves biofilm removal compared to mechanical approaches alone. 3 , 33 , 34 Therefore, the multimodal approach to implant surface decontamination implemented in this case was likely critical in achieving the observed long‐term stability, bone regeneration, and remineralization outcomes.

One of the most significant findings observed during the re‐entry procedures was the crestal bone gain achieved on the lingual aspect 8 years post‐surgery. Considering that no additional decontamination procedures were performed during the 9‐month re‐entry and that neither the prosthetic components nor the final restoration were modified throughout the follow‐up period, the only factor that may have induced the improvement in crestal bone levels is the regular supportive non‐surgical therapy.

However, it is important to acknowledge that while the supportive therapy can maintain and, in some cases, improve marginal bone levels, the presence of implant threads outside the bony housing remains a significant challenge. These exposed threads, with a highly plaque‐retentive surface, create a persistent risk for disease recurrence and make long‐term maintenance more unpredictable. 35

The unpredictable nature of this maintenance therapy, coupled with the necessity of consistent biofilm removal, implies that even a minor lapse in home care or an incomplete professional cleaning session can lead to a slight deterioration of marginal bone levels, as observed at the 10‐year follow‐up in the absence of any prosthetic variation. Despite the slight worsening observed at the last follow‐up, radiographic and clinical examinations still demonstrate a significant improvement compared to the pre‐surgical condition.

Although extensive documentation was obtained at each re‐entry, this case study has some limitations. One of the primary constraints is the absence of standardized radiographs, which may have affected the accuracy of bone level assessments. However, this limitation is compensated by direct clinical evaluation. The lack of detailed information regarding the implant surface characteristics, connection type, and prosthetic components used represents another limitation, as these factors could have significantly influenced bone levels in the long term. 36 , 37 , 38 Moreover, the fact that clinical photographs before flap elevation were taken only the day of the surgery and at the last follow‐up visit limits the ability to comprehensively evaluate the relationship between bone level changes and soft tissue morphology and to determine their impact on the esthetic outcome.

CONCLUSIONS

The presented two‐stage regenerative approach was able to restore peri‐implant health and function over time while reducing financial burden for the patient. The three re‐entry procedures highlighted the positive impact of the effective maintenance therapy on marginal bone levels and their stability.
