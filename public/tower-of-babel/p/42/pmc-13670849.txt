Reducing overconfident errors in clinical prediction models
Authors: Henry Bayly, Yorghos Tripodis, Steven Lenio, Prasad Patil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Machine Learning (ML) models are increasingly being used in clinical workflows. Evaluation of these models tends to focus on global performance metrics, which can obscure error patterns and lead to bias in clinical decision making. Here, we propose Proximal Error-Based Confidence Adjustment (PECA), a framework designed explicitly to improve the safety of ML predictions by reducing a model’s confidence in regions of the feature space associated with historical prediction errors. Rather than optimizing global metrics alone, PECA targets confident misclassifications by attenuating prediction confidence proportional to similarity with previously observed errors. Across extensive simulations, PECA reduced the rate at which a model made confident misclassifications while preserving the overall predictive ability of the model. We applied this framework to a clinical trial enrollment workflow for Alzheimer’s disease and demonstrated consistent results with the simulations while also demonstrating superior statistical power compared to baseline models. The results of this paper suggest that taming a model’s tendency to predict overconfidently using historical error patterns may be a critical step towards safer and more reliable ML systems for digital public health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03681-0.

Introduction

Machine Learning (ML) has become ubiquitous in the modern age of biomedical research and clinical decision-making workflows. It is not uncommon to observe ML models outperforming classical statistical models with regard to average performance metrics such as accuracy, area under the receiver operating curve (AUC), and Brier Score [1]. As these systems transition away from predicting in isolated research settings and towards operational real-world deployment, understanding what “model safety and reliability” truly means becomes paramount. In clinical workflows, predictive models directly influence decisions that affect patient safety [2]. Metrics that summarize average performance, like accuracy or AUC, can obscure how models predict on an individual level. Just because a model performs well on average does not guarantee that it performs well individually for each patient [3]. Therefore, ensuring safe and reliable deployment requires developing models subject to evaluation metrics that capture clinically relevant aspects of error and risk.

The confidence with which a model makes its predictions is a key component in evaluating its safety and reliability. Most predictive classifiers produce probabilities that indicate the likelihood of the patient’s event or outcome [4]. When the probability associated with the model’s chosen class is high (e.g., > 80%), the model is expressing strong confidence in its prediction. In clinical workflows, models don’t directly make final decisions for a patient’s diagnosis but are often used as second opinions that can be interpreted alongside clinical expertise [5]. High probabilities are often interpreted as strong evidence for a particular outcome which can, in turn, shape downstream decision making. When this confidence is wrongly assigned, the consequences extend past simple misclassification. A high-confidence error may counter clinical intuition and systematically bias clinical decisions. Many modern ML methods may exhibit acceptable average accuracy while still placing high confidence in erroneous predictions [6, 7]. This is a dangerous latent property of ML classifiers particularly in high-risk clinical settings.

The tendency for a model to produce overconfident predictions is often inherent in the design of contemporary ML algorithms. Many classifiers are trained to minimize loss functions that reward maximal separation of two or more classes. As an example, gradient boosting methods like XGBoost [8] force the model to “learn from its past mistakes” in subsequent training rounds by oversampling observations where it was previously wrong. Once fully trained, it is not uncommon for these models to assign high confidence to their predictions since they were forced to implicitly correct their errors during each round of training. Furthermore, in high-dimensional settings, over-parametrized models can “memorize” training data and therefore produce sharp decision boundaries with most probability density being near 0 or 1 [9, 10]. Although these characteristics frequently improve measures such as AUC, they can also generate overconfidence in regions where training data are sparse or where distributional shift occurs [9, 11]. Post hoc calibration techniques aim to correct discrepancies between predicted probabilities with the true risk of the event through a global or local monotonic transformation [6, 12–15]. A specific example is LoRe (Localized Recalibration) which seeks to improve model calibration by estimating calibration error within local neighborhoods of similar examples and applying a corresponding local adjustment to predicted probabilities [15]. While these methods often reduce global/local miscalibration, they do not explicitly address a model’s tendency to assign overconfident probabilities. Consequently, calibration may reduce average calibration error without reducing the rate at which a model makes high-confidence errors. In the context of clinical prediction, calibration alone may be insufficient for producing trustworthy, actionable predictions.

In this paper, we propose a Proximal Error-Based Confidence Adjustment (PECA), a framework that directly aims to reduce high-confidence errors made by clinical prediction models. PECA uses an adaptive kernel to determine how similar new observations are to historical error regions defined during training and adjusts a model’s predicted probabilities accordingly. PECA builds on the localized perspective introduced by LoRe but shifts the focus from learning local calibration functions to explicitly identifying and responding to regions of past model failure. Rather than estimating calibration error across all neighborhoods, PECA isolates the error space and uses it to directly attenuate overconfidence. This framework makes predictions more uncertain overall, but more certain and accurate in the most important high-confidence regions. We empirically validate PECA through a simulation study that compares its performance subject to common model generalization challenges with existing methods and approaches. We then demonstrate an application of PECA by developing a clinical trial enrollment model for Alzheimer’s disease (AD). In this paper, we seek to bridge the gap between traditional predictive modeling and robust reliability methods, addressing a fundamental barrier to deploying ML models in clinical applications.

Simulation design

We performed several simulation studies to compare the performance of PECA under different data generating structures. The central theme of the simulations was to understand how PECA affected the reliability of a model’s predictions when trained on one dataset and extended to another. Such a task is common in clinical research (e.g. a model developed in one hospital and needs to be applied in another). We compared the performance of PECA to other commonly used model generalization techniques (Kernel Mean Matching (KMM), Local Recalibration (LoRe) [15], and the Mahalanobis Out Of Distribution (OOD) adjustment). We evaluated the model’s performance using Accuracy, AUC, Conditional Misclassification Rate (CMR), Brier Score, and the number of observations with a high confident prediction. Data application description

To demonstrate the applicability of our method to a real-world scenario, we consider the task of predicting future cognitive impairment status. Current AD clinical trials are attempting to evaluate whether anti-amyloid antibodies slow progression of cognitive impairment among cognitively normal AD positive individuals (those with abnormal amyloid levels) [17, 18]. Previous work has shown this to be a complex task resulting in poor predictive performance and low statistical power [19, 20]. The difficulty arises partially from the heterogeneity in the clinical manifestations of AD (some people with similar baseline characteristics may follow significantly different trajectories). Furthermore, confirming amyloid positivity requires biomarker testing, most commonly amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, which is often costly and inaccessible to those not in a research setting. Developing predictive models for future cognitive impairment can help to (1) increase patient safety and (2) reduce trial enrollment costs since they will identify a subset of the population likely to have a clinically meaningful cognitive progression.

We trained and validated our base model using the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) dataset [21]. After training an enrollment model on the ADNI data, we tested it using the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC) Uniform Data Set (UDS) Version 3.0 [22]. Please see the acknowledgements sections for more information on the NACC and ADNI. We included age, sex, education, APOE 4 allele count, and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores as features and trained a model using XGBoost (100 rounds, learning rate 0.05, max depth 4, subsample 0.7). The estimand of interest was conversion from normal cognition to MCI/Dementia within a three-year conversion window. For both training and testing, we used baseline clinical data for those who were cognitively normal with no evidence of MCI/Dementia at their baseline assessment. We further required, as an inclusion criterion, a positive PET amyloid scan (for both NACC and ADNI, we used derived variables to determine PET positivity). We considered someone to have “future impairment” if any of their subsequent 3 visits indicated MCI/Dementia. In NACC, we used clinician derived diagnoses for cognitive status. In ADNI, we used a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) of 0 or 0.5 to indicate no MCI/Dementia and 1.0 or higher to indicate MCI/Dementia. This diagnostic discrepancy was chosen to mimic real-world data drift, where the definition of the outcome might not be consistent between sites. A CDR of 0.5 was combined with a CDR of 0 to represent normal cognition to very mild impairment that doesn’t rise to the level of MCI. This was done to maintain a moderate sample size for modeling. We applied PECA, KMM, LoRe, and Mahalanobis OOD shrinkage adjustments and compared them to baseline. Standard errors were estimated using 2,000 bootstrap rounds. In each round, the training dataset (NACC) was resampled with replacement and the full modeling pipeline was refit, while the external test set (ADNI) was held fixed. Performance metrics were evaluated on the test set for each replicate, and standard errors were computed as the empirical standard deviation of these bootstrap estimates.

This application provides an example of using non-standard α = 0.5 thresholds. Because the prevalence of 3-year conversion in our training data was low (0.33), we altered our classification threshold to reflect this. We chose α = 0.3 as our standard classification threshold and subsequently used 0.2 and 0.4 as our high-confidence thresholds. These values represent a 50% relative decrease/increase in risk of the outcome. Doing this allows our model’s predictions to be interpreted relative to baseline risk in our training population. As a result, the high-confidence regions can be thought of as clinically meaningful increases/decreases in risk.

To demonstrate the downstream clinical utility of using an approach such as PECA, we conducted a power analysis for a hypothetical clinical trial comparing three models: a standard model (no adjustment using an α = 0.3 classification threshold), a vanilla high-confidence model (no adjustment using an α = 0.4 classification threshold), and a PECA model (PECA adjustment using an α = 0.4 classification threshold). We assumed a sample size of 500 (250 per arm), which is in line with existing phase 2 AD clinical trials [23, 24]. We evaluated changes in statistical power across a range of small effect sizes: [0.01,0.15] in increments of 0.01. Small effect sizes are frequently used in modeling clinical outcomes because many AD and cognitive-decline trials have reported modest standardized effects on cognitive endpoints, often with Cohen’s d values below conventional thresholds for moderate effects [25].

Results

Simulation results

Figure 1 displays a graphical representation of the model performances for four of the metrics of interest. We observed that the relative performance behavior of each model type was consistent. Therefore, we focus on presenting the results of the XGBoost model, since is the current state-of-the-art method in the field of tabular classification. Table 1 displays the results from our core simulation task which compares model generalization techniques across varying data generating shifts. We find that across all data shift scenarios, PECA achieves the lowest CMR, best global calibration, and the most comparable AUC compared to the original model. PECA shows the largest magnitude in CMR reduction when there is no data drift (7.7%). Interestingly, PECA reduces the CMR by 7.5% in the concept shift setting – a setting in which it is particularly difficult for a model to generalize. Paired t-tests revealed that across all drift types, PECA achieved a significantly lower CMR than each of the comparison methods. Furthermore, PECA significantly reduces CMR across all data drift settings. This is an important property of the method as its benefits are not dependent on the presence of data drift but instead remain consistent regardless of the similarity between training and testing data. The improved CMR comes at the cost of fewer total high-confidence predictions, a phenomenon that we also observe for LoRe and Mahalanobis OOB as all three methods attempt to balance retention of high-confidence predictions and minimization of CMR (see Supplementary Fig. 3).

An important consideration in our results is the relatively small sample size of our testing cohort (NACC; N = 314). On average, we predicted 8% of these participants to progress within 3 years. While this number is small, it is important to note that modern AD clinical trials screen well over 5,000 people for inclusion into a trial. A4, for example, screened 6,673 people [17]. Under similar screening conditions, we would expect our model to predict about 533 people to be enrolled into the trial (which is reflective of our sample size choices for the power simulation). The use of PECA may increase the number of individuals who must be screened, thereby increasing the screen failure rate. Should the “stringent” nature of PECA become prohibitive, we propose using the workflow pictured in Fig. 3. Doing so allows PECA to function as a second opinion to be used in combination with clinically derived enrollment decisions. This workflow allows researchers to alter the amount of risk that they will tolerate with false positive enrollments. That is, if PECA does not enroll enough people, researchers may elect to enroll more using their own recommendations.

Discussion

This study presents a novel way of thinking about how ML prediction should ultimately work in clinical workflows. While many models and frameworks focus on maximizing global metrics, we empirically show that doing so masks erroneous predictions made with high confidence. Further, we rigorously showed through simulations and a real-world example that PECA consistently reduces confident misclassifications while maintaining the integrity of other global metrics (like AUC and calibration). This is important because it allows us to develop “safer” models without compromising predictive ability. PECA focuses on attenuating confidence proportional to a new observation’s similarity to historical error regions made during model training. These findings suggest that the approach to developing truly reliable models in clinical settings needs to look beyond average performance metrics and into localized evaluations tailored for clinically relevant aspects of error and risk.

We presented an application of PECA to a clinical trial enrollment workflow as a proof of concept. In AD clinical trials, ensuring that both arms of the study are properly enriched with participants who will have the outcome of interest is crucial to be able to detect a treatment effect, especially when the effect size is small [25]. Beyond just effect detection, erroneous enrollment into a trial may lead to undue drug exposure will erroneous exclusion from a trial could result in the failure to receive a beneficial therapeutic. By smoothing predictions proportional to similarity in historical error prone regions, PECA attenuated the high-confidence error rate by 2% and thereby improved the attainable statistical power uniformly across a range of effect sizes consistent with AD drug development. As mentioned in the results, using PECA inherently increases the screen failure rate for a trial. However, as biomarker tests become more available and the screening process becomes more accessible, having a high screen failure rate may become increasingly acceptable because the number of patients being screened can be increased without a large increase in cost. While this enrollment method did result in enrichment and increased statistical power, it should be seen as a byproduct of the primary goal of PECA which is making safety focused enrollment predictions.

In clinical workflows today, predictive ML models are used for early sepsis detection, triage assignment and management, radiological diagnostics, precision medication dosing, and more [26–29]. In each of these cases, the effect of a model producing overconfidently wrong predictions is a direct threat to patient safety. Therefore, while we present a demonstration of PECA to assist clinical trial researchers, the fundamental lens through which we view prediction in these high-risk clinical settings can be extended far past clinical trials. As such, we argue that anyone developing a predictive model to aid or replace an existing clinical workflow should consider using an approach such as PECA to attenuate biased overconfident predictions.

PECA is not without limitations. A primary limitation of the proposed approach is that it intentionally alters a model’s predictions by reducing its confidence on average. In particular, PECA is designed to reduce errors in high-confidence regions, which necessarily comes at the cost of assigning high confidence to fewer predictions overall. While such behavior is not universally desirable, it may be advantageous in clinical settings, where false positive or false negative predictions can have severe consequences, and a more conservative approach is warranted. An additional limitation of PECA is its sensitivity to hyperparameter selection, as the optimal values are not fixed across applications. Selection of α is generally application-dependent, as demonstrated in our data example where we used α = 0.3 due to observed low prevalence of 3-year conversion in our training data. In extreme cases, the CMR could theoretically be reduced to zero, resulting in no predictions being assigned high confidence. For clinical trial applications, we recommend selecting hyperparameters that align with the sample size requirements necessary to adequately power the study. An alternate approach would be using cross-validation to optimize a joint objective function which minimizes CMR subject to a coverage constraint (number of points predicted with high-confidence). The constraint here ensures that the CMR is not trivially reduced to 0 by reducing coverage to 0 simultaneously. Additionally, PECA assumes that the structure of prediction errors is relatively stable between datasets. In the presence of severe concept drift, where future errors no longer resemble historical errors, its effectiveness may deteriorate. Finally, PECA is intended for use in settings where the underlying model exhibits acceptable average performance. In tasks that are inherently difficult to predict, where the model is already highly uncertain across predictions, the application of PECA is unlikely to provide additional utility.

In summary, as the use of ML systems increases in clinical decision-making workflows, there is an imperative need to ensure models are safe and reliable in addition to being highly accurate. PECA directly addresses this duality by using historical error patterns to minimize the number of high-confidence errors. At a minimum, PECA can help to identify situations where a model’s predictions may be less trustworthy, prompting clinicians to weight other sources of information more heavily when making decisions. Future work might consider alternate approaches to defining historical error regions and automated hyperparameter selection that appropriately balances the tradeoff between effective sample size and reduction of confident misclassifications. Furthermore, future work could explore extending PECA beyond classification settings to regression and survival analysis. This would require reimagining an adjacent CMR metric in the regression setting and formulating a loss function which attenuates predictive variance and risk estimates. Moving beyond average performance and towards precise management of model confidence is a necessary step toward developing truly safe and reliable predictive models for digital public health.
