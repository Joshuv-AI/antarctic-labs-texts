Robotic segment III liver resection for hepatocellular carcinoma in the setting of Cruveilhier–Baumgarten syndrome: A case report
Authors: Zsolt Madarasz, Krysztof Nowakowski, Fabian Nimczewski, Miljana Vladimirov, Jens Hoeppner, Julia Michel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCruveilhier–Baumgarten syndrome (CBS) is a rare manifestation of portal hypertension characterized by recanalization of the umbilical vein in the setting of longstanding portal hypertension. Its occurrence in patients undergoing liver resection for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is rarely reported. This report presents what appears to be the first documented case of HCC in the context of CBS treated by fully robotic segment III liver resection.Case presentationWe report the case of a 71-year-old female with a history of alcohol-related liver cirrhosis, classified as Child-Pugh C in 2015, who demonstrated remarkable clinical and biochemical recovery over the following decade. Imaging in 2025 revealed a 4 × 3.4 cm lesion in liver segment III, classified as Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System category 5 (LI-RADS 5) on gadoxetate disodium-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (Gd-EOB-DTPA-enhanced MRI), and a markedly dilated recanalized umbilical vein, consistent with CBS. Preoperative alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) was elevated at 23.6 ng/ml and decreased to 4.6 ng/ml postoperatively. The patient underwent a robotic-assisted segment III liver resection using the Da Vinci Xi surgical system. Intraoperative findings confirmed both the HCC and a prominent umbilical vein within the falciform ligament. The procedure was completed without complications. Histopathology confirmed a moderately differentiated HCC with R0 resection margins.DiscussionThis case demonstrates that robotic liver resection is feasible and safe despite the presence of extensive portosystemic collaterals, including a recanalized umbilical vein in the setting of longstanding portal hypertension. Robotic assistance allowed precise dissection in this complex vascular anatomy. The patient's transition from decompensated to compensated cirrhosis over a decade suggests that portosystemic shunting may have contributed to clinical stabilization. Importantly, the recanalization of the umbilical vein is not random but reflects an adaptive response to sustained portal hypertension.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that robotic liver resection can be a feasible and safe surgical option in selected cirrhotic patients with CBS, provided that individualized preoperative planning is undertaken. This case provides additional evidence for the feasibility of robotic liver resection in anatomically complex portal venous conditions.Keywords: Robotic liver surgery, Hepatocellular carcinoma, CRUVEILHIER-Baumgarten syndrom

Introduction

Liver cirrhosis represents the end stage of chronic liver disease and is frequently complicated by portal hypertension, which underlies the development of both CBS and HCC. Portal hypertension leads to the formation of collateral circulation as a compensatory mechanism, including recanalization of the paraumbilical vein, characteristic of CBS. Hepatocellular carcinoma is the most common primary liver malignancy and develops in over 90 % of patients with cirrhosis [1].

CBS is a rare clinical and radiological manifestation of persistent portal hypertension, caused by the recanalization of the paraumbilical vein and the development of prominent portosystemic collateral circulation along the anterior abdominal wall [[2], [3], [4]]. It classically presents with caput medusae and a continuous venous hum near the umbilicus. The syndrome was first described by Jean Cruveilhier in the 19th century and further anatomically defined by Paul Baumgarten in 1907 [2,3].

Portal hypertension is not only a consequence of cirrhosis but also a prognostic marker for postoperative complications and decreased resectability in HCC patients [5,6]. The distortion of hepatic architecture in cirrhotic livers leads to the development of collateral vessels, including recanalized umbilical veins, which may complicate surgical management [6,7].

Although CBS and HCC share a common pathophysiological origin, their coexistence is rarely reported in the literature [7,8]. In particular, the surgical management of HCC in patients with CBS presents a challenge due to altered vascular anatomy and increased bleeding risk. While robotic liver resection has emerged as a safe and minimally invasive alternative to open surgery for selected HCC cases [9,10], there is a lack of published data specifically addressing robotic liver surgery in patients with CBS and cirrhosis-related collateral circulation [11].

In this report, we present the case of a female patient with CBS and segment III HCC, successfully treated with robotic liver segmentectomy. This case highlights the feasibility and safety of robotic liver resection in the presence of complex collateral anatomy and contributes to the limited body of evidence on this rare clinical scenario. This case has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria [12].

Case presentation

A 71-year-old female with a history of alcohol-related liver disease initially presented in June 2015 with signs of decompensated cirrhosis, classified as Child-Pugh class C. She was admitted following an alcohol-related fall, presenting with hematemesis and tachycardia. Laboratory tests revealed severe coagulopathy (Quick 37 %, INR 1.89), thrombocytopenia (25 × 109/l), hyperbilirubinemia (total bilirubin 8.0 mg/dl), and pronounced ascites with splenomegaly confirmed by abdominal ultrasound. She required intensive care management, including transfusions of packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelet concentrates. During her hospital stay, she developed a central venous catheter-associated Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia, which was successfully treated with intravenous antibiotics.

Although clinical and ultrasound findings clearly indicated portal hypertension—ascites and splenomegaly—no contrast-enhanced CT scan was performed during the 2015 admission. The patient was managed by the internal medicine department and, based on the severity of her condition and the poor initial prognosis, ultrasound imaging was deemed sufficient. The rationale for omitting cross-sectional imaging was not documented in the medical record and cannot be fully reconstructed retrospectively.

At the time of discharge, the prognosis was considered unfavorable due to persistent hepatic decompensation and high laboratory risk parameters.

In March 2025, she was admitted with mild sepsis due to Escherichia coli bacteremia. She reported strict alcohol abstinence over the preceding years, accompanied by marked clinical and biochemical improvement. During hospitalization, abdominal ultrasound and CT imaging incidentally revealed a tumor in the left liver lobe, with no evidence of ascites or splenomegaly. Additionally, CT imaging demonstrated signs of umbilical vein recanalization, consistent with CBS, in the absence of caput medusae. Further evaluation with gadoxetate disodium-enhanced MRI (Gd-EOB-DTPA) revealed a 4 × 3 cm hypervascular lesion in liver segment III, consistent with LI-RADS 5 (Fig. 1). The imaging findings aligned with known features of HCC and met the criteria outlined in the EASL–ESR imaging guidelines [13,14]. Laboratory tests showed a normal bilirubin level (0.9 mg/dl), INR 1.14, and an elevated AFP level of 23.67 ng/ml (Table 1).

Following evaluation by a multidisciplinary tumor board, the patient underwent robotic segment III liver resection using the Da Vinci Xi Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical, USA). The procedure was completed without intraoperative complications. Intraoperative findings included a dilated umbilical vein draining into the left portal vein, which was preserved during surgery. An R0 resection was achieved, and the postoperative course remained uneventful.

Surgical decision making

The decision to proceed with robotic liver resection was guided by several clinical and technical considerations. Despite the patient's history of advanced cirrhosis, the favorable anatomical location of the tumor in segment III and the absence of ascites supported resectability. Intraoperative Doppler ultrasound (Fig. 3) was utilized to map the recanalized umbilical vein and to guide safe trocar placement, avoiding vascular injury. Initial laparoscopic assessment confirmed suitable conditions for robotic surgery. The Da Vinci Xi system was docked with three 8 mm robotic trocars placed in the left abdomen—one to the right and two to the left of the umbilical vein—and one assistant port in the left mid-abdomen (Fig. 4). This setup allowed optimal exposure of the left liver lobe. Pringle's maneuver was not performed, as left lateral sectionectomy can typically be achieved without vascular inflow control, and dissection near the hepatoduodenal ligament was deliberately avoided to prevent injury to the prominent umbilical vein.

Histopathological analysis confirmed a 5.3 cm, moderately differentiated hepatocellular carcinoma with multifocal capsular infiltration and peripheral tumor protrusions, but no evidence of angioinvasion. A complete (R0) resection was achieved. The surrounding liver tissue demonstrated micronodular cirrhosis. According to the AJCC/UICC 8th edition, the tumor was staged as pT1b, pNX, G2, R0, L0, V0, Pn0. No additional systemic therapy was indicated.

At the three-week postoperative follow-up, the patient remained in good clinical condition, with no signs of hepatic decompensation, no ascites, and no jaundice. Liver function tests had significantly improved (Quick 77 %, total bilirubin 0.8 mg/dl, AFP 4.6 ng/ml).

Before establishing the diagnosis of HCC, differential diagnoses included regenerative nodules, focal nodular hyperplasia, and cholangiocarcinoma. However, the lesion exhibited hallmark imaging features of HCC—arterial phase hyperenhancement, washout in the venous phase, and hepatobiliary phase hypointensity—consistent with LI-RADS 5 criteria. Additionally, elevated alpha-fetoprotein levels supported the diagnosis.

The recanalized umbilical vein was preserved during surgery. Discussion

The coexistence of CBS and HCC represents an exceptionally rare clinical constellation. CBS, resulting from the recanalization of the umbilical vein in the setting of longstanding portal hypertension, is a recognized but infrequently reported vascular adaptation in cirrhosis [[2], [3], [4],7]. Its presence in patients undergoing liver resection for HCC has been only sparsely documented, and, to our knowledge, no previous case has reported a fully robotic segment III liver resection under these combined conditions [7,11].

The decision to pursue a robotic rather than a conventional laparoscopic approach was influenced by the complex vascular anatomy, including a markedly dilated recanalized umbilical vein and extensive portosystemic collaterals, which posed an increased risk of vascular injury. The robotic platform offered superior three-dimensional visualization, instrument articulation, and operative stability, enabling precise dissection and safe preservation of collateral vessels while maintaining oncologic integrity [9]. Robotic access was technically feasible and facilitated anatomical preservation, aided by preoperative Doppler ultrasound mapping and tailored trocar placement. These results are consistent with emerging evidence supporting robotic liver surgery in complex hepatobiliary cases involving altered vascular architecture [[9], [10], [11]].

Histopathological analysis confirmed a 5.3 cm, moderately differentiated HCC with multifocal capsular infiltration and clear margins (R0). The absence of vascular invasion and the complete resection support the curative potential of robotic liver resection in anatomically challenging cirrhotic livers [1,5,9].

This case also supports a physiological hypothesis. The patient presented in 2015 with Child-Pugh C cirrhosis, complicated by coagulopathy, hyperbilirubinemia, and ascites—clear signs of portal hypertension. Over the subsequent decade, she demonstrated clinical and biochemical stabilization. We hypothesize that the recanalization of the umbilical vein occurred early in the disease course, establishing a functional portosystemic shunt. This collateral circuit may have facilitated partial portal decompression, thereby reducing the likelihood of recurrent decompensation and enabling compensated liver function sufficient for curative surgical treatment ten years later [6,8,16].

Laboratory course summary

The patient's laboratory profile illustrates a marked transition from hepatic decompensation to functional stability. In 2015, significant coagulopathy (INR 1.8, Quick 39 %) and hyperbilirubinemia (8.1 mg/dl) were evident. By 2025, laboratory values had normalized (Quick 77 %, bilirubin 0.8 mg/dl), and AFP declined from 23.67 ng/ml preoperatively to 4.6 ng/ml postoperatively, reflecting biochemical response and tumor clearance. Liver enzymes remained stable, and renal function was preserved throughout the perioperative period (Table 1).

Limitations and future directions

This report is inherently limited by its single-case nature. Direct portal pressure measurements were not obtained, and thus the hemodynamic significance of the recanalized umbilical vein cannot be quantified. Additionally, oncologic follow-up is currently limited to three weeks. However, the patient has been enrolled in our institutional cancer registry with a structured follow-up protocol, including imaging every six months and AFP monitoring. Further data from ongoing surveillance will help assess long-term recurrence and survival outcomes. Despite its limitations, this case demonstrates that robotic resection is a viable and safe option in selected patients with cirrhosis and complex collateral circulation when preoperative planning is meticulously executed.

Patient perspective

The patient expressed high satisfaction with the minimally invasive approach and reported minimal postoperative discomfort. She was discharged without complications and resumed normal activity rapidly. She described the robotic surgery experience as “less frightening than expected” and appreciated the accelerated recovery.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a fully robotic segment III liver resection in a patient with CBS and HCC. The case demonstrates that robotic liver surgery can be performed safely and effectively in cirrhotic patients with complex collateral anatomy, provided meticulous preoperative planning. This report contributes to the limited existing literature and underscores the value of robotic techniques in anatomically challenging hepatobiliary procedures.
