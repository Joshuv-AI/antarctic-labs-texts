Medicine and motherhood: a cross-sectional survey of the experiences of women doctors in the Republic of Ireland
Authors: Sanjana Biju, Caoimhe Madden, Paul O’Connor, Dara Byrne, Niamh Humphries, Sarah Fitzgibbon, Jennifer Finnegan, Keelin O’Donoghue, Grant Jeffrey, Sinéad Lydon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen doctors may face challenges in their pursuit of motherhood, including a higher likelihood of delayed childbearing and infertility. The psychological and financial sequelae of their experiences around fertility, pregnancy, and motherhood have the potential to impact both the individual doctor and healthcare systems. However, most research on these topics is focused on North America. Given differences in medical training and healthcare systems, laws, and cultures across regions, this study sought to examine women doctors’ experiences of fertility, family planning, pregnancy, and motherhood in an EU Member State, the Republic of Ireland (ROI).MethodsA cross-sectional survey of women doctors working in any speciality in the ROI who were either pregnant, had one or more children in the previous 18 years, or had experienced infertility and/or accessed fertility treatments, was conducted. Women doctors answered questions regarding fertility and family planning, pregnancy, and motherhood. The analysis considered the relationship between their experiences and medical specialty and grade. Hierarchical regressions assessed the relationship between respondents’ experiences and burnout, work-life balance, and career satisfaction.ResultsData were provided by 776 women. Some positive experiences, such as high rates of breastfeeding, were evidenced. However, challenges were identified. Family considerations strongly influenced specialty choice, the incidence of self-reported infertility appeared high, maternity leave was perceived as a burden to colleagues, and women in senior grades took shorter maternity leaves. General Practice appeared as a family-friendly career choice compared to other specialities. Our analysis suggested some association between burnout and both respondent age and age of child(ren); work-life balance and grade, having a teenage child, and pregnancy complications; and career satisfaction and grade and the age of child(ren).ConclusionsThis research identified important issues. A perception of burdening colleagues by taking maternity leave, potentially higher rates of infertility, and the substantive challenges of balancing work and home must be addressed. As the number of women in medicine increases, and the impact of doctor shortages is felt in the ROI, addressing these challenges is crucial to improve retention of women doctors and ensure that medicine remains an attractive career path.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04450-x.Keywords: Women doctor, Motherhood, Doctor-mother, Republic of Ireland

Background

Internationally, the number of women becoming doctors has risen considerably [1]. In the UK, women now comprise more than half of all licensed doctors [2]. There are likely benefits to this increased diversity within the medical workforce [3]. However, the growing number of women doctors in a historically male-dominated occupation is not without difficulties [4], and changes in typical working practices, cultures, and conditions will be necessary to ensure the sufficiency and sustainability of medical workforces internationally. One important consideration is how best to support women doctors in enjoying positive family, and home, lives while remaining clinically active [5].

Women doctors’ path to becoming mothers, and experience of parenting, may be more challenging than it is for others. Research suggests that women doctors are more likely to delay childbearing and require fertility treatments than non-doctors [5, 6]. A scoping review of pregnancy among women doctors [5] reported that 24%-32% may encounter infertility, which is higher than the global lifetime prevalence of infertility (17.5%) [7], and studies [8, 9] have identified significantly higher infertility rates among women doctors than within other occupations [10]. Higher incidences of pregnancy complications (19%-57% [5]) and loss (10%—14% [5]) have also been reported. Finally, some women doctors describe considerable challenges in balancing family and a medical career, such as slowed career progression and negative attitudes to pregnancy and/or working mothers encountered [11], and a negative impact of work, and long working hours, on their relationships with their children [12].

Attending to women doctors’ experiences around motherhood is crucial. For the individual woman doctor, negative experiences around family planning, pregnancy and motherhood can have psychological impacts, contributing to a higher prevalence of burnout [11], poor work-life balance, and career dissatisfaction [5, 13]. Motherhood for women doctors can also have financial (e.g., evidenced drop in income as a result of having young children [14]) and career impacts (e.g., change in specialty [5], reduced career progression [15]) on woman doctors. Women doctors’ experiences of motherhood, or balancing work and family, have also been linked to intent to stay in/leave the profession [13, 16, 17] and addressing these experiences may be crucial for tackling doctor shortages internationally [18]. Further, poor doctor wellbeing appears linked to other important, and costly, issues within health systems such as quality of patient care and safety, workplace productivity and efficiency, and turnover rates [17, 19].

Improving women doctors’ experiences pertaining to fertility and family planning, pregnancy, and motherhood is therefore important. However, undertaking such initiatives will require greater clarity on why, where, and what issues occur. To-date, the factors shaping women doctors’ experiences of motherhood have not been fully elucidated. Women doctors’ experiences may vary, or be mediated, by certain professional or personal characteristics. For instance, specialty may influence experiences; women surgeons appear to have higher rates of infertility, use of infertility treatment, and pregnancy loss [5, 9, 10, 20]. Similarly, grade may be a modifying factor in women doctors' experiences as those in senior positions may take shorter maternity leaves and experience higher rates of burnout [21, 22]. Likewise, personal characteristics such as women doctors’ race and having a partner who is also a doctor may influence rates of pregnancy outcomes [8, 23] and the unequal burden of domestic responsibilities [24, 25] respectively. Some suggested contributory factors that have been linked to increased incidence of infertility and pregnancy loss include long working hours [26], night shift work [10, 27], and delayed childbearing due to medical culture that prioritizes career over family life [10]. The challenges of balancing childrearing and clinical practice may arise as a result of factors such as long working hours with limited part-time working options, lack of women role-models and networks, inadequate maternity leave, and maternal discrimination [11]. Some of the difficulties encountered by women doctors may be shared with other occupational groups (e.g., research [28] suggests the likelihood of adverse pregnancy outcomes increases with shift work and longer working hours) while others may be unique to women doctors (e.g., encountering physician-specific occupational hazards during pregnancy [26]; higher frequency of delayed childbearing among women doctors than in other occupational groups [29] which is associated with infertility and adverse pregnancy outcomes). While there has been some initial work on developing theory [30] on how women doctors experience motherhood alongside a medical career, there is an overall dearth of theory in the area and further research that delineates mediators of experiences, as well as elucidating potential contributory factors, will be required to shape more comprehensive theory that can support targeted, and effective, interventions.

There are also important gaps, and deficits, in the existing research literature that must be considered and addressed in order to move understanding forward. There are inconsistencies in research findings (e.g., whether women doctors’ pregnancy outcomes actually differ significantly from women in other occupations is unclear [5, 26, 31] or whether these differences are explained by number of working hours) and it is unclear whether these may be explained by methodological differences across studies or reflect aspects of participant samples (e.g., participants’ specialties, region of work). Methodological deficits including non-systematic reviews, a lack of continuous data, inconsistent definitions of outcomes, unidentified confounders [26], and limited recruitment tactics across certain studies [13, 32–35] exist. Further, extant research is dominated by data from North America (e.g., over 80% of studies within a review of pregnancy among female physicians [5] emerged from North America) and there is a lack of data on how, or if, challenges pertaining to motherhood for women doctors’ are experienced in other regions (including Europe [31]) where working conditions and cultures are markedly different (e.g., the US has no national paid maternity leave [36] and the length of maternity leave is often much shorter than similar developed countries [37]). Similarly, the research on infertility among doctors largely focuses on women in certain specialities (obstetrics and gynaecology; surgery) and ignores others (e.g. primary care) limiting our understanding [10]. Further, there are many important research questions pertaining to motherhood among women doctors that have not been adequately addressed. For instance, the experiences of women doctors with children over the ages of 0–4, the complex personal impact of infertility and pregnancy loss, and matrescence or the transition into becoming a mother, including associated psychosocial, existential, and other changes [38, 39] remains under-researched. Further research comparing women doctors’ experiences with those of women in other occupations is also required [26, 31].

The current study comprises just one step towards addressing these issues within the research literature and furthering our understanding of the experiences of women doctors internationally as they grapple with fertility and family planning, pregnancy, and motherhood. This study used a survey approach to engage women doctors in the Republic of Ireland (ROI) about their experiences. We sought to employ comprehensive recruitment tactics; profile a representative sample of women doctors working across all specialties in the ROI; consider the full span of the motherhood journey (from family planning through to parenting growing children); explore personal, family and professional characteristics, that may shape or influence experiences, and; gather data that could be usefully compared to national-level data (e.g., incidence of pregnancy complications among Irish women). ROI is an EU member state where approximately half of all practicing doctors are women [40]. The country is contending with an ongoing doctor recruitment and retention crisis [41]. However, a recent review [31] on the experiences of women doctors around fertility, family planning, pregnancy, and motherhood in the EU and the UK identified only one study conducted exclusively in the ROI. This study found women surgeons in Ireland were less likely to have children than their male counterparts and more likely to experience adverse pregnancy events than women in other specialities [42]. Other data pertaining to the ROI medical workforce suggests that women doctors in Irish primary [43] and secondary care [44] are likely to take unpaid leave and work part-time. These data further suggest the need for a deeper consideration of the experiences of women doctors working in Ireland. Developing evidence-based recommendations for supporting the participation, and positive experiences, of women doctors in the Irish medical workforce requires comprehensive data that considers women’s experiences from family planning right through to parenting their children.

Method

Design

The study utilised a cross-sectional survey design and is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [45].

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was received from the University of Galway’s Research Ethics Committee (REF: 2024.05.016).

Participants

Participants were required to be women doctors working in any speciality in the ROI who were either: currently pregnant; had one or more children in the previous 18 years; or had experienced infertility and/or accessed fertility treatments. Approximately 8,982 women doctors work across primary [43] and secondary care [46] in the ROI, and this study aimed to recruit a minimum of 369 women to ensure a representative sample was achieved (assuming a confidence interval of 95%).

Survey recruitment took place between September and December 2024. A combination of purposive and convenience sampling was undertaken, with four distinct recruitment tactics employed. First, information on the survey was distributed at six medical conferences (e.g., Women in Medicine in Ireland Network Conference, National Doctors Training Programme Conference). Second, we capitalised on existing peer support networks, both social media networks (e.g., Docs as Parents Ireland Network Facebook group) and communication channels (e.g., WhatsApp groups), to share information on the survey. Third, the survey was promoted on social media (Facebook, LinkedIn, X) via posts by the researchers and others (e.g., members of the project’s expert advisory group). A dedicated hashtag was used on social media (#MAMinMedicine). Finally, we contacted training bodies (e.g. The College of Anaesthesiologists of Ireland, Royal College of Physicians Ireland), medical schools (e.g. School of Medicine, University of Galway, School of Medicine, University of Limerick), and professional groups (e.g., The Irish Thoracic Society, The Irish Cardiac Society) to request support in raising awareness of the survey through social media, mailing lists, and/or in-person meetings. In all recruitment efforts, we encouraged individuals to share the survey with eligible women doctors.

Care was taken to ensure that the sample was inclusive of all medical specialities and grades. This was done by continually examining survey responses with a focus on determining if women from any specialities or grades were being missed or obviously under-represented. In such instances (e.g., few responses from surgeons), we identified potential means of reaching target participants- typically by contacting relevant training bodies and professional networks (e.g., Association of Women Surgeons Ireland, Irish Institute for Trauma and Orthopaedic Surgery) alongside posting targeted social media content (e.g. tweets encouraging women surgeons to participate). Participation was incentivized through a prize draw.

Data collection

The survey tool was developed by the researchers, with careful review of instruments, and direct use of items as appropriate, from previous similar studies [5] so as to facilitate comparison of ROI and international data. Further, when self-reporting experience of infertility, an established definition of the condition [47] was provided to improve the validity of the item and responses. The survey also contained validated measures of burnout [48], work-life balance [49] and career satisfaction [50]. Burnout was assessed by two single item measures examining emotional exhaustion and depersonalization (high levels of both constructs were defined as experiencing symptoms once a week or more [51]). The work-life balance scale by Hayman [49] and the career satisfaction scale by Greenhaus et al. [50] were used. The survey tool was piloted with, and carefully reviewed by, seven individuals (doctors and non-doctors) including those with experience of pregnancy loss and infertility, queer relationships, and balancing clinical work with pregnancy and/or parenting. Particular attention was paid to item wording to ensure this was suitable for the Irish context and allowed completion by doctors of all grades, specialties, and doctors in both primary and secondary care. The final tool (which included items from previous surveys; see Additional File 1) took approximately 20-min to complete and consisted of nine sections (see Table 1).

Data analysis

SPSS© Version 29 was used. In order to conduct the analysis, some recoding of data was required. For instance, participants’ specialty was categorised as: (a) GP, (b) Surgery, (c) Medicine, or (d) Other. Participant grade was divided into: (a) Non-consultant hospital doctors (NCHDs; in the ROI, these doctors work in training or standalone roles under the supervision of a consultant [52])/Trainees or (b) Consultants/GPs (doctors that have completed all specialty training requirements). Full detail on recoding of variables is provided in Additional File 2.

What are the experiences of a representative sample of women doctors around family planning, fertility, pregnancy, and motherhood?

Descriptive analysis of variables related to fertility, family planning, pregnancy and motherhood (Age of having first child; Number of children; Experience of infertility; Speciality/Training body choice and family considerations; Experience of pregnancy complications; Number of pregnancy losses; Maternity leave length; Maternity leave satisfaction; Experience of breastfeeding; Whether they took parental leave; Whether they recommend medicine to a son/daughter) was performed.

What is the relationship between these variables and a woman doctor’s speciality and grade?

The relationship between variables related to fertility, family planning, pregnancy and motherhood, and speciality and grade was examined using various bivariate tests. Relevant non-parametric tests were conducted in instances where variables were non-normally distributed [53], including a Kruskal–Wallis test to evaluate the relationship between Age of having first child and Speciality and a Mann–Whitney U test to evaluate the relationship between Age of having first child and Grade. Finally, Pearsons’s Chi Square was used to evaluate the relationship between Number of children; Experience of infertility; Speciality/Training body choice and family considerations; Experience of pregnancy complications; Number of pregnancy losses; Maternity leave length; Maternity leave satisfaction; Experience of breastfeeding; Whether they took parental leave; Whether they recommend medicine to a son/daughter; and Speciality. The same tests were repeated with Grade as the independent variable.

What is the relationship between these variables and burnout, work-life balance, and career satisfaction?

Four hierarchical regressions were performed to explore the relationship between burnout, work-life balance, and career satisfaction and variables related to fertility, family planning, pregnancy, and motherhood. Since burnout was measured with two single items, two hierarchical regressions with depersonalization and emotional exhaustion as the dependent variables were conducted. As noted earlier, the lack of developed theory, along with inconsistent findings and methodological deficits across extant research, impedes the establishment of a useful model that adequately represents the experiences of women doctors across fertility and family planning, pregnancy, and motherhood. Accordingly, our regression models, and variable selection at each step, were determined based on engagement with the extant literature, inputs from a multidisciplinary team (including the expert advisory panel which represents all stakeholder groups), and logical grouping (e.g., grouping related variables, such as Demographic variables, within steps). Step 1 contained Demographic variables (Age, Grade, Speciality). Step 2 included variables related to family characteristics (Number of children, Age of child(ren), and intent to have more children). Finally, Step 3 contained variables related to difficulties associated with conception or pregnancy (Experience of infertility, Number of pregnancy losses, and Experience of pregnancy complications).

Results

In total, 1129 respondents engaged. However, there was a substantial amount of missing data, which may partly be explained by the frequent, but necessary, use of skip logic (e.g., to avoid individuals self-reporting infertility who had no children encountering questions on pregnancy, parenting etc.). Missing data were handled by deleting cases that did not answer any items, and cases that had not answered all items required to produce burnout, work-life balance, and career satisfaction subscale scores (i.e. required for inclusion within the hierarchical regressions). While 89 paper surveys were distributed, all analysis is based on online responses as no paper copies were returned within the recruitment window. Ultimately, 776 participants were included (for an overview of participant characteristics, see Table 2). A majority (54.6%) worked in medicine and over a quarter (28.7%) in General Practice. Most (64.9%) were Consultants/GPs and worked full-time. Mean age was 41 years (SD = 7.2).

Participants were equally divided with regards to whether family considerations had/had not influenced their decision to work/train in ROI (45.7%; n = 348 reported it influenced their decision, 45.5%; n = 347 said it did not). Some degree of difficulty (e.g., rearrangement of schedule) in balancing treatment and work was reported among nearly two-thirds (63.7%; n = 121) of these individuals.

Of those who had been pregnant, 91.6% (n = 630) reported having taken maternity leave (see Additional File 4). The majority took 7 + months (61.3%), with the remaining 23.2% and 10.3% taking 6 months and < 6 months of maternity leave respectively. While an overall majority (53.0%; n = 334) agreed/strongly agreed that the length of their leave was sufficient, a substantial proportion (46.7%; n = 294) disagreed/strongly disagreed. More women also said they agreed/strongly agreed (76.2% or n = 480) that it was easy to arrange their maternity leave with team members/colleagues, compared with Human Resources/Medical manpower contacts (68.7% or n = 433), and training scheme/programme (47% or n = 296). However, the majority of participants who took maternity leave agreed/strongly agreed (69.4%; n = 437) that their maternity leave was a burden to colleagues/team members. Most women (69.8%; n = 440) returned to full-time work with no adjustments. A notable proportion (90.5%; n = 570) breastfed their baby and slightly more than half (51.4%; n = 324) described the duration as ‘about right’.

Responses to key items on pregnancy and postpartum were examined for differences by grade and specialty (see Table 4 and Additional File 3). There were no significant differences between the frequency of pregnancy complications and grade or speciality. There were significant differences in the duration of leave by specialty, with women in the speciality of medicine taking significantly longer leave, and GPs taking significantly shorter leave than others. Women in more junior grades (NCHDs/Trainees) took significantly longer maternity leaves than more senior colleagues (Consultants/GPs).

Parenting

Most participants (81.7%) had parented a child while working as a doctor (see Additional File 5). Of these, fewer than half had taken parental leave (38.8%). There were no statistically significant differences between taking parental leave and a participant’s grade [χ2 (1) = 2.288, p 0.130] or speciality [χ2 (3) = 6.930, p 0.074] (see Additional File 3).

Burnout

High emotional exhaustion and depersonalization was reported by 15.4% and 29.4% of respondents respectively. A summary of participants’ scores on measures of burnout are presented in Table 2. As described previously, hierarchical multiple regression was performed to identify variables that might predict: 1) emotional exhaustion, and 2) depersonalisation. Full detail on these regressions is presented in Additional File 6 while an abbreviated overview is offered in Table 6. The regression model conducted for emotional exhaustion was not significant. However, the model significantly predicted depersonalisation (see Table 6). Step 1 (personal characteristics) explained 1.9% of the variance. Addition of the second step (family characteristics) explained 4.7% of the variance and was significant. However, the addition of Step 3 (difficulties relating to fertility/pregnancy) was not significant. Nevertheless, the final model was significant, and the total variance explained was 5.2%; [F (17,604) = 1.936, p 0.013]. Significant predictors were age (β = -0.209, p 0.028), and having child(ren) aged 0–4 (β = 0.189, p0.001), or 5–12 (β = 0.200, p 0.000), or 13–18 (β = 0.234, p 0.002), such that younger respondents, and those with child(ren) in either age groups 0–4, 5–12, or 13–18, experienced higher depersonalization.

Work-life balance

Participants’ Work-Life Balance scores are summarised in Table 2. Once again, a hierarchical multiple regression was performed to identify any predictor variables (see Table 6, Additional File 6). Step 1 (personal characteristics) explained 2.7% of the variance. Addition of the second step (family characteristics) explained 5.4% of the variance and was significant. Step 3 (difficulties relating to fertility/pregnancy) explained an additional 2.5% of the variance. The total variance explained by the model was 7.9%; [F (17,604) = 3.040, p < 0.001]. Significant predictors were grade (β = -0.171, p 0.001), pregnancy complications (β = -0.144, p 0.000), and having child(ren) aged 13–18 (β = 0.246, p 0.001). NCHDs/Trainees and those that had experienced a pregnancy complication had lower work-life balance while women doctors with child(ren) aged 13–18 had better work-life balance.

Career satisfaction

Participants were asked whether they would recommend a career in medicine to a daughter or son. A majority (54.5%; n = 416) would not recommend medicine as a career to a daughter but would recommend it to a son (60.2%; n = 459; see Additional File 5). Significantly more women in higher grades (i.e., Consultants/GPs) would recommend medicine as a career to a daughter [χ2 (1) = 12.363, p < 0.001] or son [χ2 (1) = 3.876, p 0.049] than those in lower grades (i.e., NCHDs/Trainees; see Additional File 3). Participants’ scores on the career satisfaction scale are presented in Table 2, and multiple regression analysis was used to identify potential predictor variables (see Table 6, Additional File 6). Step 1 (personal characteristics) explained 8.9% of the variance. Entry of family characteristics at Step 2 explained 11.6% of variance and was significant. Addition of Step 3 (difficulties relating to fertility/pregnancy) was not significant. Nevertheless, the model as a whole was significant, explaining 12.8% of the variance; [F (17,604) = 5.236, p < 0.001]. Grade (β = -0.349, p 0.000) and being a GP (β = -0.168, p 0.000) were significant predictors of Career Satisfaction, with NCHDs/Trainees and GPs reporting higher career satisfaction than others. Having child(ren) aged 0–4 (β = 0.127, p 0.017) or aged 13–18 (β = 0.127, p 0.002) were also significant predictors, and associated with lower career satisfaction.

Discussion

As the Irish population grows [54], doctor shortages increase [41], and more women join the medical workforce [40], it is more important than ever to understand the challenges women doctors face. Our survey engaged a substantial proportion of women doctors in the ROI on their experiences around family planning, fertility, pregnancy, and motherhood. The data elucidate how women doctors’ experiences affect, and are affected by, their grade and medical specialty, and the relationship between their experiences and burnout, work-life balance, and career satisfaction.

The data collected from 776 women doctors in the Irish medical workforce (approx. 8.6% of all women doctors working in Ireland [43, 46], and certainly a higher proportion of women doctors meeting our eligibility criteria) offer important insights into the experiences and perceptions of women doctors from fertility and family planning, during pregnancy, and while balancing work and parenting young, growing, or older children. In some respects, the experiences of women doctors in ROI appears similar to that of other women in Ireland. Women doctors reported having their first child on average at 33.2 years while nationally the average age of first-time mothers is 33.3 years [55], and the incidence of pregnancy complications (56% of respondents) is similar to that observed in longitudinal Irish data (57.1% reporting at least 1 pregnancy complication) [56]. Interestingly, a third of respondents reported having more children (35.7% had 3 + children) than the national average of 1.5 children [57].

However, a number of aspects of Irish women doctors’ experience are concerning and warrant further consideration. First, 29% of respondents self-reported experiencing infertility. This is higher than the approximately 15% EU-wide infertility rate [58] but is in line with infertility rates of 24–32% previously reported among women doctors [5]. This is concerning particularly given that accessing fertility treatments while working was perceived as difficult by 86.3% of those that had sought treatment. Appointments for fertility treatments often cannot be scheduled in advance which is difficult to navigate with work responsibilities and the need for time off at short notice [59]. This may particularly true for women doctors in the ROI whose work schedules may be inflexible [60, 61]. There is limited research on infertility among women doctors in the EU/UK [31] and our data emphasise the need for further research which clarifies prevalence, associated factors that could be targeted, and elucidates experiences and potential workplace supports. Second, although the majority reported satisfactory maternity leave duration, a considerable number of respondents (46.7%) did not. Previous research, mostly from North America,) has identified low satisfaction with maternity leave duration among women doctors [5]. While women doctors in ROI typically have longer maternity leaves than their American counterparts [37, 62], the high rate of dissatisfaction is notable. Gathering qualitative data will be crucial to elucidating this dissatisfaction and potential solutions. The perception that maternity leave burdens a team/colleagues must also be tackled. This issue has been identified previously [5, 11] and disparaging of maternity leave, and ‘punishment’ of women taking leave, appears prevalent [34]. Previous research shows that often when women go on maternity leave, their work may be passed onto peers [5]. Fixing poor scheduling or coverage practices, increasing the supply of doctors in the Irish healthcare system (including those in training posts and locum doctors), and stopping any existing restrictions by head count within training and hiring systems, to account for the portion of doctors expected to require maternity leave may be useful solutions. Tackling negative attitudes towards maternity leave (one study found peers described maternity leave as ‘a vacation’ [34]) may be necessary. Improving policy, practice, and culture in this regard is likely to reduce the impact of a team member going on maternity leave [5, 34] and improve the experiences, and wellbeing, of women doctors and their colleagues. Third, high rates of breastfeeding, and satisfaction with the duration of breastfeeding (90.5% of respondents reporting breastfeeding and 51.4% describing the duration as ‘about right’) were reported. We did not collect data on breastfeeding duration so it is unclear whether women doctors are facilitated to meet the recommended 6–24 months of breastfeeding [63]. In a country with markedly low rates of breastfeeding (61.4% of live births leading to breastfeeding on discharge [64]) and a ‘formula-feeding culture’ [65], engagement with women doctors’ on their experiences of breastfeeding may valuably identify specific facilitators that could encourage breastfeeding more widely in the ROI.

Our analysis considered how specialty choice and professional grade related to, or may have influenced, experiences. Our findings largely align with previous Irish and EU/UK research [44], and the predominantly North American literature on pregnancy among doctors [5, 31], with women doctors reporting that family considerations strongly influenced specialty choice. Our data suggests that, as elsewhere in EU/UK [31], GP may be a ‘family-friendly’ specialty (i.e., GPs have more children and have them at a younger age). In the ROI, GPs can be in independent practice within four years of medical school graduation while the duration to independent practice is longer within other training schemes [66]. However, becoming a GP is not a panacea, and experiences of women GPs in ROI require consideration. Women GPs took significantly shorter maternity leave (< 6 months) than other specialties. This is likely because GP trainees in the ROI are entitled to paid maternity leave [67]. However, fully-qualified GPs are responsible for arranging their own leave and many experience significant personal costs (i.e., substantially reduced income) and practice costs (i.e., cost to practice of sourcing cover) [68, 69]. While further data on women GPs’ experiences would be of value, it is likely that providing paid maternity leave for GPs, or at the very least, expanding support for locum expenses to all GPs and increasing the availability of locum doctors, could support GPs to take longer maternity leave and alleviate considerable financial deterrents. This is important as longer maternity leave is associated with positive outcomes for mother and baby [70]. Furthermore, maintaining the status of GP as a family-friendly speciality will be crucial to addressing primary care shortages in Ireland [71].

Our findings also suggest that efforts will be required to improve the experience of motherhood among certain hospital-based specialties. Compared to others, surgeons and those in ‘other’ specialities had fewer children and were less likely to agree that their decision of speciality/training body was influenced by family considerations (which might suggest that family-oriented women may avoid these specialities). With growing numbers of women doctors in ROI [40], and an ongoing recruitment and retention crisis [41], there is a need to establish what is required to make surgery and other specialties more family-friendly and explicitly address perceptions that they are incompatible with family. Internationally, initiatives like the Family-Friendly Self-Audit tool (FAM-MED) for healthcare systems to assess family-friendliness of settings [72] and a time-banking system that let users ‘buy back’ time spent doing often unrecognized work (e.g., mentoring trainees) in return for supports at home (e.g., cleaning) or work [73] have been trialled and there is a clear need for such efforts in Ireland. However, this will require further engagement with doctors, managers, and policymakers to establish both what needs to change and how such changes can be actioned within an under-resourced, under-staffed healthcare system. Changing healthcare organisations and cultures is recognised as difficult [74, 75] but this work is crucial to building a sustainable workforce and high quality healthcare provision.

Our data did not suggest an impact of specialty or grade on crucial variables like experience of infertility or pregnancy complications. Much of the existing research on infertility and pregnancy complications in doctors has focused on trainees or particular specialities like surgery (characterized by long training duration, exposure to occupational hazards such as surgical smoke, and a potentially harmful work culture (e.g. pressure to not change working hours during pregnancy) that may increase the likelihood of fertility and pregnancy challenges [10, 20]) which may explain this difference. Further quantitative research examining these variables with larger sample sizes, across countries or regions, or which considers other relevant extraneous variables (e.g., having supportive colleagues or flexible working) is important to clarify if, and where, pregnancy complications or infertility are increased among women doctors.

Our study examined the relationship between the experiences of women doctors and measures of burnout, work-life balance, and career satisfaction. First, experience of high emotional exhaustion and depersonalization was reported by 15.4% and 29.4% of respondents respectively. Although concerning, this is much lower, than observed among doctors primarily from the US (Emotional Exhaustion: 44%–50%; Depersonalization: 32–38%) [76]. Outside of this, women doctors in ROI appear to have similar levels (M = 4.07) of work-life balance as do healthcare workers from different countries (e.g. M = 3.45–3.82) [77–79] assessed via the Hayman (2005) [49] work-life balance scale. Women doctors in the ROI may also experience similar (M = 3.77) career satisfaction to healthcare workers from different countries (e.g., M = 3.26–3.45) [80–82]. However, more extensive comparative research is required before drawing firm conclusions.

When exploring potential associations between motherhood and work-life balance, career satisfaction, and burnout, five important findings were observed. First, greater depersonalization was identified among younger women doctors and those women doctors with child(ren) in either age groups of 0–4, 5–12, or 13–18. Lower age has been associated with increased risk of burnout among doctors in some studies [83–85], with one mixed-methods study [86] suggesting protective factors among older doctors including working in private practice and learned ability to guard one’s wellbeing. As doctors are likely to be younger while undergoing medical training [87], the stressors of training, including long hours and exams [88], could contribute to younger women doctors more frequently experiencing depersonalization. With regards to the link between depersonalisation and childrens’ age, most studies [89] on parental burnout focus on parents of children aged 0–6. This is unsurprising as research has identified that the demands of parenting are often greater with younger children [90]. However, future research focused on doctor-mothers must specifically explore changing experiences, and challenges, as children grow to further our understanding. Second, poorer work-life balance was reported by NCHDs/trainees and by those who had experienced a pregnancy complication. Poor work-life balance among women doctors and trainees/early career doctors [91, 92] has been highlighted previously [11, 31]. Indeed, previous research in Ireland has emphasised the challenge of maintaining work-life balance for doctors and suggested that targeted efforts to improve work-life balance are imperative [17, 60, 93]. The direction of the relationship between pregnancy complications and work-life balance cannot be determined from our study. The experience of pregnancy complications has also been associated previously with poor mental health [94] while working conditions and job stress have been associated with experience of pregnancy complications [95, 96]. Further examination of the experiences of women doctors who encounter pregnancy complications is therefore of interest. It is crucial to ensure that aspects of the work environment perceived as contributing negatively to job stress and poor work-life balance are understood so that these may be proactively targeted. It is also important to understand how women doctors experiencing pregnancy complications are supported in the workplace and whether this support may be improved. Third, better work-life balance was also reported among women doctors with child(ren) aged 13–18. Compared to younger children, teenagers are increasingly independent (unlike an infant or a pre-schooler) [97, 98], and this may better facilitate work-life balance for their mothers. If these data are borne out by qualitative research, a focus on introducing supports for women doctors with younger children may be appropriate. Fourth, NCHDs/Trainees and GPs reported higher career satisfaction than others. As discussed previously, NCHDs/Trainees are more likely to experience poorer work-life balance. While research shows that generally job satisfaction is positively associated with work-life balance [99], some studies [100, 101] on doctors have identified relatively high rates of career satisfaction in spite of poor work-life balance. It is possible that protective factors like emotional resilience and personal accomplishment [100] may support women NCHDs/Trainees in the ROI. The reporting of higher career satisfaction by GPs is unsurprising as GPs across Europe report overall high level of career satisfaction than hospital-based colleagues [102]. Fifth, those with child(ren) either aged 0–4 or 13–18 (as compared to those with children of mixed age groups or school-aged child(ren)) report lower career satisfaction than others. Parenting can be a challenging endeavour. Mothers of young children report issues such as sleep deprivation, adjusting to change in lifestyle, and poor time management [103]. As children enter their teens, their expectations of autonomy may clash with parental control and differences over personal choices may arise [104]. Among women doctors, these challenges along with the demands of a medical career [105] could influence their career satisfaction negatively. However, research has devoted insufficient attention to how doctor-mothers’ experiences change as children grow [5, 26, 106] and further data would be incredibly valuable.

Suggestions for future research

Our survey has considered a variety of topics that have not been explored in Irish or EU/UK research [31] into women doctors previously, such as breastfeeding experiences, perceived burden on colleagues of maternity leave, and the use of assisted reproductive technology. However, there also remain crucial elements of women doctors’ experiences that were not adequately explored. First, it will be important to profile women doctors’ experience of childcare. This is particularly important for trainees who may have to rotate frequently to different posts and/or hospitals [107]. Women doctors may also have changing schedules across weeks or rotations that may require flexible childcare. Understanding requirements, challenges, and experiences around childcare will be crucial in supporting women to remain clinically active. Second, the financial wellbeing of doctor-mothers in ROI needs to be further explored. Internationally, there is a financial ‘penalty’ of motherhood for women doctors [14, 108], and it is uncertain if there are pecuniary disadvantages associated with being a doctor-mother in ROI. As noted, GPs face unique challenges with accessing paid maternity leave. Third, while this survey, and other related research in the EU/UK [31], has primarily focused on challenges associated with motherhood, it is important to understand supports and facilitators of motherhood as capitalizing on existing supports and good practices can be crucial to organizational change [109]. Furthermore, trialling the impact of solutions for doctor-mothers suggested by researchers previously such as mentorship, support groups, or part-time training options [11], in ROI will be crucial. Qualitative research may be especially suited for such a deeper subjective understanding of facilitators of motherhood [110] and has been used elsewhere [111] to understand supports and barriers doctor-mothers encounter. Fourth, while this survey adds understanding of the motherhood experiences of women doctors in ROI, there is no data on whether their experiences are different from women in other occupational groups and this would be of great value. For instance, research from Finland shows largely similar pregnancy outcomes between women doctors and teachers [112]. It is unknown whether in the ROI women doctors have better or worse pregnancy outcomes and other experiences of pursuing or navigating motherhood compared to women in other medical careers or demanding occupations.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. First, we used convenience sampling, which can result in self-selection bias, as individuals decide if they wish to participate [113]. Indeed, those with personal experience of illness are more likely to participate in related health research [114, 115]. Thus, women doctors with negative experiences of pursuing or navigating motherhood may be overrepresented herein, and this may influence our findings, such as the higher-than-expected self-reported infertility. Second, the survey length (91 items) may have caused survey fatigue and led to discarded responses. Skip logic was used to reduce the likelihood of this [116], and great care was taken to ensure all items were relevant and important. Third, it is impossible to estimate what percent of the national sample of doctor-mothers were included as there is no publicly available figure of the number of doctor-mothers in the ROI. Further, the difficulty or impossibility of establishing the number of individuals “reached” within survey research that includes the use of open survey links, and distributed through various means such as social media or mailing lists, is well documented [117–119]. We estimate that approximately 8.5% of all women doctors participated but this is likely a substantial under-estimation of the proportion of eligible women doctors. Fourth, the survey had a relatively modest response from surgeons (n = 94), which may have influenced the findings. As women are under-represented in surgery and the speciality is not generally considered conducive to motherhood [120], this may have limited the number of eligible respondents. Fifth, the anonymous nature of our survey limits the level of detail that can be reported and our ability to probe the effects of potentially important personal characteristics (e.g., ethnicity, sexuality) while the overall broad coverage of the survey and the focus on ensuring the duration for completion was acceptable precluded the examination of interesting but specific issues such as elective abortions or postpartum depression. Sixth, the variances explained through the regressions performed are low (4.5%-9.8%). This is not uncommon in behavioural or social sciences as it is not possible to accurately examine all variables that influence human behaviour and experiences [121, 122]. Future research including covariates not examined thus far may increase the variance and explain work-life balance and career satisfaction [121].

Conclusions

The data collated from 776 women doctors working in Ireland both offers reassurance regarding the experience of women doctors (e.g., no clear delay in childbearing, high rates of breastfeeding) and raises concerns (e.g., higher than expected prevalence of infertility, perceptions of maternity leaves as burdensome). Certainly the data, and level of engagement achieved, emphasise the importance of motherhood, and its pursuit, for women doctors and the need to support all women doctors as they pursue the family they desire, including the high proportions that may experience infertility, pregnancy complications and pregnancy loss, while delivering clinical care. Our data have highlighted important gaps in our understanding and the research literature, along with areas of policy and practice where improvement efforts are required.
