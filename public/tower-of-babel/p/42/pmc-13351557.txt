HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells are susceptible to HIV-1 and contribute to viral persistence during antiretroviral therapy
Authors: Samaa T. Gobran, Armando Espinosa Ortiz, Omar Khedr, Jessica Dion, Laurence Raymond Marchand, Jonathan Dias, Amélie Cattin, Julie Bruneau, Nicolas Chomont, Marina B. Klein, Petronela Ancuta, Naglaa H. Shoukry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAntiretroviral therapy (ART) reduces human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) replication to undetectable levels but does not eliminate HIV-1 reservoirs, which persist in memory CD4+ T-cells of various antigenic specificities. Hepatitis C virus (HCV) coinfection is associated with an increase in HIV-DNA burden, but whether HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells are susceptible to HIV-1 infection and harbour replication-competent HIV reservoirs upon HCV resolution is unknown.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we examined the impact of HCV infection on CD4+ T-cell susceptibility to HIV-1 infection in vitro and reservoir persistence during ART in subjects with chronic HCV infection and uninfected controls (n = 20/group) and longitudinally in one ART-treated HCV+HIV+ individual who spontaneously resolved multiple episodes of HCV infection.FindingsMemory CD4+ T-cells from subjects with chronic HCV exhibited superior permissiveness to productive HIV-1 infection in vitro (p = 0.03) compared to uninfected. This was proportional to plasma HCV-RNA levels (p = 0.046; r = 0.491). HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells distinguished from other antigenic specificities (e.g., Cytomegalovirus) by a CCR5+ (HIV-1 co-receptor), CXCR6+ (liver-homing marker) and CCR6+ (Th17 marker) phenotype and supported productive HIV-1 infection in vitro. In the HCV+ HIV+ subject, HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells carried replication-competent reservoir during acute HCV reinfection, with integrated HIV-DNA persisting in these cells upon HCV clearance.InterpretationWe provide evidence that HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells are targets of integrative HIV-1 infection and carry proviruses that persist during ART despite HCV resolution.FundingCanadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) (PJT-173467, PJT-153052, PJT-178127, PJT-195736, HB2-164064, BR4-197730, MOP135260, FDN-143270, CTN222 and NHC142832), the National Institutes of Health (NIH) (U19AI159819), and Fonds de recherche du Québec-Santé (FRQS)–Réseau SIDA/maladies infectieuses.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Antiretroviral therapy suppresses HIV-1 replication but does not eliminate viral reservoirs in memory CD4+ T-cells. Pathogen-specific CD4+ T-cells have been shown to be preferential HIV-1 targets. HCV co-infection is associated with larger HIV reservoirs despite treatment, but whether HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells are directly infected by HIV-1 and contribute to long-term persistence was unknown.

Added value of this study

We show that HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells are highly susceptible to HIV-1 infection and harbour replication-competent proviruses during antiretroviral therapy. HIV-1 susceptibility correlated with HCV viral load and CCR5 expression. Longitudinal analysis of one individual with recurrent HCV infections demonstrated the persistence of HIV-DNA within HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells even after HCV clearance. Thus, identifying an HIV-1 reservoir niche.

Implications of all the available evidence

HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells may undermine HIV cure strategies, particularly in populations at risk of HCV reinfection. Our results highlight the importance of targeting pathogen-specific reservoirs in integrative HIV/HCV cure interventions.

Introduction

Approximately 40.8 million individuals are living with human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1), among whom 2.3 million have serological evidence of hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, with an increased risk for HIV/HCV co-infection in people who inject drugs (PWID).1 While nearly 30% of individuals acutely infected with HCV can naturally clear the virus, the majority progress to chronic infection.2 Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) cure >95% of treated individuals, but limited access to diagnosis and treatment, as well as unavailability of an effective vaccine result in almost one million new infections yearly.3 Furthermore, DAA-mediated cure does not protect against reinfection especially among PWID who remain at high risk of HCV re-exposure.4

Shortly following HIV-1 transmission, a persistent viral reservoir is established in latently-infected CD4+ T-cells.5,6 While antiretroviral therapy (ART) controls viral replication and slows disease progression, the extended half-life of HIV-1 reservoir-harbouring cells (nearly 44 months) explains the inability of ART to eradicate the infection and leads to rebound viraemia upon treatment interruption.7 HIV-1 cellular reservoirs include a variety of functional subsets principally T follicular helper (Tfh), T helper 1 (Th1) and Th17 cells, with the central memory CD4+ T-cell compartment harbouring the majority of long-lasting HIV-1 proviral DNA,8,9 albeit naïve cells can also carry HIV-1 reservoirs.10,11

Numerous studies suggest that the pool of latently HIV-infected cells in people with HIV (PWH) on long-term ART is maintained by clonal expansion,12 driven by extensive proliferation of CD4+ T-cells responsive/specific to common pathogens (e.g., Cytomegalovirus (CMV), Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), and influenza).13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19 This clonal expansion might partially account for the recent finding that proliferation of HIV-infected CD4+ T-cells counteracts reservoirs decay, preserving replication-competent proviruses at stable frequencies in ART-suppressed PWH,20 with the exception of those treated very early upon infection.21 Several lines of evidence support that enrichment of HIV-DNA in particular antigenic specificities can be attributed to a superior susceptibility to HIV-1 infection22 and/or repeated antigen exposure resulting in proliferation of infected cells.5,9,16,19

Previous studies showed that in addition to HIV-specific CD4+ T-cells,23 HIV-1 infection is augmented in Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTb)-specific T helper 1 (Th1) cells expressing high levels of the HIV co-receptor CCR5.24,25 Our group demonstrated that CCR5+ Th17-polarised CD4+ T-cells specific/reactive to Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) are permissive to integrative HIV-1 infection and carry replication-competent HIV-1 reservoirs during ART.26 Likewise, Th17-polarised subset of Candida albicans (C. albicans)-specific/reactive CD4+ T-cells was preferentially targeted by HIV-1 for infection and depleted.27, 28, 29 Conversely, pioneering studies demonstrated that CMV-specific CD4+ T-cells are relatively protected against productive HIV-1 replication due to low CCR5 expression and high autocrine secretion of the CCR5 binding chemokine Macrophage inflammatory protein-1 beta (MIP-1β).22,30 Nevertheless, multiple groups documented the contribution of CMV-specific CD4+ T-cells to HIV-1 reservoir persistence during ART,12,13 indicative that HIV reservoirs can be established in certain CD4+ T-cell clones, such as CMV-specific CD4+ T-cells, despite the existence of innate antiviral mechanisms, likely abolished during chronic HIV-1 infection.

Several observations suggest that HCV infection may impact the HIV-1 reservoir. First, a larger HIV-1 reservoir size was observed in resting CD4+ T-cells from ART-treated PWH exposed to HCV, irrespective of infection outcome (spontaneous resolution versus chronic progression) compared to HIV-1 mono-infected individuals.31 Second, HCV viraemia levels positively correlated with the magnitude of immune activation in HCV/HIV co-infected individuals,32,33 which is well-known to correlate with CCR5 and CXCR4 expression, thus providing targets for HIV-1 reservoir seeding and persistence34,35 with enrichment of HIV-1 provirus in cells expressing immune activation markers.36 Finally, CXCR6, a liver homing marker for T-cells,37 represents a minor HIV-1 co-receptor,38 while CCR6, another liver and gut homing marker, is expressed on Th17-polarised cells highly permissive to HIV-1 infection that carry HIV-1 reservoirs in ART-treated PWH.39, 40, 41 However, it remains unknown whether HIV-1 infects HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells and whether replication-competent viral reservoirs persist in these cells during ART and upon HCV infection clearance.

Given the various outcomes of HCV coinfection in PWH and the high-risk of repeated exposures,9,42 we hypothesised that HCV infection contributes to the durability of the HIV-1 reservoir by increasing the susceptibility of CD4+ T-cells to HIV-1 infections and triggering the expansion of latently HIV-1 infected HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells by clonal expansion upon recurrent/persistent antigen encounter. To test this hypothesis, we examined the impact of HCV infection status on CD4+ T-cell permissiveness to productive HIV-1 infection in vitro and explored the susceptibility of HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells to HIV-1 infection in vitro using multiple immunological, virological and functional assays. We also investigated the contribution of HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells to HIV-1 reservoir persistence during ART in a HIV/HCV co-infected participant before and after the spontaneous resolution of acute HCV reinfections. Our results highlight the importance of targeting pathogen-specific reservoirs in integrative HIV/HCV cure interventions.

Methods

Sex as a biological variable

Our cohorts were predominantly male. In Canada, 71% of HCV/HIV co-infected individuals are male, 28% female, and 1% transgender, reflecting trends among PWIDs, where men comprise 62.4% and HCV co-infection rates range from 50 to 90%. Similar male predominance has been reported in other Canadian cohorts,43,44 suggesting that the lower representation of women likely reflects differences in risk exposure rather than biological susceptibility alone. Recruitment of women into clinical studies remains challenging due to both epidemiological factors and increased stigma.45,46 Although not designed to assess sex-specific outcomes, these differences should be considered when interpreting the findings and planning interventions for HCV/HIV co-infection.

Ethics

The Canadian coinfection biobank is approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the McGill University Health Centre (CTN 222). The HEPCO cohort biobank is approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Centre de recherche du CHUM (CRCHUM) (Protocol SL05.014). All subjects provided informed consent for sample collection and biobanking. The experiments in this study were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the CRCHUM (MP-02-2021-9133/20.387).

Study participants

The study participants are PWID recruited within the Montreal Hepatitis C cohort (HEPCO).47 For HIV-1 infection experiments in vitro, we tested PBMCs from people without HIV (HIV−) who were HCV negative (negative for both HCV antibodies and HCV-RNA) or with chronic HCV infection (positive for both HCV antibodies and HCV-RNA for more than 6 months) (n = 20 per group). Participant demographics and clinical characteristics are detailed in Table 1 and Table S1. For CFSE-based assays, eight participants who spontaneously cleared an acute HCV primary infection and two chronic HCV participants were enrolled. The demographics and clinical characteristics for this second cohort are detailed in Table 2 and Table S2. The clearance of a primary HCV infection was defined as two consecutive negative HCV RNA tests >30 days apart (Cobas Ampliprep/Cobas TaqMan HCV Qualitative Test, version 2.0; Limit of detection: 15 IU/ml), as previously described.48 The median time (months) between the last negative and first positive HCV-RNA test was used to determine the estimated date of infection (EDI). Study participants were considered spontaneous resolvers if HCV-RNA was negative at 6 months post-EDI, whereas chronic HCV persistence/infection was determined by a positive test beyond 6 months post-EDI. Participants' demographics and virological parameters are shown in Table 2 and Table S1. One ART-treated HCV/HIV co-infected participant, with multiple HCV exposures and subsequent spontaneous HCV clearances was recruited originally from the Canadian Co-infection Cohort. Viral stocks

We used the following HIV-1 molecular clones: (i) replication-competent CCR5-tropic HIVNL4.3BAL, (ii) single-round VSV-G-pseudotyped HIVVSV-G strain. The HIVNL4.3BaL plasmid was acquired from Michel Tremblay, Université Laval, Québec, Canada, provided by Roger J. Pomerantz, Thomas Jefferson University, Philadelphia, PA. The plasmid was amplified through bacterial transformation by MiniPrep (Cat# A1223, Promega) and MaxiPrep (Cat# 12162, Qiagen) and viral stocks were produced in 293 T-cells (Cat#CRL-3216, ATCC) then passed on TCR-activated memory CD4+ T-cells.

Cell culture supernatant containing the HIVNL4.3BaL virions was collected on days 9 and 12 post-infection. Viral stocks were quantified by HIV-p24 ELISA and was titrated on TCR-activated memory CD4+ T-cells to determine the optimal infectious dose.

For HIVVSV-G, pHEF plasmid expressing Vesicular Stomatitis Virus (VSV-G) (ARP-4693) obtained through the NIH HIV Reagent Program, Division of AIDS, NIAID, NIH, contributed by Dr. Lung-Ji Chang. The HIV vector containing the NL4-3 backbone encoding for enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) in place of the Envelope (Env) (NL4.3EGFPΔEnv), and for functional Nef and Vpu, was obtained through the NIH HIV Reagent Program, Division of AIDS, NIAID, NIH, provided by Dr. Haili Zhang, Dr. Yan Zhou and Dr. Robert Siliciano. The plasmids VSV-G and NL4-3ΔEnv EGFP were transfected together in a ratio 1:3 in 293 T-cells using the X-tremeGENE 9 kit (Cat# 6365779001, Roche). Cell-culture supernatant containing the virus was collected 72 h post-transfection, as described before.49

HIV-1 infection in vitro

Memory CD4+ T-cells were enriched from the PBMCs of study participants by negative selection, as we previously described.50 T-cells were stimulated using anti-CD3 (RRID:AB_395736) and CD28 (RRID:AB_396068) antibodies for 3 days (1 × 106 cells/ml in 48-well plate), prior to HIV-1 infection. A portion of CD4+ T-cells was used for phenotypic studies and flow cytometry analysis, while the rest of the cells were exposed to the CCR5-tropic HIV-1 strains HIVNL4.3BaL (10 ng HIV-p24/106 cells) molecular clone for 3 h at 37 °C. Infection with HIVVSV-G (100 ng HIV-p24/106 cells) was performed by spinoculation for 1 h at 300 g at room temperature. Unbound virions were removed by extensive washing with culture media (RPMI-1640 (Cat #350-000-CL, Wisent, Saint-Jean-Baptiste, QC, Canada), 10% FBS (Cat # CA76322-116, VWR), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Cat#450-201-EL, Wisent), then cells were cultured with IL-2 (5 ng/ml; Cat # 202-IL-050, R&D Systems) at 37 °C for 12 days for HIVNL4.3BaL or 3 days for HIVVSV-G. At day 3 post-infection, a fraction of cells was used for nested real-time PCR quantification of HIV-DNA. Culture media was renewed, and supernatants were collected every 3 days to measure productive HIV-1 infection by homemade HIV-p24 ELISA, as we previously described.29,39,51,52 In parallel, cells were harvested on day 3 or 12 post-infection and analysed by flow cytometry for surface CD3-BUV496 (RRID:AB_2870222), CD4-BUV737 (RRID:AB_2870079) and intracellular HIV-p24-FITC (RRID:AB_1575987) expression. Productively HIV-infected T-cells were identified as cells with a CD4lowHIV-p24+ phenotype, with CD4 downregulation being the hallmark of productive infection.53,54

HIV-DNA quantification

Nested real-time PCR (LightCycler 480; Roche) was performed on cell lysates using specific HIV long terminal repeat (LTR) and Alu primers, as we previously described.29,55 HIV-DNA copy numbers were normalised relative to cell numbers upon simultaneous CD3-DNA amplification with specific primers. All measurements were performed in triplicate. The ACH2 cells carrying one copy of integrated HIV-DNA per cell (Cat #ARP-349, NIH AIDS Reagent Program) were used as a standard curve. The limit of detection of these assays was three copies per test.

HIV RNA/DNA ratio

DNA and RNA were concomitantly extracted from PBMCs using an Allprep DNA/RNA/miRNA Universal kit (Cat #80224, Qiagen). The concentration and quality (260 nm/280 nm ratio) of the extracted DNA/RNA were evaluated by spectrophotometry (Nanodrop, Applied Biosystems). Integrated HIV-DNA and cell-associated HIV-RNA were quantified by nested real-time PCR; with the first PCR (ProFlex PCR System 9700; Applied Biosystems) and second real-time PCR (RotorGene instrument, Qiagen) performed as previously detailed.50,56

Activation-induced markers (AIM) assay

The AIM assay was performed according to published protocols.57 Briefly, PBMCs were thawed and suspended in RPMI 1640, 5% human Serum (Cat#100-512, Gemini Bio-Products), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, at a concentration of 4 × 106 cells/ml and were rested for 3 h at 37 °C. Antibodies for the chemokine receptors CXCR3 (CD183)-BV786 (RRID:AB_2565924), CCR4 (CD194)-BUV615 (RRID:AB_2870269), CXCR5 (CD185)-BV750 (RRID:AB_2871862), CCR6 (CD196)-BUV737 (RRID:AB_2870109) and CXCR6 (CD186)-BV605 (RRID:AB_2741611) were added to the culture, then cells were further incubated for 15 min at 37 °C, followed by the addition of a CD40 blocking antibody (RRID:AB_10839704) to prevent CD40L/CD40 interaction and its subsequent downregulation of CD40L. Cells were then stimulated for 18 h at 37 °C, with 4 μg/ml of HCV-NS3 G1a recombinant protein (Cat#227-10349, Raybiotech). Cells stimulated with 1 μg/ml of SEB (Cat#BT202, Toxin Technology Inc., Sarasota, FL, USA) and unstimulated functioned as positive and negative controls, respectively. Cells were then collected and stained with surface markers CD3-BUV496, CD4-BUV805 (RRID:AB_2870176), CD8-APC-H7 (RRID:AB_1645481), PD1 (CD279)-BV11 (RRID:AB_2738543), CD40L (CD154)-BUV563 (RRID:AB_2917809), CD69-APC (RRID:AB_1727507) and OX40 (CD134)-PE-CF594 (RRID:AB_2738357) for flow cytometry.

CFSE-based antigen presentation assay

To generate monocyte-derived dendritic cells (MDDCs), monocytes were cultured in RPMI 1640 2% FBS with GM-CSF (Cat#215-GM-010, R&D Systems) and IL-4 (Cat#204-IL-010, R&D Systems) at 20 ng/ml for 6 days. The medium was refreshed every 2 days. MDDCs were subsequently loaded with antigens, including HCV-NS3 (2 μg/ml), S. aureus lysate (5 × 106 cells per assay; a gift from the Laboratoire de Microbiologie et Immununologie, Université de Montréal, Qc, Canada), CMV grade 2 lysate (1.2 μg/ml; Cat# EL-01-02, MICROBIX, ON, Canada), C. albicans lysate (5 × 106 cells per assay; LAM strain; a gift from Dr. L. de Repentigny, Université de Montréal, Qc, Canada), or SEB (50 ng/ml) for 1 h. For CFSE staining, memory CD4+ T-cells, sorted by negative selection using magnetic beads, were washed with PBS twice and resuspended at 20 × 106 cells/ml in CFSE-resuspended in PBS (0.5 mM; Cat#21888-25 mg, Sigma–Aldrich) for 8 min in the dark. Human serum (1 ml/20 × 106 cells) was added for 2 min, then cells were washed with PBS and with RPMI 1640. Finally, memory CD4+ T-cells were resuspended in proliferation medium [RPMI 1640, 10% human serum, 1% l-glutamine (Cat# 609-065-EL, Wisent), 1% HEPES (Cat#15630-080, Life Technologies), 1% penicillin/streptomycin] and cocultured with antigen-loaded MDDCs for 5 days at 37 °C in 1:4 ratio (0.25 × 106 MDDCs: 1 × 1 06 CD4+ T-cells). At day 5 post-coculture, cells were harvested and stained with lineage markers antibodies to distinguish T-cells (CD1c−CD3+) from MDDC (CD1c+CD3-) using CD1c-PE (RRID:AB_2739006), as well as other surface markers: CXCR4 (CD184)-APC (RRID:AB_1724113), CCR5 (CD195)-PE-Cy7 (RRID:AB_396858), CCR6 (CD196)-BV711 (RRID:AB_2738489), CXCR6-BV605 and integrin b7-BUV395 (RRID:AB_2741932). The phenotype of proliferating T-cells (identified as CFSElow) was analysed by flow cytometry.

FlowSOM clustering and t-SNE visualisation

We used FlowSOM for clustering of the multi-color flow cytometry data, as used before by Van Gassen et al.58 FlowSOM or Kohonen network, is a type of neural network which acts as a dimensionality reduction method assigning cells to nodes in a NxM SOM grid via a competitive learning as opposed to error-correction learning via back-propagation. We first performed the QC and general data processing as proposed by Quintelier et al.59 Given the relatively ‘low’ number of markers used in the experiment, we opted for a N = M = 5 SOM grid, after which, the resulting clusters were subjected to consensus hierarchical clustering, using the ConsensusClusterPlus package in R, where data was repeatedly subsampled, ensuring consistent and robust results. Then, numerous values for the number of clusters were tested. The final selection was guided by biological insights and relevance, ensuring the clusters made sense in the context of the data in this study. Ultimately, we chose 5 meta-clusters based on this biological rationale, allowing for a meaningful and interpretable representation of the flow cytometry data.

CFSE-based HIV trans-infection assay

The MDDC-based HIV trans-infection assay was performed as we previously reported.26 MDDCs generated from monocytes of were exposed to HIVNL4.3BaL (10 ng HIV-p24/1 × 106 MDDCs) for 3 h at 37 °C. Unbounded viruses were removed by washing. MDDCs were then loaded with antigens, then cocultured with autologous CFSE-loaded memory CD4+ T-cells, as described above. At day 5 post-coculture, cells were harvested and stained with CD3-BUV496, CD4-BUV737 and HIV-p24-PE antibodies. The frequency of proliferating T-cells (CFSElow) productively infected with HIV (CD4lowHIV-p24+) was quantified by flow-cytometry. Levels of HIV-p24 in cell culture supernatants were quantified by ELISA.

MDDC-based viral outgrowth assay (VOA)

MDCC-based VOA was performed as we previously reported.26 Briefly, MDDCs were obtained from monocytes, as detailed above. MDDCs were loaded with antigens (i.e., HCV-NS3, S. aureus, CMV, C. albicans lysate or SEB), and cocultured with autologous memory CD4+ T-cells at a 1:4 MDDC/T-cell ratio for 15 days. In parallel, a classical VOA was performed using CD4+ T-cells alone, as we recently reported.60 Briefly, memory CD4+ T-cells (1 × 106 cells) were activated with CD3/CD28 antibodies (1 μg/ml) for 3 days, then cells washed with culture media and cultured in the presence of IL-2 for 15 days. For both MDDC-based and the classical VOA, cells were split into two new wells, with media (RPMI 1640, 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin) containing IL-2 (5 ng/ml) being refreshed every 3 days up to day 15 post-culture. Each condition was performed in triplicate. Levels of HIV-p24 in the cell culture supernatant were quantified by homemade ELISA.

Statistics

We performed statistical analyses using the GraphPad Prism 10 Software (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, Anderson-Darling test and D'Agostino-Pearson omnibus. While several datasets (Fig. 1) deviated from normality (p < 0.05), other datasets (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4) did not meet the assumption of normality due to the inherent high biological variability and relatively small group sizes in this human study. Thus, non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney and Friedman test combined with uncorrected Dunn's multiple comparisons) were used for subsequent analyses. All p-values were two-tailed and were reported as significant when less than 0.05. Pairwise correlations were performed using Spearman's method. Results

Memory CD4+ T-cells from individuals with chronic HCV are highly permissive to HIV-1 infection in correlation with HCV plasma viral loads

Chronic HCV infection is associated with overt CD4+ T-cell activation,32,33 which may increase their susceptibility to integrative HIV-1 infection. Thus, we first examined how chronic HCV infection impacts permissiveness to HIV-1 infection in vitro (Fig. 1A). We used memory CD4+ T-cells isolated from subjects with chronic HCV (HCV+) or uninfected (HCV−) (n = 20 per group) from the Montreal hepatitis C cohort (HEPCO).47 Participants’ demographics and clinical characteristics are summarised in Table 1 and the individual data are listed in Table S1. Upon TCR triggering in vitro, CCR5 but not CXCR4 expression was significantly higher on CD4+ T-cells from HCV+ versus HCV− participants (p = 0.007) (Fig. 1B–C). Upon exposure to replication-competent CCR5-tropic HIVNL4.3BaL and culture (Fig. 1A), CD4+ T-cells from HCV+ versus HCV− participants exhibited higher levels of integrated HIV-DNA at day 3 post-infection (p = 0.048) (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, they showed superior productive HIV-1 replication, as reflected by increased HIV-p24 levels in cell-culture supernatants (p = 0.007) (Fig. 1E), and increased frequency of T-cells with a CD4lowHIV-p24+ phenotype at day 12 post-infection (p = 0.03) (Fig. 1F–G). Indeed, HIV-1 accessory proteins Nef and Vpu downregulate surface CD4 expression on productively HIV-infected cells.53,54,61,62 Of note, the expression of CCR5 upon TCR triggering and before HIV-1 exposure positively correlated with the percent of productively-infected CD4lowHIV-p24+ T-cells at day 12 post-infection (p = 0.015; r = 0.533) (Fig. 1H), consistent with the fact that HIVNL4.3BaL uses CCR5 for entry63

To investigate differences in post-entry mechanisms of HIV permissiveness between CD4+ T-cells of HCV+ versus HCV− participants, we exposed TCR-activated CD4+ T-cells to a single round replication-incompetent VSV-G-pseudotyped HIV construct (HIVVSV-G)55 (Figure S1A) which bypasses co-receptor-dependent fusion.64 There was no statistically significant difference between HCV+ versus HCV− participants in terms of HIV-DNA integration at day 3 post-infection (Figure S1B) and the frequency of CD4lowHIV-p24+ cells together with HIV-p24 levels in cell culture supernatants at day 12 post-infection (Figure S1C and D). These results suggest that differences in HIV-1 permissiveness between subjects with chronic HCV and uninfected controls are likely dictated by the CCR5-dependent fusion/entry step.

Finally, to determine whether active HCV replication influences HIV-1 permissiveness in CD4+ T-cells, we investigated the correlation between HCV viraemia during chronic HCV infection and CCR5-mediated HIV-1 replication in vitro. We observed a positive correlation between the HCV plasma viral load and CCR5 expression on CD4+ T-cells post-TCR triggering (p = 0.013; r = 0.595) and the frequencies of CD4lowHIV-p24+ T-cells upon infection with HIVNL4.3BaL in vitro (p = 0.046; r = 0.491) (Fig. 1I), suggesting a CCR5-dependent mechanism by which HCV co-infection fuels productive HIV-1 replication.

Altogether, these findings demonstrate that chronic HCV infection is associated with increased expression of CCR5 leading to a superior susceptibility of CD4+ T-cells to HIV-1 infection in vitro, proportional to the HCV viral load in people with chronic HCV.

HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells express markers of HIV-1 permissiveness

To evaluate HIV-1 permissiveness in HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells, we assessed the expression of well-established markers associated with productive HIV-1 infection, including CCR5 and CXCR4 (major HIV co-receptors),65 CCR6 (Th17 marker),66 CXCR6 (minor HIV co-receptor and liver-homing marker),37,67 and integrin β7 (gut-homing marker). To identify HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells, we used a monocyte-derived dendritic cells (MDDC)-based antigen presentation assay on cells from eight spontaneous resolvers and two individuals with chronic HCV (Table 2, Table S2). Given the low frequency of HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells, samples from individuals with chronic HCV infection and spontaneous resolvers were analysed together to enhance sample size for phenotypic and functional characterisation.

As we previously reported,26 MDDCs loaded with recombinant HCV genotype 1a non-structural protein 3 (HCV-NS3), S. aureus, CMV, C. albicans lysates or Staphylococcal enterotoxin B (SEB) were cocultured with autologous memory CD4+ T-cells for five days. Antigen-specific/reactive CD4+ T-cells were identified as CFSElow cells (Fig. 2A). CD4+ T-cell proliferation was observed for all antigens, including HCV-NS3, and in all donors tested at different levels (Fig. 2B). As expected, the polyclonal stimulation by the super-antigen SEB induced the highest levels of T-cell proliferation (median 62.33%), while the frequency of HCV-NS3-specific T-cells reached a median of 7.8% (Fig. 2B). Unsupervised Flow with Self Organising Maps (FlowSOM) analysis coupled with t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t-SNE) visualisation identified five antigen-reactive CD4+ T-cell clusters based on differential expression of the tested surface markers (Fig. 2C and D). The cluster distinguished as CCR5highCCR6+CXCR6high (cluster 4) was predominant in CD4+ T-cells proliferating in response to HCV-NS3 and S. aureus (19.1% and 18.08% respectively) but not CMV, C. albicans (13.35% and 8.06% respectively) nor SEB (1.09%) (Fig. 2C and D). Of note, cluster 3 (CCR5lowCCR6highCXCR6lowCXCR4+) expressing the highest CCR6 levels was the most predominant in CD4+ T-cells proliferating in response to C. albicans (20.27%), followed by HCV-NS3 (15%) (Fig. 2C and D). We validated these findings by Boolean gating and observed increased CCR5+CXCR6+ phenotype within HCV-NS3-specific T-cells as compared to C. albicans and SEB-reactive T-cells (Fig. 2E). Single marker manual gating confirmed higher CCR6 expression on HCV-NS3-reactive T-cells, as compared to SEB-specific cells (Figure S2A, B). Similarly, CCR5 and CXCR6 were significantly higher on HCV-specific T-cells. While CXCR4 did not vary significantly among the tested antigens, integrin β7 was significantly higher on SEB-reactive T-cells (Figure S2B). Finally, the triple expression of CCR5, CXCR6, and CCR6 on HCV-specific T-cells was higher compared to the other antigenic specificities (Fig. 2F). In conclusion, these results demonstrate that HCV-reactive T-cells co-express higher levels of CCR5, CXCR6 and CCR6 relative to other antigenic specificities.

HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells are susceptible to HIV-1 infection in vitro

Next, we investigated the susceptibility of HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells to HIV-1 infection. Briefly, antigen-loaded MDDC were exposed to HIVNL4.3BaL, then cocultured with autologous CFSE-stained CD4+ T-cells for five days (Fig. 3A). T-cells proliferating in response to the tested antigens were identified as depicted in Fig. 3B–C and the CFSElow cells were further analysed for their intracellular HIV-p24 expression, as an indicator of productive HIV-1 infection (Fig. 3D). HIV-1 exposure reduced the frequecies of antigen-specific T-cells (CFSElow) for S. aureus, CMV, C. albicans and SEB (Figure S3A). In parallel, levels of HIV-p24 were measured in cell-culture supernatants (Fig. 3E). We observed significantly higher frequencies of CD4lowHIV-p24+ T-cells among HCV-reactive T-cells compared to SEB-specific T-cells (p = 0.0002), but similar when compared to T-cells proliferating in response to S. aureus, CMV, and C. albicans (Fig. 3D). Similar differences were observed for HIV-p24 levels in cell culture supernatants (p = 0.0005) (Fig. 3E). Of note, HIV-p24 expression in HCV-specific T-cells positively correlated with CCR5 and CXCR6 surface expression, individually, together, or in combination with CCR6 (Figure S3B). These results demonstrate that a fraction of CD4+ T-cells proliferating in response to HCV-NS3 exhibited high levels of HIV-1 replication, which correlated with HIV-1 co-receptors expression.

Longitudinal monitoring of HIV-1 reservoirs in one HCV/HIV co-infected participant during two episodes of spontaneously resolved HCV re-infection

A substantial proportion of PWID are at great risk of HCV re-exposure and re-infection.4,68 Therefore, it is of critical importance to examine how HCV reinfection impacts the dynamics of HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells and their contribution to HIV-1 reservoir persistence. To address this question, we used longitudinal samples collected from a rare participant who was HIV+ for more than 18 years and had experienced at least two documented acute HCV reinfection episodes that resolved spontaneously (Fig. 4A). Nine time points were tested: i. study recruitment, ii. pre-HCV reinfection, iii. early acute 2 (3 months after detection of positive HCV-RNA in the second episode), iv. early follow-up2 (6 months after resolution of the second episode), v. late follow-up2 (12 months after resolution of the second episode), vi. early acute 3 (3 months after detection of positive HCV-RNA in the third episode), vii. late acute 3 (6 months after detection of positive HCV-RNA in the third episode), viii. early follow-up3 (6 month after resolution of the third episode), and ix. late follow-up3 (18 months after resolution of the third episode). HCV was determined as genotype 1, through NS5B sequencing69 during the third reinfection (Early acute 3) but could not be assessed at the second episode due to low HCV viral loads and the unavailability of samples during peak viraemia. The HIV-1 viraemia was overall well controlled by combined integrase inhibitor and two nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors. During the study timeline (plasma HIV-RNA levels <35 copies/ml), except for the late acute 3 time point when ART was shortly interrupted, and an HIV-1 plasma load of 191,000 HIV-RNA copies/ml was detected despite the natural control of HCV infection (Fig. 4A).

To evaluate the longitudinal changes in the HIV-1 reservoir, we measured integrated HIV-DNA and cell-associated HIV-RNA levels in PBMCs using nested real-time PCR. The HIV-RNA/DNA ratio was used as a marker of residual HIV transcription during ART.70,71 Despite a slight increase during the early acute time points, HIV-DNA remained relatively stable within one log variation (102–103 copies/106 cells), apart from a massive peak observed at late acute 3, coinciding with the rise in plasma HIV-RNA levels (Fig. 4A, Figure S4A). A similar trend was observed in cell-associated HIV-RNA levels with relatively stable values during the follow-up, again except for the late acute 3 time point (Figure S4B). Of note, high HCV viraemia at the early acute 2 and early acute 3 time points (890,164 and 3,150,00 HCV-RNA copies/ml, respectively) coincided with exacerbations of transcriptionally active HIV-1 reservoir during ART (HIV-RNA/DNA ratio of 2.06 at early acute 2 and 4.64 at early acute 3 versus 0.29 at the pre-infection time point) (Figure S4B and C). There was a massive increase in the HIV-RNA/DNA ratio (48.6) at the late acute 3 time point that coincided with ART interruption, as indicated in medical records, and a spike in plasma HIV-1 viral load (Fig. 4A), indicative of active HIV-1 replication.

Detection of replication-competent HIV-1 reservoirs in HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells early but not late after spontaneous resolution of HCV re-infection

To determine the contribution of HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells to HIV-1 reservoir upon spontaneous resolution of HCV re-infection, we first examined their frequency and phenotype at three time points for which leukapheresis samples were available: early acute 3, early follow-up3 and late follow-up3 (Fig. 4A). The HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells were identified using the Activation Induced Marker (AIM) assay (Fig. 4B), as we previously reported.72 PBMCs were incubated for 18 h in the presence or the absence of the following antigens: HCV-NS3, SEB and CMV. Antigen-specific CD4+ T-cells were identified as AIM+ (CD69+CD40L+OX40+) (Fig. 4B, Figure S5A and B). The AIM assay revealed that the highest frequencies of HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells occurred at the early acute 3 time point, with a decrease at the follow-up time points (Fig. 4C), unlike the frequency of CMV-specific CD4+ T-cells that remained relatively stable (Fig. 4D). The analysis of the Th lineage markers CXCR5, PD-1, CXCR3, CCR4 and CCR6 expression on the surface of AIM+CD4+ T-cells (Figure S5C) demonstrated that at the early acute 3 time point most of the HCV-specific T-cells exhibited a Tfh (CXCR5+PD1+) and Tfh/Th1 (CXCR5+PD1+CXCR3+) phenotype (49.8% and 19.8%, respectively) (Fig. 4E). After HCV resolution, at the follow-up time points, there was a decline in the frequency of HCV-NS3-specific CD4+ T-cells with a Tfh phenotype (20.3% and 23%), an expansion of CCR6+Th17-polarised cells (30.9% and 29.5%), and a minimal increase in CCR4+ Th2-polarised phenotype (from 2.04% at early acute to 12.7% and 13.4% at follow-up time points) (Fig. 4E). These phenotypic transitions were not observed in CMV AIM+CD4+ T-cells, which largely exhibited a CXCR3+ Th1-polarised throughout the tested timeline (Fig. 4F).

To determine whether HIV-1 reservoirs persist in HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells upon spontaneous resolution of HCV re-infection, we used the MDDC-based viral outgrowth assay (VOA) in which CD4+ T-cells were cultured for 15 days with matched MDDCs loaded in antigens, as we previously described.26,73 Viral outgrowth was measured by flow cytometry quantification of intracellular HIV-p24 expression at day 15 post-coculture and by ELISA quantification of HIV-p24 in cell-culture supernatants at days 3, 6, 9, 12 and 15 (Fig. 5A). Alongside the tested antigens (i.e., HCV-NS3, S. aureus, CMV, C. albicans, SEB), we also included anti-CD3/CD28-stimulated CD4+ T-cells, as a positive control. HIV-1 outgrowth was detected in MDDC: T-cell coculture supernatant in response to NS3-HCV, with the highest levels reached at day 15 post-coculture for samples collected at early acute 3 (Fig. 5B) but not at early and late follow-up3 time points (Figure S7A–C). On day 15 post-coculture, CD4+ T-cells responsive to NS3-HCV expressed HIV-p24 at high levels similar to C. albicans but substantially higher than S. aureus, CMV, and SEB at early acute 3 HCV infection, indicative of a relatively large HIV-1 reservoir size during acute HCV infection concomitant with high HCV viraemia (Fig. 5C). However, the participant had a short period of ART interruption, when HIV-1 viral load was uncontrolled. After ART restoration, HIV-1 viral load was controlled again and HIV-1 outgrowth was undetectable at follow-up3 time points for most of the tested antigens (Fig. 5D and E). On the other hand, viral outgrowth was clearly identified in the TCR-triggered controls at the three time points (Fig. 5C–E; Figure S7A–C). Integrative HIV-1 infection persists in HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells late after natural resolution of HCV reinfection despite HCV repertoire contraction

The detection of replication-competent HIV-1 reservoirs in VOA upon HCV-NS3 activation at the early acute 3 but not early/late follow-up3 time points (Fig. 5; Figure S7) may be attributed to the contraction of HCV-specific CD4+ T-cell repertoire (Fig. 4), an expected phenomenon after the resolution of HCV infection. To mitigate this limitation, we quantified integrated HIV-DNA in flow cytometry-sorted HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells, before and after HCV resolution. To this end, we used the CFSE-based proliferation assay, in which MDDC loaded with antigens (i.e., HCV-NS3, S. aureus, CMV, and SEB) were cocultured with autologous CFSE-loaded CD4+ T-cells for five days in the presence of antiretrovirals (ARVs) to prevent HIV-1 cell-to-cell transmission (Fig. 6A). Then, the proliferating CFSElow T-cells were sorted by flow cytometry (Fig. 6B, Figure S8). Similar to results of the AIM assay (Fig. 4), the CFSE assay consistently demonstrated a decrease in the frequency of HCV-specific T-cells at early and late follow-up3 time points (Fig. 6C). Of note, such repertoire contraction was not observed for the other antigens tested (Fig. 6C). The quantification of integrated HIV-DNA normalised per 106 cells in sorted CFSElow T-cells demonstrated the presence of integrated HIV-DNA in HCV-specific T-cells at similar levels compared to CMV- and SEB-specific T-cells, and slightly lower levels compared to S. aureus-reactive T-cells at all time points (Fig. 6D). Interestingly, integrated HIV-DNA levels in HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells were slightly higher at early/late follow-up3 compared to early acute 3 time points (Fig. 6D). Discussion

In this study, we explored the impact of HCV co-infection on HIV-1 reservoir seeding and persistence in bulk and HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells during ART. We demonstrate that chronic HCV infection enhances the susceptibility of CD4+ T-cells to CCR5-tropic HIVNL4.3BaL infection in vitro. This susceptibility positively correlated with CCR5 expression and plasma HCV-RNA levels. Moreover, HCV-NS3-reactive CD4+ T-cells expressed markers of HIV permissiveness (i.e., CCR5, CXCR6 and CCR6) and were highly permissive to HIV-1 infection in vitro, similar to T-cells proliferating in response to S. aureus and C. albicans, which have been previously identified as selective HIV targets.26,27 Finally, longitudinal investigations in one ART-treated HCV+/HIV+ participant who spontaneously cleared three episodes of HCV infection revealed i) an increased frequency of HCV-NS3-reactive CD4+ T-cells with an HIV-1 permissive phenotype (Tfh/Th17 polarisation profile) and ii) the presence of integrated HIV-DNA in HCV-NS3-reactive CD4+ T-cells, which persisted despite spontaneous HCV resolution. Together these results demonstrate that HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells represent a previously unrecognised niche for HIV-1 reservoir persistence that persists during ART and upon HCV resolution, thus challenging HIV-1 cure objectives.

Several studies have explored the antigenic specificities of CD4+ T-cells carrying HIV-1 reservoirs suggesting that HIV-1 infects and persists in CD4+ T-cells specific for HIV and other antigens including MTb, C. albicans and CMV.22,24,25,27, 28, 29, 30 Herein, we compared the contribution of HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells to HIV-1 reservoirs to that of fungi (i.e., C. albicans), gram positive bacteria (i.e., S. aureus), DNA viruses (i.e., CMV), and SEB as a surrogate of polyclonal CD4+ T-cell activation. Experiments on cells from HCV-exposed individuals (chronically-infected and resolvers) showed that S. aureus and HCV-specific/reactive CD4+ T-cells were the most susceptible to HIV-1 infection in vitro. Of note, there was no direct link between proliferation and susceptibility to HIV-1 infection, since SEB elicited the highest levels of T-cell proliferation but exhibited the lowest HIV-1 infection levels. These results are in line with our previous publication showing that T-cells proliferating in response to bacterial/fungal pathogens promote HIV-1 replication, whereas the SEB-mediated polyclonal stimulation results in the expansion of diverse T-cell clones with variable permissiveness to HIV-1 infection.26

CD4+ T-cells from participants with chronic HCV infection exhibit a permissive CCR5hi phenotype. Furthermore, HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells exhibited an HIV-permissiveness profile through the co-expression of CCR5/CXCR6 and CCR6 markers. Indeed, persistent inflammation during chronic HCV infection and high HCV viraemia are associated with CCR5 over-expression,74 that promotes HIV-1 entry75 and may fuel HIV-1 infection and reservoir seeding. This is of particular concern especially that HCV viral loads and immune activation are increased in HCV/HIV as compared to HCV mono-infected individuals.32,33,46,76 Furthermore, CXCR6 is a crucial liver retention molecule that is required for residence of CD4+ T-cells in the hepatic microenvironment,37 and is involved in hepatic inflammation and fibrosis,77 besides serving as a minor HIV co-receptor.67 This CCR5+CXCR6+ phenotype was shared by S. aureus-reactive CD4+ T-cells, that are highly permissive to HIV-1 replication/integration via retinoic acid-dependent pathway.26 However, it remains to be determined whether HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells display similar mechanism, specifically that liver is an organ rich in retinoic acid.78 Finally, high CCR6 levels is also associated with an HIV-permissive phenotype in C. albicans-specific CD4+ T-cells. CCR6+ Th17 cells, through production of IL-17 A, play a crucial role in the defence against pathogens at mucosal and skin barriers.26,79 Thus, due to increased permissiveness to HIV-1 infection, C. albicans-reactive Th17 cells are depleted leading to opportunistic infections in PWH.26,27 Likewise, it is established that a substantial fraction of HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells has phenotypic and functional features of Th17 (CCR6+) cells,80 a subset of CD4+ T-cells transcriptionally programmed to be targeted by HIV-1 infection55,81 and carry HIV-1 reservoir in PWH on ART.39

Spontaneous resolution of acute HCV infection leads to generation of long-lived memory T-cells that enhance viral clearance upon re-infection.82 These long-lived memory cells can harbour HIV reservoirs that can be reactivated upon re-exposure in high-risk populations. For this study, we had access to longitudinal samples form one HCV+HIV+ person who repeatedly resolved HCV infection in the absence of DAA. The longitudinal follow-up of HIV-1 reservoir in this participant illustrated that HIV RNA/DNA ratio, indicative of transcriptionally active HIV-1 reservoir,70,71 peaked during acute HCV episodes despite ART. This suggests that acute HCV infection might favour HIV-1 transcription, a step that is not suppressed by ART.83 It is noteworthy that at late acute 3 stage of HCV re-infection, the participant voluntary interrupted ART for a few days. This episode of ART interruption was associated with a surge in plasma HIV viral load and a sharp rise in the HIV-RNA/DNA ratio, reflecting active HIV-1 replication. Of particular relevance, after ART re-initiation at early and late follow-up 3 time points, levels of replication competent HIV-1 reservoirs, detected in a MDDC-based VOA,26,73 were decreased compared to early acute 3 time point before ART interruption, suggesting substantial HIV-1 reservoir decay. This reduction was not only observed in response to HCV-NS3 stimulation, but also in response to the polyclonal CD3/CD28 stimulation. Despite the reduction in replication competent HIV-1 reservoirs detected in VOA at follow-up time points compared to the early acute 3, we noticed sustained abundant HIV-1 integration within HCV-specific CD4+ T-cell (using PCR within CFSElow cells). This suggests that HIV-1 reservoirs are not depleted within HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells despite natural HCV clearance.

HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells display a Tfh–like functional and phenotypic profile during spontaneous clearance of acute infection.84,85 Similarly, we observed that Tfh characteristics strongly shaped the phenotype of HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells during early acute HCV infection. This is significant given the well-established role of Tfh cells in HIV-1 persistence, throughout their permissiveness to infection and supporting replication-competent virions.86, 87, 88 Consistent with this, replication-competent proviruses were detected by VOA during acute HCV infection. Following HCV clearance, HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells transitioned toward a Th17-skewed phenotype and remained enriched for HIV DNA. In line with our previous work and that of others, Th17 cells represent a preferential target for HIV-1 replication and integration,39,55,89 likely due to their longevity and predominant residence within the gut mucosa, a favoured compartment for elevated levels of ongoing HIV-1 replication.29,90

Our study has a few limitations. First, because analyses were performed using peripheral blood samples, the findings may not fully capture HIV reservoir persistence within intrahepatic CD4+ T-cells. Future studies are needed to address the questions of HIV-1 reservoir persistence in HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells residing in the liver and other lymphoid organs, such as the bone marrow and draining lymph nodes, upon spontaneous HCV resolution or DAA-mediated cure. Second, while PCR-based techniques provide sensitive and quantitative measurements, they overestimate the HIV-1 reservoir size as the integrated HIV-DNA genomes are largely defective and/or replication-incompetent.91,92 Third, although the MDDC-based VOA provided a proof-of-concept for the presence of replication-competent reservoirs in HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells, it was performed without serial dilution and therefore only provides qualitative evidence rather than a precise quantification of the HIV-1 reservoir size. The number of study subjects was constrained by the availability of sufficient PBMC samples and detectable frequencies of HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells rather than an a priori power calculation (n = 20/group for in vitro assays; n = 10 for CFSE-based antigen-specific analyses). Nevertheless, significant differences were observed in our comparisons that will inform sample size calculations for future studies. Fourth, longitudinal in-depth investigations of HCV/HIV coinfection were limited to a single, rare case. Despite the comprehensive analysis of this participant, who represents a rare and difficult-to-recruit population, we cannot exclude the possibility that systemic immune activation associated with HCV co-infection may enhance HIV-1 permissiveness in CD4+ T-cells across multiple antigen-specificities other than HCV. Future studies with larger and more diverse cohorts using multivariate analyses to control for potential confounders such as age, sex, comorbidities, and clinical parameters will further define the independent contribution of HCV infection to HIV susceptibility and reservoir dynamics. Finally, leukapheresis was available for the HCV+HIV+ participant only at the 3rd reinfection episode. In addition, a brief ART interruption at the late acute 3 time point resulted in increased HIV-1 viral loads, which may have influenced reservoir marker measurements. Nonetheless, these data represent a rare opportunity to examine reservoir dynamics during HCV reinfection and serve as a valuable basis for hypothesis generation and future studies. Our study provides evidence that HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells exhibit an HIV permissive phenotype (HIV entry and gut-homing receptors), are productively infected in vitro, and carry integrated HIV-DNA in ART-treated HCV/HIV co-infected people where HIV outgrowth is induced by HCV peptides in MDDC-based VOA. Nevertheless, prospective studies using single cell RNA sequencing and TCR sequencing on multiple HCV/HIV co-infected people are needed to accurately investigate the relative contribution of HCV-specific T-cells to the total HIV-1 reservoir pool. In addition, more work is required to evaluate how HCV-specific -polarisation skew from Tfh to Th17 phenotype after HCV resolution impacts HIV-1 reservoir size. As natural HCV clearance might differ from the abrupt DAA-induced elimination of HCV virion within weeks of therapy initiation,93 differences in HIV reservoir decay in natural resolvers versus DAA-treated participants are also worth exploring. While the liver is among organs that represent an enriched HIV-1 tissue reservoir,94,95 fine needle aspirates as well as minimal invasive autopsy (MIA)96 could be a valuable tool in identifying HIV-1 reservoir burden within liver-homed cells in comparison with other tissues. Studies using intact proviral DNA assay (IPDA) could inform about the genetic intactness of the HIV-DNA carried by HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells and how it evolves over time.

In conclusion, our work identified HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells as uncharacterised targets for HIV-1 infection and viral reservoir persistence during ART. Despite the natural contraction of the HCV-specific T-cell repertoire upon spontaneous resolution, HIV-DNA integration remained enriched within HCV-specific CD4+ T-cells. These results build upon our previous longitudinal analyses of HCV+HIV+ ART+ individuals undergoing DAA therapy, where we identified a “DAA-sensitive” HIV-reservoir.50 Our findings raise concerns about the irreversible impact of HCV co-infection on the persistence of HIV-1 reservoirs in HCV/HIV co-infected people upon spontaneous resolution or DAA-mediated HCV cure. This is notably pertinent in the absence of an HCV vaccine since the risk for HCV reinfection is very high in vulnerable populations (e.g., PWID).97,98 Combined biomedical, social and political efforts are required to stop the currently active HCV/HIV epidemic.
