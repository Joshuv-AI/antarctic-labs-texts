NPO at midnight: A historical rule in need of evidence-based reform
Authors: Ethan F Kramer, Carl J Pepine, David E Winchester
Published: 2025

History dictates “NPO at Midnight”

The first documentations of common practices regarding restricting oral intake before an elective procedure were written by anesthesiologists in the 1800s. In these texts, there appears to have been a fairly consistent distinction between food and clear liquids [1]. For example, in 1883 Sir Joseph Lister recommended “no solid matter in the stomach when chloroform is administered,” however advised “a cup of tea or beef-tea about two hours previously.” [2] Most guidelines during this time recommended roughly a 2–4 h fast prior to a procedure, with some distinguishing that clear liquids could be consumed closer to the procedure time [1].

It wasn't until the 1960s when physicians widely adopted the practice of having all patients be “nil per os” (“nothing by mouth;” NPO) at midnight prior to a procedure. This practice appears to have been primarily adopted after a retrospective review in 1946, where Mendelson described 66 cases of aspiration of gastric contents during general anesthesia from 44,016 pregnancies at a single center across 13 years. Of the five patients who aspirated solid food, two experienced complete respiratory obstruction leading to immediate death. Of the 40 patients who aspirated only liquid contents, all fully recovered within 7–10 days [3].

From the 1990s until today, there has been closer inspection of the colloquial “NPO at midnight” rule for all patients undergoing procedures. Studies challenged the true risk of aspiration if patients were more liberal with their intake, demonstrating that clear liquids exit the stomach within two hours [4,5]. In fact, a Cochrane review of nine studies found that patients who had consumed clear liquids prior to a procedure had gastric volumes that were actually lower than their fasted counterparts [6]. In accordance with the accumulating studies demonstrating the lack of efficacy of fasting for a prolonged period of time prior to a procedure, the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) recommended as early as 1998 that low-aspiration-risk patients undergoing elective procedures be allowed clear liquids up to 2 h and a light meal up to 6 h prior to the procedure [7]. Current ASA guidelines support similar fasting practices [8,9].

“NPO At Midnight” is common prior to cardiovascular procedures and not evidence-based

Despite multiple decades of increasing support for allowing shorter fasting periods prior to elective procedures, patients are still often made NPO at midnight. In fact, Sorita et al. found that 46.6 % of patients on a medicine service experienced some degree of fasting, with 23.3 % of these instances being potentially inappropriate [10]. While there are no studies characterizing the fasting duration practices prior to cardiovascular procedures, pre-procedure fasting is typical [11].

In recent years, the benefit of a prolonged fast prior to cardiovascular procedures has been questioned. A meta-analysis of seven randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing nonfasting and fasting strategies prior to percutaneous procedures found that a nonfasting strategy leads to improved patient satisfaction without increasing adverse events, such as aspiration pneumonia, nausea, and vomiting [12]. Two meta-analyses of RCTs comparing fasting versus nonfasting strategies prior to cardiac catheterization have shown that adverse events are not significantly different between groups and nonfasting groups consistently reported higher patient satisfaction [13,14]. These recent studies have inspired statements from the American Heart Association (AHA) and Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions to question the current practice of strict fasting for a prolonged period prior to catheterization [11,15]. A 2021 AHA statement recommended that the decision of timing NPO prior to catheterization be left to the cardiologist acknowledging the non-inferiority of shorter fasting periods, as evidenced by RCTs demonstrating that shorter fasting times yield no difference is gastric volume or a decreased aspiration risk in the patient group who fasted for a shorter period [9,16]. They also noted relevant important risks associated with prolonged fasting, such as dehydration, hypoglycemia, and, particularly as it relates to interventional procedures involving radiographic contrast, an increased risk of acute kidney injury [11].

This trend is similar among structural interventions and inpatient procedures, as patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement or arrhythmia ablation also do not have significant differences in adverse events when more liberal fasting strategies are employed [17].

Indeed, there is no evidence that cardiovascular procedures, especially those with minimal sedation, benefit from prolonged fasting.

How can we make smarter fasting decisions?

To date, there is a paucity of quality improvement (QI) projects specifically targeting changing fasting practices prior to cardiovascular procedures. However, QI projects have been carried out across several specialties and procedures, one of which included cardiac catheterizations [[18], [19], [20]]. While culture change is an incremental process that takes years, understanding and applying initiatives that have been successful in other disciplines provides a template for cardiology. We offer three actionable steps to improve our current practices regarding fasting prior to cardiovascular procedures:

Restructuring order sets

A reasonable step in changing the practice patterns of a center is by identifying institutional protocols and order sets that rely on outdated evidence or preclude clinicians from following the most current best practices and guidelines. For example, an interdisciplinary team at University of Texas Medical Branch launched a multifaceted QI project with the aim to reduce NPO at midnight orders by 50 %, which included orders prior to cardiac catheterization. Their first step in achieving this was by identifying institutional protocols which relied on outdated evidence regarding fasting prior to the procedure and revising these protocols [20]. Another QI initiative implemented pre-determined NPO times per ASA guidelines prior to patients undergoing endoscopy if they were scheduled after 1 pm [18]. At an academic pediatric hospital, a pre-anesthesia diet order was implemented which included a special liquid diet which followed ASA guidelines for fasting prior to procedures [19].

A similar order set could be implemented specific to common cardiovascular procedures which follows ASA guidelines at any given institution. By determining the most optimal protocols for a specific center's workflow and involving anesthesiologists and cardiologists in the protocol creation a priori, we can achieve standardized, consistent, evidence-based NPO orders prior to procedures.

Involve all key stakeholders

It is also important to realize that many professionals are involved when changing a diet protocol prior to cardiac catheterization. Registered dieticians are experts of optimal nutrition and what patients may tolerate in the hospital, nurses ensure that patients are provided with their meals (or lack thereof), hospitalists may coordinate the overall care of the patient, cardiologists are experts in the procedure taking place, and nurse anesthetists and anesthesiologists ultimately take on any risk of aspiration. Based the success of prior QI projects, it is important to involve a multidisciplinary team of all stakeholders prior to initiating any changes [20]. Once changes are made, solicitation of thoughts regarding a new protocol is paramount to identifying barriers to success and achieving buy in. Ultimately, the decision to alter diet protocols is a collective one and all key stakeholders must feel heard and supported.

Prioritizing clear liquids

Finally, it is important to consider the most important practical barriers to following the ASA guidelines regarding fasting prior to procedures. Both inpatient and outpatient cardiovascular procedures may be delayed or cancelled for several reasons, altering the schedule of the catheterization laboratory, electrophysiology laboratory, or operating room and timing in which patients undergo their procedure [11]. Thus, it is challenging to time solid foods to be within 8 h for a heavy meal and 6 h for a light, nonfatty meal as recommended by the most current ASA guidelines [9]. What defines “heavy” and “light” is also unclear and may be challenging to communicate to patients who are presenting from home for an elective procedure. Making patients completely NPO at midnight ensures that procedural delays are not due to a light or regular diet. Thus, it is most practical to focus efforts on allowing for clear liquids up to two hours prior to a procedure [21]. This approach has been adopted by previous initiatives for other procedures to balance procedural delay with patient satisfaction [20].

Important considerations for patients taking GLP-1 receptor agonists

A newer and confounding consideration for NPO policies is that of patients taking a GLP-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) medication for weight loss or diabetes. This class of drugs causes a marked drug-induced delay in gastric emptying (primarily affecting solids) that is impacted by total GLP-1 RA dose and time of last dose given. The current, and continuously evolving, pre-procedure NPO best practice for the subset of patients taking a GLP-1 RA medication is different. Specifically, it is to be on a clear liquid diet for 24 h pre-procedure with clear liquids allowed up to 4 h pre-procedure as compared to the clear liquids up to 2 h pre-procedure [22]. This approach is in line with safety data from a meta-analysis of over 100,000 GI endoscopy cases which showed no increased incidence of aspiration in patients taking GLP-1 RA who had a clear liquid diet the day before the procedure [23].

In patients undergoing weekly injections of GLP-1 RA medication, the recommendation is to hold the drug the week of the procedure, with patients on daily dosing holding the drug the day of the procedure [24].

Final recommendations

In summary, evidence shows that the ASA guidelines for preoperative fasting – a “heavy” meal up to 8 h, a “light” meal up to 6 h, and clear liquids up to 2 h prior to the procedure – are reasonable to apply to most cardiovascular procedures with the exception of patients taking a GLP-1 RA medication [9]. If possible, clinicians should follow these guidelines and avoid making universal “NPO at midnight” orders. If timing of solid foods prior to a procedure presents too much of a logistical challenge as to significantly impede timeliness of the catheterization laboratory or other procedural locations, it is reasonable to focus on allowing clear liquids two hours prior to the procedure.

These recommendations only apply to patients without known limitations in gastric or esophageal emptying, such as those with diabetic gastroparesis, or esophageal disorders such as esophageal stricture or achalasia. In the case of GLP-1 RA medication the NPO modification is different as described. There are no standardized ASA guidelines for chronic conditions altering gastric emptying or esophageal function, but rather it is recommended to use individualized clinical judgement for each case [8].

Conclusion

Based on current evidence, many of the “NPO before midnight” orders prior to cardiovascular procedures are inappropriate, result in lower patient satisfaction, and carry their own risks that may outweigh risks of aspiration. While institutional culture change is a challenge and procedural schedules are often erratic, clinicians may find success through multidisciplinary initiatives to implement standardized protocols with special focus on allowing clear liquids two hours prior to a scheduled procedure.
