Descriptive analysis of screen‐detected versus clinically diagnosed lung cancer cases: evidence of earlier stage shift and treatment differences
Authors: Jazmin Guayco Sigcha, Tian Mun Chee, Ian A. Yang, Linda Passmore, Kwun M. Fong, Henry M. Marshall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung cancer is the leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide, largely due to late‐stage diagnosis. Lung cancer screening by computed tomography (CT) reduces mortality by detecting cancers earlier in high‐risk individuals, prompting Australia to launch the National Lung Cancer Screening Program (NLCSP) in 2025. Understanding the differences between screen‐detected and clinically diagnosed lung cancers is essential for evaluating the impact of screening.AimsTo compare demographic, clinical and treatment characteristics of screen‐detected versus clinically diagnosed lung cancer cases in Australia and assess evidence of earlier stage shift and treatment differences.MethodsScreen‐detected cases were sourced from the Queensland Lung Cancer Screening Study (QLCSS) and International Lung Screening Trial (ILST), including cancers identified at baseline CT and up to 5 years of follow‐up. Clinically diagnosed cases from the corresponding periods were identified through Queensland Oncology Online. Data included demographics, smoking history, comorbidities, cancer stage and treatments. Wilcoxon rank‐sum, Fisher exact and chi‐squared tests (P < 0.05) were used to compare the groups. Sensitivity analyses included 1:2 case–control and NLCSP‐eligible subgroup analysis.ResultsScreen‐detected cases were more likely to be diagnosed at stage I (63% vs 28%, P adj < 0.01) and underwent surgery (P adj < 0.05), whereas clinically detected cases were mostly stage IV (36% vs 14%) and received more radiotherapy (P adj < 0.001). These differences persisted in sensitivity and subgroup analyses.ConclusionsScreen‐detected lung cancers show a stage shift towards earlier diagnosis and higher surgical treatment rates, supporting the potential effectiveness of the NLCSP.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide, with an estimated 2.2 million new cases (11.4% of cancer diagnoses) and 1.8 million deaths (18% of cancer deaths) in 2020. 1 , 2 The high mortality rate of lung cancer can be attributed to late diagnosis, since it frequently shows non‐specific or no symptoms during its early stages. 3 When detected in its earliest stage (stage IA), individuals with lung cancer have a 5‐year survival rate of 92%, whereas only 10% of individuals with stage IV lung cancer survive longer than 5 years. 4 , 5 Unfortunately, 75% of lung cancers are diagnosed at stages III and IV. 4 Therefore, early detection of lung cancer is crucial to improving management and reducing the mortality rate.

Lung cancer screening (LCS) using computed tomography (CT) has been developed to detect the disease at an early stage before it invades adjacent tissues or causes symptoms, thus increasing the chance of successful treatment. 6 Evidence from large, randomised trials supports the benefits of screening. The National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) in the United States showed that annual low‐dose CT scans reduced the risk of lung cancer death by 20% compared to standard chest radiography. 7 Later, the Dutch–Belgian Randomised Lung Cancer Screening Trial (NELSON) demonstrated a reduction in lung cancer mortality of 24% for individuals screened with low‐dose CT compared to those who did not undergo screening. 8 , 9

In Australia, the launch of the National Lung Cancer Screening Program (NLCSP) on 1 July 2025 represents a major public health initiative aimed at improving early detection outcomes. 10 , 11 However, there is currently no recent national dataset describing stage at presentation; the most recent available data come from 2011. 12 As screening is introduced, it is crucial to understand how screen‐detected lung cancers may differ from those diagnosed through standard clinical pathways. Earlier detection through screening could result in a shift in stage at diagnosis and influence treatment patterns. Understanding these differences is essential to inform health service planning, assess the real‐world impact of the NLCSP and optimise clinical management pathways.

Rationale

Screen‐detected lung cancers are expected to be diagnosed at earlier stages compared to those identified after symptom onset. This difference has significant implications for prognosis and treatment, as early‐stage cancers are more likely to be treated with curative options such as surgical resection. Earlier diagnosis can also improve survival rates, reduce treatment complexity and enhance quality of life.

Although international trials such as NLST and NELSON provide compelling evidence for the benefits of screening, these findings may not fully reflect the Australian context due to differences in population characteristics. 13 Therefore, there is a need to validate these findings within local populations to provide relevant screening expertise and data specific to Australia. As Australia moves towards NLCSP implementation, understanding how screen‐detected cases differ from those diagnosed through standard clinical pathways is critical for assessing the potential impact of screening on population health outcomes.

Methods

The study was approved by the Metro North Research Human Research Ethics Committee (approval ID 99926).

Eligibility criteria, search and data collection

Screen‐detected lung cancers were defined as those identified via CT screening in clinical trials before symptom onset, while clinically detected lung cancers were defined as those diagnosed through standard pathways following symptoms or incidental imaging. Clinical data were eligible if recorded within 60 days before to 365 days after diagnosis, treatment data if administered from 30 days before to 365 days after diagnosis. We excluded duplicate cases and those without a diagnosis date.

Screen‐detected cases were sourced from the Queensland Lung Cancer Screening Study (QLCSS) and the Queensland site of the International Lung Screen Trial (ILST). QLCSS enrolled 256 individuals who met the following eligibility criteria: 60–74 years old, currently smoking or who had quit within the past 15 years, ≥30 pack‐years of smoking history, with forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) ≥ 50% predicted. QLCSS participants received a baseline CT and two annual scans. 14 ILST enrolled 596 individuals who met either of the following eligibility criteria: USPSTF2013 criteria (55–80 years old, currently smoking or who had quit within the past 15 years, ≥30 pack‐years of smoking history) and/or 55–80 years old with PLCOm2012 risk >1.51%/6 years. ILST participants received a baseline CT and a second follow‐up CT after 2 years. 15 Clinically detected cases overlapping diagnosis dates from the above trials (December 2008–January 2013 and May 2017–February 2022) were sourced from Queensland Oncology Online (QOOL).

Extracted variables included demographics (age, gender, ethnicity), clinical characteristics (family history, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)), smoking history (status, cigarettes/day, duration, pack‐years), pathology (cancer type, stage) and treatments (surgery, chemotherapy, radiation).

Data analysis

Sample size was calculated to detect differences in stage I prevalence (assumed 60% screened vs 20% clinical) using chi‐squared tests (α = 0.05, 80% power, 1:2 ratio), requiring 44 screened and 88 clinical cases (G*Power version 3.1.9.7).

Demographic, clinical, smoking, pathology and treatment variables were compared using Wilcoxon rank‐sum, Fisher exact, or chi‐squared tests (P < 0.05). Continuous variables were summarised as median (interquartile range (IQR)). Bonferroni correction was applied across 14 tests (P adj < 0.05). Effect sizes (risk ratio, risk difference) were calculated for significant results.

Sensitivity analyses

To address confounding, we conducted a 1:2 matched case–control analysis, pairing each screen‐detected case with two clinically detected cases based on variables significant in univariate analysis. After matching, we compared stage at diagnosis and treatment patterns. Additionally, a subgroup analysis was performed focusing on participants who met the NLCSP eligibility criteria: aged 50–70, currently smoking or having quit within 10 years and ≥30 pack‐years smoking history.

Results

Summary of search

As shown in Figure 1, our search returned 2435 lung cancer cases for review. After removing 79 duplicate records, 2356 unique cases remained. Subsequently, 602 cases without documented clinical stage were removed, resulting in 1754 lung cancer cases available for analysis. Within this cohort, 1719 cases were clinically detected, and 35 cases were screen‐detected.

Flowchart of clinical and screen‐detected lung cancer cases.

Among these clinical lung cancer cases, 1472 (85.6%) patients presented at least one symptom (bone pain, cough, dyspnoea, haemoptysis, chest pain, other nonspecific symptoms, weight loss, decreased appetite), while 247 patients (14.4%) were recorded as asymptomatic at presentation. Within the asymptomatic group, nine cases were explicitly documented as incidental findings on imaging performed for non‐cancer‐related indications.

Among the screen‐detected cases, 13 were sourced from QLCSS and 22 from ILST. Of the 35 cases included in this study, 18 were prevalent cancers (51.4%), and 17 were incidence cancers (48.6%). For the QLCSS cohort, a total of 256 (100%), 239 (93.4%) and 233 (91%) eligible participants completed assessments at T0, T1 and T2 respectively. Among the 13 lung cancer cases included in our analysis, five were diagnosed at T0, three at T1, four at T2 and one during the follow‐up (non‐screening) phase. For the ILST cohort, 596 (100%) and 533 (89.4%) eligible participants completed assessments at T0 and T2 respectively. From the 22 lung cancer cases included in our analysis, 13 were diagnosed at T0, six at T2 and three during the follow‐up (non‐screening) phase.

Median (IQR) age was 67 (7.5) in the screen‐detected group and 70 (14) in the clinical group. Within the screen‐detected cohort, QLCSS cohort age was 66 (5.5), while ILST cohort age was 68 (7.75). The screen‐detected lung cancer group tended to be younger, with 43% aged under 65 compared to 32% in clinically detected cases (P < 0.05), although this difference was not significant after Bonferroni correction (P adj > 0.05). No significant differences were observed between groups for gender or ethnicity (P adj > 0.05).

A significantly higher proportion of screen‐detected individuals reported a family history of lung cancer (37% vs 9%; P adj < 0.01). The effect size for the primary analysis was risk ratio (RR) = 4.12 (95% confidence interval (CI): 2.61–6.50) and risk difference (RD) = +0.28 (95% CI: 0.14–0.45). Because ‘Unknown’ was common in the clinical group, a sensitivity analysis excluding ‘Unknown’ yielded RR = 0.52 (95% CI: 0.33–0.80) among known values, indicating that missingness materially influenced the magnitude and direction; we therefore interpret the association cautiously. COPD prevalence was higher among screen‐detected cases (60% vs 40%; P < 0.05), but this difference was not significant after adjustment (P adj > 0.05).

Screen‐detected cases had a higher proportion of people who currently smoke (49% vs 30%; P < 0.05), but this difference was not significant after adjustment (P adj > 0.05). Differences in daily cigarette consumption remained significant after Bonferroni correction (P adj < 0.05), where medians (IQR) were 20 (11) in screen‐detected versus 25 (10) in clinical cases. However, heavier smoking (≥30 cigarettes/day) was more common among screen‐detected cases (37% vs 17%), with RR = 2.17 (95% CI: 1.39–3.38) and RD = +0.20 (95% CI: 0.06–0.37), indicating roughly twofold higher prevalence of heavy daily smoking in the screen‐detected group.

Pack‐year exposure was also significantly higher in screen‐detected cases (P adj < 0.001). Medians (IQR) were 54 (27) in screen‐detected versus 40 (33.8) in clinical cases. For the threshold ≥40 pack‐years, 77% of screen‐detected versus 48% of clinical cases met this criterion (RR = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.33–1.94; RD = +0.29, 95% CI: 0.13–0.40), highlighting greater cumulative exposure among screen‐detected cases. The more extreme threshold ≥80 pack‐years showed a smaller and imprecise difference (14% vs 10%; RR = 1.46, 95% CI: 0.64–3.33). Smoking duration (in years) did not differ significantly between groups (P adj > 0.05); medians (IQR) were 44 (11) for screen‐detected and 41 (20) for clinical cases.

Radiotherapy treatments were further categorised by treatment intent (curative, non‐curative or unspecified) and modality. Of the 821 treatments in the clinical group, 434 (52.9%) had curative intent. Most involved external beam radiotherapy (EBRT; n = 375), followed by volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT; n = 45), stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT; n = 5), stereotactic radiosurgery (n = 2) and treatments recorded as curative intent without further specification (n = 7). Radiotherapy use in the screening cohort was minimal by comparison, with three (60%) treatments delivered with curative intent using EBRT, while two (40%) treatments were delivered with non‐curative intent using EBRT.

Matching case–control analysis

To account for potential confounding, a matched analysis was conducted. Screen‐detected cases were matched to clinically detected cases based on key variables identified as significant in the univariate analysis, including age, COPD, smoking status, number of cigarettes per day and pack‐years.

As shown in Table S1, screen‐detected cases were more likely to present at early stage compared to clinical cases (P adj < 0.05). For early stage (I–II) versus late stage (III–IV), 63% of screen‐detected cases presented at early stage compared to 39% of clinical cases, with RR = 1.93 (95% CI: 1.35–2.74), RD = +0.36 (95% CI: +0.16 to +0.51), consistent with a stage shift towards earlier diagnosis in the screen‐detected group. Additionally, no significant differences were observed in cancer type between screen‐detected and clinical cases, with 89% and 79% of cases being NSCLC respectively.

Significant differences were observed across treatment modalities (Table S2). Screen‐detected cases were more likely to undergo surgery (P adj < 0.001), with 71% receiving surgical treatment compared to 29% of clinically detected cases (RR = 2.50, 95% CI: 1.63–3.83; RD = +42.9 pp., 95% CI: 24.5–61.2). Conversely, clinical cases were far more likely to receive radiation therapy (P adj < 0.001), with 61% undergoing radiation compared to 14% of screen‐detected cases (RR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.10–0.53; RD = −0.47, 95% CI: −63 to −31).

Analysis of NLCSP‐eligible participants

A sub‐analysis was conducted focusing exclusively on participants who met the NLCSP inclusion criteria: age 50–70 years, currently smoking or have quit within the past 10 years and a smoking history of ≥30 pack‐years. This refinement yielded 465 clinically detected cases (27% of the clinical cohort) and 20 screen‐detected cases (57% of the screening cohort).

As shown in Table S3, age distribution was similar between groups, with median (IQR) ages of 62.5 (6.3) years for screen‐detected cases and 63 (8) years for clinical cases. Within the NLCSP‐eligible cohort, QLCSS age was 66 (5.5), while ILST was 62 (8). Gender distribution was comparable (55% vs 57% male), and ethnicity was predominantly non‐indigenous in both groups (100% vs 95%). Smoking characteristics, including status, duration, cigarettes per day and pack‐years, did not differ significantly. In contrast, significant differences were observed for family history of lung cancer (P adj < 0.001) and COPD (P adj < 0.05), with screen‐detected cases showing higher rates of family history and lower rates of COPD. Notably, family history data were missing for 88% of clinically detected cases.

Table S4 shows that screen‐detected cancers were strongly associated with earlier stage at diagnosis (P adj < 0.05). Stage I was more common among screen‐detected cases (65%) compared to clinical cases (24%). For the contrast of early versus late stage (0/I/II vs III/IV), the effect size was RR = 2.09 (95% CI: 1.58–2.76) and RD = +0.39 (95% CI: 0.20–0.59), indicating nearly twofold higher likelihood of early‐stage diagnosis in screen‐detected cases. Significant differences were also observed across treatment modalities (Table S5), with screen‐detected cases more likely to undergo surgery (75% vs 32%, P adj < 0.001). The effect size was RR = 2.36, 95% CI: 1.73–3.21; RD = +43.2 pp., 95% CI: 26.8–59.6. Conversely, radiation therapy was more common among clinical cases (P adj < 0.001), with 52% receiving radiation compared to 15% of screen‐detected cases (RR = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.10–0.81; RD = −37.0 pp., 95% CI: −56.5 to −17.5). Chemotherapy was also more frequent in clinical cases (P < 0.01), but this difference was not significant after Bonferroni correction (P adj > 0.05).

Discussion

This study compared the demographic, clinical, pathological and treatment characteristics between lung cancer cases detected through screening (QLCSS and ILST) and those diagnosed clinically in Queensland. Participants with screen‐detected lung cancer were slightly younger than those with clinically detected cancer (median age: 67 vs 70 years), but this difference was not statistically significant after adjustment. This suggests that age differences largely reflect screening eligibility criteria rather than a true demographic disparity. 14 , 15 Family history of lung cancer appeared more common in screen‐detected cases, which may have motivated enrolment in the screening trial. However, interpretation is limited by substantial missing data (88% unknown), which introduces uncertainty and highlights the need for improved data collection.

Tobacco smoking is the primary cause of all major histological subtypes of lung cancer, and its carcinogenic effects have been well established since the 1950s. 16 Although screen‐detected cases had a higher proportion of current smoking status compared to clinically detected cases (49% vs 30%), this was not significant after adjustment, suggesting that smoking status alone may not distinguish these groups. However, differences in smoking intensity remained significant after Bonferroni correction. Screen‐detected participants showed higher heavy smoking rates (≥30 cigarettes/day: 37% vs 17%) and greater cumulative exposure (median pack‐years: 54 vs 40; ≥40 pack‐years: 77% vs 48%). These findings reflect eligibility criteria that prioritise cumulative exposure and suggest that screening programmes effectively target individuals at the highest risk. Smoking duration did not differ significantly between groups, indicating that differences are driven more by intensity and cumulative exposure than by years smoked.

Screen‐detected cases were more likely to be diagnosed at earlier stages, particularly stage I, while clinically detected cases had a broader distribution of stages, with a higher proportion of late‐stage diagnoses (III–IV). This pattern aligns with findings from major trials such as the NLST, where stage I cancers were most common among screened participants. 7 Early‐stage lung cancer is typically asymptomatic and may progress unnoticed, so the observed stage shift reflects the ability of CT screening to identify small, localised tumours before symptoms or metastasis occurs. In contrast, clinically detected cancers are usually diagnosed after symptom onset, when cancer is more advanced and less amenable to curative treatment. 17 This can be confirmed by the clinical cohort, where only 14.4% cancers were asymptomatic. This further highlights the role of screening in early‐stage detection.

Screen‐detected cases were significantly more likely to undergo surgery, whereas clinically detected cases were more likely to receive radiation therapy. This pattern reflects differences in stage at diagnosis, as early detection increases the feasibility of surgical resection, which remains the most effective curative option. In contrast, advanced disease limits surgical eligibility and requires systemic or multimodal treatment approaches. 18 , 19 For stage IIIA, surgery may be considered alongside chemotherapy or radiotherapy. 20 Unresectable stages III and IV rely on chemoradiotherapy or systemic treatments such as chemotherapy, targeted therapy or immunotherapy, aimed at prolonging survival and maintaining quality of life. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Among clinically detected cases who received radiotherapy, treatment was almost evenly divided between curative and non‐curative intent, with EBRT predominating across both categories, underscoring the substantial burden of locally advanced and metastatic disease in this group.

The lack of a significant difference in chemotherapy use likely reflects its role across multiple disease stages. Chemotherapy is used as adjuvant treatment after surgical resection as well as systemic therapy for advanced cases, leading to similar utilisation patterns once the stage is taken into account. 26 Overall, early detection through screening not only expands access to curative surgical options but also reduces reliance on the more intensive multimodal treatments required for advanced disease. This highlights the importance of screening in optimising treatment pathways and improving clinical outcomes.

Matched analysis and NLCSP‐eligible subgroup findings were directionally consistent, reinforcing the earlier stage at diagnosis and higher surgical rates in screen‐detected cases. Higher proportions of positive family history and lower frequency of COPD in the NLCSP subgroup echo literature on screening uptake and risk profiles. 27 Overall, results align with large trials showing that an earlier stage shift underpins treatment benefit. 28 , 29

Differences in participant characteristics between QLCSS, ILST and NLCSP eligible individuals arise from the distinct eligibility criteria used in these screening programmes. QLCSS eligibility applied an age range of 60–74 years, smoking or having quit less than 15 years ago and a minimum FEV1 >50%. ILST applied a broader age range of 55–80 years and also included individuals who smoke or had quit smoking within the past 15 years. Unlike QLCSS, ILST did not specify a minimum lung function threshold and instead required participants to be in generally good health. On the other hand, the NLCSP focuses on a younger age range of 50–70 years and applies a more restrictive limit of smoking or having quit in less than 10 years. NLCSP also requires participants to be asymptomatic at the time of enrolment. These inclusion differences are reflected in the age distributions observed across cohorts. In the main analysis, screen‐detected participants were slightly younger than clinically detected cases, with QLCSS participants younger than ILST due to their specific age thresholds. When restricting analyses to individuals who met NLCSP criteria, 15 screen‐detected cancers were excluded. As a result, the age difference between screen‐detected and clinically detected cases narrowed substantially. This shows that some early‐stage cancers in older participants would not be captured under a narrower population‐based programme that focuses on younger individuals and heavier or more recent smoking history. Furthermore, both screening trials showed high retention across screening rounds; however, low attrition rates may not be representative of real‐world participation within the NLCSP.

Implications

Early detection via screening produces a clinically meaningful stage shift, expanding eligibility for curative surgery and potentially improving survival and quality of life. Health‐system impacts include lower complexity of care, reduced reliance on multimodal therapies and more efficient resource allocation. Findings support targeted screening for high‐risk groups and equitable access to the NLCSP.

Limitations and future directions

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. The screened (n = 35) and clinical (n = 1719) sample sizes were below calculated requirements, reducing power for smaller effects, though key outcomes remained significant during sensitivity analyses. Missing data, particularly for family history, may introduce bias. Finally, survival or mortality outcomes were unavailable, so stage shift serves only as a proxy for prognosis.

Future research should link screening and clinical datasets to survival and mortality, conduct longitudinal follow‐up by stage and treatment and use larger, balanced samples for subgroup analyses. Real‐world NLCSP data will help validate findings nationally. Economic evaluations should assess cost‐effectiveness, and strategies to improve equitable screening uptake are needed.

Conclusion

Our data demonstrate a significant stage shift in screen‐detected lung cancers compared to clinically detected lung cancers, similar to international literature. Detection of early‐stage lung cancer results in higher rates of surgical treatment and reduced reliance on chemotherapy or radiation. Stage shift is a prerequisite for mortality benefit and thus bodes well for NLCSP.
