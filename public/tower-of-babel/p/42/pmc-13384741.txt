Inhibition of Cathepsin C attenuates osteoarthritis by modulating macrophage activated status and reducing tissue inflammatory responses
Authors: Ye Tian, Xinyue Wang, Xinnan Zhao, Shuang Liu, Xiaohan Yang, Gang Liu, ZhiMing Qi, Lina Guo, JianMei Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsOsteoarthritis (OA) is a common degenerative joint disease, with growing evidence suggesting that chronic inflammation plays a critical role in its progression. Targeting chronic inflammation could potentially slow OA development. While Cathepsin C (CatC) has been widely studied in the context of inflammatory diseases, its specific role in OA remains underexplored.MethodsTo address this question, we used clinical samples of OA patients and a murine OA model to clarify the expression of CatC and its role in OA. By further isolating and culturing OA-related primary cells, we aimed to identify the source of CatC and determine its impact on various cell types. In addition, we used CatC knockdown mice and specific CatC inhibitors AZD7986 to verify the impact of CatC inhibition on OA progress.ResultsOur results demonstrated that CatC expression was significantly upregulated in OA synovium, with infiltrated macrophages playing a crucial role in this upregulation. Furthermore, CatC was identified as a key factor in promoting the release of inflammatory mediators by facilitating the polarization of macrophages towards pro-inflammatory activation state. In turn, macrophage-derived CatC exacerbated inflammation and cartilage degradation through paracrine effects on synovial fibroblasts and chondrocytes. Knockdown of CatC or administration of the specific CatC inhibitor AZD7986 mitigates cartilage damage and synovial inflammation by reducing the expression of inflammatory factors and matrix-degrading enzymes.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that macrophage-derived CatC contributes to OA progression through upregulation of inflammatory mediators and MMP13. These findings suggest that CatC may represent a potential therapeutic target warranting further investigation.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(7):903–913.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Cathepsin C, Inflammation, Macrophages, macrophages, Osteoarthritis (OA), chondrocytes, fibroblasts, matrix metalloproteinase 13, inflammation, cartilage degradation, cartilage damage, synovial inflammation, inflammatory diseases

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA), the most prevalent joint disorder worldwide, affects 10% to 18% of symptomatic adults aged ≥ 60 years.1,2 Much higher radiological prevalence has been observed in China, reaching 62.2% among adults aged ≥ 60 years and over 80% in those aged ≥ 75 years.3 OA is now understood as a whole-joint pathology encompassing cartilage degradation, synovitis, osteophyte formation, and subchondral sclerosis. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) treatments primarily alleviate symptoms but may cause adverse effects, with no disease-modifying drugs available. For advanced OA, joint arthroplasty enhances function but risks complications, revisions, and significant expenses.4,5 Thus, a comprehensive understanding of OA pathogenesis is crucial for the development of effective new therapeutic strategies.

OA arises from multiple risk factors, with inflammation emerging as a central driver.6,7 Synovitis, which frequently precedes the structural degradation of articular cartilage, is characterized by synovial hyperplasia and diffuse or perivascular infiltration.8 The synovium contains two main cell types: macrophages and fibroblasts. After injury, macrophages, which are the predominant immune cells in synovium, activate into a pro-inflammatory state, releasing inflammatory mediators to trigger immune responses.9 Synovial fibroblasts secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines (interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β), IL-6) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), driving inflammation and cartilage degradation.10 During OA progression, activated macrophages secrete inflammatory mediators that amplify synovial fibroblast and chondrocyte catabolism, accelerating extracellular matrix degradation.11-13 While inflammation represents a promising therapeutic target in OA, the precise mechanisms remain incompletely understood.14-16 Thus, understanding these mechanisms is crucial for the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

Cathepsins, a class of lysosomal proteases, have been well documented in their association with OA, with cathepsins B/S elevated in cartilage and cathepsin K upregulated across osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and synoviocytes in OA patients.17,18 Cathepsin C (CatC) is essential for activating neutrophil serine proteases, and plays a crucial role in various inflammatory and immune responses mediated by neutrophils.19 CatC dysregulation is clinically linked to multiple inflammatory disorders, such as rheumatoid arthritis and pneumonia.20-24 Additionally, previous research has also highlighted the association between CatC and neuroinflammation.25,26 Although existing literature confirms the significant role of CatC in various inflammatory diseases, its involvement in OA has been relatively underexplored. In the present study, we provide the first evidence that CatC is downregulated in OA cartilage but upregulated in the synovium of both OA patients and mice models. Furthermore, we demonstrated that macrophage-derived CatC promotes the production of inflammatory factors through autocrine signalling and exacerbates inflammation and cartilage degradation via paracrine signalling. Inhibiting CatC alleviates OA-associated pathological changes. Collectively, these findings highlight a novel role for CatC in OA pathogenesis and suggests its potential as an effective therapeutic target for OA.

Methods

Human samples

Human articular tissue was collected from 15 knee OA patients who underwent total knee arthroplasty surgery. The OA group was defined as areas with macroscopically severe cartilage wear, accompanied by vascular proliferation and synovial tissue hyperplasia. In contrast, ‘relatively healthy’ control tissue was defined as areas with macroscopically smooth cartilage and synovium with a smooth surface and no obvious synovial hyperplasia. Due to severe cartilage wear in some samples, which precluded adequate tissue harvesting or histological assessment, the final number of samples available for analysis was as follows: OA cartilage (n = 8), OA synovium (n = 9), relatively healthy cartilage (n = 7), and relatively healthy synovium (n = 7). The articular tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for histological analysis and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA and protein extraction. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second People’s Hospital of Dalian, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to surgery.

Animals and model establishment

CatC knockdown (CatC KD) mice were generated using the CatC FAST system, with CatC STOP-tetO mice (CatCSTOP−tetO/+, C57BL/6 N background) kindly provided by Professor Kazuhiro Ikenaka.27 C57BL/6 mice were provided by our university. Genotyping was performed as described previously.28 Both wild-type (WT) and CatC KD mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment under a 12-hour light-dark cycle, with unrestricted access to food and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

For the CatC expression and functional studies involving WT and CatC KD mice, the sample size was determined as the minimum number required to observe statistically stable trends, guided by practical considerations for these exploratory investigations. At eight weeks postoperatively, joint tissues were collected from three WT and three CatC KD mice per group (OA and sham control) for subsequent analysis. For the subsequent AZD7986 administration experiments, group sizes (n = 10 per group) were chosen based on estimates to detect biologically meaningful effect sizes; formal power analysis was not performed due to the absence of prior effect size data in this context.

Prior to surgery, eligible animals aged eight to ten weeks were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence, with allocation concealment maintained until the time of surgery. Animals that died due to surgical complications or treatment administration during the study period were excluded from the final analysis; in this study, one mouse in the AZD7986-treated group died postoperatively and was excluded. Individual data points were omitted only in cases where technical failure during tissue processing (e.g. sectioning or staining artifacts) precluded valid statistical assessment, and no other exclusion criteria were applied.

Blinding was maintained throughout the study to minimize bias. Group allocation was performed by a researcher not involved in animal handling or data collection (YT). Surgical procedures and drug administrations were conducted by a second investigator (XW) who was unaware of group assignments. Outcome assessments, including histological scoring, micro-CT analysis, and IHC quantification, were performed independently by a third researcher who was blinded to the experimental groups (XZ). The blinding code was not broken until all data had been collected and analyzed.

The surgical procedure for the anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) model followed protocols described previously.29 For OA group, ACLT was performed via a knee capsule incision to induce joint instability after anaesthetizing, while sham controls received a capsule incision only. For exogenous intervention, WT ACLT mice were randomly assigned into the treatment group and the vehicle group. Mice received intraperitoneal injections of 3 mg/kg AZD7986 (MCE, USA) or vehicle daily for eight weeks, starting postoperatively. At eight weeks postoperatively, joint tissues were collected from all groups for micro-CT, histological, and immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis.

Micro-CT

Knee joints were examined using micro-CT. Briefly, all samples were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) after fixation in 4% PFA and scanned using a Siemens Inveon MM system (Germany). The scanning parameters were as follows: voltage 80 kV, current 500 μA, and isotropic pixel size 30 μm. Image acquisition and 3D reconstruction were performed using the Inveon Acquisition Workplace (Siemens, v. 4.7). The acquired DICOM data were imported into Mimics 21.0 software (Materialise, Belgium) for further reconstruction and quantitative analysis. A uniform grayscale threshold of 226 was applied to all samples to distinguish mineralized tissue from background. For region of interest (ROI) definition, the femur and tibia were digitally removed from the 3D-reconstructed model to isolate the calcified meniscus and synovium, which were retained as separate ROIs for volumetric quantification. Within each ROI, the total volume (mm³) was automatically calculated by the software.

Cell culture and treatment

Primary chondrocytes and synovial fibroblasts were isolated from the dissected cartilage and synovium following established protocols.30,31 Tissues were PBS-rinsed, trypsin-EDTA (0.25%) pre-treated for 20 minutes, then digested overnight at 37°C in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 1% penicillin and streptomycin (P/S, Hyclone, USA) with: 2 mg/mL collagenase II (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for chondrocytes and 2.5 mg/mL collagenase I (Sigma) for synovial fibroblasts. RAW264.7 (ATCC, USA) was used as macrophages. Cells were cultured at 37°C/5% CO₂ with medium changes every two to three days. First-passage chondrocytes and fourth-passage synovial fibroblasts were used for experiments.

Cell treatment

To establish an in vitro inflammatory model, RAW264.7 cells were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 100 ng/mL, Beyotime, China) for 24 hours, while interleukin-1β (IL-1β; 10 ng/mL, Beyotime) was used to treat chondrocytes and synovial fibroblasts for 24 hours. To investigate the role of CatC in macrophages, RAW264.7 cells were co-treated with LPS and AZD7986 (10 μM) for 24 hours, followed by cell collection for subsequent experiments.

Immunofluorescence staining

Chondrocytes and synovial fibroblasts on coverslips were fixed (4% PFA, 20 mins), blocked (30 mins), and incubated with following primary antibodies at 4°C overnight: Rabbit anti-Mouse Collagen II (1:800; Proteintech, China) and Rabbit anti-Mouse Vimentin (1:200; Abcam, UK). Then 488 Alexafluor (1:100; Abcam), or 594 Alexafluore (1:100; Abcam) secondary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature for one hour. Nuclear staining was performed using DAPI (1:1,000; Sigma-Aldrich). Images were captured by laser confocal microscopy (Olympus, Japan).

qRT-PCR

Quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed according to Liu et al.32 Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cells using TRlzol reagent (Takara, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). PrimeScriptTM RT reagent kit (Takara) was used for cDNA production. qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green PCR master mix (Transgene, China) and analyzed with Mx3000P detection system (Agilent, USA). GAPDH was used as the internal standard reference, and normalized expressions of targeted genes were calculated using the comparative CT method and fold changes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.33 All primers used in this study are designed by Takara Bio (China) and listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot analysis

Protein lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer (KeyGEN, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay (KeyGEN). Equal amounts (20 μg) of denatured proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (NC). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA and probed with primary antibodies (4°C overnight) followed by fluorescent secondary antibody (1:10,000, 2 hrs RT). Signal detection was performed using an Odyssey CLx imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences, USA). The primary antibodies used included: Goat anti-Mouse CatC (1:200; R&D System, USA), Goat anti-Human CatC (1:200; R&D Systems), Goat anti-Mouse MMP13 (1:800; Proteintech), Rabbit anti-Mouse collagen II (1:1,000; Proteintech), and rabbit anti-GAPDH (1:5,000; Proteintech).

Tissue preparation and histological analysis

Tissues were fixed in 4% PFA (24 hours, 4°C), decalcified in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4, 2 weeks), and paraffin-embedded for 5 μm sagittal sectioning. Sections were stained with Safranin O and Fast Green (Safranine-O; Solarbio, China) and haematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Solarbio). Cartilage damage (Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) score) and synovitis scores were assessed as previously described.34,35

IHC staining

IHC staining was performed as described by Ma et al.36 Briefly, after antigen retrieval, sections were blocked with 5% BSA (1 hour) and incubated with following primary antibodies (4°C overnight): Goat anti-Human CatC (1:40), Goat anti-Mouse CatC (1:40), and Rabbit anti-Mouse MMP13 (1:200). Followed by biotinylated secondary antibody (1:200, Vector Laboratories Inc., USA) for two hours at room temperature, sections were imaged using a Nikon microscope system (Nikon Eclipse 80i; Japan). For semi-quantitative analysis of immunostaining, randomly selected microscopic fields within the ROIs (cartilage and synovium) were captured from each mouse sample. The number of positively stained chondrocytes and the average optical density (AOD) of the synovium were quantified using ImageJ analysis software (National Institutes of Health, USA). In each field, chondrocyte positivity was expressed as the percentage of positive cells relative to the total cell number. The average percentage across all fields was then calculated for each sample. Synovial immunoreactivity was assessed by mean AOD per field, and values for each sample were normalized to the control group for comparison.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, USA). For descriptive statistics, continuous data are presented as the mean and standard error of the mean (SEM) in the text to summarize the central tendency and variability of the data. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Differences between two groups were evaluated using an independent-samples two-tailed t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test for pairwise comparisons to control the family-wise error rate. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To provide estimates of effect size and precision for the primary outcomes, particularly considering the variations in group sizes, these results are presented in figures as individual values with the mean and 95% CIs, as indicated in the figure legends. For all other outcomes, data are presented in figures as mean (SEM).

Results

CatC expression

Safranin-O and fast green staining were firstly performed on cartilage samples, and cartilage damage was evaluated using the OARSI scoring system. As shown in the top panel of Figure 1a, cartilage of OA patients showed reduced proteoglycan staining (red) and increased fast green uptake, reflecting matrix loss, with elevated OARSI scores (mean difference = 0.9111, 95% CI 2.074 to 3.029;p < 0.001, independent-samples t-test) confirming severe degeneration. Simultaneously, significant synovial hyperplasia and inflammatory cell infiltration were observed in OA patients, with markedly elevated pathological synovitis scores (mean difference = 0.81, 95% CI 1.966 to 3.477; p < 0.001, independent-samples t-test), indicating synovial inflammation coexists with cartilage damage (Figure 1a). Interestingly, WB analysis revealed that compared to control group, CatC expression was reduced in OA cartilage but increased in OA synovium (Figure 1b). Furthermore, IHC staining on synovium tissues results illustrated a significantly increased CatC expression in OA tissues, and a higher number of CatC-positive macrophage-like cells were identified in OA tissues (Figure 1c). The results of qRT-PCR were consistent with the protein expression data. Meanwhile, inflammatory factors such as IL6, CCL2, and CXCL2, as well as MMP9 and MMP13 associated with OA pathological changes, were observed to be upregulated in OA synovium (Figure 1d).

To further validate our findings, an ACLT-induced OA mouse model was established. Consistent with the data from OA patient tissue, IHC staining and the semi-quantitative assessments confirmed the downregulation of CatC in cartilage and upregulation in synovium of OA mice (Figure 1e). Taken together, these findings suggested that CatC is downregulated in the cartilage but increased in the synovium of OA patients and mice, potentially implicating CatC in synovial inflammation.

Macrophages contributed to the upregulation of CatC in OA synovium

Primary chondrocytes and synovial fibroblasts isolated from WT mouse cartilage and synovium, along with RAW264.7 cells, were used to identify the cellular source of upregulated CatC in OA synovium. Successful isolation was confirmed by IF staining, which positive expression of vimentin in synovial fibroblasts and Collagen II in chondrocytes were showed (Figure 2a). The primary cells were stimulated with IL-1β to establish an OA-mimicking inflammatory microenvironment. Results of qRT-PCR revealed that synovial fibroblasts exhibited upregulation of inflammatory markers (IL6, iNOS, CCL2, CXCL2) and catabolic enzymes (MMP9/13), while chondrocytes showed reduced expression of anabolic factors (Collagen II, aggrecan (Acan)) alongside increased catabolic and inflammatory mediators (CCL2, CXCL2, MMP9/13), both patterns reproducing OA pathological phenotypes (Figure 2b). We next examined CatC expression in primary cells with or without IL-1β treatment. While synovial fibroblasts showed no significant changes in CatC expression at either the protein (WB) or messenger RNA (mRNA) (qRT-PCR) level following IL-1β stimulation (Figure 2c), chondrocytes exhibited marked downregulation of CatC, as evidenced by both WB and qRT-PCR analyses (Figure 2d). Chondrocyte CatC downregulation correlated with reduced CatC expression in OA cartilage. However, the persistent CatC expression in synovial fibroblasts suggested that elevated synovial CatC originates from non-fibroblast cell populations. OA synovium exhibited macrophage infiltration. Given that previous studies have indicated that CatC expression is upregulated in LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells,37 we also confirmed increased CatC gene expression in these cells following LPS treatment (Figure 2e). Collectively, these data demonstrate that M1 macrophages constitute the cellular source of upregulated CatC in OA synovium.

Inhibition of CatC alleviated the progression of OA

To further investigate the role of upregulated CatC in OA synovium, an ACLT mouse model was established using CatC KD mice. While WT-ACLT mice exhibited characteristic OA histopathology, CatC-deficient mice showed milder cartilage damage with less loss of proteoglycan staining (Figure 3a). Decreased OARSI score confirmed that downregulation of CatC expression mitigated cartilage damage in OA mice (Figure 3b). H&E staining demonstrated that CatC inhibition significantly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue hyperplasia in the OA models of CatC KD mice compared to WT mice (Figure 3c) with significantly decreased synovitis score (Figure 3d). Notably, under non-inflammatory conditions, CatC KD and WT sham groups showed comparable proteoglycan preservation and synovial thickness (Figures 3a and 3c), suggesting that CatC inhibition does not affect normal joint components or morphology. These findings demonstrate that the upregulation of CatC in the synovium is associated with the progression of OA, and inhibition of CatC expression can alleviate the progression of OA.

CatC inhibitors

Our findings demonstrate that macrophage-derived CatC promotes OA progression, as its inhibition attenuated disease severity. To investigate the mechanism, we treated macrophages with the selective CatC inhibitor AZD7986. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that AZD7986 reversed LPS-induced upregulation of IL-1β and IL-6 (Figure 4a), indicating that CatC inhibition suppresses M1 macrophage activation. To investigate the effects of macrophage-derived CatC on synovial fibroblasts and chondrocytes, we treated these cells with conditioned medium (CM) from LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells, with or without the CatC inhibitor AZD7986 (Figure 4b). Compared to LPS-CM alone, AZD7986 co-treatment significantly reduced the gene expression of IL-1β, IL-6, and MMP13 in synovial fibroblasts (Figure 4c). WB analysis confirmed MMP13 downregulation at the protein level (Figure 4d). These results suggested that CatC inhibition in inflammatory conditions attenuated inflammatory responses of synovial fibroblast, reducing MMP13-mediated cartilage degradation. In chondrocytes, AZD7986 treatment significantly downregulated IL-6 and iNOS expression versus LPS-CM controls (Figure 4e). While MMP13 mRNA showed a decreasing trend ( p = 0.284, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD post hoc test), its protein levels remained unchanged (Figure 4f). Our results demonstrate that CatC inhibition attenuates OA progression via dual mechanisms: suppressing M1 macrophage activation, and protecting synovial fibroblasts and chondrocytes from inflammatory damage.

Pharmacological blockade of CatC

We next assessed the therapeutic efficacy of AZD7986 in ACLT-induced OA mice. Micro-CT analysis demonstrated that AZD7986 treatment significantly attenuated synovial and meniscal calcification (mean difference = 0.68, 95% CI 0.02272 to 0.3577; p = 0.021) in OA mice compared to untreated controls, indicative of disease-modifying effects (Figure 5a). AZD7986 treatment significantly preserved cartilage proteoglycans (Safranin O/Fast Green; Figure 5b) with reduced OARSI scores (mean difference = 0.646, 95% CI: 0.7074 to 3.415; p = 0.001) (Figure 5c), while attenuating synovial inflammation (H&E staining; Figure 5d) and lowering synovitis scores (mean difference = 0.858, 95% CI 0.07811 to 1.426; p = 0.024) (Figure 5e) versus vehicle controls. IHC results showed that AZD7986 treatment reduced MMP13 expression in cartilage and synovium, attenuating cartilage degradation (Figure 5f). These findings identify CatC as a promising therapeutic target for OA, with pharmacological inhibition effectively mitigating disease progression.

Discussion

OA progression involves pathological crosstalk among joint tissues, where macrophage-derived pro-inflammatory signals critically drive synovial fibroblast and chondrocyte dysfunction. In this work, we systematically elucidate the pathogenic functions of CatC in OA, revealing its critical involvement in synovitis regulation and strong potential as a therapeutic target.

During OA progression, synovitis-mediated activation of M1 macrophages exacerbates disease pathogenesis through sustained release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby serving as primary effectors of cartilage matrix degradation and joint functional impairment.38 Importantly, our study highlighted the critical role of CatC in regulating macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses during OA. The pro-inflammatory function of CatC in macrophage polarization has been firmly established in our previous work, which demonstrated that CatC facilitates M1 macrophage activation via multiple signalling pathways, including the FAK-triggered p38 MAPK/NF-κB pathway and the Ca²+-dependent PKC/p38 MAPK/NF-κB pathway.25,39,40 Tthe NF-κB pathway critically drives OA progression through dual mechanisms: activation of cartilage-degrading enzymes, and regulation of pro-inflammatory cytokine/chemokine networks.41 This may partially explain the CatC-mediated OA progression observed in our study, though further investigation is required to elucidate the underlying precise mechanisms.42 In the present study, we focused on investigating the therapeutic potential of CatC inhibition with AZD7986 in preventing OA progression, aiming to explore the translational and clinical significance of this approach.

We acknowledge that our mechanistic studies were primarily conducted in RAW264.7 macrophages which, despite offering a well-established model, do not fully reproduce human macrophage biology. To address this limitation, we verified consistency across multiple experimental systems, demonstrating that the pro-inflammatory role of CatC observed in RAW264.7 cells aligns with findings in primary murine macrophages and human OA cartilage samples, suggesting that the mechanism may be conserved across species. Nevertheless, future studies using primary human macrophages (e.g., peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)-derived) are warranted to confirm whether the identified pathways (including regulation of IL-1β, IL-6, and MMPs) are conserved in human cells, and to further validate the translational relevance of CatC inhibition for OA therapy.

While the expression and regulatory role of CatC in multiple pathologies are well documented in immune cells, its biological significance in non-immune cell populations remains poorly understood. Notably, our data reveal a cell-type-specific CatC expression pattern in OA: synovial fibroblasts preserve constitutive CatC expression, whereas chondrocytes display marked downregulation. The functional consequences of chondrocytic CatC downregulation warrant dedicated future study, as this aspect was not the primary focus of the present work. In the research studying Helicobacter pylori infection, gastric epithelial as a non-immune cell also showed a downregulation of CatC which impaired neutrophil activation.43 Analogously, reduced CatC in OA cartilage may represent a protective response against tissue damage. It should be noted that co-staining with a chondrocyte marker would provide more precise cellular localization. We acknowledge this as a limitation of the current study, and future investigations employing co-localization techniques are warranted to further validate the cell-type-specific expression patterns of CatC in OA cartilage. The mechanisms underlying require further analysis.

Intercellular crosstalk among macrophages, synovial fibroblasts, and chondrocytes plays a pivotal role in OA progression. Emerging evidence suggests that phenotypic alterations in pro-inflammatory macrophages critically influence pathological manifestations in chondrocytes and synovial fibroblasts, highlighting the multicellular complexity underlying OA pathogenesis.44,45 Our experimental findings also corroborate this paradigm, demonstrating that targeted inhibition of macrophage-derived CatC effectively alleviates pathological alterations in both chondrocytes and synovial fibroblasts. This observation implies that CatC exerts pleiotropic regulatory effects, functioning not only through direct actions on target cells but also via microenvironmental modulation of adjacent cellular compartments. Understanding intercellular signalling communication in OA enhances our comprehension of OA progression. While these data suggest CatC’s potential involvement in orchestrating intercellular communication networks, the precise mechanistic underpinnings remain to be systematically elucidated through further investigation.

It should be noted that our immediate postoperative dosing regimen models an early intervention for high-risk patients (e.g. post-ACL injury), not a treatment for established OA. This approach addresses the need for disease-modifying strategies in younger populations where current options are limited. Our findings suggest that early CatC inhibition during the active degenerative phase may slow cartilage degradation in these at-risk patients. We acknowledge that a delayed-start cohort – initiating treatment after OA establishment – would better reflect a therapeutic intervention for the broader OA population. Although beyond the scope of this study, such designs represent a critical direction for future research to evaluate the efficacy of CatC inhibition in modifying established disease.46

In conclusion, this study identifies CatC as a pivotal mediator of OA pathogenesis, driving both macrophage activation and synovial-chondrocyte catabolism. These findings establish CatC as a promising therapeutic target.
