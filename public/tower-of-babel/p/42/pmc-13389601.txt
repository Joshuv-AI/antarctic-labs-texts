Contributions of intra- and extracellular antibiotic degradation to collective β-lactam survival
Authors: Rotem Gross, Muhittin Mungan, Suman G. Das, Melih Yüksel, Berenike Maier, Tobias Bollenbach, J. Arjan G. M. de Visser, Joachim Krug
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAntibiotic resistance threatens our ability to treat common diseases like lung and urinary tract infections. While resistance is traditionally attributed to individual bacteria, populations often survive antibiotics through collective strategies. We studied how Escherichia coli resists treatment with the widely used broad-spectrum antibiotic cefotaxime. We found that survival depends on an enzyme that breaks down the antibiotic, acting both inside intact cells and outside when cells burst and release the enzyme to the environment. By combining experiments with mathematical modeling, we predict how these processes conspire to allow bacterial populations to recover and cancel the antibiotic effect. Surprisingly, our results demonstrate that cell death may contribute to antibiotic resistance, with important implications for treatment strategies.

The concept of antibiotic resistance extends far beyond the modern clinical use of antibiotics (1). Resistance genes have been identified in pristine settings, including the Alaskan soil and ocean floor sediments (2, 3), and β-lactamases, key enzymes responsible for resistance against β-lactam antibiotics, are thought to have originated billions of years ago (4, 5). While the roles of antimicrobials and the associated resistance mechanisms in natural ecosystems are unclear, their wide distribution and ancient origins indicate that they may have evolved as a defense against resource competitors and predators (6).

In the clinical context, β-lactam antibiotics are the most widely prescribed class of antimicrobial compounds used to treat a variety of infectious diseases (7). Correspondingly, β-lactam resistance through degradation by β-lactamases has been a major clinical concern for decades (4, 8–11). The enzymatic breakdown occurs in the periplasm of gram-negative bacteria, but it can also take place extracellularly if the enzyme is excreted or released from lysing cells (8, 12). Both modes of degradation reduce the extracellular concentration of the drug, which makes β-lactamase production a paradigm of a public good that benefits producing as well as nonproducing cells (13–21), and causes β-lactam resistance to be a collective property of the bacterial population (22, 23).

Previous work on the social effects of β-lactamase production has mostly considered communities of bacterial strains (12, 13, 17, 24, 25), focusing on the interaction between producers (“helpers”) and nonproducers (“beneficiaries”) (14, 16, 21). Studies of collective β-lactamase-induced resistance of isogenic populations have been limited to global inferences about the establishment of colonies from single cells (26–28) or the inoculum effect, where the minimal inhibitory drug concentration (MIC) increases with cell density (15, 20, 22, 26, 29). Another phenomenon that has been reported in experimental studies with isogenic populations dating back at least 50 y, is complex growth dynamics at near-lethal β-lactam concentrations, where a transient increase in biomass is followed by a decline and a subsequent recovery (8, 24, 30–35). However, a mechanistic understanding of these dynamics has only recently begun to emerge.

Here, we combine experiments and modeling to analyze the contributions of periplasmic and extracellular (after the release from lysed cells) β-lactamase-mediated antibiotic degradation to the survival of isogenic populations of Escherichia coli in liquid culture in the presence of cefotaxime, a third-generation cephalosporin. We will experimentally demonstrate, that the complex growth dynamics arise from an interplay of transient tolerance due to the formation of filamentous cells and breakdown of the antibiotic by AmpC, a chromosomally encoded β-lactamase (36) known to contribute to β-lactam resistance (37). Filamentation is a common morphological response to β-lactam antibiotics (38, 39), which reflects the mode of action of these drugs. β-lactams interfere with the synthesis of bacterial cell walls through binding to specific proteins called penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs). Cefotaxime interferes primarily with PBP3, which plays a central role in the divisome, the protein complex involved in cell division. Inhibition of PBP3 obstructs cell division and induces the formation of filamentous bacterial cells (24, 25, 40, 41).

Filamentation delays cell lysis, which leads to a transient increase of biomass by the elongation of filaments at constant cell number (42–45). At the onset of lysis of filamentous cells, the population initially collapses, but over a broad range of drug concentrations, the decline is followed by a subsequent phase of recovery. Previous work in which similar dynamics were observed has attributed the recovery to the breakdown of the antibiotic by extracellular β-lactamase released by lysing cells (32). However, enzymatic degradation also occurs intracellularly in the periplasm of intact cells, and the relative contributions of the two modes of breakdown are not known. Disentangling the effects of intra- and extracellular enzymatic activity is important for predicting the impact of β-lactamase inhibitors against β-lactamase producing bacteria (46, 47), as well as for assessing the extent of cross-protection of β-lactam susceptible bacteria in polymicrobial infections (21).

In this work, we develop a detailed kinetic model of the intra- and extracellular enzymatic breakdown of cefotaxime, which we couple to the production and decay of biomass obtained from experimental growth curves. We validate the model by comparing predicted and observed recovery times, and use it to estimate the relative contributions of intra- and extracellular breakdown over the time course of the experiment. Experiments are conducted with two common laboratory strains of E. coli that differ substantially in the contribution of intra- and extracellular β-lactam hydrolysis to the recovery kinetics. A theoretical analysis identifies the conditions under which intra- and extracellular hydrolysis are expected to occur, and points to a generic tradeoff between the private benefit of intracellular β-lactamase activity and the contribution to collective survival by the release of the enzyme through lysis.

Results

Growth of E. coli in the Presence of Cefotaxime (CTX) Shows Complex Dynamics Associated with Filamentation, Death, and Recovery.

Growth curves of E. coli strain REL606 in the presence of increasing CTX concentrations show qualitative differences (Fig. 1A). For low CTX concentrations, biomass increases monotonically, saturating at large times. For concentrations between 0.042μg/ml and 0.262μg/ml, the curves exhibit nonmonotonic behavior: The initial growth phase is followed by a decay of biomass until around 15h, before a recovery phase with the resumption of growth sets in. Finally, at even higher concentrations the initial growth is followed by a monotonic decay of biomass, and the populations do not consistently recover. Fig. 1B summarizes the growth, decay, and recovery rates in these three phases as a function of initial CTX concentration (see SI Appendix for details).

To elucidate the origins of these complex growth dynamics, we first analyzed samples from different time points of populations growing at 0.08 μg CTX/ml under the microscope (Fig. 1 C and D). Until biomass peak P, cells become increasingly elongated into filaments. Between time points P and Q, when the biomass is decaying, the number of elongated cells decreases in consecutive images. Around t=14 h (point Q in panel C), the recovery phase sets in. Already at this time, normal-size cells are visible, which by t=17.5 h have taken over the whole population.

The close similarity of the replicate growth curves measured at the same CTX concentration in Fig. 1 A and C makes it unlikely that spontaneous mutations cause the recovery of growth at intermediate CTX concentrations. Regrowth experiments with 80 replicate cultures essentially rule out this possibility (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), which was confirmed by whole genome sequencing (SI Appendix).

Growth Recovery Can Be Explained by Filaments Resuming Cell Division When CTX Is Removed.

We hypothesize that the resumption of cell division during recovery is due to the reduction of CTX in the medium. To see whether normal size cells survive CTX stress or filamentous cells resume cell division, we observed cell growth in a microfluidic chamber. We first treated the bacterial population with a standard LB medium containing 0.2μg CTX/ml (causing nonmonotonic growth) for 2.5 h, followed by the same growth medium without CTX for 20 h (Fig. 2A). By observing one or a few cells per field of view over time, we were able to follow their response to the removal of CTX. As expected, we found that during the initial flow with CTX, the cells formed filaments (Fig. 2B). About 50 min after the end of the treatment with CTX, we observed that filamented cells started to divide again (Fig. 2C and Movie S1). We, therefore, conclude that the transition from growth decay to growth recovery in Fig. 1A is due to the onset of division of filamented cells. In agreement with ref. 48, we observe that the resumption of cell division of the filaments can occur along any septum and there is no preference for budding off normal-sized cells from the tips of filaments.

Spent Medium from CTX-Treated Cultures Accelerates Growth Resumption.

The microfluidic chamber experiment suggests that growth recovery in the batch culture experiments (Fig. 1) is due to the removal of CTX by the growing cells. To determine the remaining amount of CTX at the onset of the recovery phase in the batch cultures, we designed three spent medium experiments (SI Appendix). In each experiment, we grew E. coli REL606 for 14 h in LB medium at a reference concentration of 0.08μg CTX/ml, which corresponds to the lower range of concentrations for which we see filamentation and complex growth (Fig. 1C). We then filtered out the cells and mixed the resulting spent medium with freshly made LB medium consisting of nutrients and a prescribed amount of CTX. The amount of CTX in the added fresh medium was adjusted such that, under the assumption that all CTX has been removed in the spent medium fraction, the CTX concentration in the mixture reaches a prescribed nominal value [CTX]m. If not all CTX had been removed from the spent medium fraction by the bacteria, the actual concentration in the mixed medium would thus exceed [CTX]m.

In the first experiment, growth in the absence and presence of 45% of spent medium was compared for four [CTX]m (Fig. 3A). Whereas in the absence of CTX, spent medium does not affect growth as expected, the spent medium unexpectedly enhanced growth in the presence of CTX, indicating protection beyond the complete removal of CTX in the spent medium fraction. The second experiment was a repetition of the first experiment, but with more variation in the spent medium fraction ϕ, nominal concentrations [CTX]m, and using sonication during the preparation of the spent medium to maximize the release of protective agent from the cells. The area under the growth curves (AUC) shows a negative impact of spent medium in the absence of CTX, presumably due to nutrient depletion, but for CTX concentrations above the concentration used to produce the spent medium, growth increases systematically with spent medium fraction (Fig. 3B). We also demonstrated the protectiveness of the spent medium on agar medium (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). In the third experiment, we asked whether the protective effect of spent medium is sensitive to heat, which would suggest it is conferred by a protein. To test this, the spent medium was subjected to a heat treatment prior to the mixing with fresh medium. The growth curves show that heating the spent medium reduces its protection ability in the presence of CTX (Fig. 3C), but has no effect in fresh medium (Fig. 3D), suggesting that a protein secreted by the bacteria is the cause of protection.

AmpC β-Lactamase Expression Accelerates Growth Recovery in the Presence of CTX.

Based on the results presented in the preceding section, it appears that the protective effect of the spent medium is related to a protein released by the cells. A likely candidate is the β-lactamase AmpC, since REL606 does not contain plasmids and AmpC is the only chromosomally encoded enzyme with β-lactamase activity (https://ecoliwiki.org/colipedia/index.php/Category:Gene_List:REL606).

To test this hypothesis, we performed a parallel set of experiments using E. coli K12 BW25113 strains, for two reasons. First, the availability of the ASKA (49) and Keio (50) strain collections for K12 BW25113 (see SI Appendix for details) allowed us to vary the expression level of ampC (ASKA) or knock out the gene (Keio), which K12 expresses at a basal level like REL606 (36, 51). Second, chemical rate constants and other parameters affecting the hydrolysis of CTX by K12’s AmpC have been determined (52, 53), which implies that we can link the delay times marking the onset of the recovery phase to the reaction kinetics of CTX hydrolyzation by quantitative modeling.

Growth behavior of E. coli K12 JW0333 ΔlacA (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) is qualitatively similar to REL606 (Fig. 1A), except that nonmonotonic growth sets in at a fivefold lower CTX concentration and the transient decay of biomass is shallower, suggesting lower lysis rates. In order to assess the effect of AmpC expression on growth recovery, we used strain JW4111 from the ASKA collection. This strain contains ampC under an inducible promoter on a multicopy plasmid (in addition to its copy in the chromosome), allowing regulated overexpression by varying the concentration of inducer IPTG in the medium. We grew JW4111 from the ASKA collection with three different concentrations of IPTG for 24 h without CTX, filtered out the cells, mixed the remaining spent medium with fresh medium and then let the bacteria of the knockout strain ΔampC JW4111 from the Keio collection grow in the mixed medium with a CTX concentration gradient. Expression of AmpC in the spent medium strongly accelerates growth recovery and causes complete protection for the highest expression level at CTX concentrations far beyond concentrations that are lethal in the absence of spent medium (Fig. 4A). Additionally we grew strain JW4111 from the ASKA collection in a CTX gradient with three different concentrations of IPTG, which enabled us to quantify the effect of AmpC expression on the growth of the strain itself (SI Appendix, Fig. S4).

(A) The three panels show growth curves of E. coli ΔampC JW4111 (Keio collection) in spent media harvested from overnight cultures of E. coli JW4111 (ASKA collection). The latter were grown in the absence of CTX without and with two levels of expression of AmpC induced by IPTG, as indicated in the panel headings. To confirm the causal role of AmpC in CTX removal and growth recovery, we performed three further experiments. First, we repeated the experiment shown in Fig. 4A at high AmpC induction by splitting the spent medium into two fractions. Adding the β-lactamase inhibitor boronic acid to one fraction led to a substantial reduction of growth (Fig. 4B), which proves that the protective agent is a β-lactamase. Second, to identify the causal gene, we overexpressed each amp gene separately, using strains from the ASKA collection and measured the growth on a CTX gradient. The data shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S5 clearly demonstrate that only overexpression of AmpC confers increased CTX resistance. Third, we harvested the spent medium from sonicated overnight cultures of the Keio deletion strains ΔampC and ΔlacA, and grew K12 ΔlacA in mixed media with varying CTX concentrations. At intermediate CTX concentrations, spent medium from the strain ΔlacA, which expresses AmpC, enhances growth relative to that in the spent medium extracted from ΔampC (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Taken together, these findings firmly validate our hypothesis that the observed protection is due to CTX breakdown by AmpC β-lactamase.

A Kinetic Model of CTX Hydrolysis Predicts the Recovery Time.

The scenario emerging from our results is that the observed growth recovery following decay is due to hydrolysis of CTX by AmpC. We hypothesize that growth resumes once the drug concentration has been reduced below a threshold level. To corroborate this scenario, we developed a model of CTX hydrolysis by AmpC in order to predict recovery times and compared these to observations.

We assume that CTX is hydrolyzed by AmpC at two locations: in the periplasm after diffusion of CTX through porins in the outer membrane, and in the extracellular medium after the release of AmpC by cell lysis. A small amount of AmpC may also be released to the medium by outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) (54, 55), but we estimate this contribution to be quantitatively negligible. We use the experimentally obtained growth curves to estimate the amount of nonlyzed and lysed biomass at any given time during the experiment, which we then take as input to calculate i) the amount of AmpC production (by the nonlyzed biomass), ii) the fraction released into the medium upon lysis, and coupled to this, iii) the total amount of CTX hydrolyzed over time by taking into account its reaction kinetics. Similar modeling approaches have been used by other groups (15, 29, 35, 45). Here, we aim for a maximally parsimonious description that explains the observed kinetics using a minimal set of assumptions and parameters. A detailed discussion of the model and its predictions is provided in SI Appendix. In the following, we outline the main ingredients and present our results.

We consider first the hydrolyzation of CTX in the periplasm (52, 53, 56). An equilibrium concentration inside the periplasm is achieved when the diffusion of CTX molecules into the periplasm is balanced by their hydrolysis by AmpC. The CTX concentration difference across the outer membrane is then given by the Zimmermann-Rosselet equation (57),[1]C−Cp=κCp1+Cp/Km,

which relates the extracellular and periplasmic CTX concentrations, C and Cp, via the dimensionless parameter[2]κ=kcatEpKmVpPA=kcatKmnβPA.

Here, Km and kcat are the Michaelis–Menten parameters describing CTX hydrolysis, P is the permeability constant of the outer membrane, A is the surface area of E. coli, Vp is the periplasmic volume, Ep is the enzyme concentration inside the periplasm and nβ=EpVp is the number of AmpC molecules per normal-size cell. Note that 1/κ is proportional to the membrane permeability P and has been referred to as the permeability number (58).

The parameter κ determines by how much the CTX concentration inside the cell is reduced relative to the medium. Eq. 1 shows that when κ is small, the CTX concentrations outside the cell and within the periplasm are nearly equal. Conversely, when κ is large, the concentration inside the periplasm is much lower than outside. When Cp≪Km, Eq. 1 reduces to a simple proportionality between the internal and external concentrations,[3]Cp=1κ+1C.β-lactamase production is a leaky public good that benefits both producing and nonproducing cells, but to different degrees (16, 19). In the absence of other resistance mechanisms, such as efflux pumps, the periplasmic concentration of non-enzyme-producing cells would be equal to the external concentration, and they would therefore be more strongly affected by the antibiotic. In this sense the parameter κ quantifies the private protection afforded to the producers.

We estimate the time dependence of the CTX concentration in the extracellular medium by taking into account the reaction kinetics as well as the bacterial growth kinetics. The latter are not modeled, but rather inferred from the experimental OD curves. Our working assumption is that the OD signal is proportional to the total live biomass, including filaments as well as normal-size cells (42, 43). In line with our maximally parsimonious modeling approach, we assume that the contribution of a filament to intracellular CTX degradation is the same as that of the corresponding number of normal-size cells. As the population recovery is driven by the overall CTX degradation, its onset is determined by growth and lysis of biomass rather than by cell number. Although there is therefore no need to explicitly model filamentation, our approach is consistent with a scenario of filament growth followed by lysis when filaments exceed a critical length (45), see SI Appendix.

We are concerned with the regime in which the change of biomass is nonmonotonic (Fig. 1). Initially the biomass increases at an average growth rate g up to some time tP at which the OD reaches its maximum. For t>tP, we assume that lysis sets in with a fixed rate γ, such that the net biomass decay rate is given by γ−g′, where g′ is the rate at which cells continue to grow beyond P. For simplicity we assume that g=g′. This is naturally realized in the filamentation scenario of ref. 45, because the filaments that have not yet lysed continue to elongate at a constant exponential rate. SI Appendix, Fig. S9A illustrates the extraction of the average initial growth and decay rate from empirical OD curves. The resulting estimates of the rates g, γ−g and γ are shown as a function of the initial CTX concentration in SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B. Incorporating periplasmic CTX degradation by live cells as well as extracellular degradation by AmpC released after cell lysis, the time evolution of the total amount of CTX in the medium is then given by the differential equation[4]dadτ=−EPkcatKmγγ−g1−e−(γ−g)τa1+a−e−(γ−g)τap1+ap,

where CTX concentrations are expressed in units of Km, ap=Cp/Km and a=C/Km, τ=t−tP, and EP is the concentration of enzymes in the medium that would have been attained if at tP all cells had lysed at once. It is proportional to the biomass concentration at time tP as well as to the number of enzyme molecules nβ per cell. The first and second terms inside the curly brackets on the right hand side of Eq. 4 are the contributions to the overall CTX reduction due to hydrolysis in the medium (upon lysis) and in the periplasm of intact cells, respectively. Using Eq. 1 to relate ap to a, we solve Eq. 4 numerically to predict the recovery time. Details of the derivation of Eq. 4 are provided in SI Appendix, while its mathematical analysis will be presented in the next section.

For E. coli K12, Nikaido and collaborators have measured the permeability P, as well as the reaction constants kcat and Km for CTX hydrolyzation by AmpC (52, 53), see SI Appendix. We therefore consider the behavior of this strain first. Similar to REL606, the nonmonotonic growth of E. coli K12 exhibits three phases (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). We assume that the onset of the recovery phase is determined by the time at which the extracellular CTX concentration falls below the value for which the OD growth is still monotonic, i.e. C=0.015μg/ml for E. coli K12. All parameters in our model can be inferred from experiments, except for the number nβ of AmpC molecules per normal-size cell. We therefore take nβ as the single adjustable model parameter to fit the data. Fig. 5A shows a comparison of the empirically observed recovery times τrec=tQ−tP, see Fig. 1C, for E. coli K12 with the predicted recovery times from our model. For nβ=700 AmpC molecules per cell [in line with previous estimates (59, 60)], we obtain recovery times that follow the data points reasonably well over a broad range of CTX concentrations. In view of the minimal character of our model, which does not include physiological changes in the production rate of AmpC or the cell wall permeability, the agreement is rather satisfactory.

To determine the CTX concentration gradient across the outer membrane from Eq. 1, we use the cross-over from monotonic to nonmonotonic growth as an indicator that CTX concentrations within the periplasm have reached levels at which cell growth starts to be affected. As mentioned above, this happens around concentrations of C=0.015μg CTX/ml. With the estimated value of κK12, we find from Eq. 1 that the corresponding concentration inside the periplasm is Cp=0.011μg/ml, which is comparable to the CTX concentration outside. We conclude that for E. coli K12 at sublethal drug concentrations, the transport of the drug across the outer membrane is sufficiently efficient to maintain similar CTX concentrations inside and outside the cell.

We next apply the same approach to E. coli REL606. For this strain, nonmonotonic OD growth sets in at CTX concentrations above C=0.060μg/ml (Fig. 1). Assuming that this occurs at the same periplasmic concentration of Cp=0.011μg/ml as for E. coli K12, the corresponding value is κREL606=5.0, which is about 13 times larger than κK12. From Eq. 2 we see that this could be a result of a difference in enzyme activity, the number of enzyme molecules nβ or permeability of the outer membrane. We were not able to find published estimates of P, kcat, and Km for REL606, and therefore make the simplifying assumption that the activity of AmpC is the same for both strains. This leaves nβ and the permeability P undetermined, however the value of κ fixes their ratio, leaving again only one adjustable parameter, which we choose to be nβ. With these assumptions, the recovery times for REL606 can be predicted from the OD curves in the same way as for K12, as shown in Fig. 5B. The best agreement with the empirical data occurs when nβ=600. The fits of the recovery times thus yield a comparable number of enzyme molecules for both strains. This suggests that the larger κ-value in REL606 reflects a lower outer membrane permeability.

E. coli Strains REL606 and K12 Differ in the Degree of Periplasmic versus Extracellular CTX Hydrolysis.

As we have seen, the magnitude of the CTX concentration gradient across the outer membrane is governed by κ, which is about 13 times larger for E. coli REL606 than for K12. We next use our modeling approach to examine how this difference affects the relative contributions of periplasmic and extracellular enzymatic activity to CTX removal.

Recall that the hydrolysis rate is proportional to the product of enzyme and substrate concentrations (SI Appendix, Eq. S2). Thus under conditions where AmpC concentrations are higher inside cells and CTX concentrations are higher outside cells, the release of enzymes into the medium will generally speed up hydrolysis. For E. coli REL606, we therefore expect lysis to contribute more strongly to the overall breakdown compared to E. coli K12. Our model permits us to determine the amounts of CTX hydrolyzed in the periplasm and in the medium by separately integrating the corresponding terms on the right hand side of Eq. 4. Fig. 5 C and D shows the result of this analysis. For E. coli K12, we see that for the full range of initial CTX concentrations most of the CTX is hydrolyzed in the periplasm, while for E. coli REL606, this is only the case for low initial concentrations. For initial CTX concentrations above 0.13μg/ml, increasingly larger amounts of CTX are hydrolyzed in the medium.

SI Appendix, Fig. S10 C and D illustrate how the enzymatic breakdown in the two strains progresses over time. The initial concentrations chosen, along with their empirical growth, decay, and lysis rates, are indicated by the circled data values in SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B. For E. coli K12, we see that at all times, even beyond the onset of recovery, the breakdown of extracellular CTX due to hydrolysis in the periplasm exceeds the contribution due to lysis. This is in contrast to the behavior of E. coli REL606. Here, the contribution of lysis starts to dominate well before the onset of recovery.

Factors Determining the Contributions of Intra- and Extracellular Degradation to Collective Recovery.

The analysis presented above suggests that the K12 and REL606 strains differ in two respects. On the one hand, for a given CTX concentration in the medium, the periplasmic concentration in REL606 is lower than in K12, because κREL606≫κK12; on the other hand, REL606 displays more cell lysis in the regime of biomass decay [(γ−g)REL606≫(γ−g)K12], see SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A and B. To gain a general understanding of how these factors contribute to collective survival under antibiotic stress, we now turn to an in-depth investigation of the model Eq. 4.

We start out with a qualitative analysis. As noted already, the first and second terms on the right-hand side of Eq. 4 describe the relative contributions of hydrolysis in the medium and the periplasm to the break-down of CTX. The exponential factors modulate the contributions as a function of time. The periplasmic contribution decays as e−(γ−g)τ along with the live biomass, whereas the extracellular contribution increases over time and reaches a constant limit when (γ−g)τ≫1. At this point all β-lactamase has been released to the medium where it continues to break down CTX. In the absence of growth (g=0), the maximal amount of released enzyme equals EP, the amount that was present in the live cells at the onset of lysis. For g>0, the additional β-lactamase production by newly born cells amplifies this amount by a factor γ/(γ−g), which appears as a prefactor of the extracellular contribution in Eq. 4. The amplification is ultimately caused by the continued growth of surviving filaments at rate g, and the ratio γ/(γ−g) quantifies the abruptness of the onset of lysis when filaments exceed a critical length (see SI Appendix for further explanation).

Thus, over time the dominant mode of hydrolysis switches from periplasmic to extracellular, and the time scale on which this switch occurs is set by the net biomass decay rate γ−g. Conversely, when Γ≪1, the overall break-down of CTX is much slower than the biomass decay and for a substantial reduction of CTX, hydrolyzation has to occur mainly in the medium, since the live biomass will have decayed much earlier.

A Tradeoff Between Individual and Collective Survival.

To exemplify the nontrivial tradeoffs predicted by our model, we consider the effect of changes in the membrane permeability P, which modify the dimensionless parameters κ and η, while leaving Γ and χ invariant. A change in P affects the recovery kinetics in two ways. On the one hand, the contribution of periplasmic hydrolysis in Eq. 6 is proportional to 1κ+1. Increasing κ by decreasing P therefore slows down the degradation process, because it reduces the efficiency of the periplasmic channel. On the other hand, increasing κ also reduces the periplasmic drug concentration relative to the external concentration, which implies that recovery will set in at larger external drug concentrations, hence earlier in time.

To explain this last point, recall that we assume (9, 58) that recovery begins when the periplasmic concentration falls below a critical value ap∗. The corresponding external concentration required for recovery a∗=(κ+1)ap∗ is an increasing function of κ. Since the reduction of the periplasmic drug concentration is the primary mechanism through which β-lactamase production contributes to the resistance of single cells (15), whereas the recovery time describes the resilience of the population (32), these two conflicting trends constitute a tradeoff between individual and collective survival. Such a tradeoff has been demonstrated for the ΔompF strain of E. coli K12 (21).

As a consequence of the tradeoff, the recovery time may depend nonmonotonically on the membrane permeability. Fig. 6B shows the dimensionless product EPkcatKmτrec as a function of 1κ+1 for a fixed value of ap∗/a0 and different values of χ. For simplicity it is assumed that γγ−g≈1, i.e. γ≫g, which implies that χ=Γ. The relationship between τrec and 1κ+1 is nonmonotonic for sufficiently large χ. In this regime a decrease of P (increase of κ) may either promote or compromise collective resilience. On the other hand, the recovery time is monotonically increasing with the parameter χ, and hence monotonically decreasing with the lysis rate γ.

Discussion

Understanding bacterial responses to antibiotic treatments is critical for the future efficacy of antibiotic therapies. These responses are often studied at the level of single bacterial cells, which are not always predictive for the survival of bacterial populations due to cooperative interactions (23), or by using ill-defined measures of resistance, such as the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC), which can be highly sensitive to variation in cell density and physiology (26, 29, 61, 62). Cooperative interactions that enhance collective antibiotic survival can be caused by diverse mechanisms, in particular by the production of antibiotic-degrading enzymes, such as β-lactamases (23). Given the substantial health problems caused by β-lactamase-producing infections (63), it is especially urgent to better understand the mechanisms and conditions promoting collective β-lactam survival.

Our present study was motivated by the observation of complex growth dynamics of two commonly used laboratory strains of E. coli in the presence of near-lethal concentrations of the cephalosporin cefotaxime (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3). To understand these dynamics, we performed experiments that identified the main causes of the observed sequence of growth, death, and recovery of bacterial populations, including the initial biomass growth due to cell elongation (filamentation) and growth recovery allowed by the hydrolysis of cefotaxime by the chromosomally encoded β-lactamase AmpC. Based on these key elements, we developed a kinetic model describing the growth dynamics of the two strains and validated it by comparing predicted and observed recovery dynamics (Fig. 5).

This work advances our current understanding of β-lactamase-mediated collective resistance in multiple ways. First, our model explicitly considers antibiotic hydrolysis at two distinct locations, in the periplasm of living cells and extracellularly after release of the enzyme by cell lysis (15, 46). Both hydrolysis channels affect the extracellular antibiotic concentration in different ways, and our model allows to quantify their relative contributions to recovery of the population. As cell lysis progresses, the fraction of β-lactam that is hydrolyzed extracellularly increases over time, and to what extent it contributes to recovery is therefore determined by the efficiency of hydrolysis via both channels and the amount of drug that needs to be removed (Fig. 6A). For the two E. coli strains considered in this study, our analysis shows that hydrolysis is primarily extracellular for REL606, but primarily periplasmic for K12. An immediate consequence of this result is that β-lactamase inhibitors with low periplasmic penetration (46) are predicted to be more effective against REL606 than against K12. The different modes of hydrolysis are also expected to lead to strain-specific inoculum effects on the MIC, which can be quantified along the lines of ref. 29.

Second, our model highlights two key properties of bacterial cells that determine the relative importance of periplasmic versus extracellular antibiotic degradation. One is the dimensionless outer membrane permeability number 1/κ (58), see Eq. 2. High values of κ correspond to high cell-level resistance due to lower periplasmic concentration of the antibiotic, which however also reduces the contribution of periplasmic degradation to collective survival. Conversely, low values of κ lead to larger periplasmic contribution to environmental degradation at the expense of lower single-cell resistance. Therefore, variation in the membrane permeability introduces a tradeoff between single-cell resistance and population-level resilience (Fig. 6B). The other property is the lysis rate of the cell, which contributes to extracellular degradation by releasing the β-lactamase. In experiments using an engineered strain with a fully private cytoplasmically expressed β-lactamase with an inducible suicide module, Tanouchi et al. (64) found conditions where the death rate can be tuned to optimize collective survival. In our setting such an optimal lysis rate does not generally exist, as the recovery time is a weakly, but monotonically decreasing function of the lysis rate (Fig. 6B). It is worth noting that the higher lysis rate and lower permeability number that we inferred for E. coli REL606 conspire to yield similar values of the dimensionless parameter η that quantifies the propensity for extracellular versus periplasmic CTX hydrolysis in the two strains. We can only speculate that this is indicative of a tradeoff that may exist more generally as a result of selection for population-level benefits.

Finally, our study highlights the special role of filamentation in β-lactamase-producing strains for collective survival. Filamentation is a known response to β-lactams via two pathways: as downstream response of SOS induction (65), but also directly by binding of β-lactams like cefotaxime to PBP3, an essential component of the divisome (66, 67). Filaments have been associated with enhanced β-lactam tolerance through delayed cell division (44, 68) and hence may build up larger biomass and β-lactamase concentrations before lysis sets in. Lysis rates of filaments have been shown to depend on the activation of cell division (67), as well as on filament length (35, 39, 45). A more quantitative understanding of the physiological properties of filamentous cells seems required to fully understand their role in the evolution of resistance to β-lactams.

While we demonstrate potential collective benefits from the release of β-lactamase due to cell death in relatively homogeneous liquid bacterial cultures, these benefits may be enhanced in the structured environments, such as biofilms, in which bacteria typically live (18, 69). In contrast to other resistance mechanisms with only immediate survival benefits in the presence of the antibiotic, antibiotic-degrading enzymes like β-lactamases prime the environment in a way that regrowth is facilitated. This effect seems particularly important for fragmented populations in structured environments, where bacteria are cleared at some locations, which can subsequently be recolonized (70). Another open question is how non-β-lactamase-producing community members living in close proximity to β-lactamase producers may benefit from this leaky public good (16), and how this has shaped the intra- and extracellular hydrolysis we observed within clonal populations. Clearly, these issues should be resolved to ensure the continued use of β-lactam antibiotics in combating infectious diseases.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Methodology.

E. coli strains REL606 and K12 from the ASKA and Keio collections were grown in varying concentrations of CTX, and biomass was monitored by OD. Whole genome sequencing of endpoint populations confirmed the absence of new resistance mutations. A microfluidic chamber was used to image the formation and recovery of filamented cells. Experiments using spent media and overexpression or knockouts of amp genes were performed to establish the causal role of the AmpC β-lactamase. For details we refer to SI Appendix.

Data Analysis and Modeling.

Effective growth and lysis rates were extracted from experimental OD trajectories and coupled to a kinetic model of extra- and intracellular hydrolysis of CTX by AmpC. The model was validated by comparison with experimentally determined recovery times, and then was used to infer the relative contributions of extra- and intracellular hydrolysis to CTX reduction. For details we refer to SI Appendix.
