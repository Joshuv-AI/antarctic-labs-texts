XGBoost Model With CMR Features for Prognostic Assessment in Patients With ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Yizhi Zhang, Jiyuan Chen, Zhiguo Zou, Bihe Xu, Chongwen Wu, Lai Wei, Jianxun Dong, Xincheng Zhang, Heng Ge, Song Ding, Jun Pu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate prognostic models for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) are essential to guide clinical practice.ObjectivesThis study sought to construct predictive models integrating demographic, clinical, and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging variables to forecast long-term major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs).MethodsPatients with STEMI underwent CMR imaging within 1 week and 1 month after primary percutaneous coronary intervention. Twenty-four demographic, clinical, and CMR variables were used to construct 4 predictive models (naive Bayes, logistic regression, k-nearest neighbors, and XGBoost) for forecasting MACCEs during long-term follow-up. Model interpretability was assessed using Shapley values.ResultsA total of 483 patients were included (median age: 59.6 years, IQR: 54.0-65.0 years; median follow-up: 89.3 months; IQR: 60.3-115.4 months). During follow-up, 98 of 483 patients (20.3%) experienced MACCEs. The XGBoost model demonstrated superior predictive performance compared with the other approaches. Key CMR predictors included microvascular obstruction, left ventricular ejection fraction recovery, infarct size, intramyocardial hemorrhage, infarct core T1, and remote myocardium T1. Among clinical features, Killip class, body mass index, and age were most influential. Remote myocardium T1 was inversely correlated with left ventricular ejection fraction recovery at 1 month after percutaneous coronary intervention (R = −0.34; 95% CI: –0.43 to −0.27; P < 0.01).ConclusionsAn XGBoost model integrating clinical and CMR features effectively predicted long-term MACCEs in patients with STEMI. Microvascular obstruction emerged as the most important CMR-based prognostic factor.Key words: cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, machine learning, myocardial infarction, XGBoost

Although primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and advanced post-PCI pharmacological therapies have been broadly implemented in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) for many years, the long-term prognosis for these patients remains variable.1

Research has focused on identifying high-risk patients and developing tools for better risk assessment. In recent years, cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging has proven to be a preferred method for assessing cardiac anatomy, function, and myocardial tissue characteristics. Because these factors are strongly associated with cardiovascular outcomes, some CMR imaging feature–based models have also been shown to have prognostic value. Most of these models have focused on the infarct myocardium features, such as cardiac torsion, late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) size of the infarct area, microvascular obstruction (MVO), and intramyocardial hemorrhage (IMH).2, 3, 4 T1 mapping is a technique that allows for the quantification of changes in myocardial composition based on changes in T1 relaxation times. Recent studies have shown that the traditionally considered “normal” remote myocardium with normal wall motion, stress perfusion, and no LGE has been demonstrated with elevated T1 time, providing potential and independent prognostic information for patients with STEMI.5 Thus, adding a T1-related feature into the model construction may improve the overall model performance.

Recent attempts to use machine learning algorithms in prognostic model construction have offered optimal accuracy in risk-of-event prediction in the field of acute myocardial infarction, acute stroke, and sudden cardiac death.6, 7, 8 Extreme gradient descent boosting (XGBoost) is a tree-based algorithm that is widely used in such models, and it has the benefits of requiring reasonable computing power while offering great execution speed and model performance.9 However, many of these prognostic models are often referred to as “black boxes” given their lack of interpretability. By using techniques such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), researchers can elucidate the influence of various features on prediction outcomes, identify the most significant variables, and thereby gain a sound understanding of feature importance as potential targets for clinical intervention.

In this study, we constructed an XGBoost model where clinical variables and CMR features were integrated, attempting to achieve improvement in the prediction of major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs) at the 1-month follow-up window in patients with STEMI compared with naive Bayes classifier, logistic regression, and k-nearest neighbors (KNN) models. Furthermore, we used SHAP to elucidate the model, aiming to identify the most pertinent features for potential clinical intervention.

Methods

Study design and subjects

We used patient data obtained from the EARLY-MYO-CMR (EARLY Assessment of MYOcardial Tissue Characteristics by CMR in STEMI; NCT03768453) registry, which is a multicenter prospective registry that includes all-comer patients with STEMI who have undergone CMR examination. The inclusion criteria of this study were as follows: 1) first-diagnosed STEMI characterized by typical ischemic syndromes and electrocardiography manifestation of ST-segment elevation in at least 2 contiguous precordial leads (≥2 mm) or peripheral leads (≥1 mm); 2) successful primary PCI within 12 hours after symptom onset; and 3) CMR imaging conducted within the first week of myocardial infarction. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) presence of old or aborted myocardial infarction; 2) patients who did not receive PCI within 12 hours after the onset of symptoms; 3) follow-up of <1 year; 4) absence of baseline T1 mapping data; 5) any follow-up scans with inadequate image quality for analysis, and; 6) patients experiencing MACCEs before the 1-month reassessment time spot.

All patients received standard medical treatment according to the current guidelines.10 Figure 1 shows the flowchart of patient inclusion in the study.

Flowchart of Patient Inclusion

A total of 483 patients were ultimately included in the study. CMR = cardiac magnetic resonance; MACCE = major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular event; MI = myocardial infarction; PCI = percutaneous coronary intervention.

CMR imaging protocols

All CMR images were obtained using a 3.0-T magnetic resonance scanner (Achieva TX, Philips Healthcare) within 1 week and 1 month after STEMI. The acquisition and postanalysis of cine, LGE, and MVO were the same as previously described by our group.2 Briefly, the infarct size was derived from LGE as the volume of myocardium with a signal intensity >5 × SDs higher than the mean signal intensity in the remote myocardium. MVO was defined as a hypointense core inside an area of hyperintense LGE and was manually delineated on short-axis slices. All images were analyzed offline using commercially available software (CVI42; Circle Cardiovascular Imaging) by 2 experienced cardiologists.

Left ventricular ejection fraction

Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was measured from the baseline and 1-month CMR examinations by manually tracing the endocardial contours on short-axis cine images. The analysis was performed with the investigator blinded to clinical outcomes.

T1 mapping

A single breath-hold 5(3)3 steady-state free precession modified look-locker inversion recovery was used to gain T1-mapping images with reduced respiratory motion artifacts. The first experiment time was 100 ms with an increment of 80 ms for the next look-locker trains. Other parameters of the sequence were as follows: TE: 1.1 ms; TR: 2.5 ms; flip angle: 35°; FOV: 300 × 300 mm2; and voxel size: 2 × 2 × 10 mm3. Any fitted T1 curves with R2 <0.99 were excluded.

Intramyocardial hemorrhage

IMH was caused by severe ischemia-reperfusion injury, which leads to capillary disruption and erythrocytes extravasation. At acute phase, on T2-weighted sequences, IMH was identified as a hypointense area inside an area of hyperintense T2-weighted intensity. The hemoglobin within the leaked erythrocytes will produce iron degradation products in the chronic phase, which can be detected in CMR imaging as shortened T2∗ time (Supplemental Figure 1).11, 12, 13

Identification of the remote myocardium

The strategy used to determine the remote myocardium region of interest (ROI) was the same as that published by Carrick et al.14 Specifically, the remote myocardium ROI was located at 180° from the affected zone with no visible evidence of any abnormality (Supplemental Figure 2).15 According to the contemporary guideline, special care was taken to avoid any unintended inclusion of blood pool or the surrounding pericardium tissue within the ROI.16

Interobserver reproducibility

CMR interobserver reproducibility was tested by using the intraclass correlation coefficient for continuous data (change in LVEF [dLVEF], infarct size, core T1, peri T1, remote T1, and MVO) and Cohen's κ for categorical data (IMH) (Supplemental Table 1).

Angiographic assessments

Angiographic parameters, including the TIMI flow grade, were assessed by an experienced interventional cardiologist.

Clinical endpoints and follow-up

The endpoints and follow-up strategy were the same as previously published.2 The incidence of MACCEs, which included cardiovascular death, nonfatal reinfarction, hospitalization for heart failure, and stroke, was considered the primary endpoint. Clinical follow-up was conducted by 2 specialized study nurses through phone calls or hospital return visits. All follow-ups were concluded upon the occurrence of MACCEs.

Principal component analysis

The principal component analysis was conducted using demographic, clinical, and CMR imaging–based variables. The number of principal components retained was determined based on the eigenvalues >1, ensuring that the selected components captured a significant proportion of the total variance.

Model construction

A total of 24 features were obtained from the patient demographics, PCI procedure, and CMR characteristics. These features included age, gender, body mass index, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) score, Killip class, number of lesions, multivessel disease, location of infarction, thrombus aspiration, use of aspirin, type of P2Y12R inhibitor, TIMI flow grade post PCI, change in LVEF (dLVEF) at 1 month, MVO, IMH, infarct size, infarct core T1 time, peri-infarct zone T1 time, and remote myocardium T1 time. Correlation coefficients between variables were calculated using Spearman rank correlation (Supplemental Figure 3). The variance inflation factor plot was constructed to evaluate the variables retained after feature selection (Supplemental Figure 4). No strong multicollinearity was evident among the retained predictors.

We constructed models using naive Bayes, logistic regression, KNN classification, and XGBoost. These algorithms span distinct methodological paradigms—probabilistic, generalized linear, instance-based, and ensemble learning—and are widely recognized as benchmark comparators in cardiovascular risk prediction research.

Among the study population, 290 of 483 (60%) were randomly selected for the training set and the remaining 193 of 483 (40%) were used as the test set in all models. Naive Bayes classifier and XGBoost algorithms do not require data normalization.

For multivariable logistic regression, all nonbinary values were normalized by centering and scaling. After centering and scaling, the performance of logistic regression improved without statistical significance (Supplemental Table 2). The training and test data sets were not regularized or scaled for KNN model construction given the fact that after regularization, the overall performance of the KNN model dropped (Supplemental Table 2). In the KNN model, the count of the nearest neighbors (k) was 6, which achieved the maximum model accuracy (Supplemental Figure 5).

The parameters we used after 10-fold cross validation in the XGBoost model were as follows: maximum depth: 3; learning rate: 0.9; objective: “binary:logistic”; gamma: 1; lambda: 0.9; early_stopping_rounds: 5; and subsample: 0.5, which achieved optimal low logloss of 0.038. Visualization of the ensemble of trees as a single collective unit is plotted in Supplemental Figure 6.

All models were constructed in R 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). All models produced probability estimates for the outcome. A threshold of 0.5 was applied for classification, representing the point at which predicted probabilities of event and nonevent are equal. This conventional cutoff was chosen to ensure comparability across models. The feature selection and hyperparameter tuning were strictly within the training data.

A proposed machine learning checklist following both the TRIPOD+AI statement and Proposed Requirements for Cardiovascular Imaging-Related Machine Learning Evaluation is attached to the Supplemental Appendix (Supplemental Table 8, Supplemental Figure 23).17,18

Model interpretation

The output of the XGBoost model is explained by SHAP, which is based on the game theoretically optimal Shapley values.19,20

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R 4.1.3. For the analysis of human data, continuous variables were tested for normality by using a combination of graphical methods, including histogram, Q-Q plot, and the Shapiro-Wilk test (Supplemental Figures 7 and 8). Normally distributed variables are expressed as mean ± SD, and variables with skewed distribution are expressed as median (IQRs). Categorical variables are expressed as n(%). Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for each variable. Model area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC) comparison was made by a DeLong's test. Variables were compared by unpaired Student’s t-test for means or chi-square test for contingency tables. The Cox regression model was also performed in R 4.1.3 in a right-censored length-biased manner. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. Data excluded because of missing or poor-quality T1 images were compared with included data using Little's test to assess the missing completely at random (MCAR) assumption. A pooled multiple imputation by chained equations analysis was performed for missing data analysis.

Results

Missing data

Little's MCAR test revealed that the missing data followed MCAR pattern (chi-square: 21.3; degree of freedom: 23; P = 0.56). A pooled multiple imputation by chained equations analysis was consistent with the complete-case analysis (Supplemental Figure 9, Supplemental Table 3). A direct comparison between the included and excluded missing-data patient cohorts also showed no significance in demographic and clinical indices (Supplemental Table 4).

Baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 483 patients were finally included in the study (Figure 1). Among these patients, 98 of 483 (20.3%) had MACCEs during the follow-up. The median follow-up time was 89.3 months (IQR: 60.3-115.4 months), and the median time from PCI to the baseline CMR scan was 5.6 days (IQR: 4.5-6.8 days). The median time of follow-up CMR scan was 30.5 days (IQR: 25.0-36.0 days). Demographic and clinical variables of the included patients in the database are shown in Table 1. The overall survival analysis and cumulative incidence plot for dropout bias evaluation are listed in the Supplemental Materials (Supplemental Figures 10 and 11).

The overall T1 mapping times in infarct core, peri-infarct zone, and remote myocardium did not differ significantly between the 2 groups (Table 2).

Principal component analysis

The principal component analysis could not fully separate the 2 groups of patients, yet a total of 11 components had eigenvalues >1 (Supplemental Figure 12). Separation was assessed using 2 principal components (PCs), with PC1 accounting for 8.4% of the variance and PC2 accounting for 7.4%, as demonstrated in the scree plot (Supplemental Figure 13). The quality of representation in PC1 and PC2 is illustrated in cos2 bar plots (Supplemental Figure 14).

Univariate Cox regression of long-term MACCEs

The assumption of proportional hazards was confirmed using Schoenfeld residuals (Supplemental Figure 15, Supplemental Table 5). Only Killip class, dLVEF at 1 month, MVO, IMH, infarct size, diabetes, TIMI flow grade post PCI, and remote myocardium T1 time were significantly associated with MACCE occurrence (P < 0.05), whereas age and infarct core T1 time trended toward statistical significance. Hazard ratios and their 95% CIs were reported to quantify the effect of each predictor on the hazard rate (Table 3).

Model performance

Before feature selection, the XGBoost model had better overall model performance metrics (Supplemental Table 6). Feature selection was based on a greedy recursive elimination method. The least important features in the XGBoost model were subsequently removed based on feature importance analysis (Supplemental Figures 16 to 19).

After omitting the least important features, 11 features remained: MVO, infarct size, Killip class, dLVEF, IMH, body mass index, age, peri-infarct zone T1 time, diabetes, remote myocardium T1 time, and infarct core T1 time. The optimal hyperparameter of the XGBoost model changed to max depth = 3, eta = 0.9, objective = “binary:logistic,” gamma = 1, lambda = 0.6, early_stopping_rounds = 5, and subsample = 0.5 to obtain the best model performance. The best fitted k-value for KNN model changed to 8.

Despite the AUC of the naive Bayes classifier model surpassing that of the XGBoost model without statistical significance, the XGBoost model still had a better level of precision-recall AUC, F score, sensitivity, specificity, and Brier score (Figure 2, Table 5). At an optimal high-risk threshold of 0.071, determined via the Youden index, the XGBoost model demonstrated a sensitivity of 97.3% and a specificity of 97.6%.

Alongside its discriminative ability, the XGBoost model exhibited excellent calibration. Predicted MACCE probabilities closely matched the observed event rates along the ideal reference line (intercept: 0.57; slope: 0.82) supported by a nonsignificant Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P = 0.615) and a low Brier score (0.045), indicating reliable absolute risk estimations (Supplemental Figure 20). A net decision-analytic evaluation also delineated the net benefit of the XGBoost model (Supplemental Figure 21).

We also performed a reverse Kaplan-Meier analysis and 5-year truncated model performance analysis; the results were consistent with the aforementioned data (Supplemental Figure 22, Supplemental Table 7).

Feature dependence

To investigate the reason why the infarct core T1 time did not demonstrate significance in the Cox regression analysis despite its substantial impact on the XGBoost model, we conducted a SHAP analysis to assess the dependence among features. In our model, the core of the infarcted segment often has a lower T1 level in the presence of MVO (Figure 3).

Discussion

In our study, we used multiple machine learning algorithms to construct a prognostic model that includes demographic, clinical, and CMR-based features for patients with STEMI. Novel findings are as follows:

1)XGBoost performed better than naive Bayes, KNN, and logistic regression in prediction of long-term MACCEs in patients with STEMI who underwent PCI within 12 hours after symptom onset.

CMR imaging provides the gold standard of cardiac function and the unsurpassed information in evaluating tissue level details of the heart. However, most CMR imaging–based studies have relatively small sample sizes because of the cost and complexity of CMR scanning, particularly in patients with STEMI.21 This can limit the statistical power and generalizability of the results.

In this study, we used the XGBoost algorithm, an end-to-end scalable and highly effective tree-boosting system.9 XGBoost is capable of handling sparse data exceptionally well, making it a suitable choice for clinical outcome prediction problems (Central Illustration). This is particularly relevant for CMR imaging–based studies, which are often limited by suboptimal sample sizes given the time and financial costs of CMR scans. As the data set grows over time, it may become possible to use other advanced machine learning algorithms such as deep neural networks. These algorithms may be more powerful and better suited to handling large amounts of data. However, for now, the most optimal method for our data set was XGBoost, as demonstrated in Table 5.

Moreover, we used machine learning algorithms and Shapley value to achieve better model performance and model explanation, which surpasses the limitations of conventional methods such as Cox regression and permutation feature importance analysis. The overlap between Cox regression and XGBoost highlights the robustness of Killip class, MVO, and infarct size as consistent predictors across methodologies. At the same time, the divergence—such as the prominence of binge drinking in Cox regression and dLVEF in XGBoost—illustrates how machine learning and traditional statistical approaches may capture complementary aspects of risk. Together, these findings emphasize the value of integrating both frameworks to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of prognostic determinants.

Shapley values are a powerful tool used to explain projections in the XGBoost model and to gain a better understanding of feature importance. This method assigns a contribution value to each feature in a group that contributes to a shared outcome. The underlying principle is to fairly share the overall value among the features that work together in cooperation. In the field of machine learning, Shapley values are commonly used to explain projections made by nonlinear models. By using this method, each feature gains as much or more than if they acted independently. This is important because it provides an incentive for features to collaborate and work together toward achieving the model output.20,22

In the XGBoost model, the Killip classification was the most significant feature for non-CMR-related factors. A higher Killip class indicates severely impaired heart function. During follow-up, patients who experienced MACCEs had a significantly higher proportion of class II or higher Killip classification than those who did not experience MACCEs. This suggests that baseline heart function is an important marker for outcomes.23 Interestingly, the GRACE score, which natively includes more information, did not show a remarkable impact on the XGBoost model performance (Supplemental Figures 16 and 17). The GRACE score also did not yield to a significant HR in the univariate Cox regression model. The GRACE score may be more sensitive in predicting in-hospital recurrent myocardial infarction and new onset cardiogenic shock.24 These events were rare in our study population, which could explain our results. Furthermore, the GRACE score incorporates age and Killip class, which were also entered as independent features in our model. Because these individual predictors demonstrated strong predictive strength with outcomes, the composite GRACE score may have contributed limited additional information, leading to attenuation of its predictive value. This phenomenon underscores a broader methodological issue: When composite scores are decomposed into their constituent variables in multivariable or machine learning frameworks, redundancy can reduce their apparent significance. Thus, the lack of predictive power of the GRACE score in our analysis should not be simply interpreted as a limitation of the score itself but rather as a reflection of overlapping information captured by its components.

Regarding CMR-related features, infarct size is conventionally considered as the primary CMR prognostic indicator in patients with STEMI, followed by LVEF and the presence of MVO.25 In our model, we found that the volume of MVO was the most effective predictor of MACCEs among all CMR imaging–based features. Infarct size and dLVEF were next in effectiveness, followed by the presence of IMH, and T1-related features.

The presence of MVO is widely recognized as a sign associated with MACCEs, all-cause mortality, and heart failure hospitalization. Our study showed that among all features included in the XGBoost model construction, the volume of MVO was the most significant prognostic indicator with the highest SHAP value. Even without considering the collaboration of features, MVO remained the most significant factor in the model, as evaluated by permutation feature importance (Supplemental Figure 18). These results likely stem from the critical impact of MVO on long-term left ventricular remodeling in patients with STEMI. Previous studies demonstrate that MVO is associated with persistent iron deposition, a factor closely tied to a smaller relative reduction in infarct size over a decade of follow-up.26

A recent study by Bulluck et al27 suggests that a noncontrast CMR risk score can perform comparably to a CMR score that includes established contrast-based parameters post STEMI. The study found that replacing infarct size and MVO extent with information on the presence or absence of IMH and the levels of LVEF did not significantly affect prognostic efficacy.27 In our study, we found that IMH had a significant impact on Cox regression. However, when measured by the Shapley value, it ranked lower than the contrast-based features LGE and MVO. Omitting LGE and MVO from the model will significantly reduce its performance, as demonstrated in permutation feature importance analysis (Supplemental Figure 18). In this analysis, the ranking of IMH decreased even further, whereas the rankings of other features remained relatively unchanged. This suggests that IMH had a substantial impact on some samples but not all, as supported by the results of the SHAP force plot (Figure 2C). The lack of IMH volume measurement and the binary form of IMH results may explain this phenomenon. It could also account for why LVEF ≤45% and the presence of IMH (score 3) did not significantly outperform the GRACE score in the study by Bulluck et al (AUC comparison, P = 0.03, log-rank test), where the same binary classification of IMH was used.

This is consistent with the fact that current published reports provide far less prognostic evidence for noncontrast CMR features than for LGE-related markers.28 However, adding other promising noncontrast CMR risk markers, such as T1 mapping, to the model is of interest and relevance.

The core of the infarcted segment often has a lower T1 time when MVO is present (Figure 3).29 Interestingly, infarct core T1 does not yield statistical significance in Cox regression but has a meaningful SHAP value in the XGBoost model. This may be due to the fact that a higher infarct core T1 might be associated with IMH or severe myocardium edema.30 The identification of correlation between high or low infarct core T1 and MACCEs is compromised by the nature of Cox regression's proportional hazards assumption. In a recent study, researchers used an arbitrary threshold to disclose the relationship between low infarct T1 and outcome. The authors declared that a decrease in infarcted myocardium T1 was associated with increased risk of adverse outcomes, whereas an overall lower infarct T1 (<1,300 ms) was associated with a 3-fold increase in risk of MACCEs.21 However, they did not mention or test a longer infarct T1 as a potential risk factor.

Our study demonstrated that remote myocardium T1 was a significant predictor of long-term adverse outcomes, as indicated by its positive result in the univariate Cox regression and XGBoost model. This finding is consistent with a previous research showing that higher remote T1 values (per millisecond) are associated with increased risk of adverse events (univariate Cox regression, P < 0.10).21 We also found that dLVEF at 1 month was negatively correlated with baseline remote myocardium T1 time, indicating that elevated T1 values in the remote myocardium are associated with impaired LVEF recovery, which highlights the prognostic importance of diffuse myocardial changes beyond the infarct zone. Potential mechanisms underlying such an association may include activation of innate immune responses and inflammation, mediated by local leukocyte recruitment.31 Clinically, this finding suggests that remote myocardium T1 mapping could serve as a novel biomarker for risk stratification, helping to identify patients at greater risk of adverse recovery who may benefit from closer monitoring or targeted interventions. However, it has been hypothesized that the elevated remote myocardium T1 may result from compensated increment of myocardial blood flow,32 which could be assessed using CMR quantitative myocardial perfusion imaging; this represents an area for future investigation.33 It is important to note that the overall T1 values in our study did not differ significantly between patients with and without MACCEs. The implied value of the remote T1 was only evident in the interaction-sensitive analyses. Additional studies are still warranted to validate the clinical impact of the remote T1 and to evaluate possible therapeutic interventions that influence remote myocardium T1 to improve the prognosis after revascularized STEMI.

Biologically, a 1-month landmark model offers distinct prognostic advantages over a discharge-only model. Early post-PCI LVEF assessments are frequently confounded by transient myocardial stunning and acute edema. By incorporating dLVEF at 1 month, the model successfully captures the dynamic process of early left ventricular remodeling. Patients exhibiting a lack of LVEF recovery or early deterioration by the 1-month mark represent a distinct, high-risk phenotype that a baseline-only model would fail to identify. Therefore, framing the clinical workflow around a 1-month reassessment yields a more definitive reflection of permanent myocardial damage and long-term risk. Clinically, this model functions as an actionable 1-month post-PCI reassessment tool. Identifying high-risk patients at this time point allows clinicians to proactively alter care by scheduling closer surveillance, initiating angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) or sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i), prioritizing cardiac rehabilitation, and ordering earlier follow-up imaging to monitor adverse remodeling. Conversely, patients stratified as low risk can safely transition to standard follow-up intervals, optimizing clinical workflow and resource allocation.

Study limitations

First, this study only included patients with STEMI who underwent successful primary PCI within 12 hours after symptom onset, and it may not be representative of all patients with STEMI. Second, the IMH feature is binarized as presence or absence because the information of IMH was obtained by T2-weighted sequence and T2∗.11, 12, 13 The heterogeneity of the methods hindered us from precisely measuring the IMH volume. Thus, the importance and efficacy of IMH may not be fully disclosed. Quantitative IMH volume assessment may better capture its prognostic impact, with novel methods such as high-dynamic-range quantitative susceptibility mapping possibly offering optimal sensitivity to iron deposition than conventional T2∗ mapping.34 Third, the omission of external validation is another limitation. Given that large CMR databases such as the UK Biobank do not contain remote T1 information, obtaining external validation remains a challenge in the near future.35,36 Furthermore, as an XGBoost-based model developed in a single center, its generalizability to study groups with different CMR protocols remains the primary next step for this research. Fourth, modeling MACCEs with a binary classifier instead of a survival-aware framework inadequately accounts for right-censoring and variable follow-up. Despite the 5-year truncated and reverse Kaplan-Meier analyses, this approach remains strictly exploratory. Future studies require true time-to-event architectures.

Conclusions

An XGBoost algorithm–based model incorporating demographic, clinical, and CMR-based features can optimally predict the incidence of long-term MACCEs after PCI in patients with STEMI (Supplemental Table 9). Volume of MVO was the most important predictor. The value of remote myocardium T1 remains exploratory.
