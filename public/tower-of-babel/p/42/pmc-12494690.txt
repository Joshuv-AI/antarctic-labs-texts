Diffusion and discrete temporal models of the population growth of domestic cats in urban areas
Authors: Rodrigo Perusquía-Cortés, Pablo Padilla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The survival of the domestic cat (Felis catus) in various ecosystems has become increasingly relevant due to its impact on wildlife, public health, and society. In countries like Mexico, social factors such as abandonment have led to the feralization of the species and an unexpected increase in its population in urban areas. To design and implement effective population control methods, a thorough analysis of the species’ population dynamics, along with the social factors influencing it, is necessary, this being the aim of the present paper. We propose a reaction-diffusion model to simulate the natural dispersal of the population within a bounded domain. After exploring the species’ spreading ability, we construct a discrete dynamical system based on the biological characteristics of cats and their intraspecific and interspecific interactions, which we explain and study in detail. The model includes both fixed parameters, i.e. determined by the demographic data available, and stochastic parameters, that are adjusted according to some random distribution, to enhance the realism of the simulations. Our results indicate that the population reaches a non-trivial equilibrium in the case of the reaction-diffusion model and a periodic equilibrium in the discrete dynamical system, highlighting the need for control methods combined with social regulations to achieve sustainability in the system.

Introduction

The growth of free-ranging cats in various environments has become a significant problem in several countries. One major concern is their impact on ecological conservation. Cats, with their wide dietary range, can pose a serious threat to biodiversity19,25. On the other hand, the well-documented challenges of cat welfare in urban areas have led to the creation of protective laws, recognizing the species’ important role in society37,38.

In different cities the nuisance posed by feral cats, as well as the risk of disease transmission to other species, including humans, has driven the study of control measures12,37. Understanding the population dynamics of free-ranging cats is a critical first step in designing effective public policies for control of the species.

In Mexico, for example, there is an estimated 28 million domestic animals (mainly dogs and cats), of which 70% are homeless, with data indicating an annual increase of 20%50. However, this information has been repeatedly cited since 201610,13,28,33.

In 2021, INEGI (the Spanish acronym for the National Institute of Statistics and Geography) reported a total of 80 million owned pets, of which 43.8 million are dogs and 16.2 million are cats3. However, there is no information available regarding an estimate of free-ranging domestic animals in Mexico, which would reflect the magnitude of the overpopulation problem of this species today.

Recent studies have been conducted to assess the impact of free-ranging dogs and cats on urban natural reserves in Mexico City, as well as the benefits of the control methods implemented36. Notably, cats have proven to be more resilient than dogs. This finding serves as an additional motivation to study the population dynamics of free-ranging cats in depth and identify the reasons behind this phenomenon.

The term “feral cat” refers to an unowned cat that commonly avoids or exhibits aggressive behavior toward people7 , while the term “free-roaming cat” refers to an unowned cat living outdoors47, both having limited interactions with humans. Feral cats form a subset of free-roaming cats, as the latter also includes abandoned, lost, and stray cats that were previously socialized to people9. Cats abandoned in the wild, apart from human socialization, along with the offspring of free-roaming cats, constitute the feral cat population14,31.

Since urban areas can have both free-roaming cats and owned cats allowed outdoors, we use the term “free-ranging cat” to refer to both categories. In this context, a free-ranging cat is defined as a cat that spends time outdoors with no physical limitations on its movement, regardless of its ownership status.

To conduct a comprehensive study of the growth of free-ranging cats, it is necessary to propose several approaches to this phenomenon. The first approach to modeling the population dynamics of a species and its growth is often through a system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs). To study the impact of the population in a physical domain, a more effective approach involves proposing a system of partial differential equations (PDEs) with appropriate boundary conditions.

Both models are often based on a well-mixed approximation on the interactions, since one needs to identify the natural factors that govern population dynamics (e.g., birth, death, and migration rates) as well as the main factors that affect it (e.g., competition and social intervention) to include them in the model.

Although ODE and PDE models are the classical frameworks for modeling population dynamics, some improvements have been made to develop more accurate models in terms of the spatial moment dynamics35,46. However, for the sake of simplicity, we remain within the classical framework presenting first a system of PDEs modeling the spatial growth of the population of free-ranging cats.

An alternative approach to model population dynamics is through a discrete complex system, where it is essential to establish the patterns and interactions between vertices with a high degree of precision to accurately reproduce the natural behavior of the species. In a discrete complex system, we can distinguish between individuals, allowing us to study the population in a more personalized manner.

The second model presented in this study is a complex dynamical system based on graph theory and networks. This model simulates the population dynamics of free-ranging cats, placing particular emphasis on its mating system and describing, with greater precision, the intrinsic factors that affect the population growth. To test its reliability, a sensitivity analysis is performed along with a brief study on the prediction of less well-known statistical population parameters.

It is important to notice here that these models are complementary, since they consider different spatial scales. The reaction-diffusion model focuses on a large-scale while the dynamical network model implicitly considers a local-spatial scale. Incorporating explicitly spatial dynamics in the discrete model requires an understanding of the local spatial movements of the individuals, which is not available and is hard to predict due to the biological characteristics of the species.

Finally, we draw conclusions from both models, outline the main characteristics of the most implemented population control methods for free-ranging cats, and examine how these methods relate to the models’ outcomes, including predicted effects and comparisons with results from previous studies.

Methods

Reaction-diffusion model

An interesting perspective for analyzing the population growth of free-ranging cats in urban areas is through the study of their subpopulations and their interactions. In43 this is done using ODEs, where the total population is divided into kittens, adult female cats, and adult male cats.

Individually, cats have the ability to roam over large home ranges, concept refering to a finite region of space within which they perform their daily activities. However, this ability encourages the migration of the species to other areas when there is lack of resources and mating opportunities14,42. Since cats can displace collectively through their habitat, a study using PDEs incorporating an explicit spatial component would be valuable for predicting the species’ growth and spread across a given territory.

To achieve this, the most relevant factors affecting each subpopulation must be identified and considered. These include birth, sexual maturity, and mortality intrinsic rates, as well as intraspecific competition. Additionally, factors related to abandonment, animal rescue and other human-induced causes of mortality, such as poisoning or vehicle accidents14, must also be taken into account.

Each equation has a recruitment term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_i$$\end{document} derived from the abandonment of the species. The parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_i$$\end{document} rather than representing mortality rates, are removal rates as other forms of loss of the species exist in urban areas (e.g., animal rescue).

The maturity term\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-\frac{2mx_1}{K_2+ x_1}$$\end{document}in the first equation, with m representing the sexual maturity rate of kittens, should be accounted for in the other equations, as maturity leads to a self-sustaining process within the population. As such, assuming an equal number of male and female kittens, this term is then allocated equally between the second and third equations.

It is reasonable to expect that the competition for resources in crowded populations represents an obstacle for kittens to reach adulthood as health, physical condition, and nutritional status are leading factors affecting the onset of puberty5,45. Thus, the rate of kittens attaining sexual maturity should decrease as the population grows, since the availability of resources is jeopardized and, consequently, the onset of puberty might be delayed.

Although the maturity term might be affected by different parameters of the population dynamics, such as the number of adult females or males, probably the most relevant of them is the total number of kittens as it implicitly carries information about the global status of the population. So, assuming for simplicity that the maturity factor depends solely on the number of kittens, the above discussion is well-represented by the considered term.

This loss factor is used in Michaelis-Menten harvesting models6,8. In these, the half-saturation constant is associated with an external effort (such as fishing effort). In the context of cat population, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_2$$\end{document} can be interpreted as the physical effort required by each organism to reach sexual maturity.

The term\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{bx_2}{K_1+ x_2},$$\end{document}which also follows a Michaelis-Menten growth behavior, depends solely on the population of female cats, with b representing the birth growth rate. This assumption is grounded in the idea that kitten production is more pronounced when the female population is small. As the female population increases, however, a “saturation effect” emerges: limited litter sizes and the carrying capacity of the population, constrained by resource availability, prevent further growth of the kitten population.

The density of males is assumed not to affect female fecundity, as the probability that no male encounters a female during its estrous cycle is vanishingly small. Moreover, although the size of a litter can increase when sexual interactions with different males occur48, male cats typically do not participate in the gestation process nor take responsibility for their litters. Therefore, it is assumed that the population of male cats does not contribute to the increase in the population of kittens.

The Michaelis-Menten growth term used is equivalent to Holling type II functions40. Notice that the time required for a female cat to experience consecutive estrus cycles (i.e., the transition between estrus and interestrus phases) or to complete a pseudopregnancy is shorter than the duration of pregnancy itself (see Appendix A). In particular, with this consideration and taking into account the construction of the Michaelis-Menten model for chemical reactions51, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_1$$\end{document} reflects a complex relationship, primarily between ovulation and fertilization rates, though other factors may also influence this value.

The proposed model incorporates Lotka-Volterra competitive interactions, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_{i,j}x_{i}x_{j}$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i, j=2, 3$$\end{document}, as adult cats, both female and male, physically compete for resources. Kittens have a minimal impact on adult cats and vice versa, due to the physical limitations of kittens in competition. Therefore, competition terms involving kittens are assumed to be zero.

Including diffusion dynamics is crucial, as cats are natural predators and considered an invasive species39. It is important to notice that this is a matter of scales; indeed, the spatial dynamics of a single cat appears to be random locally and assuming random short-scale movement of cats gives rise to a diffusive term.

The spatial component of the model is incorporated in the second and third equations, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_2$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D_3$$\end{document} representing the diffusion coefficients for the female and male cat subpopulations, respectively. Discrete dynamical model

We aim to describe the growth of free-ranging cats in an urban area by estimating the number of sexual interactions within the population through a discrete model that evolves over time. Its main components are the elements presented in Appendix A. In contrast to spatial individual-based models, which are used to study the impact of space on population dynamics, the discrete dynamical model presented here does not incorporate a spatial variable. Since the proposed reaction-diffusion model already includes a spatial parameter, the construction of the dynamical system places particular emphasis on the qualitative behavior of the interactions between individuals and its evolution in time. In this way, the two models complement each other, with one focusing on aspects the other does not.

The discrete dynamical model can be understood as a bipartite graph that changes its structure over time. Each vertex represents a cat and is initially labeled with its basic biological characteristics: gender and age. If the vertex represents a male cat (type I), a value for dominance is assigned; this is assumed to be a non-decreasing function of age. If the vertex represents a female cat (type II), its reproductive cycle phase is recorded along with a pregnancy/pseudopregnancy counter (PC).

On one hand, female cats in heat attract male cats through vocalizations and their scent, often marking their territory by spraying urine11,18. On the other hand, male cats have a wider home range in search of mating opportunities compared to female cats. Consequently, our graph is formed by directed edges from vertices of type I (male cats) to vertices of type II (female cats), simulating the natural sexual interactions between them. Since a dominant male is more likely to copulate than a less dominant one23, edges are created randomly with weights based on dominance and change as the system evolves, reflecting their polygamous mating system.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_0$$\end{document} represent a week within the population’s breeding season. At time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_0$$\end{document}, the process of drawing edges, which occurs in two steps, begins. The first step involves most of the total edges, as it focuses on connecting highly dominant males to females (primary interactions). The second step emphasizes the involvement of lower-dominant males in the dynamics (secondary interactions). These steps account for the weekly interactions of the population.

Edges are drawn with the following considerations, regardless of the step in which they are drawn: If a male cat (V1) encounters a female cat (V2), an edge (E1) is drawn from V1 to V2. This edge might represent a sexual or a casual encounter.If another male cat (V3) encounters the same female cat (V2) and it has the appropriate conditions to become pregnant (i.e. it is over the age of sexual maturity and it is not pregnant or pseudopregnant already), an edge (E2) is drawn from V3 to V2. This strengthens the likelihood that (E1) represented a sexual encounter (it is also possible that V3 = V2).Edges are stored within the characteristic information of the adjacent vertices. Once the weekly interactions are completed, an analysis of the vertices of type II is conducted. If a female cat has the appropriate conditions to become pregnant and has one or more directed edges connecting to a vertex of type I that represents a male cat over the onset of sexual maturity, a process begins to determine whether it becomes pregnant, pseudopregnant, or neither. When one of the first two options occurs, the PC for the respective vertex starts running. If a female cat experiences pseudopregnancy, its PC records the duration of this process. If she achieves a successful pregnancy, her PC records the weeks of gestation, lactation, and weaning before resetting.

If another male cat (V3) encounters the same female cat (V2) and it has the appropriate conditions to become pregnant (i.e. it is over the age of sexual maturity and it is not pregnant or pseudopregnant already), an edge (E2) is drawn from V3 to V2. This strengthens the likelihood that (E1) represented a sexual encounter (it is also possible that V3 = V2).

If another male cat (V3) encounters the same female cat (V2) and it has the appropriate conditions to become pregnant (i.e. it is over the age of sexual maturity and it is not pregnant or pseudopregnant already), an edge (E2) is drawn from V3 to V2. This strengthens the likelihood that (E1) represented a sexual encounter (it is also possible that V3 = V2).

Vertices representing kittens should be considered once the weeks of gestation conclude. However, due to the high mortality rates during their early neonatal period (i.e., up to 6 or 8 weeks of age)30, they are added to the dynamic only when the PC of a pregnant female cat is reset. The addition of vertices due to social factors is also taken into account.

For the removal of nodes, reflecting the natural and social causes of exit from the dynamics (e.g., natural death, vehicle accidents, rescuing), a theoretical carrying capacity is established, as the species cannot grow indefinitely. An individual survival probability S is assigned to each vertex, based on the carrying capacity, the size of the population at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_0$$\end{document}, and demographic data of the population. The product of these values forms a joint survival probability JS. The selection of vertices follows two weighted random sampling procedures: the first depends on JS and the second depends on their ages. The second procedure, referred to as SS, can be understood as the probability of being chosen among all nodes of the same type. Additionally, the number of vertices to be deleted is kept below an established upper bound that depends on the carrying capacity and the size of the population.

Afterwards, the time-dependent parameters are updated before all edges are removed from the dynamics as the system transitions from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_0$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_1$$\end{document}. If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_1$$\end{document} represents a week during the breeding season, the entire process described earlier is repeated. During the non-breeding season, only the addition and deletion of vertices, along with the update of time-dependent parameters, are carried out.Fig. 1The flux chart illustrates the steps taken into account in the construction of the dynamical network model to reproduce the population dynamics of free-ranging cats.

Discrete dynamical model

We consider an initial condition of 50 free-ranging cats of equally probable gender and randomly selected ages ranging from 7 to 36 months. We assume that our location is in North America, with a carrying capacity (CC) of 100 individuals, and that it is the start of the breeding season.

The parameters related to the population that we assume to be constant are as follows: onset of sexual maturity (8 months for both genders), duration of estrus and interestrus (one week each), and the duration for the breeding season, gestation, lactation, weaning, and pseudopregnancy (39, 9, 7, 4, and 7 weeks, respectively). Additionally, the age at which kittens are added to the dynamics is set at 10 weeks.

There is a scenario where one encounter leads to only one mating. When there is more than one encounter, we assume that there are more than two matings. Considering the ovulation and conception rates provided in48, which pertain to exactly one or three matings, we assume a constant rate of induced ovulation and conception: for one encounter, the rates are 72% and 37%, respectively; for more than one encounter, the rates are 85% and 93%, respectively.

A combination of constant and stochastic values is considered for social factors. This addition may represent an abandoned or missing cat, or an owned cat with access to the outdoors. The most common immediate scenarios for this individual are survival and adaptation, death or displacement, rescue or recovery, and return to its home on its own. Thus, the immediate continuity of the vertex in the dynamics follows a Bernoulli distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Ber\big (p=\frac{1}{4}\big )$$\end{document}.

Stochasticity plays an important role in the model, defining parameters that either have support from well-known data or do not. For example, litter size is determined through a weighted random sampling procedure: the sizes considered range from 3 to 6, with weights chosen to ensure that the expected value is 3.5, aligning with real data11,24. The removal of kittens follows a similar procedure: for each litter of size L, the number of kittens to be removed is selected from 0 to L, with weights designed to achieve an expected value of 0.45L. This is based on the fact that approximately 55% of kittens survive after ten weeks30.

We assume that the removal weighing procedure SS, mentioned in Sect. 2.2, follows a gamma distribution \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma \big (5, \frac{1}{2}\big )$$\end{document}. Additionally, we consider the immediate removal of individuals older than six years, as it is uncommon to see them in urban areas31.

Since our goal is to replicate the reproduction of free-ranging cats, the edges in the dynamics represent sexual interactions, which are drawn only during the breeding season. We categorize these interactions into primary (edges of type I) and secondary (edges of type II). The interactions reflect the species’ polygamous reproduction and the natural hierarchy among male cats: dominant males are more likely to copulate first and more frequently than their less dominant counterparts. As a result, primary interactions focus on connecting the more dominant male cats, leading to a greater number of edges of type I (Fig. 5 a). In the secondary interactions, edges of type II can connect any male, regardless of dominance, but only to a female cat that has already been selected in the primary interactions (Figure 5 b).

Let EP represent the number of female cats in estrus and DD the number of highly dominant male cats. We define a male cat as highly dominant when its dominance D satisfies \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D \ge 0.75$$\end{document}. It is reasonable to expect that at least once a week, all female cats in estrus or all highly dominant males engage in a sexual interaction. So, the number of weekly primary interactions E is randomly selected between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\min (EP, DD)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\max (EP, DD)$$\end{document}.

To determine the adjacent vertices for an edge of type I, a vertex of type I is selected with a weight equal to its dominance, and a vertex of type II is chosen with a weight based on its reproductive phase, as highly dominant male cats and female cats in estrus are more likely to encounter each other. Additionally, to prevent this stochasticity from resulting in multiple edges being drawn from the same vertex of type I to too many vertices of type II, or vice versa, we impose an upper bound on the number of edges attached to each vertex.

We say that a vertex of type II is eligible if it represents a female cat in estrus and is adjacent to a vertex of type I. Let C be the total number of eligible vertices of type II. Since it is possible that not all eligible female cats engage in secondary interactions, but a fraction of them does, we assume that the number of edges of type II is a random value bounded by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{C}{3}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{C}{2}$$\end{document}.

The drawing of edges of type II proceeds as follows: a vertex of type I with an age greater than 8 months and an eligible vertex of type II are randomly chosen. Secondary interactions reaffirm that a primary interaction was sexual in nature and demonstrate that free-ranging cats are polygamous. Moreover, these interactions allow male cats with lower dominance to participate in the dynamics.

Additionally, a color bar is included in this representation to symbolize the PC of each female cat (see Fig. 7).

Population behavior

The proposed complex dynamical model allows us to study the growth of the population over time. Since the model considers stochastic parameters, it produces different results with each execution of the code. Therefore, it is important to implement the model a sufficiently large number of times to obtain expected outcomes and draw meaningful conclusions. We conduct 1000 simulations over a 10-year period, taking the average weekly total population from all the tests.

Since the simulations start in the first week of the breeding season, it takes some time for the population to increase. Afterward, from approximately the second year onward, a periodic growth becomes noticeable, which is typical behavior for seasonal breeders (see Fig. 8).

The carrying capacity (CC) is reached for the first time around the third breeding season and this occurs periodically in subsequent years, being this the peak of the population. After reaching CC the population begins to decrease almost automatically and after that it returns to the increasing behavior but at a slower rate before decreasing again due to the non-breeding season.

The value U established as an upper bound for the weekly removal of vertices is proposed as a function of half the carrying capacity. This is motivated by the fact that the logistic growth model for populations experiences a change in the growth rate when this value is reached. This, combined with the inverse relationship between the weekly joint survival probability and the population size, facilitates effective self-regulation of the population around the carrying capacity.

Sensitivity analysis on interactions

Many parameters of the complex dynamical system are based on real data found in existing literature, supporting the model’s reliability. However, there are values for which we had to make assumptions that, at first glance, might yield unrealistic results. Probably the most significant of these is the estimate for the number of interactions.

If our assumptions overestimate the weekly number of primary sexual interactions between male and female cats, significant changes in the outcomes should occur if these interactions decrease. To understand the extent of the impact of our estimates on the total number of individuals, we perform a sensitivity study. For each n we conduct 100 simulations over a 10-year period.

Regardless of the reduction in interactions, a periodic growth behavior is observed in each case (see Fig. 9 a)). As expected, fewer interactions directly impact the growth of the population. However, it is noteworthy that this impact remains significant until the primary encounters are reduced to one-fifth of their original number, at which point the population starts to follow a clear decreasing tendency in the maximum reached (see Fig. 9 b)).

After the third year in the simulations, the periodic growth of the population begins to stabilize. In contrasting the cases of no reduction and a reduction to one-tenth of the interactions, there is a significant variation in the total population, with a difference of 30 and 20 individuals between the respective minimum and maximum values reached (see Fig. 10).

For secondary interactions, we study the impact of removing the lower bound of our estimate by performing 1,000 simulations over a 10-year period (Fig. 11 a)). Additionally, we conduct a similar sensitivity analysis by reducing the secondary interactions while keeping the original estimate of the number of primary interactions fixed (Fig. 11 b)).

Results show that the number of sexual interactions between male and female cats is an important factor contributing to the maximum population increase. Nevertheless, it is not as significant as the intrinsic biological features of the species, such as the short durations of gestation, lactation, and weaning, as well as the high ovulation and conception rates of female cats.

A reduction in interactions between female and male free-ranging cats can naturally serve as a control method for the population; however, a substantial decrease is necessary to achieve significant results. Unfortunately, preventing cats from interacting is nearly impossible, especially when a large number are already established in an area.

Statistical parameters

The life expectancy of a free-ranging cat is not well understood due to various influencing factors, including diseases, ownership status, urban landscape, and access to resources7. According to14, it is estimated to be less than five years, while20 indicates that it is around three years. Other demographic parameters, such as the mean, median, and mode age of the population, are also challenging to determine.

According to our model, after performing 1000 simulations and taking the average values of all outcomes, the weekly mean age of the population exhibits a periodic behavior attributed to the seasonal breeding of free-ranging cats (see Fig. 12). The mean age decreases when litters are produced and increases during the rest of the time. Although stability seems not to be clear as there is an increasing tendency, it is reached after approximately three more years (see Fig. 12 b)).

During each simulation, the weekly mean ages of the removed individuals were recorded (see Fig. 13 a)). We attribute the concentration of data in the lower part of the illustration to the low survival rate of young individuals and the difficulty free-ranging cats face in surviving many years. The weekly and overall mean ages at death across all simulations can be seen in Fig. 13 b). Although the weekly mean age at death is challenging to predict, it tends to stabilize over time. Notably, the proximity of the mean age and the mean age at death is an important result. Additionally, these values are also close to the mode of SS.

Discussion and conclusions

The presented models address fundamental questions about the population of free-ranging cats, such as how it grows in space and why it is self-sustaining. Both models are based on biological and social features related to the population, incorporating registered data, and the results obtained enhance the reliability of each model.

On the one hand, the PDE model effectively addresses the displacement abilities of free-ranging cats and how different colonies may connect through the population’s diffusive component. On the other hand, the dynamical network model more accurately captures the periodic growth behavior of the population. Future works should consider modifications to the time-dependent (periodic) parameters in the PDE system to achieve stability in a periodic solution, ensuring that the growth behavior is accurately represented. Furthermore, additional implementations in the complex model should explore dividing individuals into different colonies and incorporating migration parameters between them, as discussed in14.

In order to address the limitations of this study, both models should be calibrated using real data; specifically, this would make the PDE system more realistitc. This includes incorporating geographical data in both the domain and the boundary conditions. Concerning the network model, it seems appropriate to take into account spatial effects. As a natural outcome of this modifications, we could expect to compare quantitatively the results of the two models.

It is important to notice that there is a difference in the scales considered by each model. The reaction-diffusion framework focuses on a macro-spatial scale that allows us to assume a diffusive behavior of the population. On the other hand, the discrete dynamical system considers smaller spatial scales, in which the motion of every individual should be taken into account. However, this information is not available and does not seem very realistic to consider some sort of random motion for every cat.

Other studies related to the dynamics of free-ranging cats have used daily or six-month time steps14,23,27. To our knowledge, this is the first time a weekly-time step has been proposed for our discrete dynamical system, along with the prediction of statistical parameters for the population–data that has not been extensively explored. Further analyses should be conducted within the model, considering alternative distributions for the SS parameter, such as exponential, Weibull, or log-normal. Different distributions may be better suited to various real-world scenarios, and predicting additional measures of central tendency would provide valuable complementary insights.

The continuous model highlights the need for social regulation in response to the issue of abandonment. Creating laws and ensuring their proper enforcement is essential, and additional measures, such as low-cost sterilization campaigns and the rescue and adoption of free-ranging cats, should also be considered. Furthermore, the analysis of the discrete model reveals that intrinsic factors of the population (e.g., high ovulation and conception rates) enable it to self-sustain around the carrying capacity of the inhabited area. Therefore, it is crucial to design and implement effective population control methods.

The implementation of a control method in both models is an important area to explore in detail. The PDE model can be studied through the theory of Optimal Control of PDEs, where control variables are introduced into the system along with a functional to optimize, typically representing the costs associated with the control policy. A similar study is conducted in34 using the theory of Optimal Control of ODEs. In the case of the discrete dynamical system, the proposed control method should be integrated into the dynamics at varying rates to predict its effectiveness. Comparable studies are carried out in14,23, which compare different regulation methods.

The three most studied and implemented control methods for the population of free-ranging cats are lethal control (LC), trap-neuter-return (TNR), and trap-vasectomy/hysterectomy-return (TVHR). Each method leads to a reduction in the population’s interactions, which is shown to be a natural control method in Sect. 3.2. LC reduces the number of interactions through the permanent removal of individuals; TNR suppresses sexual behaviors; and TVHR causes non-effective sexual encounters, as the sexual instinct of cats is not altered at all.

LC has been shown to be an ineffective population control method unless there is a high capture rate23. The most significant limitation of LC is that if just two individuals encounter and mate, the population can continue reproducing. Additionally, this method is controversial because it involves taking lives. TNR and TVHR are more widely accepted in society, and each method reduces the probability of reproduction due to the presence of both intact and altered individuals in the population.

The analysis in14 shows that TVHR is more effective than TNR. Although TNR and TVHR are similar control methods, they lead to different dynamics. In TNR, intact male cats redirect to intact female cats, as neutered female cats are part of the population. In contrast, with TVHR, intact male cats cannot distinguish between intact and altered female cats, resulting in a positive rate of non-effective sexual encounters, which contributes to the decline of the population.

Although both TNR and TVHR have been proven effective as population control methods in practice17,26, they also have disadvantages. In the case of female cats, hormonal activity continues after hysterectomy, and long-term exposure to sexual hormones is a risk factor for the development of mammary cancer29, while ovariohysterectomy significantly decreases this risk32; the earlier it is performed, the better. Nevertheless, TNR increases the life expectancy of cats, resulting in prolonged exposure of wildlife to cats, which are known to be surplus killers21,22.

Reaching sustainability in a system does not necessarily mean eradicating the population of free-ranging cats; for example, they can contribute to controlling various pests (e.g., rodents, some insects, pigeons). Since there is no perfect control method, we emphasize the necessity for innovative regulatory policies. These could involve a strategic combination of the most common control methods, leveraging the dynamics of the free-ranging cat population.
