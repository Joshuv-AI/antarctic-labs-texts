Clinical Characteristics and Prognostic Prediction of Secondary Solid Malignancies in Patients With Diffuse Large B‐Cell Lymphoma and Follicular Lymphoma
Authors: Lulu Zhang, Jiahao Tian, Qunli Xiong, Haimei Ye, Zhao Wang, Yu Wang, Chengcheng Guo, He Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSecondary primary solid malignancies (SPSMs) significantly impact long‐term outcomes in lymphoma patients. However, subtype‐specific differences remain unclear, such as diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and follicular lymphoma (FL).MethodsThis study collected 1,377 DLBCL and 489 FL patients, identifying 50 DLBCL and 31 FL cases with SPSMs. Clinical characteristics, SPSM types, and survival were compared in these 81 patients. Demographic, clinical, treatment‐related variables (including radiotherapy for primary lymphoma, recorded as yes/no), and survival outcomes were collected. Overall survival (OS) was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier estimates and Cox proportional hazards models. The cumulative incidence of SPSMs was evaluated with competing risk analysis (death as a competing event), and group comparisons were performed with Gray's test. Prognostic factors identified in univariable analysis (p < 0.05) were included in a multivariable Cox model. A nomogram was developed, with discriminative ability assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsSPSMs were observed more frequently in FL (6.34%) than DLBCL (3.63%). Thyroid cancer predominated (22.2%, 18/81). DLBCL patients developed SPSMs earlier than FL patients (median 28.47 vs. 41.77 months, p = 0.031), though cumulative incidence accounting for competing risks did not differ significantly (Gray's test p = 0.34). DLBCL patients with SPSMs had inferior OS compared to FL patients (p = 0.04). Non‐GCB DLBCL showed greater SPSM diversity and survival disadvantage. Multivariable analysis identified FL (vs. DLBCL) subtype (HR = 0.328, p = 0.018), bone marrow infiltration (HR = 2.815, p = 0.014), initial radiotherapy before SPSM diagnosis (HR = 3.475, p = 0.005), and shorter time to SPSM development (HR = 0.973 per month, p = 0.021) as independent prognostic factors for worse OS. The nomogram model showed acceptable discrimination, with an AUC of 0.761 in the time‐dependent ROC analysis.ConclusionThis study provides novel insights into SPSM characteristics and prognostic differences in DLBCL and FL patients within a Chinese cohort. SPSMs in FL patients were observed more frequently, while DLBCL patients, particularly those with non‐GCB subtypes, experience earlier SPSM onset and poorer survival. The validated nomogram, incorporating lymphoma‐related factors, enables personalized risk stratification. However, the single‐center design, small sample size (n = 81), and lack of SPSM‐specific data limit generalizability, necessitating multi‐center studies with comprehensive SPSM characterization for validation.

Introduction

Malignant lymphomas are a diverse collection of tumors originating from lymphatic tissue, categorized into Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and non‐Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) [1]. NHL represents approximately 90% of all lymphoma cases, making it one of the most prevalent types. In recent years, advances in cancer treatment and early detection have improved the prognosis and survival of NHL patients, who now live longer with better disease control due to long‐term treatment and supportive care [2, 3]. However, all cancer survivors are at risk for developing the second primary solid malignancies (SPSMs) [4]. The SPSM was defined as the first subsequent primary cancer occurring at least 2 months after the first cancer diagnosis [5]. NHL survivors have a comparatively elevated lifetime risk of SPSMs among all cancer types [6]. Notably, the elevated risk of developing a SPSM poses a serious threat to survivors, accounting for approximately 16% of all cancer diagnoses and underscoring the need for urgent investigation [7]. While prior studies, primarily based on the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, have explored SPSM incidence and risk factors, they often lack detailed clinical characterization and are subject to demographic and regional biases [8, 9, 10, 11].

Diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common subtype, accounting for over one‐third of cases, followed by follicular lymphoma (FL), a more indolent form, as the second most frequent. Notably, both subtypes are also associated with a significantly elevated risk of SPSMs [6]. The spectrum of SPSMs also varies by lymphoma subtype, with lung, liver, skin, breast, colorectal, and prostate cancers reported more frequently among survivors [12]. However, few studies have systematically compared the clinical characteristics and prognostic outcomes of SPSMs in these subtypes within a non‐Western population. This study aims to address this gap by analyzing a Chinese single‐center cohort, providing novel insights into SPSM characteristics and prognostic differences between DLBCL and FL patients, and developing a prognostic nomogram to guide personalized surveillance.

Methods

Patient Selection

This study included DLBCL and FL patients with diagnosed SPSMs in Sun Yat‐Sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC) between May 2020 and May 2025. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) pathologically confirmed diagnosis of DLBCL or FL; (2) diagnosis of SPSMs at least two months after the initial lymphoma diagnosis. This threshold was chosen to specifically capture metachronous malignancies and to minimize the inclusion of synchronous tumors that might have been pre‐existing but detected during initial lymphoma staging. This operational definition is consistent with prior studies focusing on treatment‐associated secondary cancers [5]. Accordingly, SPSMs diagnosed within 2 months were considered synchronous and were excluded; all included SPSMs were classified as metachronous; (3) age greater than 18 years; (4) availability of complete clinical data and follow‐up information. Covariates of interest included demographic and clinical characteristics, types of malignancies, and survival outcomes. Additionally, data on initial lymphoma treatments (chemotherapy and radiotherapy) prior to SPSM diagnosis were collected. Information on radiotherapy was collected as a binary variable (yes/no) referring to initial radiotherapy administered for the primary lymphoma before SPSM diagnosis. Detailed radiotherapy parameters, including treatment indication, target site, field, and dose, were not uniformly available in this retrospective dataset and, therefore, were not included in the analysis. All data were extracted from medical charts within the hospital information system (HIS). The study was approved by the ethical committee of SYSUCC (B2025‐039‐01).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.3.1 (the R Project for Statistical Computing, www.r‐project.org) and Prism 5.01 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables were presented with their median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were described as frequency counts and proportion percentages. The overall survival (OS) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was assessed using Kaplan–Meier estimates and the Log‐rank test. OS was defined from the date of SPSM diagnosis to death from any cause or last follow‐up. To account for the competing risk of death before SPSM detection, the cumulative incidence of SPSM was estimated using the cumulative incidence function (CIF), treating death without prior SPSM as a competing event. Time was measured from the date of primary lymphoma diagnosis to SPSM occurrence, death without SPSM, or last follow‐up. CIFs were compared between DLBCL and FL using Gray's test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify prognostic factors for OS among patients with SPSMs, and variables with p < 0.05 in univariable analyses were entered into the multivariable model. For Cox proportional hazards models, FL was set as the reference category for the lymphoma subtype variable. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patients Characteristics

Between May 2020 and May 2025, a total of 1377 patients with DLBCL and 489 patients with FL were identified, among whom 50 DLBCL patients and 31 FL patients developed SPSMs. Among the cohort, SPSMs were observed in 3.63% of DLBCL cases and 6.34% of FL cases (Figure 1). These values represented crude cohort proportions rather than population‐referenced incidence risks. Given the longer follow‐up in FL (median 36.8 months, IQR 20.4–50.3) compared with DLBCL (median 24.6 months, IQR 12.3–38.5) (Table S1), the higher observed proportion in FL should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect differential survival time and surveillance intensity rather than a true excess risk. Eighty‐one patients with SPSMs were enrolled in the subsequent analysis. Baseline clinical and pathological characteristics were detailed in Table 1. The median age at first primary malignancy was 57 (range 29–75) years, and the median age at SPSM diagnosis was 61 years (range 31–81), respectively. In the overall cohort, over 30% of patients had tumor stage II and IV disease at initial lymphoma diagnosis, while over 40% had tumor stage I disease at SPSM diagnosis. 32.1% (26/81) of the patients had bone marrow infiltration. All 81 patients received chemotherapy for primary lymphoma, and 54.3% (44/81) additionally received radiotherapy prior to SPSM diagnosis. This reflects a conditional characteristic of the SPSM subcohort, as patients managed without systemic therapy are less likely to be captured in a retrospective SPSM‐focused analysis and do not represent the overall treatment patterns of DLBCL or FL at our center.

Distribution and Survival Outcome of SPSMs in DLBCL and FL

Thyroid cancer exhibited the highest incidence of SPSM, with rates of 22.2% (18/81), 18.0% (9/50), and 29.0% (9/31) in the overall cohort, DLBCL group, and FL group, respectively. In the DLBCL group, breast cancer and lung cancer followed, each with an incidence of 12.0% (6/50 and 6/50, respectively) (Table 2). In the FL group, lung cancer and colorectal cancer ranked next, with incidences of 29.0% (9/31) and 12.8% (4/31), respectively (Table 2). Notably, several SPSM types observed in the DLBCL group—including bladder, nasopharyngeal, gastrointestinal stromal tumor, endometrial, esophageal, laryngeal, prostate, and tongue cancers—were not detected in the FL group. Sex‐specific analyses revealed marked differences in SPSM distribution consistent with known epidemiological patterns (Table 3). In the overall SPSM cohort, thyroid (22.0%), breast (19.5%), and lung (17.1%) cancers were the most common SPSMs among female patients, whereas lung (20.0%) and thyroid (22.5%) cancers predominated among male patients. As anticipated, breast and endometrial cancers were observed exclusively in women, while prostate, esophageal, tongue, and laryngeal cancers occurred only in men. This sex‐specific pattern was also evident within lymphoma subtypes;e.g., in the DLBCL group, all breast SPSMs occurred in female patients (24.0%, 6/25), with no cases identified among male patients Table S2. The interval from lymphoma diagnosis to SPSM development was significantly shorter in DLBCL patients than in FL patients (median: 28.47 vs. 41.77 months; p = 0.031) (Figure 2A). This difference was pronounced in the first two years (0–24 months: Median 10.5 vs. 15.8 months, p = 0.0213) and between 2 to 4 years (24–48 months: Median 34.4 vs. 39.1 months, p = 0.0419), but not beyond 48 months (median 53.1 vs. 52.9 months, p = 0.617) (Figure 2B). To account for the competing risk of early mortality in DLBCL, a competing‐risk analysis was performed, which showed no significant difference in the cumulative incidence of SPSM between the two groups (Gray's test p = 0.34; Figure 2C). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that DLBCL patients with SPSMs had significantly shorter OS than FL patients with SPSMs (p = 0.04) (Figure 2D). Because prognostic differences between DLBCL and FL are well established, this comparison is presented primarily to describe the SPSM cohort and to support subsequent risk‐stratification analyses.

Distribution and Survival Outcome of SPSMs in GCB and Non‐GCB DLBCL

To better understand the characteristics of DLBCL patients with SPSMs, we divided them into the GCB and non‐GCB groups. Eighteen patients had a GCB subtype, and 32 patients had a non‐GCB subtype. The detailed clinical characteristics and pathological data are presented in Table 4. There was no evidence of systematic differences in baseline characteristics or primary lymphoma treatment between the GCB and non‐GCB DLBCL patients with SPSMs. Thyroid and lung cancers were more prevalent in the GCB group, whereas colorectal and breast cancers were more common in the non‐GCB group (Figure 3A). The median interval between the diagnosis of primary lymphoma and the development of SPSMs was shorter in the non‐GCB group (25.9 months) than in the GCB group (33.78 months) (Figure 3B). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated that GCB patients with SPSMs had longer OS than non‐GCB patients with SPSMs (Figure 3C). Although these differences failed to reach statistical significance, the subgroup analysis was based on a limited sample size and may have been underpowered to detect modest differences.

Analyses of Survival Status and Prognostic Factors for Overall Patients With SPSMs

We analyzed the survival status of the overall cohort by the Kaplan–Meier method. Although the median OS was not reached, the 3‐, 4‐, and 5‐year survival rates were 86%, 82%, and 73%, respectively (Figure 4A). Using Cox proportional hazards regression, we identified factors associated with the survival of SPSM patients, as shown in Figure 4B. Univariate analysis revealed that lymphoma type (p = 0.047, HR = 0.414), bone marrow infiltration (p = 0.013, HR = 2.638), initial radiotherapy (radiotherapy prior to SPSM diagnosis) (p = 0.005, HR = 3.432), and time to SPSMs (i.e., the interval between the diagnosis of primary lymphoma and the development of SPSMs) (p = 0.025, HR = 0.974) were significantly associated with OS. These variables were subsequently included in a multivariate analysis. The multivariate results identified lymphoma type (p = 0.018, HR = 0.328), bone marrow infiltration (p = 0.014, HR = 2.815), initial radiotherapy (radiotherapy prior to SPSM diagnosis) (p = 0.005, HR = 3.475), and time to SPSMs (p = 0.021, HR = 0.973) as independent prognostic factors (Figure 4C). Given the established prognostic differences between DLBCL and FL, the association between lymphoma subtype and OS in the SPSM cohort is not unexpected. We report this finding primarily to characterize the SPSM cohort and to justify adjustment for lymphoma subtype in subsequent multivariable analyses and risk‐stratification modeling. Specifically, the presence of DLBCL and bone marrow infiltration was associated with poorer survival outcomes, whereas receipt of initial radiotherapy was associated with inferior OS. Additionally, a longer time to SPSM was inversely associated with mortality in the multivariable model (HR = 0.973 per month, p = 0.021). This association should be interpreted cautiously, because time to SPSM is conditional on surviving long enough to develop SPSM and may reflect survivorship/selection and surveillance effects rather than a causal protective effect of later SPSM onset.

Cox‐Based Prognostic Nomogram Construction and Validation for Overall Survival

Based on the independent prognostic factors identified in the multivariate analysis, including lymphoma type, bone marrow infiltration, initial radiotherapy (radiotherapy prior to SPSM diagnosis), and time to SPSMs, a nomogram was developed to predict 24‐ and 48‐month overall survival probabilities in SPSM patients (Figure 5A). In the nomogram, DLBCL subtype, bone marrow infiltration, receipt of initial radiotherapy, and shorter time to SPSM were assigned higher point values, resulting in higher total scores and lower predicted survival probabilities. Notably, the inclusion of time to SPSM is intended for prognostic prediction and risk stratification rather than causal inference, given its potential susceptibility to survivorship and surveillance biases. Each variable was weighted according to its hazard ratio, and the total score derived from these variables was inversely associated with survival probability: Higher total scores corresponded to lower 24‐ and 48‐month survival rates. To evaluate the predictive performance of the model, time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis at 48 months was performed. The integrated model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.761, outperforming individual predictors (lymphoma type: AUC = 0.594; bone marrow infiltration: AUC = 0.675; initial radiotherapy: AUC = 0.649; time to SPSM: AUC = 0.545) (Figure 5B).

Discussion

This study systematically investigates the clinical characteristics and prognostic differences of SPSMs in patients with DLBCL and FL within a Chinese single‐center cohort, providing data from a non‐Western population that complements prior registry‐based analyses. Our analysis revealed that FL patients exhibited a higher crude proportion of SPSMs compared to those with DLBCL, consistent with prior population‐based studies [10]. However, DLBCL patients experienced earlier SPSM onset (median latency 28.47 vs. 41.77 months, p = 0.031) and poor OS (p = 0.04). Among DLBCL cases, non‐GCB subtypes showed a trend toward greater SPSM diversity and shorter survival (p = 0.12), though this difference was not statistically significant. A prognostic nomogram incorporating lymphoma subtype, bone marrow infiltration, initial radiotherapy, and SPSM latency demonstrated good discriminative performance (AUC = 0.761). The higher observed proportion of SPSMs in FL likely reflects its indolent disease course and prolonged survival, which provide a longer temporal window for secondary malignancies to develop, rather than a true excess risk, consistent with SEER‐based analyses [9]. In contrast, the earlier SPSM onset in DLBCL may be driven by its aggressive biology and intensive treatment exposure, with survivorship‐related factors also potentially contributing [13]. Beyond these disease‐intrinsic factors, specific treatment‐related exposures may also contribute to SPSM risk in a subtype‐dependent manner. Bendamustine, an alkylating agent widely used in FL, has been reported to be associated with an elevated risk of secondary malignancies, potentially through mechanisms involving DNA damage and prolonged T‐cell depletion [14, 15, 16]. In our cohort, approximately 19.4% (6/31) of FL patients with SPSMs had received R‐bendamustine prior to their SPSM diagnosis, whereas no DLBCL patients in the SPSM cohort had bendamustine exposure. Although detailed data on cumulative dose and treatment duration were unavailable, this observation raises the possibility that bendamustine exposure may have contributed to the development of SPSMs in a subset of FL patients. These findings underscore the importance of considering treatment‐related heterogeneity—alongside differences in disease biology—when interpreting subtype‐specific SPSM patterns. Notably, thyroid cancer was the most frequent SPSM in our cohort (22.2%, 18/81), which contrasts with Western studies where lung cancer predominates [6]. A population‐based analysis from the Guangzhou Cancer Registry reported that the age‐standardized incidence rate of thyroid cancer increased from 6.46 per 100,000 in 2010 to 33.15 per 100,000 in 2020, representing an almost five‐fold rise, with significant spatial clustering in urban and coastal districts [17]. This distinctive regional cancer epidemiology may partly explain the relatively high proportion of thyroid cancer observed among SPSMs in our cohort. Breast cancer appeared as a relatively common SPSM in the overall ranking; however, sex‐stratified analysis showed that SPSMs occurred only in female patients in the DLBCL (6/25, 24.0%) and FL (2/16, 12.5%) cohorts. This pattern is consistent with the gender‐specific epidemiology of breast cancer, as new and treatment‐related breast cancer primarily affects women [18]. Therefore, this observation does not rule out the potential contribution of lymphoma‐directed therapy or shared susceptibility factors. Larger studies with detailed treatment exposure and tumor characteristics are needed to elucidate the relative contribution of these factors. Notably, previous studies have also shown lung cancer risks were significantly elevated after CLL/SLL and FL but not after DLBCL, highlighting potential subtype‐specific risk profiles for SPSMs [6]. Consistent with this observation, lung cancer was observed more frequently in FL than in DLBCL in our SPSM cohort (29.0% vs. 12.0%). The distribution of lung cancer may also be influenced by population‐specific risk factors, including smoking prevalence, environmental or occupational exposures, and sex distribution [19]. However, smoking history and exposure‐related variables were not consistently recorded in this retrospective dataset, precluding exposure‐adjusted analyses. Therefore, the observed subtype differences in lung cancer frequency should be interpreted cautiously and warrant validation in larger multicenter cohorts with detailed exposure information. Previous studies have also reported poorer survival among NHL patients who develop SPSMs [20]. Genetic predispositions, including RET proto‐oncogene mutations, and environmental exposures, such as radiation from lymphoma therapy, may further contribute [21]. Importantly, the observed association between prior radiotherapy for primary lymphoma and inferior survival after SPSM diagnosis should be interpreted cautiously. In the absence of detailed information on radiotherapy indications, target sites, and delivered doses, this finding may reflect confounding by disease extent or treatment indication (e.g., bulky or high‐risk disease requiring radiotherapy) rather than a direct causal effect of radiotherapy‐related toxicity. Potential mechanisms such as systemic stress or reduced hematopoietic reserve, therefore, remain speculative and cannot be directly evaluated in the current study. Together, these findings underscore the importance of regional and demographic context when interpreting SPSM patterns.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective, single‐center design at Sun Yat‐sen University Cancer Center introduces potential selection bias and restricts generalizability, particularly given regional variations in SPSM patterns (e.g., thyroid cancer predominance). In addition, because this analysis focused on patients who developed SPSMs, the cohort represents a selected survivorship subpopulation. This may partly explain why all FL patients in the SPSM cohort had received systemic chemotherapy, and such findings should not be extrapolated to the overall treatment patterns of indolent FL. The small sample size (n = 81) limits statistical power, especially for subgroup analyses like GCB vs. non‐GCB comparisons. Furthermore, standardized incidence ratios (SIRs) could not be calculated because population‐level incidence data matched for age, sex, calendar year, and person‐time at risk were unavailable; therefore, the reported SPSM proportions reflect frequencies within this survivor cohort rather than absolute risk estimates. Importantly, cause‐specific mortality data were not uniformly available in this retrospective cohort. Therefore, we could not distinguish deaths attributable to primary lymphoma progression from those related to SPSMs or other causes, which may affect the interpretation of the observed survival differences between lymphoma subtypes. The inferior OS observed in the DLBCL group may partly reflect the inherently aggressive biology of DLBCL rather than SPSM‐related mortality per se. Future studies incorporating cause‐specific survival analyses are warranted to better clarify the relative contributions of lymphoma‐ and SPSM‐related mortality. The absence of SPSM‐specific data, including tumor stage, histologic subtype, and treatment modalities (e.g., surgery, radiotherapy), precluded tumor‐specific survival analyses, which are critical given the prognostic heterogeneity across SPSM types. The reliance on lymphoma‐related prognostic factors, such as non‐GCB subtype and bone marrow infiltration, may reflect underlying disease characteristics, treatment selection, and unmeasured confounding, whereas SPSM‐specific factors are likely more direct predictors of outcome. These limitations highlight the need for multicenter, prospective studies with comprehensive SPSM characterization to validate prognostic models and clarify underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the clinical characteristics and prognostic differences of SPSMs in DLBCL and FL patients within a Chinese single‐center cohort. FL patients exhibited a higher SPSM incidence, while DLBCL patients, particularly those with non‐GCB subtypes, showed a trend toward earlier SPSM onset and poorer survival. The developed nomogram, incorporating lymphoma subtype, bone marrow infiltration, initial radiotherapy (radiotherapy prior to SPSM diagnosis), and SPSM latency, offers a practical tool for survival prediction. However, the single‐center design, small sample size, and lack of tumor‐specific survival data limit the generalizability of these findings. Future multi‐center studies are needed to validate the nomogram and refine personalized surveillance strategies for high‐risk lymphoma patients.
