Sorghum husks as potential low cost adsorbent for Congo red adsorption
Authors: Salah Ud Din, Edwin Andrew Ofudje, Khairia Mohammed Al-Ahmary, Zahrah Alqahtani, Saedah R. Al-Mhyawi, Ibtehaj F. Alshdoukhi, Nuha Y. Elamin, Jamelah S. Al-Otaibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The increasing release of synthetic dyes in industrial effluents poses major environmental challenges. This study evaluated raw (SHR) and 0.1 M H3PO4 acid-treated (SHT) sorghum husks as low-cost adsorbents for removing Congo Red (CR) from aqueous solutions. SHT achieved a maximum removal efficiency of 85.26% at 40 mg dosage and pH 2, outperforming SHR (76.17% at pH 3 and dosage of 45 mg). Langmuir isotherm analysis demonstrated higher adsorption capacity for SHT (77.14 mg/g) compared to SHR (57.55 mg/g), inferring improved monolayer coverage after treatment. Kinetic modeling indicated that SHR followed a pseudo-first-order model, while SHT was better explained by a pseudo-second-order model, indicating chemisorption as the dominant mechanism. Thermodynamic results confirmed spontaneous and endothermic adsorption with positive enthalpy change (ΔH) values of 13.52 kJ/mol for SHR and 25.26 kJ/mol for SHT at 40 °C. Regeneration studies demonstrated that SHT retained over 65% desorption efficiency after six cycles, highlighting good reusability. FTIR revealed that Congo Red sorption onto treated sorghum husk is governed by hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking interactions, and possibly electrostatic attractions involving functional groups like hydroxyls, amines, and aromatic rings. These findings indicate that acid-treated sorghum husk is an effective, sustainable, and economical alternative to commercial adsorbents for azo dye removal in wastewater treatment.

Introduction

Among several pollutants, dyes are of great environmental concern to water pollution due to their annihilating environmental impacts. The paint, textiles, pharmaceutics, printing, and pigment processing sectors often contribute to the release of industrial effluents causing an increased in the biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), total organic carbon (TOC) and heavy metals contents in water bodies, which could harm the environment1. The quality of water is often compromised by the presence of dyes resulting in limiting photosynthesis, reducing sunlight penetration, altering gas solubility, and restricting aquatic growth2. Report has it that the environment every year, receives the discharged of approximately 60,000 tons of dyes, which are mainly azo dyes3. Various health challenges such as respiratory issues, eye, skin, and stomach irritation, headache, vomiting, and sleep disturbances can be caused from exposure to the breakdown of dyes releases3,4.

Congo red (CR), a dye commonly used as raw material in textile, printing, plastics, and paper industries can degrades into benzidine, a chemical already identified to be carcinogen and can as well harm the bladder, nervous system, and also poses danger to the aquatic life5,6. With reports on its toxicity, a value of 1 mg/L has been set as the maximum allowable concentration of CR in wastewater7,8.

Continued development and research of effective degradation strategies of CR is fueled by its toxic nature which presents a serious environmental and health threat. Chemical methods like coagulation-flocculation, photocatalytic, ozonation, advanced oxidation processes, and electrochemical treatment have been engaged in the treatment of wastewater contaminated with dyes9,10. But the complication of sludge disposal coupled with heighten the risk of secondary pollution often limit their applications11. Biological methods on the other hand are eco-friendly which requires little or no chemical and generates minimal sludge, but with the complex aromatic nature of synthetic dyes, it’s very difficult to break them down biologically12,13. Among the physical methods like adsorption, and ozonation which have been used to eliminate synthetic dyes from wastewater, adsorption is seen to be simple, low cost, effective, availability of adsorbents, easy to regenerate the adsorbent, and don’t produce secondary pollutants, thus making it an alternative option15–17.

Activated carbon that is widely utilised as adsorbent is facing limitations in its application because of the high costs of preparation and purity18. As a result of the above, different low-cost adsorbents, like wastes from agricultural products are currently been explored19,20. Wastes, including rice husk, sugarcane bagasse, wallnut shell, cow wastes, coal ash, eggshell, and peat, have been studied as adsorbents for dye removal9,10,21,22. It is essential to research on the use of agricultural waste that can be easily prepared to produce high-efficiency adsorbents. Sorghum, a major feedstock for liquor production, is highly rich in cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, which contains multiple free hydroxyl groups that create effective conditions for dye removal. Sorghum is the world’s fifth-largest cereal crop and generates significant amounts of agricultural waste known as sorghum husk annually. While a portion of this waste is used as animal feed, a large quantity is converted into cellulosic biofuels this practice can lead to environmental pollution and contradicts current concepts of sustainable development and ecological protection23. Chemical activation and functional modification of bio-based adsorbents have emerged as effective strategies to improve adsorption performance. For instance, chemically activated Opuntia ficus-indica (prickly pear cactus) via acid treatment followed by thermal treatment significantly increased its surface area and porosity, that resulted in enhanced methylene blue removal under optimized conditions24. Similarly, Boumezough et al.25 formulated a nanocomposite made from the combination of Opuntia ficus-indica powder and iron oxide nanoparticles which resulted in a highly efficient adsorbent for methylene blue removal, benefiting from both magnetic properties for facile recovery and improved surface chemistry.

Sorghum husk, which is a major agricultural by-product, is rich in cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, that could provide abundant hydroxyl, carbonyl and other groups that can bind effectively with dye molecules. Its annual generation in large quantities, combined with the limited existing high-value uses, makes it an environmentally friendly and a low-cost option for wastewater remediation. Unlike activated carbon, whose production is costly and resource-intensive, sorghum husk can be processed with minimal energy input while providing comparable adsorption potential. From a sustainability perspective, converting sorghum husk into an adsorbent not only addresses waste management issues but also contributes to circular economy principles by converting waste into a value-added product.

The present work investigates both raw and acid-treated sorghum husks for the adsorption of Congo Red from aqueous solutions. The research is structured to (i) characterize the physicochemical properties of the adsorbents, (ii) systematically study the effects of operational parameters such as pH, contact time, dosage, temperature, and initial concentration, (iii) model the adsorption process using isotherm, kinetic, and thermodynamic analyses, and (iv) evaluate the reusability of the adsorbent through regeneration studies. This approach enables a comprehensive assessment of sorghum husk’s efficiency and practical applicability for dye removal in wastewater treatment.

Preparation and characterization of adsorbent

The sorghum husks (SH) were collected in Mowe market, Ogun state, Nigeria and cleansed with water to eliminate dirt that adhered to the surface. The SH were then air dried for 7 days and thereafter, placed in an oven at 80 °C. Dried adsorbent material was ground using a mortar and pestle and the powdered sorghum husks were then soaked in 0.1 M of H3PO4 with occasional stirring for 12 h at a temperature of 25 °C acid activation. Thereafter, distilled water was used to washed the filtrate several times, and dried in an oven at 80 °C to dry for 3 h and stored in an airtight container. Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (CARY630 NBY, Thermo Fisher Scientific Instrument, Waltham, MA, USA) and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM; FEI Sirion200, NLD) were used to characterized the adsorbent for functional group and morphological determinations. Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) measurement was done using Quantachrome NOVA 2200 C, (USA).

Determination of point of zero charge (pHPZC)

The point of zero charge (pHPZC) of the adsorbents was examined via the pH-drift method in 0.01 M NaCl solution as the background electrolyte. Briefly, the initial pH (pH₀) of several 50 mL NaCl solutions was adjusted to values in the range of 2.0 and 8.0 using 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH. 0.05 g of adsorbent was dropped into each solution, and the suspensions were agitated at 200 rpm for 12 h at 25 ± 1 °C to allow equilibration. After this stage, the final pH (pHf) was noted, and the change in pH (ΔpH = pHf − pH₀) was determined. A plot of ΔpH against pH was drawn, and the pHPZC was taken as the point where the curve intersected the x-axis. Measurements were performed in triplicate, and mean values were reported.

Preparation of dye wastewater and batch adsorption experiment

Congo red stock solution of 1000 mg/L was made by dissolving 1 g of Congo red in 1000 ml distilled water. Desired concentration were prepared using appropriate dilution method. For the adsorption experiment, 25 mg of sorghum husk was mixed with 25 mL of CR solution (210 mg/L) in 100 mL conical flasks. The pH of CR solution was adjusted with 0.1 M NaOH or HCl prior to the addition of the adsorbent and agitated on the shaker, and at various time intervals, samples were taken and evaluated with UV-Vis spectrophotometry at 498 nm (Shimadzu UV-3600 UV-Vis-NIR). Regeneration studies

Studies on regeneration were carried out to assess the reusability and long-term stability of sorghum husk biomass as an adsorbent. Following each adsorption cycle, acetic acid was employed to desorb the retained dye molecules. The adsorbent was subsequently washed, dried, and reused in the next cycle to evaluate its performance over multiple uses. After desorption, the mixture was filtered, and UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-3600 UV-Vis-NIR) was to analyzed the filtrate. Results and discussions

Characterizations

The graph in Fig. 2 depicts the FT-IR spectra of treated sorghum husk before and after CR adsorption which showed significant changes in functional group peaks, suggesting their involvement during the adsorption process. The band seen around 3300–3400 cm−1 in both samples corresponds to O–H and/or N–H stretching vibrations, attributed to amine groups and hydroxyl often found in lignocellulosic materials33. After CR adsorption, the intensity of the band becomes intense with slightly shifted, indicating the formation of hydrogen bonds between Congo Red molecules and surface functional groups. Within 2920–2850 cm−1 region, bands corresponding to aliphatic C–H stretching vibrations remain present with minimal change, suggesting that these groups most likely do not play a significant role in the uptake process. But, a notable increase in intensity is seen near 1650–1600 cm−1 after the uptake, which can be assign0ed to carbonyl C=O or aromatic C=C stretching34. This suggests the relationship between the lignin components of the sorghum husk and the aromatic rings of the dye could be π–π interactions.

FT-IR of SHT before and after adsorption process.

Additional changes were seen around 1510–1450 cm−1 after adsorption, where enhanced aromatic vibrations appear, reinforcing the likely interaction between the sorghum husk and Congo Red’s aromatic structure35. Furthermore, the observed peaks between 1250 and 1000 cm−1, are associated with C–N and C–O stretching vibrations, which also shows increased intensity and slight shifts after the uptake process35. This suggests the possible complexation or formation of new bonds between the functional groups on the adsorbent surface and the dye. New bands seen at a position less 800 cm−1 in the post-adsorption spectrum correspond to interactions involving the sulfonate groups of Congo Red or out-of-plane bending vibrations, further confirming successful dye attachment. The FT-IR results indicate that CR uptake by the sorghum husk is enhanced by electrostatic attractions involving functional groups such as hydroxyls, amines, and aromatic rings and possibly π–π stacking interactions, hydrogen bonding.

The graph in Fig. 3 shows the SEM images of the surface morphology of treated sorghum husk before and after the adsorption process. The SEM in Fig. 3a, reveals a rough and highly porous structure which is characterized by irregularly shaped particles with some voids and gaps, suggesting a large surface area. The presence of numerous active sites in this morphology suggests that it would be favorable for adsorption, making the treated sorghum husk a suitable material for removing contaminants. On the contrary, Fig. 3b obtained after the adsorption process, indicates significant changes in the surface properties. The pores and voids which were previously visible are now less seen, with the surface appearing more compact and smoother. This is an indication that Congo Red has successfully adhered to and filled the surface pores, leading to reduction in the overall porosity. The changes in surface texture and reduction in porosity provide visual information of successful adsorption.

Figure 4a shows the pore size distribution of raw (SHR) and treated (SHT) sorghum husk. The pore diameters were found to be in the range of 2–50 nm depicting mesoporous structure for both samples, which is favorable for adsorption of large dye molecules such as Congo Red. The cumulative pore volume rose with pore diameter, and the treated sorghum husk exhibited a higher pore volume compared to the raw sample. This indicates that chemical treatment successfully improves the porosity by opening the blocked pores, thereby enhancing the accessibility of adsorption sites. Figure 4b presents the BET adsorption isotherm plots of SHR and SHT and the linear BET region indicates that SHT (86.36 m2/g) possesses a higher specific surface area than SHR (61.45 m2/g ), as indicated by its lower slope (inverse relationship between slope and surface area). This further confirms that treatment improves the surface area available for dye–adsorbent interactions. These characteristics explain the higher adsorption capacity of SHT observed in the batch adsorption experiments, since more active sites and diffusion pathways became available for the dye uptake.

Effect of Congo red concentrations and contact time

The plots in Figs. 5 and 6 illustrates the influence of initial dye concentration and contact time on the adsorption of Congo Red dye by raw and treated sorghum husk. At the beginning of the sorption process (within the first 30 min), the rate of adsorption is rapid across all concentrations. This rapid sorption is due to the abundance of available active sites on the surface of the sorghum husk and as these sites begin to become occupied with CR molecules, the rate of sorption slows down30,36. After 80 and 100 min of uptake process for treated and raw samples, the sorption process begins to level off, suggesting that equilibrium has been attained. When compared with raw sorghum husk, treated sorghum husk shows a significantly improved adsorption capacity across all conditions. For instance, at around 40 min and 210 mg/L of CR, the treated husk adsorbs 40.1 mg/g of dye, whereas only about 34.1 mg/g was adsorbs by the raw husk. However, with a much higher contact time of 100 min and at the same dye concentration, the adsorption capacity of the treated sample surged to 72.5 mg/g, while that of the raw husk rose to 54.2 mg/g. This considerable increase signify that the treatment process enhanced the adsorption characteristics of the sorghum husk possibly because of the removal of natural barriers like waxes or oils that back adsorption pathways in raw material37.

Greater adsorption capacities was observed with a clear trend at higher initial concentrations in respect to the initial concentration of Congo Red. For instance, maximum adsorption was attained for both adsorbents at 210 mg/L, and this is because higher concentrations promote more interactions between the dye molecules and the sorghum husk via the acceleration of the driving force for mass transfer. In the study reported by Sarita et al.38, it was noted that a rapid sorption rate was noticed within the first 30 min, and this was attributed to the numerous active sites available on the adsorbent surface. But the adsorption rate declined as these sites became progressively occupied, with equilibrium reached at 100 min. Furthermore, Sarita et al.38 noticed that a rise in initial dye concentration resulted in higher adsorption capacities, portraying the impact of concentration gradients in enhancing mass transfer in sorption process.

Effect of temperature

This graph which illustrates the effect of temperature on the adsorption efficiency of Congo Red dye by raw sorghum husk (SHR) and treated sorghum husk (SHT) is shown in Fig. 7. As the temperature increases from 25 to 40 °C for SHT and 45 °C for SHR, the adsorption efficiency show an upward trend in dye removal, indicating that adsorption is enhanced at higher temperatures. This behavior suggests that higher temperatures provide the energy needed to overcome activation barriers, facilitating more dye molecules to interact with and adhere to the adsorbent surface. At 25 °C, SHT achieves 57.4% removal, compared to 53.1% for SHR and as the temperature rises to 40 °C, the treated husk reaches a plateau at 85.2% removal, indicating that it has reached its maximum adsorption capacity. In contrast, raw husk only achieves a maximum of 78.4% removal, with a similar plateau behavior occurring after 45 °C. The results from this study is similar to the report by Abubakar et al.39 where it was observed that the adsorption of Congo Red dye onto sorghum husk and groundnut shell biosorbents was enhanced at elevated temperatures. This was however contrary to the study by Abdul Malik, et al.37 who revealed that the adsorption of Congo Red onto rice husk, rice husk char and chemically modified rice husk char adsorbents decreased with increasing temperature, indicating that the adsorption process is exothermic in nature.

Effect of dosage

The effect of dosage on the sorption of CR dye by raw sorghum husk (SHR) and treated sorghum husk (SHT) is revealed in Fig. 8. It was observed that as the dosage increases from 25 to 45 mg and 40 mg for SHR and SHT respectively as both adsorbents show a general increase in adsorption efficiency which could be as a result of the increase in active sites of adsorption provided by the large surface area at higher adsorbent dosage35. For SHR, the percentage adsorption rises from 52.51% at 25 mg to a maximum of 76.17% at 45 mg, followed by a slight decline to 71.11% at 55 mg. A similar trend is observed with SHT, where adsorption increases from 56.70% at 25 mg to 85.26% at 40 mg and then decreases marginally to 81.11% at 55 mg.The consistent and higher performance of SHT compared to SHR across all dosage levels highlights the positive impact of treatment. The treatment process, may have removed impurities from the pore of the adsorbent thus making more adsorption sites available for the dye molecules uptake. The slight decline in efficiency beyond the optimal dosage suggests a saturation of active sites or possible agglomeration of particles which could reduce the effective surface area available for adsorption.

pH effects

The impact of pH on the sorption of CR by raw sorghum husk (SHR) and treated sorghum husk (SHT) is shown in Fig. 9a. It was observed that maximum adsorption occurs at pH of 2 for the treated samples and pH of 3 for the raw adsorbent, followed by a decline at higher pH levels, and it is evident that acidic conditions are more favorable for the dye uptake30. At low pH values, particularly around pH 2 to 3, the surface of the sorghum husk is highly protonated, acquiring a positive charge33. Congo red, being an anionic dye, is strongly attracted to the positively charged surface through electrostatic interactions. This results in high adsorption efficiency at this pH, a the high concentration of H+ ions enhances the electrostatic attraction between the dye and the adsorbent, leading to the peak removal efficiency observed at lower pH. The values of point of zero charge (pHPZC) (Fig. 9b) measured for SHR and SHT were 3.9 and 3.3 respectively, confirming that the surface is positively charged at pH values below this point and negatively charged above it40. This explains the high adsorption rate noticed at acidic pH, where pH < pHPZC, as the electrostatic attraction between the adsorbent and the dye is maximized40,41. As the pH approaches and exceeds the pHPZC, surface deprotonation reduces positive charge and eventually imparts a net negative charge, leading to electrostatic repulsion with the negatively charged dye species and lowering adsorption efficiency. This surface charge behavior aligns with the FTIR evidence of functional groups such as –OH and –NH2 involvement in binding at low pH, as well as with the kinetic data indicating enhanced interaction strength for the treated sample.

As the pH increases beyond 3, the hydrogen ions concentration in the solution decreases, leading to the gradual deprotonation of functional groups on the adsorbent surface42. This change reduces the positive surface charge, weakening the electrostatic attraction between the dye molecules and the sorghum husk. Additionally, at higher pH levels, the surface may become negatively charged, causing electrostatic repulsion with the negatively charged dye, further reducing adsorption efficiency42. Thus, the reduced dye uptake at higher pH levels can be attributed to both loss of attractive forces and the onset of repulsive interactions. When comparing SHR to SHT, the treated sorghum husk continues to show higher adsorption across all pH levels. This improvement is typically due to the acid treatments that enhance surface characteristics by making the functional groups like hydroxyl (–OH) and carboxyl (–COOH) more accessible42.

Arnata et al.35 equally reported that as the solution pH rose from 2.0 to 10.0, congo red dye sorption capacity and removal efficiency using by cellulose from sago frond decreased surged from 9.30 to 6.20 mg/g and from 93.02 to 62.01%, respectively. Similar study by Akande et al.43 on adsorption of CR by activated corn cobs reported a clear trend of decreasing removal efficiency as pH increased, with peak uptake at acidic pH of 4.8 and as it was said that the acidic pH conditions favored the adsorption of Congo red dye since there was minimal competition from hydrogen ions for the available adsorption sites on the adsorbent.

Kinetics studies

The kinetic data for the adsorption of Congo red onto raw sorghum husk (SHR) and treated sorghum husk (SHT) offer valuable insights into the underlying mechanisms and rate-controlling steps of the adsorption process. These were analyzed using pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, and intra-particle diffusion models as revealed in Figs. 10, 11 and 12 and the values of their constants are as listed in Table 1. For raw sorghum husk (SHR), the PFO model provides a strong fit, with high R2 values ranging from 0.991 to 0.998. The calculated equilibrium sorption capacities (Qe(cal)) and the experimental (Qe(exp) closely matched, and the percentage sum of squared errors (%SSE) is low in most cases. In contrast, the treated sorghum husk (SHT) exhibits a better fit with the PSO model. This is evident from the consistently high R2 values (0.977 to 0.997) and very low %SSE, particularly at higher equilibrium concentrations. The close agreement between experimental and calculated Qe values further supports the dominance of this model for the treated husk. When the PFO model best fits the experimental data, it indicates that the rate of adsorption is primarily governed by the availability of active sites on the surface of the adsorbent, and that the adsorption process is dominated by physical interactions like weak electrostatic attractions or van der Waals forces44. On the other hand, the PSO model fit for SHT suggests that the sorption is more likely governed by chemisorption, that entails stronger interactions like hydrogen bonding, covelent bonding, or π–π interactions between functional groups on the adsorbent and dye molecules45. The difference in best-fit models between SHR (PFO) and SHT (PSO) suggests that chemical treatment not only increases the number of active sites but also changes the adsorption mechanism from physical to chemical in nature.

The intra-particle diffusion model also contributes to understanding the rate-limiting steps. For both SHR and SHT, the data indicate multi-stage adsorption processes, with reasonably high R2 values, particularly for SHT. The intra-particle diffusion rate constant (Kp) increases with equilibrium concentration, suggesting enhanced dye transport into the pores over time. However, the presence of high values of intercepts (Ci values) in both cases implies that boundary layer diffusion or external mass transfer plays a role, particularly in the early stages of adsorption. This means that intra-particle diffusion is involved but is not the only rate-limiting factor.

Isotherms evaluations

The adsorption isotherm parameters for Congo red onto raw and treated sorghum reveal notable improvements in performance after treatment, as demonstrated by the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Dubinin–Radushkevich (D-R) models (Figs. 13, 14, 15 and the values of their constants are as listed in Table 2. According to the Langmuir isotherm, which assumes monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous surface, the maximum adsorption capacity (Qmax) increased from 57.55 mg/g for raw sorghum to 77.14 mg/g for treated sorghum. This indicates a substantial enhancement in adsorption capacity post-treatment. Additionally, the separation factor (RL) decreased from 0.17 to 0.14, suggesting more favorable adsorption conditions for treated sorghum. Both the raw and treated sorghum showed better fit (R2 = 0.98 for SHR and R2 = 0.95 for SHT).

The Freundlich isotherm, which describes adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces, further supports these findings. The Freundlich constant (KF), which represents adsorption capacity, doubled from 22.15 for raw to 46.192 for treated sorghum. The intensity of adsorption, expressed as 1/n, also improved, decreasing from 0.650 (raw) to 0.327 (treated), with values less than 1 confirming favorable adsorption30. As observed, the model fit improved greatly for treated sample, with R2 increasing from 0.884 to 0.970, indicating greater surface heterogeneity introduced by the treatment. The theoretical saturation capacity (Qs) of the D-R isotherm rose from 38.18 mg/g (raw) to 54.12 mg/g (treated), aligning with the Freundlich and Langmuir results. The slightly increased from 3.86 kJ/mol to 6.19 kJ/mol in the values of E, inferred a physical adsorption mechanism (since E < 8 kJ/mol)18. The R2 value also rose from 0.920 to 0.980 for treated sorghum, indicating improved model fit and possibly enhanced microporous structure.

Table 3 presents a comparative analysis of the adsorption capacities of various adsorbents for Congo red adsorption under different experimental conditions. Among the reported adsorbents, SHT (77.14 mg/g) and SHR (57.55 mg/g), investigated in this study, demonstrated relatively high adsorption capacities, surpassing most conventional materials. For example, the SHT value exceeded that of neat chitosan (55 mg/g) and chitosan/fly ash composite (13.05 mg/g) reported by Elhady et al.46, highlighting the improved efficiency of SHT in dye removal. Furthermore, the performance of SHR (57.55 mg/g) was comparable to or higher than adsorbents such as cuticle from Cereus spp. (52.63 mg/g)47 and modified coffee waste (34.36 mg/g)48. The adsorption capacity of SHR was also slightly higher than that of Hevea brasiliensis seed shells activated with NaCl (50.51 mg/g)28, and comparable to theH2PO4-activated form (55.87 mg/g)30, despite operating at lower adsorbent dosage and milder conditions. Likewise, SHR and SHT both outperformed walnut shell (22.90 mg/g)36, showing their potential as more efficient low-cost alternatives.

Thermodynamics

Negative values for both SHR and SHT across all temperatures studied for the Gibbs free energy change (Table 4 as obtained from Fig. 16), indicating that the adsorption process is spontaneous for both materials. In the case of SHR, ΔG decreases from − 1.12 kJ/mol at 25 °C to − 5.31 kJ/mol at 55 °C, whereas in the case of SHT, it decreases from − 1.36 to − 8.42 kJ/mol within the same temperature range. This inferred that the spontaneity of adsorption is enhanced at higher temperatures. At 40 °C, the adsorption of Congo red is endothermic with both adsorbents exhibiting positive enthalpy change (ΔH) values of 13.52 kJ/mol for SHR and 25.26 kJ/mol for SHT30,39. It is evident that the treated husk requires more energy to facilitate adsorption demonstrating higher ΔH value, possibly because of stronger interactions between dye molecules and the adsorbent surface and the presence of more active sites. The value of ΔH° could also provides insight into the adsorption mechanism. For instance, ΔH° values below 20 kJ/mol indicate physical adsorption dominated by weak van der Waals forces, whereas values between 20 and 80 kJ/mol indicate chemisorption involving stronger interactions like covalent bonding or ion exchange50,51. The ΔH° value for SHR (13.52 kJ/mol) falls within the physisorption range, which is consistent with the pseudo-first-order kinetic fit, indicating predominantly physical interactions. On the contrary, the higher ΔH° value for SHT (25.26 kJ/mol) lies within the lower chemisorption range, supporting the pseudo-second-order kinetic fit and indicating that acid treatment promotes stronger chemical interactions, likely through enhanced availability of active functional groups capable of hydrogen bonding and π–π interactions.

Entropy change (ΔS) values are also positive for both materials at 40 °C: 6.82 J/mol·K for SHR and 11.06 J/mol·K for SHT. Positive ΔS values imply an increase in randomness or disorder at the solid–liquid interface during the adsorption process.

Desorption

The desorption data for Congo red adsorption by raw sorghum husk (SHR) and treated sorghum husk (SHT) illustrate important differences in their reusability and stability as adsorbents over multiple cycles (Fig. 17). For SHR, the initial desorption efficiency starts at 80.2%, indicating a good ability to release the adsorbed dye in the first cycle. However, with each subsequent cycle, the desorption efficiency gradually decreases, dropping to 62.1% by the sixth cycle. This steady decline suggests that raw sorghum husk experiences a loss in adsorption site availability or structural changes after repeated use, leading to diminished desorption and potentially reduced overall adsorption capacity. In contrary, the treatment seems to have enhanced the surface properties or accessibility of adsorption sites of SHT with higher initial desorption efficiency of 88.5%, indicating improved regenerability. The efficiency of SHT decreases more gradually than SHR over the six cycles tested, ending at 65.2%. In all, the results demonstrates that treatment of sorghum husk not only improves the sorption capacity but also enhances the material’s ability to be reused and regenerated effectively.

Proposed adsorption mechanism

The sorption of Congo Red onto sorghum husk surface could involves multiple interactions between dye molecules and functional groups. FT-IR spectra showed shifts and intensity changes in peaks assigned to hydroxyl (–OH), amine (–NH2), and aromatic (C=C) groups after adsorption, suggesting their involvement in binding. This is an indication of electrostatic attraction mechanism. At solution pH below the pHPZC, the sorbent surface is positively charged due to protonation of –OH and –NH2 groups, which enhances electrostatic attraction toward the anionic sulfonate groups (–SO2−) of CR52. This further corroborated the finding from the FT-IR investigation. In addition, π–π stacking interactions occur between the aromatic moieties of lignin present in sorghum husk and aromatic rings of the dye53. While hydrogen bonding is also likely possible between –N or –O atoms in the dye structure and –NH2/–OH groups on the adsorbent. For the treated sorghum husk, acid activation could remove surface impurities, and increase surface area, and exposes more active functional groups, strengthening these interactions. Thus, the combined action of electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, explains the adsorption mechanism. A schematic representation of these interactions is shown in Fig. 18.

Effect of interfering ions on the adsorption of congo red (CR)

The effect of coexisting ions on the adsorption of Congo Red by raw (SHR) and treated (SHT) sorghum husk is shown in Fig. 19. The results show that the presence of foreign ions reduced the adsorption efficiency compared to single-solute systems, though the extent of inhibition varied with the type of ion. It was observed that monovalent cations (Na+ and K+) showed the least interference, with only a slight reduction in dye uptake. This could be because these ions exert relatively weak competition with CR molecules for the adsorption active sites. On the contrary, divalent cations like Ca2+ and Mg2+ showed stronger inhibitory effects, causing lower CR removal. Their higher charge density could have enables them to compete favorably for the negatively charged binding sites on the surface of the sorghum husk, thus reducing the number of available sites for CR molecules. Among the anions, chloride (Cl⁻) showed mild interference, while sulphate (SO42−) had a more pronounced effect. The stronger inhibition by sulphate could be attributed to its divalent nature, which increases electrostatic interactions with surface functional groups, which limits CR sorption. Despite these competitive effects, both SHR and SHT maintained appreciable sorption performance, with SHT consistently outperforming SHR, indicating that chemical treatment improves the surface functionality and charge density. Overall, the order of interference observed was: Na+ < K+ < Cl− < Mg2+ < Ca2+ < SO42−.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the potentials of both raw (SHR) and acid-treated (SHT) sorghum husks as effective low-cost biosorbents for the sorption of Congo Red dye from aqueous media, with the treated form showing better performance. Batch experiments revealed that SHT achieved a maximum removal efficiency of 85.26% at an optimal dosage of 40 mg and pH 2, compared to 76.17% for SHR at pH 3 at an optimal dosage of 45 mg. Langmuir isotherm modeling indicated higher monolayer adsorption capacity for SHT (77.14 mg/g) compare to SHR (57.55 mg/g), suggesting the positive impact of chemical treatment on binding site availability. Kinetic studies indicated that SHR followed a pseudo-first-order model, whereas SHT fit the pseudo-second-order model, indicating chemisorption as the dominant mechanism in the treated material. Thermodynamic analysis confirmed the endothermic and spontaneous nature of adsorption, with greater spontaneity at higher temperatures. Regeneration tests revealed that SHT maintained over 65% desorption efficiency after six cycles, highlighting its reusability. These findings suggest that acid-treated sorghum husk is a promising and sustainable alternative to commercial adsorbents for azo dye removal, with potential for practical wastewater treatment applications. However, the study is limited to single-solute systems, batch-scale laboratory experiments, and acid activation as the sole modification method. Future research should explore multi-contaminant removal, examined the performance in continuous flow systems, and investigate other environmentally friendly modification techniques.
