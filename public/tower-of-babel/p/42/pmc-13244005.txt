We read with great interest the report in this issue of JACC: Case Reports by Syed and colleagues1 that describes successful transcatheter mitral valve-in-valve (ViV) replacement within a thrombosed Edwards SAPIEN M3 transcatheter mitral valve replacement (TMVR). Experience with the M3 system internationally remains limited; however, with recent CE (Conformité Européenne) marking and U.S. Food and Drug Administration approval in late 2025, broader adoption is anticipated. As use expands, refinement of patient selection, valve surveillance strategies, postimplant anticoagulation, and complication management will be essential. Ensuring reproducible access to treatment options for these high-risk patients, with complications such as bioprosthetic valve thrombosis (BPVT), is of particular importance.

BPVT remains a complex and understudied complication following bioprosthetic mitral valve replacement. Data are limited; however, in a recent pooled analysis of 6,170 patients undergoing transcatheter or surgical mitral valve replacement (SMVR), the incidence of BPVT was 5.05 events per 100 patient-years. Although not statistically significant, rates were numerically higher following TMVR (2.7% vs 1%).2 Potential mechanisms include leaflet injury during crimping, altered leaflet-frame interaction, valve underexpansion, and patient-related factors.2,3

Clinically significant BPVT is defined by clinical sequelae (eg, thromboembolism or worsening mitral stenosis or regurgitation) with either bioprosthetic valve dysfunction (BVD) stage 2-3 or confirmatory imaging (hypoattenuating leaflet thickening [HALT] or reduced leaflet motion [RLM]); in the absence of clinical sequelae, both BVD stage 3 and confirmatory imaging are required.4 Early TMVR experience suggested rates of clinically significant BPVT to be between 4% and 8%,5 consistent with the ENCIRCLE trial of the M3 system, which reported a 6.6% incidence at 1 year.6

The incidence and clinical significance of subclinical BPVT remain uncertain. Zorman et al2 reported a higher rate of subclinical BPVT compared with clinically significant thrombosis (adjusted incidence rate ratio: 4.62). In contrast, routine cardiac computed tomography (CT) was not performed in the ENCIRCLE trial, where reported rates of subclinical BPVT (5.9%) were similar to clinically significant events (6.6%).6 Further investigation is required; however, insights from transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) suggest an important association between subclinical leaflet thrombosis and progressive noncalcific valve degeneration.7,8

Evidence to guide BPVT management is limited, and current recommendations are largely extrapolated from mechanical valve thrombosis.9 Vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) are recommended for clinically significant BPVT unless urgent intervention or fibrinolysis is required.9,10 Historically, systemic fibrinolysis was associated with substantial risk, including mortality rates of 10% to 15% and embolic complications of approximately 12%.3 Surgical mortality has been reported as high as 18%.11

Contemporary fibrinolytic strategies have demonstrated improved outcomes. The “ultra-slow, ultra-low” regimen (25 mg alteplase over 25 hours, repeated up to 200 mg over 8 days) has shown a 90% success rate with a 6.7% complication rate (embolism: 1.7%, major bleeding: 1.7%, death: 0.9%) in mechanical valve thrombosis.12 However, this approach has not been validated in BPVT. In this hostile clinical context, transcatheter ViV intervention represents a potential alternative for selected cases.3,10

Syed et al1 describe a successful transseptal mitral ViV procedure using a 29-mm SAPIEN 3 Ultra Resilia valve within a thrombosed M3 valve, adding to the limited experience from the ENCIRCLE trial.6 Transseptal ViV implantation is a mature technique in other settings, including failing surgical bioprostheses, annuloplasty rings, and mitral annular calcification.13 In ENCIRCLE, the overall reintervention rate was 6.4%, including 3 ViV procedures and 4 surgical replacements.6 These data suggest that valve programs adopting TMVR must be prepared to manage complications with either high-risk surgery or urgent ViV intervention.

ViV TMVR in the setting of valve thrombosis raises important concerns, particularly the risk of systemic thromboembolism during instrumentation of a thrombosed left-sided valve. Consequently, cerebral embolic protection using the Sentinel device (Boston Scientific) was employed in the reported case. However, this provides incomplete thromboembolic protection, with no coverage of the left vertebral, upper limb, or abdominopelvic circulation, and there are no data supporting its routine use in this setting.

Another important aspect of this case was the decision to proceed without repeat cardiac CT. While understandable given the patient's instability, preprocedural CT is generally considered essential in transcatheter mitral ViV procedures to assess valve–left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) interaction and the risk of neo-LVOT obstruction.14 In this case, prior imaging suggested that the new valve would land entirely within the M3 frame without additional interaction with the LVOT. However, this may not be generalizable across TMVR platforms. Where feasible, repeat CT should be obtained to assess LVOT risk, optimal valve sizing, and thrombus burden to inform embolic risk. Routine follow-up CT was not performed in ENCIRCLE; however, future studies should consider incorporating systematic CT imaging to better define subclinical BPVT and support future procedural planning.

Current guidelines by the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology and European Society of Cardiology recommend at least 3 months of anticoagulation with a VKA following bioprosthetic SMVR but acknowledge insufficient evidence to guide therapy after TMVR.9,10 In the present case, life-threatening BPVT occurred despite more than 3 months of therapeutic anticoagulation with a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC), shorter than the 6 months mandated in ENCIRCLE.6 Given the apparently higher incidence of BPVT following TMVR, it is notable that VKAs are not routinely used, likely reflecting increased bleeding risk in this population.

In ENCIRCLE, only 19% of patients received VKAs, with no apparent difference in BPVT rates compared with DOACs; however, event numbers were small, and noninferiority cannot be assumed.6 Further study is required to define optimal anticoagulation strategies following TMVR. In the interim, VKAs should be strongly considered, and anticoagulation maintained as long as clinically feasible.

Syed et al1 should be commended for a well-executed, lifesaving intervention in a patient facing either imminent death or complex surgery with unacceptable operative mortality. The M3 TMVR system represents a promising therapy for patients unsuitable for surgical mitral valve replacement or transcatheter edge-to-edge repair. However, BPVT remains a significant challenge. Continued advances in device design, systematic evaluation of subclinical BPVT, optimization of anticoagulation strategies, and refinement of ViV techniques—including the roles of cardiac CT and embolic protection—will be critical as this technology evolves.
