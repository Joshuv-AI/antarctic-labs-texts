UK Biobank whole-genome sequencing reveals robust contributions of rare variants to complex-trait heritability
Authors: Hyein Jung, Hae-Un Jung, Ji-One Kang, Ji Eun Lim, Bermseok Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe missing heritability of complex traits remains a key challenge in human genetics. Recent studies have highlighted the potential role of rare variants in addressing this gap. However, their contribution has been difficult to quantify due to small sample sizes and large standard errors in large-scale whole-genome sequencing data.ResultsLeveraging whole-genome sequencing data from 348,977 unrelated White British participants in the UK Biobank, we analyze 5,378,300 linkage disequilibrium-independent rare variants to estimate their heritability for five complex traits. Background

Understanding the missing heritability of complex traits remains a central challenge in human genetics [1]. Although genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified thousands of common variants associated with complex traits [2, 3], they collectively explain only a fraction of the total heritability observed in pedigree-based estimates [1]. This gap, known as the missing heritability problem, has motivated the search for genetic contributors beyond common variants [4–6], including rare variants [7–9], structural genetic variations [10], gene–gene interactions [11] and gene–environment interactions [12].

Rare variants are of particular interest, as they are hypothesized to account for a substantial portion of unexplained heritability [13–15]. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) provides a comprehensive view of both coding and non-coding regions, enabling the detection of rare variants often missed by array-based genotyping or exome sequencing [16, 17]. A recent WGS-based study by Wainschtein et al. [15] demonstrated that rare variants contribute to heritability for traits such as height and body mass index (BMI), but their estimates were limited by a relatively small sample size (~ 25,000) and large standard errors.

To overcome these limitations, we leveraged WGS data from 348,977 unrelated White British individuals in the UK Biobank [18] —over tenfold larger than previous WGS studies—to obtain more precise and robust estimates of rare variant heritability. Specifically, we estimated the contribution of rare variants (minor allele frequency [MAF]: 1 × 10⁻4–1 × 10⁻2) to the heritability of five complex traits—standing height, BMI, platelet count, systolic blood pressure, and glycated hemoglobin— broadening the phenotypic scope beyond height and BMI examined in earlier work.

Using over 5.3 million linkage disequilibrium (LD)-independent rare variants, we applied both genome-wide complex trait analysis (GCTA) [19] and Bayesian Optimized Linear Transformation for Restricted Maximum Likelihood (BOLT-REML) [20] to obtain consistent heritability estimates. Consistent estimates were obtained across three independent 110,000-sample subsets using both GREML and BOLT-REML, and additional replicated subsampling and meta-analysis further reduced standard errors and confirmed the robustness of the findings. These findings refine our understanding of the genetic architecture of complex traits through large-scale rare variant analysis.

Results

Selection and genomic annotation of rare variants

We divided the WGS dataset of 348,977 unrelated White British samples in the UK Biobank into three independent subsets (A, B, and C; N = 110,000 each Set A was used as the baseline to define a core set of rare variants, which were filtered using stringent criteria (see Methods). To ensure consistency, only variants passing the same filters in all three sets were retained.

Through this process, we selected 5,378,300 independent rare variants (MAF between 1 × 10⁻4 and 1 × 10⁻2) from the WGS data. Detailed variant selection criteria and LD pruning parameters are described in the Methods section. The distribution of rare variants across the three independent datasets (sets A, B, and C) is summarized in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Annotation of rare variants using the RefGene database (updated on 2020–08–22 at UCSC) [21] with ANNOVAR (version 2020–06–07) [22] revealed a diverse distribution across genomic regions. Most variants were in the intergenic (48.04%; 2,583,912 variants) and intronic (36.98%; 1,989,164 variants) regions (Additional file 1: Table S2; Fig. 1). Additionally, ncRNA intronic (6.16%, 331,326 variants) and exonic (0.38%, 20,321 variants) variants were identified. The untranslated regions (UTR3 and UTR5), comprising 1.34% (71,844) of the variants, were also annotated.

Genomic distribution of rare variants identified in WGS. The pie chart shows the distribution of rare variants across genomic regions, based on annotations using the RefGene database

Additionally, exonic variants accounted for 1.11% (59,536 variants) of the total variants; among the exon variants, 97.17% (57,852 variants) were single nucleotide variants (Additional file 1: Table S3). A subset of variants included frameshift substitutions (1.23%, 732 variants), non-frameshift substitutions (1.12%, 666 variants), stop-gain mutations (0.12%, 73 variants), stop-loss mutations (0.02%, 12 variants), and non-synonymous single nucleotide variants (0.01%, six variants), all of which have the potential to disrupt protein structure and function. These results show that the vast majority of rare variants reside in non-coding regions, which may have important implications for understanding their contribution to complex trait heritability.

To further characterize the functional landscape of these non-coding rare variants, we performed additional annotation using FAVOR (Functional Annotation of Variants Online Resource), which integrates conservation, regulatory, and functional predictive scores across the entire genome [23]. Aggregating GENCODE-based categories into broader functional groups revealed that UTR variants, upstream/downstream regulatory regions, and exonic (coding + ncRNA) regions harbored substantially higher functional scores compared with intergenic or intronic variants. For example, the median CADD-phred scores were 3.71 for UTR variants and 5.68 for exonic (coding + ncRNA) variants, whereas intergenic and intronic groups showed markedly lower median scores (1.95 and 1.80, respectively). Similarly, the proportion of variants with CADD ≥ 15—a commonly used threshold for deleteriousness—was higher in UTR (10.1%) and exonic/non-coding exonic regions (27.6%) compared with intergenic (1.49%) or intronic variants (1.84%). LINSIGHT and conservation scores showed consistent patterns, with regulatory or UTR variants displaying elevated signals of purifying selection and evolutionary constraint (Additional file 1: Table S4).

Heritability estimation using GCTA

We estimated the heritability of five traits (standing height, BMI, platelet count, systolic blood pressure, and glycated hemoglobin) across three independent subsets (A–C; each ~ 110,000 individuals; Additional file 1: Table S5) using the rare variant set. We used GCTA tools [19] to calculate heritability using a genetic relationship matrix (see Methods), with adjustments for age and sex. The estimated heritability for standing height was approximately 0.43 across all three sets, BMI ranged from 0.15 to 0.23, platelet count ranged from 0.20 to 0.25, systolic blood pressure from 0.13 to 0.16, and glycated hemoglobin from 0.10 to 0.12 (Additional file 1: Table S6).

To account for population stratification, principal component analysis was performed on the dataset (see Methods). After adjusting for the top 20 principal components (PCs) to account for population structure, the heritability estimates remained consistent across subsets (Additional file 1: Table S7 and Additional file 2: Fig. S1).

Additionally, we estimated the heritability of the rare variant set by adjusting 20 PCs of the rare variants. Height heritability ranged from 0.37 to 0.38 across the three sets, while BMI ranged from 0.11 to 0.20. Platelet count heritability ranged between 0.19 and 0.21, systolic blood pressure ranged from 0.12 to 0.13, and glycated hemoglobin ranged from 0.10 to 0.12 (Table 1 and Fig. 2).

Meta-analysis across datasets

Meta-analysis is a statistical method used to combine results from multiple datasets or studies, providing a comprehensive estimate of the effect size or parameters. We performed an inverse-variance weighted random-effects meta-analysis (using a REML estimator for between-subset variance) implemented in the metafor package (R version 4.4.0) [24] to integrate heritability estimates from all three subsets into a unified and robust summary. The meta-analysis results showed that the heritability estimates were 0.37 (standard error (S.E): 0.01) for standing height, 0.15 (S.E: 0.02) for BMI, 0.20 (S.E: 0.01) for platelet count, 0.13 (S.E: 0.01) for systolic blood pressure, and 0.11 (S.E: 0.01) for glycated hemoglobin (Additional file 1: Table S8).

Validation with BOLT-REML

To validate the robustness of our heritability estimates, we reanalyzed the data using BOLT-REML [25], a method well-suited for large-scale genomic datasets [20]. Unlike Genomic Relatedness-based Restricted Maximum Likelihood (GREML), BOLT-REML employs a Bayesian optimization approach for mixed-model parameter estimation. The analysis was adjusted for age, sex, and the top 20 PCs. Heritability estimates from BOLT-REML closely matched those from GREML across all traits and sets (A, B, and C; S.E.: 0.02 for all) (Table 2).

Sensitivity analysis

To further evaluate the robustness of our heritability estimates, we conducted additional sensitivity analyses. First, to ensure that the effects of common variants were not confounding the rare variant heritability estimates, we included a polygenic risk score (PGS) constructed from common variants as an additional covariate in the model (see Methods). Second, we generated five randomly sampled datasets (each containing 110,000 individuals, allowing sample overlap; Additional file 1: Table S9) and estimated heritability using BOLT-REML for each dataset. The resulting heritability estimates from the five replicates are summarized in Additional file 1: Table S10. When comparing the PGS-adjusted sensitivity results (Table 3) with the unadjusted BOLT-REML estimates (Additional file 1: Table S8), we observe an overall reduction in rare‐variant heritability after including the common-variant PGS as a covariate. Despite this attenuation, standing height, BMI, platelet count and systolic blood pressure still show clear evidence of rare-variant contributions, with heritability estimates of approximately 0.23, 0.10, 0.05, and 0.06, respectively, each accompanied by low standard errors. In contrast, glycated hemoglobin shows more substantial reductions, with heritability estimates decreasing to around 0.02 although their standard errors also decrease. These findings suggest that, while a portion of the variance attributed to rare variants is absorbed by the common-variant PGS, meaningful and independent rare-variant heritability remains for traits such as height, BMI, platelet count, and systolic blood pressure.

To further assess potential confounding due to LD between rare and common variants, we performed a joint multi-component (multi-GRM) analysis across 20 randomly sampled subsets (N = 30,000 each). Jointly modeling rare and common variants yielded rare-variant heritability estimates that were generally more conservative and exhibited substantial variability across replicates compared to the full dataset estimates (Additional file 1: Table S11). Specifically, the rare-variant contribution was reduced to near zero in most replicates for BMI and showed wide ranges for glycated hemoglobin (0 to 0.28). Partial persistence of rare-variant heritability was observed for standing height (0.02 to 0.18), platelet count (0 to 0.13), and systolic blood pressure (0.02 to 0.19). These findings suggest that while the joint model attenuates the estimates—indicating some shared variance with common variants—the rare-variant signal may not be fully explained by LD with common variants alone. However, the magnitude of these effects is smaller and less stable and therefore should be interpreted with caution.

Additionally, we adjusted systolic blood pressure [26] and glycated hemoglobin [27] for medication use and major lifestyle factors to assess their impact on rare-variant heritability. The results are summarized in Additional file 1: Table S12. For systolic blood pressure, the meta-analyzed rare-variant heritability was 0.05 (standard error = 0.009), which is consistent with the estimates obtained before medication adjustment. In contrast, for glycated hemoglobin, rare-variant heritability approached zero after medication and covariate adjustment, indicating that the underlying rare-variant signal for this trait is very weak.

Discussion

While GWAS have identified thousands of loci associated with complex traits [2, 3], much of the heritability remains unexplained [1]. Rare variants have been proposed as potential contributors to this missing heritability, but their precise impact remains uncertain due to limited sample sizes and methodological challenges in previous studies [15]. In this study, we leveraged whole-genome sequencing data from over 300,000 individuals in the UK Biobank to estimate the contribution of over 5 million LD-independent rare variants to the heritability of five complex traits. Using GREML and BOLT-REML, we found that rare variants explained 0.37 (S.E. 0.01) of heritability for height, 0.15 (0.02) for BMI, 0.20 (0.01) for platelet count, 0.13 (0.01) for systolic blood pressure, and 0.11 (0.01) for glycated hemoglobin. Additionally, a complementary sensitivity analysis adjusting for a PGS constructed from common variants revealed attenuated—yet still substantial—rare-variant heritability for height 0.23 (0.010), BMI 0.10 (0.008), platelet count 0.05 (0.011), and systolic blood pressure 0.06 (0.009), although their magnitude and stability vary across traits and should be interpreted with caution.

Our study builds on prior research, such as that by Wainschtein et al. [15], who demonstrated the contribution of rare variants to complex traits using WGS data. While their estimates focused on the overall heritability of traits using WGS, with values of 0.68 for standing height and 0.30 for BMI, these estimates reflected the cumulative contribution of all genetic variants across the genome and not only rare variants. Moreover, their estimates had significantly larger S.E (> 0.03 for both traits), likely because of the smaller sample sizes used in their cohorts. In contrast, we analyzed over 300,000 unrelated individuals from the UK Biobank and focused on more than 5 million LD-independent rare variants (MAF between 1 × 10⁻4 and 1 × 10⁻2). This enabled a more precise estimation of rare variant heritability, with standard errors ranging from 0.01 to 0.02. In addition to height and BMI, we examined three additional traits—platelet count, systolic blood pressure, and glycated hemoglobin—demonstrating that rare variants contribute to heritability across multiple complex traits, although the magnitude and stability of these effects vary across traits.

Investigating rare variants contributes not only to more accurate heritability estimation but also to elucidating the molecular pathways that drive complex trait variation and disease risk [28]. Importantly, quantifying the heritability attributable to rare variants also addresses a key component of the missing heritability problem, improving our ability to detect or localize potential causal variants and refining our understanding of the genetic architecture underlying complex traits [6]. Understanding the contributions of rare variants enhances our ability to uncover the biological mechanisms driving complex traits and disease [29], which may inform the development of precision medicine approaches. These insights can support more accurate disease risk prediction, identify potential therapeutic targets, and guide the design of targeted interventions [28, 30–34]. Moreover, the identification of functional variants across the genome provides a basis for generating new hypotheses, paving the way for future discoveries in genetic research.

This study demonstrates the utility of WGS in identifying rare variants that contribute to heritability, particularly in non-coding and regulatory regions that are often overlooked in genotyping arrays or exome sequencing [17]. Unlike methods focused solely on exonic regions, WGS provides a comprehensive view of the entire genome, allowing us to capture variants in intergenic (48.1%) and intronic regions (37%) [35, 36] as well as untranslated regulatory elements (e.g., UTR3 and UTR5) [37]. This approach enables not only heritability estimation but also a deeper understanding of genetic variants in previously underexplored regions. The inclusion of such non-exonic regions in our dataset enhances the ability to uncover the genetic contributions of rare variants, particularly in the regulatory and non-coding regions, which are crucial for understanding the mechanisms underlying complex diseases [35, 37, 38]. Consistent with this, recent studies have shown that rare noncoding variants—particularly those in UTRs and splice regions—can have substantial regulatory effects on protein levels, highlighting their impact on complex traits [39].

One limitation of this study is the lack of population diversity. Our analysis was conducted using data from the UK Biobank, which includes individuals of European ancestry. Consequently, the generalizability of our findings to populations with different ethnic backgrounds is constrained, potentially limiting the applicability of the results to more genetically diverse groups [40]. Additionally, although we successfully quantified the heritability contributions of rare variants, we did not investigate the biological mechanisms or functional pathways through which specific variants influence complex traits. This absence of functional validation reduces the clinical interpretability and applicability of our findings, highlighting the need for future research to integrate functional studies and diverse populations into similar analyses. Additionally, we did not adjust for sequencing batch effects (Data Field ID 32053) in the WGS data. Although the dataset includes 26 batch categories, preliminary analyses showed that heritability estimates remained nearly unchanged after batch adjustment, indicating that batch effects were minimal. Nonetheless, because including numerous batch covariates considerably increases computational burden, future work should explore more efficient model-fitting strategies to account for potential batch effects more precisely. Lastly, although we adjusted for the contribution of common variants using PGS, most of the available PGS were derived from GWAS that included UK Biobank participants (see Methods). As a result, the PGS and our WGS-based rare variant set are not fully independent, and a portion of the variance attributed to common variants may not have been completely accounted for. This limitation prevents full separation of common- and rare-variant contributions, which could be addressed more rigorously with multi-GRM (LDMS) models once computationally feasible in future work. Moreover, even after adjusting for major lifestyle factors such as BMI, smoking, and physical activity, the estimated rare-variant heritability for glycated hemoglobin remained close to zero. However, this pattern should be interpreted with caution, because the use of UK Biobank–derived PGS in a one-sample setting may have introduced over-adjustment bias. Future studies using fully independent discovery and target datasets, or multi-component models such as LDMS, will be required to disentangle true rare-variant effects from one-sample bias.

Conclusions

In summary, our results suggest a role for rare variants in explaining some of the missing heritability of complex traits. Using WGS data and analytical methods, we gained insights into the genetic factors that contribute to complex traits. Importantly, even after adjusting for polygenic scores derived from common variants, rare variants continued to account for a meaningful proportion of heritability for traits such as height, BMI, platelet count, and systolic blood pressure, although the magnitude and stability of these contributions vary across traits. These findings pave the way for future research to integrate rare variants into genetic studies and improve the ability to predict, diagnose, and treat complex diseases.

Methods

Study population and design

We used the UK Biobank Resource, a population-based database that recruited over 487,409 individuals between 2006 and 2010 [40]. For quality control of the samples, we used the following filter parameters of the Neale lab (http://github.com/Nealelab/UK_Biobank_GWAS): PC analysis calculation filter for selecting unrelated samples; sex chromosome filter for removing aneuploidy; filtering of PCs for European sample selection for determining British ancestry; and filters for selecting self-reported “White British”, “Irish”, and “White”. The total number of unrelated White British participants was 348,977.

The 348,977 samples were divided into three subsets: set A (N = 110,000), set B (N = 110,000), and set C (N = 110,000). This division was necessary because of the computational constraints of the system used to calculate the Genomic Relationship Matrix (GRM), as the maximum processing capacity was limited to 110,000 samples at a time. For each trait, individuals with missing phenotype values were excluded from the analysis. The final sample size for each trait is shown in Additional file 1: Table S5.

Set A consisted of unrelated White British Europeans, 54.5% of whom were female. Phenotypic information for these participants was collected during the initial assessment period (2006–2010; instance 0).

Set B consisted of unrelated White British Europeans, 53.4% of whom were female. Phenotypic information for these participants was collected during the initial assessment period (2006–2010; instance 0).

Set C consisted of unrelated White British Europeans, 54% of whom were female. Phenotypic information for these participants was collected during the initial assessment period (2006–2010; instance 0).

To ensure that this division introduced no bias, we compared key covariates (age and sex) across the three subsets (Additional file 1: Table S5). The mean age was highly comparable across sets, ranging from 56.75 to 56.82 years. The proportion of male participants was also similar, ranging from 45.54% to 46.64%. In addition, we generated a scatter plot of PC1 versus PC2 for Sets A, B, and C, which showed nearly identical distributions across the three subsets, indicating the absence of population stratification (Additional file 2: Fig. S2).

Selection of rare variants

WGS of 500,000 subjects was performed using Illumina Novaseq technology. The dataset underwent stringent quality control, including the identification of single nucleotide polymorphisms, indels, and structural variants, and was analyzed using tools such as GraphTyper and DRAGEN [18].

From the WGS data of 110,000 individuals in set A, we selected rare variants to establish a baseline for heritability estimation. Initially, 565,599,041 variants were identified. We applied the following filtering criteria [15]: a genotype missingness rate > 0.05 and Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium test P < 1 × 10⁻6. To identify LD-independent rare variants, we used the GCTA tool, which calculates LD scores within a 100 kb window and applies an R2 threshold of 0.05. Additionally, ultra-rare and common variants were further excluded using an MAF filter, retaining only those with MAF between 1 × 10⁻4 and 1 × 10⁻2.

To ensure consistency across datasets, we validated whether the baseline variants identified in set A were also present in sets B and C and retained only those meeting the same filtering criteria across all three datasets. Variants that satisfied the criteria in set A but were classified as ultra-rare or common in sets B and C were also excluded. This process ensured that the final rare variant maintained consistency and comparability across datasets. We identified 5,378,300 independent variants in the final rare variant set.

WGS data for set A were obtained in BED/BIM/FAM format, whereas WGS data for sets B and C were provided in VCF format via DNAnexus. The VCF files of sets B and C converted the data into BED/BIM/FAM format for further analysis using PLINK v2.00a3.1LM (May 19, 2022) [41].

Annotation of rare variants

Rare variants were functionally annotated using ANNOVAR (version 2020–06–07) with the RefGene database (UCSC, updated August 22, 2020) to obtain gene-based functional consequences within coding regions [22]. In addition, we applied the FAVOR annotator (version 1.1.1) to capture regulatory and functional information across both coding and non-coding regions [23]. FAVOR integrates diverse annotation resources, including ENCODE, RegulomeDB, CADD, LINSIGHT, PhyloP, PhastCons, and GTEx eQTLs, and was executed through the STAAR-compatible pipeline on a per-chromosome basis. The resulting annotations were merged and used for downstream analyses of variant distribution and functional enrichment.

Principal component analysis

Given the large sampling variance of rare variants across populations owing to their low frequency, principal component analysis was conducted on the rare variant set to capture the population structure. The explained variance decreased after the first 20 PCs, indicating that the top 20 PCs captured most population-specific variance within the rare variant set (Additional file 1: Table S7 and Additional file 2: Fig. S1).

BOLT-REML

Heritability was estimated using BOLT-REML [25], a Bayesian-inspired mixed linear model approach implemented in BOLT-LMM software v.2.4.1. This method is designed for efficient heritability estimation in large-scale genomic datasets and does not require explicit computation of the GRM. Instead, BOLT-REML models genetic relationships implicitly while partitioning phenotypic variance into genetic (σg2) and residual (σe2​) components. BOLT-REML applies a Monte Carlo algorithm that is much faster than eigendecomposition-based methods for variance component analysis (e.g., GCTA) for large sample sizes.

For this analysis, we focused on rare variants with MAF between 1 × 10⁻4 and 1 × 10⁻2. Polygenic risk score

To calculate PGS for the five phenotypes, we utilized the PGS Catalog. For each phenotype, we selected the PGS derived from the largest available GWAS sample size. The selected PGS identifiers were as follows: glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) (PGS002331), platelet count (PGS002343), standing height (PGS002804), systolic blood pressure (SBP; PGS004603), and body mass index (BMI; PGS005203).

The BMI PGS (PGS005203) was constructed using GWAS summary statistics from 160 independent cohorts, explicitly excluding the UK Biobank; however, the subsequent tuning stage incorporated UK Biobank data [43]. For glycated hemoglobin and platelet count, the underlying GWAS data originated exclusively from the UK Biobank [44]. The standing height PGS included GWAS results that incorporated UK Biobank samples [45], and the SBP PGS similarly relied on GWAS data including the UK Biobank [26]. All PGS were downloaded directly from the PGS Catalog, and individual-level scores were computed using PLINK by applying the risk-allele weights provided in each PGS file via the –score function [46].

Medication and lifestyle adjustment for systolic blood pressure and glycated hemoglobin

To address potential confounding due to medication use, we identified antihypertensive medication users using UK Biobank self-reported medication data (Field 6153) and medication codes (Field 20,003). For these individuals, systolic blood pressure measurements were adjusted by adding 15 mmHg, following standard procedures in large-scale blood pressure GWAS [26]. We additionally generated a medication-restricted dataset by excluding all individuals taking any antihypertensive drug. For glycated hemoglobin, individuals diagnosed with type 1 or type 2 diabetes were excluded to minimize the influence of glucose-lowering medication. We also evaluated the effect of key demographic and lifestyle covariates by adjusting for body mass index, smoking status, and physical activity (MET score) in accordance with previous studies [27].

Data availability

Whole-genome sequencing data used in this study were obtained from the UK Biobank under application number 83990. Access to these data can be requested through the UK Biobank (https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/) under an approved application. PGS used in this study were obtained from the PGS Catalog (https://www.pgscatalog.org/) using the following identifiers: PGS002331 (glycated hemoglobin), PGS002343 (platelet count), PGS002804 (standing height), PGS004603 (systolic blood pressure), and PGS005203 (body mass index). Custom scripts used for data processing and heritability estimation are publicly available at the Zenodo repository (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19378367) [47] and the GitHub repository (https://github.com/dkssud24/Rare-variant-heritability-Genome-Biology-) [48]. The source code is released under the MIT License. To ensure full reproducibility, a version of the code has also been archived in Zenodo and is linked to the Github repository. Due to UK Biobank data access restrictions, individual-level data cannot be publicly shared. However, all analyses can be reproduced using the UK Biobank resource. The data used in this study were accessed under UK Biobank application ID 83990. Researchers wishing to reproduce the analyses must obtain their own UK Biobank approval and follow the sample selection and quality control procedures described in the Methods section. All summary statistics supporting the findings of this study are provided within the article and its Additional files.
