Aging-driven transcriptional programs in diabetic kidney disease: multi-omics discovery of diagnostic biomarkers and drug-repurposing targets
Authors: Xue Hu, Yingzhuo Li, Yang Wang, Bosheng Luo, Chunjuan Xia, Jiaping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic kidney disease (DKD) is the primary global cause of end-stage renal disease. However, the aging-related gene networks driving its progression remain unclear.MethodsIn this study, we integrated bioinformatics and experiments to screen for age-related hub genes in DKD and explore their diagnostic and therapeutic values. Transcriptomic datasets and aging-related gene databases were combined to obtain candidate differentially expressed genes enriched in the PI3K–Akt and JAK–STAT pathways. LASSO and Random Forest algorithms were applied to screen core hub genes, and a logistic diagnostic model was constructed for verification. Single-cell sequencing was utilized to clarify the main cell populations expressing key genes. Molecular docking and dynamics simulations were performed to analyze the binding stability of candidate drugs and target genes. High-glucose cell models and DKD rat models were established, and RT-qPCR, western blot, and pathological staining assays were used to validate the expression changes of key genes and the regulatory effects of drugs.ResultsNine hub genes (CLU, EGF, SLC16A7, MYC, RPA1, RB1, APOC3, SYK, and NR3C1) were finally screened out. The constructed logistic diagnostic model achieved an AUC of 0.881 in external verification. Glomerular endothelial cells and mesangial cells were identified as the main cell populations expressing these hub genes. Molecular simulation results confirmed the stable binding of fostamatinib to SYK and selexipag to APOC3. In vivo and in vitro validation experiments demonstrated that the two candidate agents could suppress NF-κB pathway activation, inflammatory cytokine release, and cellular senescence.ConclusionIn this study, we identified age-related hub genes, constructed a reliable diagnostic signature, and suggested SYK–fostamatinib and APOC3–selexipag as potential drug-repurposing candidates for DKD, which requires further experimental and clinical validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13148-026-02207-9.

Background

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) affects 20–40% of patients with diabetes worldwide and remains the leading cause of end-stage renal disease [1, 2]. Despite widespread adoption of renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system blockade, SGLT2 inhibitors, and GLP-1 receptor agonists, a substantial proportion of patients continue to progress toward dialysis dependency [3, 4]. This persistent therapeutic gap underscores the need for a deeper mechanistic understanding of the novel molecular targets.

The dimensions of DKD pathobiology, which have received growing attention, intersect with biological aging. Aging hallmarks, including genomic instability, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cellular senescence, mechanistically overlap with the core pathological processes driving DKD [5, 6]. Senescent cells release a pro-inflammatory senescence-associated secretory phenotype that amplifies tubular injury and interstitial fibrosis [7, 8], whereas dysregulated nutrient-sensing axes, such as PI3K–Akt and mTOR, disrupt podocyte survival and tubular energy metabolism [9, 10]. Importantly, the diabetic milieu appears to accelerate tissue aging, creating a vicious cycle in which metabolic injury and biological senescence mutually reinforce the loss [11]. However, specific aging-related gene (ARG) programs that are aberrantly activated or silenced in kidneys affected by DKD remain incompletely characterized.

High-throughput transcriptomics combined with machine learning offers a powerful strategy to bridge this gap. Integrating bulk transcriptomic differential expression analysis with curated ARG databases enables the systematic identification of disease-relevant aging signatures at the scale [12]. Complementary machine learning algorithms, such as LASSO regression and Random Forest, provide regularization-based feature selection and non-parametric importance ranking, respectively, allowing robust dimensionality reduction from thousands of transcriptomic features to a compact, clinically actionable hub gene set [13, 14]. Embedding these hub genes into diagnostic models and validating them in independent cohorts can yield molecular biomarkers with genuine translational potential [15].

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) adds further resolution by dissecting cellular heterogeneity inherently obscured by bulk transcriptomics [16]. The microenvironment of kidneys affected by DKD comprises diverse dynamically interacting cell populations such as glomerular endothelial cells, mesangial cells, podocytes, tubular epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and immune infiltrates, each contributing distinctly to disease initiation and progression [17]. Reconstructing pseudotime developmental trajectories and mapping intercellular communication networks through tools such as CellChat [18] reveals how aging-related molecular programs are spatially and temporally organized across the renal microenvironment, providing mechanistic insight beyond what bulk analysis can offer.

Translating these molecular insights into therapeutic strategies requires the characterization of the regulatory architecture governing hub gene expression and pharmacological tractability. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations allow rigorous in silico evaluation of small-molecule candidates, providing atomic-resolution evidence of binding affinity and interface stability prior to experimental validation [19].

In this study, we employed an integrative multilayer framework to comprehensively characterize aging-related transcriptomic dysregulation in DKD. By intersecting bulk transcriptomic data with curated ARG databases and applying dual machine learning algorithms, we identified nine hub genes and constructed a validated diagnostic model. scRNA-seq analysis resolved cell-type–specific expression dynamics, intercellular signaling roles, and pseudotime trajectories. Using molecular docking and 100 ns MD simulations, we identified SYK–fostamatinib and APOC3–selexipag as high-priority drug-repurposing candidates. Experiments were performed using in vitro and in vivo disease models to verify the expression patterns of key genes and confirm the therapeutic effects of fostamatinib and selexipag against the disease, thereby integrating computational predictions with biological evidence (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Workflow figure of DKD multi-omics study

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The DKD-related transcriptomic and single-cell datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database. Datasets were included if they met the following criteria: human renal samples, availability of DKD/DN and control groups, complete expression matrices, phenotype annotations, and suitability for gene-level downstream analysis. Datasets from non-human species, non-renal tissues, lacking controls, and lacking clear clinical information were excluded. GSE30529 was used as the discovery cohort [20],GSE142025 as the independent validation cohort, and GSE209781 as the single-cell RNA-seq cohort. For the bulk transcriptomic data, each dataset was processed independently to avoid direct cross-platform batch effects. GSE30529 expression data were normalized before differential expression analysis, whereas hub gene expression in GSE142025 was Z-score standardized for external validation. For scRNA-seq data, low-quality cells were filtered, followed by normalization, scaling, Harmony-based batch correction, clustering, and cell-type annotation. DEGs were intersected with curated ARG databases via Venn diagram to extract aging-related DEGs. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler (P < 0.05) [21].

Machine learning–based screening of hub genes

Three complementary algorithms were applied to the candidate aging-related DEGs. LASSO logistic regression was performed using the glmnet package with five-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal lambda value [22, 23] and Random Forest classification was conducted with 500 trees, and genes were ranked according to the Mean Decrease Gini index [24, 25]. In addition, Boruta analysis was used as an independent wrapper–based feature selection method to evaluate the stability of candidate genes against shadowed features. Genes consistently supported by these methods were retained as robust hub genes for subsequent diagnostic modeling.

Diagnostic model construction and external validation

Hub gene expression matrices in the training set (GSE30529) and the external validation cohort (GSE142025) were Z-score standardized. A logistic regression model was constructed, and its performance was evaluated using the ROC curve (pROC package, AUC with 95% CI) [26], Decision Curve Analysis (dcurves) [27], calibration curve, and Brier Score. Between-group differences in expression were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Single-cell transcriptomic data processing and cell clustering

The scRNA-seq data (GSE209781, three DKD and three normal samples) were processed using Seurat [28]. The quality control retained cells with a mitochondrial proportion of < 70%, RNA count of < 25,000, and detected features of < 5000. After log-normalized scaling, Harmony batch correction [29], and PCA, UMAP embedding and unsupervised clustering (resolution = 0.5, top 15 PCs) were performed. The cell types were annotated using SingleR with the BlueprintEncodeData reference [30].

Single-cell expression dynamics, cell communication, and pseudotime trajectory

Pseudo-bulk differential expression between the DKD and control subpopulations was computed using DESeq2 [31] and visualized using DotPlots/VlnPlots. Intercellular communication networks were inferred using CellChat (Secreted Signaling database) [18]. Pseudotime trajectories were reconstructed with Monocle 2 (negbinomial.size model) [32], and hub gene expression dynamics along trajectory branches were visualized.

Establishment of high glucose injury model in human glomerular endothelial cells (HGECs) and human mesangial cells (HMCs)

HGECs and HMCs were cultured in high-glucose complete DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin mixture at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. The cells were passaged when they reached approximately 90% confluence. HGECs and HMCs in the logarithmic growth phase were divided into normal control and high-glucose model groups. Cells in the normal control group were cultured in serum-free DMEM containing 5.5 mmol/L glucose, whereas those in the high-glucose model group were treated with serum-free DMEM containing 30 mmol/L glucose. All the cells were incubated for 48 h. After completion of culture, cell samples were collected for subsequent RNA extraction, protein extraction, and detection of molecular biological indicators.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‑PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells or renal tissues of each experimental group using the TRIzol method. The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Using cDNA as a template, quantitative amplification was performed using the SYBR Green qPCR kit. The total reaction volume was 20 μL, consisting of cDNA template, forward and reverse primers, SYBR Green Mix, and RNase-free water. PCR conditions were based on previously published studie of our research group [33]. Finally, melting curve analysis was performed to verify the specificity of the amplification. Western blot analysis

In total, 1 × 106 HGECs, 1 × 106 HMCs, or 50 mg of rat renal tissue was collected. The samples were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer or fully homogenized and incubated on ice. After lysis, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using the BCA method. Subsequently, 5 × SDS loading buffer was added proportionally and the mixture was denatured in a water bath for 10 min to prepare the protein samples. Equal amounts of protein were separated using 10–15% SDS-PAGE. Proteins were then transferred onto PVDF membranes using the sandwich wet-transfer method at a constant current of 250 mA. The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk at 37 °C for 1 h. Afterwards, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C on a shaker with primary antibodies, including SYK (#PT0468R, Immunoway, China), APOC-III (#YT5550, Immunoway, China), NF-κB p65 (#YM8209, Immunoway, China), and P21 (#YM8148, Immunoway, China) at a dilution ratio of 1:1000. The following day, the membranes were washed three times with TBST, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit, 1:5000) at 37 °C for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit and images were captured using a gel imaging system. GAPDH was used as the internal control. The gray values of the target bands were analyzed using ImageJ software to calculate the relative expression levels of the target proteins.

Core target–drug regulatory network and molecular docking

Drug–target interactions for hub genes were predicted using DGIdb [34]. Protein structures and ligand conformations were retrieved from public databases and preprocessed using PyMOL (water removal, polar hydrogens, and charge assignment). Molecular docking was performed on CB-Dock2 to calculate binding free energies (ΔG) and characterize key residue interactions [35].

MD simulation

All-atom 100 ns MD simulations were performed using GROMACS for three drug–target complexes (APOC3–selexipag, NR3C1–fluticasone furoate, and SYK–fostamatinib) [36]. The protein topology used was AMBER99SB-ILDN; the ligand topology was generated using Acpype (GAFF force field) [37]. The systems were solvated (TIP3P water, neutralizing NaCl ions) and sequentially equilibrated (50,000-step EM, 100 ps NVT at 300 K, and 100 ps NPT at 1 bar). Production simulations ran in 2 fs steps with PME electrostatics and LINCS bond constraints. RMSD, RMSF, Rg, binding contacts, and GROMOS conformational clustering (0.2 nm cutoff) were calculated from the trajectories. MM/PBSA binding free-energy analysis was performed using representative frames extracted from the MD trajectories. The total binding free energy was calculated as the sum of the gas-phase molecular mechanics energy and solvation free-energy components. Hydrogen bond analysis was conducted to calculate the average number of hydrogen bonds and dominant hydrogen bond occupancy during the simulation. Conformational clustering was performed using the GROMOS algorithm, and the percentage of dominant clusters was used to evaluate conformational convergence and structural stability.

Animal ethics

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (Approval No: kyfty2026029). All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by the National Institutes of Health (NIH), and strictly complied with relevant animal care and ethical guidelines.

Establishment of DKD rat model

A total of 42 SPF-grade male Sprague–Dawley rats aged 16–18 weeks with body weights ranging from 200 to 250 g were used in this study. All rats were housed under appropriate temperature and humidity conditions, with free access to food and drinking water. The rats were randomly divided into normal control (n = 18) and DKD model (n = 24) groups. Rats in the model group were fed a high-glucose and high-fat diet for 8 weeks, followed by intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin dissolved in 0.1 mol/L citrate buffer at a dose of 40 mg/kg. Rats in the normal control group were injected with an equal volume of citrate buffer solution. At 72 h after injection, blood samples were collected from the tail vein to detect fasting blood glucose levels. Rats with a blood glucose level of ≥ 16.7 mmol/L accompanied by typical polydipsia and polyphagia were considered as successfully established DKD models. DKD rats were continuously fed a high-glucose, high-fat diet for 16 weeks. Renal tissue and blood samples were collected for subsequent index detection. The rats in the normal group were randomly divided into three subgroups: NC, NC + Fostamatinib, and NC + Selexipag. The DKD rats were randomly assigned into four subgroups: DKD, DKD + Fostamatinib, DKD + Selexipag, and DKD + Fostamatinib + Selexipag, with six rats in each group. All drugs were administered by intragastric gavage twice daily. Fostamatinib was dissolved in 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na) solution at a dose of 30 mg/kg [38]. Selexipag was prepared in 0.5% methylcellulose (MC) solution at a dose of 10 mg/kg [39]. Rats in the NC and DKD control groups were administered with equal volumes of a mixed solution containing 0.5% CMC-Na and 0.5% MC by gavage. After 8 weeks of continuous intervention, renal tissue samples from each group were collected for subsequent experimental detection.

Detection of biochemical indicators

Urine samples were collected from rats in each group and centrifuged to obtain supernatants. Blood samples were harvested via tail vein puncture and serum was isolated after centrifugation. Subsequently, the levels of serum creatinine (Scr; #S0291S, Beyotime, China), blood urea nitrogen (BUN; #S0574S, Beyotime, China), and 24-h urinary protein (24-h UP; #C035-2-1, Beyotime, China) were measured in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Renal histopathological staining

Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE) staining

Paraffin-embedded renal tissues were cut into 4 μm-thick sections and mounted on glass slides. After baking, sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated using a graded ethanol series. According to the manufacturer’s instructions of the Hematoxylin–Eosin Staining Kit (#C0105S, Beyotime, China), the sections were stained with hematoxylin solution for 5 min and rinsed with tap water for blue reversion. Subsequently, the sections were differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid–alcohol for several seconds and washed again with tap water. After staining with an eosin solution for 2 min, the sections were dehydrated with a gradient of ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Structural changes in the glomeruli and renal tubules as well as inflammatory cell infiltration were observed under a light microscope.

Masson staining

After paraffin sections were prepared, staining was performed sequentially with the corresponding reagents following the protocols of the Masson Staining Kit (#C0189S, Beyotime, China). Sections were rinsed with 1% glacial acetic acid, dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared with xylene, and sealed with neutral balsam. Finally, histological changes were observed under an optical microscope and quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Prediction of APOC3-targeting MiRNAs

To investigate the potential post-transcriptional regulation of APOC3, candidate APOC3-targeting miRNAs were predicted using three independent databases: miRDB, miRWalk, and TargetScan 8.0. Human mature miRNA IDs were standardized, duplicate entries were removed, and the miRNAs predicted using all three databases were retained as high-confidence candidates. Overlapping miRNAs were visualized using a Venn diagram, and the top candidates were ranked according to the TargetScan context++ score.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were analyzed using SPSS (version 26.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. An independent sample t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, and one-way analysis of variance was applied for comparisons among multiple groups. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were independently repeated at least thrice.

Results

Identification of aging-related DEGs in DKD

To identify aging-related DEGs in DKD and explore their biological functions, differential expression analysis was performed using the GSE30529 dataset (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Material 1). A total of 13,237 genes were profiled, of which 542 reached the significance threshold (|log2FC|> 1, adjusted P < 0.05), comprising 467 genes with upregulated and 75 genes with downregulated expression. These DEGs were intersected with a curated set of 542 ARGs (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Material 2) using a Venn diagram, yielding 21 overlapping aging-related DEGs (accounting for 2.0% of the screened space). These 21 genes included both members with upregulated expression (e.g., CLU [log2FC = 2.48], AXL [log2FC = 1.71], CXCL1 [log2FC = 1.67], MYC [log2FC = 1.25], SYK [log2FC = 1.13], and NR3C1 [log2FC = 1.04]) and members with downregulated expression (e.g., EGF [log2FC = − 2.23], SLC16A7 [log2FC = − 1.36], GHR [log2FC = − 1.16], and APOC3 [log2FC = − 1.14]). Machine learning–based screening of hub genes

To further screen the most diagnostically valuable hub targets among the 21 candidate aging-related DEGs, LASSO regression (Fig. 3A) and Random Forest algorithms (Fig. 3B) were applied for feature dimensionality reduction and importance evaluation. The LASSO model converged feature variables at the optimal λ value (− Log(λ) ≈ 4.2), effectively eliminating redundant predictors while retaining core features (Supplementary Material 3). The Random Forest model, using 500 decision trees, ranked feature genes by Mean Decrease Gini index, with EGF (Gini ≈ 1.22) and CLU (Gini ≈ 1.18) ranking the highest, followed by SLC16A7, IL-7R, BRD7, SYK, and RPA1, achieving a maximum Gini value of approximately 1.2. The intersection of the LASSO-retained genes and top-ranked Random Forest genes yielded nine hub genes: CLU, EGF, SLC16A7, MYC, RPA1, RB1, APOC3, SYK, and NR3C1, which collectively constitute the core molecular signature of DKD. Boruta analysis was performed as an additional feature selection strategy to further verify the robustness of machine learning–derived hub genes (Fig. 3C). All nine hub genes showed importance values exceeding the shadowMax threshold, supporting their stable contribution to DKD classification. External validation and diagnostic model assessment of hub genes

To verify the diagnostic performance and expression level differences of the nine screened hub genes in the independent cohort, the external dataset GSE142025 was used to construct a logistic regression diagnostic model. The model demonstrated excellent discriminative ability in cross-platform validation, achieving an AUC of 0.881 (95% CI 0.778–0.984) (Fig. 4A). Analysis of the confusion matrix (Fig. 4B) revealed that the model achieved 100% sensitivity in the control group (9/9 correctly classified) and 62.96% prediction accuracy in the disease group (17/27 correctly classified). Validation of individual hub gene expression differences between the DKD (DN) and control groups (Fig. 4C) confirmed statistically significant and consistent differences for seven of nine hub genes: CLU (p = 0.019), EGF (p = 0.00012), NR3C1 (p = 0.0027), RPA1 (p = 0.0017), SLC16A7 (p = 2.1 × 10−6), SYK (p = 7.7 × 10−5), and APOC3 (p = 0.00065). These results confirm that the core gene feature set has high diagnostic value and cross-platform reproducibility. Single-cell transcriptomic atlas and cell clustering of DKD

To determine the cellular heterogeneity and microenvironmental landscape of kidneys affected by DKD at single-cell resolution, scRNA-seq data (GSE209781) from three DKD samples (DKD01–03) and three normal controls (NM01–03) were processed. After stringent quality control (Fig. 5A) and cell filtering based on nFeature_RNA, nCount_RNA, and mitochondrial gene percentage, high-quality cells were retained for downstream analysis. Following Harmony batch effect correction and UMAP-based nonlinear dimensionality reduction (Fig. 5B), the cells were mapped by sample origin (Fig. 5C) and disease grouping (Fig. 5D). Unsupervised clustering (resolution = 0.5, top 15 PCs) resolved 22 distinct UMAP clusters, which were subsequently annotated into 11 major cell types using SingleR and canonical markers (Fig. 5E), including endothelial cells, mesangial cells, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, adipocytes, B cells, CD8 + T cells, NK cells, monocytes, neutrophils, and HSCs. Single-cell expression dynamics, cell communication, pseudotime trajectory, and experimental verification of hub genes

Multidimensional single-cell analyses were performed to explore the expression specificity of hub genes across cell subpopulations and the intercellular communication dynamics. Dot plot visualization of hub gene expression across 11 cell types under control and DKD conditions (Fig. 6A) revealed marked shifts in expression, particularly in endothelial, mesangial, and epithelial cells. Feature scatter plots and dispersion-mean expression relationships (Fig. 6B) confirmed the high variability of the hub genes across the single-cell landscape. Pseudotime trajectory analysis using Monocle 2 reconstructed a continuous developmental trajectory divided into three States (Fig. 6C), revealing dynamic state transitions during DKD progression. Feature plots of hub gene expression along the pseudotime axis (Fig. 6D) demonstrated that genes such as CLU, EGF, and SLC16A7 exhibit gradual expression changes along specific trajectory branches, consistent with their roles in disease progression. Cell–cell communication analysis using CellChat (Fig. 6E) demonstrated that endothelial cells, mesangial cells, and fibroblasts occupy dominant positions in both the interaction number and weight/strength networks, suggesting that hub targets may drive DKD progression by modulating the fate trajectories of specific cell populations and reinforcing signal communications within the key microenvironmental niche. To further validate the regulation of expression of core genes identified through bioinformatics analyses in DKD-associated renal parenchymal cells, we established a high-glucose stimulation model using primary HGECs and HMCs. Epithelial cells were excluded from this validation because of their relatively low abundance among the three cell types mentioned above and their susceptibility to epithelial–mesenchymal transition under high-glucose conditions, which results in phenotypic instability and may interfere with the accurate assessment of basal core gene expression. The expression levels of seven core genes (CLU, EGF, SLC16A7, APOC3, SYK, NR3C1, and MYC) were evaluated using qPCR and western blot analysis. Among the nine initially screened core genes, MYC and RB1 did not reach statistical significance in the independent validation cohort (Section “Results”). Accordingly, these two genes were excluded from subsequent experimental verification. qPCR (Fig. 6F) and western blot results (Fig. 6G) showed that, in HGECs, compared with the normal-glucose (NG) group and high-glucose (HG) group exhibited significantly increased mRNA and protein expression of CLU, SYK, RPA1, and NR3C1 (p < 0.05), whereas the expression of EGF and SLC16A7 was significantly decreased (p < 0.05). Notably, APOC3 exhibited a discordant pattern of decreased mRNA and increased protein expression upon high-glucose stimulation, suggesting the existence of post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms for APOC3 in DKD renal cells. Because APOC3 showed a discordant expression pattern with decreased mRNA but increased protein expression under high-glucose stimulation, we performed miRNA-target prediction to preliminarily explore potential post-transcriptional regulation. By integrating miRDB, miRWalk, and TargetScan 8.0, 18 miRNAs were predicted to target APOC3 (Figure S2A). Among these candidates, hsa-miR-6808-5p, hsa-miR-6893-5p, hsa-miR-940, hsa-miR-1286, and hsa-miR-6134 showed relatively favorable TargetScan context++ scores (Figure S2B). These results provide preliminary bioinformatics evidence that APOC3 may be regulated at the post-transcriptional level, although further experimental validation is required. Construction of core target–drug regulatory network and molecular docking validation

To explore the druggability of core targets at the molecular scale, a drug–target interaction network was constructed using DGIdb (Fig. 7A). The network revealed that NR3C1 serves as a major hub connecting numerous glucocorticoid compounds (including fluticasone furoate, dexamethasone, and budesonide), SYK interacts with fostamatinib and paclitaxel, and APOC3 connects with selexipag and epoprostenol. Molecular docking simulations were subsequently performed for three representative drug–target pairs (Fig. 7B–D). For APOC3– selexipag (Fig. 7B), the key interaction residues were D66, E63, F64, K60, T56, V71, L67, D68, A76, S75, R72, and A79, with the binding interface stabilized by a combination of hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts. For NR3C1–fluticasone furoate (Fig. 7C), the drug occupied the ligand-binding domain and formed critical interactions with residues M639, Q642, C643, Y735, M560, L563, N564, L566, G567, W600, K605, L608, L621, F625, M646, L753, and F749, consistent with the known glucocorticoid receptor binding mode. For SYK–fostamatinib (Fig. 7D), the key contact residues included A412, E416, I420, D415, A419, V423, G514, S516, K517, L515, Y526, A528, W534, K533, F382, and I493, reflecting tight active site engagement. MD simulation analysis of hub target–ligand complexes

To evaluate the structural stability and conformational dynamics of the three docked complexes over physiological timescales, 100 ns all-atom MD simulations were performed for System A (APOC3–selexipag), System B (NR3C1–fluticasone furoate), and System C (SYK–fostamatinib) (Fig. 8). For System A (Fig. 8A), the RMSD gradually increased during the first ~ 40 ns and subsequently converged at approximately 2.0 nm, whereas the Rg curve showed a marked compaction from ~ 2.4 to ~ 1.3 nm over the same period, reflecting a structural reorganization into a tighter, more stable bound conformation. The number of binding contacts stabilized at a high level in the latter half of the simulation, confirming the maintenance of a stable drug–protein interface. For System C (Fig. 8C), an exceptionally flat RMSD profile was observed throughout the entire 100 ns trajectory, fluctuating within a narrow range of 0.2–0.3 nm, accompanied by a smooth and stable Rg curve (~ 1.95 to 2.00 nm) and a progressively increasing binding contact count, indicative of outstanding dynamic stability and tight ligand retention. In contrast, System B (Fig. 8B) exhibited severe, step-wise conformational transitions in the mid-to-late simulation phases, with RMSD violently oscillating between 0.2 and 2.0 nm and correspondingly erratic Rg fluctuations, revealing fundamentally unstable binding behavior despite initial docking affinity. Collectively, these MD results confirm that Systems A and C (APOC3–selexipag and SYK–fostamatinib) represent high-potential, dynamically stable drug–target scaffolds, whereas System B (NR3C1–fluticasone furoate) requires further structural optimization before advancing drug development.Fig. 8Molecular dynamics simulation of drug–target complexes. To further quantify the dynamic stability of the three ligand–target complexes, MM/PBSA binding free-energy calculations, hydrogen bond occupancy analysis, and conformational clustering were performed based on the MD trajectories (Tables S1 and S2). The APOC3–selexipag complex showed a favorable binding free energy of − 42.87 ± 15.18 kcal/mol, with a mean hydrogen bond number of 3.11 ± 1.07, dominant hydrogen bond occupancy of 73.3%, and dominant cluster proportion of 59.4%. The SYK–fostamatinib complex exhibited the strongest binding stability, with a binding free energy of − 64.29 ± 14.58 kcal/mol, mean hydrogen bond number of 4.54 ± 0.90, dominant hydrogen bond occupancy of 84.2%, and dominant cluster proportion of 77.2%. In contrast, the NR3C1–fluticasone furoate complex displayed markedly weaker and more variable binding free energy (− 8.47 ± 37.22 kcal/mol), fewer hydrogen bonds (1.07 ± 0.85), low dominant hydrogen bond occupancy (24.8%), and dispersed conformational clustering, with only 22.8% of frames assigned to the dominant cluster and 10 total clusters. These quantitative results support the RMSD and Rg observations, indicating that NR3C1–fluticasone furoate undergoes unstable ligand–receptor interactions during the simulation, whereas APOC3–selexipag and SYK–fostamatinib represent more dynamically stable drug–target complexes.

In vivo validation of the renoprotective effects of a SYK inhibitor and an APOC3-targeting agent in DKD rats

To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of fostamatinib (SYK inhibitor) and selexipag (APOC3-targeting agent) in DKD, a series of experimental validations was performed in each treatment group. Serum biochemical indices (Fig. 9A) showed that, compared with the NC group, the DKD group exhibited significantly elevated levels of Scr, BUN, and 24-h UP (P < 0.05). Compared with the DKD group, the DKD + Fostamatinib, DKD + Selexipag, and combination treatment groups showed significant reductions in the above three indices (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the NC + drug groups and the control group. Western blot results (Fig. 9B) revealed that the protein expression levels of p-SYK/SYK, APOC3, p-NF-κB/NF-κB, P21, and P53 in renal tissues were significantly higher in the DKD group than in the control group (P < 0.05). Compared with the DKD group, the DKD + fostamatinib group showed significantly reduced levels of p-SYK and its downstream effectors, p-NF-κB, P21, and P53. The DKD + selexipag group showed significantly reduced levels of APOC3, p-NF-κB, P21, and P53. The combination treatment group exhibited the greatest inhibitory effects on all the aforementioned proteins (P < 0.05). HE and Masson staining (Fig. 9C–F) demonstrated that the DKD group exhibited enlarged glomeruli, increased mesangial matrix, tubular vacuolation, marked glomerulosclerosis, and glycogen deposition. These pathological alterations were notably ameliorated in all the treatment groups. ELISA results (Fig. 9G) showed that the levels of the inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β in renal tissue homogenates were significantly higher in the DKD group than in the control group (P < 0.05). All treatment groups exhibited varying degrees of reduction in cytokine levels. In summary, the SYK inhibitor fostamatinib and the APOC3-targeting agent selexipag ameliorated renal dysfunction, inflammation, and fibrosis in DKD rats. Discussion

In this study, we employed an integrative multilayer bioinformatics framework to systematically characterize ARG expression dysregulation in DKD, culminating in the identification of nine hub genes, a validated diagnostic model, and computationally and experimentally supported drug-repurposing candidates. By combining transcriptomic analysis, dual machine learning, single-cell resolution profiling, ceRNA network construction, molecular simulation, and in vivo and in vitro experimental verification, we provided a comprehensive molecular portrait spanning from biomarker discovery to mechanistic and therapeutic insights.

The 21 aging-related DEGs identified from the intersection of the DKD transcriptomic data and curated ARG databases were significantly enriched in the PI3K–Akt and JAK–STAT signaling pathways, both of which serve as central integrators of metabolic stress, inflammatory cytokine responses, and cell survival decisions in diabetic kidneys. PI3K–Akt signaling regulates podocyte survival and tubular cell hypertrophy, whereas JAK–STAT activation is a recognized driver of renal inflammation and fibrosis [40]. Concurrent enrichment in receptor-mediated endocytosis and hemopoiesis regulation further suggests that aging-related transcriptomic reprogramming in DKD extends to immune cell recruitment and microenvironmental remodeling, consistent with the emerging concept of sterile inflammation in diabetic nephropathy [41].

The nine hub genes identified through dual machine learning carry independent biological plausibility within the DKD context. CLU expression, the most strongly upregulated hit, is associated with a stress-responsive chaperone involved in complement regulation and apoptosis suppression and has previously been linked to tubular injury responses in acute and chronic kidney diseases [42]. Markedly downregulated EGF expression is a well-characterized indicator of functional tubular mass, with urinary EGF proposed as a prognostic biomarker for DKD progression in multiple clinical cohorts [43]. SLC16A7, which encodes monocarboxylate transporter 2, reflects impaired metabolic substrate transport, which is consistent with the recognized shift away from fatty acid oxidation in DKD tubules [44]. RPA1 and RB1 have been implicated in replication stress and cell cycle dysregulation as components of the aging-related DKD transcriptome, whereas APOC3 links the hub gene network to dyslipidemia-driven glomerular injury [45]. SYK and NR3C1 connect the aging-related signature to the inflammatory kinase and glucocorticoid receptor axes with established clinical relevance in nephrology [46]. The convergence of LASSO regularization and Random Forest importance ranking on this gene set provides strong methodological confidence, as the two algorithms operate on fundamentally different mathematical principles yet identify the same targets, substantially reducing the probability of overfitting.

The diagnostic model achieved an AUC of 0.881 in the independent GSE142025 cohort, with SLC16A7 and SYK showing the most robust cross-platform replication. The model attained 100% sensitivity in the controls, which is a clinically desirable property for screening applications. The 37% misclassification rate in the DKD group most likely reflects molecular heterogeneity across disease stages, and future integration of clinical variables, such as eGFR and proteinuria grade, could further improve predictive precision.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis resolved 22 clusters into 11 major cell types, revealing profound microenvironmental remodeling in the DKD and control groups. Hub genes, including CLU, EGF, and SLC16A7, showed the most pronounced shifts in expression in endothelial and mesangial cells. Endothelial dysfunction is an early and central event in the DKD pathogenesis [47], and the specific dysregulation of ARGs within this compartment suggests that accelerated endothelial aging may be a primary driver rather than a secondary consequence of the disease. Mesangial hub gene dysregulation is consistent with the pathological mesangial matrix expansion characteristic of diabetic glomerulopathy [48]. Pseudotime trajectory analysis further demonstrated that cellular states evolve continuously along three developmental branches, with hub gene expression dynamics tracking disease progression in a temporally ordered manner, implying that therapeutic intervention may need to be stage-specific to achieve optimal efficacy. Cell–cell communication analysis identified endothelial cells, mesangial cells, and fibroblasts as the dominant nodes in both the interaction number and weight networks, reinforcing the view that DKD is fundamentally a disease of aberrant intercellular crosstalk rather than isolated cell-autonomous dysfunction [49]. Importantly, we conducted qPCR and western blot experiments on HGECs and HMCs stimulated with high blood glucose. The experimental results indicated that the expression of mRNA and proteins of the seven key differentially regulated genes was abnormal. Additionally, it has been suggested that there may be post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms of APOC3 in DKD renal cells. This cellular-level verification significantly strengthened confidence in the key gene map derived from bioinformatics and extended its relevance from computational predictions to physiological experimental contexts.

The most direct translational findings have emerged from the integration of molecular simulations and cellular experimentation. The SYK–fostamatinib complex exhibited outstanding dynamic stability throughout the 100 ns MD trajectory, with RMSD confined to 0.2–0.3 nm and progressively increasing binding contacts confirming sustained active-site engagement. Fostamatinib is an orally bioavailable SYK inhibitor already approved for the treatment of immune thrombocytopenia, making its repurposing for DKD immediately feasible from a regulatory standpoint [50]. The key point is that the in vivo animal experiments confirmed that fostamatinib markedly suppressed SYK phosphorylation in the kidneys of DKD rats, thereby attenuating the abnormal activation of downstream NF‑κB p65, P21, and P53 proteins. This treatment effectively alleviated renal pathological lesions, significantly reduced renal function markers including Scr, BUN, and 24‑h UP, and inhibited the overproduction of inflammatory cytokines such as IL‑6, TNF‑α and IL‑1β in renal tissues. In this study, we only observed prominent downregulation of NF‑κB, P21, and P53 expression in rat kidneys following drug administration, and such molecular changes occurred in parallel with the recovery of renal function and relief of renal pathological injury. We were unable to distinguish whether the inhibitory effects on these pathways stemmed from the direct regulation of target pathways by the drug or indirect reversal resulting from the overall improvement of renal injury and alleviation of inflammatory stress. Fostamatinib also markedly reduced high-glucose–induced apoptosis and reversed the upregulation of fibrosis marker levels, indicating that SYK pathway inhibition confers broad cytoprotective effects encompassing the inflammatory, apoptotic, and fibrotic dimensions in DKD pathology [51]. These experimental findings directly corroborate the computational predictions and establish a solid cellular evidence base for SYK–fostamatinib as a high-priority drug-repurposing candidate for DKD. Although molecular docking and MD simulations demonstrated stable binding and favorable interaction profiles for SYK–fostamatinib and APOC3–selexipag, these results only reflect in silico binding behavior and cannot be directly interpreted as therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, these compounds should be considered as computationally predicted drug-repurposing candidates, and their pharmacological effects require further experimental and clinical validation before translational conclusions can be drawn.

Importantly, hub gene status does not necessarily indicate a direct therapeutic priority. Although NR3C1 was retained as a hub gene in the transcriptomic and machine learning analyses, subsequent MD-based quantitative evaluation suggested that the NR3C1–fluticasone furoate complex had limited dynamic stability. Compared with APOC3–selexipag and SYK–fostamatinib, NR3C1–fluticasone furoate showed weaker and more variable MM/PBSA binding free energy, lower hydrogen bond occupancy, and more dispersed conformational clustering. These findings provide a structural explanation for the unstable RMSD and Rg profiles observed in the simulations. Therefore, NR3C1 is considered a diagnostically relevant hub gene rather than a prioritized therapeutic target in the current drug-repurposing workflow. Based on this prioritization strategy, the subsequent in vivo validation focused on APOC3–selexipag and SYK–fostamatinib, which showed more favorable ligand–target stability.

Interestingly, APOC3 showed a discordant expression pattern in high-glucose–stimulated HGECs, with decreased mRNA expression but increased protein expression. To provide preliminary evidence for possible post-transcriptional regulation, we performed miRNA-target prediction using miRDB, miRWalk, and TargetScan 8.0. Eighteen miRNAs were predicted to target APOC3 across the three databases, and several candidates, including hsa-miR-6808-5p, hsa-miR-6893-5p, hsa-miR-940, hsa-miR-1286, and hsa-miR-6134, showed relatively favorable TargetScan context++ scores. These findings suggest that miRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulation may partially contribute to the inconsistency between APOC3 mRNA and protein expression under high-glucose stimulation. However, these predictions should be regarded as preliminary bioinformatic evidence and require further validation using miRNA mimic/inhibitor assays, luciferase reporter assays, or protein stability experiments.

The APOC3–selexipag pair also demonstrated satisfactory long-term simulation stability, with the RMSD converging at approximately 2.0 nm and a high binding contact count maintained throughout the latter simulation phase. Exogenous recombinant protein APOC3 was significantly overexpressed in DKD rats. Selexipag inhibited the expression of APOC3 and p-NF-κB, partially suppressed the expression of P21 and P53, and alleviated the pathological state of the kidneys and the secretion of related inflammatory factors in DKD rats. The improvement may not be as significant as that of fostamatinib, which is mechanistically coherent given APOC3’s contribution to the dyslipidemic environment that accelerates glomerular injury [52]. In contrast, the NR3C1–fluticasone furoate complex showed severe conformational instability during the simulation, indicating that structural optimization is required before this interaction can be advanced, despite the known clinical relevance of glucocorticoid receptor modulation in nephrology [53].

This study had several limitations. First, our bioinformatic analyses were performed on public datasets with limited sample sizes. Cross-sectional transcriptomic data cannot support causal conclusions regarding the temporal link between dysregulated ARGs and DKD initiation. Despite validation across independent datasets (GSE30529 and GSE142025), batch heterogeneity persisted owing to differing sequencing platforms, renal tissue sources, and disease stages. We mitigated batch bias via intracohort normalization and separate dataset analysis; however, unified large prospective cohorts with standardized clinical metadata are required. Second, our results lack verification in large clinical patient cohorts and multiethnic single-cell RNA-seq datasets with more donors, which restricts the generalizability of our cellular and molecular observations. Third, we failed to conduct in vitro rescue assays using SYK/APOC3 knockout and overexpression cell models, thus weakening the causal evidence supporting our mechanistic hypothesis. In addition, in vivo functional validation of NR3C1 was not performed in the current study. Although molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations predicted stable binding between the candidate compounds and target proteins, these in silico findings do not confirm therapeutic efficacy and require further experimental validation. Fourth, we did not assess the long-term efficacy, safety, or pharmacokinetics of fostamatinib and selexipag. Finally, an independent solvent control group receiving only 0.5% CMC-Na + 0.5% MC was not included in the animal experiments. Published pharmacotoxicological data and FDA records confirm that fostamatinib and selexipag show no evident nephrotoxicity in normal rodents. However, we cannot fully exclude the potential renal bioactivity of the solvent itself without corresponding control data. Follow-up research should include a dedicated solvent control group to distinguish the biological effects derived from drugs versus solvents for more robust experimental outcomes.

Conclusion

This integrative study provides a comprehensive molecular portrait of ARG expression dysregulation in DKD, spanning transcriptomic biomarker discovery, machine learning–based diagnostic modeling, single-cell microenvironmental dynamics, and computationally and experimentally supported drug–target interactions. The identified hub genes and drug-repurposing candidates, particularly the SYK–fostamatinib and APOC3–selexipag combination, which was supported by molecular dynamics simulations and in vitro and in vivo experiments, provide a mechanistic basis for further investigation of potential therapeutic targets for DKD.
