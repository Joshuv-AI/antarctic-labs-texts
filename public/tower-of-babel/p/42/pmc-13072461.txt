Caesarean section in the first stage of labor and risk of cesarean uterine lacerations: a multicenter study in China
Authors: Guannan Feng, Zhiyi Yang, Qianqian Shen, Qian Meng, Kun Yu, Xuepiao Zhao, Jun Yan, Yongfei Yue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe surgical difficulty of cesarean delivery after a trial labor is often increased, leading to uterine lacerations, postpartum hemorrhage and other adverse outcomes. In the study, we aimed to identify and quantify risk factors that cause cesarean uterine lacerations during the first stage of labor.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort review of all women with a singleton, cephalic fetus at term delivered by caesarean section at four large departments in China between January 2023 and December 2024. A least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and multivariate analysis were performed to identify labor related risk factors for cesarean uterine lacerations among patients that underwent cesarean delivery in the first stage of labor. The risk factors were used to construct a new score model to identify patients at risk for cesarean uterine lacerations.ResultsDuring the study period, there were 11,689 women delivered by caesarean section, 206 women had cesarean uterine lacerations and 824 pregnant women without cesarean uterine lacerations were included in the study. The following 6 high-risk factors were identified for cesarean uterine lacerations by LASSO regression and multiple regression: occiput transverse position, occiput posterior position, cervical dilatation (> 5 cm), station of the fetal head at the ischial spines ( > + 1), caput succedaneum (> 3 cm), and duration of labor (> 12 h). In clinical variables model, the ROC curve analysis showed that the sensitivity and specificity to predict cesarean uterine lacerations were 91.372% and 92.205% respectively, with AUC 0.892. Using our new score model, when the score point ≥ 6, the sensitivity and specificity in identifying cases at high risk for cesarean uterine lacerations were 92.168% and 91.605%, respectively, with AUC 0.902.ConclusionsIncreased risk of cesarean uterine lacerations in the first stage of labor is attributable mainly to fetal head position, larger cervical dilatation, lower fetal head, caput succedaneum, and duration of labor.

Background

The cesarean section rate has remained persistently high worldwide over the past 20 years [1]. It is usually perceived that cesarean section was a simple and quick delivery compared to vaginal delivery. However, in some instances, cesarean section may be a difficult operation that can be harmful to the mother and fetus. Cesarean section is associated with increased maternal morbidity and mortality when compared with vaginal delivery, such as postpartum hemorrhage, cesarean uterine lacerations, postpartum fever, neonatal hypoxia, and so on [2–4]. There are many possible reasons that can make cesarean section more difficult, such as a cesarean section during labor and impacted fetal head, obstetricians having difficulty delivering the fetus and even causing uterine incision laceration and postpartum hemorrhage [5].

In the first or second stage of labor, the fetal head is often low and wedged deep in the woman’s pelvis, making it difficult to deliver the fetus during cesarean section, which is known to obstetricians as impacted fetal head. Ozcan et al. [6] reported that the incidence of uterine lacerations in cesarean section was about 12.9%. Multiple studies have reported that cesarean uterine lacerations often occurred in the second stage of labor [7, 8]. In clinical practice, we have found that cesarean uterine lacerations was also a common complication of cesarean section in the first stage of labor, which will increase the amount of blood loss and operation time, injury to adjacent organs, infection, and bring great trouble to the operation of obstetricians [9]. Caesarean section during labor can also cause neonatal complications including neonatal asphyxia, skull fractures, brain hemorrhage, and admission to neonatal intensive care [10].

We believe that a greater awareness of the risk factors for cesarean uterine lacerations during labor can help obstetricians take better measures to reduce its occurrence. It is an important concern of obstetricians to identify the risk factors that lead to cesarean uterine lacerations, and avoid cesarean uterine lacerations as much as possible to reduce maternal complications. We designed this study to examine the risk factors for cesarean uterine lacerations in the first stage of labor.

Materials and methods

This was a retrospective, multi-center cohort study enrolling patients who underwent cesarean section during the first stage of labor between January 2023 and December 2024. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the affiliated Suzhou Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. A computer-based search yielded 11,689 patients in four large tertiary referral hospitals. The medical record of cesarean section in the first stage of labor was reviewed throughout the study period. All women were admitted to the delivery ward to attempt vaginal delivery. Cesarean uterine laceration was defined as an extension of the uterine incision exceeding two centimeters during a cesarean section, which was recorded in detail by the surgeon in the surgical record. Inclusion criteria for all the research subjects were women underwent cesarean section in the first stage of labor, gestational age ≥ 37 weeks, singleton fetus, live birth, and cephalic presentation at admission. Both spontaneous and induced labors were included. We excluded women with multiple pregnancy, previous cesarean section, placental abruption, placenta previa, and lack of complete case data or detailed surgical records. Ultimately, a total of 206 patients with cesarean uterine lacerations were included in the uterine lacerations group, while 824 control patients were randomly selected at a 1:4 ratio and assigned to the non-laceration group.

The first stage of labor was defined as the time interval between the onset of labor and 10 cm cervical dilation [11]. For women with labor arrest or premature rupture of membranes, oxytocin was used for the induction of labor. Cesarean section was performed during vaginal delivery if labor arrest after oxytocin induced or the cardiotocograph was abnormal. Thus, cesarean section for a first stage labor progress included all women with a cesarean birth before 10 cm dilation. When an impacted fetal head is encountered, the obstetrician should first identify the space to introduce their hand below the fetal head to flex and elevate it toward the incision (abdominal cephalic disimpaction). If this maneuver proves ineffective, an assistant standing at the lower end of the operating table should insert a hand into the vagina to dislodge and elevate the fetal head upward into the abdominal cavity [12]. We compared maternal and neonatal outcomes while dividing the cohort into two groups: cesarean section with uterine lacerations group (CUL group) and cesarean section without uterine lacerations group (non-CUL group). The neonatal asphyxia included all babies with the following events: Apgar score ≤ 7 at 1 or 5 min, umbilical artery base excess ≤๣8 mmol/L and umbilical artery pH < 7.2 [13].

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous data with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using independent samples t-tests. Chi-square analyses or Fisher’s exact test were used to measure the bivariate associations between the two groups. Potential predictors were selected through least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression in the development cohort. To assess the model’s performance and guard against overfitting, we performed 10-fold cross-validation. The dataset was randomly divided into training and validation subsets at a ratio of 70% to 30%. Based on the results, a prediction model was developed via multivariate logistic regression in the training cohort. Discriminative ability of the model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and calibration performance was visualized through a calibration plot. Additionally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to assess clinical net benefit. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Patients’ clinical features of the study groups

As regards the primary outcome, the CUL group had a higher incidence of postpartum hemorrhage (4.369% vs. 1.820%; P = 0.030), blood transfusion (3.398% vs. 1.092%; P = 0.017), longer operation time (53.298 ± 17.553 vs. 48.193 ± 16.548 min; P < 0.001), and ligation of uterine artery (14.078% vs. 6.917%; P = 0.001) than the non-CUL group. Additionally, the hemoglobin 24 h after cesarean was significantly lower in the CUL group than the non-CUL group (107.371 ± 13.051 vs. 113.509 ± 11.473 g/L; P < 0.001). We found no statistical difference in neonatal asphyxia, Apgar score ≤ 7 at 1 or 5 min, umbilical artery pH < 7.2, and umbilical artery BE <-8 (all P > 0.05). A significant difference of NICU admission was observed between the groups (P < 0.001) (Table 2). In Fig. 1, the distribution of fetal head position, station of fetal head, gestational weeks, and cervical dilatation in the CUL group were shown. There was no significant difference in all clinical variables between training and validation set (Table 3).

The characteristics of training and validation sets

To prevent overfitting and identify the most relevant predictive features, we applied LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation to all variables (Fig. 2), followed by multivariable regression analysis using the selected features (Table 4). Overall, we established a multivariate regression clinical variables model. The following clinical variables were significant predictors of cesarean uterine lacerations (all P < 0.05): occiput transverse position, occiput posterior position, cervical dilatation (> 5 cm), station of the fetal head at the ischial spines ( > + 1), caput succedaneum (> 3 cm), and duration of labor (> 12 h). To construct a practical integer-based risk score, the regression coefficient (β) for the variable ‘Duration of labor (> 12 h)’ was used as the reference, as it was the smallest among the selected variables. It was assigned a score of 1 point. The score for each specific level of the other variables was calculated using the formula: Score point = (β coefficient of the variable level / Reference β coefficient) × 1. The resulting values were then rounded to the nearest integer to obtain the final point contribution for each variable. We developed a new scoring model for patients with cesarean uterine lacerations based on the multivariable logistic coefficient of significant predictors, two points were assigned to occiput posterior position and station of fetal head at ischial spines > + 1, one point to occiput transverse position, cervical dilatation > 5 cm, caput succedaneum > 3 cm, and duration of labor > 12 h (Table 4).

The model demonstrated consistent discriminative performance, achieving AUCs of 0.891 in the training set and 0.887 in the validation set (Fig. 3.A). In clinical variables model, the ROC curve analysis showed that the sensitivity and specificity to predict cesarean uterine lacerations were 91.372% and 92.205% respectively, with AUC 0.892. Using our new score model, when the score point ≥ 6, the sensitivity and specificity in identifying cases at high risk for cesarean uterine lacerations were 92.168% and 91.605%, respectively, with AUC 0.902 (Fig. 3.B). The validation results demonstrated excellent model performance, with a well-fitted calibration curve reflecting high predictive accuracy and a favorable DCA indicating clinical utility (Fig. 3.C-F).

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to investigate the risk factors for cesarean uterine lacerations during cesarean section in the first stage of labor. Caesarean section during labor may lead to uterine incision lacerations, postpartum hemorrhage, hematoma and infection [14]. Bligard et al. reported that the incidence of cesarean uterine lacerations was about 6.8% [15]. Risk factors identified for this complication include neonatal weight, arrest of labor in the first or second stage, and non-reassuring fetal status [16]. Impacted fetal head might be caused by a variety of factors, including fetal malposition, cervical dilatation, and fetal head circumference. Notably, impacted fetal head was a key contributing factor to uterine lacerations during cesarean section [17, 18]. When impacted fetal head occurs during labor, the lower segment of the uterus is overstretched, edematous, and compressed by fetal head and reduced elasticity, which can lead to cesarean uterine lacerations. Our findings also indicated that a labor duration > 12 h was associated with cesarean uterine laceration, suggesting that prolonged labor may raise the likelihood of impacted fetal head.

In cephalic presentation cesarean section, the obstetricians usually insert their fingers and palm between the fetal head and the uterine wall to gradually lift the fetal head and deliver it from the uterus. Cervical dilatation and fetal head descent often indicate that the fetal head is deeply engaged in the pregnant woman’s pelvis. Cesarean section during labor is a more challenging procedure because the fetal head may become molded and impacted in the woman’s pelvis. The finding of this study was that occiput posterior position and station of fetal head at ischial spines(>+1)were the highest risk factors for cesarean uterine lacerations, both with a score point of 2 points. Occiput posterior position is the most common malposition during labor, with an incidence of about 5–8% [19], and it is associated with prolonged labor, an increased risk of cesarean section and postpartum hemorrhage [20]. It has been reported that occiput posterior position and occiput transverse position increased the risk of cesarean section (2-to 6-fold increased risk) and instrumental delivery (1.5- to 4-fold increased risk) [21]. If the fetal head is in the occiput posterior position or the cervical dilatation is extensive, the fetal head tends to sink deep into the woman’s pelvis, and the obstetrician needs to reach further into the pelvis, which may lead to uterine incision laceration and even hemorrhage.

Caput succedaneum is a temporary fetal scalp edema compressed by both the uterine and vaginal walls, which usually disappears 24 h after delivery and does not cause long-term effects on the newborn [22]. The common risk factors for caput succedaneum include primipara, prolonged stage of labor, operative vaginal delivery, and premature rupture of membranes [23]. We speculated that the presence of caput succedaneum often indicated that the fetal head was pressed against the uterine walls, which made it difficult for the obstetrician to raise the fetal head, and even causes cesarean uterine lacerations. In our study, the BMI of patients in the CUL group was significantly higher than that in the control group, which was consistent with a cohort study published six years ago by Rice etc. [24]. However, multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that BMI was not a risk factor for cesarean uterine lacerations. We speculated that the operation space of cesarean section in pregnant women with large BMI might be occupied by pregnant women’s fat, which might increase the occurrence of cephalopelvic disproportion, preventing the large fetal head from entering the deeper maternal pelvis. Pregnant women with larger BMI may have undergone cesarean section at a time when the cervical dilatation was smaller and the fetal head was higher, so the rate of cesarean uterine lacerations was not significantly increased.

The study cohort was recruited from tertiary hospitals, a setting that reflects a patient population characterized by a higher prevalence of advanced maternal age and multiparity, often accompanied by comorbidities such as gestational diabetes and hypertension. These risk profiles predispose women to seek delivery care at advanced medical facilities, which consequently result in the overrepresentation of multiparous women in our study population. During a cesarean section, if the newborn weighs more than 4000 g and is accompanied by uterine atony or other high-risk factors for postpartum hemorrhage, the surgeon might perform a preventive uterine artery ligation or uterine binding. Therefore, the rate of B-Lynch sutures or uterine artery ligations was relatively high in the cases of this study. Boukerfa-Bennacer Y et al. found that abnormal labor progression typically occurs at 4–5 cm cervical dilation and at full dilation [25]. In contrast, our study observed a higher proportion of abnormal labor at 2 cm and at full dilation. This discrepancy may be attributed to more active management by obstetricians during the latent phase in our clinical setting. At our institution, we typically conduct an intrapartum assessment when cervical dilation reaches 2 cm. If the labor progresses well, pregnant women usually choose intraspinal analgesia for delivery. Conversely, in cases of slow labor progression or pathological fetal heart rate, there may be a greater inclination toward cesarean delivery. At full cervical dilation, uterine contractions are generally strongest, exerting maximal pressure on the fetus and increasing the risk of cord compression (e.g., due to nuchal cord), which can lead to acute fetal hypoxia and pathological fetal heart rate. This clinical context likely explains the higher proportion of cesarean deliveries observed at both 2 cm and full dilation in our study. We found no significant difference regarding intraoperative bladder injury and neonatal asphyxia between the two groups. In order to evaluate the neonatal outcomes, we analyzed Apgar scores, birth weights, umbilical cord blood gas, neonatal asphyxia and NICU admissions. The high NICU admission in the CUL group was due to the high rate of prenatal fever, which may be caused by analgesic anesthesia and long labor duration.

At present, there is no ideal clinical prediction model for predicting cesarean uterine lacerations during the first stage of labor. In this study, an ROC curve model was constructed based on six predictors (occiput transverse position, occiput posterior position, cervical dilatation (> 5 cm), station of the fetal head at the ischial spines ( > + 1), caput succedaneum (> 3 cm), and duration of labor (> 12 h)) to predict cesarean uterine lacerations during the first stage of labor. Compared with clinical variables model, our new score model had similar predictive efficiency, when the score point ≥ 6, the sensitivity and specificity were 92.168% and 91.605% respectively with AUC 0.902. This ROC curve model provides clinicians with a quantitative tool to assess the risk of cesarean uterine lacerations during the first stage of labor, which has good application potential in clinical practice. By combining the model’s predictions, physicians can identify high-risk patients earlier and take preventive measures, such as enhancing labor monitoring and preparing appropriate operative maneuvers (including vaginal push or reverse breech extraction for impacted fetal head) [26], to reduce complication rates.

Limitations of the study

The strength of this study included a large population database from different hospitals with detailed data collection. Due to the multicenter study, the data analysis might be influenced by residual confounding from unrecognised factors, as fetal descent and the size of uterine cervix during labor judged by different obstetricians. Furthermore, this study may be influenced by factors such as the ratio of nulliparous and induction of labor. In the future, we will increase the sample size to conduct subgroup analyses and discussions on these factors.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study provided important information that might help obstetricians to identify patients at high risk for cesarean uterine lacerations during the first stage of labor and take appropriate labor management or choosing more appropriate surgical modalities.
