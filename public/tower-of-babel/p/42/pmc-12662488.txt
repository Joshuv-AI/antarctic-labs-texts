Complementary intervention effects of traditional Chinese exercise therapies on T2DM: A scoping review
Authors: Huixia Ren, Naijin Zhang, Yonghui Li, Yingxiang Li, Mengyang Wang, Huaien Bu, Hongwu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is commonly associated with metabolic and vascular disorders. Traditional Chinese exercise (TCE) shows potential benefits for glycemic control, but evidence is fragmented. This review summarizes current evidence on TCE for T2DM management.Methods:Following the PRISMA-ScR framework, this scoping review was conducted based on the PICOS principle to define the research question. Eight English and Chinese databases were systematically searched for randomized controlled trials published up to April 30, 2025, focusing on the use of TCE for T2DM, including Tai Chi, Baduanjin, Qigong, Yijinjing, and Wuqinxi. After applying predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 2 reviewers independently conducted study selection, data extraction, and analysis, while identifying differences across studies.Results:A total of 78 randomized controlled trials published between 2002 and 2024 were included. Baduanjin and Tai Chi were the most commonly applied interventions. The number of studies on T2DM increased steadily over the years, peaking in 2020 before showing a slight decline. Among the included studies, 73 applied a single traditional exercise modality, while 5 involved a combination of 2 or 3 practices. Fasting plasma glucose (84.61%, 67/78) and glycated hemoglobin (75.64%, 60/78) were the most frequently used efficacy evaluation indicators.Conclusion:TCE such as Tai Chi, Baduanjin, Qigong, Yijinjing, and Wuqinxi may serve as daily exercise options for patients with T2DM. However, the current body of evidence remains inconsistent and even contradictory. Future research should focus on the standardized integration of TCE with conventional pharmacotherapy and select more appropriate outcome measures.

1. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance, relative insulin deficiency, and progressive pancreatic β-cell dysfunction.[1] One of its primary characteristics is prolonged hyperglycemia, which not only disrupts homeostasis regulation of glucose metabolism but also induces vascular and tissue damage through various mechanisms such as oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, leading to chronic damage to vital organs such as the nervous system, retina, and kidneys,[2–4] significantly increasing the risk of serious complications such as diabetic retinopathy, diabetic neuropathy, cardiovascular disease, and diabetic nephropathy.[5,6] In addition, T2DM may contribute to cognitive decline, frailty, liver diseases, and other chronic complications.[7]

According to data from the Global Burden of Disease study, T2DM is currently the 8th leading cause of disease burden worldwide and is projected to rise to second place by 2050.[8] The 11th edition of the Diabetes Atlas reports that approximately 589 million adults aged 20 to 78 years were living with diabetes globally in 2024, a figure expected to rise to 853 million by 2050.[9] This growing prevalence imposes a substantial economic burden on individuals and society, while significantly reducing patients’ quality of life and exerting immense pressure on public health systems.

A growing body of evidence has shown that physical exercise confers wide-ranging benefits across multiple physiological systems.[10–12] Its mechanisms of action include reducing inflammatory markers, improving insulin signaling pathways, enhancing glucose uptake, and promoting lipid metabolism, thereby increasing insulin sensitivity and reducing ectopic fat accumulation.[13,14] Compared to other therapeutic approaches, exercise is cost-effective, demonstrates tangible outcomes, and can be easily integrated into daily life due to its flexible and individualized nature. Therefore, it is widely recommended as a key complementary strategy in the management of T2DM.

Traditional Chinese exercise (TCE), a vital component of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), integrate health preservation principles with slow, continuous, and gentle physical movements grounded in TCM theories.[15] Tai Chi, one of the most widely practiced TCE, has shown potential benefits in glycemic control and metabolic regulation among patients with T2DM. Other modalities such as Qigong, Baduanjin, Yijinjing, and Wuqinxi have also demonstrated favorable intervention effects.[16] Compared to high-intensity physical activities, these forms of exercise are milder, suitable for a broader population, and associated with fewer adverse effects, making them promising options in the management of chronic diseases.

Although increasing evidence supports the potential of TCE as complementary therapies for the treatment and care of T2DM, existing studies often exhibit fragmentation and heterogeneity in scope, methodological design, and outcome selection. Therefore, it is necessary to systematically review the current literature to gain a comprehensive understanding of the safety and effectiveness of these interventions in T2DM management. To the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive scoping review has yet synthesized the available evidence on traditional Chinese exercise therapies for T2DM. In response, this study conducts a scoping review to examine the characteristics of existing research and explore the potential mechanisms by which traditional Chinese exercise may benefit patients with T2DM.

2. Methods

2.1. Registration

This study is a scoping review based on previously published literature. As it did not involve direct recruitment of human participants or collection of individual patient data, neither institutional ethical approval nor informed consent was required.[17]

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

2.2.1. Study type

This review included all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the effects of TCE as complementary treatments for patients with T2DM. The included exercise modalities comprised Tai Chi, Baduanjin, Yijinjing, Wuqinxi, and Qigong. Only studies published in English or Chinese with full-text availability were considered.

2.2.2. Subjects

Participants were patients clearly diagnosed with T2DM without any complications or comorbidities. There were no restrictions regarding age or body weight status.

2.2.3. Interventions

The intervention group received TCE, either alone or in combination with conventional treatments such as oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin injections. Studies involving other TCM therapies (such as auricular acupressure, moxibustion, massage, or herbal decoctions) were excluded. The control group received oral hypoglycemic drugs, insulin injections, and other therapies.

2.2.4. Outcome indicators

There were no restrictions on the types of outcome indicators in the included studies. All reported outcomes were collected and analyzed.

2.2.5. Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: participants were not clearly diagnosed with T2DM; interventions involved combined therapies (e.g., exercise combined with herbal medicine or diet) that precluded independent evaluation of TCE; duplicate publications or studies with overlapping data from the same authors; nonclinical research such as reviews, commentaries, expert consensus documents, conference abstracts, case reports, animal or cellular studies, or academic theses; gray literature, low-quality studies, or inaccessible full texts.

2.3. Retrieval strategy

Two researchers independently conducted a systematic search of the following databases: CNKI, VIP, Wanfang Data, SinoMed, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase, and Web of Science. The search covered studies published from database inception to April 30, 2025. A combination of subject terms (MeSH/Subject Headings) and free-text terms was used, incorporating keywords related to type 2 diabetes and traditional Chinese exercise therapies (e.g., Tai Chi, Qigong, Baduanjin, Wuqinxi). Detailed search strategies are presented in Appendix 1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q781.

2.4. Literature screening

Two researchers (LYY and LYX) independently screened the literature. All retrieved records were imported into NoteExpress software (AegeanSoft, Beijing, China). After removing duplicates, the titles and abstracts of the remaining studies were independently screened by 2 reviewers, and the full texts of potentially relevant articles were retrieved for further evaluation. Discrepancies during the selection process were resolved by a third researcher (BHE). The study selection process followed the PRISMA 2020 flowchart (Appendix 2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q781).

2.5. Data extraction and analysis

Two reviewers (LYY and LYX) independently extracted key information from the included studies into a predesigned Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Extracted data included first author, year of publication, sample size, type of exercise, training frequency and duration, intervention period, and outcome measures. All data were subsequently summarized and analyzed to evaluate the effectiveness of traditional Chinese exercise therapy in the management of type 2 diabetes and to identify research trends. Data management and analysis were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond) and RStudio software (RStudio, PBC, Boston).

3. Results

3.1. Literature retrieval results

According to the predefined search strategy, a total of 1744 relevant articles were retrieved from 8 databases, including: PubMed (167 articles), Cochrane Library (260), Embase (45), Web of Science (104), CNKI (263), CBM (351), VIP Database (206), and Wanfang Data (348). After removing duplicates, 1121 articles remained. Based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 78 articles were ultimately included in this review. 3.2. Basic characteristics of the included literature

Five major types of TCE were identified in the included studies. Baduanjin was the most frequently studied (n = 39), followed by Tai Chi (n = 23), and Qigong (n = 9). In contrast, studies focusing on Yijinjing or combined interventions (e.g., Baduanjin + Qigong, Tai Chi + Wuqinxi) were relatively scarce. Overall, there was considerable heterogeneity in intervention protocols, including frequency, duration per session, and total intervention period. Exercise frequency ranged from once to 7 times per week, with session durations varying from 15 to 120 minutes. Intervention periods ranged from 2 weeks to 18 months. These variations reflect significant methodological heterogeneity across studies. 3.4. Intervention characteristics of included studies

3.4.1. Commonly used outcome indicators

Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) was the most frequently reported outcome indicator (66/78), with 59 studies reporting improvements. Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was reported in 59 studies, with 52 studies showing reductions post-intervention. The homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance was measured in 14 studies, with 9 reporting improvements in insulin sensitivity. Fasting insulin was assessed in 14 studies, with 8 reporting positive outcomes.

In terms of lipid metabolism, 39 studies reported total cholesterol and triglyceride (TG) levels, with 27 and 33 studies respectively indicating reductions. High-density lipoprotein and low-density lipoprotein were assessed in 35 and 25 studies, with favorable changes reported in 21 and 16 studies, respectively. Additionally, 30 studies reported on postprandial 2-hour blood glucose, of which 26 showed reductions. Body mass index and waist-to-hip ratio were evaluated in 22 and 10 studies, respectively, with beneficial trends observed in 14 and 7 studies.

Fewer studies evaluated blood pressure (n = 6), with only 2 showing significant effects. Hemorheology and malondialdehyde were each reported in 5 studies, with 4 studies supporting the positive effects of TCE. Among the exercise modalities, Baduanjin and Tai Chi were the most frequently used, with growing suggesting regulatory effects on blood glucose, lipid levels, body weight, and insulin sensitivity.

Additionally, some studies suggested that TCE may help improve antioxidant capacity (e.g., superoxide dismutase, SOD), reduce inflammation (e.g., C-reactive protein [CRP]), enhance muscle function, and improve quality of life and psychological well-being (e.g., anxiety and depression). The distribution of intervention frequency across outcome indicators for different exercise modalities is illustrated in Figure 3 and Table 2. Overall, these figures illustrate the frequency with which positive effects were reported across studies. However, they reflect descriptive counts rather than weighted estimates, and do not account for heterogeneity in intervention duration, frequency, intensity, or adherence. These findings should therefore be interpreted as indicative trends rather than definitive pooled outcomes.

3.4.2. Analysis of additional outcome indicators

Beyond glycemic and lipid control, the included studies also examined a wide range of secondary outcomes. Several trials reported effects on pancreatic function markers such as fructosamine, homeostasis model assessment of β-cell function (HOMA-β), and C-peptide, though findings were inconsistent. A number of studies assessed inflammatory and oxidative stress markers, showing reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), alongside increases in antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and glutathione peroxidase, suggesting potential anti-inflammatory and antioxidative benefits of TCE. Evidence for vascular function was mixed: some studies reported improvements in endothelial markers including nitric oxide, endothelial nitric oxide synthase, and vascular endothelial growth factor, as well as arterial stiffness indices such as cardio-ankle vascular index (CAVI) and ankle-brachial index (ABI), while others found no significant effects.

Regarding physical fitness, Qigong and Tai Chi were associated with improvements in muscle strength, flexibility, and pulmonary capacity, though changes in aerobic capacity were inconsistent. Limited studies also suggested possible benefits for neurological function, including enhanced nerve conduction velocity and reduced sensory thresholds, as well as for other biochemical indicators such as leukocyte count and lipid peroxides. Adverse events were rarely reported, with only 5 studies addressing safety; overall, no severe adverse events were documented, though underreporting remains a concern. Overall, glycemic indicators such as HbA1c and FPG were the most consistently validated outcomes, demonstrating improvements in many trials. Lipid-related measures, including TG, total cholesterol, HDL, and low-density lipoprotein, were also frequently investigated, although the findings were more heterogeneous. By contrast, outcomes such as blood pressure, renal function parameters, vascular indices, and neurological measures were evaluated in relatively few studies, with results that were often inconsistent or inconclusive. Accordingly, these findings should be regarded as tentative findings rather than definitive conclusions.

4. Discussion

4.1. Main findings

This scoping review included 78 RCTs published between 2002 and 2024, analyzing the application of TCE interventions in patients with T2DM. The findings suggest a growing trend in research interest on this topic, reaching a peak around 2020, followed by a potential decline likely influenced by the COVID-19 pandemic. Overall, this trend reflects increasing attention to the potential role of TCE in diabetes management. Most studies reported that TCE could serve as an effective complementary therapy for T2DM, with reported benefits for glycemic control, lipid metabolism, inflammation, neural function, and psychological well-being. However, the vast majority of studies were published in Chinese, with only a few appearing in international journals. Many studies had relatively small sample sizes, highly variable intervention durations, and heterogeneous designs, including differences in whether TCE was used alone or combined with oral hypoglycemics or other exercise modalities. These methodological inconsistencies limit the interpretability and generalizability of the findings.

In addition to conventional endpoints such as HbA1c, FPG, and lipid profiles, several studies assessed broader outcomes, including pancreatic function indices such as C-peptide and HOMA-β; inflammatory and oxidative stress markers such as CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, SOD, and malondialdehyde; vascular and cardiopulmonary measures such as ABI, CAVI, VO2 max, and vital capacity; as well as neurological parameters such as nerve conduction velocity and vibration perception thresholds. However, the findings across these domains were heterogeneous and often derived from single or small-scale studies. Thus, the evidence should be considered exploratory and preliminary rather than conclusive.

The perceived robustness of the current evidence must also be interpreted with caution. The synthesis here is based on counts of studies reporting positive findings, without weighting for intervention duration, intensity, adherence, or methodological rigor. Intervention protocols varied substantially, ranging from 2 weeks to 18 months, 1 to 7 sessions per week, and 15 to 120 minutes per session. Such variability, combined with differences in adherence reporting, undermines comparability across trials. Moreover, trials of differing methodological quality (including blinded RCTs and small open-label studies) were treated equivalently in this review, which may exaggerate the overall strength of the evidence.

Of particular concern are the conflicting findings across trials. Some trials demonstrated improvements in insulin sensitivity, such as reductions in the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance or increases in HOMA-β, whereas others reported no significant changes, and a few even documented declines in C-peptide levels. These divergent findings may be attributable to differences in study populations, including variations in age, obesity status, and disease duration, as well as disparities in intervention frequency, duration, and intensity. Additional contributing factors may include the use of concomitant treatments, variability in laboratory assessment methods, and limited statistical power. Together, these issues highlight the importance of standardized intervention protocols, stratified analyses, and more rigorously designed trials to clarify the conditions under which TCE exerts measurable metabolic benefits.

Overall, the current evidence suggests that TCE may improve glucose regulation and potentially influence lipid metabolism, inflammation, vascular health, and neuropathy. However, these conclusions are based on descriptive mapping of reported outcomes rather than weighted quantitative synthesis, and should therefore be considered preliminary. Future research should prioritize adequately powered, multicenter RCTs with standardized protocols to confirm these findings and establish a more reliable evidence base.

4.2. Multiple advantages of TCE

Most of the included studies examined the combined use of TCE with conventional treatments, such as oral antidiabetic medications or insulin injections. While pharmacological treatments are effective for glycemic control, they may also lead to adverse events such as hypoglycemia, gastrointestinal discomfort, weight gain, and electrolyte disturbances.[19,20] In contrast, TCE not only supports glycemic management but also improves neuromuscular function, enhance overall health and quality of life, and potentially reduce the risk of medication-related adverse effects.[21,22]

TCE is characterized by slow, gentle movements and low physical load on the joints and cardiopulmonary system.[23] These exercises typically incorporate breathing techniques, mental focus, and lifestyle adjustments, reflecting the holistic principle of harmonizing the body, breath, and mind. The emphasis on coordination and joint mobility contributes to the regulation and restoration of internal organ function and overall physiological balance.[24] As moderate- to low-intensity aerobic activities, TCE has low physical risk and good clinical safety, making them suitable. However, not all trials consistently confirmed these benefits; for instance, some Tai Chi studies reported no significant improvements in vascular indices such as ABI and CAVI, or in cardiopulmonary fitness measures, despite regular practice.

From a physiological perspective, the study by Chang et al[25] indicated that TCE facilitates the degradation, transport, and excretion of lipid peroxidation products, thereby reducing oxidative stress and improving the cellular microenvironment. These exercises may also enhance insulin sensitivity and promote glucose uptake and utilization, supporting glycemic homeostasis.[26] Nevertheless, several investigations did not observe meaningful changes in lipid profiles or body weight, suggesting that metabolic responses may vary by population or intervention intensity.

In terms of anti-inflammatory effects, Shu et al[27] found that Tai Chi significantly reduced TNF-α levels, and high-volume Tai Chi training further lowered IL-6 and CRP levels. Hu et al[28] additionally reported that Tai Chi increased serum irisin levels while inhibiting the activation of the nuclear factor kappa B signaling pathway, reducing inflammatory cytokine expression. This mechanism may help alleviate chronic low-grade inflammation, protect pancreatic β-cells from damage, and improve insulin resistance.

Regarding lipid metabolism, these moderate-intensity aerobic exercises enhance lipolysis and fatty acid oxidation, accelerate the metabolism of TG and cholesterol, and promote the transport and utilization of free fatty acids by peripheral tissues. This may reduce lipid peroxidation and mitigate the reciprocal disturbances between lipid and glucose metabolism.[29–31]

Beyond somatic benefits, TCE also places strong emphasis on psychological regulation. Mental focus training, deep breathing, and meditative components can modulate the central nervous system, improve emotional stability and self-regulation, and help alleviate symptoms of anxiety and depression.[32,33] Dong et al further suggested that TCE activates the parasympathetic nervous system, triggers relaxation responses, and reduces stress hormone levels. Kong et al[34] demonstrated that Tai Chi can modulate neural activity and connectivity in brain regions and networks involved in mood regulation, providing neurobiological support for its mental health effects. Additionally, Luo et al[35] found that the slow stretching and meditative aspects of Baduanjin may modulate cortical function and influence the hypothalamic–pituitary–target gland axis, thus promoting pancreatic β-cell recovery and achieving dual benefits in both mood improvement and glycemic control.

4.3. Limitations and methodological issues

Several methodological limitations constrain the robustness and generalizability of the current evidence. First, outcome measures were unevenly distributed. While most trials focused on HbA1c and FPG, relatively few examined mechanistic endpoints such as inflammatory markers, oxidative stress indices, vascular function, or nerve conduction, and findings across these domains were inconsistent.

Second, intervention parameters varied substantially in frequency, duration, intensity, and session length, with no standardized prescription for TCE. Frequencies ranged from once to 7 sessions per week, durations from <2 months to 2 years, and session lengths from 15 to 120 minutes. Such heterogeneity in exercise “dose” complicates comparability and may partly explain divergent results. Moreover, the present review summarized positive findings based on study counts without weighting for intervention duration, intensity, or adherence, which may exaggerate the apparent strength of the evidence.

Third, study quality was frequently suboptimal. Although all included studies were RCTs, most lacked detailed reporting on randomization or allocation concealment, and only 4 reported any form of blinding. The equal treatment of higher-quality blinded RCTs and small open-label trials may have overstated the robustness of the evidence. Future trials should adhere to rigorous randomization protocols, improve transparency in reporting, and incorporate blinding strategies where feasible.

Fourth, follow-up and safety assessments were rarely reported. Only 6 studies mentioned any follow-up protocols, and only 5 studies reported intervention-related adverse reactions, with very low reporting rates that may reflect insufficient monitoring or selective reporting bias rather than the true absence of risk. Given that patients with T2DM may face exercise-related complications such as musculoskeletal injuries, hypoglycemia, or cardiovascular events, systematic and standardized safety monitoring is essential. The lack of long-term efficacy and safety data hinders comprehensive evaluation of TCE.

Fifth, geographic and population characteristics also limit generalizability. Most studies were conducted in mainland China, with only 7 originating from other countries. This geographic and cultural concentration constrains external validity, underscoring the need for multicenter and cross-cultural RCTs (particularly in Western and Southeast Asian populations) to confirm the applicability of findings.

In terms of participant age, the studies showed wide variation. Three studies included participants aged 23 to 71 years, 14 enrolled those over 30, and 13 targeted populations over 60, with most focusing on adults aged 40 and above. Yet none performed subgroup analyses by age, despite its influence on metabolic status, comorbidities, and exercise tolerance. Future trials should incorporate stratified analyses to explore potential age-related differences in treatment response.

Lastly, most included trials had small sample sizes. Twelve studies had group sizes of 10 to 20 participants, fifteen had 20 to 30 per group, and only 8 included >100 participants per group. The remainder had group sizes between 30 and 60. Insufficient sample sizes reduce statistical power, limit the feasibility of subgroup analyses, and long-term effect evaluations, thereby weakening the reliability of conclusions. Larger, multicenter trials with adequate power calculations are needed to enhance study credibility.

In summary, future research should focus on: defining standardized TCE prescriptions, including frequency, duration, intensity, and cycle length; improving study design by implementing rigorous randomization and blinding protocols, and increase sample size through multicenter collaboration; expanding the outcome indicator system with particular emphasis on mechanistic outcomes such as inflammation, oxidative stress, and nerve function; strengthening follow-up and adverse event monitoring to assess long-term efficacy and safety; promoting international, cross-cultural studies to evaluate the intervention’s applicability in diverse populations and improve global generalizability.

5. Conclusions

This review systematically evaluated the current clinical application of TCE in the management of T2DM. The findings indicate that such interventions hold promise for improving metabolic parameters and overall health outcomes in T2DM patients and may serve as a beneficial complement to conventional treatments. However, the limited number and suboptimal methodological quality restrict the establishment of definitive clinical practice guidelines. High-quality clinical trials are still needed to provide more robust evidence to support standardized clinical application of TCE in T2DM management.
