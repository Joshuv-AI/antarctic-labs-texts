SplitTurboID mapping of dimeric protein phosphatase complex interactomes
Authors: Abishankari Rajkumar, Antoine Gaudreau-Lapierre, Curtis LF Anthony, Vincent Nguyen, Sarah Ooi, Denise Campuzano, Laura Trinkle-Mulcahy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryUnlike its counterpart Ser/Thr kinases, the predominant Ser/Thr protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) is a promiscuous enzyme that gains its subcellular localization and substrate specificity from a large panel of regulatory proteins with which it associates. Mapping the interactomes of catalytic and regulatory subunits on their own provides evidence for the targeting of PP1 to a wide range of cellular pathways, but does not provide a conditional analysis of individual holophosphatase complexes. To achieve this, we adapted a splitTurboID fragment complementation approach for the proximal interactome mapping of distinct catalytic-regulatory subunit pairs. We demonstrated the reliability and high spatial resolution of this technique, using an analysis pipeline that highlights unique and enriched near neighbors above shared common contaminants. In addition to confirming that a newly identified regulatory subunit has both PP1- and non-PP1-related associations, we further demonstrate the general applicability of this approach to other types of protein-protein pairs.Subject areas: Biological sciences, Biochemistry, Biochemical research method

Introduction

Reversible protein phosphorylation, the most common post-translational modification (PTM), involves the covalent attachment of phosphate groups to amino acid residues by protein kinases and their subsequent removal by protein phosphatases. This acts as a molecular switch that can modulate protein conformation and/or protein-protein interactions, leading to alterations in enzymatic activity, subcellular localization, turnover of targets, and signaling by other PTMs. Recent advances in mass spectrometry (MS)-based phosphoproteomics revealed that the majority of cellular proteins can be phosphorylated, and most on multiple sites.1 Whereas thousands of these sites have been linked to specific upstream kinases, only ∼7% have been mapped to a cognate phosphatase.2 Mapping each phosphatase to its physiological substrates is essential for understanding the specific roles that they play in complex phospho-signaling networks.

Although the human genome encodes a similar number of Tyr phosphatases to counteract Tyr kinase activity, Ser/Thr kinases outnumber Ser/Thr phosphatases by a factor of 10. This difference reflects a broader in vitro substrate specificity for the phosphatases, with in vivo specificity provided by additional regulatory mechanisms that govern their localization and activity. The two most abundant Ser/Thr phosphatases, protein phosphatase type 1 (PP1) and protein phosphatase type 2 (PP2), both achieve their substrate specificity through the association of the catalytic subunit with a range of regulatory subunits.3 This results in the combinatorial generation of a large and diverse group of multimeric PP1 and PP2A holoenzyme complexes, each with its own subset of substrates and mechanism(s) of regulation.

The PP1 catalytic subunit (PP1c), which is ubiquitously expressed in all eukaryotic cells, is estimated to account for 40–70% of Ser/Thr dephosphorylation events.4 Mammalian PP1c is found primarily as three isoforms (ɑ, β/d, ɣ) that are encoded by three distinct genes, with the additional tissue-specific splicing of PP1ɣ generating the testis-enriched and sperm-specific isoform PP1ɣ2.5 The PP1c isoforms are ∼90% identical in amino acid sequence, with minor variations primarily at their NH2 and COOH termini.6 Loss of function and biochemical studies of individual PP1 genes suggest some level of compensation but also highlight distinct phenotypes associated with the disruption of a single gene.5 The three PP1c isoforms show distinct and dynamic localization patterns in cells that reflect underlying differences in their distribution between regulatory complexes.7,8,9

A challenge in identifying substrates and other functionally relevant interactions for individual dimeric PP complexes is that the catalytic and regulatory subunits can play roles independent of their association. Regulatory subunits, for example, are expressed in excess over the catalytic subunit, and are not always found in complex with it.3,10 That means that, although mapping the interactomes of individual regulatory subunits can capture associations related to their roles in dimeric PP1 complexes, it cannot distinguish associations that are specific to these holophosphatase complexes. An example is the PP1:c20orf27 complex that we demonstrate here to play a predominantly nuclear role, in contrast to an unrelated and spatially distinct role for c20orf27 on its own.

We thus set out to identify a robust and reliable method to conditionally map the proximity interactomes of specific PP1c:regulatory subunit dimeric complexes. To do this, we adapted a recently described splitTurboID approach and confirmed that, in contrast to previous split sites in the original BirA∗ biotin ligase that yielded ligase fragments with some residual activity,11,12 a T194/G195 split in the TurboID variant yields N- and C-terminal fragments that are catalytically inactive, similar in size (15 and 22 kDa), and have low intrinsic affinity.13 Co-transfecting cells with PP1c fused to the C-terminal domain (CTD-PP1) and a regulatory subunit to the N-terminal domain (NTD-regulatory subunit) led to the reconstitution of biotin ligase activity. Subcellular biotinylation patterns were in agreement with the observed localizations of all complexes tested, and affinity purification/mass spectrometry (AP/MS) confirmed the high specificity and spatial resolution of the splitTurboID approach for mapping distinct PP1 complex interactomes. We further show that this is an approach that can readily be extended to the comparison of complex-dependent interactomes under different conditions, and to the analysis of other types of dimeric protein-protein complexes.

Results

SplitTurboID allows for conditional biotin proximity labeling by dimeric protein phosphatase 1 complexes

To study the conditional interactomes of specific PP1 holophosphatases, we adapted the SUMO-ID protein fragment complementation strategy developed by Barroso-Gomila et al.13 The strategy was derived from the more active TurboID mutant of the E. coli-derived BirA∗ (R40G) biotin ligase.14 Like us, the group had noted that previous BirA∗ split sites left residual ligase activity (and hence background labeling) in the N-terminal fragment. They used structural analyses to identify a new split site (T194/G195) and demonstrated that both fragments were catalytically inactive. When the fragments were tagged to SUMO and a protein known to be SUMOylated, the resulting re-formed TurboID supported biotinylation of proteins proximal to the SUMOylated protein.

To test the applicability of this splitTurboID strategy to map dimeric PP1 complex interactomes, we fused the N-terminal fragment (FlagNTD; aa1-194) to the nucleolar PP1 regulatory subunit RRP1B and the C-terminal fragment (mycCTD; aa 195–320) to the gamma isoform of PP1c (Figure 1A). Both were co-transfected into HEK293 cells, which were then provided with excess biotin (50 μM) for 0, 30, 60, 120 or 240 min. Biotinylation of cellular proteins was detected by 30 min and increased with longer labeling times. This was demonstrated both by the streptavidin-HRP staining of total protein on a Western blot and by the streptavidin-AlexaFluor488 staining of fixed cells (Figure 1B). Biotinylation was enriched in nucleoli, consistent with our identification of RRP1B:PP1 as the predominant nucleolar phosphatase complex.15,16 An interesting observation was that the overall biotinylation levels were more on par with those of the less active miniTurboID variant rather than full-length TurboID, when similar co-/transfection efficiencies were achieved. This may reflect incomplete reconstitution due to unmatched levels of NTD- and CTD-tagged proteins or the inability of the reconstituted enzyme to fully recapitulate the structure of the translated/folded full-length protein. Given the disparity in activity, we do not recommend using full-length TurboID as a control for splitTurboID experiments. Based on our experience with miniTurboID proximal interactome mapping,17 we decided to use the same 4 h labeling strategy for our splitTurboID experiments to ensure sufficient sensitivity of detection.

We next confirmed that a detectable biotinylation signal was only observed when both FlagNTD-RRP1B and mycCTD-PP1ɣ were expressed in cells (Figures 1C and 1D). Our strategy was then extended to demonstrate the formation of distinct cytoplasmic, nucleoplasmic, and nucleolar PP1 dimeric complexes (Figure 1E). NIPP1 is a regulatory subunit that binds all PP1c isoforms and localizes to the nucleoplasm, with additional accumulation at splicing speckles,18 and this biotinylation pattern was observed for splitPP1ɑ:NIPP1, splitPP1β:NIPP1, and splitPP1ɣ:NIPP1. Similarly, in keeping with the known localizations of the respective regulatory proteins, filamentous biotinylation patterns were observed in the cytoplasm with splitASPP2:PP1ɑ (actin cytoskeleton, centrosome),19,20 splitMYPT1:PP1β (actomyosin network),21 and splitPP1ɣ:TPRN (actin cytoskeleton).22 RRP1B can bind both PP1ɣ and PP1β,16 and nucleolar biotinylation was observed for both pairings. The nucleolar protein NOM1 was originally described in complex with PP1ɑ,23 and we observed nucleolar biotinylation with this pair. These experiments confirmed the subcellular biotinylation specificity of the re-formed holoenzyme complexes.

Affinity purification/mass spectrometry analysis confirms the specificity of splitPP1 complex interactomes

To validate our splitPP1 method as a means of conditionally mapping specific holoenzyme complex interactomes, we chose 3 that have distinct subcellular distributions: predominantly cytoplasmic TPRN:PP1, nucleoplasmic NIPP1:PP1, and nucleolar RRP1B:PP1. In each case, the FlagNTD-tagged regulatory protein was paired with mycCTD-PP1ɣ. Western blot analysis of lysates with streptavidin-HRP and anti-Flag and anti-myc antibodies confirmed that the expression of any of the fusion proteins on their own did not support biotinylation, whereas strong (and unique) biotinylation signatures were observed for each pair (Figure 2A).

We first used an internally controlled quantitative stable isotope labeling by amino acids (SILAC) AP/MS approach24 to compare the biotinylation patterns of the paired complexes to those of the NTD-tagged regulatory subunits on their own. This served two purposes, first to confirm that proximal labeling is conditional on the formation of the complex, and second, to assess the nonspecific background binding of proteins to the affinity matrix (Figure 2B). We used the mean log2H:L ratio for the mitochondrial carboxylases as our baseline (orange circles), as these are known contaminants that bind biotin directly and should be equally represented in both conditions. The dashed red line indicates a threshold for enrichment that is > 2-fold higher than this baseline. Known interactors for each complex were significantly enriched above this threshold in the Heavy condition, confirming robust biotinylation only in the presence of the re-formed biotin ligase. As with all AP-based MS experiments, the background primarily consisted of abundant and/or sticky proteins that bound non-specifically to the affinity matrix.24 Using the same color-coding to highlight specific groups of proteins (Figure 2C) demonstrates how some can be significantly enriched near neighbors in one dataset (e.g., large ribosomal proteins in the splitPP1:RRP1B dataset and splicing factors in the splitPP1:NIPP1 dataset) yet appear as background contaminants in others (e.g., splicing factors in the splitPP1:TPRN dataset and large ribosomal proteins in the splitPP1:NIPP1 and splitPP1:TPRN datasets). This highlights the drawback of discarding certain proteins simply because they are known to be common contaminants in AP/MS experiments, as it risks removing context-dependent bona fide near neighbors. Indeed, although we identified a large number of unique near neighbors for each dataset (Figure 2D), there was also a significant amount of overlap. Our next goal was thus to develop a robust and reliable analysis method to mine these regions of overlap and determine contextually relevant near neighbors based on their weighted distributions between distinct PP1 holoenzyme complex interactomes.

We used a Python-based workflow to generate weighted comparisons of all proteins detected in the 3 splitPP1 datasets. The MaxQuant output files were first put through a Sifter script to discard environmental contaminants (e.g., keratins) and proteins identified in the reverse peptide database, as well as proteins with no quantified intensity value. Sifter also calculates the relative abundance of each protein (summed peptide intensities normalized by molecular weight) and uses that value to determine the proportional representation of each protein in the dataset (relative abundance/summed relative abundance for all proteins detected). The Sifter results are then put through an Overlapper script, which calculates the distribution of each protein across the 3 datasets. This is again based on proportional representation, in this case, the fraction of the total that a protein represents in dataset 1 vs. the summed fractions of the total that it represents in all 3 datasets. Based on this, the protein is assigned a value for each dataset between 0 (not detected) and 1 (only detected here). As an example, the F-actin capping protein CAPZB that was only detected in the splitPP1:TPRN dataset was assigned a value of 1 for that dataset, and values of 0 for the splitPP1:RRP1B and splitPP1:NIPP1 datasets. It is thus considered a unique hit for splitPP1:TPRN. A protein that was detected in all 3 datasets but only enriched in one of them, such as the abundant nucleolar phosphoprotein NPM, had a high value for splitPP1:RRP1B (0.97) and low values for splitPP1:NIPP1 (0.014) and splitPP1:TPRN (0.016). NPM is thus considered a high confidence near neighbor for splitPP1:RRP1B (distribution value >0.75) and a background contaminant for splitPP1:TPRN and splitPP1:NIPP1. A common contaminant such as the mitochondrial acetyl-CoA carboxylase, ACACA, had a more central distribution as it was equally represented in all 3 datasets (0.29, 0.39, 0.32). Our Plotter script uses these values of distribution as coordinates to generate a 3D graph. For the graph shown in Figure 2E, we highlighted the unique and high confidence hits for each complex, using their respective color-coding. A strength of this approach is that known abundant/sticky proteins, which are often discarded as contaminants in AP/MS datasets, are assessed with higher specificity and contextuality.

Given that the Overlapper script achieves the same goal as SILAC labeling with a built-in negative control, i.e., clusters common contaminants while highlighting unique and enriched near neighbors, we repeated the splitPP1:RRP1B, splitPP1:NIPP1, and splitPP1:TPRN AP/MS experiments with no labeling (co-transfection of each pair in 1 × 15 cm dish). These replicates showed a similar distribution of hits, confirming that these experiments are amenable to label-free quantitation (Data S1). For our functional analysis, we compared the results and used Cytoscape ClueGO and the Gene Ontology (GO) Resource-based GO analysis to cluster proteins that were unique or significantly enriched (distribution value >0.75) in both replicates.

Functionally grouped biological process networks for the splitPP1:RRP1B interactome demonstrated the enrichment of proteins involved in ribosome biogenesis (particularly large subunit biogenesis) and chromatin organization/assembly (Figure 2F), while GO analysis revealed the selective enrichment of related molecular functions such as rRNA and snoRNA binding (Figure 2I). This was consistent with previously defined roles for RRP1B in these pathways.15,25 In contrast, the splitPP1:NIPP1 interactome was functionally enriched in RNA splicing, ribonucleoprotein complex, and DNA damage response factors (Figure 2G), with GO analysis highlighting the selective enrichment of mRNA and nucleic acid binding proteins (Figure 2I). This was again consistent with previously described functions for NIPP1.18,26 Although TPRN has not yet been studied to the same extent, it is known to associate with the actin cytoskeleton network,22 and the splitPP1:TPRN interactome was enriched in factors related to the organization of this network, intracellular protein transport, and cell morphogenesis (Figure 2H). GO analysis confirmed the selective enrichment of various filament-binding factors, chaperones, and cell adhesion molecules (Figure 2I). Our AP/MS results thus demonstrated the ability of splitTurboID to clearly distinguish the subcellular roles of distinct PP1 holoenzyme complex interactomes. This prompted us to examine whether splitTurboID could distinguish between 2 distinct PP1 complexes located in the same subcellular compartment.

Affinity purification/mass spectrometry analysis of distinct nucleolar complexes confirms the high spatial resolution of splitPP1

Although we identified RRP1B as the predominant nucleolar regulatory subunit responsible for recruiting PP1β and PP1ɣ to this structure,15,16 another regulatory subunit that can localize to the nucleolus is NOM1.23 When expressed as GFP fusion proteins, RRP1B and NOM1 show a similar localization pattern within this spatially organized structure. Both are predominantly distributed within the granular component (GC), where pre-ribosomal subunit assembly takes place. Counter-staining for the dense fibrillar component (DFC) marker (and RNA 2′-O-methyltransferase) Fibrillarin showed additional accumulation of GFP-RRP1B but not GFP-NOM1 within this subcompartment, where pre-rRNA processing occurs (Figures 3A and 3B). Colocalization analysis of co-expressed GFP-RRP1B and mCh-NOM1 (Figure 3C) confirmed significant overlap of their signals within the nucleolus, but also distinct regions that were enriched more with RRP1B (green; arrow) or NOM1 (magenta; arrowhead).

Western blot analysis of splitPP1:RRP1B and splitNOM:PP1 lysates with streptavidin-HRP showed similar patterns, albeit with some distinct bands (Figure 3D). We used our Sifter/Overlapper workflow to generate distribution values that were plotted in a 2D heatmap (Figure 3E) ranging from green (unique to splitPP1:RRP1B) to magenta (unique to splitPP1:NOM1). A total of 540 proteins were identified, with 25% unique to the splitPP1:RRP1B dataset and 32% to the splitPP1:NOM1 dataset. Additional high-confidence near neighbors for each complex were highlighted in the overlapping region (distribution value > 0.75 for a specific dataset). As noted on the heatmap, a key difference is that factors involved in ribosomal large subunit (LSU) biogenesis were enriched in the RRP1B interactome, while factors involved in ribosomal small subunit (SSU) biogenesis were enriched in the NOM1 interactome. This is consistent with work by our lab and others that linked both proteins to the regulation of ribosome biogenesis,15,27 albeit suggesting distinct roles for these two dimeric PP1 complexes in this pathway.

A similar distribution of hits was detected when we repeated the splitPP1:RRP1B and splitPP1:NOM1 AP/MS experiments (Data S2). For our functional analyses, we compared the results and used Cytoscape ClueGO and the Gene Ontology Resource-based GO analysis to cluster proteins that were unique or significantly enriched (distribution value >0.75) in both replicates. As observed previously in our comparison of 3 splitPP1 datasets, functionally grouped biological process networks for the splitPP1:RRP1B interactome demonstrated the enrichment of proteins involved in ribosome biogenesis (particularly large subunit biogenesis) and chromatin organization (Figure 3F). Proteins involved in rRNA processing were also enriched, consistent with the accumulation of RRP1B in the subnucleolar DFC region (Figures 3A–3F, and 3H). In contrast, the splitPP1:NOM1 interactome showed the enrichment of factors involved in small subunit biogenesis and ribosome assembly, as well as cytoplasmic translation (Figures 3G and 3H). GO enrichment analysis of molecular function showed some overlap, including the enrichment of RNA- and nucleic acid-binding proteins in both datasets (Figure 3I). This is consistent with the involvement of both complexes in ribosome biogenesis, albeit at different steps in the pathway.

Further optimization and extension of the splitTurboID approach

We found that some of the NTD-regulatory protein:CTD-PP1 pairs that we tested did not express well by co-transfection, with <20% of the cells showing a detectable biotinylation signal by streptavidin-fluorophore labeling (Figure 4A) and a correspondingly weak streptavidin-HRP signal in the lysate as shown by Western blot analysis (Figure 4B). Not surprisingly, the resulting AP/MS datasets were dominated by the contaminant mitochondrial carboxylases, with few genuine near neighbors identified (Figure S1). Using the PP1:RepoMan pair to test delivery options, we found that transfecting NTD-RepoMan into a cell line stably expressing CTD-PP1 increased the fraction of cells that supported biotinylation, while the most robust signal was obtained by the infection of the CTD-PP1 stable line with a lentiviral plasmid that drives the expression of NTD-RepoMan (Figures 4A and 4B). This led to a correspondingly significant increase in the number of proteins detected by AP/MS analysis (Figure S1).

In addition to mapping steady-state interactomes, splitTurboID can also be used to capture changes in complex interactomes that are triggered by cellular perturbations. We demonstrate this here using splitPP1:RepoMan to monitor changes in proximity labeling patterns as the complex accumulates at facultative heterochromatic regions that form in response to sodium arsenite stress (Figure 4C).28

Importantly, splitTurboID is not limited to the analysis of dimeric PP1 complexes, as any 2 proteins that bring the fragments together should support re-formation of an active ligase. We show this here by pairing the histone reader HP1ɣ with the TRIM28/KAP1/TIF1β transcriptional co-repressor with which it has been shown to interact (Figure 4D).29 SplitTurboID can also be exploited to focus on the proximity interactome of a particular subcellular pool of a protein, as shown in Figure 4E where the CTD-tagged actin-binding protein TPRN was paired with an NTD-tagged LifeAct peptide that binds filamentous actin.30

splitTurboID confirms a distinct role for PP1:c20orf27

Our ongoing PP1c IP/MS-based interactome analyses recently flagged c20orf27 as a putative regulatory subunit, as it was significantly enriched in more than one dataset and contains a variant of the “RVxF” short linear motif (SLiM) that binds a hydrophobic groove on the catalytic subunit (consensus sequence aa 54–57). When expressed as a GFP-tagged protein, c20orf27 distributes homogenously throughout the cell (Figure 5A). Direct interaction with PP1 was confirmed using a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay,31 in which the binding partners were fused to the N- and C-termini of yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) and co-expressed in cells to assess the re-formation of an active fluorophore (Figure 5B). No fluorescence was observed when mutant c20orf27 (V54A/F57A) was co-expressed with PP1, confirming that their association is mediated by the KVGF motif. This was further validated using a complementary fluorescence two-hybrid approach, in which GFP-tagged wild type, but not mutant, c20orf27 bound to mCh-PP1 that was tethered to an exogenous gene locus (Figure S2A).

To determine potential regulatory roles for a c20orf27:PP1 complex, we first employed our quantitative SILAC-based IP/MS technique24 to map the whole cell interactome of GFP-tagged c20orf27 in HeLa cells (Figure 5C and Data S3). PP1 and the E3 ubiquitin ligase substrate specifier APPBP2 were top hits, along with other proteins that have been linked to APPBP2 as part of a C-terminal end-directed ligase complex (CUL2, Elongins B/C).32,33,34 Although c20orf27 does not contain the C-end degron motif found in APPBP2-targeted substrates, we wanted to rule out the possibility that we were simply capturing c20orf27 that was being targeted for proteasomal degradation by this complex. The amount of endogenous APPBP2 in complex with c20orf27 did not change with proteasomal inhibition, nor did GFP-c20orf27 show evidence of a ladder of ubiquitinated forms under this condition (Figure 5D). None of the factors related to the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex was detected in any of our PP1c interactome datasets, suggesting that the association of c20orf27 with the APPBP2/CUL2 complex may be independent of its association with PP1. This is in contrast to other regulatory subunits that we have analyzed using this approach, such as the ribosome biogenesis factor overlap that we originally demonstrated for independently mapped RRP1B and PP1ɣ interactomes15 and also observed for splitPP1:RRP1B.

Data S3. IP/MS dataset for GFP-c20orf27 and Sifter and Overlapper tables for splitPP1:c20orf27, related to Figure 5

Indeed, conditional splitTurboID mapping of the PP1:c20orf27 holophosphatase complex by the transfection of NTD-c20orf27 into HEK293A cells stably overexpressing CTD-PP1ɣ did not detect any of the APPBP2 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex members, supporting our hypothesis that the association of c20orf27 with this complex is likely independent of its role as a PP1 regulatory protein. To rule out potential cell type differences, we repeated the splitPP1:c20orf27 experiment in HeLa cells and did not detect biotinylated APPBP2 by AP/Western blot analysis using anti-APPBP2 antibodies (Figure S2B). In contrast, APPBP2 was biotinylated by miniTurboID-tagged c20orf27 on its own (Figure S2B).

The splitPP1:c20orf27 complex instead biotinylated a cluster of proteins linked primarily to RNA processing and mRNA metabolic processes, including several splicing-related factors (Figure 5E). When compared directly to the splitPP1:NIPP1 interactome, splitPP1:c20orf27 showed the enriched labeling of hnRNP proteins that have been linked to the regulation of pre-mRNA splicing, whereas splitNIPP1:PP1 labeled a wider range of splicing factors that included core spliceosome components (Figures 5F and S2C, and Data S3). The AP/MS dataset was also reflected in the biotinylation pattern observed in cells, which confirmed the accumulation of splitPP1:c20orf27-biotinylated proteins at splicing speckles marked by anti-SC35 (Figure 5G). Given the apparent homogenous distribution of GFP-c20orf27 throughout the cytoplasm and nucleus in both live and formaldehyde-fixed cells (Figure 5A), we tested methanol fixation to see if loss of diffuse pools of protein (due to membrane permeabilization during fixation) would reveal any underlying association with cellular structures. Indeed, we confirmed the retention of a pool of GFP-c20orf27 at splicing speckles marked by anti-SC35 staining in methanol-fixed cells (Figure 5H). Interestingly, methanol fixation also revealed the accumulation of GFP-c20orf27 (but not biotinylation by splitPP1:c20orf27) at the perinuclear region (Figures 5G and 5H). GFP-tagged APPBP2 was also found to accumulate at this region with mCh-c20orf27 (Figure 5I). Additional accumulation in nuclear puncta was only observed for c20orf27, which likely represents the PP1 complexes. Future work will address the functional relevance of the distinct c20orf27-PP1 and c20orf27-APPBP2 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex associations.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to gain a better understanding of the holoenzyme complexes that regulate the subcellular localization, substrate specificity, and catalytic activity of PP1c. Our previous work confirmed that GFP-PP1 is an accurate molecular reporter for endogenous pools of the protein when overexpressed at low levels (∼2-fold above endogenous), and can be used to identify and/or validate novel holophosphatase complexes.9,35 Here, we extended our global analyses of PP1c distribution to a more focused analysis of specific holophosphatase complexes by adapting a splitTurboID approach to conditionally map dimeric protein-protein interactomes. The approach we adapted was published as SUMO-ID, as it was developed to map the proximal interactomes of SUMOylated proteins.13 The authors used structural information to choose an optimal split site at T194/G195. This results in fragments that are more similar in size (22 and 15 kDa) than those utilized in another splitTurboID approach (L73/G74) that has been predominantly utilized to map the composition of specific subcellular regions and inter-organelle interactions.36,37 Importantly, we confirmed in our hands that neither half has detectable ligase activity on its own, which was a confounding factor when we tried other split sites originally published in BirA∗(e.g., K140/R141, E256/G257).11,12

To demonstrate the high selectivity and spatial resolution of this technique, we mapped the proximity interactomes of multiple holophosphatase complexes. We also developed an accessible Python-based analysis workflow to compare datasets and mine the overlapping hits more effectively, distinguishing high-confidence near neighbors from common contaminants. This data mining is a critical step, as most BioID strategies rely on the inclusion of a negative control (e.g., untagged biotin ligase) that usually expresses at a higher level than the ligase-tagged protein. This results in a very large number of nonspecifically biotinylated proteins, and it can be difficult to determine which should be subtracted from the experimental dataset. As an example, the abundant ribosomal proteins turn up in all AP/MS datasets (immunoprecipitation and BioID) and are often treated as contaminants and removed. If such an approach were implemented in our datasets, we would have lost the genuine enrichment of RPLs with RRP1B:PP1 that highlights the previously defined role of this complex in ribosomal large subunit biogenesis.15 Our workflow only removes environmental contaminants such as keratins to ensure that we do not bias the analysis. As another example, the “mitochondrion organization” node in our GO analysis of the splitPP1:TPRN high confidence hits may represent a cluster of contaminants (as TPRN does not show any obvious localization to mitochondria and mitochondrial proteins are also common contaminants in AP/MS experiments), but it could also represent proteins that that are biotinylated by the complex in the cytoplasm prior to mitochondrial import, or proteins that are annotated as mitochondrial but also localize to other regions of the cell. The node comprises 6 proteins, only 2 of which (ATAD3A and HSPD1) are annotated solely as mitochondrial-localized in the Human Protein Atlas38 (proteinatlas.org), while the other 4 have been detected in additional compartments (CTTN, HSP90AA1, NDUF9A, and STOML2). Follow-up validation would be required to determine whether or not these represent bona fide near neighbors for the splitPP1:TPRN complex.

In our approach, the important factor is that genuine hits are either unique to a specific dataset or represent a fractionally larger proportion of it. We did include a built-in negative control for our first runs (cells expressing the NTD-regulatory subunit alone), which identified a background of sticky abundant proteins and those that bind biotin directly (i.e., mitochondrial carboxylases). As these will appear in all datasets, our second runs were label-free and comprised co-expressed NTD-regulatory subunit and CTD-PP1 pairs. In both cases, direct comparison of different holoenzyme complex datasets reliably flagged unique and high-confidence near neighbors that were enriched above the shared background.

For all of the PP1 holophosphatases that we profiled using splitTurboID/MS, functional enrichment of biological processes and molecular functions was consistent with both their subcellular localization and previously published observations. As an example, a top hit in the splitPP1:NIPP1 interactome was SF3B1 (aka SAP155), a splicing factor previously identified as a substrate for this complex.39,40 It remains to be seen whether this is the only spliceosome component that is a direct substrate for PP1:NIPP1, with the other factors biotinylated as near neighbors in the complex. Other previously described NIPP1 binding partners not identified here (e.g., the mitotic kinase MELK and the histonemethyltransferase EZH2 that bind the same domain in NIPP1 that SF3B1 binds) may not have been captured due to cell cycle-specific associations (and our narrow 4 h labeling window). They were also not detected in a previous splitBirA∗-based screen.12 TPRN has not been studied in as much detail as NIPP1, however we did previously map the interactome (by IP/MS) of the shorter isoform 2 that lacks the C-terminal actin-binding domains and is predominantly nuclear.41 In that study, we validated the association of TPRNiso2 with the DNA damage response factors Ku70/80 under steady-state conditions and demonstrated the accumulation of the protein at sites of DNA damage. While the longer isoform 1 has a more obvious cytoplasmic pool, there is also a nuclear pool, and the PP1:TPRN proximal interactome identified biotinylation of Ku70/Ku80 by this complex. Further work will be required to determine whether or not the Ku heterotrimer is a substrate for PP1:TPRN or a near neighbor in a larger complex.

Although no specific substrates have so far been identified for PP1:RRP1B, our previous work demonstrated significant overlap of the IP/MS-based nuclear interactome of RRP1B with that of nucleolar PP1ɣ.15 In that study we annotated a subset of pre-60S ribosome biogenesis factors enriched in both datasets, based on an overlay of mammalian homologues on their yeast counterparts in a yeast-derived map of the complex (49 were found in both, representing 74% of the total). Our splitTurboID datasets identified 20 that are specific near neighbors to PP1:RRP1B. They include the PeBoW complex (PES1, BOP1, WDR12) that has been shown to be essential for cell proliferation and maturation of the large ribosomal subunit in mammalian cells.42,43 SplitPP1:RRP1B also selectively biotinylated CEBPZ (C/EBP family transcription factor), NOC2L (histone deacetylase inactivator), and NOC3L (DNA replication regulator), which are the mammalian homologues of the yeast NOC1, NOC2, and NOC3 proteins that are indispensable for the proper processing of rRNA. These are essential mammalian genes and, as in yeast, were recently shown to play a conserved role in rRNA processing and ribosomal maturation in Drosophila.44 GO analysis also suggests association with RNA processing and rRNA maturation factors in human cells. Identifying which of the pre-60S ribosome biogenesis factors may be substrates for the PP1:RRP1B is the next step in understanding the role(s) of the phosphatase in regulating this pathway.

Even less is known about PP1:NOM1, so this conditional interactome is a key first step. We were intrigued by the enrichment of ribosomal small subunit biogenesis factors, given that the subnucleolar localization of NOM1 shows significant overlap with that of RRP1B, yet they highlight different steps in the ribosome biogenesis pathway. Another top hit in the PP1:NOM1 proximal interactome was AATF (aka Che-1), a multifunctional protein originally identified as an RNA polymerase II interactor but more recently implicated in the regulation of rRNA synthesis and stress response through its association with RNA Polymerase I and the upstream binding factor (UBF).45 Future work will focus both on the identification of substrates and the impact of altered levels of NOM1 and RRP1B on various steps in ribosome biogenesis, from rRNA transcription and processing through ribosome subunit assembly and export.

The advantage of conditionally mapping dimeric PP1 complex interactomes over that of the individual catalytic and regulatory subunits on their own was highlighted here by our identification of c20orf27 as a protein with apparent PP1-dependent and -independent roles in the cell. Initial IP/MS screens confirmed co-precipitation of PP1 with c20orf27, but also highlighted the association of c20orf27 with APPBP2 (amyloid beta precursor protein binding protein 2), the human homolog of Drosophila PAT1. Originally linked to the microtubule trafficking of the amyloid precursor protein (APP) and androgen receptor transactivation,46,47 APPBP2 was shown more recently to act as a novel VHL-type Elongin BC-box CUL2 binding E3 ubiquitin ligase substrate specifier.48 Consistent with this, our interactome analyses also identified the enrichment of CUL2, the ElonginB/C proteins TCEB1/2, and the ubiquitin ligase UBR2 with c20orf27. The first substrate for the APPBP2-directed E3 ligase complex was identified as the PR domain-containing protein 16 (PRDM16), which plays a key role in the biogenesis of beige adipocytes.49 Targeted depletion of CUL2-APPBP2 in adipocytes activated the PRDM16 pathway to counteract diet-induced obesity and insulin resistance. Similarly, c20orf27 has been shown to be enriched in brown fat and a key regulator for white adipose tissue thermogenesis and glucose homeostasis.50 It was thus named adipose-secreted signaling protein (Adissp). Future work will be required to confirm whether or not its link to adipose biogenesis is via association with the APPBP2 E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. Although we did not identify this complex in the PP1:c20orf27 interactome, we cannot rule out the possibility that the holophosphatase still plays a role in this pathway, perhaps via the regulation of critical splicing events related to adipogenesis.

Although initially a surprising finding, biotinylation of splicing speckle-related proteins by PP1:c20orf27 made more sense when methanol fixation revealed a similar pattern for the apparently homogenously distributed nuclear pool of GFP-tagged c20orf27. Although we did not detect the significant enrichment of splicing factors in our original IP/MS-based c20orf27 analyses, several were found close to threshold in an MCF7 cell dataset. Although disregarded at the time, in light of the PP1:c20orf27 results, we will be taking a closer look at the potential role of the complex in splicing regulation. Of particular interest is the fact that PP1:c20orf27 and PP1:NIPP1 biotinylate distinct pools of proteins that are related to pre-mRNA splicing. The RNA-binding hnRNP proteins biotinylated by PP1:c20orf27 are splicing regulators that have a complex and varied relationship with this structure. While an immunostaining-based RNA binding protein database identified the accumulation of hnRNPF (in HeLa and HepG2 cells) and hnRNPU (in HepG2 cells) in speckles, they are generally found in the surrounding nucleoplasm.51 Recent studies have proposed a model in which RNA-binding SR proteins are enriched inside speckles while hnRNP proteins are excluded to the outside, allowing RNA molecules containing a splice site flanked by binding motifs for both to be positioned with the splice site straddling the speckle outer layer. This would provide better spatial overlap with spliceosomal components, which are found in this region.52,53 Although a causal relationship remains to be determined, facilitation of splicing through of pre-mRNA substrates by hnRNP and SR protein binding is an intriguing concept, and PP1 may play a regulatory role in this process through its association with both NIPP1 and c20orf27 in distinct holophosphatase complexes.

As noted, a caveat in any complementation assay, including splitTurboID, is that the fusion proteins must co-express well. We demonstrated how this can be further optimized for specific pairs by stably expressing one and delivering the other more efficiently by viral infection. Alternate approaches that could be utilized include lentiviral co-delivery using a bidirectional promoter, or a P2A/T2A or IRES-based bicistronic plasmid. If the cells do not tolerate the expression of one or both proteins in the longer term, an inducible system is also an option. We are continuing to optimize this step and anticipate a concomitant increase in sensitivity of detection.

A powerful extension of the splitTurboID approach is to monitor changes in the interactome of a specific PP1 complex. This could be in response to a specific cellular perturbation, as shown here, or under other conditions, such as specific cell cycle stages or during the switch from proliferation to differentiation. A limitation is the amount of labeling time required for splitTurboID, as the re-formed ligase is less active than full-length TurboID. In our hands, 4 h biotin labeling is sufficient for robust and reliable splitTurboID-based interactome mapping, which puts it on par with our miniTurboID experiments. If higher temporal resolution is required, an alternate approach may need to be considered. As an example, we overlap our 4 h splitTurboID cell perturbation experiments with 1 h ultraID experiments mapping the interactome of the regulatory subunit on its own at different stages within that time span. The conditional interactome provides the holophosphatase complex specificity, while the ultraID54 interactome provides higher temporal resolution and sensitivity of detection. Another potential caveat to bear in mind for all BioID experiments is a concern that was recently raised with respect to biotinylation in Arabidopsis, impacting protein ubiquitylation and turnover.55

Importantly, this splitTurboID approach is not limited to PP1 dimeric complexes, but can be applied to the conditional analysis of other protein-protein pairs. We demonstrate this here for HP1-TRIM28, but have also reconstituted splitTurboID activity with other pairs of chromatin-associated proteins, and with different combinations of the filamentous actin-binding peptide LifeAct with actin-binding proteins that are known to accumulate at specific actin structures. Moving forward, we are excited to continue to expand our use of this technique for the analysis of dynamic and contextual protein-protein complex interactome mapping.

Limitations of the study

Some limitations of our study should be considered. First, although our BioID results suggest PP1- and non-PP1-related roles for c20orf27, future work will be required to identify functionally relevant substrates within the PP1:c20orf27 interactome. Second, our identification and validation of the stable association of c20orf27 with the APPBP2-guided E3 ubiquitin ligase suggest, but cannot rule out, that c20orf27 is a substrate for this complex. Further analysis is required to better understand the cellular role(s) of c20orf27 in both the nucleus and cytoplasm, and how they may relate to its previously reported contribution to the regulation of adipogenesis.

Experimental model and study participant details

SplitTurboID AP/MS experiments were carried out in female-derived human embryonic kidney (HEK-293; RRID:CVCL_0045) cells. Additional imaging, IP/MS and Western blot experiments were carried out in female-derived osteosarcoma epithelial (U-2 OS; RRID: CVCL_A4CF) and human adenocarcinoma epithelial (HeLa; RRID:CVCL_0030) cells. Lentiviruses were prepared using SV40 T-antigen+ HEK-293T cells (RRID:CVCL_0063). Authenticated cell lines were purchased from ATCC, incubated in 5% CO2 at 37°C, and propagated in high glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagles’ medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum and 100 U/mL penicillin and streptomycin (Wisent Bioproducts Inc). Although this initial study utilized female-derived cells lines, splitTurboID is broadly applicable and future studies will compare conditional holophosphatase interactomes mapped in both male- and female-derived lines of similar origin in order to assess any sex-specific differences.

Method details

Plasmids and antibodies

To create in-house splitTurboID expression plasmids, the N-terminal aa2-194 and C-terminal aa195-320 domains of the TurboID biotin ligase, fused to a Flag or myc tag, respectively, were amplified from donor constructs (gift from Dr. Maxime Rousseaux) and subcloned into the backbones of pEGFP-C1 and N3 (Clontech), replacing EGFP. Coding sequences for the PP1 isoforms were amplified from previously described constructs8,57 while PP1 regulatory subunits were amplified from donor constructs obtained from the uOttawa Genome Editing and Molecular biology (GEM) Facility ORFeome collection. For lentiviral transduction, FlagNTD-RepoMan was subcloned into the pLVX-IRES-mCherry vector (Takara Bio). All expression constructs were confirmed by whole plasmid sequencing (Plasmidsaurus). Details for antibodies and other reagents utilized in this study can be found in the key resources table.

Cell transfection and transduction

For transient expression of splitTurboID fusion proteins, cells were transfected using either 1 mg/mL of polyethylenimine (Polysciences, Inc.) or Effectene transfection reagent (Qiagen). For lentiviral transduction, polybrene was added to enhance efficiency (8 μg/mL). Stable cell lines were generated by G418 antibiotic (Wisent Bioproducts Inc.) selection for random integration.58

Lentivirus was produced by transfecting HEK293T cells with a 1:1 ratio of liposomes to cell mix using PEI. The liposome solution consisted of shuttle vector plasmid (pLVX-NTD-RM-IRES-mCherry vector), psPAX2 packaging plasmid and pMD2G (codes for the broad range VSV-G envelope). Culture medium was changed 6–8 h post-transfection followed by supernatant collection on Days 2 and 3 post-transfection. A virus precipitation kit (Benchmark Bioscience) was used to concentrate the virus.

Metabolic labeling

Stable isotope labeling with amino acids in cell culture (SILAC) for label-based quantitative MS was carried out as previously described.24 For double encoding experiments, cells were grown for 7–10 passages in high glucose DMEM containing either L-arginine and L-lysine (Light), or the isotopes L-arginine13C/15N and L-lysine13C/15N (Heavy). SILAC media was prepared by supplementing high glucose DMEM minus Arg/Lys/Leu/Met (AthenaES) with 10% dialyzed FBS (ThermoFisher) and the appropriate amino acids, mixing well and filtering through a 0.22 μm filter (Millipore).

Fluorescence microscopy

Cells seeded on No. 1.5 glass coverslips or μ-Slide 8 Well chamber slides (Ibidi) were fixed for 10 min at room temperature in 3.7% (wt/vol) paraformaldehyde (PFA) in CSK buffer (1 mM PIPES pH 6.8, 100 mM NaCl, 300 mM sucrose, 3 mM MgCl2, 2 mM EDTA). Following a 10 min permeabilization with 1% Triton X-100 in PBS, nonspecific binding sites were blocked by incubation in PBS containing 0.2% Tween 20 and 1% BSA. In some experiments, cells were fixed for 5 min in ice-cold methanol. For immunostaining, cells were incubated with the appropriate primary antibody, followed by incubation with the appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody. Biotinylated proteins were stained by incubating cells with Alexa Fluor 488, 568 or 647-conjugated streptavidin (ThermoFisher). DNA was stained with Hoechst 33342 and coverslips were prepared for imaging by mounting in Vectashield (Vector Labs) or ProLong Gold (Thermofisher) liquid mounting media.

Images were acquired using GE DeltaVision restoration deconvolution-based widefield fluorescence systems (CORE and Elite) and 60x/NA 1.4, 40x/NA 1.4 or 40x/NA1.25 PlanApochromat oil immersion objectives (Olympus). The CORE is fitted with a CoolSNAP charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (Roper Scientific) and the Elite with a Complementary Metal-Oxide Semiconductor (CMOS) camera (Scientific). Both systems run SoftWorX acquisition and deconvolution software (GE Healthcare) and all images are single deconvolved optical sections.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays

For Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BiFC) assays, PP1ɣ fused to the N-terminal half of YFP (aa 1–154) was co-transfected in HEK293 cells with c20orf27 (wild type or KAGA mutant) fused to the C-terminal half of YFP (aa 155–238). DNA was stained with Hoechst33342 and reconstituted EYFP fluorescence assessed by live imaging.

Fluorescence two-hybrid (F2H) screens

For the F2H assays, a plasmid expressing mCherry-NLS-LacR-PP1ɣ was co-transfected with plasmids expressing GFP-tagged c20orf27 (wild type or KAGA mutant) in a HeLa cell line that contains an integrated lacO array.59 Negative controls included co-expression of mCherry-NLS-LacR-PP1ɣ with free GFP, and co-expression of GFP-tagged regulatory proteins with mCherry-NLS-LacR. DNA was stained with Hoechst33342 and cells imaged live to monitor co-localization of mCherry and GFP signals.

Preparation of cell extracts

Whole cell extracts for AP/MS and western blot analysis were prepared by sonication in ice-cold high salt RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% deoxycholate, protease inhibitors) and cleared by centrifuging at 21100g for 10 min at 4 °C. For the experiments shown here, 1 × 15 cm dish of cells was harvested for each condition.

Western blot analyses

Protein lysates were resolved by electrophoresis on a NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris gradient gel (Thermo Fisher) and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Following staining with Ponceau S to confirm efficient transfer and equivalent loading, membranes were incubated for 18 h with PBS containing 0.5% Tween and 5% milk to block nonspecific binding sites. Primary and secondary antibodies were prepared in the same blocking buffer for sequential 1 h incubation steps, with PBS washes in between. For streptavidin detection of biotinylated proteins, membranes were blocked with PBS containing 0.5% Tween and 1% BSA and incubated for 1 h with Streptavidin-HRP (ThermoFisher) prepared in this same blocking buffer. Protein bands were detected using ECL Plus chemiluminescent substrate (ThermoFisher) and images captured on a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Affinity purification

For affinity purification of biotinylated proteins, the salt concentration in the lysates was first reduced to 250 mM by adding an equal volume of 0 NaCl RIPA buffer. Equal amounts of total protein extract for each condition were pre-cleared for 10 min on protein G Sepharose beads (Cytiva), followed by incubation with Streptavidin-agarose beads (ThermoFisher) at 4°C for 4 h. The lysates were removed and the beads washed 4× with 250 mM NaCl RIPA buffer. For the SILAC AP/MS experiments, beads from the control and experimental pulldowns were first washed separately with 250 mM NaCl RIPA buffer, then combined for the last 3 washes. Proteins were eluted with first a bead-equivalent volume of 2% SDS/30 mM biotin and then a 4× volume of ddH2O. The volume of the eluate was reduced by SpeedVac at 60°C and the proteins reduced and alkylated by treatment with DTT and iodoacetamide, respectively. LDS sample buffer was added to the reduced/alkylated eluants and the proteins resolved by electrophoresis halfway down a NuPAGE 10% BisTris mini gel (Thermo Fisher). The gel was stained using SimplyBlue Safestain (Thermo Fisher) and the entire lane cut into five slices. Each slice was cut into 2 × 2 mm fragments, destained, and digested overnight at 30°C with Trypsin Gold (ThermoFisher).

For affinity purification of GFP-c20orf27 vs. GFP alone, transiently transfected HeLa cells were harvested by trypsinization and fractionated into cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions. Briefly, cells were incubated for 5 min in ice-cold swelling buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.9, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM KCl, 0.5 mM DTT, protease inhibitors), broken open using a Dounce homogenizer (20 strokes with a tight pestle) and centrifuged at 228xg for 5 min at 4°C to pellet nuclei. The supernatant was retained as the cytoplasmic fraction and adjusted to 1× ice-cold RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% deoxycholate, protease inhibitors) by adding the required amount of 5× buffer. The nuclear pellet was resuspended in a sucrose buffer (0.25 M sucrose/10 mM MgCl2), layered on a sucrose cushion (0.88 M sucrose/0.5 mM MgCl2) and centrifuged at 2,800xg for 10 min at 4°C. The cleaner nuclear pellet was resuspended in ice-cold RIPA buffer. The cytoplasmic and nuclear lysates were cleared by centrifuging at 21,100xg for 10 min at 4°C and incubated with GFP-Trap Agarose beads for 1 h at 4°C. The lysates were removed and the beads washed 4 times with 250 mM NaCl RIPA buffer. The beads were washed as described above, albeit with 150 mM RIPA buffer, and proteins eluted with a bead-equivalent volume of 2% SDS and 4× volume of ddH2O. All subsequent steps were identical to the splitTurboID workflow.

Mass spectrometry and data analysis

Tryptic digests were analyzed by LC-MS/MS on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos system (Thermo Scientific) coupled to a Dionex UltiMate 3000 RSLC nano HPLC. The raw files were searched against the UniProt human database using MaxQuant software v2.1.4.0.60 The following criteria were used: peptide tolerance = 10 ppm, trypsin as the enzyme (2 missed cleavages allowed) and carboxyamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification. Variable modifications were oxidation of methionine and N-terminal acetylation, and heavy SILAC labels were Arg10 and Lys8. Minimum ratio count was 2 and quantitation was based on razor and unique peptides. Peptide and protein FDR was 0.01.

Custom-made Python scripts were designed to analyze the overlap of splitTurboID datasets (label-free or SILAC). All scripts, plus a readme.txt file detailing the requirements and general procedures and concepts underlying each Python script, were deposited on Mendeley Data (Mendeley Data: https://doi.org/10.17632/xxrcfxwd35.1). Briefly, the Sifter script opens a proteinGroups.txt file from the MaxQuant output and generates a simplified.csv file by discarding flagged contaminants, proteins identified in the reverse dataset and proteins with no quantified intensity values. The Overlapper script opens multiple “Sifted” files and generates a comparison of the distribution of identified proteins across them. Lastly, the Plotter script opens the summary file from the Overlapper script and generates a 3D graph of the proportional distribution of identified proteins across the datasets. Each Python script is provided as two versions, one that can be used to analyze non-labeled datasets and another that can be used to analyze SILAC datasets (Arg0/Lys0 vs. Arg10/Lys8 labeling). It is important to note that the Sifter script needs to be adjusted manually to fit specific needs and file formats (e.g., if using a different version of MaxQuant or different isotopic amino acids). The Overlapper can compare up to 3 datasets and will generate a .csv file containing a summary of the distribution of the identified proteins across those datasets. The Plotter can also be customized to highlight specific protein groups. The scripts can be run using a browser-based compiler such as cliprun (http://cliprun.com), and instructions are provided in the readme.txt file. For more detailed optimization of the 3D graphs generated by the Plotter script, a downloadable Python compiler such as PyCharm (JetBrains) is recommended. To compare unique and enriched hits identified in experimental replicates, we used the online interactive Venny tool (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/index.html).

Functionally grouped biological process networks to assess high-confidence hits identified in the splitTurboID experiments were generated using ClueGo: Functional Analysis (Cytoscape 3.10.2; ClueGo v2.5.10). Accession IDs were input, followed by selection for Homo Sapiens and Gene Ontology Pathway Biological Process (BiologicalProcess-EBI-UniProt-GOA-ACAP-ARAP_25.05.2022). GO Term Fusion and pathways with pV ≤ 0.05 were selected. A Bonferroni step down method was used with Min GO Level = 3 and Max GO Level = 6. For GO Term/Pathway Selection, Number of Genes = 2 with Min. Percentage = 1.0. The GO Term/Pathway Network Connectivity (Kappa Score) was set at 0.4. Initial markers not found in selected annotations were also selected. Network Layout was Prefuse Force Directed Layout.

To generate the GO molecular function and biological processes bubble graphs from the lists of high-confidence hits (unique and enriched), we used the online PANTHER resource (https://pantherdb.org/) and a customized makeGO Python script from Lucas Servi (https://github.com/Lucas-Servi/makeGO.git). The test type for statistical analysis was Fisher’s exact and the FDR threshold was 0.05. Only the top 7 GO terms are shown for each dataset.
