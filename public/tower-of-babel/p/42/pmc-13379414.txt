Immigrant Fertility in Canada 2001–2021: A Brief Report
Authors: Yue Teng, Rachel Margolis, Yoko Yoshida, Howard Ramos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCanada has low fertility and relies on immigration to support the labor force and population growth. However, if recent waves of immigrants have the same or lower rates of fertility than native-born Canadians, then immigration becomes a demographic revolving door rather than long-term solution for the country’s population pressures. This paper provides estimates for immigrant fertility in Canada 2001–2021, examining heterogeneity by age at migration, immigration streams, and country of origin. We use the long-form Canadian census (2001, 2006, 2016, 2021) and the National Household Survey (2011). Using the “Own Infant Method”, we estimate total fertility rates for different population subgroups for the period prior to each census. Immigrants to Canada have higher fertility than native-born Canadians, but the gap is narrowing, from 0.24 births in 2001 to 0.11 in 2021. Within immigrant categories, there is great variation. First-generation immigrants have the highest TFR, while 1.5-generation immigrants have even lower fertility than both first-generation immigrants and those who are native-born. Among immigration streams, family stream immigrants have the highest fertility, followed by refugees, and then economic immigrants. Fertility rates of immigrants from almost all of the top ten source countries declined substantially between 2001 and 2021, with most being below replacement level in Canada and much lower than the fertility in the source country. These findings are essential for policymakers as they debate the right levels of immigration, and which immigration streams and source countries best promote long-term population growth.Keywords: Immigration policy, Low fertility, Census, Population decline, Immigrant fertility

Introduction and Background

As a country with very low fertility, Canada relies on immigration to support the labor force and population growth (Dion et al., 2015; Doyle et al., 2025; Hiebert, 2025). About a quarter of the country’s population are immigrants according to the 2021 Census (Statistics Canada, ). If immigrants have the same or lower rates of fertility than native-born Canadians, then immigration becomes a demographic revolving door rather than long-term solution for the country’s population pressures. Immigrants to Canada have historically had higher fertility than the native-born population, but this finding is based on research from the late 1990s (Statistics Canada, 2003). Despite the rapid expansion of immigration to Canada, studies on the fertility patterns of immigrants have not kept pace. This paper estimates immigrant fertility trends in Canada over the past two decades, exploring variation by age at migration, immigration streams, and country of origin to offer a look at the heterogeneity within the immigrant population. These findings are essential for policymakers as they debate the right level of permanent and temporary residents to admit and which immigration streams best promote long-term population goals.

Often missed in the research on immigrant fertility is that immigrants are a heterogenous group, with important implications. For example, socialization in birth countries, which is captured by age at the time of migration, affects the social norms people adopt during their formative years and in turn influences fertility (Baek et al., 2025; Kulu et al., 2019). Those who migrate as children are more likely to adapt to the destination-country ideals about family size, gender roles, and the timing of parenthood, while those who migrate as adults are more likely to bring origin-country norms to their fertility behavior (Adsera & Ferrer, 2013; González-Ferrer et al., 2017).

A second source of heterogeneity is the class of immigration stream through which migrants come to Canada. Immigrants arrive with different motivations for migration and different socioeconomic characteristics, both of which shape fertility behavior. The main immigration classes are economic stream, family reunification, and refugees. Although the annual intake of immigrants to Canada has fluctuated somewhat, since the 2000’s, about 60% are economic immigrants, 25–30% are family stream immigrants, and 10–15% are refugees or protected persons (Statistics Canada, 2016). Economic immigrants are workers selected for their ability to contribute to Canada’s economy. Selections are made based on their education, work experience, language skills, and occupation to address labor demands. This group of immigrants may have very low fertility because they aim to secure employment and financial stability, which may lead to fertility postponement. Female economic immigrants also likely have higher educational attainment and career focus compared to other immigrants, both of which are associated with a high opportunity cost and may result in low fertility. Research in other countries has shown that economic migrants have quite low fertility. For example, in Sweden, economic migrants have fewer children than family migrants (Carlsson, 2025), and in Norway economic migrants have low fertility, similar to that of the native-born (Tønnessen & Wilson, 2023).

Family stream immigrants, in contrast, largely consist of those who migrate for spousal reunification, but also include the sponsorship of parents and grandparents as well as children who did not accompany the principal applicant parent. Immigrants coming to Canada through this admission stream may have higher fertility than economic migrants since they already have a partner and may be more focused on settling down. Research from Norway (Tønnessen & Mussino, 2020), the Netherlands, and Poland (Mondal et al., 2025) found that immigrants who migrated for marriage are more likely to have children, especially in the period immediately following migration. Whether this is the case in Canada is unknown.

Refugees are the third category of immigrants. Compared to economic and family stream immigrants, refugees arriving in Canada generally have lower levels of formal education and official language proficiency compared with economic and family stream immigrants (Picot et al., 2019). They also come from different countries than immigrants from the economic and family streams. For example, in 2025, the top source countries for the economic and family streams were India, the Philippines, and China, while many refugee and protected-person admissions were from Eritrea, Afghanistan, and Syria reflecting global conflicts and humanitarian crises (IRCC, 2025). This category of immigrants may have higher fertility rates than other migrant groups, which could be attributed to higher fertility in their country of origin, lower levels of education, or limited labor market attachment. Refugees in Norway and Sweden have stable, high fertility after migration (Carlsson, 2025; Tønnessen & Wilson, 2023). It is also possible that refugees’ fertility may decline in Canada after migration because of life disruption, trauma, or the high cost of living.

In the years after the COVID-19 pandemic began, Canda admitted high numbers of temporary residents, foreign nationals that were permitted to stay in Canada for a limited period of time. The majority of temporary migrants are international students, but temporary foreign workers are also part of this group. Temporary residents are a smaller component of the population than the three major immigration streams, comprising 2.5% of recent newcomers in 2021 (Statistics Canada, 2023). They may have lower fertility due to their uncertain temporary status, distance from a partner, or because their permit is tied to work or study, both of which are hard to combine with childbearing. To our knowledge, no study has estimated the fertility of temporary residents. Understanding immigrant fertility by immigration stream could provide more insights into the heterogeneity of immigrants in Canada, as well as the indirect impacts of immigration policy on demographic change.

A third important source of variation within the immigrant population is migrants’ country of origin. Immigrant source countries to Canada have changed dramatically since the early 2000s. The 2001 census reported that the top three source countries for immigrants in Canada were the United Kingdom (11%), China (6%), and Italy (6%) (Statistics Canada, 2005). By 2021, the top three source countries shifted to India (10.7%), Philippines (8.6%), and China (8.6%) (Statistics Canada, 2022a, 2022b). They are all countries that currently have low fertility, potentially shaping the fertility behavior of those who immigrate to Canada. Examining the differences in fertility between the country of origin and migrants from those countries to Canada allows us to more accurately estimate the potential for population change in Canada and to better understand which immigrant source countries are most likely to contribute to future population growth. Previous research on this topic in Canada is outdated and relies on regional categorization of countries (Adsera & Ferrer, 2013; Ng & Nault, 1997), obscuring the huge variation in fertility rates within regions. More recent research has estimated the percentage of births in Canada to foreign-born mothers (42% in 2024), but not the fertility rate of these groups per se (Provencher, 2025).

This manuscript addresses two questions. First, how has immigrant fertility in Canada changed over the last two decades? Second, how does immigrant fertility vary by age at the time of migration, immigration stream, and source countries?

Data and Methods

Our analysis uses the long-form Canadian census micro-files from 2001, 2006, 2016, 2021 and the National Household Survey 2011. The long-form Canadian census contains a 25% sample in 2016 and 2021 and a 20% sample in 2006 and 2001. The National Household Survey collected comparable data in 2011, but because it was a voluntary survey rather than a mandatory census, it had a lower weighted response rate (77.2%) than in the prior census (93.8%) (Statistics Canada, 2015).

The long-form Canadian census is ideal for reconstructing fertility rates because of the large nationally representative sample and detailed information on households, immigration status, immigration admission stream, and country of birth. The information on relationships within households and census families allow us to link children to their mothers and calculating fertility rates by maternal age and characteristics. Moreover, the census includes newcomers at a similar rate of the native-born population. This is because immigrants have very high housing occupancy (Statistics Canada, 2022b, 2025; Zhang & Hou, 2025).

We use the “Own Infant Method” to estimate fertility rates for different subgroups for the period prior to each census (Timæus, 2021). To link infants (age 0–1) to mothers (age 15–49), we examine the census family. The census family is defined as one couple with or without children and single parent with children. To avoid double counting the infants in the census family of same-sex couples, we identify infants with two mothers and assign the infant to the younger mother in the family. The matched children age 0–1 in the census family exclude the children living with grandparents with no parent(s) present, children living only with their father(s), and children not in the census family. After linking infants to their mothers, we calculate age-specific fertility rates using the weighted numbers of matched children age 0–1 divided by the weighted numbers of women age 15–49. The age-specific fertility rates are then used to calculate the total fertility rate. Population weights are applied across all analysis.

We begin by comparing the fertility of native-born Canadians to immigrants. We then examine three subgroups of immigrants. First, we examine first-generation immigrants, who are foreign born and immigrated to Canada after age 15, and compare them to 1.5-generation immigrants, who migrated at or before age 15. This measurement choice aligns with Statistics Canada’s definition of first-generation and 1.5-generation immigrants as well as international research capturing the period of mid-adolescence, when peer networks, educational trajectories, and identity formation become influential in shaping values on family and gender roles (Cebulko, 2014; Krapf & Wolf, 2015; Mikolai et al., 2025). Second, we examine fertility rates by immigration stream for the 2016 and 2021 census years when this variable is available. There are three major categories of immigrants: economic migrants, family stream migrants, and refugees. We also consider temporary residents of Canada defined as foreign nationals whose usual place of residence is Canada and who has a work or study permit or who has claimed refugee status. Third, we estimate fertility rates for the top ten source countries for immigrant women ages (15–49) for census years using the “place of birth” variable in the census. Then we compare the total fertility rate (TFR) of immigrant women in Canada from each source country with the fertility rate of women in their birth country for the same year using data from the United Nations (United Nations, 2024). Results

Canadian fertility was 1.44 in 2001, increased to 1.60 in 2011, and then declined to 1.31 in 2021 (Fig. 1). Over these two decades, immigrant fertility has been consistently higher than native-born fertility. Immigrant TFR was 1.68 in 2001, stayed steady between 1.65 and 1.70 through 2016, and then declined to 1.42 in 2021. Comparing immigrant fertility to that of the native-born shows a trend towards convergence, as the immigrant fertility decline outpaces the one among native-born Canadians. The TFR gaps between these groups narrowed from 0.24 births in 2001 to 0.11 births in 2021.

Figure 2 examines immigrant fertility by age at migration. First-generation immigrants, who moved to Canada after age 15, had a total fertility rate that hovered around 2.0 between 2001 and 2016 and then declined to 1.75 in 2021. In contrast, 1.5-generation immigrants who moved to Canada before age 15, have fertility rates that are much lower than first-generation immigrants, and even lower than native-born Canadians. The TFRs for 1.5 generation immigrants were between 1.36 and 1.46 for the period 2001 to 2016 and 1.25 in 2021.

The country from which the most women immigrated is shown on the top, and the tenth country of immigration is shown at the bottom. We highlight three findings. First, in 2021, immigrant women from all top ten immigration countries have below replacement fertility in Canada. The estimates range from the extremely low values of 0.75 for Iranian women and 0.92 for Chinese women to a high of 1.7 for Pakistani women, while immigrants from other countries had TFRs between 1.1 and 1.4. Second, fertility rates of immigrants from many of the top ten countries of origin have declined substantially between 2001 and 2021. The primary countries of origin of recent immigrants in Canada, such as China, the Philippines and India, are either experiencing or have already accomplished demographic transitions.

Third, almost all immigrant women from the top ten source countries had lower fertility in Canada relative to the TFR of women in their country of origin (see numbers in parentheses in Table 2). The differences are particularly large for immigrants from India and the Philippines between 2001 and 2021, as immigrant women from these countries have TFRs in Canada that were more than 50% lower than in their countries of origin. Additionally, immigrant women from Pakistan (2006–2021) and Iran (2011–2021) have TFR more than 50% or even 100% lower than in their source countries.

Discussion and Conclusion

Immigrants to Canada have had higher fertility than native-born Canadians over the past two decades, but the gap has decreased substantially. In 2001, the fertility rate of immigrants in Canada was 0.24 births higher than that of non-immigrants, but by 2021, the fertility of both groups declined, and the difference decreased to 0.11 births. In comparison, previous research from 1976 to 1981 showed a gap in TFR of 0.39 between immigrant and native-born women (Bélanger & Gilbert, 2002). This represents a nearly 72% decrease in the fertility gap between immigrants and non-immigrants between 1976 to 2021, indicating a decline in immigrant fertility in Canada and a convergence of fertility rates between the two groups. One important factor behind this convergence is that the major source countries of recent immigrants to Canada have also experienced declining fertility and now have relatively low fertility levels, reflecting the broader global pattern of steep declines in fertility (Peeples, 2025; Vandermotten & Dessouroux, 2024).

We find that first-generation immigrants who move to Canada after age 15, have substantially higher fertility than the native-born population (about half a birth higher), but convergence happens rapidly. Those who move to Canada as children, the 1.5 generation of immigrants, have even lower fertility than the native-born population. These patterns align with immigrant generation gradients in fertility. First-generation immigrants’ fertility behaviours may be shaped by socialization and family norms from their countries of origin, whereas those who migrate as children may be more strongly influenced by Canadian education and cultural contexts, including norms around family formation and gender roles (Adsera & Ferrer, 2013; González-Ferrer et al., 2017). Research on the children of immigrants also shows that younger immigrants tend to socially integrate more than those who arrive at later ages (Karpinski et al., 2024). Further, research from the Netherlands shows clear intergenerational gaps in fertility preferences, with children preferring smaller families than their immigrant parents (De Valk, 2013). U.S. evidence similarly finds that second-generation women who retain strong ties to high-fertility cultures are more likely to have more children, whereas those who are more acculturated adopt the lower fertility norms of the host society (Salari, 2018).

Immigrant fertility varies widely among immigrants from different immigration streams. Economic immigrants, half of Canada’s immigration flow, have the lowest fertility in Canada among all permanent immigrant groups and family stream immigrants have the highest fertility. Economic migrants have high aspirations of economic mobility, and many of them might prioritize work and career advancement. The high fertility rates of family stream immigrants are likely due to spousal reunifications and the fact that marriage and childbearing are interrelated goals for migration (Andersson, 2004; Kulu, 2005; Mondal et al., 2025; Tønnessen & Mussino, 2020). Refugees have the second highest fertility among immigrant groups in Canada at 1.77 in 2021. This could be because many refugees come from countries with strong norms and expectations for large families and having children is central to their culture, religion, and identity (Hawkey et al., 2018). Another possibility is that refugees experience conflict, loss, and insecurity, and having children is a way to rebuild families and establish a new life. In contrast, temporary residents have lowest fertility among all immigration categories. The instability associated with their non-permanent status may negatively impact their reproductive plans.

Immigrants from nearly all of Canada’s top ten countries of origin have had below-replacement fertility over the past two decades. This is a big shift from the situation in 1980s and 1990s, when new immigrants from some regions such as South Asia, Middle East, and Africa had above replacement rate fertility in Canada (Ng & Nault, 1997; Statistics Canada, 2003). This shift is likely due to the fact that many immigrant source countries now have very low fertility (Neyer, 2013) and also that there is positive selection of immigrants by socioeconomic characteristics, resulting in the lower fertility of immigrants in Canada compared to their country of origin.

We note five caveats. First, our estimated total fertility rates refer to the year prior to the census date (mid-May to mid-May), not the calendar year. Second, the analysis does not include infants who were born in the year before the census but died before the census date. Because infant mortality rates are very low in Canada at 5.2, 4.9, and 4.3 per 1000 births in 2001, 2011, and 2021, respectively (Statistics Canada, 2024), the estimates of TFR with the own-children method are little affected by this omission. Third, the small number of infants (3%) that could not be matched to their mothers may introduce some measurement error (see Table 3). Unmatched children cannot be included in TFR calculations because we do not know the mother’s age, or immigration status. However, the low rate of unmatched children is not likely to bias TFR estimates. Fourth, fertility estimates for 2021 may be affected by the COVID-19 pandemic, as almost one quarter of those age 15–49 reported changing their fertility behavior (Fostik & Galbraith, 2021; Provencher & Galbraith, 2024). Last, we do not examine how fertility may be postponed or sped up through the migration process, but this can be examined in future work.

The findings presented in this paper show that Canada’s future will likely see even smaller gaps between the fertility of immigrants and native-born Canadians. Keeping a consistent flow of new immigrants is important for coping with the aging population in Canada, and contributing to the economy, but is not a sustainable solution to long term demographic pressures. That is, immigration offers a short-term solution to population loss, but does not create population replacement.
