ChEA-KG and ChEA-KG-TS: a network-based transcription factor enrichment analysis tool with an accompanying time-series workflow
Authors: Anna I Byrd, John Erol Evangelista, Andrew Van Dusen, Daniel J B Clarke, Maha Berrada, Sherry L Jenkins, Avi Ma’ayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTranscription factor (TF) modules interact to regulate key biological processes and cell-state transitions in normal physiology and disease. Understanding these modules and how they evolve over time can be accomplished by constructing gene regulatory networks (GRNs). To identify context-specific TF subnetworks, we developed ChEA-KG, which generates enriched TF regulatory subnetworks for input gene sets. ChEA-KG is based on a GRN connecting 1559 human TFs via 131 181 signed and directed edges inferred from diverse published ChIP-seq (chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing) and mRNA (messenger RNA)-sequencing experiments. We demonstrate ChEA-KG’s utility by applying it to uncover master regulators of aging, mechanisms of action (MoA) for drug classes, pan-cancer subtypes, and cell types from across 14 major human tissues. Next, we extend ChEA-KG to develop the webserver application ChEA-KG Time Series (ChEA-KG-TS), which identifies TF modules from time-series mRNA-sequencing datasets. Results from this workflow are automatically summarized as reports that include enrichment analysis, regulatory subnetworks, and UMAP projections of enriched TFs. We use ChEA-KG-TS to explain transient responses in two use cases. ChEA-KG and ChEA-KG-TS are available from https://chea-kg.maayanlab.cloud/ and https://chea-kg-ts.maayanlab.cloud/.

Introduction

Unravelling the regulatory mechanisms underlying genome-wide gene expression has been a major goal of experimental and computational biologists for decades. Transcription factors (TFs) are key regulators of gene expression. TF activity directly and indirectly responds to extracellular and intracellular cues to determine the behavior of the cell by binding and unbinding to specific DNA sequences to increase (upregulate) or decrease (downregulate) the production of mRNA (messenger RNA) transcripts. TFs regulate the expression of other TFs forming an intricate and complex regulatory network commonly termed gene regulatory network (GRN) [1, 2]. Understanding the connectivity and dynamics of GRNs is critical to explaining disease mechanisms and other key intracellular physiological processes such as development and cellular differentiation. Time-series mRNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) studies are critical to unraveling gene regulatory mechanisms because they enable the characterization of transient cellular responses not captured by a single point snapshot of a unique cellular state. Time-series experiments are conducted by taking repeated measurements at defined time intervals, and performing differential gene expression by comparing consecutive time points or comparing each time point to the initial state (or in other words to “time point 0″) [3]. Such analyses identify gene sets that are up- and downregulated over time. Examples of such studies include characterizing the molecular effects of the oxidative phosphorylation inhibitor atovaquone on epithelial ovarian cancer [4], comparing enriched pathways in developing kidney organoids treated with and without PI3K inhibitors [5], and establishing a relationship between transcriptomics profiles and lifespan in the short-lived vertebrate Nothobranchius furzeri [6].

GRNs can be represented in silico as directed and signed graphs with nodes representing TFs and edges representing their regulatory relationships. To infer these associations, experimental data that measure TF activity directly or indirectly can inform computational methods. For example, various experimental methods have been developed to identify the binding sites of TFs on the DNA. Such experimental techniques include chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) [7] or microarray (ChIP-chip) [8]; open chromatin assays, including DNase sequencing (DNase-seq) [9], formaldehyde-assisted identification of regulatory elements followed by sequencing (FAIRE-seq) [10], and DNase footprinting [11]; and chromatin interaction assays such as chromatin interaction analysis with paired end tag sequencing (ChIA-PET) [12, 13]. Aggregating the results from many studies that utilized these assays can be used to reconstruct GRNs [14–17]. However, typically such GRNs only have a subset of the entire repertoire of TFs and contain directed but unsigned edges. Alternative and complementary computational methods to reconstruct GRNs include text-mining, PWM (position weight matrices) analysis [18], and TF–gene co-expression analysis [19]. However, these methods may suffer from literature biases, lack of specificity, and reliance on indirect evidence. Regardless of the advantages and disadvantages of the methods used to reconstruct GRNs, once these GRNs are formed, they can be analyzed for their topological features such as network motifs [20–22], hub analysis [23], positive and negative feedback loops [24], and other topological features [25, 26].

Many tools have been developed to use GRNs to predict TF-regulatory modules for datasets with both stand-alone and serially collected datasets. A prevalent method to analyze RNA-seq data is TF enrichment analysis (TFEA) [14, 15, 27]. Several web server and command line tools are available for performing TFEA. For example, GREAT (Genomic Regions Enrichment of Annotations Tool) [27] takes as input genomics locations to predict enriched pathway and biological processes based on knowledge about the binding sites of TFs in the nearby regions. Other tools such as oPOSSUM [28], Pscan [29], HOMER [30], and i-cisTarget [31] rely only on PWMs to infer the most likely TFs given a set of genes or a file with genomics regions annotations. Several other tools developed to perform TFEA are available offering various features and input types (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1). TF-focused time-series analysis tools include the application of Dynamic Bayesian Networks (DBNs), which produce regulatory networks using genes commonly differentially expressed across all time points [32]. The DREM (Dynamic Regulatory Events Miner) method applies an input–output hidden Markov model (IOHMM) to predict likely TF regulators at points where expression of a subset of genes begins to diverge from the rest [33]. DREM 2.0 [34] is an improved implementation of DREM that also provides static protein–DNA binding datasets for use in the analysis. Interactive DREM (iDREM) [35] supports multi-omic analysis and provides interactive visualizations; whereas the Signaling and Dynamic Regulatory Events Miner (SDREM) [36] integrates transcription regulatory networks with signaling pathways. More recently, time-series miner (TSMiner) [37] has adapted the IOHMM method established by DREM to predict time-point-specific activators and repressors. TSMiner introduces pathway gene sets as an additional required input. DREM was recently integrated into NetSeekR [38], a network analysis pipeline, demonstrating its continued relevancy. These and other tools are described in Table 2. However, like most TFEA applications, these tools often suffer from limited TF coverage or require that users upload their own TF–gene interaction data. For example, the human TF–target interactions provided by DREM 2.0 cover only 127 TFs for the ChIP-seq dataset and 349 TFs in the computational predictions option [34]. This is a relatively small fraction of the ∼1600 known and suspected human TF genes [39].

To increase the coverage of existing regulatory analysis tools, we previously developed ChIP-x enrichment analysis 3 (ChEA3) [14], which expands on ChEA [15] to aggregate known target gene sets for 1632 human TFs from six primary libraries. These libraries are produced by processing published datasets from ChIP-chip, ChIP-seq, and RNA-seq (co-expression) experiments, as well as co-occurence of genes in gene sets submitted for analysis with Enrichr [40]. For each input gene set, enrichment analysis with a proportion test is performed on each of these libraries. The output is a ranking of all TFs based on the MeanRank method, in which the mean rank score of each TF across all libraries containing that TF is used to re-rank all TFs. Top-ranked TFs are therefore the most likely to be regulating the input gene set. This method was shown to have the highest predictive performance and highest coverage compared to other similar tools such as DoRothEA [41], BART [42], TFEA.ChIP [43], MAGICACT [44], and VIPER [45]. In this way, ChEA3 can provide accurate, full-coverage TFEA that is informed by direct experimental observations.

Here, we introduce a different method to reconstruct the human TF GRN and extend it to create a network-based version of ChEA3 called ChEA-KG. By combining TFEA with ChEA3 [14] with a massive collection of differentially expressed gene sets created from the RummaGEO database [46], we infer the most likely regulatory relationships between all human TFs. We devise two filtering schemes to identify the most significant relationships and then benchmark the filtered networks against TF–target libraries constructed from TRRUST [47, 48] and TRANSFAC [49]. The inferred GRN is stored in a knowledge graph (KG) database and served via a web server application called ChEA-KG. ChEA-KG is an interactive TFEA application that returns subnetworks of the connected top enriched TFs based on the topology of the reconstructed GRN. ChEA-KG also provides interactive network visualization and supports querying for relationships within the full GRN. We apply ChEA-KG to create four atlases featuring curated collections of TF regulatory subnetworks predicted to control marker gene sets from 131 human cell types, 69 cancer subtypes from 10 cancer types, 30 major mechanisms of actions (MoAs), and 24 aging human tissues. We provide a use case analyzing the aging tissue networks to identify global TF modules that are master regulators of aging.

Additionally, we combine the full-coverage, network-based TFEA introduced by ChEA-KG with time-series transcriptomics analysis. The ChEA-KG time-series (ChEA-KG-TS) workflow and web server application extends the ChEA-KG enrichment analysis workflow to time-series RNA-seq datasets. CHEA-KG-TS identifies TF–TF networks that regulate temporal changes in gene expression. ChEA-KG-TS improves on existing tools by covering all known human TFs, offering the workflow as a web server application with minimal required input, and facilitating accessibility and reproducibility by producing persistent online reports that can be shared and published. The ChEA-KG-TS workflow first computes differentially expressed gene sets from an input time-series RNA-seq data matrix and sample metadata files. Alternatively, users can provide a gene-set library that contains the precomputed differentially expressed genes across the time points. It then computes TFEA for each gene set with ChEA3 and ChEA-KG to produce reports that contain tables, bar charts, network diagrams, and UMAP projections of the results that demonstrate how TF modules regulate each other over time. To demonstrate the utility of ChEA-KG-TS in a variety of contexts, we applied it to four use cases that span various time intervals.

Materials and methods

Aggregating and testing the ChEA-KG GRN

Construction of the TF–TF GRN with TFEA

The GRN construction method assumes that the up- or downregulation of genes resulting from a single perturbation are controlled by TFs that directly regulate the transcription of those genes. These upstream TFs can be inferred by submitting many differentially expressed up- and downregulated gene sets for TFEA with ChEA3 [14]. Differentially expressed gene sets were downloaded from RummaGEO [46] on August 22, 2024. Gene sets were kept if they had at least five genes. Only studies with clear control and perturbation groups were included to ensure that differential expression resulted in a clear direction of transcription factor activity. These studies were identified using regular expressions for terms that describe the experimental conditions, for example, “ctrl” and “wt”, and by manual classification of samples by DiSignAtlas [50]. TFEA was performed on each gene set using a local version of ChEA3 [14]. The 10 top-ranked TFs for each gene set were identified using the MeanRank method, and source–target edges were counted between the top 10 enriched TFs (sources) and TF-encoding genes from the input set. The sign of the regulatory relationship was determined based on the direction of differential expression comparing the perturbation group to the control group (“up” or “down” gene sets). Edges with a count <10 were discarded.

Filtering the network to preserve significant edges

To remove edges with low probability from the results of applying ChEA3 to thousands of gene sets, we determined the significance of each edge by comparing its observed frequency to its expected frequency in shuffled networks. Two methods were developed for generating expected edge counts. Both methods shuffle the original network. The “target set swap” (TSS) method swaps the source TF randomly, preserving the degree of the target nodes. The “node draw” (ND) method randomly swaps pairs of source and target nodes. Nodes are selected by the weighted frequency of occurrence as either a source or target role. This is repeated until the same number of edges have been sampled as were counted in the unfiltered network, preserving the relative connectivity distribution of the source and target nodes. For each shuffling method, 50 shuffled networks are produced. Then, the average and standard deviation of each edge is calculated. Next, the observed edge counts are compared to the average and standard deviations of the counts of each set of shuffled networks to calculate a z-score for the edge. The z-score was used to calculate a right-tailed P-value. Edges with a P-value ≥.01 were discarded. Due to the large number of initial edges, some source–target node pairs were connected by both upregulated and downregulated edges. This conflict was resolved by preserving only the sign of the more significant edge. This produced two filtered GRNs: TSS-filtered and ND-filtered. Each of these GRNs was evaluated for accuracy by comparing the edges within these networks to edges from other sources of TF–TF associations not used to construct the GRNs.

Benchmarking the filtered networks against other GRNs

Each filtered network was independently benchmarked against two reference GRNs to determine whether the GRN reconstruction process produces known TF–target associations, as well as which network pruning method produces the most reliable network. The two reference GRNs used to benchmark the ChEA-KG GRN are from text mining of TF–target interactions from TRRUST [47, 48], and PWMs from TRANSFAC [49] and JASPAR [18]. These networks were selected because they are not used by ChEA3 [14] to perform enrichment analysis and were processed and available for download from Enrichr [51]. The TRRUST GRN has 379 source TFs, 553 target TFs, and 2597 edges. The TRANSFAC and JASPAR PWMs GRN contains 246 source TFs, 1652 target TFs, and 32 067 edges. The statistical properties of the ChEA-KG filtered and unfiltered networks are summarized (Supplementary Table S2). The filtered and unfiltered ChEA-KG GRNs were compared to the benchmarking GRNs to determine the number of overlapping edges, and whether the number of overlapping edges is greater than what would be expected in shuffled networks with the similar size and structure. To generate the shuffled networks, source–target pairs in the original ChEA-KG filtered and unfiltered networks were randomly sampled from the source and target node distributions. This process ensures that each shuffled network has the same number of nodes and edges and similar connectivity distribution. The number of overlapping edges were normalized to the size of the filtered network. To determine statistical significance of the expected versus observed overlapping edges between the ChEA-KG original and shuffled networks the Z test was applied. One-hundred shuffled networks were generated to produce an expected overlap distribution. The benchmarking procedure is outlined in a diagram (Supplementary Fig. S1). This procedure was performed twice for each comparison, once with directed edges and once with undirected edges, for a total of eight comparisons.

Visualizing the GRN

To better understand the global structure of the GRN, several dimensionality reduction methods were applied to visualize and cluster the GRN’s TFs based on their connectivity similarity. First, we created a uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) [52] of the source TFs based on similarity between their target TF sets. Term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) for each TF was computed [53]. Then, the Leiden algorithm was applied to identify clusters of similar TFs [54]. Next, we visualized the network edges as a clustered heatmap. To create the heatmap, first the network was converted to a directed, signed adjacency matrix where rows represent sources and columns represent targets. Each (source, target) entry in the matrix was assigned a 0 if no edge exists, or a 1 or −1 for upregulated or downregulated edges, respectively. In addition, a heatmap of the Jaccard similarity scores between all TF pairs was generated. We calculated the Jaccard similarity between each pair of source TFs based on their shared targets. Both matrices were plotted as hierarchically clustered heatmaps using the Seaborn Python package [55].

Developing the ChEA-KG web server application

The ChEA-KG web server application enables users to interact with and query the GRN. The application was built using a customizable Knowledge Graph User Interface (KG-UI) [56] that uses Cytoscape.js [57] to visualize Cypher query results from a Neo4j database [58]. The GRN was ingested into a Neo4j database by serializing the GRN into node and edge lists. The user interface provides the ability to perform queries for finding neighbors of single TFs, finding shortest paths between pairs of TFs, displaying the subnetworks returned from the enrichment analysis with ChEA3 [14], displaying the cell, cancer, MoA, and aging atlases subnetworks using various layouts, expanding and shrinking the size of the displayed subnetwork, viewing properties of nodes and links, and downloading the displayed associations in tabular format.

Building the ChEA-KG TF atlases

The ChEA-KG Aging Atlas was created using differentially expressed gene sets pre-computed for the GeneSetCart project [59] using the GTEx [60] aging signatures gene set library downloaded from Enrichr [51]. Methods for creating the Cell Atlas, MoA Atlas, and Cancer Atlas are described in Supplementary Text S1. For the Aging Atlas, the 20–29 versus 70–79 age group comparison was used as the representative set of signatures, up/down gene sets, for each tissue. The visualization and integration of the Aging Atlas follows the same procedure as described for the other Atlases. The resulting subnetworks were tested for significant overlap to identify tissues with similar TF subnetworks. Significance was assigned via permutation testing. Given a pair of subnetworks A and B with sizes M and N, respectively, and overlapping edges S0, 1000 pairs of networks with sizes M and N and overlap S1 were generated by randomly sampling edges from the full ChEA-KG background GRN. The P-value was calculated by dividing the number of times the randomized overlap, S0, is greater than or equal to the observed overlap, S1, divided by the number of permutations (1000). A pan-tissue regulatory TF–TF signed and directed network was constructed by aggregating all edges present in the Aging Atlas subnetworks. The resulting global network was visualized using Cytoscape [57]. The frequency of observing each node and link in the Aging Atlas subnetworks informs the size of the nodes and links in the global network.

The ChEA-KG-TS Workflow

Differential gene expression

Differential gene expression is performed on the raw RNA-seq gene-count matrices uploaded by the user using DESeq2 [61] or the Characteristic Direction (CD) [62] methods. DESeq2 [61] is implemented using PyDESeq2 [63]. If the user chooses to apply both methods, then each method is applied independently, and the workflow produces two sets of output, one from each method. Differential expression is computed by comparing each time point to the baseline zero time point, or by comparing each time point to the previous time point. In the case of using the baseline time point as the control reference, each successive time point is considered a case group. When using the successive time point option, the samples from time point N are used as the control and the samples from time point N+1 are used as the case group, starting from time point zero. The gene sets are identified by first filtering the differential gene expression results for genes with a P-value <.05. If the combined size of the up and down gene sets is larger than 2000, the P-value cutoff is incrementally decreased from 0.01 to 1e−20, by factors of 10e−1, until the set has fewer than 2000 genes. The resulting gene set is split into up- and downregulated gene sets based on positive and negative logFC from DESeq2 results. For CD results, the top 600 genes regardless of statistical significance are selected based on the absolute value of the CD coefficient. Then, the gene sets are partitioned into up- and downregulated genes using the sign of the CD coefficient. ID mapping, batch correction, and removal of lowly expressed genes are expected to be applied by the user.

Identifying enriched TFs and generating time-series subnetworks

Once the up and down gene sets for each time-point comparison have been calculated or extracted from the input gene set library file, the enriched TFs for each gene set are identified using the ChEA3 API. Individual library ranks for each of the top TFs returned by the enrichment results are visualized as bar charts using Plotly (https://plotly.com/). Next, for each pair of adjacent time-point comparisons, we query the ChEA-KG network via its API for existing regulatory edges, where the earlier time point is the source node and the later time point is the target node. To emphasize temporal transitions in the regulatory network, edges connecting TFs within a time-point comparison are not shown but may be analyzed by independently submitting the gene sets to ChEA-KG. The resulting subnetworks are visualized using NetworkX [64].

Creating the UMAP visualizations

To generate the animated UMAP projections, we first projected the TF–target co-expression gene set library created from the ARCHS4 resource [65] on the UMAP space. TF–target sets are vectorized by computing TF-IDF on the target TF sets [66]. The resulting vectors are embedded using UMAP and the embeddings are plotted using Plotly (https://plotly.com/). Clusters of similar source TFs are identified with the Leiden clustering algorithm [54]. The enriched TFs for each time point are highlighted on the UMAP plot to create static plots that are collated into an animated GIF file.

ChEA-KG-TS use case

We developed four use cases using publicly available data from MoTrPAC [67], GTEx [60], and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) [68]. Here, we focus on one use case, but descriptions of additional use cases can be found in Supplementary Text S2.

TRAIL treatment of TNBC

The gene expression count matrix from the study GSE271120 (GSE271120_RawCountFile_rsemgenes.CCBR1062.csv.gz) was downloaded from GEO [68]. Metadata assigned to each sample were manually created using the sample descriptions in GEO. Differential expression was performed with DESeq2 [61].

ChEA-KG-TS website development

The ChEA-KG-TS website is developed using the Appyter framework [69]. Appyters are Jupyter Notebook workflows that are converted into light-weight web server applications. Original Jupyter notebooks are converted into full-stack web-based applications by converting global variables into HTML input forms. Inputs are controlled via components defined by Jinja2 and Svelte templates and referenced within the notebook. The central pipeline, including differential gene expression and enrichment analysis, subnetwork, and UMAP visualizations, are all implemented with Python. Executed reports produced by running the ChEA-KG-TS workflow are stored in an S3 bucket along with the results, including figures, tables, and output CSV files. The results from each unique set of inputs are permanently accessible via a persistent URL.

Results

The ChEA-KG GRN

The ChEA-KG human GRN of TF–TF interactions was created by applying TFEA with ChEA3 [14] to a massive collection of gene sets created RummaGEO [46]. RummaGEO is a search engine that hosts up and down gene sets from automated differential gene expression analysis applied to thousands of RNA-seq studies deposited in GEO. By combining TF-binding site knowledge with differential gene expression data, we can capture the most likely direct effects of one TF over another to construct a high fidelity GRN (Fig. 1A). The RummaGEO resource serves 171 441 human and 195 265 mouse gene sets spanning 29 294 GEO studies. For establishing ChEA-KG, only a subset of 29 328 gene sets extracted from 10 901 unique studies were used because the sets from these studies came from signatures where there is a clear separation between a control and a perturbation group. These gene sets were created by first downloading the uniformly aligned sample vectors of expression from ARCHS4 [65]. Then, samples from studies with control and perturbation conditions were analyzed for differential expression with the limma-voom method [70]. TFEA was performed using ChEA3 [14], which covers 1632 human TFs. During the construction process 10 405 833 signed edges were established between TFs. 373 250 of these edges are unique, connecting 727 source TFs to regulate the expression of 1560 target TFs. The network has self-loops and TFs can serve as both sources and targets. The GRN was filtered to exclude edges that did not occur more than expected when comparing the GRN to shuffled networks. After filtering, the GRN was reduced to 131 181 edges (ND filtering method) and 109 813 edges (TSS filtering method). Other network properties such as number of edges by direction, average links per node, self-loops, and two-node feedback loops were recorded (Table 3).

Benchmarking the ND- and TSS-filtering methods

Since the TSS- and ND-filtered GRNs have different nodes and edges, the two methods were benchmarked by comparing the edges in those networks to edges from other TF–TF GRNs that are not included in ChEA3 [14] and RummaGEO [46], namely, literature curated TF–TF interactions from TRRUST [47], and TF–TF interactions based on PWMs from TRANSFAC [49] and JASPAR [18]. Each comparison was made with and without considering edge direction, for a total of four comparisons per network (Fig. 1B). Both filtered GRNs showed significantly higher overlap with interactions from TRRUST and TRANSFAC/JASPAR compared to edges in the shuffled networks of the same size and similar connectivity. The ND-filtered GRN showed the most significant results in three out of the four comparisons. The ND-filtered GRN was only outperformed by the TSS-filtered GRN when compared against the TRANSFAC/JASPAR PWMs library (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Table S3). Based on the benchmarking result, the ND-filtered GRN was selected for the ChEA-KG webserver application.

Identifying clusters within the GRN

Next, we visualized the entire GRN to examine its topology and identify TF modules. First, the network was clustered with the Leiden algorithm [54] and then visualized with a UMAP [52] (Fig. 1C). The sets of TFs from the 26 identified clusters were uploaded to Enrichr [51] to characterize the collective functions of each cluster of TFs. Thirteen clusters had clearly defined collective functions such as “patterning,” “cell cycle,” “hair and skin regulation,” and “stem cell maintenance.” Next, the GRN adjacency matrix was visualized as a clustered heatmap (Supplementary Fig. S2). Only the 700 TFs that act as both sources and targets are included in the heatmap. The densely colored areas in the heatmap show several regions of TF modules that regulate similar targets. Lastly, the Jaccard similarity scores between GRN TFs were visualized as a clustered distance table heatmap (Supplementary Fig. S3). Only 381 highly connected TFs were visualized because this subset of TFs had clearly visible defined clusters. The dendrogram from the distance table was extracted, and clusters were determined by cutting the dendrogram at 65% of the maximum tree height to produce 12 unique clusters. These clusters were annotated with enriched terms identified with Enrichr. The heatmap clusters were enriched for terms such as “protein folding,” “immune response,” and “cell cycle.”

The ChEA-KG web server application

ChEA-KG is an interactive web server application that enables users to interact with the GRN in multiple ways (Fig. 1A). The human GRN is stored in a Neo4j database as a KG and the ChEA-KG UI queries the KG database to extract and display GRN subnetworks. Users of ChEA-KG can query the KG database for creating subnetworks centered on a single TF and subnetworks that connect two TFs based on the shortest paths between them. The results page has a toolbar that enables users to customize the subnetworks by limiting the number of nodes and edges, viewing the subnetwork as a table, adding a legend, changing the subnetwork layout, and displaying a tooltip with information associated with each TF. Users can also save the subnetwork as an image, or as node and edge lists in CSV files. ChEA-KG also has an enrichment analysis feature that enables users to associate their input gene sets with subnetworks of regulatory interacting TFs. The input gene sets are queried using the ChEA3 API [14] to identify the top enriched TFs for each gene set. These TFs are then visualized as a subnetwork. Users can adjust the number of edges in the displayed subnetwork by filtering by significance. The number of TFs can be adjusted to return between 5 and 25 top-enriched TFs. Because the number of TFs in the GRN (1559) is less than the number of TFs in the ChEA3 database (1632), it is possible that some top-ranked TFs are not connected to any other TF in the GRN. Even for TFs that are in the GRN, the enrichment results may return TFs that are not directly connected to each other. If an enriched TF does not share edges with any other enriched TFs, including self-loops, they will be displayed as a single unconnected node. To facilitate hypothesis generation via the enrichment analysis feature, we created four TF Atlases that display results for context-specific collections of gene sets including 131 cell types from Enrichr cell-type libraries [51], 69 cancer subtypes from the CPTAC-3 project [71], 30 consensus gene sets representing MoAs from the LINCS project [72], and 24 aging tissue signatures from GTEx [60] (Supplementary Text S3, Supplementary Figs S4–S7, and Supplementary Tables S4–S6).

The TF subnetworks of the aging atlas

The ChEA-KG aging atlas consists of aging-specific up and down gene sets for 24 tissues computed from the GTEx v8 publicly available RNA-seq dataset [60]. To explore how the various tissue-specific aging TF subnetworks may share components, a composite network was created by combining all edges and nodes from all the aging atlas subnetworks (Fig. 2A). This network has visible clusters of TFs associated with specific tissues sharing similar aging specific regulatory modules (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S8). Clusters of co-regulating TFs were also identified by hierarchically clustering the adjacency matrix of the composite network (Supplementary Fig. S9). One distinct network module was identified to regulate upregulated aging genes in breast, lung, blood vessel, thyroid, liver, and skin tissues with the TF intestine-specific homeobox (ISX) appearing as a central node in this module (Fig. 2C).

TF submodules corresponding to specific tissues are highlighted in purple and labeled. (B) Heatmap of hierarchically clustered P-values representing similarity between regulatory subnetworks for genes upregulated in each tissue in response to aging. (C) Subnetwork from panel (A) consisting of ISX and its immediate neighbors, based on gene sets upregulated in response to aging.

ISX is an intestinal tissue master regulator that is also responsible for controlling vitamin A production [73]. ISX has been also suggested as a potential prognostic and therapeutic target for hepatocellular carcinoma [74]. The ISX-associated subnetwork includes three hepatocyte nuclear factors (HNFs), namely HNF4ɑ, HNF4ɣ, and HNF4β. These TFs are primarily expressed in the liver, pancreas, and kidney and their dysregulation is implicated in diabetes [75]. Other TFs found in the subnetwork are also involved in liver and pancreatic functions including NR1H4, a regulator of bile acid synthesis [76], MLXIPL, a master regulator of lipogenesis [77], and RFX6, which regulates pancreatic islet cell differentiation [78]. The remaining TFs in the subnetwork, CDX1, CDX2, ELF3, GATA5, TBOX10, SPDEF, and AOTH1, are cellular differentiation regulators in both embryonic and adult tissues [79–83]. Dysregulation of developmental programs in adult tissues has recently been suggested as a cause of aging and age-related disease [84]. This module therefore implicates metabolic function and tissue development in the process of aging.

The ChEA-KG-TS application

ChEA-KG-TS is a reproducible, stand-alone analysis pipeline that infers likely TF modules from time-series datasets. The workflow visualizes the results as layered, connected regulatory subnetworks made of human TFs. The ChEA-KG-TS application is built using the Appyters framework [69]. Unlike standard Appyters hosted on the Appyters catalog, ChEA-KG-TS is an enhanced standalone application hosted at its own domain. ChEA-KG-TS is further distinguished from standard Appyters by its customized UI that includes sample analyses on the landing page, enhanced input fields, and comprehensive persistent reports. The ChEA-KG-TS pipeline is designed to be compatible with any time-series RNA-seq dataset, including the support for RNA-seq count matrices or gene set libraries derived from experiments where gene expression is measured as a time series.

To use ChEA-KG-TS, users first choose between submitting raw RNA-seq counts with metadata or uploading their own precomputed gene sets in the form of a gene set library, namely a Gene Matrix Transpose (GMT) file. If raw RNA-seq gene-count matrix input is chosen, users need to further choose between the DESeq2 [61] or CD [62] methods to perform differential gene expression between adjacent time points or between each time point and the initial time point. For precomputed gene sets, users may upload up to two GMT files: one with gene sets comparing each time point against time zero, and one with adjacent time-point comparison. A minimum of one GMT is required to run the pipeline. Additionally, each GMT file should have a minimum of two gene sets in both directions, e.g. two up gene sets and two down gene sets, to enable the network visualization. Each term should be annotated as “(time-point comparison) up genes” or “(time-point comparison) down genes.” The two types of comparisons are analyzed and results are reported independently. The inclusion of both types of comparisons is intended to provide users with two unique perspectives on their datasets. Additionally, this independence gives users the option to focus on both sets of results, or just one, depending on what best suits their data. For both input types, example files and instructions are provided on the ChEA-KG-TS site to show proper formatting. The GMT upload may be useful to users who have calculated differential expression over time with custom methods. Before submitting their chosen datasets, users may optionally specify the number of enriched TFs to return for each time point (5 or 10), restrict the number of edges in the final subnetwork by choosing a minimum z-score for each relationship, and provide a study title and a description.

Once gene sets have been identified, they are submitted to ChEA3 [14] and ChEA-KG to identify enriched TFs and construct time-series regulatory subnetworks. TFEA results from ChEA3 are displayed as interactive bar charts. Results from ChEA-KG are displayed as connected subnetworks organized in layers by time point. Additionally, the enriched TFs are contextualized within the entire human TF regulatory space by creating animated UMAP visualizations that highlight the enriched TFs at each time on UMAP projection (Fig. 3). The UMAP displays the 1348 TFs from the ChEA-KG network based on TF–TF co-expression correlations created from the ARCHS4 resource [65]. Such a UMAP provides a more complete view of the ChEA-KG TF modules than using the ChEA-KG TF-target relationships, which would inherently limit the plot to only show the 700 source TFs. The output report is styled to resemble a scientific paper that includes an abstract, introduction, methods, results, and citations. The results section contains interactive figures, tables summarizing the differential expression results, and text that includes figure and table legends. Based on the user’s specified inputs, the methods section will include or exclude the relevant differential expression steps (Fig. 4). All ChEA-KG-TS generated reports and report elements can be downloaded as static files. For example, the TFEA and subnetwork visualization figures can be downloaded as PNG or SVG files, the tables can be downloaded as CSV files, and the UMAP projections as an animated GIF or individual PNG files. The entire report can be downloaded as a PDF or an HTML document.

The ChEA-KG-TS workflow. Users start by selecting whether to have the differentially expressed genes automatically computed by DESeq2 or CD, or by uploading their own differentially expressed gene sets as a GMT file. Once their data are uploaded, the ChEA-KG-TS workflow processes the uploaded data to produce regulatory subnetworks, time-series UMAP projections, and bar charts of the most enriched TFs at each time point.

ChEA-KG-TS use case: TRAIL treatment of TNBC

We applied ChEA-KG-TS to produce four use cases that utilize publicly available RNA-seq datasets from MoTrPAC, GTEx, and GEO. These use cases study temporal responses to exercise, aging, and chemical and CRISPR knockout perturbations, respectively (Supplementary Text S4 and Supplementary Figs S10–S12). Here, we describe results from a previously published time-series RNA-seq dataset [85]. This study consists of three replicates of the TNBC cell line MDA-MB-231 measured at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h following the administration of TRAIL. The precomputed GMT file created from this study is made available on the ChEA-KG-TS landing page as an example dataset. The resulting time-series regulatory subnetwork from the baseline timepoint comparisons using DESeq2 (Fig. 5A) shows enrichment of FOSB, JUN, and FOS for genes upregulated in hour 0 versus 1 and hour 0 versus 3. FOSB and JUN are also enriched for the downregulated genes at these time points. Clustering analysis reveals that the TFs enriched for the up- and downregulated genes at these early time points share similar functions (Fig. 5B) and are organized into two modules that work in concert to control the initial response to TRAIL. FOSB, JUN, and FOS are known immediate-early genes (IEG). These TFs are known to be rapidly upregulated in response to extracellular stimuli and function as proto-oncogenes [86]. Conversely, NR4A3, which is enriched at both time points, is a known tumor suppressor [87]. One possible explanation of having a tumor suppressor and proto-oncogenes enriched for the same module is the possibility of NR4A3 functioning as a repressor to counteract the rapid activation of oncogenic pathways.

ChEA-KG-TS results for the GEO study GSE271120. (A) ChEA-KG-TS time-series subnetwork. Blue and red nodes are enriched for upregulated and downregulated genes, respectively. Green and red edges correspond to activation and inhibition interactions, respectively. TFs of interest are outlined. Solid line = IEGs, dotted line = members of the NFκB2 signaling pathway. (B–D)Time-series UMAP visualizations for hour 1 versus 0, hour 12 versus 0, and hour 24 versus 0. Enriched TFs are annotated.

At later time points, ChEA-KG-TS identified NFkB2 and RELB as enriched for the upregulated genes. This observation is consistent with the original study reporting that the NFkB2 pathway is activated in response to TRAIL treatment. NFkB2 and RELB are positively linked to HIVEP1, a known driver of NFkB2 signaling [88]. The TF NFAT5 is also found to be enriched for the upregulated genes at the latest time point. A link between the NFAT and NFkB/Rel TF families has been previously suggested [89]. Both HIVEP1 and NFAT5 are found to be regulated by ZNF267 (Fig. 5A). ZNF267 is not well-studied and is likely a yet-to-be-discovered member of the NFkB2 signaling pathway (Fig. 5C). These TFs are also targets of the TF MGA (Fig. 5A and D). NFAT5 has been suggested as a biomarker of inflammatory breast cancer and shown to be co-expressed alongside MGA [90]. The ChEA-KG-TS subnetwork suggests that NFAT5 and HIVEP1 are positively regulated by MGA contributing to a late inflammatory response.

Discussion

The formation of the ChEA-KG GRN presents a unique new method to reconstruct in silico GRNs. By combining a massive collection of diverse gene expression signatures with a comprehensive TFEA, a reliable data-driven GRN was constructed. The GRN contains almost all known human TFs, expanding on the size and coverage of most current GRN reconstruction techniques. For example, it includes almost all known human TFs (n = 1559) in its background GRN. In contrast, TRRUST [47], a highly used human GRN reconstructed from text mining, is considerably smaller (n = 800). The accuracy of the GRN edges is supported by the benchmarking results. However, testing it against other sources of known TF–TF interactions would strengthen the confidence of the links. Ultimately, testing such regulatory interactions in vitro and in vivo would fully confirm the inferred regulatory relationships. This can be achieved by knockdown, knockout, and overexpression of the source TF followed by gene and protein expression of the target TF. Examining the topology of the GRN identified clear clusters with known shared functions for each module. For example, we identified a module of TFs that is related to the cell cycle, immune response, differentiation, and development.

The ChEA-KG web server application facilitates the querying of the GRN for knowledge discovery. The enrichment analysis feature of ChEA-KG can be used to uncover how the most enriched TF master regulators are forming subnetworks of tight regulatory interactions. In the future, such subnetworks could provide the basis for dynamical simulations to further understand how groups of TFs work together as a functional regulatory unit. In addition, the GRN can be expanded to include non-TF-encoding genes such as kinases, chromatin modifiers, and other relevant components that can enrich the contents of each subnetwork. The ChEA-KG-TS web server application combines ChEA3 [14] TFEA and the TF–TF regulatory edges inferred by ChEA-KG to identify time-lapsed TF regulatory subnetworks that consider all known human TFs. The ChEA-KG-TS reports provide interactive and downloadable figures including TFEA results, time-series subnetworks, and UMAP visualizations that place the enriched TFs in the context of the global human GRN. We demonstrate that the ChEA-KG-TS workflow is useful to generate testable hypotheses about transient changes in gene expression in response to drug, single gene CRISPR KO, exercise, and aging perturbations. Already known and novel TF interactions and regulators were identified for each one of the four use cases developed to demonstrate CHEA-KG-TS. A key limitation of ChEA-KG-TS is that it does not provide users the option to automatically construct gene sets using a separate control dataset for each time point. While we plan to implement this feature, temporarily users can circumvent this feature by precomputing gene sets and uploading them as a GMT. Other possible improvements to the workflow and web server application include adding a sequence alignment step to support processing raw RNA-seq reads, adding support for single-cell analysis via pseudotime methods such as Monocle [91], and expanding the number of differential gene expression methods, e.g. limma-voom [70] and edgeR [92]. Overall, ChEA-KG and ChEA-KG-TS are two new extensions of ChEA3 that can be applied to identify connected TF modules for a wide range of standalone and serial RNA-seq experiments.

There are several limitations inherent to both ChEA-KG and ChEA-KG-TS. First, because ChEA3 and ChEA-KG do not use binding site knowledge to confirm TF–target associations, the predicted relationships may be indirect. Interactions between time points in the ChEA-KG-TS networks are likely indirect because time intervals between measurements vary across studies. Additionally, TFs are known to function in pairs or interact with each other via protein–protein interactions, not considered by ChEA3, ChEA-KG, and ChEA-KG-TS. Although the ChEA3, ChEA-KG, and ChEA-KG-TS tools mostly rely on evidence from omics studies such as ChIP-seq and RNA-seq which are considered unbiased because they are not literature-based, even with this type of data there are biases. This is because some TFs are studied more in ChIP-seq experiments than others, some TFs are highly expressed at the mRNA and protein levels across tissues, some TFs are more commonly identified as differentially expressed, and some TFs appear in more gene set queries. In addition, most ChIP-seq studies are extracted from individual studies using different cell types and tissues. It is uncertain how these diverse contexts transfer into new conditions. We also make the assumption that an increase in mRNA of a TF may result in an increase in the TF activity at the protein level, which is not consistently true. Finally, edges observed in these tools between TFs and their targets are not tissue and cell-type specific. It is likely that such interactions are indeed context specific, so users of ChEA3, ChEA-KG, and ChEA-KG-TS should be aware of such limitations. However, regardless of these limitations, ChEA-KG and ChEA-KG-TS are powerful tools that unravel many novel regulatory interactions across the human TF–TF regulatory network. ChEA-KG and ChEA-KG-TS should also assist researchers to place their own studies in context of the human TF–TF GRN.
