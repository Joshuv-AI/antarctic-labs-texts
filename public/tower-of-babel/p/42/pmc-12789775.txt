Late Transcatheter Valve Migration Managed With Redo TAVR
Authors: Adrián Valverde Tavira, Darío Sanmiguel Cervera, Eva Rumiz González, Andrés Mauricio Cubillos Arango, Jorge Manuel Solano López-Morel, Guillem Llopis Gisbert, Helena Coquillat Mora, Mykola Harvat Shchesniy, Alberto Berenguer Jofresa, Rafael Payá Serrano
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundValve-in-valve-in-valve procedures, typically due to late transcatheter valve degeneration, are infrequently reported.Case SummaryA 76-year-old man underwent surgical aortic valve replacement in 2016 with a 25-mm Mitroflow bioprosthesis for severe aortic stenosis. Eight years later, he developed acute heart failure due to severe aortic regurgitation from prosthetic degeneration. A valve-in-valve (ViV) procedure was successfully performed using a 25-mm self-expandable Navitor valve. Ten months later, he experienced recurrent symptoms. Imaging revealed late downward migration of the transcatheter valve, resulting in severe paravalvular leak and moderate ventricular dysfunction. The heart team decided to proceed with a repeat ViV procedure using a second self-expandable valve.DiscussionAlthough ViV is an established alternative to redo surgery, evidence on outcomes after a second procedure is limited.Take-Home MessageCareful imaging, anatomical assessment, and strategic planning are critical to ensure success in third-time transcatheter aortic valve replacement.

History of Presentation

We present the case of a 76-year-old man who underwent surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) in 2016 with a 25-mm Mitroflow bioprosthesis (Sorin) for severe aortic stenosis. Eight years later, he presented to the emergency department with acute heart failure secondary to severe aortic regurgitation due to structural valve degeneration, requiring hospital admission. Past Medical History

The patient had a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and former smoking. His cardiovascular history consisted of the SAVR procedure in 2016 and the ViV transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) procedure in February 2024. In March 2024, he received a dual-chamber pacemaker after syncope due to high-grade atrioventricular block. Additional history included paroxysmal atrial fibrillation managed with edoxaban 60 mg, hepatic cysts/hematomas, and colonic angiodysplasias.

Differential Diagnosis

In our case, <1 year had passed since the transcatheter aortic valve–in–surgical aortic valve (TAV-in-SAV) procedure, making structural degeneration of the bioprosthesis an unlikely cause. The patient had no fever or elevation of acute-phase reactants on laboratory tests, and blood cultures were negative, arguing against early infective endocarditis. Undersizing or malposition of the prosthesis would typically result in severe aortic regurgitation immediately after implantation, not 10 months later. This left prosthetic migration as a plausible hypothesis, which was subsequently confirmed by imaging studies.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiograph at the 1-month follow-up after the TAV-in-SAV procedure showed a normally functioning and well-positioned prosthesis, with a mean transvalvular gradient of 14 mm Hg and mild anterior paravalvular regurgitation.

Ten months later, due to recurrence of heart failure symptoms, a three-dimensional transesophageal echocardiograph was performed. It revealed downward migration of the Navitor prosthesis toward the LVOT, in close proximity to the anterior mitral leaflet, resulting in severe aortic regurgitation due to paravalvular leak, along with moderate left ventricular systolic dysfunction (Figure 3, Videos 8 and 9). Fortunately, the prosthetic migration did not result in significant mitral stenosis.

Management

The case was rediscussed in a multidisciplinary heart team meeting, during which several therapeutic options were evaluated:1.SAVR: Deemed unsuitable owing to the patient's high surgical risk profile and the increased mortality risk associated with TAVR prosthesis explantation.2.Balloon postdilation: Considered ineffective, as the issue was related to downward migration of the transcatheter valve rather than underexpansion.3.Snaring and repositioning the valve 4 to 5 mm higher: This approach was considered technically demanding and associated with a high risk of procedural complications, including upward migration and coronary obstruction, as well as the possibility of recurrent late migration.4.Redo TAVR (TAV-in-TAV-in-SAV procedure): This approach was selected as the most feasible and definitive option, offering the best balance between efficacy and safety.

After the decision to proceed with redo TAVR, careful anatomical and hemodynamic assessment was performed to determine the optimal prosthesis type. The internal diameter of the initially implanted Mitroflow prosthesis was 21 mm, thus the resulting gradient with a 20-mm balloon-expandable valve (BEV) inside the 25-mm Navitor was expected to be high. The height to the left main was 17 mm, and the valve-to-coronary distances were 9 mm and 12 mm, respectively, which minimized the risk of coronary artery compromise (Figure 4). However, supra-annular SEVs are associated with longer neoskirts, which may increase the risk of low-flow conditions in the neosinuses and promote late thrombotic events.

Ultimately, a second 25-mm Navitor SEV was deemed most appropriate. A strategy of direct implantation without snaring (unless necessary in case of dragging the index valve during advance) was chosen, with planned deployment 4 to 5 mm above the inflow plane of the previously implanted prosthesis. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged 2 days after the procedure, with outpatient follow-up arranged. Predischarge transthoracic echocardiography showed a mean transvalvular gradient of 14 mm Hg, with no evidence of paravalvular leak (Figure 6). At follow-up visits, he remained clinically stable, was assessed as NYHA functional class I-II, and had not required any further hospitalization.

Discussion

Late migration of a transcatheter aortic valve into the LVOT is a rare but clinically significant complication, particularly in ViV scenarios. This phenomenon may occur months or even years after the initial implantation, and it is associated with a combination of anatomical, technical, and device-device interaction factors.1 Clinical manifestations include acute aortic regurgitation, paravalvular leak, LVOT obstruction, or secondary mitral valve dysfunction.2 Migration may result from insufficient anchoring, underexpansion, degeneration of the prior bioprosthesis, absence of calcification, and undersizing or unfavorable LVOT geometry.

In our case, the leading hypothesis for the migration into the LVOT is that it resulted from the interplay of multiple contributing factors. First, the TAV-in-SAV procedure was deliberately performed with a slightly low implantation depth to avoid compromising the coronary ostia. Second, the failure mechanism of the 25-mm Mitroflow was leaflet retraction leading to regurgitation, with minimal calcification, which may have limited the anchoring capacity for the transcatheter valve. Importantly, the annular perimeter of the Mitroflow prosthesis was 72 mm (Figure 7), supporting the selection of a 25-mm Navitor valve (recommended for annuli of 66-73 mm) over a 23-mm Navitor (indicated for annuli of 60-66 mm). Furthermore, the ViV Aortic app (KRUTSCH Associates), widely used in clinical practice, lists both the 23-mm and 25-mm Navitor valves as viable options for ViV procedures in 25-mm Mitroflow prostheses. Regarding the timing of the migration, symptom onset was abrupt, suggesting that the development of a significant periprosthetic leak may have occurred over a short period. However, we cannot definitively determine whether the valve migrated gradually until the leak became hemodynamically relevant or if it shifted suddenly. The only imaging certainty is that both the postprocedure and 1-month follow-up echocardiography showed the valve positioned correctly with only trivial regurgitation (Figure 2, Videos 6 and 7), whereas at 10 months, echocardiography prompted by new symptoms revealed valve migration and severe leak (Figure 3, Videos 8 and 9).

The management of late prosthetic migration must be individualized. Surgical explantation allows for removal of the migrated valve, correction of anatomical complications, and implantation of a larger prosthesis. However, it carries significantly higher procedural risk, including increased hospital mortality and perioperative complications, as shown in multicenter series. In cases involving SEV, the need for concomitant aortic root surgery may be as high as 14% (TAVR explant). Therefore, surgical explantation is typically reserved for unfavorable anatomies, active endocarditis, prosthetic dysfunction not amenable to percutaneous treatment, or the need for concomitant cardiac surgery.3,4

Percutaneous reintervention (redo TAVR) represents a less invasive option, with lower short-term mortality and morbidity compared with surgical explant, particularly in high-risk patients or those with prior interventions.3,5 Redo TAVR can stabilize the migrated valve and restore prosthetic function. However, the risk of elevated residual gradients, patient-prosthesis mismatch, and impaired future coronary access increases with each sequential implantation, especially in small annuli or in low-profile surgical bioprostheses.6 Preprocedural planning with computed tomography and three-dimensional simulations is essential to minimize the risk of coronary obstruction and optimize the hemodynamic result.7

The multicenter THIRD registry provides valuable data on outcomes of up to 3 aortic interventions, reporting a 30-day technical success rate of 85% for a third percutaneous valve procedure.5 These findings support redo TAVR as a safe and effective strategy in selected high-risk patients. A critical consideration in redo TAVR is preservation of coronary access, particularly in the presence of a high neoskirt resulting from multiple stent layers and leaflet overhang.8 This anatomical configuration may increase the risk of coronary obstruction, a complication observed in 6.1% of redo TAVR cases in the THIRD registry.5

The choice between SEV versus BEV in the context of redo TAVR should be based on annular anatomy, the size and type of the prior bioprosthesis, the risk of prosthesis-patient mismatch, and the hemodynamic goals. Randomized trials and meta-analyses suggest that SEV, particularly those with supra-annular leaflet design, are associated with lower residual gradients and reduced incidence of mismatch, advantages that may translate into improved durability and a lower risk of prosthetic dysfunction, especially in small annuli. On the downside, SEV are more frequently associated with coronary compromise, conduction disturbances, and permanent pacemaker implantation.9,10

In our case, an intra-annular SEV was chosen for the third implant to maximize the effective orifice area, minimize residual gradients, and achieve the shortest possible neoskirt, thereby reducing the risk of thrombotic events in narrow neosinuses or even late sinus sequestration.

Conclusions

Late migration of an SEV into the LVOT is an uncommon but serious complication requiring individualized management. Redo TAVR is the preferred strategy in most cases, with surgical explant reserved for anatomically or clinically complex situations. Valve selection and meticulous preprocedural planning are critical to procedural success and long-term durability. Data from the THIRD registry5 reinforce the feasibility and safety of sequential percutaneous treatment in appropriately selected patients. The choice between SEV versus BEV should be guided by anatomical constraints and mismatch risk, weighing the risk of procedural or late coronary obstruction against the goal of achieving optimal hemodynamic performance.
