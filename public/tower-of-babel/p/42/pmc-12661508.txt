Unilateral palpebral and bilateral orbital xanthogranuloma: An unusual presentation of adult-onset xanthogranuloma responding to tocilizumab
Authors: Karla Itatí Llerenas-Aguirre, Zair Omar Salgado Mancilla, Julio Enrique Cortés Vargas, Óscar Daniel Hernández Delgado, Abelardo A Rodríguez-Reyes, Sara Isabel Plazola-Hernández
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report a rare presentation of adult-onset xanthogranuloma (AOX) with unilateral palpebral involvement and bilateral orbital disease, and to highlight the importance of systemic evaluation and the role of tocilizumab as an effective corticosteroid-sparing treatment.ObservationsA 65-year-old woman presented with a two-year history of left-sided proptosis, periorbital swelling, and epiphora. Examination revealed a unilateral palpebral mass with bilateral orbital involvement, an atypical distribution for AOX. Imaging demonstrated bilateral extraocular muscle thickening and axial displacement of the left globe. Histopathology from an incisional biopsy showed xanthomatous histiocytes, Touton-type giant cells, and mature lymphocytes, consistent with xanthogranulomatous inflammation. Systemic evaluation revealed elevated serum IgG and pelvic lymphadenopathy. Due to disease progression and the need to minimize corticosteroid use, the patient was treated with tocilizumab, which led to clinical and radiological improvement. Surgical excision of the eyelid lesion was later performed for cosmetic reasons, with no evidence of recurrence to date. Follow-up PET-CT imaging, obtained three months after the final tocilizumab infusion, demonstrated complete resolution of prior orbital findings and no evidence of systemic involvement.ConclusionThis case illustrates a rare anatomical presentation of AOX with unilateral eyelid involvement and bilateral orbital extension. It underscores the importance of thorough systemic evaluation, long-term follow-up to monitor for progression to other AOXGD subtypes such as AAPOX, and the potential role of biologic agents like tocilizumab in achieving disease control while reducing corticosteroid exposure.Keywords: Adult-onset xanthogranuloma, Orbital xanthogranuloma, tocilizumab

Introduction

Adult-onset xanthogranulomatous orbital disease (AOXGD) comprises a group of rare inflammatory orbital disorders classified within non-Langerhans cell histiocytosis type II.1, 2, 3 It is subdivided into four primary subtypes: adult onset xanthogranuloma (AOX), adult-onset asthma and periocular xanthogranuloma (AAPOX), necrobiotic xanthogranuloma (NBX), and Erdheim-Chester disease (ECD), all of which share histopathological features such as foamy histiocytic infiltration and Touton giant cells, with varying degrees of fibrosis and necrosis.2,3.

Clinical presentation varies by subtype. While AOX, AAPOX, and NBX primarily affect the anterior orbit, ECD often involves intraconal structures and systemic manifestations.2, 3, 4 Periocular skin lesions, particularly in AAPOX and NBX, are frequently observed, with known associations with monoclonal gammopathy and immune dysfunction.2,3,5.

Due to the broad differential diagnosis and potential systemic involvement, a comprehensive evaluation including imaging, biopsy, and immunohistochemical analysis is required, focusing on CD8+ lymphocytic infiltration. Treatment selection varies by subtype and may include surgical resection, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, or systemic chemotherapy.6,7 We present the case of a patient with an atypical inflammatory orbital lesion ultimately diagnosed as bilateral orbital xanthogranulomatosis, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and a multidisciplinary approach.

Clinical case

A 65-year-old female with no relevant medical history presented with a two-year history of left-sided proptosis, periorbital swelling and epiphora (Fig. 1). Examination revealed best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 20/40 in the right eye (RE) and 20/250 in the left eye (LE), with no afferent pupillary defect in either eye. Refraction showed −0.25 = −1.75 × 178° in the RE and +1.50 = −4.25 × 169° in the LE, consistent with significant antimetropia. The patient also reported longstanding decreased vision in the LE since childhood. Intraocular pressure, measured with Goldmann applanation tonometry, was 12 mmHg in both eyes. Ocular motility evaluation showed a 15 prism diopter (PD) left exotropia with a −3 limitation in adduction and overaction of the left inferior rectus muscle, as well as a −2 limitation in supraduction of the RE (Fig. 2). Additional findings included axial proptosis with inferior displacement, bipalpebral edema with yellowish discoloration, and a soft, mobile, non-tender orbital mass causing mechanical ptosis (Fig. 1). Biomicroscopy revealed mild conjunctival hyperemia in the LE, while the RE was unremarkable. Fundoscopic examination of both eyes revealed no pathological findings.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the orbits demonstrated an isodense soft-tissue lesion involving the superior and inferior eyelids of the LE, along with bilateral thickening of the lateral, medial, inferior, and superior rectus muscles and axial left globe displacement. No bone erosion was observed (Fig. 3).

Contrast-enhanced orbital CT scan reveals an isodense soft-tissue lesion involving the superior and inferior left eyelids. There is bilateral thickening of the lateral, medial, inferior, and superior rectus muscles, along with axial displacement of the left globe.

An incisional biopsy of the left eyelid lesion was performed. Histopathological examination showed an inflammatory infiltrate composed of mature lymphocytes, xanthomatous histiocytes, and scattered Touton-type multinucleated giant cells, consistent with xanthogranulomatous orbital inflammation (Fig. 4, Fig. 5). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated strong positivity for CD68 in histiocytes and negative staining for CD1a and S100, supporting the diagnosis of AOX and helping to exclude Langerhans cell histiocytosis. There was no evidence of IgG4-positive plasma cell infiltration. Additional staining revealed diffuse CD45 positivity, with a mixed population of CD3+ T cells and CD20+ B cells in a reactive pattern, which further supports a benign inflammatory process and helps rule out orbital lymphoma.

Based on clinical, radiological, and histopathological findings, a diagnosis of AOXGD was made. Further evaluation was conducted to assess for systemic involvement and guide therapeutic planning. An abdominopelvic CT scan revealed lymphadenopathy in the bilateral external iliac chains and the right femoral region. Laboratory studies showed elevated total serum IgG (1907 mg/dL), along with positive IgG serologies for cytomegalovirus (116 U/mL), Epstein-Barr virus (642 U/mL), and herpes simplex virus, suggesting prior viral exposure. Additional findings included an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (41 mm/h), hypercholesterolemia (216 mg/dL), and hypertriglyceridemia (266 mg/dL). A thorough workup was performed to exclude infectious, autoimmune, and vasculitic causes of orbital inflammation.

A multidisciplinary evaluation involving Hematology, Immunology, Pneumology and Internal Medicine was undertaken. The elevated IgG was considered reactive, likely secondary to prior viral infections. Hematology recommended a whole-body positron emission tomography (PET) scan to rule out systemic lymphoproliferative or histiocytic disease. The PET scan revealed diffuse thickening of the cutaneous plane in the ciliary region and lateral canthus of the LE. No intra or extraconal lesions were observed bilaterally and no hypermetabolic foci suggestive of lymphadenopathy were identified. Given the progressive course of the disease and the need to minimize corticosteroid exposure, the patient was started on tocilizumab, an interleukin-6 receptor antagonist. This decision was based on growing evidence supporting its efficacy in orbital inflammatory disorders and its favorable safety profile compared to systemic corticosteroids, immunosuppressants (methotrexate, azathioprine) and chemotherapeutic agents (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, interferon alpha). Tocilizumab was well tolerated and clinical stabilization was achieved after five doses.

Following initiation of tocilizumab therapy, the patient was closely monitored both clinically and through imaging studies. A PET-CT scan performed three months after the fifth and final dose demonstrated complete resolution of the previously noted right orbital involvement, with no evidence of systemic disease. At the time of this report, total follow-up duration since treatment initiation is approximately nine months.

The patient showed marked improvement with resolution of periorbital swelling and tenderness. Previously noted limitations in RE supraduction (−2) and LE adduction (−3) resolved, with full restoration of extraocular motility and improvement of LE exotropia (Fig. 6). Imaging confirmed decreased extraocular muscle enlargement and reduced orbital soft tissue involvement on CT and PET-CT scans.

Five months after initiating tocilizumab, the patient requested surgical excision of the palpebral mass for aesthetic reasons. The surgical team advised about the high recurrence rates reported in the literature following isolated resection of xanthogranulomatous lesions without systemic disease control. Despite these considerations, surgical excision of the palpebral mass was performed successfully and without complications (Fig. 6). One month later, the patient underwent bilateral upper eyelid blepharoplasty to address residual dermatochalasis (Fig. 6). The postoperative course has been favorable, with no signs of recurrence at the four-month follow-up after completing tocilizumab therapy. The patient remains under regular clinical surveillance to assess long-term response to treatment, monitor for potential systemic manifestations and detect any recurrence.

Discussion

AOX is a rare, benign, non-Langerhans cell histiocytic disorder characterized by the infiltration of lipid-laden foamy histiocytes and Touton giant cells, typically limited to the orbit and periocular tissues. It is one of the four recognized subtypes within the AOXGD spectrum, alongside NBX, ECD and AAPOX. Among them, AOX is generally the most localized and self-limited form.3,8.

Our case presents several clinically and diagnostically relevant features. The patient had a two-year history of progressive proptosis, periorbital swelling, and a soft tissue mass. Histopathological findings consistent with xanthogranulomatous inflammation including xanthomatous histiocytes and Touton-type multinucleated giant cells confirmed the diagnosis.3,8 Notably, this case exhibited an uncommon anatomical pattern: a unilateral palpebral xanthogranuloma associated with bilateral orbital involvement. Most documented cases of AOX involve either unilateral or bilateral disease affecting both eyelids and orbit.3,8,9 The asymmetrical distribution in our case, palpebral involvement on one side with bilateral extraocular muscle infiltration, highlights the importance of comprehensive orbital imaging, even when the clinical presentation appears unilateral.

Although AOX is classically considered localized, systemic workup is essential at diagnosis to rule out other AOXGDs, particularly NBX and ECD, which are more frequently associated with systemic manifestations.3,8,9 Our patient's elevated total serum IgG and pelvic lymphadenopathy raised initial concern for systemic disease; however, the absence of monoclonal gammopathy, B symptoms, or visceral organ involvement, along with the histologic pattern and clinical course, supported a diagnosis of AOX. A PET-CT scan was performed three months after the fifth and final dose of tocilizumab to rule out subclinical systemic involvement. The scan revealed no hypermetabolic lesions or lymphadenopathy, further supporting the absence of systemic disease at this stage.

These clinical and radiologic improvements suggest that tocilizumab effectively stabilized the disease and led to remission. Given the nine-month follow-up, continued surveillance is essential to monitor for potential delayed systemic manifestations or disease relapse.

That said, systemic evaluation should not be dismissed, as cases of AAPOX have been described to evolve months or even years following an initial AOX diagnosis.10 AAPOX is characterized by periocular xanthogranuloma in association with adult-onset asthma and lymphadenopathy, and early orbital inflammation can precede the respiratory symptoms. Thus, long-term follow-up is essential to detect any future transition to this or other subtypes.10.

The differential diagnosis of orbital xanthogranulomatous disease includes not only AOXGDs but also Langerhans cell histiocytosis, idiopathic orbital inflammation, IgG4-related disease, sarcoidosis and orbital lymphoma. Immunohistochemical staining plays a critical role in establishing a precise diagnosis. AOX lesions typically exhibit CD68+ histiocytes and CD8+ T-cell infiltration, with negative staining for CD1a and S100, helping distinguish them from Langerhans cell histiocytosis.5,7, 8, 9 The absence of BRAF V600E mutation also helps rule out ECD.11,12 Absence of IgG4-positive plasma cells and no storiform fibrosis and obliterative phlebitis differentiate the AOX from IgG4-related disease.13 In contrast to AOX, NBX more often shows necrosis, fibrosclerosis, and deeper tissue invasion, with a high rate of systemic involvement, including hematologic malignancies, hypocomplementemia, and paraproteinemia.14.

AOX etiology remains incompletely understood, although immune dysregulation and macrophage proliferation are believed to play central roles.5,8 Some case reports have suggested a genetic predisposition, supported by familial occurrences of lipid-rich histiocytic lesions such as xanthelasma palpebrarum.9 A case reported by Chen et al. described diagnostic overlap between AOX and NBX, and proposed a possible underlying genetic susceptibility.9 Unlike their case, our patient had no personal or family history of lipid disorders, which adds to the variability in presentation and underscores the need for individualized diagnostic assessment.

Treatment of AOX is not standardized and should be tailored to the severity and progression of the disease. Corticosteroids are usually the first-line therapy; however, long-term use is limited by well-known systemic toxicities, including immunosuppression, glucose intolerance, and osteoporosis.1,3,5,8 Immunosuppressive agents such as methotrexate and azathioprine have been used with variable results, while cytotoxic agents like cyclophosphamide are generally reserved for refractory or aggressive cases.15, 16, 17, 18.

In our patient, progressive orbital inflammation, eyelid disfigurement, and the need to minimize corticosteroid exposure prompted the initiation of tocilizumab, an interleukin-6 (IL-6) receptor antagonist. The rationale for its use is supported by the known role of IL-6 in the inflammatory cascade and macrophage activation. While its application in AOX specifically is still emerging, tocilizumab has demonstrated effectiveness in other orbital inflammatory diseases, such as thyroid eye disease and idiopathic orbital inflammation.19 Compared to systemic corticosteroids or conventional immunosuppressants, tocilizumab offers a more targeted therapeutic effect with a favorable safety profile. In this case, the patient achieved clinical stabilization after five infusions without adverse effects, supporting the growing role of biologic therapy in selected AOX cases.7,8,19 The PET scan findings after completion of five doses of tocilizumab support resolution of the previously observed right orbital involvement. Moreover, the absence of hypermetabolic lesions or lymphadenopathy effectively rules out systemic disease involvement at this stage.

Surgical excision of the palpebral mass was later performed at the patient's request for aesthetic reasons (Fig. 6). Although recurrence rates are reportedly high when xanthogranulomatous lesions are excised without systemic control, no recurrence has been observed to date, likely due to concurrent immunomodulatory therapy. Nevertheless, long-term surveillance remains critical.7,8,20.

This case represents an unusual presentation of adult-onset xanthogranuloma, characterized by unilateral palpebral involvement and bilateral orbital disease, a rare anatomical distribution that expands the clinical spectrum of AOX.8 Histopathological confirmation and thorough systemic work-up were essential to establish the diagnosis and exclude other xanthogranulomatous disorders. The use of tocilizumab, an IL-6 receptor antagonist, resulted in clinical stabilization and offered a corticosteroid-sparing alternative, reinforcing its emerging role in orbital inflammatory diseases.7 Although systemic involvement was not evident at the time of diagnosis, long-term surveillance remains crucial given the potential for delayed manifestations, including progression to AAPOX.8 This case underscores the importance of early recognition, multidisciplinary evaluation and individualized immunomodulatory therapy in managing this rare and heterogeneous condition.
