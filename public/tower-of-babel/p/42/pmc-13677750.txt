The paradox of high IQ in ADHD: dissociable effects on inattention and impulsivity – insights from behavior, cognition & brain in 5138 children
Authors: Ningning Liu, Gaoding Jia, Yinshan Wang, Litanshunbuer A, Zelin Mu, Meixue Wang, Meirong Pan, Haimei Li, Yufeng Wang, Qiujin Qian, Lu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile Intelligence Quotient (IQ) is widely considered a linear protective factor against attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), this paradigm fails to capture domain-specific nuances. We investigated whether non-linear relationships between IQ profiles and ADHD symptoms exist in a large cohort of children diagnosed with ADHD using a multidimensional dataset that integrates behavioral assessments, cognitive testing, and neuroimaging data. We examined a cohort of 5138 children aged 6–16 years diagnosed with ADHD, spanning a broad IQ range, and employed Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) models to characterize the relationships between IQ measures [Full-Scale IQ (FIQ), Verbal IQ (VIQ), and Performance IQ (PIQ)] and core ADHD symptoms [Total scores, Inattention (IA), and hyperactivity/impulsivity subscales (H/IMP)]. This analytical framework was subsequently extended to executive function performance and brain surface area measurements to provide convergent mechanistic evidence. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) was utilized to validate the non-linear relationship between functional impairments and IQ levels. FIQ demonstrated a linear negative association with total ADHD symptoms (β = −0.026, t = −3.27, p = 0.001). We identified a “double-edged sword” effect for IQ: while VIQ was linearly associated with lower IA scores (β = −0.015, t = −4.10, p < 0.001), it was associated with higher IMP scores (β = 0.007, t = 2.66, p = 0.008). Conversely, PIQ exhibited a dynamic developmental trajectory; the negative correlation between PIQ and IMP observed in childhood inverted into a significant U-shaped relationship in adolescence (p = 0.008). These behavioral findings were substantiated by convergent neuropsychological and neuroanatomical evidence. Finally, LPA validated a distinct “High IQ/High Impulsivity” subtype characterized by unique cognitive profiles. In conclusion, higher IQ appears to be associated with lower levels of inattention but greater impulsivity, potentially through distinct neurocognitive mechanisms. This refutes the model of IQ as a uniform protector and highlights the need to target inhibitory deficits, especially in high-IQ populations.Subject terms: ADHD, Human behaviour

Introduction

Cognitive ability is widely recognized as a factor associated with psychiatric disorders, including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [1]. Previous studies have found that a high Intelligence Quotient (IQ) could potentially mitigate the challenges of ADHD [2] and mask associated deficits [3, 4]. However, some literature theorizes that the relationship between IQ and ADHD is more nuanced [5, 6]. Paradoxically, empirical evidence shows that high intellectual abilities could actually elevate the risk of developing psychopathological conditions [7–10], including ADHD and its related features [11, 12]. Some scholars propose that an average intelligence level may represent a balanced genetic predisposition, offering both evolutionary advantages and disadvantages [6, 13]. This raises the possibility of a nonlinear relationship between IQ and ADHD symptoms, where both tails of the IQ distribution could exacerbate symptom severity.

While previous studies have demonstrated that IQ influences ADHD manifestations—particularly comparing high-IQ and average-IQ individuals [4, 14]. Comprehensive investigations into the continuous nature of this relationship have been hindered by small sample sizes and restricted age ranges. Notably, Full-scale IQ (FIQ) is a composite score derived from Verbal IQ (VIQ) and Performance IQ (PIQ), which are influenced by different cerebral functions according to previous research [15, 16]. Moreover, existing research has largely treated IQ as a monolithic construct (FIQ), overlooking the distinct contributions of its subcomponents: VIQ and PIQ. Given that VIQ and PIQ are subserved by different neural circuits, their associations with ADHD’s core symptoms—inattention (IA) and hyperactivity/impulsivity (HI)—may diverge. Furthermore, previous studies have shown a link between working memory (WM) impairment of executive function (EF) and inattentiveness [17, 18], as well as inhibitory (IB) processing impairments and hyperactivity and impulsivity in ADHD [19–21]. Similarly, the core symptoms of IA and HI are associated with different neural mechanisms [22]. If the relationship between IQ and ADHD symptoms is not linear, can we explain this intricate connection at their neurocognitive and neuroanatomical level?

In addition, the diagnostic criteria for ADHD require that symptoms result in functional impairment [23]. Previous research has suggested a link between IQ and functional impairment, indicating that higher IQ may be linked to less impairment from ADHD [6, 24]. However, the intricate interplay between IQ and symptom manifestation may imply that individuals can experience functional impairments that do not follow a simple monotonic correspondence with IQ level profiles. Addressing these gaps is critical for the precision management of ADHD across the intellectual spectrum.

To address these limitations, we comprehensively analyzed the relationship between IQ and core ADHD symptoms in a large cohort of 5138 children diagnosed with ADHD by experienced psychiatrists. Clinical assessments, including IQ, were rigorously evaluated using a standardized approach. We aimed to determine: (1) if there is indeed a non-linear relationship between IQ and ADHD symptoms; (2) whether similar non-linear patterns are present in EF performance and brain morphological features associated with inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity; (3) whether the distinct functional impairments in daily life caused by ADHD symptoms may not follow a monotonic correspondence with IQ level profiles, considering the intricate interplay between IQ and symptom manifestation.

Participants

A clinical registry from Peking University Sixth Hospital, spanning two decades (1998–2021), involved 5138 children with ADHD. The diagnoses and comorbidities followed the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) criteria. Trained and skilled psychiatrists assessed the ADHD diagnosis and comorbidities using the Clinical Diagnostic Interview Scale (CDIS) [27] through semi-structured interviews with both guardians and children. The inclusion criteria consisted of: (a) being between 6 and 16 years old; (b) having a Full-Scale intelligence Quotient (FIQ) ≥ 70; (c) no history of head trauma, neurological illness, or severe diseases; (d) having either never used stimulants or having discontinued their use for a minimum of 48 h before evaluation. Written informed consent/assent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. The study received approval from the Research Ethics Review Board of Peking University Sixth Hospital.

A total of 5138 individuals met the primary inclusion criteria for the behavioral analysis (Goal 1). Subsequent analyses (Goals 2–4) had varying sample sizes based on the availability of specialized assessment modules introduced at different stages of the registry’s evolution. 1) Behavioral Cohort (Goal 1, N = 5138): This largest cohort provided the primary data for testing the non-linear relationship between IQ (PIQ/VIQ) and ADHD symptoms (IA/HI). 2) Cognitive Subset (Goal 2, N = 3676): Standardized EF testing (Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function [BRIEF]), which includes WM and IB assessment, was fully integrated into the routine clinical intake protocol to conduct a more comprehensive assessment. 3) Neuroimaging Subset (Goal 3, N = 262): Participants underwent high-resolution structural MRI scanning initiated in the later years, with additional quality control measures and specific guardian consent for neuroimaging research. 4) Classification Subset (Goal 4, N = 1100): This sub-sample completed functional impairment testing using the NICHQ Vanderbilt ADHD Screen Assessment Scale—PARENT informant (VATS) to assess their academic and social impairments, which is similar to the Cognitive Subset. Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared across these four tiers. As shown in Table S1, the mean IQ and symptom severity (IA and HI scores) were consistent across all sub-samples, with no significant differences in age or gender distribution. This consistency confirms that the smaller sub-samples are representative of the parent discovery cohort (N = 5138), minimizing the risk of selection bias.

Clinical assessments

ADHD rating scale-IV (ADHD-RS-IV)

Guardians rated their children’s ADHD symptom severity [28]. This 18-item scale, rated on a four-point scale, assesses IA symptoms (first nine items) and HI symptoms [The last nine items, with the first five representing hyperactivity scores (H) and the last four representing impulsivity scores (IMP)]. Summing the corresponding items yields IA and HI (H/IMP) scores. The scores for all items were summed to produce a total score (TO), with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. In this study, the IA and HI Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.795 and 0.878, respectively, demonstrating good internal consistency of the instrument (refer to Table S2).

Chinese Wechsler intelligence scale III (C-WISC)

We used the C-WISC [29], which has been extensively employed in our previous research [30, 31], to assess intelligence. Trained and certified psychiatrists evaluated children according to standardized protocols, with final scores computed and cross-checked by two independent operators. The raw scores from each subtest were then converted into standard scores to calculate VIQ, PIQ, and FIQ, with higher scores indicating higher levels of intelligence [29]. In our current study, the internal consistency of the instrument was demonstrated by Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.864–0.873 for VIQ, and from 0.722–0.796 for PIQ, across different subsamples (refer to Table S2-S5).

Behavior rating inventory of executive function (BRIEF)

The BRIEF [32] consists of 86 items, each rated on a 3-point scale. Our group has confirmed the good reliability and validity of the BRIEF in China [33]. The scale is subdivided into eight subscales: BRI includes IB, shift, and emotional control; MI includes initiation, WM, planning, organizing, and monitoring. In this study, we focused on the IB (10 items, score range 10–30) and WM (10 items, score range 10–30) subscales of the BRIEF, where higher scores indicate more deficits. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for WM and IB were found to be 0.741 and 0.861, respectively, indicating a strong internal consistency of the instrument (refer to Table S3, S6). Despite the BRIEF not being tailored for ADHD patients, confirmatory factor analysis confirmed the reliability and validity of the WM and IB dimensions within the ADHD cohort (refer to Table S6-S8). Correlation analyses further revealed stronger associations between these EF dimensions and IA as well as HI, compared to other variables (refer to Table S9). Thus, the linkage between BRIEF’s WM and IB dimensions and ADHD symptoms is deemed acceptable.

NICHQ Vanderbilt ADHD screen assessment scale—PARENT informant (VATS)

VATS is among the most commonly utilized tools for evaluating symptoms and performance impairment in children with ADHD. The final eight items (Performance questions 48–55) specifically assess academic (questions 48–51) and social impairments (questions 52–55). Total scores for academic and social impairment are calculated by summing the raw scores, with higher values indicating greater functional impairment. The scale’s psychometric properties have been extensively evaluated and well-documented [34]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for academic and social impairments were 0.728 and 0.847, demonstrating good internal consistency of the instrument (refer to Table S5).

More details are in Supplementary Materials Appendix 1.

MRI imaging

T1-weighted (T1w) data for this study were acquired using two 3.0 Tesla scanners. The first scanner, a Siemens model, was located at Beijing Normal University, Beijing, China. The second scanner, a General Electric Discovery MR750 model, was located at Peking University Sixth Hospital, Beijing, China. The standard preprocessing of T1w images was conducted using the Connectome Computing System (CCS). Subsequently, we selected 34 bilateral brain regions from the Desikan–Killiany parcellation for regional analysis. Additionally, a harmonization technique named ComBat was applied to eliminate unwanted scanner variability [35, 36]. For more details, see Supplementary Materials Appendix 2 and our previous study [37].

Statistical analysis

The relationship between IQ and core symptoms of ADHD

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3; R Core Team, 2024) on Windows 11 x64 (build 26100). Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians (interquartile ranges, IQR), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

To explore potential non-linear relationships between IQ (e.g., PIQ, VIQ) components and clinical outcomes (e.g., IA, HI, TO) in individuals with ADHD, Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) regression models from the rms R-package were utilized. This method allows for flexible modeling of curvilinear associations without imposing strict parametric assumptions. The presence of non-linearity was formally assessed using a Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT) by comparing the models with only a linear IQ term to those incorporating cubic spline IQ terms. The RCS models were configured with 4 knots (positioned at recommended percentiles) to achieve a balance between model fit and simplicity [38], with sensitivity analyses performed using 3 and 5 knots.

For Covariate Adjustment and Model Parsimony, to address the potential moderation of IQ effects by developmental and biological factors, we employed a pre-specified modeling strategy [38]. Given the non-linear nature of developmental changes in ADHD, age was modeled using RCS with 3 knots as a standard covariate in all regression analyses to allow for more flexible adjustment than a simple linear term. Sex was included as a fixed factor. Subsequently, we went beyond independent covariate adjustment and conducted a global interaction test (“chunk test”) to examine potential effect modification. A full model was constructed including interactions between IQ (modeled as RCS or linear) and pre-specified covariates [i.e., rcs(Age, 3) and Sex]. Interaction terms were retained in the final model only if the global LRT was statistically significant (p < 0.05); otherwise, the more parsimonious main-effects model was adopted to avoid overfitting.

To ensure the validity of statistical inferences, we assessed the homoscedasticity assumption using the Breusch-Pagan test for all regression models [39]. Given the large sample size and potential non-constant variance in behavioral data, Heteroscedasticity-Consistent (HC3) standard errors were calculated using the sandwich and lmtest packages [40–42]. The HC3 estimator was chosen over other robust estimators (e.g., HC0 or HC1) for its conservative and reliable approximation of standard errors in the presence of heteroscedasticity, reducing the risk of inflated Type I error rates. In addition, to identify turning points, the first derivative of the fitted spline function was calculated. A turning point was defined as the value where the first derivative crossed zero.

The mechanism of the complex relationship between IQ and ADHD

Analysis of non-linear associations between IQ and EF of ADHD

Research suggests that IB impairment has been linked to impulsivity [19], while WM impairment has been linked to the symptoms of inattention in ADHD [17, 18]. Therefore, in this study, the IB and WM subscales of BRIEF were used to validate the correlation between IQ and the core symptoms of ADHD. Specifically, the IB subscale was used to assess the relationship with HI, while the WM subscale was used to assess the relationship with IA. We suspect that if a linear or non-linear association between IQ and the core symptoms of ADHD is revealed in the first step, a similar pattern between IQ and EF would be identified using the same data analysis framework.

Analysis of non-linear associations between IQ and brain surface area of ADHD

Similarly, to explore the neural basis of the association between IQ and core symptoms of ADHD revealed in the first step, we conducted a similar analysis to measure the relationship between IQ and surface area. Brain regions of interest (ROIs) were selected based on previous studies [22]. Specifically, the rostral anterior cingulate cortex (rACC), the superior frontal gyrus (SFG), and the total surface area (TotalSA) were selected to examine the association between IQ and HI. The caudal middle frontal gyrus (MFG), middle temporal gyrus (MTG), and TotalSA were selected to examine the association between IQ and IA.

IQ characteristics in profiles of functional impairment of ADHD

To investigate whether individuals might experience functional impairments that do not exhibit a straightforward, linear relationship with IQ levels, contrary to previous assumptions, the study used Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) to create IQ profiles of ADHD based on functional impairment using Mplus (Version 8.3). LPA was conducted under validated assumptions [43–45]. Six fit indices were used to evaluate the models: Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR), Bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and entropy [44]. Lower values of AIC, BIC, and aBIC indicate superior model fit, with their ‘elbow points’ (where the decrease levels off) used to determine the most parsimonious solution. A significant p-value (p < 0.05) for LMR and BLRT suggests that the K-profile model is notably better fitting than the K-1 model. LMR was chosen over BLRT to avoid excessive profile extraction in large sample [46, 47]. Additionally, Entropy values > 0.70 were considered indicative of acceptable classification accuracy. The selection of the best profile solution was guided by both substantive and statistical considerations [47, 48]. This dual emphasis ensures that the selected solution is both statistically optimal and theoretically interpretable. Lastly, the DCON command in Mplus [49] was utilized to analyze the differences in IQ and ADHD symptom scores among the identified profiles. More details are in the Supplementary Materials Appendix 3.

Sensitivity analysis

The Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedure [50] was used in all primary regression analyses to correct for multiple outcomes. Internal validation was performed by subsampling, which involved 100 iterations of random sampling at three different proportions (50%, 70%, and 90% of the total sample) to assess the stability of the findings. A stable association was defined as one where the direction and non-linearity of the IQ effect remained consistent in the majority of iterations. Additionally, two more sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) varying the number of knots in modeling IQ terms (3–5), and (2) testing model performance at different reference levels. Lastly, considering the potential impact of comorbidities, we also incorporated the presence of comorbidities as a confounding factor in our sensitivity analysis.

Results

The study included 5138 children diagnosed with ADHD, predominantly boys (82.74%) with a mean age of 117.36 ± 30.62 months. Among them, 3676 children (83.13% boys, mean age: 115.63 ± 30.38 months) underwent EF assessment, 1100 children (81.64% boys, mean age: 113.18 ± 30.49 months) were evaluated for functional impairment, and 262 children (78.62% boys, mean age: 123.56 ± 29.80 months) underwent structural MRI brain scans.

The relationship between IQ and core symptoms of ADHD

In our study, a linear FIQ model was found to be the most suitable for predicting TO scores (β = −0.026, t = −3.27, p = 0.001, adj R2 = 0.035) (Fig. 2A), suggesting that ADHD symptoms generally showed an inverse relationship with FIQ, particularly for IA (β = −0.024, t = −0.602, p < 0.001, adj R2 = 0.009) but not for HI (β = −0.0024, t = −0.042, p = 0.671, adj R2 = 0.08) (refer to Table S10). Interestingly, although marginally significant, we found a non-linear association between FIQ and IMP [χ2(2)=5.49,ptotal none−linear=0.068], indicating a more intricate developmental trajectory among the subdimensions of ADHD symptoms (refer to Figure S1A, B).

Sequential analyses were conducted to examine the relationship between PIQ/VIQ and ADHD symptoms. Similar to FIQ, VIQ was found to significantly negatively predict IA (β = −0.015, t = −4.10, p < 0.001, adj R2 = 0.005) but not HI (β = 0.0015, t = 0.29, p = 0.771, adj R2 = 0.080). However, it positively predicted IMP (β = 0.007, t = 2.66, p = 0.008, adj R2 = 0.035) (Fig. 2B, C). Similarly, although not statistically significant, PIQ negatively predicted IA (β = −0.008, t = −0.700, p = 0.460, adj R2 = 0.012). PIQ also did not predict HI (β = −0.006, t = −1.03, p = 0.304, adj R2 = 0.08). However, likelihood ratio comparisons revealed that PIQ exhibited a non-linear association with IMP [χ2(2)=5.24,ptotal none−linear=0.062] (Fig. 2D, E). Moreover, a global interaction test indicated significant interactions between PIQ and sex or age [χ29=19.74,pglobal interaction test=0.019] (refer to Table S10). Further analysis using analysis of variance (ANOVA) unveiled a significant interaction between the non-linear age term and the non-linear PIQ term [F(54,980)=2.67,pnon−linearinteraction=0.020], suggesting developmental changes in the association between PIQ and IMP. Subsequent examination through subgroup analysis based on age showed a decreasing trend in the association between PIQ and IMP in younger children (Age ~ 12 y), and transitioning to a U-shaped pattern in younger adolescents (Age 12 y ~ ) (Fig. 2F).

The mechanism of the complex relationship between IQ and ADHD

IQ and Executive function in ADHD

The study initially identified a relationship between IQ (PIQ and VIQ) and ADHD core symptoms (HI and IA). Subsequently, an examination was conducted to determine if a similar correlation exists between EF and ADHD symptoms in children. The WM and IB subscales of the BRIEF were utilized as EF proxies. The findings revealed that a linear model was the most suitable for the relationship between VIQ and WM. VIQ was a significant negative predictor (β = −0.023, t = −9.91, p < 0.001, adj R2 = 0.017) (Fig. 3A). Although the main effect of VIQ on predicting IB was not significant, there was a positive trend (β = 0.005, t = 0.37, p = 0.711, adj R2 = 0.064) (Fig. 3B), a simple slope analysis on the significant linear interaction term VIQ × age showed that children with one standard deviation below the mean age (~7.77 years old) had a significant positive impact on IB (β = 0.024, t = 4.111, p < 0.001) (Figure S1C). Concerning PIQ, a negative linear relationship with WM was observed (β = −0.022, t = −6.24, p < 0.0001, adj R2 = 0.014) (Fig. 3D). A non-linear association between PIQ and IB was found χ2(2)=12.03,ptotal none−linear=0.002 (Fig. 3E), and a global interaction test indicated significant interactions between PIQ and sex or age χ2(9)=19.00,pglobal interaction test=0.025. Furthermore, an analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed a significant interaction between the linear age term and the non-linear PIQ term F(6,3560)=2.67,plinear interaction=0.007, suggesting that the developmental modulation of the PIQ-IB relationship primarily manifests as a linear scaling of the effect size rather than a fundamental transformation of the non-linear functional form (i.e., the non-linear aspect of PIQ in predicting IB remained relatively “stable” during children’s development) (refer to Table S11).

IQ and brain surface area in ADHD

We observed a significant linear association between VIQ and rACC (β = 1.879, t = 1.98, p = 0.049, adj R2 = 0.142) (Fig. 3C). In contrast, PIQ exhibited a significant non-linear relationship with TotalSA χ2(2)=6.99,ptotal none−linear=0.030 (Fig. 3F). IQ characteristics in profiles of functional impairment of ADHD

The LPA implementation was conducted under the validated assumptions of local independence, multivariate normality, and linear collinearity, with detailed results provided in Table S13. Model fit indices were compared across 1–5 profile solutions, revealing that AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased as the number of profiles increased. The most substantial reductions in these values were observed when transitioning from the 2-profile to the 3-profile solution, suggesting a point of diminishing returns. The BLRT remained significant (p < 0.001) for all models, while the LMR-LRT became non-significant for the 4-profile model (p = 0.1004), indicating that adding a fourth profile did not notably enhance model fit over the 3-profile solution. Additionally, The 3-profile solution exhibited an acceptable Entropy of 0.762 and maintained well-distributed group sizes (ranging from 26.5%–40.5%), ensuring classification stability and clinical representativeness. Considering these statistical indicators and aiming for theoretical parsimony, the 3-profile solution was deemed the optimal model. The final profile classification is shown in Fig. 4. Mild Impairment Profile (n = 446, 40.5%): This group displayed minimal overall functional impairment. It was marked by the highest VIQ and PIQ scores, the fewest attention symptoms, but had relatively more pronounced hyperactivity/impulsivity symptoms. Severe Academic Deficit Profile (n = 362, 32.9%): Characterized by the most severe academic impairment, this profile contrasted sharply with the least impairment in social functioning. Participants in this group had the lowest VIQ and PIQ scores and exhibited the most severe inattentive symptoms. However, they showed minimal hyperactivity/impulsivity symptoms. Social-Academic Deficit Profile (n = 292, 26.5%): This group experienced moderate functional impairment in social domains and significant impairment in academic areas. Individuals in this group had intermediate levels of VIQ, PIQ, and ADHD symptoms when compared to the other profiles (Fig. 4, Table S14).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis results revealed a consistent and stable nonlinear relationship between IMP, IB, TotalSA, and PIQ (as shown in Table S15–23). In 100 repeated samplings across various subgroup analyses, IMP, IB, and TotalSA exhibited a higher proportion of nonlinear associations with PIQ compared to other indicators (see Table S21-23). The internal validation through subsampling confirmed the reproducibility of the functional form (the RCS curve shape) of these measures across different sample proportions, indicating a robust underlying relationship despite the conservative Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction adjustment failure (refer to Table S15–23 Sensitivity Analysis in the Supplementary Materials for details). Likewise, sensitivity analysis on different numbers of knots in model construction, and model performance using various reference levels to evaluate model stability exhibited high consistency with subsample examination (refer to Table S15–23).

Given the intricate nature of comorbidity types and the need to balance model complexity and interpretability, the comorbidity variable used here is simplified as a binary classification (i.e., present or absent). Similarly, model results incorporating comorbidity as an additional covariate indicated that the overall trend aligned with our primary findings (refer to Table S24–26).

Discussion

This in-depth study systematically explored the relationship between IQ and ADHD using a sizable clinical sample and a rigorous assessment methodology. Our findings reveal that while higher FIQ is generally associated with reduced ADHD symptomatology, VIQ and PIQ exhibit dissociable, domain-specific effects. Notably, VIQ demonstrates a “double-edged sword” effect: it acts as a protective factor against inattention but paradoxically increases the risk for impulsivity. Conversely, PIQ exhibited a distinct non-linear developmental path with impulsivity, transitioning from a linear decrease trend in childhood to a U-shaped pattern in adolescence. Crucially, these behavioral phenotypes are rooted in biology, supported by consistent findings from EF (IB and WM), neuroanatomical substrates, and distinct clinical subtypes identified via LPA.

Investigating the relationship between IQ and the core symptoms of ADHD is a compelling research area that has not been thoroughly explored before. Through the stringent diagnostic criteria and the analysis of a substantial dataset, we have presented compelling evidence on this topic. Our findings showed a negative correlation between FIQ and the core symptoms of ADHD, aligning with previous research [3, 4]. Notably, we did not view intelligence as a monolithic construct. By leveraging our large sample size, we demonstrated that VIQ and PIQ exert divergent influences on the core symptom dimensions of ADHD. Specifically, we found that while both VIQ and PIQ demonstrated a consistent trend toward ameliorating IA symptoms, these protective effects were lost or even reversed when predicting HI, particularly IMP. This implies that having high intelligence may not always shield against ADHD. The relationship between IQ and ADHD symptoms is intricate. Even though individuals with ADHD and high IQ may experience fewer attention issues, they may not necessarily exhibit fewer impulsivity symptoms.

The dual impact of VIQ warrants closer examination. While the expected negative correlation between VIQ and inattention was observed, the positive association between VIQ and impulsivity was counterintuitive. Previous research, primarily conducted on non-ADHD cohorts, has suggested that lower VIQ is associated with increased impulsivity [51–53]. However, this relationship has not been thoroughly examined in ADHD populations with stringent clinical diagnosis and thorough cognitive evaluations. We propose that the positive VIQ-IMP association may be explained by the multifaceted nature of impulsivity. Although traditionally linked to negative characteristics such as antisocial behavior [54], recent studies suggest that IMP in ADHD is also equally linked to heightened creativity [55]. Given the established link between verbal ability and creativity [56], the observed positive correlation between VIQ and IMP in our study may reflect a distinctive cognitive profile in ADHD, where higher VIQ coexists with specific creative-impulsive traits. Biologically, our data support this dichotomy. Higher VIQ was associated with superior WM, which likely mitigates inattention. Although not statistically significant, VIQ exhibited a trend toward exacerbating impulsivity symptoms. Neuroanatomically, our research demonstrates a positive correlation between VIQ and rACC. However, Hoogman’s study indicates that higher impulsivity symptoms are correlated with a smaller brain surface area [22]. Therefore, we propose that in ADHD, the increased impulsivity symptoms caused by VIQ do not result from the pathological basis of ADHD, but rather because high VIQ in ADHD affects other regions of the brain [57], leading to a relative weakening of inhibitory behavior. In addition, environmental reinforcement may play a role: high VIQ children often navigate environments using verbal agility, leading to faster responses that are inadvertently reinforced, strengthening impulsive style. This interpretation remains speculative. While this trend was identified in our large sample, the findings require further validation in future studies.

In contrast, the PIQ-IMP relationship revealed a distinct developmental shift. While the associations between PIQ and IB and TotalSA remained relatively stable, the link between PIQ and impulsivity transitioned from a linear pattern in childhood to a significant U-shaped trajectory in adolescence. We speculate that this shift reflects the interplay between cognitive maturation and evolving social demands, aligning with Kohlberg’s stages of moral development [58, 59]. During childhood, high PIQ appears to function as an “inhibitory buffer”, particularly in the preconventional and conventional stages [60], where children are highly sensitive to rules and social approval. Those with high PIQ may tend to internalize norms more effectively, utilizing their intelligence to resist impulsive behaviors and conform to social norms. However, as individuals reach adolescence, physiological maturation and the shift to postconventional reasoning foster greater cognitive autonomy. Adolescents with exceptionally high PIQ may exhibit more impulsivity, indicating a deliberate departure from social constraints to explore their environment and self-expression. In this context, the resurgent impulsivity at the high level of the PIQ spectrum may not represent a lack of control, but rather an active, intelligence-driven engagement with a more complex social environment, where high-IQ individuals pioneer testing and surpassing normative boundaries.

Furthermore, our findings underscore the unique phenotypic status of impulsivity. Research has suggested that while ADHD is traditionally classified into two subtypes based on the two-factor model distinguishing “IA” and “HI”, a three-factor model separating “H” from “IMP” aligns better with the data [61]. Previous studies have also consistently shown a correlation between higher intelligence and improved attention skills in individuals with ADHD. However, hyperactivity appears to be a less specific marker of pathology [2, 6, 62]. These findings underscore the unique importance of IMP, indicating that IA and IMP are distinct phenotypic entities with varying etiological sensitivities. Our data suggest that while IA is highly sensitive to cognitive compensation (IQ), IMP represents a more stubborn behavioral vulnerability that may persist or even intensify in high-IQ individuals.

In terms of daily functional impairment, we found that children with ADHD who showed less academic functional impairment tended to have higher IQ levels. However, an increase in IQ performance did not necessarily lead to a decrease in social functional impairment. Surprisingly, individuals with the least social functional impairment tended to have lower IQ scores. Their level of impairment was even lower than that of individuals with ADHD who had high IQ scores. This highlights that higher intelligence does not always result in lower functional impairment, aligning with the findings of our previous study sections. This could explain why highly intelligent individuals with ADHD often experience similar impairments as those with average intelligence who have ADHD [2, 14, 63]. Understanding these neurocognitive patterns could enhance clinical identification and tailored intervention strategies for individuals with ADHD across the IQ spectrum. Our findings indicate three distinct clinical profiles: ADHD presentations with lower IQ levels predominantly manifest through core inattentive symptoms, necessitating targeted academic interventions over social skills remediation; those within average IQ ranges exhibit intermediate symptom severity across core domains, requiring dual-focused support addressing both academic and social functioning impairments; while ADHD with higher IQ demonstrated increased hyperactive-impulsive symptoms, potentially benefiting from structured behavioral interventions for social functioning.

Limitations

A substantial sample of individuals diagnosed with ADHD across a wide range of IQ levels was used to examine the relationship between IQ and ADHD-related performance. However, it is important to note several limitations of the study. Firstly, there existed an age gap between our sample and the reference study used for ROI selection. The study by Hoogman et al. focused on individuals aged 8.5–11.9 years [22], raising uncertainty about the generalizability of the results to a broader age range of 6–16 years. Despite this discrepancy, Hoogman’s findings indicated consistent trends in brain regions for ADHD in childhood around 14 years and adolescence (15–21 years) compared to healthy controls. By adjusting for the non-linear age effects using RCS, we ensured that the identified effects are independent of age-related changes. However, potential developmental heterogeneity may still impact our results. Future investigations should consider using age-specific or functionally-defined ROIs to validate these neurodevelopmental associations. Secondly, our selection of brain regions related to inattention is based on data from the general population rather than specifically on individuals with ADHD [22]. Despite being derived from the general population, these regions exhibit significant differences between the ADHD and healthy control groups in the study. Currently, there is a lack of high-quality, large-sample studies on brain structures associated with inattention in ADHD. Therefore, we used inattention-related brain regions identified in the general population by Hoogman et al. [22]. Notably, numerous studies have pinpointed inattention-related functional brain regions using fMRI and other methods [64]. It has been proposed that symptoms like inattention may arise from various trajectories of brain development [65]. Future research could potentially provide additional evidence to support our findings based on different brain networks. Finally, few associations reached the threshold of statistical significance after FDR correction. Despite our cautious approach to these findings, we highlight their potential clinical significance. Harrell advised against strictly adhering to adjusted p-value thresholds, as this could lead to increased Type II errors, particularly in intricate models with non-linear elements [38]. Our sensitivity analyses demonstrate the robustness of these patterns, suggesting that these non-linear trends likely present authentic developmental processes rather than random fluctuations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study challenges the traditional narrative of intelligence as a monolithic, linear protective factor in ADHD. Leveraging a substantial clinical cohort and multi-modal assessments, we demonstrate that while higher IQ shields against inattention, the relationship between IQ and impulsivity is far more complex and domain-specific. Specifically, we identified a “double-edged sword” effect: VIQ appears to safeguard against inattention yet simultaneously exacerbates impulsivity. Conversely, PIQ may exhibit a distinct developmental trajectory, shifting from a protective buffer in childhood to a U-shaped risk factor in adolescence. This research emphasizes the importance of considering domain-specific profiles in clinical assessments beyond just overall IQ scores. For high-IQ children with ADHD, interventions should focus on behavioral inhibition training and social skills rather than solely on inattention and academic support.
