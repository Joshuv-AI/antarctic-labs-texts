High-intensity interval training modulates neural oscillations and inter-regional connectivity in college students
Authors: Huipeng Lei, Zixuan Guo, Talifu Zikereya, Lulu Chen, Jiazheng Peng, Zhongshu Shao, Kaixuan Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-intensity interval training (HIIT) has been proposed as a time-efficient strategy to enhance brain health, yet its longer-term neural signatures remain unclear.MethodsIn a randomized, assessor-blinded, parallel-group study, 32 healthy undergraduates (18–25 years) completed either 8 weeks of supervised HIIT (30 min/session, 3 sessions/week) or a usual-activity control. Body composition was measured before and after the intervention, and maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max) was assessed using the 20-m shuttle run test (20 m SRT). Resting-state EEG was recorded at baseline and post-intervention. Power spectral density (PSD) and phase-based connectivity were quantified across canonical frequency bands.ResultsHIIT increased VO2max and skeletal muscle mass and reduced body fat percentage compared with baseline, while no meaningful changes were observed in controls. Resting-state EEG showed increased delta-theta-alpha power and inter-regional phase synchronization, alongside reduced beta-gamma power/connectivity.Conclusions8 weeks of HIIT were associated with frequency-specific modulation of cortical oscillations and large-scale functional coupling at rest. These findings suggest that time-efficient exercise paradigms may promote neural network reorganization, with potential benefits for cognitive and motor function.

Introduction

Insufficient physical activity is a global health concern, associated with increased risks of cardiovascular and cognitive decline [1]. This has driven the demand for effective, time-efficient exercise strategies. High-intensity interval training (HIIT), characterized by short bursts of high-intensity exercise (> 85% HRmax) interspersed with short recovery periods, has emerged as a time-efficient alternative to traditional endurance training and can elicit comparable or superior cardiopulmonary benefits in less time [2]. Beyond improving physical health, high-intensity exercise can acutely influence central physiological adaptations(e.g., lactate and catecholamine signaling, autonomic balance, neurotrophic and cerebrovascular responses), which may in turn shape large-scale neural dynamics [3–7]. Furthermore, physiological adaptations, such as improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness, indexed by VO2max, have been theoretically and empirically linked to altered electrocortical activity and functional network plasticity, suggesting that peripheral physiological improvements directly map onto measurable neural responses [8]. However, the neural mechanisms underlying these effects remain poorly understood, particularly after sustained interventions.

Acute bouts of exercise can transiently modulate intrinsic brain dynamics, including resting-state neural oscillations and connectivity in healthy adults, although the direction and magnitude of effects vary with exercise mode, intensity, and timing [9–12]. Whether long-term HIIT can induce reproducible, frequency-specific modulation of brain activity and inter-regional coupling at rest in healthy young adults remains largely unknown.

Resting-state electroencephalography (EEG) offers a non-invasive method to assess such brain dynamics [13]. Power spectral density (PSD) captures frequency-specific oscillatory power, while phase-based metrics such as the phase-locking value (PLV) index inter-regional coupling strength. Together, these metrics provide sensitive markers of neurophysiological function and plasticity [14].

Here, we investigated the effects of an 8-week HIIT intervention on resting-state brain activity in healthy young adults. By analyzing PSD and PLV before and after the intervention, we aimed to determine whether HIIT modulates spectral power and inter-regional phase coupling. Based on prior exercise-EEG literature and physiological responses to high-intensity exercise, we hypothesized frequency-specific modulation characterized by higher low-frequency (delta-alpha) power/connectivity and lower high-frequency (beta-gamma) metrics at rest. Given the limited availability of long-term HIIT-EEG data in this population, the EEG analyses were considered partially exploratory.

Methods and materials

Participants

Sample size estimation (Cohen’s d = 0.447, 80% power, α = 0.05) indicated a minimum of 30 participants. Because prior long-term HIIT studies reporting resting-state EEG outcomes in comparable samples are scarce, the effect size was selected as an assumed medium effect based on the broader exercise–EEG intervention literature [9]. The power calculation was anchored to the primary physiological outcome (VO2max) and the group×time interaction. We initially recruited 36 undergraduates (18–25 years) from China University of Geosciences (Beijing). Four were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria or withdrawing, leaving 32 participants (28 males, 4 females) for final analysis.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) no regular exercise or dietary control for ≥ 6 months; (2) failure to meet WHO physical-activity guidelines (≥ 150 min/week moderate or ≥ 75 min/week vigorous activity); (3) BMI 18–24 kg/m²; and (4) no history of neurological, cardiovascular, psychiatric, or physical disorders, and normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Participants provided written informed consent. Randomization was performed by an independent coordinator. Outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group allocation. Due to the nature of the intervention, training supervisors and participants were aware of group assignments. This study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional ethics committee of China University of Geosciences, Beijing (Approval number: CUGB-EC-202511).

Experimental procedure

Sample size estimation (Cohen’s d = 0.447, 80% power, α = 0.05) indicated a minimum of 30 participants. Participants were randomly assigned to HIIT (n = 16) or Control (n = 16) groups. Baseline testing was performed 5 days before the intervention and repeated within 48 h after its completion. Assessments included body composition (InBody 570, InBody Co., Seoul, South Korea), the 20-meter shuttle run test (20 m SRT), and resting-state EEG recordings (Fig. 1). EEG recordings were collected individually within the baseline and post-test windows. Where possible, participants were tested at a similar time of day to reduce circadian effects.

Throughout the intervention, all participants completed dietary records and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) to ensure that their usual dietary habits and daily routines were maintained, apart from the exercise regimen.

HIIT program

The HIIT intervention consisted of fully supervised on-site training sessions conducted in a dedicated exercise room on campus. Participants in the HIIT group trained three times per week at a fixed time (6:00 PM) for 8 consecutive weeks. Each session lasted approximately 30 min and comprised three components: a 5-min warm-up, a 20-min interval block alternating 40 s of high-intensity exercise (85–90% HRmax) with 20 s of active recovery (60–75% HRmax), and a 5-min cool-down. During the first two weeks, participants performed bodyweight resistance exercises to establish baseline fitness and reduce injury risk. From week 3 onward, training incorporated a progressive bodyweight high-intensity circuit (e.g., burpees, squat jumps, mountain climbers, and high knees) to help maintain the target heart-rate zones.

All sessions were delivered by two certified trainers with over four years of experience. Attendance was recorded at every session. HR was continuously monitored using Polar H10 devices; if a participant’s HR exceeded 95% HRmax, trainers instructed them to reduce intensity. Coaches provided continuous instruction and feedback throughout each session to ensure protocol fidelity and safety. For each session, the HR trace was reviewed in real time to confirm that work intervals reached the prescribed intensity (85–90% HRmax) and that recovery intervals remained within 60–75% HRmax. Participants in the Control group were instructed to maintain their usual daily activities and to avoid starting any new structured exercise program during the 8-week period. They were contacted weekly and completed the IPAQ to screen for major changes in activity levels; any reported deviations were documented and discussed with the participant.

Cardiorespiratory fitness was assessed using the standardized 20 m SRT [15]. Participants were required to run continuously between two parallel lines 20 m apart, following an audio pacing signal (Beep Test v3.0, Sports International). EEG data acquisition

Resting-state EEG was recorded using a 32-channel cap positioned according to the international 10–20 system (BioSignal Technologies). Signals from 29 scalp electrodes were continuously recorded with Cz as the online reference. To avoid confusion, we used the modern 10–20 nomenclature (e.g., T7/T8 and P7/P8, corresponding to T3/T4 and T5/T6 in older naming). Impedances were maintained below 5 kΩ, and data were sampled at 500 Hz. Participants sat quietly in a dimly lit room and underwent a 5-min eyes-closed recording.

EEG data analysis

Data preprocessing and analysis were performed in MATLAB using the EEGLAB and HERMES toolboxes. Preprocessing followed standard recommendations to preserve data rank and avoid introducing artificial correlations. Steps were: (1) band-pass filtering at 1–50 Hz (Butterworth) and notch filtering at 50 Hz to remove line noise; (2) initial visual inspection to remove segments with gross movement artifacts or electrode drifts; (3) independent component analysis (ICA) to identify and remove ocular, cardiac, and muscle-related components (including components consistent with electromyographic(EMG) activity contamination, which is important for gamma-band analyses); (4) identification of persistently noisy channels and spherical spline interpolation after ICA; (5) re-referencing to the common average. After preprocessing, data were segmented into 2-s epochs and epochs with residual artifacts were rejected using a combination of automated amplitude screening and visual inspection.

PSD was computed for delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (30–50 Hz) bands using Welch’s method. PSD values were expressed as power spectral density (µV²/Hz) and were log10-transformed for statistical testing to improve normality and reduce inter-individual variability. PSD values were averaged across frontal (F3/Fz/F4), central (C3/Cz/C4), and posterior (P3/Pz/P4) regions (Fig. 2). The frontal/central/posterior regions were defined a priori using midline and adjacent lateral electrodes to capture a canonical anterior–posterior gradient while limiting the number of statistical comparisons and improving interpretability in a modest sample [9].

Statistical analysis

All continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. For baseline characteristics, between-group differences in continuous variables were evaluated using independent-sample t-tests. For intervention effects, a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was used to evaluate group×time interactions. Sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test; when violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. When significant interactions were observed, Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc comparisons were performed to assess within- and between-group differences at each time point.

For frequency-band-specific EEG outcomes, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons across frequency bands and brain regions. All analyses were conducted in SPSS 21.0, and two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Description of the participants

Descriptive statistics of participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. Baseline morphological and physiological characteristics did not differ significantly between groups (all P > 0.05). Within-group analyses revealed that, relative to pre-intervention values, the HIIT group showed significant increases in skeletal muscle mass and VO2max, accompanied by a reduction in body fat percentage (all P < 0.05). In contrast, the Control group showed increases in body weight and body fat percentage, and BMI at post-intervention (all P < 0.05). Between-group comparisons indicated that VO2max was marginally higher in the HIIT group than in the Control group at follow-up (P = 0.050).

HIIT modulates the power spectral density (PSD) of multiple bands

Figures 4A-C illustrate the electrode location and resting-state signal acquisition, and representative PSD changes. Following the intervention, the HIIT group exhibited significant frequency- and region-specific alterations in PSD: delta-PSD increased significantly at the frontal and posterior sites (frontal, t = 10.374, P = 0.009; posterior, t = 4.676, P = 0.043); theta-PSD in the posterior region was also significantly higher post-intervention (t = -5.648, P = 0.030); alpha-PSD increased at both frontal (t = -7.766, P = 0.016) and central sites (t = -29.035, P = 0.022); beta-PSD decreased significantly at frontal (t = -5.769, P = 0.029) and central sites, (t = -4.333, P = 0.049); gamma-PSD at central site significantly reduced (t = 15.386, P = 0.041). In contrast, the Control group showed no significant changes in PSD across any frequency band or region (all > 0.05). The mean spectral power values for frontal, central, and posterior regions are summarized in Table 2.

Between-group analysis revealed consistent PSD differences between the HIIT and Control groups (Fig. 4D), with delta activity differing at frontal and posterior sites, theta at the central region, alpha across frontal, central, and posterior regions, beta at the posterior region, and gamma spanning frontal, central, and posterior regions. Collectively, these findings indicate that HIIT selectively modulates cortical oscillatory dynamics across multiple frequency bands and distributed brain regions.

PLV analysis

The PLV is a widely used index of neural synchronization that quantifies the phase relationship between pairs of EEG electrodes within the same frequency band. PLV values range from 0 (no synchrony) to 1 (complete synchrony) and approache 1 when the phase angles of two signals remain consistently aligned over time [17].

As shown in Fig. 5A, the most prominent post-intervention changes occurred in the alpha band, with increased functional connectivity between frontal and central regions (Z = -1.913, P = 0.050 (marginal)). Connectivity between frontal and posterior regions also increased in the delta (Z = -2.179, P = 0.023) and theta bands (Z = -2.017, P = 0.044). By contrast, functional connectivity between frontal and central regions decreased in the beta (Z = -1.913, P = 0.017) and gamma bands (Z = -1.913, P = 0.050 (marginal)).

As illustrated in Fig. 5B, post-intervention PLV values in the theta (Z = − 2.664, P = 0.007) and alpha bands (Z = − 4.867, P < 0.001) were significantly higher in the HIIT group than in the Control group, whereas the gamma band showed a significant decrease (Z = 4.081, P = 0.015).

Discussion

This study examined whether 8 weeks of HIIT are associated with changes in resting-state EEG oscillations and functional connectivity in healthy young adults. PSD analysis showed increased low-frequency (delta, theta, and alpha) power and decreased high-frequency (beta and gamma) power in several regions. In parallel, PLV analysis showed stronger connectivity in low-frequency bands and weaker connectivity in high-frequency bands, including higher alpha-band connectivity between frontal and central regions. Overall, these findings suggest that HIIT may modulate intrinsic neural activity and functional coupling patterns.

Our results partially align with prior work showing that acute or short-term exercise can modulate low-frequency power, including alpha activity, which has been linked to arousal regulation and sensorimotor readiness [10–12]. Differences in exercise duration, intensity, age, and health status likely contribute to heterogeneous findings across studies [9–12]. Prior research also consistently showed that acute high-intensity exercise can alter brain mechanical properties and cognition, that exercise-related neural plasticity has been observed in both neuroimaging and translational models, and that exercise can modulate motor-system function and dopaminergic markers relevant to brain adaptability [18–23]. Specifically, the present increase in low-frequency activity may reflect a training-related shift in resting cortical state, but the available literature does not yet support a single mechanistic interpretation. The decreases observed in beta and gamma activity should be interpreted more cautiously. Beta- and gamma-band rhythms have been linked to motor control, action organization, and cognitive processing in prior work [24–31], but gamma-band estimates derived from scalp EEG are particularly vulnerable to residual EMG contamination [32, 33]. Therefore, our high-frequency results should be regarded as preliminary and in need of confirmation in larger samples and with complementary methods. Because the present study did not include behavioral or cognitive outcomes, these EEG findings should not be taken as direct evidence of improved cognition or network efficiency, but rather as neurophysiological changes that may help inform future mechanistic studies.

PLV analysis suggested that HIIT modulated inter-regional phase consistency, with stronger low-frequency coupling and weaker high-frequency coupling after training. Because PLV at the sensor level can be influenced by volume conduction, these results are best interpreted as changes in scalp-level phase consistency rather than definitive reconfiguration of source-level networks. Nevertheless, the pattern of fronto-central and fronto-posterior low-frequency coupling is broadly consistent with the idea that exercise can influence large-scale functional integration in a frequency-dependent manner [9]. Future studies using source reconstruction or connectivity metrics less sensitive to zero-lag coupling (e.g., imaginary coherence) will be important to clarify the neuroanatomical specificity of these effects.

Although exercise is often associated with cognitive and affective benefits at the population level [34–37], the magnitude of these effects likely depends on exercise modality, dose, and participant characteristics. Previous work has also suggested that functional high-intensity exercise can acutely improve working memory, that cerebrovascular regulation may represent one pathway linking exercise to brain dynamics, and that exercise training may support memory function in other populations [38–40]. However, the behavioral and cognitive outcomes were not assessed in this study, we do not attribute the observed EEG changes to specific cognitive processes or performance gains. Instead, our results provide electrophysiological evidence of frequency-specific modulation after HIIT that motivates future work combining neural and behavioral endpoints.

Limitations and future work

This study has several limitations. First, the sex distribution was unbalanced (28 males, 4 females). This limits generalizability to female populations and prevents analysis of potential sex-based differences. Second, cognitive outcomes were not directly assessed, so we cannot link EEG changes to behavioral improvement. Third, the 20 m SRT was stopped when participants reached the age-predicted maximum heart rate (220 − age) for safety; this criterion may underestimate true maximal HR, VO₂max, and relative training intensity for some individuals. Fourth, although the Control group was monitored by weekly contact and IPAQ, daily activity outside the intervention was not fully controlled. Fifth, we did not strictly control for prior sleep quality or caffeine intake on the day of recording, which can influence resting-state EEG. Finally, gamma-band results should be interpreted cautiously because of possible residual EMG contamination despite ICA-based cleaning. In addition, PLV can be influenced by volume conduction; therefore, our connectivity results should be interpreted as phase consistency at the sensor level rather than direct evidence of source-level coupling. Third, although session attendance and heart-rate monitoring were implemented to support intervention fidelity, we did not predefine a quantitative adherence metric (e.g., time-in-zone summaries) for reporting; future work should report standardized compliance indicators for both intervention and control activity.

Future work should include larger and more diverse cohorts, incorporate longitudinal cognitive testing, and use complementary neuroimaging/physiological measures (e.g., autonomic indices or cerebrovascular measures) to better link exercise dose to neural dynamics. Such efforts will be essential for strengthening causal inference and improving generalizability.

Conclusion

Our findings show that an 8-week HIIT intervention was associated with frequency-specific changes in resting-state EEG power and large-scale functional connectivity in young adults. These results suggest that EEG measures may be useful for tracking exercise-related modulation of intrinsic brain activity. Future studies should include balanced cohorts and behavioral outcomes to clarify the functional significance of these changes.
