CCL28 promotes progression of hepatocellular carcinoma through PDGFD-regulated MMP9 and VEGFA pathways
Authors: Youyi Liu, Xingyi Chen, Xiading Zhang, Jingrou Guo, Yifei Tang, Cheng Jin, Minchen Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a major global health concern with limited therapeutic options and poor prognosis. Chemokines have emerged as critical regulators in the progression and metastasis of HCC. This study aims to investigate the mechanisms involved in CCL28-promoted progression of HCC and provide novel therapeutic targets for HCC treatment. Relationship between CCL28 expression and HCC progression were investigated by bioinformatic analysis and immunohistochemical staining assays. CCK-8, Transwell, and colony formation assay were conducted to explore the impact of CCL28 on the growth, migration and invasion of HCC cells. Quantitative real-time PCR and western blotting assays were performed to learn potential molecular mechanisms underlying the transformation of HCC driven by CCL28. The results showed that there was a direct link between increased CCL28 levels and the advancement of HCC, leading to a worse outcome. CCL28 significantly augmented malignant transformation of HCC cells, containing proliferation, migration, invasion, and clonogenicity, via activation of PDGFD-regulated MMP9 and VEGFA pathways. CCL28 emerges as a pivotal contributor to HCC tumorigenesis, propelling HCC development through the PDGFD signaling pathway. Our findings unveil potential therapeutic targets for HCC treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01185-9.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), accounting for 85 − 90% of primary liver tumors, is now ranked as the third deadliest cancer due to its high mortality rate, frequent recurrence, aggressive invasion, and grim prognosis [1, 2]. Currently, surgical resection remains the mainstay and the most effective treatment for HCC, but it’s not suitable for the patients with advanced stages, which are major challenges in the management of HCC [3]. The success of tumor immunotherapy in treating various advanced cancers has led to a revolution in cancer treatment strategies, with immune checkpoint inhibitors playing a pivotal role [4]. A comprehensive understanding of the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is crucial for the effective implementation of immunotherapy and combination therapy in advanced cases.

Numerous studies in both animals and humans suggest that chemokines play crucial roles in the growth and advancement of different types of cancers [5], including stimulating growth [6] promoting blood vessel formation [7], avoiding detection by the immune system [8], aiding cancer stem cells [9], triggering spread and migration [10], and causing genetic instability [11]. Chemokines are a family of cytokines that direct the movement of immune cells to sites of inflammation or injury by binding to their corresponding receptors [12–14]. They converge infiltration of multiple immune cells in chronic hepatitis, profoundly influence the hepatic microenvironment and mediate the development of HCC through various pathways [15]. CCL28, known as mucosae-associated epithelial chemokine (MEC), is a member of the CC chemokine subfamily [16]. Its elevated expression in tumor hypoxia sites has been associated with the promotion of tumor progression and metastasis in various cancer types. [17]. Studies have shown that CCL28 is significantly elevated in pancreatic tumors, and promotes stellate cell infiltration through a concentration gradient, which in turn induces inflammatory fibrosis and affects the malignant progression and metastasis of pancreatic cancer [18]. CCL28 is also highly expressed in hypoxic sites of ovarian tumors, and promotes tumor growth and angiogenesis, ultimately leading to poor prognosis of patients [19]. Zhou et al. have discovered that CCL28 is upregulated in HCC and significant in the migration and invasion of HCC induced by hypoxia. However, the specific mechanisms involved remains unclear [20].

This study utilized bioinformatics to analyze the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database in order to investigate the correlation between CCL28 levels and the advancement and outlook of HCC. Immunohistochemical staining (IHC) was then used to validate the expression of CCL28 in HCC tumor tissues and adjacent parecancerous liver tissues. Subsequently, a series of in vitro experiments, including CCK-8, wound healing, Transwell, and colony formation assay, were conducted to evaluate the effect of CCL28 on the malignant characteristics of HCC cells. Furthermore, the underling molecular mechanism of CCL28 on HCC progression were explored. The results indicated that CCL28 enhanced the proliferation and migration of HCC via the PDGFD pathway, suggesting novel approaches and targets for treating HCC.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

RNA-sequencing data and clinical information for HCC (371 cases) were obtained from the TCGA database and analyzed by R software (v4.0.3.) to explore the levels of CCL28 expression in both HCC tissues and noncancerous normal liver tissues, as well as the correlation between CCL28 levels and the overall survival (OS) of patients with HCC. Based on the expression levels of CCL28, the data were divided into CCL28 high-expression group (CCL28high) and CCL28 low-expression group (CCL28low). The TIDE algorithm was utilized to forecast the potential immune response to therapy in HCC patients with varying levels of CCL28 expression. CCL28 expression levels in various HCC cell lines were obtained from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE). The TCGA database was also employed to identify the positively correlated genes co-expressed with CCL28 in HCC, and the correlations were assessed by Pearson correlation analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using R software. P-value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Clinical samples

A total of 30 pairs of HCC samples and their adjacent paracancerous liver tissues were obtained from the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangnan University (Wuxi, China). The sample collections were in accordance with consensus agreements and were approved by the Medical Ethical Committee at Jiangnan University. Prior to involvement in this study, every patient provided written consent. The clinical samples underwent an IHC assay following the International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research with Human Participants.

Immunohistochemistry assay

Liver tissue samples were fixed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into thin slices. Then, the liver paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated and treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide, followed by being boiled in Tris/EDTA Antigen Retrieval Solution (pH 9.0) for 2 min to retrieve the antigen. Fifteen percent goat serum was used to prevent non-specific antibody binding for a duration of 30 min. The slides were treated with Anti-CCL28 antibody (1:200, ab232837, Abcam) and incubated overnight at 4 °C for IHC staining. Following PBS washing, the sections were incubated with secondary antibody conjugated with HRP for 30 min, then subjected to DAB staining. Finally, the parts were stained again with haematoxylin. High-resolution digital images of the stained tissue sections are captured using a microscope equipped with a slide scanner. Image J software was utilized to evaluate the IHC quantifications.

Cell culture and transfection

The human HCC cell lines, HepG2 (SCSP-510) and Li-7 (SCSP-5062), were kindly provide by Stem Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cell lines were validated by STR profiling and passaged in our laboratory for fewer than 6 months after resuscitation. The cells were cultured in H-DMEM (D5796, Sigma) supplemented with 10% FBS (900–108, GEMINI) at 37 °C and CO2 (5%).

The human CCL28 cDNA was amplified using PCR (Table S1) and inserted into the pcDNA3.1 (-) vector following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer (V790-20, Invitrogen). Human PDGFD small interfering RNA (siPDGFD) and negative control small interfering RNA (siNC) were purchased from the GENEWIZ Incorporated (Table S2). Transfection experiments were conducted with Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the provided guidelines.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from each sample using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) and then converted to cDNA using the One-Step RT-PCR Kit (CW2020M, CoWin Biosciences). SYBR Green-based real-time PCR (11201ES08, Yeasen Biotechnology and LightCycler 480 II, Roche) was used to measure mRNA expression levels, with β-actin serving as the internal control gene. QPCR data were analyzed using relative quantification based on the 2−ΔΔCt algorithm. The primers sequences were listed in Table S3.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

3000 cells were added to each well of 96-well plates, and their growth was measured at 24 h, 36 h, and 48 h using the CCK-8 reagent manual (FH783, DOJINDO). The microplate reader (Multiskan GO, Thermo) was used to measure the optical density at 450 nm in each well.

Transwell assays

Transwell assay with or without Matrigel (356,234, BD Biosciences) was used to investigate cell invasion and migration, respectively. In a nutshell, 100,000 cells were placed in the upper chamber (3422, Corning) with or without 200 μg/mL Matrigel, while the lower chamber had medium with 20% FBS as the chemoattractant. After a 48 h incubation period, the cells that had traversed the membrane were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and enumerated under a microscope (Olympus).

Colony formation assay

To evaluate the clonogenic potential of HCC cells with different expression level of CCL28, colony formation assays were conducted. HCC cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 200 cells per well. The medium was replaced every three days with fresh medium. After 14 days of incubation, the colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 min. The stained colonies were washed with distilled water and air-dried. Colonies consisting of 50 or more cells were counted under a light microscope. The colony formation efficiency was calculated as the number of colonies divided by the number of seeded cells, multiplied by 100%.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed to analyze the expression levels of proteins involved in the PDGFD-regulated MMP9 and VEGFA pathways. HCC cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, Switzerland). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove debris. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% polyacrylamide gels and then transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST buffer (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were appropriately trimmed before antibody incubation according to the molecular weight of the antibodies to facilitate different antibody incubations. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CCL28 (18,214–1-AP, Proteintech), PDGFD (14,075–1-AP, Proteintech), VEGFA (26,381–1-AP, Proteintech), MMP9 (10,375–1-AP, Proteintech), and GAPDH (AF1186, Beyotime) (used as a loading control) diluted 1:1000 in TBST containing 5% BSA. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). The relative protein expression levels were normalized to GAPDH.

Statistical analysis

Each trial was repeated at least three times, and statistical analysis was carried out with the help of SPSS (26.0) and R (4.1.0) software. The results of all bar graphs are presented as mean (−X) ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test was utilized to compare two groups, while one-way ANOVA was employed for comparing multiple groups in data that follows a normal distribution. For non-normally distributed data, non-parametric tests were employed. Descriptive statistics such as sample mean (−X) and SD, or median and interquartile range, were used when reporting the results. Statistical significance was determined at P < 0.05 level for assessing differences.

Results

Elevated CCL28 expression correlates with HCC progression

Clinical information and mRNA expression data were downloaded from the TCGA database, which included 371 samples of HCC and 50 samples of normal liver tissues (Table S4). Out of the 371 HCC samples, 366 had tumor pathology data, with 55 cases of well-differentiated tumors (grade 1, G1), 177 cases of moderately differentiated tumors (grade 2, G2), and 134 cases of poorly differentiated and undifferentiated tumors (grade 3, G3). Analysis with R software (v4.0.3) showed a notable increase in CCL28 expression in HCC tumor tissues compared to normal liver tissues (Fig. 1A), and the levels of CCL28 showed a clear up-regulation with increasing malignancy grade of the tumors (Fig. 1B). Based on the expression levels of CCL28, the samples were divided into CCL28 high-expression group (CCL28high) and CCL28 low-expression group (CCL28low), consisting of 186 and 185 samples, respectively. Result of the TIDE algorithm indicated that CCL28high group had notably higher TIDE scores than the CCL28low group (Fig. 1C, Table S5). This finding suggested a correlation between high CCL28 level and poor prognosis in HCC patients treated with immune checkpoint blockade. Survival curves using Kaplan–Meier method were created for patients based on their CCl28 expression levels, dividing them into top 25% and bottom 25% groups (n = 93 in each group). The findings indicated a strong association between elevated levels of CCL28 and unfavorable outcomes in patients with HCC (Fig. 1D). Results of CCLE database analysis indicated a direct relationship between the level of CCL28 expression and the differentiation status of HCC cell lines, as shown in Fig. 1E. CCL28 promotes the malignant transformation of HCC cell line

In order to examine the impact of CCL28 on the advancement of HCC, various in vitro experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of CCL28 on the proliferation, migration, invasion, and formation of clones in HCC cells. The HepG2 cell line, expressed the lowest level of CCL28 among the HCC cell lines (Fig. 1E), known for its relatively lower malignancy compared to other HCC cell lines, was chosen as the primary model for this particular study. Compared with control group, the CCK-8 assays showed that upregulation of CCL28 enhanced the proliferation rate of HepG2 cells significantly (Fig. 2A–B). The results of wound healing and Transwell assays showed that increasing CCL28 levels promoted the migration and invasion of HepG2 cells, as illustrated in Fig. 2C, D. Moreover, the colony formation assay showed that the clone number of HepG2 cells was markedly increased by upregulating CCL28, which also indicated that upregulation of CCL28 promoted the proliferation of HepG2 cells (Fig. 2E). CCL28 enhances proliferation and migration of HCC cells via the PDGFD signaling pathway

In order to demonstrate the role of CCL28/PDGFD pathway in promoting proliferation, migration and invasion of HCC, a set of assays were conducted using PDGFD siRNA and CCL28 upregulated HCC cells. The results of CCK-8 assay indicated that silencing PDGFD reversed the enhanced proliferation phenomenon in CCL28 upregulated HCC cells (Fig. 4A, B). Discussion

Prior research has shown the significant functions of CCL28 in the growth and advancement of different types of cancers via both intrinsic and extrinsic mechanisms of tumor cells [21]. CCL28 promotes the progression of breast cancer by activating the ERK/MAPK pathway, which inhibits cell death and promotes metastasis [22]. By encouraging the infiltration of regulatory T cells, CCL28 alters the tumor microenvironment, resulting in the proliferation and migration of pancreatic cancer cells [17]. Researches have also demonstrated that CCL28 contributes to the tumor growth and migration of HCC via cell–cell interactions, especially ligand-receptor interactions [23, 24]. However, the tumor cell-intrinsic mechanisms of CCL28 on HCC have rarely been reported.

This study revealed that CCL28 expression was increased in HCC tumor samples compared to normal liver tissues, and levels rose with advancing tumor stage. Additional analysis forecasted that elevated levels of CCL28 were linked to an unfavorable outcome in HCC individuals who underwent treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Immune checkpoint inhibitors work by blocking inhibitory signals that prevent T cells from attacking cancer cells, thereby reactivating the immune response against tumors [25]. For example, nivolumab and pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) have been approved for the treatment of HCC following the demonstration of their efficacy in clinical trials [26]. However, our findings suggested that high CCL28 expression might be associated with resistance to these therapies. The presence of elevated CCL28 levels might contribute to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, potentially leading to reduced effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitors. CCL28 could enhance the recruitment of regulatory Tregs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which are known to inhibit antitumor immunity and promote tumor progression [27]. Future research should focus on understanding the mechanisms by which CCL28 influences the immune microenvironment and its interactions with immune checkpoint pathways.

Our findings also demonstrated that the elevated expression of CCL28 was correlated with HCC progression. Following in vitro experiments revealed that the overexpression of CCL28 markedly enhanced the proliferation and migration of HCC cells. This indicated that CCL28 might mediated HCC progression through a tumor cell-intrinsic mechanism. To clearly define the potential mechanisms, Pearson correlation analysis of co-expression relationship between CCL28 and related gene in HCC was performed based on TCGA database, which showed a positively correlation between CCL28 and PDGFD.

The PDGF family, containing four different polypeptide chains (PDGFA, PDGFB, PDGFC, and PDGFD), have been studied intensely since they were first discovered in the 1970s [28, 29]. PDGFs exerts their oncogenic effects through the activation of two receptors, PDGFR-α and PDGFR-β, triggering a series of downstream signaling pathways, such as PI3K, Akt, NF-κB, ERK, etc. [30–32]. Interestingly, different member of PDGFs promotes carcinogenesis through different targets [33]. Numerous research studies have shown that PDGFD is crucial in the advancement of human cancers through its control of cell growth, invasion, blood vessel formation, and spread [34]. Studies have shown that PDGFD enhances tumor development and spread by increasing MMP9 expression, a key element in promoting cancer invasion and metastasis through the breakdown of basement membrane collagen and blood vessel remodeling [35, 36]. PDGFD have also been found to regulate the VEGF signaling in various cancer types, while the significant role of VEGF signaling in angiogenesis is unquestioned [37, 38]. The current research revealed that CCL28 enhanced proliferation, migration and invasion of HCC cells, while also increasing the levels of PDGFD, MMP9, and VEGFA. While the knockdown of PDGFD with siRNA completely abolished these changes. Therefore, it suggested that CCL28 might enhanced the progression of HCC via PDGFD-regulated MMP9 and VEGFA pathways. The specific mechanism deserved further study.

In conclusion, we revealed that overexpression of CCL28 promoted HCC cells proliferation, migration and invasion through activating PDGFD-regulated MMP9 and VEGFA pathway. Targeting CCL28 may effectively suppress tumor growth and metastasis in HCC. It provides a potential therapeutic strategy for clinical treatment of HCC. Further studies of these specific molecular mechanisms are worth considering in the future.
