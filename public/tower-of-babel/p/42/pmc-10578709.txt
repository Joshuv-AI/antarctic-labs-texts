Effect of presbyopic correction lens on humphrey visual field testing in patients with multifocal intraocular lens
Authors: Naris Kitnarong, Sakaorat Petchyim, Pennapar Jampathong, Isaraporn Treesit, Arnan Limmahachai, Tanakorn Aurboonsong
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Most previous studies on the effects of multifocal intraocular lenses (MIOL) on Humphrey visual field (HVF) used presbyopic corrective lens (PC). There has been insufficient data from patients with MIOL performing HVF with and without PC. This study aimed to determine the effect of PC on HVF testing in patients with MIOL. This was a prospective, comparative crossover study at the Department of Ophthalmology, Faculty of Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University between June 2015 and July 2018. Early-stage glaucomatous and non-glaucomatous pseudophakic patients with 2 models of MIOL completed HVF testing with and without PC. Mean deviation (MD), pattern standard deviation (PSD), and reliability indices (fixation losses, false positives, and false negatives) were compared. Ninety-four eyes of 59 patients were enrolled. After excluding patients with confirmed unreliable visual field, 82 eyes of 51 patients (male, 22; female, 29) with a mean age of 65.4 years were included in the study. Forty-nine eyes were non-glaucoma and 33 eyes were early-stage glaucoma. Forty-four eyes received TECNIS ZM900 and 38 eyes received Acrysof IQ ReSTOR SN6AD1. The overall MD standard deviation was −3.5 (2.1) with PC and −3.1 (2.1) without PC (P = .01). The overall PSD standard deviation was 2.8 (1.5) with PC and 2.7 (1.5) without PC (P = .93). The reliability indices were not statistically significantly different with and without PC. There was a good intraclass correlation (ICC) of MD (ICC = 0.87) and PSD (ICC = 0.88) with and without PC. Eyes with TECNIS ZM 900 had significantly better MD when performing HVF without PC (mean difference −0.7, P < .01), but not with Acrysof IQ ReSTOR SN6AD1 (mean difference −0.1, P = .74). Pseudophakic eyes with MIOL may not require PC during HVF testing regardless of the MIOL model. Although eyes with TECNIS ZM900 performed HVF without PC resulted in a significantly better MD value compared with PC, the difference was not clinically significant.

1. Introduction

Standard automated perimetry (SAP) is a static visual field evaluation method that assesses differential thresholds of light sensitivity at different locations in the central retina. SAP is routinely performed in patients with glaucoma to ascertain clinical visual field loss. In clinical practice, SAP is also used as a follow-up method in patients with glaucoma and to manage neurological and retinal diseases.[1–3] Humphrey visual field (HVF), a widely used SAP, reports the data as a pattern of a grayscale map, with numerical values in decibels (dB) and global indices in mean deviation (MD) and pattern standard deviation (PSD). In HVF testing, patients are required to see light stimuli clearly projected on a bowl of radius 33 cm, which is considered the normal reading distance. The Humphrey Field Analyzer manufacturer recommends the use of corrective lenses for near vision during the test. Generally, phakic and pseudophakic patients with monofocal intraocular lens require a presbyopic corrective lens (PC) due to the loss of accommodation. Multifocal intraocular lenses (MIOL) have become popular in modern cataract surgery.[4] MIOL provides good overall vision (both near and distant) and greater spectacle independence.[5–8] It is common for patients with MIOL to have good near vision, both with and without presbyopic spectacles. As each MIOL model has a different design and technology, performing HVF testing using different MIOL models may produce different test results.

This study aimed to determine the effect of presbyopic correction lenses on HVF testing in patients with MIOL. In addition, we evaluated the differences in the HVF test results between 2 types of MIOL designs.

2. Materials and methods

This was a prospective comparative study of consecutive patients who underwent uneventful phacoemulsification with MIOL implantation at the Department of Ophthalmology, Faculty of Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, between June 2015 and July 2018. The study protocol was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board and complied with all principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and subsequent amendments (IRB certificate approval number: Si 003/2010). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to their participation. The inclusion criteria were early-stage glaucomatous and non-glaucomatous patients > 18 years, history of uneventful phacoemulsification with MIOL implantation at least 3 months before participation, distant best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) equal to or better than 20/30, and near BCVA of at least Jaeger 2. Exclusion criteria were the inability to perform reliable visual field testing (fixation loss > 20%, false positive or false negative > 15%), a history of postoperative complications, moderate to advanced visual field defects (MD worse than–6 dB), and any other concurrent ocular diseases, except well-controlled glaucoma. If both eyes of the same patient were eligible, both were included in the study. Each patient underwent a comprehensive eye examination using slit-lamp biomicroscopy, distant and near visual acuity, and auto-refraction. Eligible participants underwent HVF 24 to 2 on enrollment day with and without a presbyopic correction lens. Participants were randomized to undergo HVF either with PC followed by without PC or without PC followed by with PC. Data collection included demographic data, distant (logMAR) and near (Jaeger reading chart) BCVA, and HVF test results, including MD, PSD, test duration (minutes), fixation loss, false positives, and false negatives. The datasets generated during and/or analyzed during the current study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

The paired t test of equivalence of means for the crossover study design was used to calculate the sample size. When the sample size in each group is 23, a 2 group 0.05000 1-sided t test will have 80.00% power to reject the null hypothesis that the test and standard are not equivalent (the difference in means, MD with PC – MD without PC, is 1.50000 or farther from zero in the same direction) in favor of the alternative hypothesis that the means of the 2 groups are equivalent, assuming that the expected difference in means is 0.00000 and the common standard deviation is 2.00000. The total anticipated participants were 92 eyes.

2.1. Multifocal intraocular lens characteristics

Two types of aspheric diffractive MIOL were used in this study: TECNIS ZM900 (Abbott Medical Optics Inc., Santa Ana, CA) and AcrySof IQ ReSTOR SN6AD1 (Alcon Laboratories, Inc., Irvine, CA). The TECNIS ZM900 is a silicone, 3-piece aspheric diffractive lens. The power of the add is + 4.00 D with a 50/50 distance/near light distribution. The AcrySof IQ ReSTOR SN6AD1 is an acrylic single-piece aspherical diffractive lens. The power of the add was + 3.00 D with a 65/35 light distribution.

2.2. Perimetry

Perimetry was performed using a Humphrey Field Analyzer (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc.). Participants underwent a 24 to 2 Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm standard test with stimulus size III and a standard Humphrey background luminance of 31.5 apostilbs. Participants were selected by simple random sampling to undergo the HVF test with a PC, followed by without PC, or without PC followed by a PC. The power of the PC at a testing distance of 33 cm (bowl radius) depended on the subjective BCVA, which in turn depended on the current refractive error.[9,10] The participants took at least a 5-minute break between the tests. A test result with a fixation loss > 20% and a false positive or false negative rate > 15% was considered unreliable. The HVF test was repeated after comprehensive instruction if the results were unreliable. Participants with repeated confirmations of unreliable results were excluded.

2.3. Statistical analysis

The primary outcome measures were the global indices of HVF, including MD and PSD. The secondary outcome measures were reliability indices, including fixation loss (FL), false positives (FP), false negatives (FN), and test duration. Primary and secondary outcomes were compared between eyes with and without a presbyopic correction lens. In addition, we performed a subgroup analysis comparing the 2 types of MIOL: TECNIS ZM900 and AcrySof IQ ReSTOR SN6AD1. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The correlation and limit of agreement between the 2 methods were analyzed using intraclass correlation (ICC) and Bland-Altman plots. Statistical significance was set at P ≤ .05. ICC coefficient of 0.75 to 0.9 indicated good reliability and > 0.9 was considered excellent.

3. Results

Ninety-four eyes of 59 patients were enrolled. After excluding patients with confirmed unreliable visual field, the data analysis included 82 eyes of 51 patients (male, 22; female, 29) with a mean age of 65.4 years. Of the 82 eyes, 49 were non-glaucomatous, and the remaining 33 had early-stage glaucoma (primary open angle glaucoma; POAG, 15 eyes; primary angle-closure glaucoma, 18 eyes). The mean duration from cataract surgery to HVF testing was 5.4 months (range: 3.0–9.4 months). Demographic data and overall outcomes of HVF testing with and without presbyopic correction are shown in Table 1. The reliability indices (FL, FP, and FN) and test duration did not differ significantly between the 2 groups. The mean of the overall MD without PC (−3.1 ± 2.1 dB) was significantly better than the overall MD with PC (−3.5 ± 2.1) (mean difference: −0.4, 95% confidential interval −0.7 to −0.1) (P = .01). There was no statistically significant difference between the median overall PSD in patients with PC (2.2, inter-quartile range 1.8–3.0) and without PC (2.2, inter-quartile range 1.8–3.0) (P = .93). There was a good ICC for MD (ICC = 0.87) and PSD (ICC = 0.88), with and without PC. Moreover, Bland-Altman plot showed good limits of agreement between MD and PSD with and without PC. The mean difference of MD with and without PC (−0.4 dB) and MD of most eyes (76/82) fell within +/− 1.96 standard deviation. There was no mean difference in PSD with and without PC, and PSD of most eyes (78/82) were between +/− 1.96 standard deviation. The ICC and Altman plots of the overall MD and PSD are shown in Figures 1 (MD) and Figure 2 (PSD).

4. Discussion

This comparative study aimed to evaluate the need for presbyopic correction lenses in patients with MIOL while performing HVF measurements. We observed a good correlation and a limit of agreement for HVF outcomes with and without PC in eyes with MIOL. The HVF Analyzer displays white-on-white light signals at a bowl radius of 33 cm, which is regarded as the near distance and requires the patient to clearly see the light signals at this distance. Thus, patients require a suitable corrective lens during HVF measurement, especially under presbyopic conditions. Generally, the powers of near spectacles are auto-calculated using the HVF Analyzer and subjective BCVA, depending on the current refractive error and the patient’s age. In principle, the MIOL distributes light entering from various planes to focus on the retina and produces multifocality. The MIOL produces multiple foci, resulting in simultaneous retinal images at both near and far distances.[5,11–13] With its multifocal property, MIOL has been reported to produce excellent near and distant vision, resulting in spectacle independence.[14–16] On the other hand, MIOL have unwanted side effects, including glare, halos, and decreased contrast sensitivity. In patients with glaucoma, the quality of vision, such as contrast sensitivity, is a concern after cataract surgery.[17] Implantation of MIOL in glaucomatous patients may exacerbate the loss of contrast sensitivity.[18–20] Thus, patients with glaucoma who naturally have reduced contrast sensitivity should be well-informed about the application of MIOL.[18,21,22] Several studies have reported a depression of MD on HVF measurement with MIOL.[23,24] The reduction in contrast sensitivity from the MIOL design, as well as glaucoma, is hypothesized to reduce MD on automated perimetry. Aychoua et al[23] compared SAP test results using HVF 30 to 2 testing among groups of patients with diffractive MIOL (TECNIS ZM900, Zeiss 809M), Monofocal IOLs (ZA9003), and phakic eyes. They concluded that the MIOL group had a mean reduction in MD values of 2.4 dB compared with the phakic control group after adjusting for age and pupil size. In the MIOL group, a corrective lens was used only in participants whose near vision was less than J2. However, they did not demonstrate the direct effects of corrective lenses on HVF testing in patients with MIOL. According to this recommendation, pseudophakic patients who lose their accommodative ability after cataract surgery require presbyopic spectacle correction when undergoing HVF testing. As previously reported, 1 diopter of refractive blur can reduce retinal sensitivity by approximately 1 dB when testing with stimulus size III on the Octopus perimeter.[25] However, the use of spectacles can accidentally induce misinterpretation of results by erroneous refractive correction[1] and produce lens rim artifacts that occur in more than 6% of visual field tests, despite the purposeful attention of the perimetrist.[26] Most previous studies on the effects of MIOL on HVF used a presbyopic correction lens, as calculated and recommended by the HVF analyzer. However, to date, there has been insufficient data from patients with MIOL who may have good near-reading visual acuity, both with and without PC. In the current study, we found that patients with MIOL in whom HVF testing was performed without PC resulted in a statistically significantly better MD value (mean difference −0.4 dB, P = .01); however, the difference was very minimal and not clinically relevant. The PSD, reliability indices (FL, FN, and FP), and test duration were not significantly different between with and without PC. These findings imply that patients with MIOL can undergo HVF testing with or without PC without any clinically significant differences.

In addition to the current refractive error, the ability of patients with MIOL to see clearly at the reading distance generally depends on the specific properties of each MIOL model. There are several MIOL designs available. The differences in IOL technology and design may have affected the HVF results. With TECNIS ZM900, patients demonstrated better MD values when performing HVF testing without PC (mean difference −0.7 dB, P < .01). In contrast, there was no difference in MD with and without PC found in the AcrySof IQ ReSTOR SN6AD1 IOL subgroup (mean difference −0.1 dB, P = .74). We hypothesized that the difference in MIOL technology and design, especially in the light distribution for near and distant vision, plays a role in the difference in the quality of near vision. TECNIS ZM900 distributes light 50/50 for distance/near (add power + 4.00 diopters for near vision), whereas AcrySof IQ ReSTOR SN6AD1distributes light 65/35 for distance/near (add power + 3.00 diopters for near vision). The TECNIS ZM900 has more light distribution and more add + power for near vision than the AcrySof IQ ReSTOR SN6AD1. With the aforementioned properties, the TECNIS ZM900 demonstrated a significantly better MD without PC in the overall analysis and in both the glaucoma and non-glaucoma groups, even though the near BCVA without PC was significantly lower than that with PC. In contrast, the eyes in the AcrySof IQ ReSTOR SN6AD1 group did not show any difference in MD with or without PC. Most MIOL designs distribute a larger proportion of light in distant vision than in near vision. With presbyopic correction lenses in eyes with MIOLs, the largest amount of light distribution for distant vision was converted to near vision. This phenomenon may account for the comparable MD and PC values between the MIOL models. In the subgroup analysis, there was no significant difference in PSD between the different MIOL models when HVF testing was performed with or without PC. Because of the small margin of difference between models with and without PC, the ability to perform HVF testing without presbyopic corrective lenses may generally be applied to MIOL models with similar designs and technologies.

Finally, our study found no significant differences in the reliability indices or test duration between the MIOL models with and without PC. Reliability indices are important indicators for HVF result interpretation. Although FL was approximately 1.2% higher in the HVF without the PC, the difference was not statistically significant. However, FL rarely affects the visual field results, especially in patients with glaucoma.[10] The good to excellent ICC and limit of agreement of the HVF results could imply that eyes with MIOL could perform HVF with and without PC without a statistically significant difference. We observed that PC did not affect HVF testing in eyes in either MIOL model.

4.1. Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we excluded patients who were unable to produce reliable HVF. Many of our participants were inexperienced in HVF testing; therefore, they did not develop a learning effect that might improve their test performance over time.[27,28] Each participant was required to perform HVF testing twice in each eye on the same visit. This may have resulted in fatigue and unreliability in some patients, resulting in a smaller sample size than anticipated. Second, HVF measurements were performed at a median duration of approximately 5 months after cataract surgery in the absence of posterior capsular opacity and/or fully developed neuroadaptation (approximately 6 months).[29–31] Both these factors can affect the visual quality and HVF results.

5. Conclusion

We suggest that pseudophakic patients with aspheric diffractive multifocal IOL, including mild-stage glaucoma patients, can undergo HVF testing with and without presbyopic spectacle correction without clinically significant differences. Different MIOL models may play a role in HVF testing, owing to variations in design and technology. We observed no clinically significant differences in HVF testing between patients who received TECNIS ZM900 or AcrySof IQ ReSTOR SN6AD1 implantation with or without presbyopic spectacle correction. This result may not apply to the other MIOL models. Both models reduced the MD values, and ophthalmologists should consider this when evaluating automated perimetry test results in patients with MIOL.
