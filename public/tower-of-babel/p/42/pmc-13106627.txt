A comprehensive cross-sectional study of bedside monitor alarm characteristics and alarm load across hospital units
Authors: Karina Kraevsky, Sajida Aqtash, Fanurs Chi-En Teh, Michael R. Pinsky, Gilles Clermont, Marilyn Hravnak, Salah S. Al-Zaiti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bedside physiologic monitor alarms negatively contribute to clinician cognitive burden and patient wellbeing in hospital intermediate (IMCU) and intensive (ICU) care units. This was a cross-sectional study to characterize the types, sources, annunciation modality and prevalence of alarms generated by bedside patient monitors, as well as their distributions and hourly alarm load across 3 ICUs and 1 IMCU. Analyzing > 2 million monitoring hours from 17,442 patient encounters (59 ± 18 years, 14% Black, 43% female) over 5 years, we identified 65.6 million alarms. Of all alarms, 74% were silent while 26% were audible. Most alarms (88%) were technical, unrelated to patient condition, while 12% were physiologic, suggesting clinical instability. Among audible alarms, 68% arose from technical issues. The IMCU averaged 5 audible alarms per patient-hour, of which 67% were technical. ICUs experienced up to 10 audible alarms per patient-hour, with 54% of them technical. This comprehensive study of monitoring alerts shows that alarms in IMCUs and ICUs are predominantly technical, highlighting opportunities to refine monitoring technologies and management strategies. Such improvements would help clinicians focus on alarms signaling true changes in patient physiologic status and reduce the cognitive burden of frequent alarms, which can contribute to alarm fatigue and worsen patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43028-3.

Introduction

Intermediate and intensive care units (IMCU and ICU) in hospitals are high-stakes environments characterized by high-acuity patients, complex medical conditions, and clinical decision-making under time pressure. Concurrent monitoring of multiple acutely ill patients, interpretation of vast amounts of data, and decision making about competing demands create a heavy cognitive load for clinicians in these settings1. This constant need to analyze information from multiple environmental sources can contribute to cognitive overload, which can become a critical factor influencing patient safety and care quality2. Because patient monitor alarms are ubiquitous in these settings and consistently compete for clinician attention3,4 they are a key contributor to cognitive overload, creating challenges that can impact both clinician performance and patient outcomes.

Bedside physiologic monitors are used to detect changes in patients’ condition and alert clinicians to potential deterioration. However, high rates of false-positive alarms contribute to alarm fatigue, a state of sensory overload from excessive, often irrelevant alerts, which can lead clinicians to be non-responsive to alarms, increasing the risk of missed complications and preventable adverse events3,5,6 The Joint Commission incorporated alarm management into its National Patient Safety Goals in 2014. However, despite years of research, meaningful, widely accepted solutions to alarm management remain elusive. While studies propose interventions, problems persist across clinical settings a decade later7–10. The current body of monitor alarm studies primarily focus on specific units or patient populations (e.g., cardiac ICU) and usually examine only a short time span (e.g., one month)10–14. Furthermore, to our knowledge no study has comprehensively investigated the landscape of multiple bedside monitor alarm types over a multi-year period. The purpose of this study was to provide a broad overview of bedside monitor alarms on multiple care units across multiple years, establishing a reference point that reflects real-world alarm activity across diverse care settings. Specifically, we sought to comprehensively characterize the types, sources, annunciation modality and prevalence of alarms generated by bedside patient monitors, and their distributions across unit types. Further, we quantified the magnitude of alarm activity, referred to here as alarm load, by aggregating alarms by patient and care unit to describe the extent of alarm activity in each setting. These data can help identify priority targets for alarms reduction, support evaluation of future interventions, and inform both workflow improvements and the development of smarter monitoring technologies.

Methods

This observational study was conducted in accordance with pre-specified protocol of a National Institutes of Health-funded study (R01NR13912). Patients were recruited under a waiver of informed consent after receiving human subject protections approval by the University of Pittsburgh’s (UPITT) Institutional Review Board.

Setting and sample

The study took place at an urban level 1 trauma center, with data collected from four monitored care units – a 16-bed surgical-trauma IMCU, a 10-bed neuro-trauma ICU (NTICU), a 24-bed medical ICU (MICU), and a 26-bed surgical-trauma ICU (STICU) – between April 2018 to November 2022 (Fig. 1). The IMCU is a unit that provides continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring for patients who require a higher level of observation than general wards, but do not meet ICU admission criteria. We included the IMCU in this analysis to provide a more representative picture of continuously monitored, acutely ill individuals across the hospital, reflecting the spectrum of patient acuity and alarm activity beyond traditional ICU settings. All ICUs generally maintain a 1:2 nurse-patient ratio, while the IMCU has a 1:3 or 1:4 nurse-patient ratio, depending on individual patient acuity. Data were collected from all adult patients (> 18 years or older) admitted to the units during the study period. Each admission to a study unit was counted as a separate encounter, thus patients treated in multiple study units had multiple encounters.

Data elements

Monitors and monitoring parameters

The data stream used for this analysis is an ongoing pipeline of monitor and patient data from participating units (Fig. 1). Comprehensive bedside monitor data captured in the data stream includes bed designation, timestamped waveforms (> 110 Hz), numerics (< 10 Hz) and alarms. Timestamped waveforms refer to high-resolution continuous physiologic signals, such as electrocardiogram (ECG) or arterial blood pressure waveform tracings, captured more than 110 times per second to preserve full waveform detail. Numerics are processed parameter values derived from these signals, such as heart rate, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) or blood pressure readings, recorded fewer than 10 times per second to reflect trends rather than continuous raw signal. However, for this analysis we utilized only alarms data, which were collected via a single-manufacturer, multiple-channel bedside monitors (IntelliVue MX Series, Philips, Amsterdam, Netherlands).

All monitoring occurred according to hospital and unit-based standards of care. Throughout the data collection period, monitoring sensors were applied according to established unit protocols and routine nursing workflows, with no changes to monitoring procedures introduced for this study. Alarm data were collected passively without real-time feedback to clinicians, minimizing the likelihood that awareness of data collection influenced monitoring practices. All patients underwent intermittent non-invasive blood pressure measurements via automated cuff sphygmomanometer at a minimum frequency of every two hours. All patients received continuous monitoring of heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR) and SpO2 using the following standard continuous monitoring modalities: 5-channel ECG for HR and rhythm; thoracic bioimpedance for RR; and finger plethysmography for SpO2. Patient clinical status dictated need for invasive monitoring. Continuous invasive pressure monitoring (arterial, central venous, pulmonary artery, or intracranial pressure) occurred only in the ICUs using catheter pressure transducer devices. Continuous invasive temperature measurements (e.g., esophageal, bladder, etc.) were performed in both ICUs and IMCU using temperature sensing probes. All alarm data generated from bedside monitors were collected via the data collection architecture described below.

Demographics and clinical characteristics

Patient information was obtained from the electronic health record (EHR) to characterize the cohort. These data included demographics variables (age, sex, race) and clinical characteristics (admission diagnoses by International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision [ICD-10] codes, patient location, hospital length of stay [LOS], and hospital discharge disposition).

Data collection and storage architecture

Figure 1 illustrates the data pipeline that streamed real-time data from bedside monitors and patient-level data from the EHR to the processing, storage, and analytics databases. Bedside monitor data were first transmitted to the Philips Data Warehouse Connect (DWC) database, and EHR data were sent to data servers. Then, EHR and monitoring data were merged, time-shifted, deidentified using a study identifier (ID) to uniquely identify each patient and moved into the research environment for entry into a Structured Query Language (SQL) database for secure access by the research team.

Alarm type labels and categories

To streamline comprehensive alarm data extraction, we first compiled data for all alarm types generated by all patients during the study period. Each alarm type received a label as assigned by the monitor software, with labels listed and defined in the bedside monitor user manual.15 Multiple co-occurring alarms were registered separately by the monitoring system and thus were counted separately. We conducted alarm categorization to reduce label redundancy for analysis. Clinician researchers then reviewed all manufacturer-defined alarm type labels and organized them into alarm categories based on physiologic parameter (e.g., blood pressure, respiratory, etc.), technical cause (e.g., signal loss, equipment disconnection, etc.), and manufacturer pre-programmed alarm annunciation modality (audible and/or silent [i.e., an alarm with visual indication only and not audible by design, rather than silenced by clinician]). Physiologic parameter alarms were further grouped into clinically relevant subcategories that reflect numeric threshold violations set by bedside clinicians (e.g., temperature high/low, BP high/low, SpO2 low, HR high/low, etc.) and heart rhythm (e.g., atrial fibrillation, pacemaker function, etc.). Alarms representing multiple kinds of the same alarm (i.e., ST segment elevation in different ECG leads, temperature measurement sources) were grouped into a single category.

The resulting categories included technical alarms (containing subcategories of signal quality, measurement parameters [i.e., “overrange,” “change scale”], lost sensor/transducer signal, device off/deactivated, monitoring equipment function, other), and physiologic alarms (containing subcategories of respiratory-, pressure-, heart rate-, other cardiac-, and temperature-related). Following categorization, a corresponding coding scheme was applied to the alarm data within the SQL database (Supplemental Figure S1). Each category and subcategory also contained audible or silent annunciation modality information.

Data extraction

We relied on an existing SQL environment for data merging and analysis (Supplemental Table S1). We used a multi-step process to time-align alarm data with care unit location and other EHR data. First, we matched every alarm event with the recorded start and end times of the patient’s stay in each care unit. We excluded from the analysis patients with inconsistent or misaligned stay times. These were defined as non-chronological admission and discharge records for a given unit, which created uncertainty in mapping alarms to the correct location. Two scenarios met this definition: (1) admission to a second unit occurred before discharge from the first unit; or (2) discharge from the first unit occurred immediately after admission to another unit. These inconsistencies made it impossible to determine when the patient was physically transferred between units. Then, we used SQL queries to join and group data from the alarms and locations tables. Finally, we ran SQL queries to group alarms by types (i.e., physiologic, technical) and annunciation modality (i.e., audible, silent). We populated total alarms data from queries into Excel workbooks separated by study care unit for ease of final data manipulation and analyses.

Data analysis

Data in each alarm category and subcategory were reviewed and consolidated to exclude those with fewer than 2% of total category data points. Descriptive statistics characterized patients, encounters, and alarm data. For continuous variables, means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) were reported, as appropriate. Categorical variables were described with frequencies and percentages. To gain insight into the magnitude of alarm activity on patients and care units, total alarms per patient per monitoring hour, defined as patient alarm load, and total alarms per patient monitoring hour per monitoring unit, defined as unit alarm load, were calculated. Alarm loads were expressed using alarms per patient monitoring hour, rather than per bed per hour, to mitigate daily fluctuations in unit census. Alarm load calculations included audible and silent alarms because both contribute to cognitive load by capturing clinicians’ attention and requiring cognitive processing and decision making.13,16 Finally, in an exploratory analysis to assess variability in alarm burden at the encounter level, we stratified patient encounters by alarm load and compared baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between encounters with high alarm burden (upper quartile) and those with lower alarm burden using appropriate statistical tests. Study findings are reported per the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies17.

Results

Study population

After excluding 61 patients who had misaligned or inconsistent stay times, our sample included 17,442 encounters among 16,621 patients who were 59 ± 18 years of age, predominantly of White race (n = 12,478/16,621, 75%), and male (n = 9458/16,621, 57%) (Table 1). The patients were primarily hospitalized for acute injuries (n = 5543/16,621, 33%), followed by digestive system related (n = 1798/16,621, 11%) and circulatory system related (n = 1701/16,621, 10%) diagnoses. The median encounter length of stay (LOS) was 7 ± 11 days. At the end of the entire hospitalization, the majority of individuals (n = 8714/16,621, 52%) were discharged home, and the overall in-hospital mortality was 13%.

Overall alarms

Alarm groupings

Bedside monitors generated 422 unique alert types during the study period, which were the sources of alarms reported here. Of the unique alerts, 139 (33%) were physiologic in nature and 283 (67%) were technical. A total of 321 (76%) alert types were audible, including 190 (24% of all alerts) that were audible technical alarms. To facilitate analysis and interpretation, these alerts were consolidated into 59 clinically meaningful groups based on similarity in type of clinical context (e.g., all invasive pressure alerts, or all “no transducer” alerts). Of the 59 aggregated groups, 36 represented physiologic alerts and 23 represented technical alerts (Supplemental Table 2).

Distribution

The 17,442 patient encounters across 4 study units (36% STICU, 31% MICU, 24% IMCU and 9% NTICU) aggregated more than 2.1 million (M) monitoring hours (Table 2). The total sample generated more than 65.6 M alarms across the study units during approximately 4.5 years. The IMCU generated the fewest alarms (4.8 M, 7.3%), followed by NTICU (7.6 M, 11.6%), MICU (21.2 M, 32.4%), and STICU (31.9 M, 48.6%).

Annunciation modality

In the total sample, 48.8 M alarms (74.4%) were silent, generating only visual cues, while 16.8 M alarms (25.6%) were audible, which generated both visual and audible cues (Table 2). Most audible alarms were in ICUs (14 M, 23.1%), with STICU generating the most audible alarms (7.6 M, 11.6%), followed by MICU (4.8 M, 7.3%) and NTICU generating the fewest audible alarms (1.6 M, 2.4%). IMCU was the only unit that generated proportionally more audible than silent alarms (2.8 M vs. 2 M).

Alarm types

Silent alarms were primarily technical with the minority of a physiologic cause (88 thousand [K], 0.07%) and the majority of a technical nature (48.7 M, 74%). Audible alarms accounted for only a quarter of total alarms (17 M, 25.6%) but were somewhat evenly split between those of a physiologic cause (8 M, 12.2%) or of a technical nature (8.8 M, 13.4%).

Sources, prevalence and distribution

Silent alarms. Figure 2 demonstrates the distribution of silent alarms by unit type. Silent alarms were primarily generated due to signal quality issues (e.g., artifact, low perfusion) in both ICU (31 M, 66%) and IMCU (1.5 M, 75%), followed by parameter calibration alerts (e.g., prompts to adjust acceptable waveform amplitude parameters, prompts to calibrate the transducer) in ICU (11.1 M, 24%) and loss of signal (e.g., SpO2 searching) in IMCU (354 K, 17%). The least common were silent physiologic alarms in both ICU and IMCU, generating 65 K (< 1%) and 22 K (1%) of alarms, respectively.Audible technical alarms. Of the 8.8 M total audible technical alarms, most (7.1 M, 81%) were related to loss of signal, followed by poor signal quality (74 6 K, 8.4%) and equipment related (659 K, 7.5%) alarms (Fig. 3). Additionally, the ICUs experienced technical alarms related to parameter calibration issues (e.g., waveform amplitude outside assigned parameters, prompts to calibrate transducer) (262 K, 3%). The primary sources of signal loss were ECG leads in IMCU (55%) and SpO2 probes in ICUs (50%). Equipment related alarms were due to battery status (71%) in IMCU, likely generated by portable telemetry devices, and in ICUs due to device deactivation (60%). Signal quality alarms contributed 17% of all audible technical alarms in IMCU and 6% in ICUs and were due to poor quality signal generated by any sensor (e.g., SpO2, ECG leads, etc.).Audible physiologic alarms. Respiratory related alarms were the primary reasons for audible physiologic alarms in both unit types, with 602 K (68%) in IMCU and 2.9 M (41%) in ICUs (Fig. 4). Desaturation (70% in IMCU, 65% in ICUs) was the most common cause of respiratory alarms, followed by bradypnea in IMCU (19%), and tachypnea in ICU (15%). The remaining physiologic alarms in IMCU were HR related (192 K, 22%), with tachycardia making up more than half of heart rate alarms at 58%; followed by heart rhythm related alarms (92 K, 10%) with exactly half being atrial fibrillation related alarms. Blood pressure and temperature related alarms made up less than 1% of all audible physiologic alarms with 1228 and 216 total alarms, respectively, 52% of which were high non-invasive blood pressure alarms and 100% were low temperature alarms. In the ICUs, the remaining physiologic alarms were blood pressure related (2.3 M, 33%), with 55% related to low invasive blood pressure; 1.4 M (20%) HR related with majority (70%) tachycardia; 423 K (6%) heart rhythm with 48% related to atrial fibrillation; and approximately 23 K (< 1%) caused by temperature readings with low temperature (87%) as the most prevalent cause.

The least common were silent physiologic alarms in both ICU and IMCU, generating 65 K (< 1%) and 22 K (1%) of alarms, respectively.

Additionally, the ICUs experienced technical alarms related to parameter calibration issues (e.g., waveform amplitude outside assigned parameters, prompts to calibrate transducer) (262 K, 3%). The primary sources of signal loss were ECG leads in IMCU (55%) and SpO2 probes in ICUs (50%). Equipment related alarms were due to battery status (71%) in IMCU, likely generated by portable telemetry devices, and in ICUs due to device deactivation (60%). Signal quality alarms contributed 17% of all audible technical alarms in IMCU and 6% in ICUs and were due to poor quality signal generated by any sensor (e.g., SpO2, ECG leads, etc.).

Alarm load

To quantify the magnitude of alarm activity within a given timeframe, we calculated alarm loads as the number of alarms per patient per monitoring hour. Overall, the distribution of total alarm loads was highly right skewed across all units (Fig. 5). Median alarm loads ranged from 7.11 alarm per patient monitoring hour in the IMCU to 30.56 in the NTICU, with interquartile ranges between 15.33 and 36.94. Maximum alarm loads varied widely, exceeding 800 alarms in some cases. Table 3 further demonstrates the breakdown of alarm loads. The patients in the IMCU generated approximately 9 alarms per patient monitoring hour, 5 of which were audible and 4 were silent. Virtually all silent alarms were of a technical nature. Of the audible alarms, 2 alarms per hour per patient were related to physiologic parameters and 3 were technical. On average, patients in ICUs generated approximately 37 alarms per patient monitoring hour, 8 of which were audible and 29 were silent. Similarly to IMCU, almost all silent alarms were technical in all ICUs. Between 3 and 5 audible alarms per hour were related to physiologic measurements, and between 4 and 5 were technical. We also determined care unit alarm load by determining the total rate of alarms on the unit per monitoring hour. When evaluating the care unit alarm load for each unit, the IMCU on average experienced 150 alarms per hour, more than half of which (n = 86/hr, 57%) were audible, with approximately two thirds (n = 58/hr) of a technical nature and one third (n = 28/hr) physiologic. The ICUs each experienced between 407 and 1,064 alarms per hour, 21–24% of which were audible, with 45–54% of them technical. The MICU experienced the highest number of audible technical alarms (54%), while STICU experienced the lowest number of audible technical alarms (45%).

At encounter-level, in unadjusted comparisons we observed significant associations between baseline patient characteristics and alarm loads among those with high versus low alarm burden (Supplemental Table S3). Specifically, high alarm burden encounters were associated with older age (62 ± 17 vs. 59 ± 18, p < 0.01), self-reported Black race (15.5% vs. 12.5%, p < 0.01), admission to ICU (91.2% vs. 71.5%, p < 0.01), and primary admission diagnosis of infection (13.1% vs. 7.6%, p < 0.01) or circulatory disease (11.6% vs. 9.4%, p < 0.01). In addition, high alarm burden encounters were associated with higher in-hospital mortality (25.1% vs. 8.2%, p < 0.01) and lower proportion discharged to home (35.1% vs. 58.8%). No significant associations were observed between alarm burden and sex or LOS.

Discussion

The purpose of continuous bedside monitoring in hospitalized patients is to provide clinicians with real-time updates on a patient’s physiologic status, and alert them to potential instability. In this large-scale cross-sectional study spanning multiple years, we analyzed over 65 million alarms from more than 17,000 patient encounters across multiple care units. This work offers several distinctive contributions. It represents one of the largest and most granular analyses of monitor alarms to date, with detailed classification by alarm type, source and annunciation modality. The inclusion of IMCU data, which is an underrepresented setting in alarm research, extends the applicability of our findings beyond critical care and provides new insight into alarm characteristics in intermediate care settings. The multi-year timeframe overcomes seasonal and short-term variability, offering a stable and representative glimpse into true alarm activity. Additionally, by quantifying both audible and silent alarms and grouping them into clinically meaningful subcategories, this work provides unit- and modality-specific reference points that may inform future alarm management efforts and support ongoing discussions about how monitoring systems could better support clinical care.

First, this study reveals that most alarms, regardless of care setting, are due to technical issues. These technical alarms (both silent and audible) are manufacturer defined as low priority15 and mostly related to monitoring sensors and signal quality. These findings are contrary to those of prior studies. For example, Drew and colleagues13 reported that technical alarms were the second most prevalent, behind premature ventricular contraction related alarms and more recently, Nguyen and colleagues18 found that only 11% of alarms in the study were technical. Some possible explanations for the differences in technical alarms in prior studies could be due to hospital monitoring policy differences or data capture methods. In our study, most technical alarms were related to pulse oximetry sensor loss followed by ECG sensor loss in ICUs, and reversed in IMCU, suggesting different priority targets for each care setting, such as improving lead adherence and connection stability in IMCU versus enhancing pulse oximetry sensor reliability in ICUs. Other studies had similar findings where ECG lead disconnections were prominent culprits of signal loss10,13. Although less frequent than signal loss, signal quality alarms still accounted for 17% of audible technical alarms in IMCU and 6% in ICUs. Signal quality alarms encompassed a variety of monitor-generated alerts indicating problems with the accuracy or reliability of physiologic waveforms and measurements (e.g., “noisy signal,” “poor signal,” “cannot measure,” “check sensor”) across parameters such as ECG, pulse oximetry and arterial pressure. Though some of these issues can be addressed with more stringent clinician oversight (e.g., replacing leads, adjusting parameters, etc.)8,19,20 monitoring technology has not changed much despite general healthcare technological advances, and hospitals are long overdue for improved monitoring tools21.

Second, the way alarms annunciate is important. In our study, silent alarms made up the majority of alarms on care units. Few studies report on silent (or visual only) alarms, but when examined, silent alarms make up a substantial proportion of all alarms. Similarly to this study, where approximately 85% of technical alarms were silent, in their 2014 study, Drew et al.13 found that 80% of technical alarms were silent. While they do not add to environmental noise, silent alarms still likely contribute heavily to cognitive load because they capture attention and require processing and decision making16. Audible alarms, which accounted for roughly a quarter of all alarms, are more likely to gain clinician attention. For example, Drews et al. found that audible device alarms during non-structured tasks (i.e., tasks that do not follow a structured set of steps, such as medication preparation) were associated with care delays22. Within this audible category, only half were physiologic in nature; the remainder were technical, typically reflecting poor signal capture or quality, and equipment status (Table 2; Figs. 3 and 4). These audible technical alarms are necessary for safe and accurate monitoring10 as they prompt correction of equipment or sensor issues. However, because they share the same annunciation characteristics (i.e., audible tone) as physiologic alarms, they do not help clinicians quickly distinguish between situations that require equipment correction and those requiring direct clinical intervention.

Our findings point to two related opportunities for improvement. First, further differentiating annunciations for technical versus physiologic alarms could help clinicians triage attention more effectively. Many technical alarms in our dataset were already silent, yet they still require clinician time and attention to interpret. Additional differentiation could focus on making the most urgent physiologic alarms more salient, for example through distinct tones, prioritized routing or vibrotactile annunciation for most critical alarms23 while ensuring that low-priority technical alarms remain non-disruptive and are clearly recognizable as non-physiologic. Second, in the United States, and elsewhere, registered nurses are typically responsible for addressing all monitor alarms24,25 thus routing certain technical alarms (e.g., ECG electrode disconnection) directly to qualified non-registered-nurse personnel (e.g., nursing assistant, patient care technician, etc.) could reduce unnecessary interruptions for bedside nurses. While some of these strategies have been suggested previously3,26,27 our unit- and modality-specific results indicate where such approaches would be more effective at mitigating alarm fatigue, focus clinician attention on clinically relevant events, and improve patient safety.

Third, the breakdown of true physiologic alarms according to the triggering vital sign is interesting. Regardless of care setting, most of the physiologic alarms were of a respiratory nature, from pulse oximetry and RR (Fig. 4). Conversely, in similar descriptive studies this pattern is not always the case. For instance, Andrade-Mendez et al.28 reported blood pressure alarms (37.3%) followed by oximetry (33.4%) in a general ICU. And Unal and colleagues29 reported ECG alarms as the most frequent in a neurological ICU. However, to our knowledge, no other studies have examined alarm break down data in IMCUs, thus we are unable to compare our IMCU findings. Furthermore, in ICUs, respiratory related alarms were followed by pressure alarms and heart rate alarms, while in IMCU, the reverse was true (Fig. 4). This is likely the result of more frequent opportunities for alarms to be generated by continuous invasive blood pressure monitoring in the ICU versus less frequent and externally measured non-invasive blood pressure monitoring in the IMCU.

Fourth, our findings on alarm load, defined as the number of alarms per patient per monitoring hour, give insight into the magnitude of alarm activity and how its frequency may contribute to the visual and auditory demands of the clinical environment. Overall alarm activity was unevenly distributed, with a small proportion of encounters accounting for a disproportionately large share of total alarms (Fig. 5). There were 30–40 total alarms per patient per hour in the ICUs and 9 in the IMCU (Table 3). Of these, audible alarms accounted for 7–10 alarms per patient per hour in the ICUs and 5 in IMCU, translating to approximately 168–234 alarms per patient per day and 129 alarms per patient per day in ICUs and IMCU, respectively. Our findings for the ICUs were higher than those previously reported by Poncette10 and Drew13, who determined there were 152 and 187 alarms per bed per day, respectively, which could be due to variability in hospital and unit practices, as well as more comprehensive nature of longitudinal data in our study. When considered alongside typical 1:2 and 1:3–4 nurse-to-patient ratios in ICUs and IMCUs, respectively, these alarm frequencies illustrate the scale of alarm activity clinicians manage in routine practice. Furthermore, alarm activity may become especially problematic on care units with a greater number of patients, because clinicians can hear alarms generated by all patients due to the presence of central monitoring stations30. While we did not measure direct effects on workflow or patient experience, prior studies have linked frequent alarms to alarm fatigue in clinicians31, and noise in patient rooms to sleep disruption, anxiety, and delirium32–35. Importantly, our analysis reflects only alarms generated by bedside monitors. Additional device alarms such as from infusion pumps, ventilators, and dialysis machines, would further increase the total alarm activity in these environments36,37.

We examined the distribution of alarm burden across encounters using a percentile-based stratification (Supplemental Table S3). Higher alarm counts were associated with demographic characteristics, level of care, admission diagnosis and worse discharge outcomes. These findings suggest that alarm burden varies across patient subgroups and clinical contexts. The observed concentration of alarm activity within a subset of encounters underscores the heterogeneity of alarm burden across care settings and patient trajectories. Future work is needed to examine potential mechanisms underlying this variability and to evaluate whether targeted alarm management strategies may be warranted in clinically complex patients or in high-intensity care settings.

Our study indicates that alarms remain highly prevalent across both ICU and IMCU, despite decades of published work describing clinician-level interventions aimed at reducing alarm frequency, such as timely lead replacement, skin preparation, and use of appropriate SpO2 probe sites3,5,8,19,20,38. While these strategies can reduce certain alarm types in targeted settings, our multi-year analysis suggests that the overall alarm burden persists, suggesting that practice changes alone may be insufficient. Addressing the ongoing prevalence of alarms will require a multi-level approach that complements adherence to best practices and better sensor design with technology-based solutions. For example, monitoring systems could automatically differentiate technical from physiologic alarms and route them accordingly23,39, differentiate between actionable and non-actionable alerts using structured annotation frameworks40, or apply intelligent algorithms that integrate multiple physiologic parameters and account for alarm duration and recurrence to better identify clinically significant events41–45. The detailed unit- and modality-specific reference points provided in this study serve as a foundation for evaluating the effectiveness of such strategies and guiding both hospital policy and device development toward reducing alarm burden for staff and patients.

This study has several limitations. First, our analysis was conducted in a single level 1 trauma academic medical center, using a single type of bedside monitors, which may limit generalizability to hospitals using other technologies, patient populations, or alarm management practices. Second, although the multi-year data collection allowed for a large and diverse sample, this prolonged period overlapped the height of the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced patient characteristics, unit census, or monitoring practices. We did not specifically assess whether such changes occurred. Third, although we quantified alarm prevalence, sources and annunciation modalities, we performed limited analysis of clinical and patient-level context, urgency or patient outcomes and did not assess alarm duration, whether alarms were actionable or related to adverse events. Alarm categorization was based on the manufacturer’s predetermined labels and was not independently validated. While we determined that technical alarms were primarily attributable to signal loss, due to the retrospective nature of this study, we could not determine the precise underlying cause of each alarm (e.g., total loss of signal versus artifact from patient movement or other clinical events). Finally, we examined only alarms generated by bedside monitors based on manufacturer-programmed settings; alarms from other clinical devices (e.g., infusion pumps, ventilators, etc.) were not captured.

In conclusion, this large multi-unit study found that bedside monitor alarms are frequent in both ICU and IMCUs and are predominantly related to technical factors, such as signal loss, poor signal quality, and equipment status. While these alarms do not indicate physiologic deterioration, they substantially contribute to the overall alarm burden experienced by clinicians and patients. Further investigation is needed to understand the underlying reasons for the high frequency of technical alarms, including the roles of device design, sensor reliability and clinical practices. Such insights will be essential to guide the development of innovative strategies, both in nursing workflows and in monitoring technology, which can reduce unnecessary alarms while maintaining patient safety.
