Acute psychosis with convulsive episodes following total thyroidectomy induced by concurrent hypothyroidism and hypocalcemia: a case report
Authors: Ahmad Al-Bitar, Israa Tellawi, Dana Al-Masalma, Alaa Awad, Souheb Al-mahasna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTotal thyroidectomy is a common surgical procedure, with postoperative anxiety and depression being recognized complications. However, the development of acute psychosis is a rare and severe manifestation. The etiology of such presentations is often complex, involving metabolic derangements that directly impact central nervous system function. We report a case of severe psychosis with convulsive features precipitated by the synergistic effects of two distinct postoperative complications: iatrogenic hypothyroidism and hypoparathyroidism-induced hypocalcemia. Background

Total thyroidectomy is one of the most frequently performed endocrine surgical procedures worldwide. While it has evolved into a safe operation with mortality rates below 1%, it is associated with a spectrum of potential complications [1]. Beyond the well-known risks of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and hemorrhage, patients may experience neuropsychiatric sequelae. Mood disturbances such as anxiety and depression are relatively common in the postoperative period, affecting a significant portion of patients even after euthyroidism is achieved [1]. However, the emergence of severe psychiatric syndromes, particularly acute psychosis, is a rare but profound complication that presents a formidable diagnostic and therapeutic challenge [2–5].

The pathophysiology of post-thyroidectomy psychosis is multifactorial, typically rooted in the acute metabolic derangements that follow the removal of the thyroid and potential incidental damage to the parathyroid glands. Two primary pathophysiological pillars underpin these presentations. The first is iatrogenic hypothyroidism, an inevitable consequence of total thyroidectomy. The abrupt cessation of thyroid hormone production can lead to a state of profound central nervous system (CNS) dysfunction. In its most severe form, this can manifest as an acute psychosis, a condition historically termed "myxedema madness" [3, 5, 6]. This syndrome, while well described, remains a rare clinical entity, often characterized by delusions, hallucinations, and cognitive disorganization [7].

The second pillar is iatrogenic hypoparathyroidism, which results from the inadvertent devascularization or removal of the parathyroid glands during surgery. This leads to deficient parathyroid hormone (PTH) secretion and subsequent hypocalcemia [2, 8]. Hypocalcemia is a potent disruptor of neuronal function, capable of inducing a wide array of neuropsychiatric symptoms, including delirium, emotional lability, psychosis, and, critically, seizures due to increased neuronal excitability [9].

While hypothyroidism and hypocalcemia are recognized as individual causes of postoperative psychosis, the clinical scenario in which both severe insults occur simultaneously is not well documented in the literature. This convergence creates a "perfect storm" for neuropsychiatric decompensation, where the cerebral metabolic depression of hypothyroidism may synergize with the neuronal hyperexcitability of hypocalcemia to produce a uniquely severe and complex clinical picture.

Here, we present the case of a 59-year-old Arab male who, one month after total thyroidectomy, developed acute psychosis with prominent convulsive episodes, driven by concurrent severe hypothyroidism and hypocalcemia. The unique cyclical nature of his presentation, followed by post-episode amnesia and somnolence, offers critical insights into the synergistic effects of these dual metabolic insults and represents a novel aspect of this presentation.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old Arab married male, a farmer with a history of active smoking, presented to our department with a long-standing, untreated right-sided neck mass (Fig. 1). The mass had been progressively enlarging, leading to symptoms of partial airway obstruction. He had delayed seeking medical attention. His past medical, surgical, and family histories were unremarkable. Critically, there was no personal or family history of psychiatric illness.

The mass before the surgery

Pre-operative evaluation included an ultrasound of the neck, which revealed a large cystic mass measuring 6.5 cm × 10.5 cm originating from the right thyroid lobe. A subsequent computed tomography (CT) scan confirmed the presence of a large, complex cystic and solid mass measuring 8 cm × 10 cm, arising from the right lobe of the thyroid (Fig. 2). The mass contained calcifications and caused significant displacement and compression of the trachea and adjacent vascular structures. A separate, smaller 2.5 cm nodule was identified in the left thyroid lobe. Given the size of the goiter, the compressive symptoms, and the presence of bilateral nodular disease, the patient was scheduled for a total thyroidectomy. The surgery was performed without any reported intraoperative complications (Fig. 3). The histopathology of the resected gland was consistent with a benign multinodular goiter.

The mass after the surgery

One month following the uneventful surgery and discharge, the patient's family brought him to the emergency department with a dramatic change in mental status and behavior. They reported the acute onset of severe agitation and confusion. The patient exhibited bizarre and disinhibited behaviors, including episodes of inappropriate singing and clapping, which were interspersed with periods of intense aggression directed toward family members and the destruction of household property. Furthermore, he experienced episodes described by the family as convulsions.

A unique and striking pattern emerged in his presentation. These severe neuropsychiatric episodes recurred on a monthly basis. Each florid episode would last for a period of hours to days and was invariably followed by a state of prolonged sleep lasting up to 24–48 h. Upon waking, the patient had complete amnesia for the events of the episode.

Upon presentation to the emergency department during one of these episodes, a comprehensive diagnostic workup was initiated. The laboratory findings were pivotal and provided a clear metabolic basis for his presentation. Blood tests revealed a markedly elevated Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone (TSH) level of > 75 mIU/L, confirming severe iatrogenic hypothyroidism. Concurrently, his serum calcium level was found to be significantly low at 6.1 mg/dL, consistent with postoperative hypoparathyroidism.

The differential diagnosis was broad. Primary psychiatric disorders were initially considered due to the florid and acute nature of the psychotic symptoms, which can be indistinguishable from primary psychoses like schizophrenia at initial presentation. Other considerations included Postoperative Delirium and a Primary Seizure Disorder.

Based on the clinical picture and definitive laboratory evidence, a final diagnosis of Psychotic Disorder specifically caused by concurrent severe hypothyroidism and hypocalcemia was established.

A multidisciplinary treatment plan was immediately implemented to address both the underlying metabolic derangements and the acute psychiatric symptoms. The patient was started on presumptive hormone and electrolyte replacement, including oral levothyroxine for hypothyroidism and oral calcium with vitamin D supplementation for hypocalcemia.

For the acute and severe behavioral disturbance, a combined psychopharmacological regimen was initiated, consisting of the atypical antipsychotics olanzapine 10 mg daily and aripiprazole 15 mg daily, along with the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) sertraline 50 mg daily. This combination was chosen to target the psychotic and agitated symptoms effectively while minimizing side effect burden.

The patient's response to this comprehensive treatment was remarkable. Two days after the initiation of antipsychotic medications, he experienced another, albeit much milder, episode of agitation. Over the subsequent weeks, with continued metabolic correction and psychiatric support, his condition steadily improved. At his two-month follow-up appointment, the aggressive behaviors, bizarre conduct, and convulsive episodes had completely ceased. The cyclical pattern of episodes followed by prolonged sleep and amnesia was fully resolved. He had returned to his baseline daily functioning and was no longer a danger to himself or his family. This robust and complete response to the combined therapeutic strategy strongly validated the diagnosis of a psychosis secondary to the dual metabolic insults. Discussion and conclusion

This case presents a severe neuropsychiatric syndrome following total thyroidectomy, driven by the synergistic effects of concurrent iatrogenic hypothyroidism and hypocalcemia. While thyroidectomy is a common procedure, neuropsychiatric complications, though rare, can be devastating without prompt recognition and treatment [1, 5, 10].

The mixed presentation—florid psychosis with convulsive activity—is best understood as a synergistic outcome of dual metabolic insults. Hypothyroidism leads to cerebral metabolic depression and neurotransmitter dysregulation, historically termed "myxedema madness" [3, 6, 11–13]. Thyroid hormones critically modulate serotonin, norepinephrine, and dopamine systems [14–16]; their acute deficiency disrupts limbic and cortical function, precipitating psychosis [17–19].

Concurrently, hypocalcemia due to postoperative hypoparathyroidism induces neuronal hyperexcitability and lowers the seizure threshold—a phenomenon known as the "calcium paradox" [20–22]. Low extracellular calcium reduces the screening effect on neuronal membrane surface charges, causing voltage-gated sodium (NaV) channels to activate at more negative potentials [23, 24]. This lowers the neuronal firing threshold, leading to hyperexcitability and predisposing to convulsive activity [9, 25–27].

The unique cyclical pattern of monthly episodes, each followed by prolonged sleep and amnesia, suggests a potential epileptiform process—such as non-convulsive status epilepticus or complex partial seizures with postictal psychosis—masquerading as pure psychiatric illness [28–31]. This pattern underscores the importance of considering EEG monitoring in similar presentations.

Diagnosis relied on key differentiating factors: late age of onset, absent psychiatric history, clear temporal link to surgery, identifiable metabolic abnormalities, and complete resolution with treatment [11, 32–34]. Management followed a multidisciplinary approach: levothyroxine for hypothyroidism; calcium and calcitriol for hypocalcemia; and short-term antipsychotics (olanzapine and aripiprazole) for acute stabilization [35–38]. The prognosis for such secondary psychosis is generally excellent with metabolic correction, though subtle cognitive sequelae may occasionally persist [5, 39, 40].

This case underscores the necessity of dual-parameter postoperative monitoring (TSH and calcium) after total thyroidectomy. A high index of suspicion for combined metabolic derangements is essential. Management requires a coordinated, multidisciplinary strategy to address both underlying causes and acute behavioral manifestations.
