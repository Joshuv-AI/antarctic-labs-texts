Effect of Roux-En-Y Gastric Bypass on Albuminuria in Morbidly Obese Patients with Type-2 Diabetes Mellitus: A Prospective Non-Randomized Controlled Study with 15-Month Follow-Up
Authors: Ahmed Abdallah Salman, Mohamed Abdalla Salman, Ahmed E Taha, Asmaa Awwad, Ahmed Marwan, Ahmed Elewa, Mona Yousry, Ahmed Safina Abdallah, Ahmed Youssef
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity increases the risk of chronic kidney disease and has been linked to proteinuria and albuminuria. Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) plays a significant role in the treatment of obesity and type-2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). This study aimed to examine the effect of RYGB on albuminuria in patients with morbid obesity and T2DM.MethodsThis prospective, non-randomized, controlled study included two groups of patients with morbid obesity and T2DM; allocation was by patient preference rather than randomization. The RYGB Group (n = 64) underwent RYGB, and the Control Group (n = 58) received conservative (lifestyle) measures only. Micro- and macroalbuminuria were determined from spot urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) on a first-morning urine sample. Patients were monitored for 15 months for remission and improvement of albuminuria. Groups were compared using the independent-sample t-test, Mann–Whitney test, or chi-square test as appropriate, and multivariable logistic regression was used to adjust for baseline differences.ResultsThe groups differed significantly at baseline in age, body mass index (BMI), HOMA-IR index, LDL-cholesterol, and ALT. RYGB achieved a significantly (p < 0.001) greater rate of albuminuria remission (57.8%) than diet control (24.1%). None of the baseline characteristics were associated with albuminuria remission except lower baseline triglyceride (p = 0.012) and LDL-cholesterol (p = 0.040) levels. Albuminuria remission was associated with significantly greater weight loss (p = 0.003) and lower BMI (p = 0.019), fasting plasma insulin (p = 0.014), HOMA-IR (p = 0.011), total cholesterol (p = 0.026), LDL-cholesterol (p = 0.001), ALT (p = 0.033), and AST (p = 0.009). After adjustment for baseline differences, RYGB remained the only independent predictor of albuminuria remission (adjusted odds ratio 3.27, 95% CI 1.23–8.67; p = 0.017).ConclusionIn this non-randomized cohort, RYGB was associated with a significantly higher rate of albuminuria remission than lifestyle intervention over 15 months. Remission was associated with greater weight loss, lower plasma insulin, and improved insulin sensitivity rather than with diabetes remission itself. Randomized or better-adjusted prospective studies are warranted to confirm these findings.Keywords: Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, type-2 diabetes, bariatric surgery, morbid obesity, diabetic nephropathy, albuminuria

Introduction

Obesity, the epidemic of the 21st century, is a systemic disease associated with a high risk of comorbidities such as type-2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease, and chronic kidney disease (CKD).1 Obesity has been reported to increase the risk of CKD two- to three-fold.2,3 Although the mechanism is not fully understood, obesity can affect the kidney indirectly through the development of diabetes and hypertension2 and directly through incompletely defined molecular pathways.4 It has been linked to glomerular hyperfiltration,5 proteinuria and microalbuminuria,6 and, in patients with T2DM, obesity-related indices correlate with albuminuria and more advanced kidney disease,7 ultimately contributing to loss of kidney function and progression to end-stage renal disease.8

Microvascular complications, including nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy, are eventual consequences of T2DM.9 Microalbuminuria is an early marker of diabetic kidney disease and is a frequent finding in patients with T2DM,10,11 and it also signals an increased cardiovascular risk.12

In obese patients with impaired kidney function, bariatric surgery may prevent deterioration by improving glomerular hyperfiltration and reducing proteinuria and albuminuria,13 and it can also improve albuminuria in patients with T2DM.14

Owing to its consistent long-term outcomes, Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) is frequently regarded as the “gold standard” bariatric procedure,15 with an estimated weight loss of about 56.7%.16 It improves glycemic control, blood pressure, and lipid profile and reduces the need for antidiabetic medication,17 and the ARMMS-T2D study reported higher diabetes remission after RYGB than after sleeve gastrectomy or adjustable gastric banding.18 RYGB nonetheless carries short- and long-term risks, including surgical complications, nutritional deficiencies, and dumping syndrome.19–21

Despite this evidence, data on the effect of bariatric surgery on albuminuria remain heterogeneous and inconclusive: most prior studies are small, retrospective, or of short follow-up, combine diabetic and non-diabetic patients, and frequently lack a concurrent non-surgical comparator. We previously reported the impact of sleeve gastrectomy and one-anastomosis gastric bypass on albuminuria,22 but the specific effect of RYGB in morbidly obese patients with T2DM, relative to a contemporaneously managed control group, has not been well defined. The present study therefore examined the effect of RYGB on albuminuria in morbidly obese patients with T2DM, compared with lifestyle intervention, in a 15-month, prospective, non-randomized, controlled study.

Patients and Methods

This prospective, non-randomized, controlled clinical study was conducted from May 2024 to August 2025. It enrolled morbidly obese patients with T2DM, aged 18–60 years, who were found to have albuminuria. Participants were allocated to two groups according to their subsequent management and their own preference: the RYGB Group comprised patients scheduled for RYGB, and the Control Group comprised patients who preferred to avoid surgery and undertook lifestyle intervention. Because allocation was based on patient preference rather than randomization, the potential for selection bias is acknowledged and is addressed in the analysis and limitations. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The protocol was approved by the Cairo University Research Ethics Committee (approval N-90-2024), obtained before the start of enrollment, and written informed consent was obtained from every participant. All patients were followed for at least 15 months.

Well-informed and motivated patients with T2DM and morbid obesity (body mass index ≥ 35 kg/m2) who had attempted appropriate non-surgical measures but were unable to achieve adequate, clinically beneficial weight loss, and who had no endocrine cause for obesity, were eligible. Patients were excluded if they were psychologically unstable, unwilling to alter their lifestyle after the operation, had a history of bariatric surgery, were pregnant or lactating at screening or surgery, or had severe medical conditions that made anesthesia risky.

The American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria were used to diagnose T2DM based on fasting plasma glucose (FPG). Clinical diabetes was defined as an FPG of 126 mg/dL or an HbA1c of 6.5%, confirmed by repeated sampling, unless the patient had clinical symptoms, a glucose level of 200 mg/dL, a history of diabetes, and/or was taking diabetes medications.23 Remission was defined as improvement in diabetes per ADA guidance (HbA1c < 5.7% without diabetes medication) at the conclusion of the study.24

Data collected included demographics and pre- and post-operative FPG, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), urine albumin/creatinine ratio (uACR), blood pressure, cholesterol levels, and body weight. Preoperative and 15-month postoperative data were compared in both groups.

Based on spot uACR measurement of a first-morning urine sample, micro- and macroalbuminuria were determined. For males, microalbuminuria was defined as uACR > 2.5–30 mg/mmol and for females > 3.5–30 mg/mmol; macroalbuminuria was defined as uACR > 30 mg/mmol for both sexes.25

Abdominal ultrasound was performed for all patients to assess parenchymal echogenicity and size and to exclude post-renal obstruction. Patients were categorized as improved or not improved according to the change in albuminuria 15 months after treatment. Improvement was defined as a decrease in macroalbuminuria level or conversion of macro- to microalbuminuria; remission was defined as conversion of macro- or microalbuminuria to normoalbuminuria. The CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) equation was used to estimate GFR from serum creatinine, age, sex, and race,26 as creatinine-based formulas such as the MDRD study equation may underestimate GFR in obese patients with T2DM.27

The gastric bypass technique involved creation of a restrictive component (a small gastric pouch) and a malabsorptive component (bypass of the gastric remnant, duodenum, and proximal small bowel). The degree of malabsorption depends on the length of the Roux limb (120–150 cm). Our standard laparoscopic proximal gastric bypass used a 25-mL gastric pouch.

Diet Regimen and Medical Management in the Control Group

A diet and exercise regimen was prescribed and supervised by our dietitians as part of the lifestyle intervention. Moderate-intensity exercise for at least 30 minutes on at least five days per week, together with a diet providing 1200–1500 kcal/day, was initially advised, and was subsequently adjusted according to weight change, daily calorie intake, and exercise intensity. The weight trend of these patients was assessed in the clinic every two weeks, and adherence to the dietary and exercise regimen was reviewed and reinforced at these visits. Throughout follow-up, glucose-lowering therapy (oral agents and/or insulin) and antihypertensive treatment were adjusted by the treating physicians according to standard care.

All patients in both groups received an angiotensin-receptor blocker (ARB); candesartan (Candesar 8 mg, PHAROPHARMA, Egypt) was started at 8 mg once daily and titrated gradually to the maximally tolerated dose, not exceeding 32 mg/day. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists were not used as part of the study protocol.

Sample Size Estimation

Prior data indicated an albuminuria remission rate of 0.82 after bariatric surgery and 0.60 among diet-regimen controls.28 On this basis, a sample of 66 subjects per group was required to reject the null hypothesis of equal remission rates with a power of 0.8 and a Type I error of 0.05. The sample was increased by 10% to accommodate probable loss to follow-up, giving a target of 73 patients per group.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analysis was performed with IBM SPSS Statistics version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Numerical data are presented as mean and standard deviation or median and range, as appropriate, and qualitative data as frequency and percentage. Associations between qualitative variables were examined with the chi-square test. The independent-sample t-test or Mann–Whitney test was used to compare two groups of quantitative data. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of albuminuria remission; variables significantly associated with remission on univariate analysis, together with clinically relevant baseline covariates that differed between the groups (age, BMI, HOMA-IR, LDL-cholesterol, and ALT), were entered into the model. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used for risk estimation. Analyses were performed on a per-protocol basis for participants who completed the 15-month follow-up; these participants had complete data for the study variables, so no imputation for missing data was required. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 167 patients were evaluated for eligibility; 21 were excluded (17 for not meeting inclusion criteria and 4 who declined to participate). The remaining 146 participants were split into two groups of 73 according to the treatment protocol. Nine participants in the RYGB Group did not complete the 15-month follow-up. In the Control Group, 5 patients were lost to follow-up and 10 discontinued treatment and were scheduled for bariatric surgery. The final analysis included 64 patients in the RYGB Group and 58 in the Control Group (Figure 1).

Discussion

In this cohort of morbidly obese patients with T2DM, RYGB was associated with albuminuria remission in 57.8% of patients, compared with 24.1% in the Control Group. Remission was associated with greater weight reduction and with declines in hyperinsulinemia, insulin resistance, and hyperlipidemia. RYGB was the only independent predictor of albuminuria remission in the adjusted model.

To our knowledge, few studies have addressed the effect of bariatric surgery on albuminuria, especially in diabetic patients, and the available data are diverse and inconclusive. Many previous studies had small samples and/or short follow-up and included both diabetic and non-diabetic subjects. In a group of 92 non-diabetic patients undergoing bariatric surgery, micro- and macroalbuminuria were found in 41% and 4% of patients, respectively, with significant improvement two years after surgery.29 A similar improvement was reported in another study.30 Mohan et al observed significant improvement in albuminuria as early as 30 days after RYGB in 38 non-diabetic patients.31

Other retrospective studies confirmed a reduction in albuminuria,32 particularly in patients with diabetes and metabolic syndrome.33 A pilot study of 15 severely obese patients with T2DM reported a significant reduction in uACR six months after RYGB that correlated with improvement in insulin sensitivity.34 Another retrospective study of 52 diabetic obese patients identified micro- and macroalbuminuria in 31.3% and 6.3% of patients, with marked improvement after bariatric surgery in 58.3%; this improvement was associated with remission and improvement of diabetes and hypertension.35 This was a point of difference with the current study, in which we did not observe a significant relationship between improvement of diabetes or hypertension and remission of albuminuria.

Another prospective study of 96 patients reported albuminuria remission of 58.5% and 76.9% at one and two years, respectively, although albuminuria was defined as ACR > 30 mg/dL; an important conclusion was that BMI reduction was the only independent predictor of ACR normalization at 12 months.36 In a large study of 1307 patients, 16% had microalbuminuria and 3.1% macroalbuminuria, rising to 28% in the diabetic subgroup; a significant decrease in albumin levels two years after surgery was observed in 318 patients (64 with diabetes), and patients undergoing gastric bypass showed a steeper decline in albumin excretion than those undergoing sleeve gastrectomy.37

Remission of diabetes itself (HbA1c < 5.7% without medication) was not associated with albuminuria remission in our cohort (Table 4), and neither was improvement of hypertension. This dissociation suggests that the reduction in albumin excretion after RYGB is not driven by normalization of glycemia alone; rather, it appears to be linked to the magnitude of weight loss and to improvements in insulin sensitivity and lipid profile, together with the favorable renal-hemodynamic effects of marked weight reduction. Similar observations, with improvement in albuminuria tied more closely to weight loss and metabolic improvement than to diabetes remission itself, have been reported previously.36

All participants in both groups received the ARB candesartan, titrated as tolerated. ARBs reduce albuminuria through both blood-pressure-dependent and blood-pressure-independent (intraglomerular pressure-lowering) mechanisms, and their use could have contributed to the reductions in albumin excretion in both arms. Because ARB therapy was applied uniformly to both groups, however, it is unlikely to explain the significantly greater remission after RYGB, which is more plausibly attributable to surgery-induced weight loss and metabolic improvement. Nonetheless, differences between groups in the degree of ARB up-titration were not controlled for and may have influenced the results, which we acknowledge as a limitation.

Our study showed a significant relationship between remission of albuminuria and improvement of insulin resistance measured by HOMA-IR. Insulin resistance disturbs the balance of endogenous vasoactive substances in the renal endothelium, leading to oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction.38 Insulin resistance and hyperglycemia induce mesangial expansion, perhaps through increased matrix production or glycation of matrix proteins, contributing to the progression of diabetic nephropathy.39 Therefore, improvement of insulin sensitivity may contribute to the reduction in albuminuria observed in the current study, although estimated GFR did not change significantly over the follow-up period (Table 4).

In contrast to much of the existing literature, this study specifically targeted diabetic patients with morbid obesity (n = 122), included a concurrent comparison group, and applied an adequate follow-up duration, features that strengthen the internal validity of the comparison. Our findings indicate that improvements in insulin sensitivity, hyperinsulinemia, and hyperglycemia, together with substantial weight loss, are the factors most consistently associated with improvement in albuminuria, irrespective of frank remission of diabetes or hypertension. In the adjusted analysis, bariatric surgery was the only independent factor associated with remission of albuminuria.

This study has several limitations. First, allocation was not randomized but was based on patient preference, introducing potential selection bias and confounding by indication; despite adjustment for measured baseline differences, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the analysis was per-protocol rather than intention-to-treat. Second, there were baseline imbalances between groups (age, BMI, HOMA-IR, LDL-cholesterol, and ALT); although these variables were entered into the multivariable model, unmeasured confounders may remain. Third, urinary albumin was determined from a single first-morning specimen at each time point. Although the first-morning sample is more reliable than a random spot sample and is acceptable for tracking within-patient change over time, a single measurement may misclassify albuminuria status and cannot exclude regression to the mean as a contributor to the observed improvement, particularly given the higher baseline values. Fourth, the final analyzed RYGB group comprised 64 patients, marginally below the calculated minimum of 66 per group, which may modestly reduce statistical power. Fifth, concomitant use of glucose-lowering agents with known renal effects (eg, SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP-1 receptor agonists) and the degree of ARB up-titration were not fully accounted for in the analysis. Finally, adipokine levels, which have been implicated in the pathogenesis of albuminuria in morbidly obese patients with T2DM, were not measured.34 Early assessment of albumin excretion and longer follow-up would provide a clearer picture of the trajectory of kidney outcomes.

Conclusion

In this 15-month, prospective, non-randomized, controlled study, RYGB was associated with a significantly higher rate of albuminuria remission (57.8%) than lifestyle intervention (24.1%) in morbidly obese patients with T2DM, and it remained the only independent predictor of remission after adjustment for baseline differences. Remission of albuminuria was associated with greater weight loss, lower plasma insulin, and improved insulin sensitivity rather than with diabetes remission itself. These observational findings should be interpreted with caution given the non-randomized design and baseline imbalances, and they require confirmation in randomized or better-adjusted prospective studies.

Novelty of the Study

This study contributes to the limited and inconclusive evidence on bariatric surgery and albuminuria. Unlike most previous reports, which are small, retrospective, of short duration, or combine diabetic and non-diabetic patients, it is a prospective, controlled investigation conducted specifically in morbidly obese patients with type-2 diabetes and established albuminuria, with a concurrently managed lifestyle-intervention comparator and a 15-month follow-up. Because all participants in both arms received a uniformly titrated angiotensin-receptor blocker, the greater albuminuria remission after RYGB is unlikely to be explained by renin-angiotensin blockade alone, which supports an additional, weight-loss-mediated renal benefit of surgery. After adjustment for baseline imbalances, RYGB was the only independent predictor of albuminuria remission. Remission of albuminuria was also associated with the magnitude of weight loss and with improvements in insulin sensitivity and lipid profile rather than with remission of diabetes or hypertension, a mechanistic observation that helps explain why albuminuria may improve after RYGB even without full diabetes remission. These findings support a role for RYGB in the early management of diabetic kidney disease in this population and provide a rationale for future randomized or better-adjusted prospective studies.
