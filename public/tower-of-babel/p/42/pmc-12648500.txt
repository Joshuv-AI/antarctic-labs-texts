Efficacy of faricimab for recalcitrant multifactorial cystoid macular edema☆
Authors: Justin Hanson, Marcus Yamamoto, Edmund Tsui, Irena Tsui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMore information is emerging on faricimab-svoa (faricimab) used for etiologies beyond wet age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and diabetic macular edema (DME), including ongoing trials for use in macular edema following retinal vein occlusion (RVO). This manuscript describes a case of recalcitrant postoperative cystoid macular edema (CME), confounded by a history of branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO), in which faricimab resulted in resolution of CME after incomplete response to topical anti-inflammation drops, intravitreal aflibercept and bevacizumab, a corticosteroid intravitreal implant, grid laser therapy, and suprachoroidal triamcinolone acetonide (SCS-TA).ObservationsAn 83-year-old pseudophakic male with history of steroid-induced ocular hypertension and BRVO with mild preoperative CME and visual acuity (VA) of 20/160 presented with significant worsening CME following a pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) and internal limiting membrane (ILM) peel for an epiretinal membrane (ERM) in the right eye. CME persisted for eight years despite treatment with topical anti-inflammatories, anti-VEGF injections, a dexamethasone intravitreal implant, grid laser therapy, and a SCS-TA injection. However, after 3 faricimab injections, CME resolved with a CST change from 748 μm to 339 μm and VA improved from 20/50 to 20/40.Conclusions and importanceThis case demonstrates the effectiveness of faricimab in treatment of CME resistant to other therapies and the importance of continued attempts at using new agents for chronic, recurrent CME. The unique benefits of new agents like faricimab, with both VEGF and Ang-2 inhibition, may be particularly helpful in mixed or inflammatory CME when other proven therapies have failed.Keywords: Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor, Branch retinal vein occlusion, Dexamethasone intravitreal implant, Postoperative cystoid macular edema, Suprachoroidal triamcinolone, Faricimab

Introduction

Cystoid macular edema (CME) is a relatively common cause of vision loss following retina surgery, with studies reporting 10–17 % of patients developing CME following pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) and internal limiting membrane (ILM) peel.1,2 Although multiple therapies including both topical and intravitreal medications have been effective for treating postoperative CME, there remains a lack of data on the approach to multifactorial CME in postoperative patients with chronic confounding disease like retinal vein occlusion (RVO) and/or diabetes. In addition, CME is the most common cause of vision loss in branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO), with one study reporting CME in up to 67 % of affected eyes.3 Furthermore, vitreoretinal surface abnormalities like an epiretinal membrane (ERM) are also associated with BRVO and can make treatment of an RVO more challenging, sometimes requiring additional injections.4

Recurrence or need for re-treatment for multi-focal etiologies of CME ranges anywhere between 14 and 62 % depending on the type of macular edema.5,6 Postoperative CME following PPV often recurs despite treatment, with one study reporting short term benefits of intravitreal triamcinolone acetonide and no added benefit with bevacizumab.7 Recalcitrant CME due to multifactorial etiologies is an area for patient care improvement.

Current therapies for CME include topical non-steroidal anti-inflammatories and corticosteroids, intravitreal corticosteroids like triamcinolone or anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and sustained-release drug delivery systems like the dexamethasone intravitreal implant. Other methods of drug delivery, including suprachoroidal triamcinolone acetonide (SCS-TA), have been shown to improve postoperative CME and also reduce macular edema due to RVO without significant intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation.8,9 Novel therapies targeting multiple areas of angiogenesis such as faricimab for macular edema due to RVO have been studied through clinical trials.10 It is the first humanized, bispecific, immunoglobulin G monoclonal antibody designed for intravitreal injection, which binds and neutralizes both Angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2) and VEGF, enabling dual inhibition of pathways involved in RVO.10 However, there are no reported cases of it being used for postoperative CME. This report details a case in which faricimab demonstrated significant efficacy for recalcitrant postoperative CME, in a patient with a history of BRVO and failure of multiple different therapies to resolve CME including topical anti-inflammatories, aflibercept and bevacizumab, dexamethasone intravitreal implants, grid laser therapy, and SCS-TA.

Case report

An 83-year-old male with a history of steroid-induced ocular hypertension and BRVO many years prior in the right eye, initially presented with an ERM in the right eye with decreased vision (visual acuity (VA) of 20/160) and metamorphopsia. The patient had no history of diabetes and was pseudophakic in both eyes. There was also preoperative CME at baseline (central subfield thickness (CST) on optical coherence tomography before surgery was 519 μm) (Fig. 1A). One week following a PPV with ILM peel, CST was 620 μm, with CME managed with topical prednisolone acetate and ketorolac. Three months following surgery, CST improved to 502 μm, however, by five months post-operation, CST increased to 622 μm (Fig. 1B).

Optical coherence tomography imaging of the right eye from A) before pars plana vitrectomy and internal limiting membrane peel, B) five months post-operation C) after seven aflibercept injections, D) after three bevacizumab injections, E) after a dexamethasone intravitreal implant, F) after grid laser therapy, G) before suprachoroidal triamcinolone acetonide (SCS-TA), H) four weeks after SCS-TA injection, I) before faricimab injection, and J) eighteen weeks after faricimab injection.

Given the persistent CME in the right eye despite topical medications and the patient's history of glaucoma, anti-VEGF injections were trialed. Following seven total aflibercept injections, the maximum reduction in CST was 154 μm, with minimum CST of 455 μm. There was significant IOP elevation (IOP ≥21 mmHg and ≥10 mmHg IOP elevation from baseline) following one of the injections, with IOP of 34 mmHg, and CME recurred (Fig. 1C). An additional series of three bevacizumab injections resulted in a 20 μm reduction in CST, and minimum CST of 486 μm. However, CME recurred again 28 weeks following bevacizumab initiation (Fig. 1D). Given minimal improvement of CME despite multiple topical and intravitreal anti-VEGF therapies, the dexamethasone intravitreal implant was trialed. Of note, there was no concomitant topical therapy at the time of intravitreal anti-VEGF or dexamethasone implant injections.

Ultimately, there was persistent CME at both 1-month and 2-months following injection of the dexamethasone intravitreal implant (Fig. 1E). Maximum improvement in CST following the dexamethasone intravitreal implant was 33 μm two months following injection. Grid laser was then trialed and resulted in no improvement in CME during the three months following treatment (Fig. 1F). Six months following grid laser therapy, CME worsened, and CST was at the highest since the initial surgery (677 μm).

Persistent CME was present seven years after PPV and 3.5 years following the last injection. However, the patient's vision remained stable, with VA of 20/60, and he elected for no further treatment during this time. When SCS-TA became available, this was offered to the patient. CST before SCS-TA injection was 689 μm (Fig. 1G). Three months following SCS-TA injection, there was 235 μm improvement in CST (minimum CST of 454 μm (Fig. 1H)), with improved VA from 20/50 to 20/30 (best postoperative vision), no signs of worsening CME, and IOP of 18 mmHg. There was no increase in IOP. The patient was offered a second injection but declined due to the long travel distance from the clinic and lack of subjective improvement given that his other eye saw 20/20.

Three months after the SCS-TA injection, CME recurred with worsening VA (20/50) and CST (598 μm). The patient preferred to not receive additional SCS-TA and 2 months later CME progressed further with CST of 748 μm. Given the patient's preference to not receive additional SCS-TA, intravitreal faricimab (6.0 mg/0.05 mL) was instead injected. 1 month after the first faricimab injection, CST improved to 461 μm and IOP remained the same as before the injection (17 mmHg). A second dose was given and 7 weeks later CST improved further to 348 μm with VA of 20/40 and IOP of 19 mmHg. A third dose was given and another 7 weeks later, CST was 339 μm (lowest CST since initiating any therapy) with VA of 20/40 and IOP of 19 mmHg.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the safety and efficacy of the faricimab intravitreal injection to treat recalcitrant postoperative CME, in a patient with a history of BRVO, steroid response, and multiple failed trials of other standard treatments for CME. This case was primarily difficult to manage because of the mixed nature and persistence of the patient's CME. At first, the CME was thought to be a result of postoperative inflammation following PPV and ILM peel. However, it also could have been further confounded by CME from previous BRVO or persistent vitreomacular traction. The CME was resistant to various drug delivery systems including topical, suprachoroidal, intravitreal, sustained-release implants, and laser therapies. There are multiple similar reports in which RVO and postoperative inflammation results in persistent CME despite intravitreal anti-VEGF therapies and intravitreal corticosteroid.7,11

There was relative efficacy of SCS-TA for treating CME in our patient. In clinical trials examining SCS-TA for uveitic CME, treatment demonstrated notable reductions in CST through 24 weeks.12 Studies thus far examining SCS-TA for postoperative CME or RVO have shown even greater CST reduction through 12 weeks.8,13 In our patient, there was a 235 μm improvement in CST only four weeks following SCS-TA injection. One SCS-TA injection resulted in a greater reduction in CST and lower minimum CST than seven aflibercept injections. SCS-TA also resulted in greater improvement of CME compared to the dexamethasone intravitreal implant. It is possible that more localized exposure to the site of inflammation resulted in greater medication effect. In this case, it could have also been that the dexamethasone implant in a vitrectomized eye may have been limited due to anterior migration of the implant. However, there was still residual CME and the effects of SCS-TA diminished by 12 weeks following injection, and the CME recurred.

While our patient only experienced a modest reduction in CST following multiple aflibercept and bevacizumab, there was near resolution after only one faricimab injection. It is possible that the dual inhibition of vascular permeability through anti-VEGF and Ang-2 pathways, faricimab provided greater vascular stability than anti-VEGF agents alone. More importantly, Ang-2 contributes to inflammatory signaling and endothelial cell activation.14 Inhibiting Ang-2 signaling would potentially reduce leukocyte adhesion, cytokine expression, and microglial activation – all factors contributing to postoperative CME and CME due to chronic RVO.14 The BALATON and COMINO trials examining faricimab for macular edema due to RVO is hoping to show improved anatomic outcomes and longer durability.10 This may explain the benefits in our patient who was extremely treatment-resistant and with multiple episodes of recurrence with other treatment modalities.

For neovascular AMD and DME only partially responsive to anti-VEGF therapy, faricimab has been shown to improve CST further.15 It is possible there may be a similar superior effect for resistant eyes with postoperative edema or edema from RVO. There are also studies which demonstrate the combined effects of steroid and anti-VEGF therapies.16,17 However, faricimab was given 20 weeks following SCS-TA so a synergic effect was also not likely between faricimab and SCS-TA. The SAPPHIRE clinical trial investigating SCS-TA and aflibercept for macular edema following RVO was terminated given no additional benefit for subjects receiving a corticosteroid together with anti-VEGF.18

The results of this case demonstrate that faricimab may be effective as acute monotherapy for reducing postoperative CME following retina surgery, potentially improving both macular thickness and visual acuity without elevation in IOP in a known steroid responder. Furthermore, it demonstrates the effectiveness of faricimab in treatment of macular edema resistant to other anti-VEGF therapy and even after 8 years of duration. Ang-2 inhibition may be particularly helpful in mixed or inflammatory CME. Ultimately, further studies are needed to investigate the optimal medication regimen for recalcitrant edema and support these positive findings.
