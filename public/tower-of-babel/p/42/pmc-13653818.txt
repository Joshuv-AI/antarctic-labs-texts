Biocontrol potential of Trichoderma erinaceum It-58 and T. afroharzianum P-8 against Neoscytalidium sp., the pathogen responsible for root rot in cucumber (Cucumis sativus)
Authors: Moïse Ntah A Ayong, Marie Amperes Boat Bedine, Victor Davy Moussango, Henri Tibo Ambata Ambata, Georges JuniorChakoua, Severin Nguemezi Tchameni, Modeste Lambert Sameza, Wansi Jean Duplex
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGreen agricultural development requires safe, effective, and environmentally friendly plant disease control measures. This study evaluated the antagonistic properties of Trichoderma erinaceum It-58 and Trichoderma afroharzianum P-8 against Neoscytalidium sp., as well as their ability to promote growth and induce biochemical resistance in cucumber seedlings. The pathogen responsible for both cucumber fruit rot and seed damping off was isolated and identified morphologically. The antagonistic potential of the Trichoderma strains was assessed using dual culture, volatile, and non-volatile organic compound methods. For pot experiments, seeds coated with antagonists were pre-germinated and transplanted. Twenty-one days later, roots were infected with the pathogen. Fifteen days post-infection, seedlings were harvested to evaluate growth parameters (height, biomass, and diameter) and biochemical parameters (polyphenols, proteins, peroxidases, polyphenol oxidases). The pathogen was identified as Neoscytalidium sp.. In dual culture, T. erinaceum and T. afroharzanium inhibited the pathogen by 74.36% and 68.20%, respectively. Diffusible non-volatile substances showed even greater inhibition of mycelial growth at 71% (T. erinaceum) and 97% (T. afroharzanium). No inhibition was observed with volatile organic compounds. In pot experiments, both antagonists significantly stimulated plant growth parameters and reduced disease incidence (16.67% for T. afroharzianum, 33.33% for T. erinaceum). They induced a significant increase in polyphenol content while significantly decreasing peroxidase and polyphenol oxidase activities in treated and infected plants compared to controls. There was a significant positive correlation between disease incidence, polyphenol content, and peroxidase activity, and a negative correlation with polyphenol oxidase. Thus, T. erinaceum and T. afroharzanium can effectively promote cucumber growth and prevent seedling wilt caused by Neoscytalidium sp.. However, molecular identification of the pathogen and field trial are needed.Keywords: Trichoderma, Neoscytalidium sp., Plant growth promotion, Biochemical resistance, Cucumis sativus

Introduction

Cucumber (Cucumis sativus L.) is an herbaceous, creeping, and climbing vegetable crop of the Cucurbitaceae family (Pandey And Kujur 2022). It is recognized as one of the most economically important vegetable crops worldwide. In Cameroon, cucumber cultivation is concentrated in subtropical and tropical regions, particularly in the Northwest, Southwest, West, and Littoral areas. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, global cucumber production reached 94,718,396.55 tons in 2022, with Cameroon contributing an estimated 262,120 tons (FAO 2022). Cucumbers play a vital role in food security by providing essential nutrients that support a healthy diet. They are an excellent source of dietary fiber, vitamins (notably vitamin C and vitamin B6), and minerals including magnesium, calcium, potassium, and iron (Murray And Horswill 2022). In addition to their nutritional value, cucumbers possess medicinal importance due to their rich composition of phenolic compounds and flavonoids, which are well known for their antioxidant properties (Gelaye 2023). These bioactive compounds help preventsof oxidative stress-related disorders, thereby enhancing the functional food value of cucumbers.

Cucumber cultivation is increasingly impacted by climate change, which heightens crop vulnerability to a broad spectrum of pathogenic microorganisms, including viruses, bacteria, nematodes, and filamentous pathogens such as fungi and Oomycetes. The pressure exerted by these phytopathogens reduced yields and compromises the nutritional and commercial quality of cucumbers. Among them, soilborne fungi are considered the most destructive, as they can cause severe yield losses of 15% to 60%, leading to substantial economic damage for farmers (Shi et al. 2019). Numerous fungal phytopathogens infect diverse host species and are responsible for emerging and re-emerging diseases across different geographical regions (Dean et al. 2012; Cacciola And Gullino 2019; Morris And Moury 2019; Fones et al. 2020). Neoscytalidium sp., a tropical and subtropical fungus belonging to the Botryosphaeriaceae family of ascomycetes, has recently emerged as a serious threat to economically important crops, including members of the Cucurbitaceae family such as cucumber (Crous et al. 2006; Mirtalebi et al. 2019; Derviş and Göksel 2023). This phytopathogen infects host tissues either through natural openings and wounds or by secreting cell wall-degrading enzymes such as mannanases, α-glucosidases, and pectate lyases, which facilitate colonization of fruits and soft tissues. Neoscytalidium species can spread via air, soil, seeds, and fruit, ultimately causing root and fruit rot in cucumber plants (Peja et al. 2025).

Management of this pathogen has traditionally relied on synthetic fungicides such as methyl bromide, Ridomil, and Pencozeb. However, the prolonged and indiscriminate use of these chemicals can accelerate the emergence of resistant pathogen strains and poses significant risks to both environmental sustainability and human health (Satapute et al. 2019). In the context of the current climate crisis, it is imperative to reconsider conventional agricultural practices and adopt strategies that safeguard ecological integrity while ensuring consumer safety.

One of the most promising approaches is the use of beneficial microorganisms in biological control. Several bacterial genera, including Agrobacterium, Pseudomonas, Streptomyces, and Bacillus, as well as fungal genera such as Trichoderma, Gliocladium, Ampelomyces, Candida, and Coniothyrium, have demonstrated strong antagonistic potential against plant pathogens (Vinale et al. 2008). Among these, fungi of the genus Trichoderma stand out as particularly effective biocontrol agents due to their multifaceted mechanisms of action and consistent performance in diverse cropping systems (Yao et al. 2023).

Trichoderma species employ multiple modes of action in suppressing plant pathogens, including competition for nutrients and space, secretion of hydrolytic enzymes (e.g., chitinases, cellulases, proteases, lipases), production of volatile and non-volatile antifungal metabolites, direct mycoparasitism, and stimulation of host resistance mechanisms (Yao et al. 2023; Sarma et al. 2014). Induced resistance in plants is typically characterized by the synthesis of defense-related biochemical compounds such as phenolics, soluble proteins, enzymes that regulate oxidative stress, and enzymes associated with pathogenicity responses (Rai et al. 2024). Collectively, these complementary mechanisms contribute to a significant reduction in disease incidence. Beyond their biopesticidal properties, Trichoderma spp. Also exhibit biofertilizing effects by enhancing nutrient solubilization and uptake, thereby improving plant growth and vigor (Nishad et al. 2020). For effective disease management, Trichoderma is commonly applied through seed treatment, biopriming, seedling soaking, soil incorporation, or wound treatment. Among these, seed coating represents a particularly simple and efficient strategy for controlling soil-borne diseases, as propagules of the antagonist colonize the seed surface and subsequently establish in the rhizosphere and root tissues of germinating seedlings (Sanjeev et al. 2009).

The use of Trichoderma not only promotes healthy plant development but also contributes to environmental sustainability by reducing dependence on chemical pesticides (Yao et al. 2023). Within this genus, T. afroharzianum and T. erinaceum have demonstrated strong antagonistic potential, producing volatile and non-volatile metabolites that inhibit the mycelial growth of several common bean pathogens, including Fusarium oxysporum, Fusarium solani, Macrophomina phaseolina, and Pythium ultimum (Bedine et al. 2020). However, their efficacy against Neoscytalidium sp. in cucumber crops remains unexplored.

Accordingly, the present study aims to evaluate the antagonistic potential of T. erinaceum It-58 and T. afroharzianum P-8, assess their ability to promote cucumber plant growth, and determine their effectiveness as seed-coating agents in preventing seedling root rot and wilt by including biochemical resistance markers.

Materials and methods

Trichoderma strains

Two Trichoderma species used in this study were obtained from the core culture collection of the Laboratory of Biochemistry (University of Douala). These antagonist agents were previously isolated from rhizosphere bean plants taken in the center region in Cameroon and identified by Bedine et al. (2020) as Trichoderma erinaceum It-58 (CTCC-SJ-G-HB40350) and Trichoderma afroharzanium P-8 (NBAII TN-9). These strains of Trichoderma were maintained on PDA slants at 4 °C and sub-cultured bimonthly until use.

Seeds damping off pathogen isolation

The pathogens responsible for cucumber rot were isolated and then evaluated for their ability to inhibit cucumber seed germination.

Pathogens were isolated from cucumber fruits harvested in the Littoral Region of Cameroon, displaying symptoms of disease. The tissue fragments (1 to 2 cm) taken from the edges of the lesions were superficially disinfected in ethanol 70% for 60 s, rinsed three times with sterile distilled water, and then inoculated into Petri dishes containing Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) medium (10 ml) supplemented with penicillin and ampicillin (250 g/L). After 3 days of incubation at 28 ± 2 °C, the emerging fungal morphotypes from the tissue fragments were purified by successive subculturing. The pathogenicity test on the fruits, as described by Sameza et al. (2014), was used to assess the ability of the obtained isolates to induce disease. Isolates that induced necrosis were re-isolated, purified through successive subcultures and their spore suspensions were used to select pathogens responsible for inhibiting cucumber seed germination.

The inoculum was obtained by adding 5 ml of sterile distilled water to a 7-day-old pre-culture of the pathogens. Subsequently, the surfaces of the Petri dishes containing these pathogens were scraped with a sterile loop. The resulting solution was filtered using Whatman No. 4 paper. The filtrate was adjusted to 2 × 104 spores/ml with a colloidal carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) solution (2%).

Pathogens that induced pre-germination inhibition of cucumber seeds were isolated according to the method described by Dorrance et al. (2004), with some modifications. Indeed, the apparently healthy cucumber seeds (Variety F1 Classico) were washed with running water, disinfected with 70% alcohol for 1 min, rinsed 3 times with sterile distilled water, and then dried on sterile paper towels. These seeds (10 per batch) were separately immersed 5 ml of a pathogen inoculum solution for one minute. In the control seed lot, the inoculum was replaced with 2% aqueous CMC. All treated seeds were dried under a hood at room temperature (28 ± 2 °C) for two hours, then placed in Petri dishes containing sterile filter paper soaked in sterile distilled water and incubated for 7 days. The pathogens that induced predamping off were selected and subjected to morphological identification using the identification key of Chabasse et al. (2002) and Botton et al. (1990).

In vitro antagonism test

An in vitro antagonist assay was done by dual culture and antibiosis.

Dual culture test

The dual culture assay was performed according to the method described by Ntah et al. (2023). Pathogen and antagonist plugs (5 mm in diameter), excised from the margin of actively growing mycelia (2 days old), and were inoculated on opposite sides of potato dextrose agar (PDA) plates. The cultures were incubated at 28 ± 2 °C for seven days. Control plates were inoculated only with pathogen plugs. Each treatment was replicated three times to ensure reproducibility. Radial growth of both microorganisms was measured, and the time required for Trichoderma to establish contact with the pathogen was recorded. Volatile’s organic compounds test

The antagonist and the pathogen were co-inoculated at the center of 9 cm Petri dishes containing 10 mL of potato dextrose agar (PDA). The dishes, without lids, were stacked and hermetically sealed with two layers of parafilm, then incubated at 28 ± 2 °C for seven days. For the control treatment, the Trichoderma plug was replaced with a sterile agar disc. Pathogen growth inhibition was quantified using the previously described formula (1).

Non-volatiles organics compounds test

Cellophane discs were sterilized and aseptically placed over the entire surface of 10 mL of potato dextrose agar (PDA) medium. A Trichoderma mycelial explant (5 mm in diameter, 2 days old) was positioned at the center of the dish and incubated at 28 ± 2 °C for two days. Following incubation, the membrane containing the Trichoderma mycelium was carefully removed, and a pathogen mycelial disc (5 mm) was subsequently inoculated at the center of the Petri dish. Plates were then incubated at 28 ± 2 °C for three days. Control treatments were prepared by replacing the pathogen disc with a sterile agar plug. The percentage of pathogen growth inhibition was calculated using the previously described formula (1).

Experimental pots

Spore production of Trichoderma strains and pathogenic fungi

Spore production was achieved using liquid culture multiplication in potato dextrose broth (PDB). Conical flasks (1 L) containing 200 mL of sterile PDB medium were inoculated separately with six explants derived from three-day-old precultures of Trichoderma strains and the pathogen. The flasks were incubated at 28 ± 2 °C under stationary conditions for 14 days. Following incubation, the cultures were homogenized using a blender, and the resulting suspensions were standardized using a hemacytometer to 1 × 10⁸ conidia·mL⁻¹ for the antagonists and 5 × 10⁶ conidia·mL⁻¹ for the pathogen.

Control suspensions were prepared by replacing microbial explants with sterile agar discs. To improve adhesion and uniform distribution, 2% carboxymethylcellulose (CMC) was added to the spore suspensions of the Trichoderma strains and to the antagonist control.

Experimental design

Seeds of the F1 Classico cucumber variety, known to be susceptible to the pathogen, were used in this study. The seeds were first washed with running water, disinfected with 70% ethanol for 10 min, and rinsed three times with sterile distilled water. Subsequently, five grams of disinfected seeds were immersed in separate suspensions of antagonist spores for 24 h, drained, air-dried under a laminar flow hood, and subjected to pre-germination in pots (10 cm in diameter) for three days. Each pot contained 300 g of a sterilized substrate composed of agricultural topsoil and quartz mixed at a 3:1 (w/w) ratio. Sterilization was performed twice at 121 °C for 1 h, with 2-days intervals, followed by cooling for 48 h. Control seeds were immersed in a sterile Trichoderma solution.

Seedlings obtained from pre-treatment and pre-germination, displaying uniform morphological characteristics, were transplanted into holes 7 cm deep in polyethylene pots (18 × 18.5 cm) containing the same sterile soil mixture. One plant was potted and incubated at room temperature, then watered daily with 200 ml of sterile distilled water. Twenty-one days after transplanting, each plant was infected at the rhizosphere with 20 ml of a 10⁶ spore/ml spore suspension of Neoscytalidium sp. Uninfected plants received 20 ml of either sterile distilled water or benomyl at the roots and served as negative or positive controls, respectively. (Ti), inoculated with T. afroharzanium and infected with Neoscytalidium sp. (T1i), inoculated with T. erinaceum and infected with Neoscytalidium sp. Experiment was conducted at the nursery of the Laboratory of Biochemistry, Faculty of Science, University of Douala, Cameroon (September – October 2023).

Fifteen days after infection of the plants, symptoms (on leaves and roots) were observed, and disease incidence (%I) was calculated using the following formula:

The plants were harvested, washed with tap water to remove soil particles, and the growth parameters were evaluated. The height (cm) was measured using a tape measure, determining the distance from the base of the stem to the growth tip of each plant. After separating the aerial part (leaves and stems) from the root system, the biomass of both root and aerial parts was obtained by measuring the fresh mass (g) of each part with a balance. The collar diameter was measured with a tape measure at the transition zone between the root system and the petiole. Aerial parts were stored at −20° C for the assessment of biochemical parameters.

Biochemical parameters assay

Polyphenol compounds assay

Phenolic compounds were quantified following the method of Singleton and Rossi (1965), with minor modifications. Briefly, one gram (1 g) of fresh cucumber leaf tissue was crushed and macerated at 4 °C for 1 h in 10 mL of 70% (v/v) methanol. The homogenate was centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 20 min, and the resulting supernatant was collected for analysis. For the assay, 100 µL of the extract was mixed with 2 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and 2.5 mL of 20% aqueous sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃). The reaction mixture was incubated in the dark for 60 min, after which absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a spectrophotometer. A blank was prepared by replacing the extract with 70% methanol. Catechol (100 µg/mL) was used as the standard reference compound. Results were expressed as catechol equivalents, calculated in micrograms (µg) of total phenols per gram of dry weight of cucumber leaves.

Soluble proteins content

The soluble protein assay was performed using the colorimetric method of Bradford (1976). One gram of leaf material was crushed with 1 mL of sodium phosphate buffer, 0.05 M, pH 7, and centrifuged at 3000 prm for 10 min. Twenty microliter of supernatant was added at 80 µl of sodium phosphate buffer pH 7 (0.05 M) followed by 2 ml of Bradford reagent (Coomassie G250 + orthophosphoric acid + ethanol 95%). The mixture was incubated in the dark for 10 min, and the optical density at 595 nm was measured with a spectrophotometer against a blank tube where the supernatant was replaced with the buffer. The standard curve was obtained from an aqueous solution of bovine serum albumin (BSA) 0.1%. Protein content was expressed in µg/g of fresh matter.

Enzymes assay

For enzyme extraction, one gram (1 g) of fresh leaf material was homogenized at 4 °C with 2 mL of 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) for the study of polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity, and with 2 mL of 1/15 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) for peroxidase (PO) activity. The homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the resulting supernatants were collected as crude enzyme extracts.

The specific activity of PPO was determined spectrophotometrically following the method of Ambata et al. (2025), with slight modifications. Briefly, 20 µL of enzyme extract was added to a reaction mixture containing 500 µL of phosphate buffer (pH 7.0; 100 mM) and 700 µL of catechol (0.01 M). The reaction was initiated by incubation at 30 °C for 2 min, then terminated by placing the tubes in an ice bath. Absorbance was measured at 420 nm against a blank in which the enzyme extract was replaced by buffer. PPO activity was expressed as ΔOD/min/mg protein per gram of fresh leaf tissue.

PO activity was assayed as described by Ambata et al. (2025). The reaction mixture consisted of 2% H₂O₂ (1 volume), 1% guaiacol (2 volumes), and 1/15 M phosphate buffer (5 volumes, pH 6.0). Two milliliters of this mixture were placed in test tubes, and the reaction was initiated by adding 10 µL of enzyme extract, followed by incubation at 25 °C for 3 min. Reaction progress was monitored with a stopwatch, and absorbance at 420 nm was recorded every minute against a blank (buffer replacing enzyme extract). The change in absorbance (ΔOD) between the third and first minute was used to calculate specific activity, expressed as ΔOD/min/mg protein per gram of fresh weight.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS software version 22 for Windows (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Qualitative and quantitative variables are presented as percentages and means ± standard error of the mean (SEM) in tables and graphs (histograms). A repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the mean values of more than two groups. Duncan’s post-hoc test was then used to perform pairwise comparisons. Bartlett’s test was used to verify that the data collected during the experiments met the conditions for using ANOVA. Pearson’s correlation test was used for correlations between defense parameters. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Identification of the pathogen

Isolation from rotting cucumber fruits yielded several morphotypes of fungal colonies emerging from tissue fragments. Among these, only a single isolate reproduced fruit rot symptoms (Fig. 1 A) and inhibited cucumber seed germination (Fig. 1B).

A1: control, A2: cucumber infected, B1: control seeds, B2: cucumber seeds infected, C1: macroscopic appearance of the isolate on PDA medium after 5 days of incubation, C2: microscopic appearance of the mycelium, C3: microscopic appearance of the spores

Macroscopic characterization of this isolate on potato dextrose agar (PDA) revealed a fluffy, woolly colony texture with a gray coloration on the first day, which progressively darkened to a blackish shade by day three. The reverse side of the Petri dish exhibited a distinct black pigmentation. The isolate displayed a rapid growth rate of approximately 2 cm/day (Fig. 1C1).

Microscopic examination showed a branched mycelium composed of septate, hyaline hyphae (Fig. 1C2). The hyphae fragmented into arthrospores, either solitary or arranged in chains, which were ellipsoidal in shape and contained zero to one septum (Fig. 1C3). These morphological characteristics were compared with the identification keys of Chabasse et al. (2002) and were consistent with the diagnostic features of Neoscytalidium sp..

Antagonism activity of Trichoderma against Neoscytalidium sp

In paired culture assays, T. erinaceum and T. afroharzianum not only invaded the pathogen (Fig. 2) but also significantly inhibited (p ≤ 0.05) its radial mycelial growth by 74.36% and 68.20%, respectively.

Trichoderma strains versus Neoscytalidium sp. in dual culture on PDA medium

The non-volatile metabolites produced by both Trichoderma strains also exerted strong inhibitory effects on pathogen growth, reducing mycelial development by 71.11% for T. erinaceum and 97.04% for T. afroharzianum (Fig. 3 A). In contrast, volatile compounds produced by these strains did not induce measurable inhibition of Neoscytalidium sp. mycelial growth (Fig. 3B).

Effect of the antagonists on the disease incidence and on the growth parameters of cucumber plants

The incidence of disease was significantly reduced (p ≤ 0.05) in pots treated with both Trichoderma strains and subsequently infected with Neoscytalidium sp.. Disease incidence values were 41.66% for T. afroharzianum and 25.00% for T. erinaceum, corresponding to reductions of 16.67% and 33.33%, respectively. In contrast, plants treated with benomyl exhibited a significantly higher disease incidence (50%) compared to those treated with biological control agents (Fig. 4).

The effect of treatments (Trichoderma strains and benomyl) on the incidence of cucumber diseases. The histograms bearing the same letter are not significantly different according to the Duncan test at 5%

Beyond disease suppression, plants treated with T. afroharzianum and T. erinaceum showed significant improvements (p ≤ 0.05) in key growth parameters, including seedling length, collar diameter, root biomass, and shoot biomass, when compared to untreated controls. Notably, these two Trichoderma strains were more effective than benomyl in promoting plant growth under non-infected conditions. However, infection with Neoscytalidium sp. reduced all measured parameters, although values in treated plants remained consistently higher than those in infected controls (Table 1; Figs. 4 and 5).

Biochemical results

Polyphenol content

The results showed, after statistical analysis, that plants inoculated with both strains of Trichoderma significantly increased (p ≤ 0.05) polyphenol content in both healthy (260.00 and 194.67 mg/g of fresh matter respectively for T. afroharzianum and T. erinaceum) and infected plants (371.67 and 220.00 mg/g of fresh matter respectively for T. afroharzianum and T. erinaceum) compared to controls (118.67 and 180.33 mg/g of fresh matter respectively for healthy and infected plants). However, polyphenol content decreased with infection in plants treated with Trichoderma. Similar results were observed in healthy and infected plants treated with benomyl. Conversely, in control plants, the polyphenol content increased with infection (Fig. 6). There appears to be a significant positive correlation (P = 0.00; R2 = + 0.88) between phenolic compound content and disease incidence.

Soluble proteins content

Results showed that treated plants (healthy and infected) did not exhibit any significant difference (p ≤ 0.05) in their soluble protein content. However, soluble protein content increased significantly with infection in control plants (untreated plants) (Fig. 7). There was a significant positive correlation (P = 0.00; R2 = + 0.69) between soluble protein and disease incidence.

Discussion

Soil-borne fungal diseases represent one of the most significant challenges for global crops, as they pose numerous difficulties due to the long survival of pathogens, their resting structures, and their wide host range. Among the crops affected by soil-borne pathogens are cucumber plants. In the present study, morphological characterization of the fungal phytopathogen isolated from rotten cucumber fruits that can inhibit seed germination has linked this phytopathogen to the probable species Neoscytalidium sp.. Neoscytalidium sp. is a well-documented pathogen of Cucurbitaceae crops (Mirtalebi et al. 2019).

The use of antagonistic fungi from the Trichoderma genus represents a promising alternative for sustainable crop protection, owing to their capacity to suppress plant pathogens, enhance yields, conserve biodiversity, and maintain soil fertility (Woo et al. 2014; Tchameni et al. 2020). In vitro evaluation of the antagonistic potential of T. harzianum and T. erinaceum strains demonstrated their capability to significantly inhibit the growth of Neoscytalidium sp.. Dual method assays revealed that these strains significantly (p < 0.05) suppressed pathogen growth at varying rates. Numerous studies have shown that the efficacy of biological control agents varies not only among antagonists but also across different pathogens (Tchameni et al. 2017; Lin-Qi et al. 2020; Bedine et al. 2020). Indeed, the degree of antagonism is influenced by the genetic profile of the antagonist isolate (Anand And Reddy 2009; Sanjeev Kumar et al. 2009). Furthermore, the biocontrol activity of Trichoderma species is typically mediated by multiple mechanisms, which differ among strains (Jogaiah et al. 2018; Cai et al. 2021; Ruangwong et al. 2021). One of the most prominent mechanisms is mycoparasitism, whereby Trichoderma directly attacks the pathogen. This process is morphologically evident as the antagonist’s ability to overgrow and sporulate on the pathogen during dual-culture assays, as observed in the present study (Abo-Elyousr et al. 2014). The antagonist strains used have grown on the pathogen and have sporulated on it. This observation could be due by the fasther growth of the antagonists than the pathogen, the completion of the nutrient and the mycoparasitism.

Antibiosis test using the cellophane membrane method Revealed a significant inhibition of pathogen growth (p < 0.05), with inhibition rates of 71.11 ± 2.22% and 97.04 ± 1.28% depending on the strain of the antagonistic tested. In contrast, antibiosis assays conducted under micro-atmosphere conditions did not result in inhibition of radial mycelial growth. This suggests that volatile compound production by Trichoderma species is highly variable and strain-dependent, consistent with previous reports (Benitez et al. 2004; Ntah et al. 2018; Anees et al. 2010; Tchameni et al. 2020). The absence of inhibition observed against Neoscytalidium sp. may be attributed either to the production of antagonistic substances at concentrations insufficient to exert a measurable effect or to the intrinsic insensitivity of Neoscytalidium sp. to these metabolites. This lack of direct inhibition underscores the complexity of biocontrol interactions, in which efficacy is not solely dependent on metabolite toxicity but rather on the interplay among multiple mechanisms. Accordingly, the overall antagonistic effect observed in vitro is likely the result of a synergistic combination of mechanisms, including mycoparasitism, antibiosis, and competition for nutrients and ecological niches (Yao et al. 2023).

Trichoderma species are well recognized not only for their ability to reduce the incidence of diseases caused by plant pathogens but also for their contribution to soil fertility and the production of natural fertilizers (Yao et al. 2023; Ambata et al. 2025). The application of these biological agents to cucumber seeds enabled the evaluation of their effects on plant growth parameters. Overall, the Trichoderma strains used significantly (p < 0.05) enhanced the morphological traits, including plant height, collar diameter, root, and shoot biomass. Mei et al. (2019) demonstrated that T. asperellum 525, T. harzianum 610, and T. pseudokoningii 886 significantly improved plant height, stem diameter, leaf area, shoot, and root fresh weight. Furthermore, Satyendra and Rekha (2021) highlight the dual role of T. harzianum in stimulating cucumber growth parameters while counteracting the negative impacts of soil-borne pathogens such as Rhizoctonia solani and Fusarium oxysporum. The ability of Trichoderma to improve agronomic characteristics of seedlings can be attributed to its dual colonization strategy: the fungus not only colonizes the root surface but also penetrates and resides within root tissues, thereby facilitating the efficient assimilation of essential mineral elements required for plant growth (Contreras-Cornejo et al. 2024).

In addition, this ability of Trichoderma to improve agronomic characteristics is also attributed to the secretion of bioactive substances, including diverse secondary metabolites and cell wall-degrading enzymes (Jaiswal et al. 2020; Tseng et al. 2020; Domínguez et al. 2016). By contrast, soil-borne pathogens occupy the same ecological niche in the rhizosphere, where they disrupt the host plant’s vascular system and interfere with critical physiological processes involved in water and nutrient uptake (Ayala-Donas et al. 2020).

Biological control agents that promote plant growth would also contribute to inducing plants’ resistance. Induced resistance refers to the enhanced defensive capacity of plants triggered by external biotic or abiotic factors. Colonization of plant roots by Trichoderma spp. initiates profound physiological and metabolic changes, leading to the production of diverse secondary metabolites that act as elicitors of defense responses (Al-Hazmi And TariqJaveed, 2016). The induction of resistance in plants by Trichoderma is typically manifested as a visible reduction in disease incidence. In the present study, cucumber plants treated with T. afroharzianum and T. erinaceum and subsequently infected with Neoscytalidium sp. exhibited reductions in disease incidence of 16.67% and 33.33%, respectively. Similarly, Satyendra and Rekha (2021) demonstrated that the application of T. harzianum on cucumber plants reduced the severity of the disease caused by R. solani and F. oxysporum.

The Trichoderma strains used in this study demonstrated the ability to stimulate the polyphenol biosynthetic pathways. These findings are consistent with those of Nawrocka et al. (2018), who reported that Trichoderma atroviride enhances the synthesis of phenolic compounds in cucumber plants. The accumulation of these compounds in plant tissues following infection is closely associated with their linked to their dual role: exerting direct antimicrobial activity and reinforcing cell wall structure. Slatnar et al. (2016) further demonstrated that polyphenol content increases significantly during plant-microorganism interactions. Beyond their role in stimulating plant defense, polyphenols also contribute to plant growth and reproduction of microorganisms (Slatnar et al. 2016). As secondary metabolites, they serve as substrates for several key enzymes involved in plant responses to both biotic and abiotic stress. Moreover, during plant–microorganism interactions, plants synthesize a wide array of structural proteins and defense-related enzymes that safeguard cellular integrity and enhance resilience (Chakraborty et al. 2023).

The results regarding peroxidase (PO) activity demonstrate that Trichoderma strains can modulate both the synthesis and activity of this enzyme. In fact, although all healthy plants exhibited similar PO activity and a significant increase in this activity following infection, the infected plants treated with Trichoderma exhibited significantly lower PO activity. Ngadze et al. (2012) demonstrated that PO activity increases during interactions with compatible host-pathogen systems, while Almaghasla et al. (2023) showed a significant reduction in PO activity in cucumber plants infected by R. solani and treated with T. asperellum KSATC or T. asperellum KSAT1E compared to the control. Specifically, PO catalyzes the degradation of hydrogen peroxide, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that can interact with pathogen cell wall components, ultimately causing structural damage and pathogen death. In addition, PO contributes to the accumulation of toxic molecules that inhibit pathogen growth, and to the regulation of key metabolic processes, such as protein modification mediated by nitric oxide (Chakraborty et al. 2017). Furthermore, the low PO activity observed in the treated and infected plants may be attributed to the actions of Trichoderma strains, which could have modulated the plant’s response to stress, as the plant had already been sensitised. Moreover, the decrease in PO activity observed in treated and infected plants could also be explained by the limited availability of its substrate, hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). This molecule may have been redirected toward other metabolic processes, such as diffusion into uninfected cells to activate enzymes involved in salicylic acid biosynthesis, a key signaling molecule in systemic acquired resistance.

PPO plays a pivotal role in plant defense, primarily by oxidizing phenolic compounds into quinones, which generally exhibit greater antimicrobial activity than their precursor phenols (Taranto et al. 2017). In this study, PPO activity displayed distinct patterns depending on infection status and treatment with antagonistic agents. Uninfected plants treated with Trichoderma spp. showed a significant elevation in PPO activity compared to untreated controls, suggesting that antagonistic fungi can prime host tissues by enhancing basal defense enzyme activity. Interestingly, when treated plants were subsequently challenged with Neoscytalidium sp., PPO activity declined, whereas untreated control plants exhibited a marked increase in PPO activity upon infection. This contrasting response highlights a nuanced regulatory mechanism: Trichoderma treatment may initially stimulate PPO activity in healthy tissues, thereby activating defense pathways, but subsequently modulates enzymatic activity to prevent excessive stress responses. Such regulation could mitigate the detrimental effects of uncontrolled PPO overactivity, including oxidative damage and disruption of cellular homeostasis. The variability in PPO activity may also be explained by biochemical and structural factors. Certain PPO isoforms require activation by detergents or proteases to achieve full catalytic potential (Constabel et al. 2000; Gandía -Herrero et al. 2005). Microbial colonization, whether pathogenic or antagonistic can alter rhizosphere and apoplastic pH through the secretion of organic acids or alkalinizing metabolites, thereby influencing PPO activity. Taken together, these findings suggest that PPO activity is not merely a static defense marker but a dynamic component of the plant’s biochemical arsenal. Its modulation by Trichoderma treatment underscores the complexity of plant–microbe interactions, in which antagonistic fungi act as both inducers and regulators of host defense responses. This dual role positions PPO as a valuable indicator of induced resistance and highlights the potential of Trichoderma-based biocontrol strategies to fine-tune enzymatic defense pathways for sustainable disease management.

Conclusion

T. erinaceum and T. afroharzianum exhibit strong antagonistic activity against Neoscytalidium sp., the causal agent of cucumber root rot. Seed coating with these biocontrol fungi not only enhanced cucumber growth parameters, such as germination rate, shoot and root development, but also stimulated key biochemical responses, including elevated antioxidant enzyme activity and accumulation of defense-related metabolites. These physiological and biochemical changes collectively indicate the induction of systemic resistance in cucumber plants against Neoscytalidium sp.. Given their dual roles in promoting plant growth and suppressing pathogen infection, the bioagents used are promising candidates for incorporation into biopesticide formulations for sustainable management of cucumber root rot. Future investigations will focus on molecular identification of the pathogen, evaluating the synergistic effects of combining these antagonists, field trail and assess their integrated bioprotective potential and optimize their application in integrated pest management strategies.
