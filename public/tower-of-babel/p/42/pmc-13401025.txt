Surface charge of LNP and V-ATPase activity of target cell synergize in functional delivery of nucleic acid in vitro and in vivo
Authors: Zhenyu Yang, Guanghui Zi, You Zhang, Rui Wang, Ting Li, Nuo Qu, JuanJuan Zhang, Yuhong Xu, Baowei Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Efficient delivery of nucleic acid therapeutics by lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) depends on complex interaction between vectors and targets. Here, we propose a model wherein LNP tropism is orchestrated synergistically by the particle's surface charge and the target cell's endo/lysosomal vacuolar H+-ATPase (V-ATPase) activity. In vitro, neutral LNPs transfected Fed cells (cells in +FBS medium) with optimal V-ATPase activity more efficiently than -FBS cells, while positively-charged LNPs transfected -FBS cells (cells in -FBS medium) with reduced V-ATPase activity more efficiently than Fed cells.In vivo, this synergy was applicable to liver, versus lung tropism of neutral versus positively-charged LNPs respectively. In addition, pre-condition of the reticuloendothelial system (RES) with empty LNPs enhanced functional delivery to lung but not liver mediated by positively-charged LNPs. Pharmacological inhibition of V-ATPase activity reduced in vitro and in vivo delivery mediated by neutral LNPs. On the other hand, elevating V-ATPase activity via genetic knockdown of ATP6-V1H enhanced in vitro delivery mediated by negatively-charged LNPs. Moreover, we found that the major components of protein corona adsorbed to in vivo neutral LNPs and positively-charged LNPs were shared by each other, except that the latter contained higher levels of coagulation factors and hemoglobins. In summary, our findings uncover a synergistic interaction between LNPs and targets in vitro and in vivo orchestrated by surface charge of LNP and V-ATPase activity of target.

Introduction

In 1987, Felgner et al. published the first report of a lipid-mediated DNA transfection procedure where cationic lipids DOTMA were used to encapsulate DNA (Felgner et al., 1987). The cationic lipids complexed with DNA through electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged phosphate backbone, and was thought to enable cellular uptake by interacting with negative charges on cell membranes (Xu and Szoka Jr., 1996). However, such positively charged systems induce pronounced toxicity in vivo due to immune activation (Akinc et al., 2019). In order to reduce toxicity in vivo, neutral LNPs employing ionizable cationic lipid have been developed later on (Cullis and Felgner, 2024).

Over the past decade, considerable efforts have been devoted to modulating LNP organ tropism through rational design of lipid components. For instance, ionizable lipids with tailored pKa values have been developed to achieve extrahepatic delivery, including targeting the pancreas, spleen, and brain (Shen et al., 2025; Suzuki et al., 2025; Bian et al., 2025). Alternatively, incorporating anionic lipids into LNP formulations has been shown to redirect biodistribution toward the spleen (Álvarez-Benedicto et al., 2023), while the inclusion of permanently cationic lipids enables lung-selective delivery, as demonstrated in several studies (Fei et al., 2024; Radmand et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2020). Despite these advances, most current strategies focus primarily on modifying the physicochemical properties of the nanoparticles themselves, with relatively limited attention paid to the intrinsic characteristics of the target cells that actively participate in LNP internalization and intracellular processing.

The endo/lysosomal system plays a central role in the fate of internalized nanoparticles, governing endocytosis, intracellular trafficking, endosomal escape, and eventual degradation (Ba et al., 2018; Johnson et al., 2016; Jongsma et al., 2016). This system is highly dynamic and exhibits considerable spatial heterogeneity, with endo/lysosomal positioning and function being modulated by various physiological and pathological cues, including nutrient availability, cellular stress, and pathogen infection (Yap et al., 2022; Erie et al., 2015; Korolchuk et al., 2011; Pu et al., 2016). However, whether and how the functional state of the target cell's endo/lysosomal machinery—particularly vacuolar H+-ATPase (V-ATPase) activity—influences the tropism of differentially charged LNPs remains largely unexplored. Addressing this gap could provide a mechanistic basis for the long-standing empirical observation that LNP surface charge correlates with organ-selective delivery.

Liver tropism is a prominent feature of neutral LNPs and considered as a major barrier to extrahepatic delivery (Cullis and Felgner, 2024). For example, the benchmark LNPs used in Onpattro are inevitably liver tropic following systemic administration (Akinc et al., 2019; Cullis and Felgner, 2024). To achieve extrahepatic delivery without the help of targeting module, one common strategy is to develop ionizable lipids with new structures and desirable pKa (Shen et al., 2025; Suzuki et al., 2025; Bian et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2025; O'Brien Laramy et al., 2025). Other strategies employ incorporation of anionic lipids for spleen specific targeting (Álvarez-Benedicto et al., 2023; Kranz et al., 2016; Fenton et al., 2017; Buschmann et al., 2021), or incorporation of permanent cationic lipids for lung specific targeting (Su et al., 2024; Fei et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2023a; Zhou et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2024; Radmand et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2023). It is well recognized that nanoparticle biodistribution is also influenced by other parameters such as particle size, lipid composition, protein corona formation, and administration conditions. Nevertheless, these strategies failed to consider the role of target cells in determining vector tropism.

The cell's endo/lysosomal system, which is responsible for endocytosis, endosomal escape and degradation of internalized substance, is dynamic and of high spatial heterogeneity (Ren et al., 2025; Ba et al., 2018; Johnson et al., 2016).For example, the dynamic positioning of endosomes and lysosomes are common in cells in different physiology and pathology (Jongsma et al., 2016; Yap et al., 2022), and can be regulated by various physiological and pathological factors including nutrient, extracellular pH changes, cellular stresses, infection by intracellular pathogens (Erie et al., 2015; Korolchuk et al., 2011).

Here, we sought to explore this possibility by investigating the interplay between LNP surface charge and the endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity of target cells. we took both surface charge of LNP and the endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity of target cells into account of organ tropism of LNP. We found the endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity in -FBS cells were suboptimal as compared to that in Fed cells, including elevated expression of V-ATPase subunit ATP6V1H, elevated endo/lyosomal pH, reduced activation of lysosomal proteases, and impaired autophagy. By using positively-charged LNPs (DOTAP, DOTAP/Chol, or DOTAP/Chol/PEG) and neutral LNPs (SM102/DSPC/Chol/PEG), we found that positively charged LNPs showed higher internalization in -FBS cells, whereas neutral LNPs showed higher internalization in Fed cells. Respectively. This led to effectively transfection in corresponding cells. To validate these observations in vivo settings, lung and liver were chosen as paired organs. We found that endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity of lung versus liver mirrored that of -FBS versus Fed cells. Importantly, positively charged LNPs exhibited tropism for the lung, whereas neutral LNPs exhibited tropism for the liver. We used pharmacological inhibition of V-ATPase and genetic knockdown of V-ATPase subunit to further validate our findings. In addition, analysis of in vivo LNP protein corona was performed and the results suggest that protein corona might have played a minor role in the differential tropisms of positively charged and neutral LNPs. Overall, we propose a model of synergy between LNP and target cell orchestrated by surface charge and endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity, which provides.

mechanistic explanation for the long-standing practice of employing charge to guide LNP organ tropism in the literature.

Material and methods

Rabbit anti ATP6V0A1 (SAB2108042), mouse anti-ATP6V0D1 (WH0009111M1), mouse anti-Cathepsin D (00715) were purchased from Sigma. HRP conjugated GADPH antibody (AC033) was purchased from Abclonal. Goat anti-rabbit and goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies, labeled with Alexa Fluor 488 (A-11011) or Alexa Fluor 555 (A31572), for immunostaining were purchased from Molecular Probes. SM102 (2089251–47-6), disteroylphosphatidyl choline (DSPC) (816–94-4), mPEG2000-DMG (160743–62-4), 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium propane (chloride salt) (DOTAP-CI) (106040009900) were purchased from Sinopeg.

Distearoyl Phosphatidylglycerole (DSPG) was purchased from A.V.T. Opti-MEM (31985062), Biotin-11-dUTP (R0081), DAPI (4′,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride) (62248), Hoechst 33342 (H21486), donkey-anti-rabbit-Alex568 (A10042) were purchased from Thermofisher. Streptavidin-FITC (K1081), Cy3-UTP (B8330), Cy5-UTP (B8333), T7 RNA polymerase (K1041), and EZ Cap Cy5 Firefly Luciferase mRNA (5-moUTP) were purchased from APExBio. CCK-8 kit (HY-K0301) was obtained from MCE. Annexin V Apoptosis Detection kit (556547) was obtained from BD. OCT (4583) was obtained from Sakura. Bafilomycin A1 (BA1) (BML-CM110–0100) was obtained from Enzo Life Sciences.

Cell culture and transfection

HeLa cells (ATCC) were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cell cultures were maintained between passages 2 and 3 and were grown up to 70–80% confluency. For FBS starvation, cells were cultured in plain DMEM. For amino acids (AAs) starvation, cells were cultured in DMEM devoid of AAs supplemented with 10% FBS which had been dialyzed overnight to remove AAs (molecular weight cutoff = 10 kDa, ThermoFisher, 88243). For transfection experiments, cells were seeded in 24-well plates. For transfection with benchmark LNPs, 2.5 μg pGFP loaded LNPs were added to each well. For transfection with lipoplexes and cationic LNPs, 1 μg pGFP loaded nanoparticles were added to each well, unless stated otherwise. When transfection was complete at indicated times with change of medium, cells were allowed to grow until imaged alive at the same time with cells transfected overnight. Nuclei of live cells were stained with 10 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 for 30 min. GFP fluorescence images were captured with a computer assisted fluorescence microscope (NIB610-FL, NEXCOPE). Transfection efficiency was determined by calculating GFP expressing cells over total nucleus. At least 6 imaging fields were counted by ImageJ. Endosome/lysosome pH was measured by LysoSensor (L7545, Invitrogen).

Linear DNA amplification and imaging platform

Linear DNA of ∼1000 bp was obtained by PCR, incorporating 15% - 20% of dUTP-11-biotin as described previously (Pu et al., 2016; Luo et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2024a). The linear DNA was purified by ethanol precipitation. Biotin-Streptavidin-FITC (Biotin/SA-FITC) complex was employed to visualize the DNA by incubating LNP-DNA pulsed/treated cells with streptavidin-FITC for 4 h at room temperature. Images of cells were acquired using Pelkin Elmer Operetta CLS high-content imaging system equipped with a 63× water lens (N/A 1.14) as described previously (Pu et al., 2016; Luo et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025a).

Preparation of neutral LNPs, positively charged LNPs

Benchmark LNPs were prepared using ionizable lipid SM-102, DSPC, cholesterol, and mPEG2000-DMG at a molar ratio of 50:10:38.5:1.5. Briefly, an aqueous phase containing nucleic acids (linear DNA, plasmid DNA, or mRNA) diluted in 20 mM citrate buffer (pH 4.0) and an organic phase containing lipids dissolved in absolute ethanol were mixed at an aqueous-to-organic volume ratio of 3:1 via pipetting 120–150 times or using a microfluidic chip (at a flow rate of 12 mL/min) to obtain LNPs with a defined nitrogen-to-phosphate (N/P) ratio. For fluorescent labeling of the LNPs, DOPE-Atto647 (∼0.2 mol%) was added to the ethanol solution before mixing with the citrate buffer. Positively charged LNPs with defined N/P ratio were prepared in similar way to LNPs. Briefly, an ethanol solution containing DOTAP, DOTAP/Chol, or DOTAP/Chol/mPEG2000-DMG in 1 volume was added to 3 volumes of nucleic acids (linear DNA, plasmid DNA or mRNA) in opti-MEM with vigorous mixing. The molar ratio of cholesterol in DOTAP/Chol or DOTAP/Chol/PEG was 33.5% or 65%. To supplement DOTAP to benchmark LNPs, the indicated molar ratio of DOTAP was added.

Characterization of nanoparticles

Particle size and zeta potential were measured using a Zetasizer Advance Pro instrument (Malvern, UK). DNA encapsulation efficiency by LNPs was determined by agarose gel electrophoresis. LNPs was incubated with or without Triton-X 100 to final concentration of 1% at 37 °C for 10 min before gel electrophoresis. Nanoparticle concentration was determined using a high-sensitivity single-particle NanoCoulter™ Particle Analyzer (ResunTech, Shenzhen, China) based on the resistive pulse sensing (Coulter principle). For sucrose density gradient ultracentrifugation (S-DGC), discontinuous sucrose gradient with 0, 10, 20, 30% sucrose was prepared in centrifuge tubes. Nanoparticles were laid on top of the sucrose gradient and centrifuged at 110,000g for 16 h at 4 °C with Avanti JXN-30 high-speed centrifuge (Beckman). After centrifuge, samples were collected from top to bottom with ∼400 μL for each fraction. Fractions were analyzed for size, zeta and nucleic acid content as described above. In some cases, fractions were used for endocytosis and transfection. To separate unbound protein from LNP/protein corona complex, LNP protein mixture was loaded on sucrose density column and centrifuged as stated above.

Immunofluorescence and uptake assay

Cells were grown on top of coverslips in 24-well plates and pulsed (short time incubation followed by change of medium) with DNA-dUTP-11-biotin loaded or mRNA-Cy3/Cy5 loaded lipoplex/LNPs, which were additionally labeled with Atto647-DOPE (∼ 0.2 mol%) for various times. LNP dose was derived from the encapsulated DNA concentration. For endocytosis experiments with benchmark LNPs, LNPs encapsulating 2.5 μg linear DNA were added to each well. For endocytosis experiments with lipoplex/cationic LNPs, 1 μg linear DNA or mRNA-Cy3/Cy5 loaded nanoparticles were added to each well, unless specified. After endocytosis, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 30 min, followed by 3 washes with PBS containing 1% Tween-20 (PBST). Subsequently, cells were permeabilized with 100 μg/mL digitonin for 20 min, blocked in 1.5% BSA solution. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies in 1.5% BSA, intensively washed with PBST for 3 times, 5 min each time. Secondary antibodies and/or Streptavidin-FITC, complemented with DAPI were applied for 4 h at room temperature. After intensive washing, coverslips were mounted on glass-slides. Images were captured with Operetta CLS high-content imaging system with 63× Water Immersion Objective (NA 1.15). Multiple layers of images along the z-axis were acquired. Images were analyzed with Harmony software or ImageJ.

Flowcytometry and cytotoxicity

Cell apoptosis or cell membrane permeabilization was detected using BD apoptosis detection kit according to manufacturer's instruction. Briefly, cells were incubated with FITC Annexin V in a buffer containing propidium iodide (PI) and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II). Cell viability was detected with CCK-8 kit according to manufacturer's instruction.

In vivo animal transfection studies

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Dali University (Protocol Number: [2022-P2–24]) and conducted in accordance with the Dali University Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Male Balb/c mice (8 weeks old, weighing 20–25 g) were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (License No.: SCXK(Xiang)2021–0002). The mice were housed in individually ventilated cages (5 per cage) under a 12-h light/dark cycle at 22 ± 2 °C and 50–60% relative humidity, with free access to standard chow and water. Before the experiment, the animals were allowed to acclimatize for at least 7 days. Animals were assigned to treatment groups based on body weight to ensure group balance (n = 3 per group). Mice received DiR (∼0.1 mol%) labeled or unlabeled benchmark LNP (SM-102/DSPC/cholesterol/mPEG2000-DMG at a molar ratio of 50:10:38.5:1.5), DOTAP lipoplex (DOTAP alone), or cationic LNPs (DOTAP/cholesterol/mPEG2000-DMG at a molar ratio of 65:33.5:1.5) loaded with 10 μg of EZ Cap Cy5 Firefly Luciferase mRNA (5-moUTP) via intravenous (i.v.) or intramuscular (i.m.) injection, respectively (n = 3 per group). At 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 24, 48 h after LNP injection, in vivo fluorescence and bioluminescence were detected by IVIS Lumina III, (PerkinElmer). For bioluminescence imaging, mice were injected with luciferase substrate luciferin (200 μL of 15 mg/mL), followed by imaging 15 min later. The fluorescence and bioluminescence intensities in each region of interest (ROIs) were quantified using the Living Image 4.8.2 software (PerkinElmer).

For inhibition of V-ATPase activity in vivo, mice were treated with BA1 (10 μM, in 100 μL of 20% ethanol, 20% tween-20 PBS solution) via intraperitoneal injection 1 h prior of LNP-mRNA injection. For pre-conditioning of RES, mice were treated same lipid dose of empty LNP 1 h prior of LNP-mRNA.

No animals were excluded from the analysis. All mice remained healthy throughout the experimental period, and no unexpected deaths occurred. The experiment was repeated twice independently with similar results.

Frozen sections and immunostaining

Liver and lung were collected from euthanized naïve C57BL/6 N mice. Tissues were fixed in 4% PFA at 4 °C for overnight and then in 30% sucrose 4 °C for overnight. The tissues were embedded in O.C.T., snap frozen in dry ice. Frozen sections of 8 μm were made with Minus FS800 (RWD). Sections were wash with PBS to remove O.C.T. The sections were blocked with 1.5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h, stained with 1st antibodies against EEA1 or LAMP2 at 4 °C for overnight. After thorough washing with PBST, the sections were stained with Alexa 568 conjugated 2nd antibody at room temperature for 4 h. Nuclei were stained with 1 μg/mL DAPI (PBS) for 10 min and coverslips were then mounted on slides using Fluoroshield (F6182, Sigma) antifade mounting medium. Fluorescence images of stained frozen sections were captured with a computer-assisted confocal microscope 60× oil objective (NA 1.4) (TCS SP8, Leica).

In vivo LNP protein corona analysis

Positively charged LNPs (DOTAP/Chol/PEG with 33.5 mol% cholesterol) and neutral benchmark LNPs (SM-102/DSPC/Chol/mPEG2000-DMG = 50:10:38.5:1.5) were prepared as described above and tail vein injected into male Balb/C mice (6–8 weeks of age). Fifteen to thirty minutes late, blood was collected via heart puncture. LNP containing plasma was isolated by centrifuge at 2000 g for 10 min. LNP/protein coronas were separated from LNP-plasma mixture on a Akta Pure (Cytiva) through a HiScale 16/20 size exclusion column (Cytiva, 28964424), containing 30 mL Sepharose CL-4B (Cytiva, 17015001) and PBS as the mobile phase (Yao et al., 2025). Fractions were collected in 0.8–1 mL/fraction. All fractions were examined by SDS-PAGE and CBB staining to identify LNPs/lipids and proteins. Fractions No.1–5 (the first peak of SEC chromatogram) and Fractions No. 12–18 (the 2nd peak of SEC chromatogram) pooled together and concentrated in 10 kDa Aimcon Ultra Centrifugal filter for MS proteomics and TEM examination.

LC MS/MS

After washing with ultrapure water, the desired SDS-PAGE gel was cut into an Eppendorf tube. Then, 500 μL of destaining solution, which consisted of 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (NH4HCO3) and 50% acetonitrile (ACN), was used for decolorizing until the gel pieces became colorless at 37 °C, 600 rpm. After washing with 50 mM NH4HCO3, the gel pieces were solidified with acetonitrile (ACN). Then, the gel pieces were reduced with 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), which was dissolved with 50 mM NH4HCO3 at 37 °C, 500 rpm, for 1 h. After cooling to room temperature, ACN was added to solidify the gel. Then, 55 mM iodoacetamide (IAM), which was dissolved with 50 mM NH4HCO3, was added to the gel to alkylate the sample at room temperature for 40 min in darkness. An appropriate volume of 13 ng/μL trypsin solution, which was dissolved with 50 mM NH4HCO3, was added to digest the gel pieces and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) was added to the sample to a final concentration of 1% to terminate the digestion process. Finally, 0.1% TFA with 60% ACN and 0.1% TFA with 90% ACN was used to extract the peptides from the gel pieces. The peptide was desalted by a Pierce C18 Spin Tips and dried in a speed vacuum concentrator.

The peptides were re-dissolved in solvent A (A: 0.1% formic acid in water) and analyzed using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 coupled to an EASY-nanoLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then, 2 μL peptide sample was loaded onto a 25 cm analytical column (75 μm inner diameter, 1.9 μm resin (Ammerbuch, Germany) and separated with 60 min-gradient starting at 2.2% buffer B (80% ACN with 0.1% FA) followed by a stepwise increase to 90% in 54.5 min, 99% in 0.5 min, and remaining there for 5 min. The column flow rate was maintained at 350 nL/min, with a column temperature of 40 °C. The electrospray voltage was set to 2 kV. The mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) was run under data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode and automatically switched between MS and MS/MS mode. The survey of the full scan MS spectra (m/z: 350–1500) was acquired in the Orbitrap (Thermo Fisher) with a 60,000 resolution. A normalized automatic gain control (AGC) target of 300% and a maximum injection time of 25 ms were used. Then, the precursor ions were selected into the collision cell for fragmentation by higher-energy collision dissociation (HCD), and the normalized collection energy was 30%. The MS/MS resolution was set at 15,000, the Normalized automatic gain control (AGC) target was 50%, the maximum injection time was 22 ms, and the dynamic exclusion was 30 s.

CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing ATP6V1H

CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing was performed as previously described (Hald Albertsen et al., 2022). Briefly, HeLa cells were transfected with LNP encapsulating plasmids encoding Cas9 protein and a pair of guide RNA. The cells were selected with puromycin and blasticidin. Successful gene editing was verified by PCR of target gene and Western blot analysis of target protein.

Statistics

Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m. Statistical significance was determined by Student's unpaired t-test for comparison between two groups, and one-way ANOVA followed by a Turkey test was used for comparisons among more than two groups. Difference was considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

DOTAP LNPs show higher endocytosis in -FBS cells than in Fed cells

DOTAP LNPs (previously known as Lipoplex) were formulated with DOTAP and DNA/mRNA at defined N/P ratios, i.e., 6, 3, 1.5. The physicochemical properties of bulk DOTAP LNPs were shown in Fig. 1A. DOTAP LNPs N/P = 6 (Lipoplex-6) and N/P = 3 (Lipoplex-3) were relatively stable in aqueous solution, while DOTAP LNP N/P = 1.5 (Lipoplex-1.5) was fairly unstable. Global characterization of bulk LNPs with DLS and ELS may mask critical variation in sample properties. Thus, we employed sucrose density gradient ultracentrifugation (S-DGC) to fractionate LNPs, which should result in relatively homogeneous samples. As shown in Fig. 1B, the density of DOTAP LNPs decreased as N/P ratio increased. A large portion of Lipoplex-6 was found on top of the sucrose gradients after 16 h of S-DGC at 110,000 g (Fig. 1B). Most of this part is likely nanoparticles with low payload copy number resulting from excessive stoichiometry of DOTAP to DNA. Lipoplex-3 distributed in a wide range of sucrose gradients (0% - 20%) after S-DGC, indicating Lipoplex-3 was quite heterogenous in terms of density (Fig. 1B). Lipoplex-1.5 was pelleted in a thin layer between the 20% and 30% sucrose gradients after S-DGC (Fig. 1B), probably due to excessive DNA to DOTAP. Consistently, agarose gel electrophoresis showed that most payload of Lipoplex-6 distributed in the 0% -10% sucrose gradients (Fig. 1C, top), of Lipoplex-3 distributed evenly in the 0% - 20% sucrose gradients (Fig. 1C, middle), and of Lipoplex-1.5 concentrated in the interface between the 20% and 30% sucrose gradients (Fig. 1C, bottom). Zeta potential of Lipoplex-6 fractions decreased linearly from approx. +60 mV to approx. -1 mV (R2 = 0.949, linear regression) (Fig. 1D, top). As zeta potential decreased, LNPs in Lipoplex-6 fractions became radically unstable judging from drastically increased particle size and PDI (Fig. 1D, bottom). Zeta potential (+40 mV) and size (200 nm) of Lipoplex-3 fractions were almost constant through the 0% - 20% sucrose gradients (Fig. 1E), which fitted well with the nonlinear plateau followed by one phase decay model (R2 = 0.953). Most fractions of Lipoplex-1.5 were negatively charged (Data not shown). The payload-rich fractions of Lipoplex-1.5 formed extremely large aggregates, however, their zeta potential was measured to approx. +30 mV (Data not shown), suggesting the presence a few independent positively charged nanoparticles in them. Next, we examined the endocytosis of DOTAP LNPs by both -FBS cells and Fed cells. For this purpose, biotin-11-dUTP was incorporated into linear DNA by PCR as previous reported (Pu et al., 2016; Luo et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025a). Meanwhile, LNPs were further labeled with Atto647-DOPE (∼ 0.2 mol%). Cells were transfected with LNPs for desired time, fixed, stained for endo/lysosomes, and then stained for cargo DNA-biotin with streptavidin-FITC. The results showed that DNA-FITC and Atto647-DOPE colocalized with each other in both -FBS cells (Fig. 1F) and Fed cells (Fig. 1H), indicating that lipid and cargo tightly associated with each other after LNP being internalized. In -FBS cells transfected with Lipoplex-6 for 2–4 h, huge and irregular aggregates (FITC bright, Atto647 bright) at cell periphery were observed (Fig. 1F-G). The aggregates were easily stained for EEA1, but had lost endosome morphology (Fig. 1F). Trace experiments using Lipoplex-mRNA-Cy3 and Lipoplex-mRNA-Cy5 showed near 100% colocalization of the two nanoparticles as early as 30 min, indicating endocytosis and subsequent fusion were acute, which followed sigmoidal kinetics in -FBS cells (Fig. 1J-K). The fusion and aggregation became greater in -FBS cells as transfection time extended, which consumed the cell's endosomes and caused overt cell membrane rupture and cell death (Fig. 1F, bottom panel). The endocytosis of Lipoplex-6 by Fed cells was slow and limited (Fig. 1H), which followed linear kinetics (Fig. 1L-M). Small and independent LNP/endosomes (FITC+, Atto647+) were observed in the perinuclear region in Fed cells after 2–4 h of transfection with Lipoplex-6 (Fig. 1H). Interestingly, we observed that nanoparticles precipitated in space between Fed cells (not over the cells) in a time dependent manner (Fig. S1 A, S1B). The rate of Lipoplex-6 internalization was faster than the rate of Lipoplex-6 precipitation (Fig. S1B). Thus, the insufficient internalization of Lipoplex-6 by Fed cells was unlikely due to limited availability of nanoparticles.

The endocytosis of Lipoplex-3 by -FBS and Fed cells was similar to that of Lipoplex-6, only that the level of endocytosis and subsequent fusion were smaller and slower after 2–4 h of transfection (Fig. S2A-B). It required higher dose of Lipoplex-1.5 for its endocytosis to be efficiently detected by fluorescence microscope (Fig. S2C-D). Similar to Lipoplex-6, both Lipoplex-3 and Lipoplex-1.5 led to overt aggregation of LNP/endosomes in -FBS cells after overnight transfection (Fig. S2A, S2C). Since the overall zeta potential of Lipoplex-1.5 is negative (Fig. 1A), it suggests that a small number of high zeta potential (> +30 mV) nanoparticles were sufficient to induce massive endosome aggregation overtime. It is worth noting that the peripheral aggregates (DNA-FITC and DOPE-Atto647 positive) did not colocalize with LAMP2+ lysosomes in -FBS cells (Fig. 1I), suggesting the nanoparticles were not delivered to lysosomes for degradation. On the other hand, a large proportion of internalized nanoparticles colocalized with LAMP2+ lysosomes in the perinuclear region in Fed cells (Fig. S2D). Additional endocytosis experiments with DOTAP LNPs (N/P 0.5–8) further demonstrated that DOTAP LNPs were preferentially internalized by -FBS cells and the extent of endocytosis and fusion of LNP/endosomes was proportional to N/P ratio or zeta potential (which correlates to N/P ratio), of nanoparticles (Fig. S3).

Flow cytometry analysis showed that propidium iodide (PI) penetrated live -FBS cells readily and in positive relation to N/P ratios of DOTAP LNPs (Fig. 1N-O), which is consistent with increased cell membrane permeabilization. By contrast, cell membrane of Fed cells was not severely compromised after transfection judging from the extent of PI penetration (Fig. 1P-Q). In fact, PI penetration decreased as N/P ratios of DOTAP LNPs increased (Fig. 1P-Q). The PI penetration results negatively correlated with CCK-8 assay results (Fig. S3H). Consistent with endocytosis, DOTAP LNPs transfected -FBS cells more efficiently than Fed cells (Fig. S4). It is noticed that transfection efficiency of DOTAP LNPs positively correlated with their ability to induce PI penetration (Fig. 1R) until the cells were overwhelmed by cytotoxicity (Fig. S3E). Together, these results demonstrate that positively charged DOTAP LNPs show higher endocytosis in -FBS cells than in Fed cells, and the extent of endocytosis correlates with transfection efficiency.

DOTAP/Chol and DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs exhibit enhanced endocytosis in -FBS cells

Next, we supplemented cholesterol to DOTAP LNPs, forming DOTAP/Chol LNPs. Cholesterol supplementation had more than doubled particle concentration in a cholesterol content dependent manner as compared to DOTAP LNPs (Fig. 2A), and increased particle size and zeta potential (Fig. S5A). It also increased particle density and reduced particle stability (Fig. 2B-C, S5B, S5D). Cells transfected with DOTAP/Chol LNPs exhibited higher levels of endocytosis as compared to cells transfected with DOTAP LNPs (Fig. 2D-G). The increase in endocytosis is cholesterol content or particle concentration dependent (Fig. S6A-B). Like DOTAP LNPs, DOTAP/Chol LNPs were preferentially internalized by -FBS cells (Fig. 2D-G). However, DOTAP/Chol LNPs exhibited significantly lower levels of transfection efficiency in -FBS cells as compared to corresponding DOTAP LNPs (Fig. 2H-I), which inversely correlated with cholesterol content (Fig. S6C-E), Meanwhile, DOTAP/Chol LNPs exhibited higher levels of transfection efficiency in Fed cells as compared to corresponding DOTAP LNPs (Fig. 2H-I, S6C-D), which positively correlated with cholesterol content. This departure in transfection performance from DOTAP LNPs is likely due to the enhanced endocytosis of DOTAP/Chol LNPs resulting in elevated cytotoxicity in -FBS cells while leading to higher cumulative endosomal escape in Fed cells. Consistent with this idea, flow cytometry analysis showed that DOTAP/Chol LNPs induced significantly higher levels of apoptosis in -FBS cells but not in Fed cells relative to DOTAP LNPs, in a cholesterol content dependent manner (Fig. 2J-K). Similar transfection performance switch was observed for DOTAP/Chol LNPs with higher N/P ratio (Fig. S7A-C). In addition, transfection experiment with S-DGC fractions of DOTAP/Chol LNPs further demonstrated that Fed cells were preferential transfected (Fig. S7D). PEGylation negatively affects LNP cellular internalization and subsequent endosome escape due to formation of a hydrophilic out-layer on the surface of LNPs (Akinc et al., 2019; Buschmann et al., 2021; Cheng and Lee, 2016; Wang et al., 2024b). Since excessive endocytosis of highly charged LNPs reduced the transfection efficiency, we hypothesized that PEGylation of DOTAP/Chol LNPs (forming DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs) would reduce endocytosis, in turn, cytotoxicity; and this would rescue their transfection capacity in -FBS cells. PEGylation stabilized nanoparticles (Fig. 2B-C) and reduced particle size and zeta potential (Fig. S5A). S-GDC revealed that DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs were highly heterogeneous in terms of density (Fig. 2B-C) but highly homogeneous in terms of particle size and zeta potential (mostly > +30 mV) (Fig. S5C, S5E). As expected, endocytosis of DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs was limited in -FBS cells as compared to that of DOTAP/Chol LNPs (Fig. S8A-B). In agreement, flow cytometry analysis showed that DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs were less cytotoxic to -FBS cells as compared to DOTAP/Chol LNPs (Fig. 2J-K). Interestingly, DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs induced higher levels of cytotoxicity in Fed cells as compared to DOTAP/Chol LNPs (Fig. 2K), probably due to higher levels of endocytosis. -FBS cells exhibited significantly higher uptake of DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs than Fed cells (Fig. S8A-B).

In line with endocytosis results, DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs transfected -FBS cells with relatively high efficiency (Fig. 2H-I), while they transfected Fed cells with low efficiency (Fig. 2H-I). Transfection experiments with S-DGC fractions of DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs further demonstrated that -FBS cells were preferential transfected (Fig. S7E). Moreover, pre-incubation with serum did not alter the preferential internalization (Fig. S8C-D) and transfection (Fig. S8E-F) of DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs in -FBS cells over Fed cells. Pre-incubation with serum appeared to have enhanced the endocytosis of DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs by -FBS cells and led to great LNP/endosome aggregation (Fig. S8C-D). Of note, positively charged LNPs tend to form large aggregates after incubation with serum for longer period of time. Together, these results demonstrate that positively charged DOTAP/Chol and DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs exhibit significantly higher internalization in -FBS cells than in Fed cells.

Neutral LNPs are preferentially internalized by Fed cells over -FBS cells

Next, we examined the endocytosis of neutral LNPs by Fed and -FBS HeLa cells. Benchmark LNPs (ionizable lipid/helper lipid/cholesterol/PEG-lipid in molar ratios of 50:10:38.5:1.5) with N/P ratio 2 to 6 were prepared, which are near neutral in zeta potential (Fig. 3A). Similar to positively charged LNPs, neutral LNPs exhibited decreased density as N/P ratio increased (Fig. 3B). After S-DGC, a large part of LNP NP6 was found on the top of sucrose gradients (Fig. 3B), while most of LNP NP2 was pelleted in the interface of 20% and 30% sucrose gradients (Fig. 3B). In contrast to positively charged LNPs, neutral LNPs were preferentially internalized by Fed cells over -FBS cells (Fig. 3C-E). Internalized LNPs were trafficked intracellularly to the perinuclear region (Fig. 3C-E). Neither prolonged time of transfection nor increased dose of transfection was sufficient to compensate for the deficiency in uptake of neutral LNPs by -FBS cells (Fig. S9). The pattern and dynamics of endocytosis of LNP-mRNA by Fed and -FBS cells were similar to that of LNP-DNA (Fig. 3F-H). In line with endocytosis results, GFP transgene expression was consistently higher in Fed cells than in -FBS cells, both HeLa (Fig. 3I-K) and HepG2 (Fig. S10A) cells. Fed and -FBS cells exhibit contrasting V-ATPase activity

Given the central role of the endo/lysosomal network in LNP endocytosis, intracellular trafficking and degradation, we hypothesized that it also played a key role in target tropism of LNP. First, we analyzed the expression/activation of endo/lysosomal proteases (asparagine endopeptidase (AEP) and cathepsin D), autophagy proteins (LC3 and p62) (Hald Albertsen et al., 2022; Zoncu et al., 2011), V-ATPase subunits (ATP6-V1H, ATP6-V0A1 and ATP6-V0D1), and endo/lysosome markers (EEA1, LAMP2) in cells by Western blot. In the acidic environment of endo/lysosomes, pro-AEP (∼56 kDa) undergoes autoactivation to form active AEP (∼36 kDa) (Hald Albertsen et al., 2022). Since amino acid depletion (-AAs) has been shown to activate the V-ATPase and induce endo/lysosomal acidification (Cheng et al., 2025a; Yim and Mizushima, 2020), we employed -AAs as a control. In addition, we employed pharmacological inhibition of V-ATPase with Bafilomycin A1 (BA1) as an additional control. The results revealed distinct protein activation/expression profile between -FBS and Fed cells: specifically, -FBS cells exhibited reduced AEP activation alongside increased expression of LC3-II and ATP6-V1H (Fig. 4A, B) compared to Fed cells. FBS withdrawal (-FBS) had minimal effect on the expression of p62, ATP6-V0A1, ATP6V0D1, EEA1 and LAMP2 in cells (Fig. 4A). Treatment with BA1 and -AAs had greater effect than -FBS and Fed, respectively, on the activation/expression of AEP and LC3-II, but not ATP6-V1H (Fig. 4A, B). In the last stage of autophagy, autophagosomes fuse with lysosomes for degradation (Hu et al., 2015). The status of autophagy reflects the degradation activity of endo/lysosomes, thus reflects endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity. We employed the tandem fluorescent-tagged LC3 marker (mRFP-GFP-LC3) to examine autophagy in cells (Ma et al., 2012; Di et al., 2022). The results showed that -FBS cells contained much more autophagosomes than Fed cells (Fig. 4C), indicating impaired autophagosome clearance. Moreover, autophagosomes in -FBS cells displayed significantly lower ratio of mCherry fluorescence to green fluorescence as compared to that in Fed cells (Fig. 4D). In contrast, -AAs treatment resulted in significantly less autophagosomes in cells compared to both -FBS and Fed treatment (Fig. 4C), indicating enhanced autophagosome clearance. These results suggest that -FBS cells exhibit lower endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity than Fed cells.

It has been shown previously that the position of endo/lysosomes within the cell reflects their luminal pH (Ba et al., 2018). EEA1 and LAMP2 immuno-staining, and Lysotracker labeling revealed that endo/lysosomes concentrated in the perinuclear region of -FBS cells whereas endo/lysosomes scattered in the cytoplasm of Fed cells (Fig. 4E-G). In addition, chase experiment with Lysotracker showed that endo/lysosomes in -FBS cells were more fusogenic as compared to that in Fed cells (Fig. 4H-I). Indeed, endo/lysosomal pH was significantly higher in -FBS cells as compared to that in Fed cells (Fig. 4J), consistent with the notion that -FBS cells exhibit lower V-ATPase activity.

Next, we used pharmacological inhibition and genetic knockdown to modulate endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity to investigate its role in LNP endocytosis. Treatment with BA1, resulting in reduced endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity (Fig. 4A), impaired the endocytosis of neutral LNPs by Fed cells (Fig. S13A) and reduced transfection (Fig. S13B). On the other hand, treatment with BA1 increased the endocytosis of positively charged LNPs by -FBS cells (Fig. S13C) and, in turn, enhanced transfection (Fig. S13D). By contrast, genetic knockdown of ATP6-V1H (ATP6-V1H KD) by CRISPR-Cas9 resulted in enhanced endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity, as shown by increased AEP activation, reduced LC3-II and p62 accumulation (Fig. 4K). mRFP-GFP-LC3 trace experiment showed that autophagosome clearance was enhanced in ATP6-V1H KD cells as compared to WT cells (Fig. 4L-M). Endocytosis of neutral LNPs was significantly lower in ATP6-V1H KD cells than WT cells (Fig. 4N-O). In agreement, transfection was significantly lower in ATP6-V1H KD cells than WT cells (Fig. 4P-Q). Similarly, -AAs treatment, resulting in elevated endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity (Fig. 4A), also suppressed endocytosis of neutral LNPs (Fig. S14A) and reduced transfection (Fig. S14B-C). ATP6-V1H KD -FBS cells were not efficiently transfected by positively charged LNPs (Data not shown). However, ATP6-V1H KD Fed cells were efficiently transfected by negatively-charged LNPs as compared to neutral LNPs (Fig. S15).

Taken together, these results indicate that -FBS and Fed cells exhibit contrasting endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity, and endocytosis of LNP can be regulated by modulating endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity.

Surface charge and endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity synergize in in vivo delivery by LNP

The above results support a model wherein cells with reduced endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity are preferentially transfected by positively-charged LNPs, whereas those with optimal endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity are preferentially transfected by neutral LNPs. We investigated whether or not this model is applicable to in vivo organ tropism of LNPs. Since lung and liver are known targets of positively-charged LNPs and neutral LNPs, respectively (Cullis and Felgner, 2024; Su et al., 2024; Fei et al., 2024), we chose them as paired organs for this purpose. First, we examined the activation/expression of aforementioned proteases, autophagy proteins and V-ATPase subunits in liver and lung of mice (Fig. 5A). We utilized lung and liver tissues from AEP knockout mice to validate the detection of active AEP in vivo by Western blot (Fig. 5A). Similar to that between -FBS and Fed cells, lung tissues exhibited reduced AEP activation and increased expression of LC3-II and ATP6-V1H compared to liver tissues (Fig. 5A). In addition, expression of p62 was markedly higher in lung than in liver (Fig. 5A). On the other hand, expression of LAMP2 was markedly lower in lung tissues than liver tissues, as shown by results of Western blot and immunostaining of frozen tissue sections (Fig. 5B). Next, we examined in vivo functional delivery to lung and liver of mice by positively-charged LNPs (DOTAP/Chol/PEG) and neutral LNPs. To this end, mRNA encoding firefly luciferase was encapsulated in LNPs, which were labeled with fluorescent long-chain carbocyanine dye DiR to track the biodistribution of LNPs. As revealed by IVIS imaging, neutral LNPs primarily distributed in the liver after intravenous injection (Fig. 6A upper panel), which peaked at 1–2 h after LNP administration (Fig. 6B). Consistent with previous reports (Wu et al., 2024; Cabukusta and Neefjes, 2018), the expression of luciferase mRNA in the liver could be detected at high levels as soon as 30 min and peaked at 6–9 h after LNP administration (Fig. 6C). No obvious functional delivery to the lung was achieved after intravenous injection of neutral LNPs (Fig. 6C upper panel). By contrast, the massive accumulation of positively-charged DOTAP/Chol/PEG LNPs in the liver after intravenous injection (Fig. 6A lower panel), albeit with delayed pharmacokinetics (Fig. 6B), did not result in functional delivery (Fig. 6C lower panel). Meanwhile, the small portion of positively-charged LNPs found in the lung (Fig. 6A lower panel) was able to achieved functional delivery (Fig. 6C lower panel), albeit at relatively low levels (Fig. 6D). Interestingly, the expression of transgene in the lung mediated by positively-charged LNPs peaked faster than that in the liver mediated by neutral LNPs (Fig. 6C-D). In addition, neutral LNPs achieved significantly higher functional delivery in the muscle than DOTAP/Chol/PEG after intramuscular injection (Fig. S16A-D), suggesting muscle is not an ideal target for positively charged LNPs either. Consistently, no functional delivery to the muscle was achieved by the highly positive DOTAP LNPs after intramuscular injection (Fig. S17). Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of V-ATPase activity in vivo with BA1 significantly reduced functional liver delivery mediated by neutral LNPs (Fig. 6E-F). By contrast, it had no significant impact on functional lung delivery mediated by positively-charged LNPs (DOTAP/Chol/PEG) (Fig. 6G-H), probably due to V-ATPase activity is already at low levels in lung tissues. Positively-charged LNPs adsorbed a protein corona promoting their phagocytosis by the RES (see below), we reasoned that a major barrier for functional lung delivery mediated by positively-charged LNPs would be the availability of nanoparticles. Thus, we pre-conditioned the RES with empty LNPs. As expected, pre-condition of the RES significantly enhanced functional lung delivery mediated by positively-charged LNPs (Fig. 6I-J). Notably, RES pre-condition did not increase functional liver delivery mediated by positively-charged LNPs (Fig.6I). Taken together, these results validate our model in vivo that surface charge of LNP and endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity of target organ synergize in functional delivery.

Discussion

The results reported here support a model in which cell/organ tropism of LNP is orchestrated by both zeta potential of the particle and endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity of target cell/organ, specifically, cells with optimal V-ATPase activity are preferentially transfected by neutral LNPs while cells with sub-optimal V-ATPase activity are preferentially transfected by positively-charged LNPs. This model takes into account of target cell/organ, specifically the endo/lysosomal system, as active participant in determining LNP organ tropism. We acknowledge that FBS withdrawal may affect multiple cellular pathways beyond V-ATPase activity; however, our conclusions regarding the specific role of V-ATPase activity are primarily supported by orthogonal experiments including pharmacological inhibition with bafilomycin A1 (BA1) and genetic knockdown of ATP6V1H by CRISPR-Cas9.

Endo/lysosomes are dynamic intracellular organelles. They distribute in a relatively immobile pool dubbed “cloud” around the microtubule-organizing center in the perinuclear region and in a highly dynamic pool in the cell periphery (Korolchuk et al., 2011; Luzio et al., 2007). Their spatiotemporal distribution enables them to carry out multiple biological functions, such as endocytosis, cargo degradation after fusing with endosomes (Poüs and Codogno, 2011) or autophagosome (Luzio et al., 2007; Forgac, 2007). In the current study, we show that -FBS cells contain endo/lysosomes with suboptimal V-ATPase activity as compared to Fed cells. The hallmark of suboptimal endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity is elevated expression of ATP6V1H, which in turn results in elevated endo/lysosomal pH, impaired endo/lysosomal protease activation and autophagy clearance (Fig. 4 and Fig. 5). Fed cells with optimal V-ATPase activity are preferentially transfected by neutral LNPs while -FBS cells with sub-optimal V-ATPase activity are preferentially transfected by positively-charged LNPs. More importantly, the orchestration between surface charge and endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity found in vitro is recapitulated by in vivo delivery to liver and lung by neutral LNPs and positively-charged LNPs respectively. We did not investigate the type of cells transfected in each organ. Differentially charged LNPs may have transfected different types of cells in tropic organs, which needs further investigation in the future. We acknowledge that the term “synergy” as used here describes a conceptual interplay between LNP surface charge and target cell V-ATPase activity, rather than a formally quantified synergistic interaction (e.g., by isobologram analysis). Future studies with controlled modulation of both parameters in the same system will be required to quantitatively assess true synergy.

V-ATPase (vacuolar H+-ATPase) is a universal proton pump and plays an important role in endo/lysosome acidification (Yim and Mizushima, 2020; Harvey, 2009). The primary action of V-ATPase is to charge the membrane capacitance and impose a Δψ across the membrane, thus generating membrane potential (Banerjee et al., 2022). All subsequent events, including establishing pH difference in the bulk solution, are secnodary (Banerjee et al., 2022). Peripheral lysosomes are physically close to early plasma-membrane event such as endocytosis. Notably, V-ATPase activity may influence endocytosis efficiency indirectly through its role in regulating endo/lysosomal positioning and membrane fusion dynamics, rather than solely through post-uptake luminal acidification. Previous studies have shown that lysosomal positioning coordinates endocytic membrane availability and that V-ATPase subunits interact with cytoskeletal elements to control organelle distribution. Our observation that -FBS cells (with suboptimal V-ATPase activity) exhibit perinuclear clustering and increased fusogenicity of endo/lysosomes (Fig. 4E-I) supports this notion. This altered spatial organization may enhance the efficiency of initial LNP internalization by increasing the local availability of endocytic membranes and promoting rapid endosome fusion, thereby facilitating the uptake of positively-charged LNPs.

Our data indicate that Fed cells, with robust endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity possibly generating high endo/lysosome membrane potential, favors interactions with neutral LNPs over positively-charged ones. Whereas -FBS cells, with suboptimal endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity possibly generating low endo/lysosome membrane potential, preferentially uptake positively-charged LNPs. Due to the negative charge nature of cell membrane (Tang et al., 2023b), LNPs with modestly positive charge is actually internalized at higher levels by cells in vitro. Thus, V-ATPase activity is possibly acting as the second layer of charge-interaction with highly charged LNPs. Future studies to directly interrogate the interactions between surface charge and V-ATPase activity, such as in vitro experiments with isolated endo/lysosomes with different V-ATPase activity, are warranted. Due to technique difficulty, quantitative analysis of V-ATPase activity in vivo was not available for this study.

We employed pharmacological inhibition of V-ATPase activity to establish a causal relationship between V-ATPase activity and LNP tropism to some extent. Pharmacological inhibition of V-ATPase significantly reduced transfection of innate target mediated by neutral LNP in vitro (Fig. S13A) and in vivo (Fig. 6E-F). On the other hand, it increased transfection mediated by positively-charged LNP in vitro (Fig. S13D), although it did not significantly alter in vivo transfection mediated by the same LNP (Fig. 6G-H). By contrast, genetic knockdown of ATP6-V1H in Fed cells, resulting in elevated V-ATPase activity, reduced and enhanced transfection mediated by neutral and negatively-charged LNPs respectively (Fig. 4P-Q and Fig. S15). We acknowledge that liver and lung differ in multiple physiological parameters, including blood flow, vascular fenestration, and resident macrophage populations, all of which may influence LNP biodistribution independently of V-ATPase activity. However, the observation that pharmacological inhibition of V-ATPase activity in vivo with BA1 significantly reduces neutral LNP delivery to the liver (Fig. 6E-F) supports a causal contribution of V-ATPase activity beyond these anatomical differences. This pharmacological evidence, together with the in vitro V-ATPase modulation data (BA1 treatment and ATP6V1H knockdown), provides convergent support for the role of V-ATPase activity in LNP tropism. Notably, both Fed and -FBS cells utilize similar endocytosis pathways, namely macropinocytosis and caveolae-mediated endocytosis, for internalization of neutral and positively-charged LNPs, respectively (Fig. S18). This observation suggests that the differential uptake efficiency between Fed and -FBS cells is not due to a switch in endocytic route, but rather reflects differences in endocytic capacity or membrane dynamics modulated by V-ATPase activity, consistent with our findings on endo/lysosomal positioning and fusogenicity (Fig. 4E-H).

The protein corona adsorbed to LNPs has been shown to play an important role in their liver tropism due to interaction between ApoE and LDL-R (Cheng et al., 2025a; Akinc et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2025b; Liu et al., 2023; Bussin et al., 2025; Dilliard et al., 2021). As positively charged LNPs concerned, it has been proposed that their lung tropism was due to adsorption of a specific protein corona highly enriched in vitronectin (Love et al., 2012). When investigating protein corona, most studies employed the in vitro LNP-serum incubation strategy (Suzuki et al., 2025; Akinc et al., 2010; Cheng et al., 2025b; Liu et al., 2023; Bussin et al., 2025; Dilliard et al., 2021; Love et al., 2012). In this study, we directly isolated in vivo LNP-protein corona complex from mouse plasma (Fig. 7). We found that major proteins in neutral LNP protein corona and positively charged LNP protein corona overlapped with each other (i.e. ApoE, ApoB, IGHM, Fgg, Fgb, C3, A2m, Fga). We found that vitronectin, which has been proposed in literature as leading protein in determining lung tropism of positively-charged LNPs (Love et al., 2012), was in very low abundance in protein corona of both neutral and positively charged LNPs (MS source data file: in vivo). Adding to the above, pre-incubation of LNPs with serum did not alter endocytosis or subsequent transfection tropism of LNPs (Fig. 3, S8, S10, and S12). We found positively charged LNPs absorbed more hemoglobins than neutral LNPs (Fig. 7H), which is consistent with the hemolysis nature of positively-charged LNPs [57]. Positively charged LNPs also absorbed more coagulation factors than neutral LNPs (Fig. 7H). The unique protein corona may promote the recognition of positively-charged LNPs by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), which is responsible for the clearance of LNPs from blood circulation [58]. Indeed, pre-condition the RES with empty LNPs significantly increased lung delivery mediated by positively-charged LNPs (Fig. 6I-J).

Conclusion

In summary, this study presents a model of cooperation between LNP and target cell/organ in nucleic acid therapeutics delivery, wherein charge-dependent LNP tropism arises from the interplay between LNP's surface charge and target cell/organ's endo/lysosomal V-ATPase activity. Our work provides a mechanistic basis for the long-standing practice of employing charge to navigate organ tropism of LNP, especially between liver and lung.
