Investigating a Systematic and Widespread Misidentification in the Metabolic Profiling Literature: Phenylacetylglutamine and Phenylacetylglycine Signal Misassignment in Proton NMR Spectra of Human and Rodent Urine
Authors: Samuele Sala, Andres Bernal, Andres Castillo Robles, Philipp Nitschke, Reika Masuda, Janonna Kadyrov, Joram M. Posma, Paul Elliott, John C. Lindon, Ian D. Wilson, Elaine Holmes, Julien Wist, Jeremy K. Nicholson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate assignment of endogenous and exogenous constituents within complex biological matrices remains a central challenge in metabolomics. Phenylacetylglutamine (PAGln) and phenylacetylglycine (PAGly) are terminal metabolites of the host-microbial co metabolism of phenylalanine, predominantly observed in humans and rodents, respectively. Motivated by a pervasive misidentification within the literature, we demonstrate that PAGln in human urine has been erroneously identified as PAGly in 82 of 166 NMR-based studies, yielding an estimated misannotation rate of 49%. Herein, we provide definitive assignments for both PAGly and PAGln, and present a comprehensive statistical evaluation of the NMR resonances attributed to both species, alongside their quantitative determination via targeted line-fitting approaches in extensive human and rodent urine samples obtained from the INTERSALT cohort (N = 1589) and the historical COMET study (N = 4399) respectively. In agreement with canonical hepatic metabolic pathways, PAGln was found to be exclusively present in human urine, whereas PAGly was restricted to rodent (i.e., rat and mouse) urine. These findings underscore the need for systematic re-evaluation of prior metabolomic annotations involving these structurally related yet biochemically distinct metabolites.

Introduction

Metabolic phenotyping, metabonomic and metabolomic studies encompass the study of the metabolite composition, sources and temporal dynamics of metabolic processes within biological systems in health and disease. Such studies provide the ability to provide a direct and dynamic readout of the biochemical status of an organism, capturing the integrated systemic outcomes of gene expression, protein activity, and environmental interactions. , Among the analytical platforms available, proton nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy-based methods have become a standard tool for metabolic phenotyping (metabotyping) across diverse scientific domains, with applications spanning applied disciplines such as environmental monitoring, food and nutrition studies, , biomedicine, toxicology, clinical trials and epidemiology, as well as more fundamental disciplines such as microbiology, cell biology and plant biochemistry, among others.

Although NMR spectroscopy exhibits lower sensitivity than mass spectrometry (MS), it provides several distinct advantages, including high reproducibility, excellent linearity, and a noninvasive, nondestructive approach. Its ability to simultaneously detect, characterize, identify and quantify hundreds of metabolites within complex mixtures underscores its complementary role alongside liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) for biological exploration. In clinical applications, urine offers clear advantages over other biofluids such as serum, plasma, bronchoalveolar lavage, or seminal fluid. Its facile (noninvasive) collection, ease of handling, and high patient compliance make it an attractive matrix for large-scale studies without the need for trained medical personnel. To this end, advances in NMR data acquisition and processing, along with thorough identification of endogenous and exogenous compounds, have deepened our understanding of complex biological processes. However, these logistical benefits are counterbalanced by significant analytical challenges. Unlike blood-based matrices, urine is not under homeostatic control, resulting in highly variable composition influenced by diet, medication, microbiome activity, and environmental exposures. This biochemical complexity and variability affect the properties of the observed signals, including their positions (chemical shifts), line shapes and widths and even intensities. Combined with the lack of unambiguous/accurate identifications for many observable features, this constitutes one of the principal bottlenecks within the typical metabolic profiling workflow, complicating downstream biological interpretation.

Although not unique to NMR spectroscopy, a review of the literature highlights a widespread issue in metabolic phenotyping, where studies reporting flawed metabolite identifications without adequate posterior validation are frequently published and subsequently replicated in the literature. These inaccuracies not only misguide research efforts and waste resources but also undermine the confidence in the approach as a reliable method for biomarker discovery and metabolic pathway analysis. To address this long-standing issue, historical initiatives such as the Summary Recommendations for Standardization and Reporting of Metabolic Analyses, the Metabolomics Standards Initiative and other best practice guidelines have been introduced, aiming to improve accuracy and standardize methodology in metabolomics and lipidomics. Here, compelled by a pervasive misidentification in NMR spectroscopy-based metabolic profiling literature, we provide a case study, selecting the identification and quantification of phenylacetylglutamine (PAGln) and phenylacetylglycine (PAGly) (Figure ). The microbial conversion of dietary phenylalanine to phenylacetic acid is facilitated by gut bacteria, including families such as Christensenellaceae, Ruminococcaceae, and Lachnospiraceae, and species such as Proteus mirabilis, Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Staphylococcus aureus. This process, occurring in the mammalian gut, involves phenylpyruvate ferredoxin-oxidoreductase (PPFOR) and phenylpyruvate decarboxylase (PPDC) enzymes for the formation of the phenylacetate moiety, with subsequent amino acid conjugation in the liver prior to urinary excretion. Indeed, the gut microflora are involved in many aspects of the metabolism of aromatic amino acids such as phenylalanine (and its downstream metabolite tyrosine) to phenylacetic acid and benzoic acids, leading ultimately to the urinary excretion PAGln and PAGly as well as hippuric acid (the glycine conjugate of benzoic acid, N-benzoylglycine) and phenylethylamine. In humans and other primates, phenylacetic acid is primarily conjugated to glutamine, forming PAGln as the major urinary metabolite, whereas in cats, dogs, pigs and rodents (i.e., rats and mice), the scaffold conjugates mainly to glycine, yielding PAGly. − Of interest, conjugation of phenylacetic acid to ornithine has also been observed in the domestic hen, and trace amounts of phenylacetyltaurine have also been detected in pigeons and ferrets. Species differences in conjugation of amino acids is a consequence of variation in the relative expression and substrate preference of hepatic acyltransferase enzymes responsible for conjugating aromatic acids.

A recent high-profile LC–MS-based metabolomic study first established a positive association between PAGln and thrombosis, along with platelet activation. Additional studies have shown association of the metabolite with cerebrovascular and neurological disorders, , underscoring the role of this specific metabolite in signaling along the Gut-Heart and Gut-Brain axes. The role of PAGln in human health is complex and context-dependent, with a body of literature suggesting elevated levels of PAGln in humans are linked to increased risk of disease, − while there is some evidence indicating that it may also participate in adaptive host–microbial interactions and nitrogen balance, raising questions as to whether its biological effects are universally adverse or reflect a more nuanced, bidirectional relationship between the host and gut microbiota. We present here the quantification of PAGln and PAGly in urine using a 1H NMR spectroscopy-based line fitting approach across human and rodent populations available to our laboratories in the form of the INTERSALT (an international study of urinary electrolyte excretion and blood pressure) and COMET (COnsortium for MEtabonomic Toxicology) cohorts, respectively. Our analysis demonstrates that PAGln is only consistently detectable in human urine (N = 1589) with high confidence using NMR spectroscopy, while PAGly is confidently identified exclusively in rodent urine by NMR (N = 4399), consistent with known hepatic pathways ,− and at odds with a number of notable, published reports to the contrary. − These findings suggest that metabolite annotation within a large volume of prior studies may require reassessment.

Methods

Literature Review

Relevant literature was retrieved from the Scifinder database (CAS) (Accessed 23rd September 2025) using the chemical structures of PAGln and PAGly as search queries. For both compounds, the subsearch was refined via the use of Boolean operators, specifying articles making reference to “NMR”, as well as “(NMR) AND (Human)” and “(NMR) AND ((Rodent) OR (Mouse) OR (Rat))”. Collated references were then inspected manually to ascertain relevance to the present study.

Study Design and Sample Cohorts

Human Cohort: INTERSALT

The design of the study and methods have been detailed elsewhere. Briefly, 52 centers from 32 countries participated. Each center was asked to recruit 200 men and women aged 20–59, 25 in each of eight age and sex groups. Whenever possible the samples were selected at random from population lists or by chunk sampling of defined populations. The population bases included electoral registers, factory or hospital workers, and whole village or island communities by trained investigators operating according to a defined Field manual of operations. Participants were asked to provide both a casual (“spot”) urine specimen and a 24 h urine collection into standard 1.0 L wide mouthed plastic jars with boric acid being used as a preservative. The height of urine in each jar was measured on a standard platform and scale and later converted into volume by computer in London. Aliquots were refrigerated at 4 °C within 24 h and frozen at −20 °C within 7 days, then shipped frozen to the central laboratory in Louvain, Belgium.

All human studies were carried out in accordance with national legislation and were subject to appropriate local review.

Rodent Cohort: Consortium for Metabonomic Toxicology (COMET)

Male Sprague–Dawley rats aged 6–8 weeks from Charles River Laboratories (Crl/CD­(SD)­IGS BR) were housed in standard stainless steel wire mesh cages in groups of five and acclimated to the laboratory environment for at least 6 days before being transferred to individual metabolism cages for urine collection. Throughout the studies the cages were sterilized daily. All animals were fed powdered Purina chow 5002 (Nestle, Purina, St. Louis, MO) and had free access to water and food throughout the study. The temperature and humidity in the room was maintained at 21 ± 2 °C and 55 ± 10% respectively and continuously monitored. An automatic timing device was used to provide an alternating 12 h light (6 AM to 6 PM)/dark cycle. A computer-assisted randomization procedure was used to allocate rats to the metabolism cages. As part of the larger toxicology study, for each test compound, 30 rats were assigned to one of three groups: control, low dose and high dose. The test compound was administered in saline or corn oil, depending on the solubility of the test compound and the route of dosing was selected based on extant literature and the time when animals were given their respective dose (dose vehicle or model toxin delivered in saline or corn oil) was set to zero hours. In the current study data from only the predose and control animals were used. Urine samples were collected in chilled tubes containing 1 mL of the preservative 1% NaN3 solution 16 h predose and then continuously fractionated at 0, 8, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, and 168 h postdosing. On collection, urine samples underwent a 10 min centrifugation at ∼1200g and were then stored at −40 °C until analysis.

Male B6C3F1 mice (n = 24), 12–16 weeks of age, were obtained from Charles River Laboratories. All animals were fed powdered Purina chow 5002 (Nestle, Purina, St. Louis, MO) and had free access to water and food throughout the study. As part of the larger toxicology study, for each test compound, 8 mice were assigned to one of three groups: control, low dose and high dose. In the current study, data from only the predose and control animals were used. Urine samples were collected in chilled tubes containing 1 mL of 1% NaN3 solution 16 h predose and then continuously fractionated at 0, 8, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, and 168 h postdosing. On collection, urine samples underwent a 10 min centrifugation at ∼1200g and were then stored at −40 °C until analysis.

All animal studies were carried out in accordance with national legislation and were subject to appropriate local review.

Sample Preparation

Human Cohort: INTERSALT

Urine samples were thawed at 4 °C for 2 h then centrifuged for 15 min at 13,000g at 4 °C. Urine samples were prepared in 5 mm outer diameter SampleJet NMR tubes, following the recommended procedures for in vitro analytical and diagnostics procedures using 540 μL of Urine mixed with 60 μL phosphate buffer (1.5 M KH2PO4, 2.0 mM NaN3, 0.1% sodium trimethylsilyl propionate-[2,2,3,3-2 H 4] (TSP) in D2O, pH 7.4 ± 0.1). Additionally, the samples were sonicated for 5 min at ambient temperature prior to analysis.

Rodent Cohort: Consortium for Metabonomic Toxicology (COMET)

All samples were centrifuged at approximately 1800g for 5 min to remove any solid debris prior to analysis. Samples were buffered in sodium phosphate and combined with an internal reference (TSP) and antibacterial agent (sodium azide). 400 μL of each urine sample was added to 200 μL of sodium phosphate buffer (0.2 M Na2HPO4, 0.04 M NaH2PO4 in 80:20 H2O/D2O, pH 7.4 ± 0.1) containing 1 mM TSP (sodium trimethylsilyl propionate-[2,2,3,3-2 H 4]) and 9 mM NaN3.

NMR Data Acquisition

Human Cohort: INTERSALT

1H NMR spectroscopic analyses were performed on a 600 MHz Bruker AVANCE III HD spectrometer, equipped with a 5 mm BBI probe and fitted with the Bruker SampleJet robot cooling system set to 5 °C. A full quantitative calibration was completed prior to the analysis using a protocol described elsewhere. Phosphate buffer and all NMR tubes (5 mm outer diameter SampleJet NMR tubes) with the corresponding sealing caps were purchased from Bruker A.G. (Rheinstetten, Germany). All IVDr methods were acquired at 300 K. The standard one-dimensional (1D) experiments with solvent suppression (pp: noesygppr1d) were acquired with 32 scans (+4 dummy scans), 64k data points, relaxation delay of 4.0 s, and a spectral width of 20 ppm resulting in a total experiment time of approximately 4 min per sample (in line with the Bruker In Vitro Diagnostics research IVDR methods). Time domain data were Fourier transformed and processed in automation using Bruker Topspin 3.7.0 and an exponential line broadening of 0.3 Hz was applied to the 1D water suppressed experiment.

Rodent Cohort: Consortium for Metabonomic Toxicology (COMET)

1H NMR spectra were acquired at 600 MHz and 300 K using a robotic flow-injection system (Bruker Biospin, Karlsruhe, Germany). The standard one-dimensional (1D) experiments with solvent suppression (pp: noesygppr1d) were acquired with 64 scans, 64k data points, relaxation delay of 2.0 s, and a spectral width of 20 ppm resulting in a total experiment time of approximately 4 min per sample.

Data Analyses

Quantification of metabolites was achieved using in-house developed routines written in both JavaScript and R. For the INTERSALT cohort, NMR spectra were processed according to the Bruker IVDr standard procedures with no further processing deemed necessary. For the COMET cohort, all spectra were processed using an in-house script. Normalization was not required prior to quantification, and can be applied on the extracted concentrations when necessary. Peaks from each metabolite of interest were grouped into spectral regions of interest (SROI). For each SROI, a list of signals in that region was established with attributes that defined their characteristic patterns (chemical shift, line width, multiplicity and scalar coupling). All the patterns present in an SROI were included, even unknown signals. These parameters were used as initial input for a gradient descent optimization (Levenberg–Marquardt) algorithm to minimize the difference between the experimental and the fitted pattern. The resulting models were graded using a heuristic based on residuals and correlations between different signals of the same analyte. Models with lower grades were visually inspected using a suite of interactive web tools built in JavaScript.

Pathway Enrichment Analysis

Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted in MetaboAnalyst version 6.0. Five and 20 component analyte mixtures were generated using the provided example list with addition of PAGln and PAGly respectively as follows; analyte list 1: phenylacetylglutamine, acetoacetic acid, beta-alanine, creatine, l-phenylalanine; analyte list 2: phenylacetylglycine, acetoacetic acid, beta-alanine, creatine, l-phenylalanine; analyte list 3: phenylacetylglutamine, acetoacetic acid, beta-alanine, creatine, dimethylglycine, fumaric acid, glycine, homocysteine, l-cysteine, l-isoleucine, l-phenylalanine, l-serine, l-threonine, l-tyrosine, l-valine, phenylpyruvic acid, propionic acid, pyruvic acid, sarcosine, caffeic acid; Analyte list 4: phenylacetylglycine, acetoacetic acid, beta-alanine, creatine, dimethylglycine, fumaric acid, glycine, homocysteine, l-cysteine, l-isoleucine, l-phenylalanine, l-serine, l-threonine, l-tyrosine, l-valine, phenylpyruvic acid, propionic acid, pyruvic acid, sarcosine, caffeic acid. Over-representation analysis was performed using the small molecule pathway database (SMPDB) set derived from normal human metabolic pathways. Results were visualized in EasyPubPlot.

Results and Discussion

Motivated by a perceived, widespread misidentification in the literature, we conducted a formal meta-analysis of PAGly and PAGln annotations using the SciFinder (CAS) database. A comprehensive review of published reports at the time of analysis indicated that PAGly has been documented in 1257 studies, with 297 references reporting its detection via nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based metabolomic investigations (Table ). Of these, 82 references (28% of all NMR reports) describe its presence in human urine, while 169 references (57% of all NMR reports) report detection of the metabolite in rodent urine, with the remainder involving bovine, porcine, canine, feline, and other urine sources. In contrast, PAGln has been cited in 912 studies, with 104 reports related to urinary NMR analyses. Notably, 84 references (81% of all NMR reports) identify the metabolite in human urine, while only six references (6% of all NMR reports) pertain to rodent urine. Strikingly, our analysis suggests that PAGln in human urine has been systematically misidentified as PAGly in 83 out of 167 NMR-based reports, yielding an estimated misidentification rate of 49%.

To further investigate these findings, we acquired and annotated the full 1H and 13C NMR spectra of PAGly and PAGln respectively in 90:10, H2O/D2O at 600 MHz (Figure and Table ). In addition, we applied a combination of multivariate and univariate analytical approaches to two large and well-characterized rodent and human cohorts available to our laboratory, namely the COMET (N = 4399) and INTERSALT (N = 1589) studies. As anticipated, a preliminary statistical total correlation spectroscopy (STOCSY) analysis using the aryl envelope resonances of PAGln as drivers signal in human urine revealed strong correlations to glutamine-associated signals at δH 1.93 (m), δH 2.11 (m), and δH 2.28 (m) (Figure ). Similarly, STOCSY analysis using the aryl envelope resonances of PAGly as drivers in rat and mouse urine identified distinct correlations to signals resonating at δH 3.67 (s) and δH 3.78 (d) consistent with the structure of PAGly. To corroborate these findings, both metabolites were semiquantitatively assessed in selected samples from each cohort using a targeted line-shape fitting protocol. Specifically, relative concentrations of PAGln were determined by fitting the δH 2.28 aliphatic multiplet, supplemented by fitting the δH 7.42 aromatic envelope. PAGly levels were subsequently derived by fitting the same δH 7.42 aromatic region, followed by subtraction of the PAGln contribution, an approach that may yield small, nonzero, or occasionally negative values in the absence of the analyte. Data were filtered to retain only fits with a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) below 15%. This analysis revealed PAGln to be reliably and exclusively detected in human urine, with PAGly absent aside from low-level residuals attributable to method artifacts. Conversely, PAGly was unambiguously identified in rodent urine, with PAGln not observed beyond background artifact levels (Figure ).

To rationalize these findings, we propose three key factors contributing to the widespread misidentification observed in the literature. First, we note the absence of authentic NMR reference spectra for PAGln in three spectral repositories widely used in metabolomics research: the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB), the Biological Magnetic Resonance Data Bank (BMRB), and the Chenomx spectral library (V11.0), the latter accessible through a free evaluation of the otherwise proprietary software. In contrast, all three databases contain authenticated reference spectra for PAGly, potentially biasing identification efforts: to this end, we note that the HMDB reports on the controversial status of PAGly annotations in urine, noting in the compound description section that the metabolite may be mistaken for PAGln by 1H NMR, this warning is not available on the BMRB or when applying the Chenomx metabolite search function. Second, we observe that once a misidentification reaches a critical threshold in the literature, it is likely to propagate through a self-reinforcing cycle. This issue is exacerbated in untargeted urinary metabolomics studies, where researchers often rely on prior reports for metabolite assignments. Finally, confusion is further compounded by the shared three-letter abbreviation “PAG” for both phenylacetylglutamine and phenylacetylglycine. To mitigate this ambiguity, we strongly advocate for discontinuing the use of “PAG” as a generic identifier. Instead, we recommend that phenylacetylglycine adopt the abbreviation “PAGly”, while phenylacetylglutamine should be designated as “PAGln” to ensure clarity in future studies. Alternatively, both metabolites may be referred to in the literature by their full names.

To assess the impact of this misidentification on downstream pathway analysis, we conducted a pathway enrichment analysis using MetaboAnalyst software, employing metabolite sets of five and 20 compounds, representative of typical NMR-based metabolomics studies. By systematically substituting PAGln with PAGly, we compared pathway perturbations under both conditions (Figure ). As anticipated, the results demonstrated a modest but measurable effect, which diminished as additional metabolites were incorporated into the analysis, highlighting the potential for metabolite identification errors to influence metabolic pathway interpretations.

Taken together, the findings presented in this study highlight a critical shortcoming in the current practice of NMR-based metabolomics. The fact that one of the most abundant metabolites in human urine is routinely misidentified in close to half of reported cases, raises significant concerns about the field’s ability to reliably interpret complex spectral data sets and advance analytical and biochemical understanding. Moreover, if such a pervasive misidentification occurs with a well-documented metabolite, it is reasonable to suspect that similar errors may be even more prevalent for lower-abundance metabolites, where the burden of proof is (paradoxically) reduced, within the context of editorial and peer-review. This work is not intended to criticize individual studies but rather to urge the metabolomics community, including researchers, authors, reviewers, and editors, to adopt more rigorous validation practices and to uphold higher standards in metabolite identification, to the end of ensuring greater accuracy and long-term reproducibility within the corpus of scientific literature.

Conclusion

Notwithstanding developments in NMR and LC–MS technologies, the unambiguous identification of entirely novel metabolites and metabolic features continues to present a major challenge in molecular phenotyping. In contrast, the reproducible, high-fidelity annotation and quantification of known metabolites, central to routine metabolomics workflows, has become increasingly standardized and is often regarded as a solved or routine aspect of the field. PAGln and PAGly are urinary metabolites of microbial-hepatic phenylalanine metabolism, with the former linked to chemical signaling and cardiovascular risk in humans. Using NMR line fitting, we analyzed urine samples from the INTERSALT cohort, and the historical COMET study, detecting PAGln exclusively in humans and PAGly exclusively in rodents. These findings suggest that a significant proportion of the metabolomics literature may require reassessment. Notably, nearly 50% of reported cases within the scientific literature misidentify PAGln as PAGly in human urine, revealing a major limitation in current NMR-based metabolomic workflows and additionally hinting at the fact that if such errors occur with a well-characterized metabolite, then the potential for misannotation of lower-abundance compounds is even greater. This study underscores the continued need for more rigorous validation practices to enhance the accuracy and reliability of metabolic profiling research.
