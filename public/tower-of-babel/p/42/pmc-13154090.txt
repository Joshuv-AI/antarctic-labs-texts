When evaluating the causes of aortic regurgitation (AR) in younger adults, it is essential to recognize a group of disorders characterized by abnormalities in connective tissue formation. Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is a congenital condition arising from defects in type I collagen,1,2 leading to systemic effects. It is often associated with cardiovascular complications, including AR, mitral regurgitation (MR), and aortic root dilation.3, 4, 5 In OI, collagen abnormalities may impair collagen-platelet interactions and increase capillary fragility, potentially leading to perioperative bleeding even when routine coagulation parameters are normal.6 We report a case of early-onset severe AR with OI complicated by postoperative mediastinal hematoma after aortic valve replacement.

Case Description

A man in his 40s with a history of multiple pathologic fractures in childhood, sometimes occurring with minimal trauma such as throwing a ball, presented with abnormal electrocardiographic findings during a routine health checkup.

The family history revealed that his daughter had hearing impairment and his father had Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome. On physical examination, he was of short stature (157.8 cm) and weighed 52 kg. His pulse rate was 115 beats/min and blood pressure was 145/80 mm Hg, indicating widened pulse pressure. Blue sclera (Figure 1A) was observed; however, ophthalmologic evaluation excluded elongated ocular axes or lens subluxation. Only mild scoliosis was noted, with no chest wall deformity. Pulmonary function tests were within normal limits. A grade Ⅴ/Ⅵ diastolic murmur (Austin Flint murmur) was audible along the left sternal border.

Electrocardiography showed sinus tachycardia (Figure 1B) and increased left ventricular voltages. Chest radiography demonstrated cardiomegaly with a cardiothoracic ratio of 55% (Figure 1C). Laboratory testing revealed that the B-type natriuretic peptide level was 35.8 pg/mL (reference value: <18.4 pg/mL), while there was no evidence of osteoporosis, autoimmune disease, or vasculitis. Transthoracic echocardiography showed left ventricular dilatation (left ventricular end-diastolic dimension 60 mm, left ventricular end-systolic dimension 46 mm) with diffuse hypokinesis and a reduced ejection fraction (EF) of 48% (Figure 1D, Video 1). Severe AR with a vena contracta width of 7.2 mm and a regurgitant volume of 69 mL was identified, whose eccentric regurgitant jet directed toward the anterior mitral leaflet. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed prolapse of the right coronary cusp (RCC) due to cusp bending (Figure 1F, Video 2), corroborated by cardiac computed tomography (CT) imaging (Figure 1G). Aortography confirmed severe regurgitation (Sellers grade IV) and markedly increased left ventricular volumes (end-diastolic 295 mL, end-systolic 150 mL). Coronary angiography showed no significant stenosis. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with severe AR. Although asymptomatic, surgical intervention was indicated due to cardiac dilatation with reduced EF.

Initially, aortic valve repair was attempted. Intraoperative findings revealed multiple fenestrations at the junction of the left and noncoronary cusps, and the RCC was retracted and prolapsed. Central plication of the RCC was performed. However, after aortotomy closure and cardioplegia administration, inadequate pressure at the aortic root was noted, and intraoperative echocardiography confirmed residual regurgitation from both the cusp center and fenestrations. Consequently, the strategy was converted to aortic valve replacement, and a 23-mm On-X mechanical valve was implanted.

Postoperatively, the patient developed persistent bleeding from the mediastinal drains, although coagulation parameters (prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time) remained within normal ranges. On postoperative day 2, progressive anemia required transfusion. Serosanguinous drainage continued, necessitating prolonged drain placement until postoperative day 8. Subsequent CT revealed a mediastinal hematoma at the drain insertion site, which was managed conservatively, with gradual improvement of anemia. At the 8-month postoperative follow-up, transthoracic echocardiography showed only trivial AR, an EF of 56%, and reduced left ventricular dimensions (left ventricular end-diastolic dimension 48 mm, left ventricular end-systolic dimension 30 mm), indicating reverse remodeling. At more than 1 year after surgery, the patient remains clinically stable without complications.

Discussion

Connective tissue disorders are an important etiologic consideration in young patients with early-onset AR. In this patient, OI was strongly suggested by blue sclera and childhood pathologic fractures. OI is caused by quantitative or qualitative defects in type I collagen,1,2 the major structural protein of bone, sclera, and cardiac valves. According to the Sillence classification, this case most closely resembles type IA, a milder form in which survival to adulthood is common, but cardiovascular manifestations may arise.1,2 Cardiovascular complications in OI commonly include AR, MR, and dilatation of the aortic root. In a Danish nationwide cohort study,3 patients with OI had a significantly higher cumulative incidence of MR, AR, and heart failure than matched control patients. Furthermore, surgical intervention for MR was significantly more frequent in the OI group, and there was a trend toward a higher rate of surgery for AR compared with patients in the control group.3 In a study comparing 99 adults with OI with 52 control patients, patients with OI were more likely to have aortic dilatation, supporting the concept that the aortic root is particularly vulnerable in this population.7 Although our patient did not show marked aortic root dilatation at presentation, these data underscore the need for careful longitudinal imaging follow-up of the aortic root in individuals with suspected OI.

A recent report described a similar RCC bending phenotype in a patient with OI. In that case, electrocardiogram-gated CT demonstrated RCC bending associated with aortic root dilatation, resulting in cusp prolapse and severe AR.8 In our patient, multimodality imaging likewise identified RCC prolapse caused by cusp bending, supporting the concept that cusp bending may represent a characteristic morphologic substrate of AR in collagen-related connective tissue disorders. However, unlike the prior report, our patient did not exhibit marked aortic root dilatation at presentation.

Intraoperatively, multiple fenestrations were observed, and histopathologic examination of the excised valve provided mechanistic insight into AR. The fibrosa, normally the main load-bearing layer on the aortic side, was markedly thinned, implying impaired capacity to withstand systemic pressure.5 Conversely, the ventricularis was thickened, and the spongiosa was expanded with Alcian blue–positive myxomatous material (Figure 1H). These changes likely represent compensatory remodeling, as the ventricularis and spongiosa attempt to buffer regurgitant flow when the fibrosa can no longer maintain tensile strength.5,9 Such maladaptive remodeling may explain the cusp bending and prolapse that produced severe AR. In essence, the primary collagen defect leads to structural weakness of the fibrosa, whereas secondary remodeling of the other layers reflects the hemodynamic burden of chronic regurgitation. Thus, our case complements the prior imaging-based observation by adding tissue-level evidence that links a collagen disorder to the macroscopic bending phenotype.

Beyond the valves themselves, collagen abnormalities in OI extend to the vasculature and hemostatic system. Type I collagen is a key ligand for platelet adhesion; impaired collagen-platelet interactions can result in defective aggregation. Meanwhile, abnormal collagen within capillary walls predisposes to microvascular fragility. Together, these mechanisms may increase the risk of perioperative bleeding despite normal coagulation profiles.6 In this case, the development of a mediastinal hematoma with progressive anemia, despite normal prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time, exemplifies this vulnerability.

Conclusions

This case highlights 2 key teaching points. First, in young patients with unexplained AR, OI and other collagenopathies should be recognized as potential etiologies, and histopathology may reveal characteristic alterations in the fibrosa and spongiosa. Second, in patients with connective tissue disorders, perioperative management should account for increased bleeding risk that is independent of coagulation factor abnormalities, necessitating heightened vigilance and meticulous hemostatic control during and after cardiac surgery.
