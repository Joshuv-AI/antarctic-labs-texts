Escape-induced temporally correlated noise driven crossover in growth kinetics and universality class
Authors: Mrinal Manna, Sourav Mukherjee, Soumen Giri, Pramod Bhakuni, Sajal Barman, Arnab Kumar Pariari, Anil Gome, Markus Hücker, V Raghavendra Reddy, Anupam Roy, Sudipta Roy Barman, Smarajit Karmakar, Chandana Mondal, Rajib Batabyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUniversal behavior in far-from-equilibrium systems is driven by interactions between transport processes and noise structure. The Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) framework predicts that extensions incorporating conserved currents or temporally correlated noise give rise to distinct growth morphologies and universality classes, yet direct experimental realization has remained elusive. Here, we report atomically resolved Sn thin-film growth on Sb-doped MnBi2Te4, revealing a sharp dynamical crossover between two fundamentally different regimes. Early-stage growth follows Villain-Lai-Das Sarma (VLDS) scaling, forming two-dimensional islands and stanene layers. Beyond a critical deposition time, temporally correlated noise becomes the dominant factor, driving the nucleation of α-Sn clusters, their development into faceted grains, and the coexistence of faceted β-Sn. Molecular dynamics simulations and Auger electron spectroscopy reveal that adatom escape serves as the microscopic origin of this temporally correlated noise, offering a mechanism for the observed universality crossover. These findings establish that temporal noise correlations can fundamentally alter the universality class of a growing interface, linking atomistic kinetics to emergent universal behavior.Subject terms: Scaling laws; Statistical physics; Surfaces, interfaces and thin films

Introduction

Understanding how large-scale order emerges in systems far from equilibrium remains a central challenge in statistical physics. Across diverse driven phenomena—from thin-film growth and bacterial colony expansion to flame fronts and fluid invasion—interfaces evolve through stochastic, kinetically roughened dynamics that self-organize into scale-invariant forms1–9. In this regime, microscopic details become irrelevant, and the evolution is governed by general features such as dimensionality, conservation laws, symmetries, and noise structure—leading to universal scaling exponents and distinct dynamical universality classes1,7–12.

Among these, the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) framework13 has emerged as the paradigmatic description of nonequilibrium surface growth, capturing the interplay between nonlinear growth, noise, and relaxation. Its various extensions—incorporating conserved surface currents14,15, spatial or temporal noise correlations16–26 —span a rich landscape of universality classes that describe real interfaces. The Villain-Lai-Das Sarma (VLDS) class models ideal molecular-beam epitaxy (MBE)14,15, where surface diffusion dominates and surface current is conserved, while temporally correlated noise in the KPZ (TCN-KPZ) equation profoundly modifies interfacial dynamics, producing anomalous roughening, clustering, and faceted morphologies23–25. Despite extensive theoretical advances predicting the effects of temporally correlated noise within the KPZ framework, a direct experimental observation of the dynamical crossover from VLDS to TCN-KPZ—together with a microscopic identification of the origin of such correlations—has remained elusive, leaving a crucial gap in our understanding of how atomistic kinetics govern emergent universality in nonequilibrium surface growth.

Here, we report an experimental realization of a universality crossover from the VLDS class to a TCN-KPZ regime during nonequilibrium Sn thin-film growth on 30% Sb-doped MnBi2Te4 (MBST). By combining atomically resolved scanning tunneling microscopy (STM), scaling analysis, and classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, we uncover a striking transition at growth time, t = 5.4 min—from two-dimensional island and stanene-layer growth obeying VLDS scaling to a late-time regime characterized by clustered mounds and faceted grains governed by temporally correlated noise. The extracted exponents quantitatively match theoretical predictions for VLDS and TCN-KPZ classes, respectively, confirming a dynamical universality crossover. Simulations reproduce this transition and reveal its microscopic origin: the spontaneous escape of adatoms during growth generates temporally correlated fluctuations in the effective noise term. These escape-induced noise correlations—power-law at short times and exponentially decaying at longer scales—drive the interface from VLDS to TCN-KPZ behavior.

Our results establish a direct microscopic mechanism—escape-induced temporally correlated noise—that bridges two nonequilibrium universality classes, connecting atomistic kinetics to emergent scaling laws. This finding not only confirms experimentally a long-standing theoretical prediction25,26 but also provides a predictive framework for controlling non-equilibrium morphology through noise correlations.

Results and discussion

Island to faceted grain growth

Sn growth on MBST provides a versatile platform to explore a spectrum of growth morphologies across multiple crystalline phases, captured in real time via scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) over extended deposition time (Fig. S1). At early stages of growth, the Sn interface forms two-dimensional (2D) islands atop MBST. In this regime, strong surface alloying occurs: two layers of Sn intermix with the topmost Te and Bi/Sb layers of the substrate, forming a buffer layer27. Upon continued deposition, few-layer stanene islands nucleate and grow on this alloyed surface (Fig. 1A, I). By t = 4.2 min, up to three stanene layers are observed, accompanied by a marked decrease in lateral coverage (Fig. S2). Atomically resolved STM imaging of stanene (Fig. 1E) reveals a surface lattice constant of 0.46 nm—approximately ~5% larger than that of the substrate (~0.44 nm). LEED analysis confirms that this enhanced lattice constant originates in the buffer layer and persists across successive stanene layers27. A larger lattice constant in the buffer layer signifies substantial strain relaxation through chemical interactions, providing a favorable template for low-strain stanene growth due to the small residual lattice mismatch of ~1.5% with respect to the free-standing stanene. On this reduced-strain platform, stanene nucleates as discrete islands on the buffer layer and subsequently evolves into bilayer structures atop the first layer, leaving the buffer only partially covered. With continued growth, the first-layer stanene eventually achieves nearly full coverage of the buffer, while the lateral extent of the bilayer stanene also increases —signifying a growth behavior reminiscent of the Stranski-Krastanov (SK) mode27. Moreover, unlike graphene stabilized by planar sp2 bonding, stemming from the intrinsic bonding of α-Sn, stanene favors an sp3-like hybridization due to the larger ionic radius of Sn, yielding a buckled lattice rather than a flat honeycomb. The bond angle, θ = 111. 4°, corresponds to an effective hybridization index spD with D=−1/cosθ~2.74, larger than in germanene (2.63), silicene (2.27), or graphene (2.00). This enhanced sp3 character makes stanene intrinsically less stable as a 2D material, with dangling bonds in the buckled lattice promoting a transition toward 3D clustering28,29. However, due to residual hydrogen atoms in the ultra-high vacuum chamber, H-passivated stanene films exhibit enhanced stability compared to their bare counterparts30, facilitating the initial formation of stanene layer.

This behavior is observed at t = 5.4 min, where the morphology exhibits a transition: Sn growth no longer proceeds as extended stanene layers but instead forms randomly distributed clusters (Fig. 1B, J, Supplementary Fig. S1). With further deposition, these clusters expand both vertically and laterally, coalescing into mound-like structures and eventually forming faceted grains (Fig. 1C, D, Supplementary Fig. S3A–F). Atomic-resolution STM reveals that, up to t = 25.2 min, the faceted grains are composed exclusively of the α-Sn phase (Fig. 1F); at later times, faceted grains exhibiting both α- and β-Sn phases coexist (Fig. 1G, H). This fact is also checked from grazing incidence X-ray diffraction experiments (Fig. S3G). Similar phase coexistence in Sn thin films has been reported on various substrates31,32, even down to the nanowire length scale33. These structural transitions are corroborated by height profiles (Fig. 1J–L), taken along lines indicated in the STM topographies (Fig. 1B–D), and their 3D view (Fig. 1M–O), which vividly capture the morphological evolution from 2D islands to faceted grains. Here, in describing system dimensionality, 2D and 3D are used for structural or morphological characteristics, whereas (2 + 1) dimensions refer to growth dynamics.

Universality crossover and temporally correlated noise

This sequence of morphological transitions as a function of growth time—from 2D alloyed islands and stanene layers to mound formation and faceted grains—reflects the complex interplay of nucleation, coalescence, and surface diffusion in non-equilibrium growth, giving rise to kinetic roughening and scale-invariant behavior. This motivates a quantitative analysis of dynamical scaling, characterized by the root-mean-square (rms) surface roughness, ω(t)=⟨[h(r,t)−⟨h⟩]2⟩, where h(r, t) is the interface (surface to vacuum) height at position r = (x, y) and t. During growth, the roughness evolves as ω(t) ~ tβ, where β is the growth exponent. The measured ω(t) as a function of deposition time reveals two distinct power-law regimes (Fig. 1P), with growth exponents β1 = 0.21 ± 0.03 during the early-stage island growth, and β2 = 0.66 ± 0.13 during the later stages of mound and faceted grain formation (onset is marked by black arrow). The rms roughness was computed as the standard deviation of the STM topography height map, providing an unbiased estimate independent of scanning direction or instrumental anisotropy. This results indicate a dynamical crossover between two fundamentally different growth modes. The physical processes underlying these growth modes can be quantitatively characterized through critical scaling exponents: the roughness exponent (α), the dynamic exponent (z) (share the scaling relation z = α/β), and the growth exponent β, which together describe the surface across spatiotemporal scales pinning down the universality class and growth modes12,34–39.

The height-height correlation function is defined as H(r,t)=⟨[h(r0+r,t)−h(r0,t)]2⟩, which measures the mean-squared height difference between two surface points separated by a distance r = ∣r∣. For r ≪ ξ(t), it exhibits power-law scaling as H(r,t)~ρ2r2αloc, where ρ is the average local slope, αloc is the local roughness exponent, and ξ(t) is the lateral correlation length. This was extracted from 2D autocorrelation function C(r, t) = 〈h(r0, t) h(r0 + r, t)〉 (Fig.S4), which quantifies how height fluctuations at two points separated by r are correlated. The correlation length ξ(t) was defined as the distance at which the angularly averaged correlation function 〈C(r, t)〉θ decays to 1/e of its maximum (Fig.S5), and is plotted in Fig. 2F. For r ≫ ξ(t), the height-height correlation function saturates as H(r, t) ~2ω212,23,25,39,40. We estimated the 2D H(r, t) for all data sets (fig.S6), and present representative examples from the early-stage island regime and the late-stage mound/faceted-grain regime in Fig. 2A, B. To extract αloc, we performed power-law fits to the angularly averaged height-height correlation function, 〈H(r, t)〉θ, as shown in Fig. 2C, D (see Fig. S7 for individual fits). The resulting local roughness exponent, αloc, with growth time (Fig. 2E) reveals two distinct scaling regimes separated by a sharp crossover at t=5.4min (marked by a black arrow), with average exponents 〈αloc1〉 = 0.71 ± 0.03 and 〈αloc2〉 = 0.87 ± 0.07 characterizing the early and late growth regimes, respectively. To determine the dynamic exponent z, we analyzed the time evolution of the lateral correlation length ξ(t) ~ t1/z. A clear power-law scaling of ξ(t) is observed in both regimes with the exponents 1/z1 = 0.31 ± 0.07 and 1/z2 = 0.74 ± 0.05 respectively, with the same crossover time at t = 5.4 min (Fig. 2G). We next compare the critical exponents αloc, β, and 1/z, each independently extracted from experimental data in the two growth regimes, without relying on interdependent scaling relations. In the initial regime, these exponents (αloc1 = 0.71 ± 0.03, β1 = 0.21 ± 0.03, and 1/z1 = 0.31 ± 0.07; where global roughness exponent, α1 = β1z1 = 0.68 ± 0.18 close to the αloc1 within error limit) closely match those predicted by two established models of MBE growth: the Lai-Das Sarma (LD) model14 and the Das Sarma-Tamborenea (DT) model41,42. The LD model describes conserved surface currents in the KPZ framework, mimicking ideal MBE growth with continuous atomic flux and bonding interactions. The DT model assumes random deposition with perfect surface relaxation, where atoms instantly settle into nearest kink sites. Although both models belong to the same asymptotic universality class, they differ in non-asymptotic corrections, particularly in the local slope exponent λ, defined by ρ(t) ~ tλ, where λ ≠ 0 for DT and λ = 0 for LD40,43. We also performed power-law fitting of the local slope ρ(t) to extract λ in both regimes (Fig. S8). In the early growth regime, the data exhibit minimal variation with time and are too scattered to reliably support a power-law, effectively consistent with λ1 = 0. This result aligns with the LD model, which predicts a vanishing slope exponent. Furthermore, the DT model predicts mound-like morphologies, which are clearly inconsistent with our observation of island growth. These results support the conclusion that the early-time growth dynamics are governed by the VLDS framework, specifically within the LD continuum universality class. Note that the observed VLDS scaling further suggests that Ehrlich-Schwoebel (ES) barrier effects are either absent or negligibly small in our system, as VLDS behavior is incompatible with ES-barrier-dominated kinetics that favor mound formation44.

In the subsequent growth regime, beyond the crossover time, the surface dynamics exhibit a distinctly different scaling behavior characterized by the extracted critical exponents αloc2 = 0.87 ± 0.07, β2 = 0.66 ± 0.13, and 1/z2 = 0.74 ± 0.05; yielding a global roughness exponent α2 = β2z2 = 0.89 ± 0.19, consistent with αloc2 within uncertainty. These values represent a clear departure from the VLDS scaling of the early regime, indicating the emergence of a different universality class governing the late-time dynamics. Interestingly, these values closely align with theoretical predictions of the KPZ framework extended to include temporally correlated noise (TCN-KPZ)25. The introduction of long-range, power-law temporal correlations in the KPZ noise term, ⟨η(r,t)η(r′,t′)⟩=2Dδr,r′∣t−t′∣2θ−1, where η(r, t) is the noise, D its amplitude, and θ∈[0,12) the correlation exponent, profoundly alters the growth dynamics, yielding modified scaling exponents and macroscopic surface features. Strong correlation (θ → 0.5) drives the surface to form clusters or mounds, which at longer times evolve into macroscopic faceted patterns that dominate the morphology23–25. Notably, our experimentally extracted exponents are consistent with θ ~ 0.45 in (2 + 1) dimensional TCN-KPZ framework, indicating a crossover from the VLDS class to a new universality class governed by long-range temporally correlated noise. At t = 5.4 min, the onset of temporally correlated noise manifests as a sharp transition from 2D islands to clustered mounds, which subsequently evolve into faceted grains, as shown in 3D topographies (Fig. 1M–O) and corresponding height profiles (Fig. 1I–L), consistent with theoretical predictions for surfaces subject to strongly correlated noise23–25. To provide a stringent test of universality in the growth dynamics, we examine whether the measured observables collapse onto a single universal curve under appropriate rescaling, as r→rξ and H(r,t)→H(r,t)2ω212αloc, applied separately for each region with its respective critical exponents. The resulting data collapse of H(r, t) across different growth times, shown in Fig. 2H, I, demonstrates robust convergence, thereby confirming the universality crossover from the VLDS class to the TCN-KPZ class. The sticking probability of adatoms could be a key parameter, determining the fraction of incident atoms that incorporate into the film versus those that desorb and act as noise in the dynamics. Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) as a function of growth time (Figs. 2J, S9) shows a striking change in the Sn/Te intensity ratio: the slope decreases from ~0.4 in the island-dominated VLDS regime to ~0.13 in the mound-to-faceted grain region governed by TCN-KPZ dynamics. This transition, occurring at t ~ 5.4 min, coincides precisely with the universality crossover of the critical exponents and the island to cluster morphological transition, demonstrating a strong suppression of Sn sticking on the surface when temporal noise correlations dominate45. This close correspondence in the onset time is unlikely to be incidental; rather, as discussed below, it marks the point at which temporal noise correlations emerge.

Generic kinetic roughening theory predicts that faceted surfaces, owing to their intrinsic geometric morphology, display distinct scaling functions and exponents. A complete characterization of these surfaces is most effectively achieved in Fourier space via the structure factor, S(k, t) = 〈h(k, t)h( − k, t)〉, where h(k,t)≡(1L)d/2∫drh(r,t)e−ik⋅r, and k the wave vector12,23. Notably, S(k, t) directly reveals the spectral roughness exponent αs, which characterizes faceted growing surfaces. In such surfaces, αs > 1 and the global roughness α differs from αs (here, α2 ≠ αs2), indicating that two independent exponents (α and αs) are required to fully capture the scaling behavior23. We estimated the 2D S(k, t) for the entire dataset (Fig. S10),with Fig. 3A, B depicting the island-dominated regime at t = 3.0 min and the mound-to-faceted grain regime at t = 150 min as illustrative examples. The spectral roughness exponent αs was obtained from power-law fits to the linear scaling regime of the angularly averaged structure factor, 〈S(k, t)〉θ (Fig. 3C, D; see Fig.S11 for individual fits). Its temporal evolution (Fig. 3E) reveals a sharp crossover at t = 5.4 min, separating two scaling regimes. In the early-time regime, we obtain 〈αs1〉 = 1.09 ± 0.13, for island-dominated growth, whereas at late times 〈αs2〉 = 1.40 ± 0.15, satisfies the condition αs2 > 1 and global α2(0.89 ± 0.19) ≠ αs2 for the onset of faceted morphology. We further performed data collapse of the structure factors by rescaling k → k(2α+2)(t−δ)(2α+2)/z and S(k, t) → S(k, t) k2α+2 46 (Fig. 3F, G). The most consistent collapse was achieved with the exponents 1/z1 = 0.36, α1 = 0.58 in the island-dominated regime with δ = 0, and 1/z2 = 0.75, α2 = 0.88 in the mound-to faceted grain regime with effective growth time δ = 1.2 min46, which are very close to the measured coefficients. These results further provide experimental evidence that temporally correlated noise drives KPZ interfaces into a faceted regime, a phenomenon anticipated by theory and observed in simulations23–25.

Origin of escape-induced correlated noise

To probe the universality crossover from VLDS to TCN-KPZ and uncover the origin of temporally correlated noise, we carried out classical MD simulations of non-equilibrium Sn growth (simulation details in “Methods”). In simulations, reproducing the experimental crossover required two complementary model systems. A Bi2Te3 slab was adopted as an analog of MBST as reliable interatomic potentials for Mn and Sb are unavailable; moreover, Bi2Te3 faithfully reproduces the characteristic buffer-layer formation that precedes stanene growth27,47. The late-stage regime was modeled using a buckled honeycomb Sn layer to represent the stanene phase (see growth Movies S1, S2). This combination reproduce the experimental observation here. Figure 4A–C display the simulated surface evolution (Fig. S12 for entire regime), showing an initial island nucleation that progressively transitions to clustered and mounded morphologies, eventually developing into faceted grains. To highlight the morphological evolution, corresponding area-selected 3D topographies (Fig. 4D–F) and representative height profiles along the marked arrows in Fig. 4A–C are presented (Fig. 4G–I), closely reproducing the experimental observations (Fig. 1). On these simulated topographies, we performed similar analyses to determine the critical exponents across the entire growth regime. The simulated rms roughness, ω(t), as a function of deposition time exhibits two distinct power-law regimes (Fig. 4J), mirroring the experimental data (Fig. 1P). The growth exponents were found to be β1 = 0.24 ± 0.10 during the early-stage island nucleation and β2 = 0.72 ± 0.04 during the subsequent mound and faceted grain growth, with the onset of the crossover indicated by a black arrow. Likewise, the temporal evolution of the local roughness exponent αloc (Fig. 4K) and dynamic exponent z (Fig. 4L, M) reveals two clearly separated regimes, characterized by αloc1 = 0.68 ± 0.04 and 1/z1 = 0.37 ± 0.07 for the early-stage growth, and αloc2 = 0.77 ± 0.04 and 1/z2 = 0.63 ± 0.05 for the late-stage morphology evolution. All simulated exponents exhibit excellent agreement (within uncertainties, Table 1) with the experimentally determined values for the VLDS and TCN-KPZ regimes, and the corresponding data collapse demonstrates consistent convergence (Fig. S13). During the simulations, we observed a notable interplay between Sn growth and the underlying substrates. On the Bi2Te3 slab, the universality crossover is clearly captured near t ~ 5.4 min, in agreement with experimental observations, however, continued growth on this substrate beyond t = 7.8 min leads to a deviation of the critical exponents that define the universality class. Examining the experimentally observed stanene island morphologies near the transition time t = 5.4 min reveals that the first-layer stanene largely covers (~97.76%) the underlying buffer (Fig. S2B–D), before clusters begin to form, marking the onset of the TCN-KPZ regime. Notably, in simulations performed on Bi2Te3, a stanene layer with comparable coverage is never realized. Guided by experimental observations, we switched the substrate to stanene, and subsequent growth reproduces the experimentally measured exponents with quantitative consistency. Further calculations including the presence of residual hydrogen in the MD simulations exhibit similar escape events and do not alter their temporal correlations (Fig.S15 and the additional simulation details48).

To uncover the origin of temporally correlated noise, we analyzed the particle dynamics during growth. Up to t = 5.4 min, all deposited particles are incorporated into islands, contributing fully to growth (top panels of Fig. 4N). Beyond t = 7.8 min, a significant fraction of particles take part in the dynamics and escape the system (see growth movie S3 with escaped adatoms marked in red), giving rise to a distinct escape-induced noise component (bottom panels of Fig. 4N). The temporal correlations of this noise reveal a complex structure (Fig. 4O). Rather than a simple power-law decay, the correlations exhibit two regimes: short-time lags follow a power-law, while longer lags decay exponentially. This behavior is accurately described by, ⟨η(r,t)η(r′,t′)⟩=2Dδr,r′∣t−t′∣2θ−1exp[−∣t−t′∣2/t02], where t0 is the characteristic decay time. Fitting this model to the simulation data yields a correlation exponent θavg = 0.48 ± 0.01 (Fig. 4P), in close agreement with the theoretical prediction (θ ~ 0.45)25. This microscopic mechanism is fully consistent with the experimental AES measurements: the slope of Sn/Te intensity ratio as a function of growth time sharply decreases from ~0.4 in the island-dominated VLDS regime to ~0.13 in the mound-to-faceted grain regime (Fig. 2J), precisely coinciding with the onset of temporally correlated dynamics. Together, the AES data and simulations demonstrate that the reduction in effective sticking probability arises from escape-induced temporally correlated noise, linking atomistic desorption events to the emergent universality crossover from VLDS to TCN-KPZ behavior. These findings demonstrate that particle escape during growth acts as a microscopic source of temporally correlated noise, with a dual character: power-law correlations at short times and exponential decay at longer times.

This mechanism provides a concrete origin for the crossover from VLDS dynamics to TCN-KPZ behavior, underscoring the fundamental role of adatom escape in shaping interface fluctuations. Early-stage 2D islands and stanene layers follow VLDS scaling, but beyond t ~ 5.4 min, long-range temporally correlated noise in the deposition dynamics drive a transition to the TCN-KPZ regime, producing clustered mounds and faceted grains. MD simulations quantitatively reproduce both regimes, linking microscopic particle escape to macroscopic surface universality, and establishing a predictive framework to control non-equilibrium growth and engineer surface morphology in (2 + 1) dimension.

Methods

Material details

Substrate

The 30% Sb-doped MnBi2Te4 (MBST) single crystals were grown using the flux method. To synthesize an n% Sb-doped MBST crystal, high-purity (99.999%) Mn powder, Bi shots, Te chunks, and Sb shots were mixed in a molar ratio of Mn:Sb:Bi:Te = 1 : 10 × n : 10 × (1 - n) : 16 placed in an alumina crucible of a Canfield crucible set. The assembly was sealed in an evacuated silica ampule under a pressure below 10−5 mbar. The ampule was heated to 1173 K over two days, held for 12 h, and then slowly cooled to 878 K at a rate of 10 K h−1. In the final stage, the temperature was lowered from 878 K to 863 K over 336 h and maintained for 72 h before centrifugation at ~ 1200 × g to remove the residual flux. Shiny, plate-like single crystals were obtained from the bottom crucible. The Sb concentration in our samples was determined to be ~30% 27.

Interface Growth

Sn (99.99%) was deposited on MBST surfaces using a water-cooled Knudsen cell49 at a cell temperature of 1143 K, while the sample was maintained at room temperature (~303 K). The deposition rate was fixed at 0.83 ML min−1 during all deposition cycles. The chamber pressure remained at ~2 × 10−10 mbar throughout the growth.

Measurement details

Atomically clean MBST(0001) surfaces were prepared in situ by the scotch-tape exfoliation method inside an ultrahigh-vacuum (UHV) chamber with a base pressure of 1.0 × 10−10 mbar. Scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) measurements were performed using a Scienta Omicron GmbH variable-temperature STM system maintained at ~4 × 10−11 mbar throughout the experiments and operated at room temperature. A chemically etched tungsten (W) tip was used for all STM measurements. Auger Electron Spectroscopy (AES) was conducted immediately after each adlayer deposition cycle with an electron beam energy of 1.5 keV. The Sn MNN/Te MNN intensity ratio was quantitatively determined by considering the respective normalized sensitivity factors of the Sn MNN and Te MNN transitions. Grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) measurements were performed at ambient temperature and pressure.

Simulation details

Atomistic growth models and simulation protocol

MD simulations were performed to model Sn deposition on two substrates corresponding to distinct experimental regimes.

Regime I: Sn was deposited on a Bi2Te3 slab comprising a single quintuple layer (QL) with a Te-Bi-Te-Bi-Te stacking sequence, having a thickness of ~1 nm and lateral dimensions of ~32 nm. Bi2Te3 was chosen as an analog of the experimentally used MBST-based substrate since the latter contains magnetic Mn and Sb dopants for which reliable empirical potentials are unavailable. To mimic bulk anchoring, the bottom atomic layers of the substrate were fixed, while the upper layers were allowed to relax dynamically.

Regime II: In this case, Sn atoms were deposited on a buckled honeycomb Sn layer, representing a stanene sheet with a buckling height of 0.085 nm and a lattice constant of 0.467 nm. This model represents an idealized free-standing stanene layer, serving as a reference for strain-free growth conditions.

In both regimes, periodic boundary conditions were applied along the lateral (x, y) directions, and free boundaries were used along the growth direction (z). The simulation box extended up to 20 nm in the z-direction, providing sufficient vacuum for film growth.

Interatomic interactions among Bi, Te, and Sn atoms were modeled using the Elliott-Akerson (2015) universal Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential distributed via OpenKIM50. The pair potential between atoms i and j separated by a distance r is given by

While this LJ form captures adsorption and diffusion behavior, it does not include directional bonding, charge transfer, or spin-orbit effects; thus, our results focus on morphological evolution and universality-class identification rather than electronic or magnetic properties.

Sn atoms were introduced sequentially above the instantaneous surface at a flux of one atom per ten MD steps, corresponding to an experimental deposition rate of ~0.83 ML min−1 when mapped to MD timescales. One monolayer (ML) was defined as the number of Sn atoms required to completely cover the topmost substrate surface. All simulations were performed in LAMMPS51 using the metal unit system with a timestep of 1 fs. The neighbor list was updated every timestep with a skin distance of 0.2 nm. During deposition, the incoming atoms were maintained at 1143 K to emulate the experimental Knudsen-cell temperature, while the substrate was held at 300 K. This setup ensured that the incoming atoms acquired realistic kinetic energies consistent with the experimental flux, while the substrate remained thermally stable. After each deposition cycle, the system was cooled to 300 K over 1 ns and equilibrated for an additional 1 ns. Atomic configurations, potential energies, and per-atom stresses were recorded every 5000 steps. Density profiles along the growth direction were extracted to monitor film thickness and surface roughness. Atomic trajectories were visualized and analyzed using OVITO52 to generate structural snapshots and perform qualitative post-processing of the evolving morphology.
