Autoimmune reactions to ACE2 triggered by SARS-CoV-2 S protein and their histopathological consequences
Authors: Liubov Beduleva, Kseniya Fomina, Tatyana Khramova, Alexey Terentiev, Daria Menshikova, Oksana Terentieva, Igor Menshikov, Alexandr Sidorov, Polina Makhankova, Nadezhda Abisheva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSARS-CoV-2 has been shown to induce autoimmunity. Due to the idiotype-antiidiotypic interactions between lymphocytes to SARS-CoV-2 S protein and lymphocytes to angiotensin converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), the immune response to S protein can cause induction of anti-ACE2 lymphocytes. The ubiquity of ACE2 within the human body endows autoimmune reactions to ACE2 with the role of the main factor causing injuries of various tissues. Immunization of Wistar rats with S protein caused hyperplasia and dedifferentiation of type II pneumocytes, extensive injury of the proximal tubules, infiltration of CD4+ T, CD45RA+ B lymphocytes in the lungs and CD4+ T, CD8+ T lymphocytes in the kidneys. Both type II pneumocytes and proximal tubule epithelium express ACE2. Damage to ACE2 expressing cells in the absence of other lesions in the studied organs suggests that ACE2 might be the target of an autoimmune attack induced by S protein. Our findings clarify the mechanism of multiple tissue damage in COVID-19.Keywords: Autoimmunity, Proximal tubules, Type II pneumocyte hyperplasia, Angiotensin converting enzyme 2Subject terms: Immunology, Autoimmunity

Introduction

SARS-CoV-2 infection has been shown to induce generation of multiple types of autoantibodies and autoimmune diseases1–4. The variety of tissues damaged by autoimmune reactions during SARS-CoV-2 infection has not yet been satisfactorily explained.

Cross-reactivity of antibodies against SARS-CoV-2 S protein to human tissue antigens is one of the possible mechanisms for multi-system disorders. Vojdani et al. have indicated that the potential for cross-reactivity of antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 is substantial5,6. Antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 had reactions with a wide range of different human tissue antigens, among which there are myelin basic protein, collagen, thyroid peroxidase, transglutaminase 3, transglutaminase 2, α-myosin and others5,6. As follows from the above works, monoclonal antibodies are usually used to study the cross-binding of anti-S protein antibodies to body proteins5,6. It is not known whether cross-reactivity is realized in an organism where the immune response against S protein is polyclonal.

In addition to anti-SARS-CoV-2 S protein antibodies that cross-react to autoantigens, autoantibodies and autoreactive lymphocytes specific to angiotensin converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) may be equally important factors in a variety of autoimmune manifestations in COVID-19. Autoantibodies and lymphocytes against ACE2 can be induced in the course of SARS-CoV-2 infection through idiotype-antiidiotypic interactions with lymphocytes against S protein receptor-binding domain. Many patients with a history of SARS-CoV-2 infection have autoantibodies specific for ACE22,3,7. Expression of ACE2 was found in many cells and tissues including vascular endothelia, kidney tubular epithelium, intestinal epithelial cells of the gut, cardiovascular tissues, testes, ovary and breast8–12. Ubiquitous of ACE2 within the human body is the reason to consider autoimmunity to ACE2 as the main factor of damage to many tissues during SARS-CoV-2 infection. Here, we aimed to determine whether ACE2-expressing cells are damaged in response to SARS-CoV-2 S protein immunization.

It has been shown that S protein from various SARS-CoV-2 variants (Alpha, Delta and Omicron) binds to rat ACE213. This allows rats to be considered a suitable model for studying the autoimmune reactions to ACE2, triggered by SARS-CoV-2 S protein.

Materials and methods

Rats

Outbred Wistar rats of both sexes (8–10 weeks old, body weight 198 ± 15 g) were obtained from the Rappolovo breeding facility (Rappolovo, Russia). Animal experiments were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines, the U.K. Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act, 1986, and EU Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments. For all the procedures, we sedated the rats using conventional formulation of Xylazine (Xylanit, NITA-FARM, Saratov, Russia), (10 mg/kg body weight, subcutaneously). At the end of the study, rats were euthanized using a 50% volume per minute displacement rate of carbon dioxide for 2 min. Exposure times of at least 11 min in 100% carbon dioxide were required to ensure irreversible euthanasia of rats. Food and water refusal, loss of more than 15% of body weight over two days, convulsive breathing and breathlessness were considered as humane end-point criterion. No rats reached the above criteria; therefore, all animals were euthanized based on the predetermined time-point (10 weeks after immunization). All procedures were performed by veterinary staff. The protocol and procedures employed were ethically reviewed and approved by the Bioethics Committee of Udmurt State University (Date January 20, 2022/ No. 2201).

All methods reported in this study were conducted in compliance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulatory standards.

Detection of SARS-CoV-2 in Wistar rat tissues

RNA extraction from rat lung, kidney and spleen was carried out using the RNA extraction kit RealBest Sorbitus (Vector-Best, Koltsovo, Russia) as per manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time RT‐PCR technique was used to verify the presence of SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA. PCR was performed with SARS-CoV-2 RNA reverse transcription kit (Vector-Best, Koltsovo, Russia).

Immunization

Wistar rats (5 males and 5 females) were immunized with 200 μl emulsion, containing 18 μg of SARS-CoV-2 S protein (ACROBiosystems Inc., Newark, USA) and 100 μl incomplete Freund’s adjuvant (IFA) intradermally. Control group of Wistar rats (4 males and 3 females) were injected with 200 μl saline/IFA. After immunization the rats were monitored daily. The rats were sacrificed 10 weeks after immunization.

Histological analysis of lungs and kidneys

Tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h without inflation and embedded in paraffin. The paraffine sections of kidneys and lungs were cut into slices of 5‑µm thickness and mounted on glass slides. The sections were dewaxed at 40 °C, immersed in two changes of xylene, and then rehydrated in a graded ethanol series. The lungs sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin and the kidney sections were stained with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) reaction (Biovitrum, Saint-Petersburg, Russia). After coloring, all sections were carried out using alcohols of ascending concentration, soaked in xylene, enclosed in BioMount (Bio Optica Milano Spa, Italia) and studied under a light microscope. All the sections were viewed and assessed blindly by three independent investigators.

The pathological changes in the kidneys were assessed by tubular simplification (absence or thinning of brush border of the proximal tubules, destruction of epithelium, dilation of proximal tubules lumens). The changes were classified on a semiquantitative scale from 0 to 3, depending on the degree of proximal tubules involvement: 0—normal; 1—mild—involvement by 10–25%; 2—moderate—involvement by 25–50%; 3—severe—involvement > 50%.

Analysis of lymphocytic infiltration of kidneys and lungs

The paraffin sections of kidneys and lungs were dewaxed and rehydrated according to the standard procedure described in “Histological analysis of lungs and kidneys” section. The sections were autoclaved at 120 °C in a sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 10 min for antigen retrieval. The tissues were then incubated in 1% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min, washed in PBS/Tween 20 and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin in PBS/Tween 20 at room temperature (RT) for 1 h. Then, the sections were incubated with the primary antibodies for 1 h at RT, followed by incubation with the secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. The primary antibodies used in the present study included mouse monoclonal antibodies to rat CD3 (Elabscience Bionovation Inc., Houston, USA), rabbit polyclonal antibodies to CD4 (Affinity Biosciences Ltd., Cincinnati, USA), rabbit polyclonal antibodies to CD8 (Affinity Biosciences Ltd., Cincinnati, USA), and mouse monoclonal CD45RA antibodies (Elabscience Bionovation Inc., Houston, USA). HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG, IgM, IgA antibodies and goat anti-rabbit IgG, IgM, IgA antibodies (IMTEK Ltd., Moscow, Russia) were used as secondary antibodies. Both primary and secondary antibodies were diluted in PBS/Tween 20 containing 1% BSA. The conjugate’s peroxidase label was visualized by DAB (DAB Substrate Kit, Abcam Ltd., Cambridge, USA). Immunostaining was contrasted with hematoxylin. The sections were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, immersed sequentially in two changes of xylene and mounted with a coverslip.

Immunohistochemical staining of lungs for ACE2, surfactant associated protein C and aquaporine 5

Sections of the lungs were prepared and stained by the method described in “Analysis of lymphocytic infiltration of kidneys and lungs” section. Type 1 pneumocytes were identified using rabbit polyclonal antibodies to AQP5 (Affinity Biosciences Ltd., Cincinnati, USA). Type 2 pneumocytes were stained with rabbit polyclonal antibodies to ACE2 (Affinity Biosciences Ltd., Cincinnati, USA) or rabbit polyclonal antibodies to SP-C (Affinity Biosciences Ltd., Cincinnati, USA). To reveal bound antibodies, HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, IgM, IgA antibodies (IMTEK Ltd., Moscow, Russia) and DAB Substrate Kit (Abcam Ltd., Cambridge, USA) were used.

Serum creatinine measurement

Creatinine levels were measured in rat blood plasma obtained 10 weeks after S protein immunization using the kinetic Jaffe method, kit Creatinine-Novo-A (Vector-Best, Koltsovo, Russia). The working solution, containing picric acid (21.7 mmol/L), sodium tetraborate (20 mmol/L), and sodium hydroxide (0.192 mol/L), was preheated to 37 °C. Then, 100 μL of either the test serum sample or the calibration sample (a creatinine solution with a concentration of 240 μmol/L in hydrochloric acid) was added to 1000 μL of the working solution. The mixture was thoroughly mixed, and after 30 s of incubation, the absorbance was read at 500 nm against water. The measurement was repeated after 60 s. The concentration of creatinine (C) in the blood serum was calculated as follows: C, μmol/L = (∆E/∆Ek) × Ck, where ΔE is the difference in optical densities between the first and second measurements of the test sample; ΔEk is the difference in optical densities between the first and second measurements of the calibration sample; Ck is the creatinine concentration in the calibration sample (μmol/L).

ELISA of antibodies to SARS-CoV-2 S protein and autoantibodies to ACE2

Sera of the rats, immunized with SARS-CoV-2 S protein were tested for antibodies to S protein, autoantibodies to ACE2. Antibodies were determined weekly for 10 weeks.

To determine anti-S protein antibodies, we used plates coated with recombinant SARS-CoV-2 S glycoprotein (full-sized trimerized surface glycoprotein S, including a receptor-binding domain—RBD) (Vector-Best, Koltsovo, Russia). Serum samples were added in a 1:400 dilution with PBS/Tween 20. After 30-min incubation at 37 °C and subsequent washing of the wells with PBS/Tween 20, 100 µl of antibodies to rat immunoglobulins conjugated to horseradish peroxidase were added to the wells (IMTEK Ltd., Russia). Then, the substrate mixture (5 ml citrate buffer solution [pH 5.0]/3 mg ortho-phenylenediamine/15 ml 3% H2O2) was added. Absorbances were read after 15 min at 492 nm.

For the detection of autoantibodies to ACE2, plates (Corning-Costar, Acton, MA, USA) were coated overnight at 4 °C with ACE2 (ProSpec-Tany TechnoGene Ltd., Rehovot, Israel) 10 μg/mL in PBS, pH 7.4. Plates were blocked with 150 μl of 0.15 mol/l PBS/0.05%BSA/Tween 20. Serum samples were added in a 1:100 dilution in PBS/Tween 20 and incubated for 1 h at RT. The plates were then incubated for 1 h at RT with 100 μl of goat anti-rat Ig (IgG, IgM, IgA) conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (IMTEK Ltd., Moscow, Russia). Then, the substrate mixture (5 ml citrate buffer solution [pH 5.0]/3 mg ortho-phenylenediamine/15 ml 3% H2O2) was added. Absorbances were read after 15 min at 492 nm.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism Software. Shapiro–Wilk test for testing the normality of data was used. Statistical significance between groups was determined by t test. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Autoantibodies against ACE2 in rats immunized with SARS-CoV-2 S protein

Immunization with SARS-CoV-2 S protein caused the production of autoantibodies to ACE2 in 70% rats (Fig. 1, Table 1). The production of autoantibodies to ACE2 reached its maximum level in the blood after 2 weeks, whereas the maximum level of autoantibodies to S protein was observed 3–4 weeks after immunization with S protein (Fig. 1).

The phenomenon of the production of antiidiotypic autoantibodies (including anti-idiotypic autoantibodies induced by a viral protein) ahead of idiotypic antibodies to an immunogen was observed by us earlier14 and described by other researchers15,16. The level of antibodies to S protein and the level of autoantibodies to ACE2 vary reciprocally (Fig. 1), which is a characteristic feature of the kinetics of antibodies bound in idiotype-antiidiotypic interactions.

Viral RNA in tissues

The Wistar strain belongs to the species Rattus norvegicus, which has been shown to be susceptible to infection with Alpha, Delta, and Omicron variants13. Therefore, Wistar rats can be carriers of SARS-CoV-2. In the tissues of Wistar rats used in the present experiment, SARS‐CoV‐2 RNA has not been detected.

Kidneys

In the kidneys of rats immunized with S protein, thinning or loss of brush border of the proximal tubules, destruction of epithelial cells and focal ruptures of tubular basement membrane of the proximal tubules were found (Fig. 2a,c).

(a, c, e) Alterations in kidney of rats immunized with SARS-CoV-2 S protein: thinning or loss of the brush border (open arrows), dilatation of the lumen of the proximal tubules (asterisks), proximal tubule rupture (arrowheads) and enlargement of Bowman’s space (double arrows). (b, d, f) IFA injected rat. Normal proximal tubules have a prominent apical brush border (arrows). Periodic acid–Schiff staining.

The tubular lumen of proximal tubules was dilated (Fig. 2a,c). Alterations/loss of brush border was revealed in 100% rats immunized with S protein (Table 1).

The intensity of the damage varied from local to total, when the brush border was damaged in most of the proximal tubules (Fig. 2e). In some animals, the enlargement of Bowman space was revealed (Fig. 2a,c). No kidney changes were found in the control group that received the IFA injection.

It is known that ACE2 is expressed in the apical brush borders of the proximal tubule epithelial cells as well as the podocytes in less intensity17. Inasmuch ACE2 is expressed in the brush border of the proximal cells, it is considered that these cells are susceptible to direct injury caused by SARS-CoV-2 infection8,18,19. We found damage to the brush border in response to immunization with SARS-CoV-2 S protein in the absence of SARS-CoV-2 infection.

There was no connection between the presence of autoantibodies to ACE2 in the blood and damage to the proximal renal tubules. However, CD3+, CD4+ and CD8+ lymphocytic infiltration in the renal cortical interstitium was found in rats with damaged proximal tubules (Fig. 3). Consequently, damage to the epithelial cells of the proximal renal tubules expressing ACE2 may be mediated by autoreactive T cell response.

Lymphocytic infiltration in the kidneys of rats immunized with SARS-CoV-2 S protein. Representative images. (a) CD3+ T lymphocytes (arrows). (b) CD8+ T lymphocytes (arrows). (c) CD4+ T lymphocytes (arrows).

The creatinine level measured in rat sera 10 weeks after immunization with SARS-CoV-2 S protein varies extremely (Table 1). However, no significant changes in creatinine level in S protein immunized rats relative to the control group receiving IFA were detected (t test, p ≤ 0.05).

Thus, immunization with SARS-CoV-2 S protein caused damage to epithelial cells expressing ACE2 of the proximal renal tubules in rats. Damage to the epithelium of the proximal tubules is associated with their T lymphocytic infiltration.

Lungs

In 50% of rats immunized with S protein alveolar epithelial hyperplasia was identified (Table 1, Fig. 4a,c, Fig. 5a–c). Mitotic figures (Fig. 4c) and binucleated cells (Fig. 4c) can occasionally be found in foci of alveolar epithelial hyperplasia.

As it is known, alveolar epithelial hyperplasia is a proliferation of cuboidal type II pneumocytes20,21. Since type II pneumocytes express ACE2, we expected an increase in the number of ACE2 positive cells in hyperplastic foci. However, only a few cuboidal type II pneumocytes in the focus of hyperplasia were stained with antibodies against ACE2 (Fig. 4a).

The type II pneumocytes have the capacity to transdifferentiate into type I pneumocytes. Within the process of transdifferentiation, type II cells dedifferentiate, proliferate and some of the daughter cells differentiate into type I cells22. Jia et al. have shown that the expression of ACE2 in type II pneumocytes positively correlates with the state of cell differentiation. Undifferentiated cells express little ACE223. We assumed that the unstained cells in hyperplastic foci are type II pneumocytes, undergoing dedifferentiation and proliferation.

Staining for surfactant-associated protein C (SP-C), which is known to be type II pneumocyte-specific marker, showed single type II pneumocytes in hyperplastic foci (Fig. 5a,b). Hyperplastic foci were clearly negative for aquaporin AQP5, which is type I pneumocytes marker (Fig. 5c), whereas non-hyperplastic areas of the lung in the same section showed strong staining for anti-AQP5 (Fig. 5d). Thus, hyperplastic foci mainly contain cells, which bear neither type II pneumocytes markers (ACE-2 and SP-C) nor type I pneumocytes marker (AQP5).

The morphological analysis of hyperplastic cells showed that they combine features of both type I and type II pneumocytes. These cells exhibited round nuclei and residual lamellar bodies with positive staining for SP-C, features consistent with type II pneumocytes (Fig. 5b). Unlike type II pneumocytes, the cells exhibited asymmetrically located nuclei and were larger in diameter (12–17 µm) (Fig. 5b). Moreover, hyperplastic cells had pure cytoplasm similar to type I pneumocytes (Fig. 5b).

Hyperplastic cells in lungs of rats immunized with S protein are likely to be transitional cells, which were described in diverse mouse models of lung injury and in postmortem lung tissue of patients with COVID-19 acute respiratory distress syndrome21. Transitional cells refer to type II pneumocytes that have proliferated in an attempt to replace damaged alveolar epithelial cells, but failed to complete trans-differentiation into type I pneumocytes21. The transitional state is characterized by markers of cell cycle exit, down-regulation of type 2 pneumocyte markers (ACE-2 and SP-C), absence of type 1 pneumocyte markers, a cuboidal or partially spread morphology, and expression of unique signature genes, including KRT821.

Immunostaining of rat lung sections for CD3, CD4, CD8 and CD45RA revealed the infiltration of CD4+ T lymphocytes and CD45RA+ B lymphocytes (Fig. 6). Therefore, we cannot rule out that alveolar type II pneumocyte hyperplasia is caused not only by serum autoantibodies to ACE2, but also by antibodies locally produced by infiltrating B cells.

In addition to lymphocytic infiltration in lung parenchyma, 2 out of 10 rats immunized with S protein revealed perivascular lymphocytic infiltration and lymphocytic infiltration in the walls of lung vessels (Fig. 7).

Perivascular lymphocytic infiltration in the lungs of rats immunized with SARS-CoV-2 S protein. H&E staining.

Thus, immunization with SARS-CoV-2 S protein caused alveolar hyperplasia in rats. The foci of hyperplastic alveolar epithelium consist of dedifferentiated type II pneumocyte, contain atypical cells, T and B lymphocytic infiltrates.

Discussion

Histological analysis of the lungs and kidneys of rats immunized with SARS-CoV-2 S protein revealed hyperplasia and dedifferentiation of type II pneumocytes in the lungs, CD4+ T, CD45RA+ lymphocytic infiltration and perivascular lymphocytic infiltration in the lungs, thinning or loss of brush border and destruction of epithelial cells of the proximal renal tubules, CD4+ T and CD8+ T lymphocytic infiltration in areas of proximal tubule damage.

We compared the lesions detected in rats with the histopathology detected in COVID-19 patients. Hammoud et al. summarizing 50 articles reporting organ pathology in COVID-19 patients showed that pneumocyte hyperplasia and lymphocyte and/or monocyte infiltrates were found in 60% of cases24.

The review of articles made by Malik et al. proves that type II pneumocyte hyperplasia is one of the most common shared findings among lung-histological sections of COVID-19 patients25. Zeng et al. found perivascular inflammatory infiltration in the lungs of COVID-19 patient26.

Patients who died of COVID-19 showed clinical signs of kidney injury that included increased serum creatinine and/or new-onset proteinuria. By light microscopy, proximal acute tubule injury manifested as the loss of brush border, vacuolar degeneration, dilatation of the tubular lumen with cellular debris, and occasionally even frank necrosis and detachment of epithelium with bare tubular basement membrane were noted17,27. Larsen et al. found damage to the epithelium of the proximal tubules, which included reactive nuclei with mitotic figures and diffuse simplification with the exposure of brush edges in the deceased patient28. Consequently, the lesions found in the lungs and kidneys of S protein immunized rats are similar to those found in COVID-19 patients.

The mechanism of kidney and lung damage during SARS-CoV-2 infection in humans remains unclear. Viral particles in kidneys were not identified and prominent interstitial inflammation characteristic of many viral nephropathies was not seen in people died from COVID-1929. The RNA of the virus in the kidneys of dead people was also not detected. These facts raise doubts that the cause of tissue damage in SARS-CoV-2 infection is the direct effect of the virus on cells. Induction of SARS-CoV-2 infection-specific damage in rats by S protein immunization without SARS-CoV-2 infection indicates that organ damage is caused by autoimmune reaction induced by viral S protein.

Both type II pneumocytes and epithelial cells of the proximal tubules of the kidneys express ACE2. Damage to ACE2 expressing cells in the absence of other obvious damage of the studied organs in rats immunized with S protein indicates that the target of an autoimmune attack induced by S protein immunization is ACE2.

Geanes et al. demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 infection can increase autoantibody levels to ACE230. The levels of these autoantibodies were associated with COVID-19 disease severity2,31. At the same time, no deposits of immunoglobulins were found in the kidneys28. We also found the production of autoantibodies to ACE2 in rats immunized with S protein. Induction of autoreactive lymphocytes to ACE2 may occur via their idiotype–anti-idiotypic interactions with lymphocytes specific to SARS-CoV-2 S protein. Existence of idiotype–anti-idiotypic interactions between autoreactive lymphocytes to ACE2 and lymphocytes to SARS-CoV-2 S protein is likely to take place, as SARS-CoV-2 S protein and ACE2 are complementary13.

However, no relationship was found between production of autoantibodies to ACE2 and the degree of damage to ACE2 expressing cells. Moreover, in a few rats immunized with S protein, lung and kidney damage occurred in the absence of production of autoantibodies to ACE2.

At the same time, infiltration of CD4+ T lymphocytes and CD45RA+ B lymphocytes was observed in damaged rat lungs. As is well known, B lymphocytes infiltrating lesion sites may produce disease-specific autoantibodies in some autoimmune diseases32. Based on these facts, we assume that the damage of ACE2-expressing cells in the lungs may be caused not only by serum autoantibodies to ACE2 but also by autoantibodies locally produced by infiltrating B cells. The CD4 + T and CD8 + T lymphocyte infiltration observed in the kidneys suggests the key role of anti-idiotypic T cell-mediated autoimmune response against ACE2 in the damage of ACE2-expressing epithelial cells in the kidneys.

In human an inflammatory infiltrate was also detected in the interstitial cortex of the kidneys, which consisted of lymphocytes and plasma cells with scattered eosinophils28. Not only infection, but also mRNA vaccination with the COVID-19 vaccine can lead to T lymphocytic and macrophage infiltration in the lungs, kidneys, pericardium and myocardium, gastrointestinal tract, bladder and diaphragm, including fatal33–36. The chronic exposure of a target organ to a complex cytokine mixture, produced by T lymphocytes, could have varying consequences depending on the organ. Target cells could be killed by T lymphocytes. Alternatively, cytokines may alter the normal growth and differentiation of target cells leading to abnormal growth or deficits in cell populations37.

Conclusion

SARS-CoV-2 S protein immunization induces autoimmune reaction attacking ACE2- expressing cells, as a result of which type II pneumocytes undergo hyperplasia and dedifferentiation, epithelial cells of the proximal tubules of the kidneys are destroyed.
