Relationship of body mass index and kidney outcomes in patients with primary glomerulonephritis: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Qinling Liu, Tianhua Yao, Yan Chen, Fei Xiao, Yazhou Wu, Huanzi Dai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Primary glomerulonephritis, is a major contributor to the development of chronic kidney disease and end-stage renal disease. Previous studies on the relationship between overweight/obesity with primary glomerulonephritis in the chronic kidney disease was inconsistent. This study aimed to systematically review and meta-analyze the relationship between body mass index and adverse kidney outcomes in patients with primary glomerulonephritis.Methods:PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure were searched. Patients were divided into an overweight/obesity group (group O) and a non-overweight/obesity group (group N) according to their body mass index values.Results:Twenty-six studies with 11,038 patients were selected. Patients in group O had deteriorated baseline characteristics, including worse baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (weighted mean differences [WMD] = ‐5.99), higher Scr (WMD = 7.34), uric acid (WMD = 47.53), 24-hour proteinuria (WMD = 0.40), and a higher prevalence rate of hypertension (odds ratio, OR = 2.70) and diabetes mellitus (OR = 2.644). On the histopathologic level, patients in group O had a significantly lower total glomerular number (WMD = ‐2.30); however, no difference was observed in mesangial cell proliferation, endocapillary hypercellularity, crescents, sclerosis, and tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis between the 2 groups. The incidence of adverse renal outcomes in the 2 groups was similar (OR = 0.96). The meta-analysis of hazard ratios from Cox regressions indicated a strong association between overweight/obesity and poor renal outcomes in glomerulonephritis (HR = 1.17).Conclusion:Overweight/obesity glomerulonephritis patients had remarkably poor baseline clinicopathological characteristics. Being overweight or obesity would likely impact the progression of primary glomerulonephritis but not serious enough to contribute to significant poor renal outcomes, which could be prevented by aggressive therapy.

1. Introduction

Primary glomerulonephritis, which mainly comprises IgA nephropathy (IgAN), membranous nephropathy (MN), minimal change disease (MCD), and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), is the leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD).[1] Therefore, it is important to manage renal endpoint risk factors in patients with primary glomerulonephritis. Clinically severe proteinuria, a reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at the time of diagnosis, combined with hypertension, smoking and obesity are the main risk factors for progression of primary glomerulonephritis.[2]

The terms “overweight” and “obesity” refer to an abnormal or excessive buildup of fat, and the body mass index (BMI) is the standard method for classifying these conditions. The global prevalence of overweight and obesity was 39% in 2015,[3] and it is projected to reach 57.8% by 2030.[4] Obesity-related glomerulopathy (ORG) was first characterized by Weisinger and colleagues in 1974 as CKD in obese individuals with considerable proteinuria but no nephrotic syndrome, with glomerulomegaly as the predominant pathologic feature,[5] implying that obesity is thought to induce and accelerate CKD. Previous studies have reported that obesity promotes cardiovascular disease (CVD) morbidity both directly and indirectly through co-existing CVD risk factors such as hypertension[6] and affects inflammatory cytokines, adipokines, enzymes, growth factors, and adipocyte/cancer cell interaction, which accelerates cancer development.[7]

A retrospective cohort study conducted in the UK[8] stated that individuals with primary glomerulonephritis who were normal weight, overweight, or obese had equivalent baseline eGFR and urine protein to creatinine ratio. On the contrary, Yonekura, Y. et al reported that obesity had a substantial influence on the amount of urine protein excretion in primary glomerulonephritis.[9] Concerning the relationship between overweight/obesity and the progression of disease, Nagaraju SP et al reported that BMI had no effect on the proteinuria and eGFR in the IgAN patients at the end of follow-up.[10] Interestingly, Zhang and colleagues showed that a poor prognosis in ORG + IgAN patients was strongly associated with BMI.[11] A few studies demonstrated that individuals who are obese and have chronic conditions may benefit from their obesity. In patients receiving long-term hemodialysis, Kalantar-Zadeh K and colleagues reported that increased body size and muscle mass were related with a higher survival rate.[12] Similarly, having a high BMI was closely linked to a lower risk of death from any cause after percutaneous coronary intervention.[13]

Taking all of this into account shows that there was no evident relationship between being overweight/obesity and glomerulonephritis. As a result, we systematically review and meta-analyze the relationship of BMI with kidney outcomes in patients with glomerulonephritis.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Literature search

Three reviewers independently used the databases PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Clinical Trails, and the China National Knowledge Infrastructure to identify relevant studies published before October 2022. The MeSH terms and free text words of the following terms were applied in our search: “primary glomerulonephritis,” “IgA nephropathy (IgAN),” “membranous nephropathy (MN),” “minimal change disease (MCD),” “focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS),” “membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN),” “rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (RPGN),” “body mass index,” “overweight” and “obesity.” Furthermore, the language of enrolled studies was restricted to English and Chinese.

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) study design: cohort or case–control, randomized or non-randomized, prospective or retrospective studies; (2) study population: adult patients with kidney biopsy-proved primary glomerulonephritis, including IgAN, MN, MCD, membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis, FSGS, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, and endocapillary proliferative glomerulonephritis, which were grouped according to the BMI value; (3) study details: studies analyzed the correlation of overweight/obesity or BMI, with clinical and/or pathology characteristics and/or kidney outcomes. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) reviews, letters, mechanism studies, nonhuman studies, or nonclinical studies; (2) studies that did not report the relationship between BMI and primary glomerulonephritis; (3) duplicate or overlapping study.

2.3. Data extraction and quality assessment

The titles and abstracts of all candidates were scanned by 3 reviewers independently, and the full-text was investigated to see if the study met the inclusion criteria. If disagreement occurred, a meeting was organized and the investigators (Huanzi Dai and Yazhou Wu) were invited to provide arbitration. For each enrolled study, the following data were extracted by 3 reviewers independently: first author, year of publication, country, type of study design, number of patients, clinical and pathology characteristics of patients, therapy strategy, follow-up period, and outcomes. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) by Cox regression analysis were also extracted.

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale was used to assess the quality of enrolled cohorts or case–control studies.[14] This assessment contained 3 parts, including selection, comparability, and exposure or outcome. The highest score was 9, and the study would be excluded if the score was <6. The result of the quality assessment is shown in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N656.

2.4. Definition of overweight/obesity and outcome

BMI was commonly used to classify overweight and obesity. For adults, the World Health Organization defines overweight and obesity as BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 and ≥30 kg/m2 respectively. To define overweight/obesity, there were varied cutoff values in different studies, so that patients were divided into an overweight/obesity group (group O) and non-overweight/obesity group (group N) according to BMI values in our study. Likewise, the outcomes among the enrolled studies were diverse, so a composite outcome event was adopted, which was defined as ESRD or CKD5, eGFR decreased ≥30%, doubling of serum creatinine (Scr), renal replacement therapy, or mortality.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Data from the enrolled studies was pooled using STATA software version 15.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). Weighted mean differences (WMDs) with 95%CIs were used to analyze continuous variables, while odds ratios (ORs) were used to analyze categorical data, and HRs with 95%CIs were used to analyze survival data. If continuous data were presented as median and interquartile range or extremum, mean ± standard deviation was obtained by the methods established by Luo et al and Wan et al.[15,16] Statistics Toolkit was used to combine the means and standard deviations of multi-groups into 1 group. Statistical heterogeneity among studies was assessed by the I2 index. The I2 values of 0% to 25%, 25% to 50%, and >50% were used to quantify heterogeneity as low, moderate, and high.[17] A random-effects model would be taken if I2 > 50%, which demonstrated significant heterogeneity; otherwise, a fixed-effect model would be employed. Subgroup analysis was applied to explore heterogeneity. Publication bias was assessed by a funnel plot, Egger test and Harbord test. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by leave-one-out to assess the influence of each study on the pooled results. And the result of publication bias and sensitivity analyses were shown in Figure S5, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N656. A two-sided P < .05 was regarded as statistically significant. Subgroup analysis was conducted according to country (Asia, non-Asia), follow-up duration (≥5years, <5years).

3. Results

3.1. Search results and study characteristics

A total of 1557 articles were identified and the process of study selection is shown in the flow diagram (Fig. 1). Of these 335 publications were from PubMed, 862 from Embase, 217 from the Cochrane Library, and 143 were identified from China National Knowledge Infrastructure. After removing117 duplicates, 124 review, 164 case reports, and 1093 irrelevant records, 59 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Two studies were removed because of poor quality, 4 studies were excluded for overlapping, and 27 studies were discarded according to inclusion criteria. Finally, 26 studies with 11,038 participants were enrolled in the meta-analysis.[8–11,18–39]

The cutoff values of BMI applied to identify overweight/obesity were 23 kg/m2, 24 kg/m2, 25 kg/m2, 27 kg/m2, 28 kg/m2, and 30 kg/m2 in different articles. The mean age of patients at baseline ranged from 27 to 57 years old and ten articles reported the follow-up endpoints.

3.2. Clinic characteristics at renal biopsy

First of all, the meta-analysis of renal function at the baseline was performed (Fig. 2). The result demonstrated that baseline eGFR (WMD = ‐5.99, 95%CI: ‐7.60 to ‐4.39, P = .000; I2 = 19.30%) was significantly lower in group O with a mean eGFR of 72.98 mL/min/1.73 m2 when compared to group N with a mean eGFR of 84.28 mL/min/1.73 m2. Simultaneously, the levels of Scr (WMD = 7.34, 95%CI: 2.53 to 12.16, P = .003; I2 = 61.20%) were dramatically higher in group O with high heterogeneity, and the random-effect model was applied. The P value of Egger test for uric acid was .048, which indicated publication bias because all the enrolled studies were from Asia. The meta-analysis of 21 studies by random effect model showed that patients in group O had greater 24-h proteinuria at baseline when compared to patients in group N (WMD = 0.40, 95%CI: 0.20 to 0.60, P = .000; I2 = 79.50%). Subgroup analysis of Scr and 24-hour proteinuria is shown in Figures S1 and S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N656. Additionally, uric acid, hematuria, the blood pressure, glucose, and fat were analyzed (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N656). The result revealed that patients in group O had a lower incidence rate of hematuria (OR = 0.52, 95%CI: 0.30 to 0.91, P = .022; I2 = 57.50%) and higher uric acid level (WMD = 47.53, 95%CI: 34.85 to 60.21, P = .000; I2 = 75.10%). The meta-analysis of the 15 studies showed that patients in group O had a significantly higher prevalence of hypertension (OR = 2.70, 95%CI: 2.30 to 3.17, P = .000; I2 = 38.60%) by fixed pooling model. Similarly, the pooled WMD of systolic blood pressure (WMD = 6.69, 95%CI: 5.59 to 7.79; P = .000; I2 = 23.80%) and diastolic blood pressure (WMD = 4.08, 95%CI: 3.36 to 4.79; P = .000; I2 = 48.00%) were significantly higher in group O than group N by fixed effects model. As expected, patients in group O had an ascending rate of diabetes mellitus (OR = 2.64, 95%CI: 1.77 to 3.93, P = .000; I2 = 0.00%) with higher level of fasting blood glucose (WMD = 0.28, 95%CI: 0.21 to 0.35, P = .000; I2 = 0.00%). Furthermore, blood levels of total cholesterol (WMD = 9.38, 95%CI: 1.90 to 16.85, P = .000; I2 = 65.20%) and triglyceride (WMD = 61.39, 95%CI: 45.67 to 77.11, P = .000; I2 = 68.10%) went up notably in group O.

3.3. Histopathologic characteristics at renal biopsy

To investigate the pathological features between overweight/obesity and non-overweight/obesity patients with primary glomerulonephritis, meta-analysis of histopathologic characteristics was conducted (Fig. 3 and Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N656). Pooled total glomerular number was significantly lower in overweight/obesity patients (WMD = ‐2.30, 95%CI: ‐3.42 to ‐1.18, P = .000; I2 = 0.00%), which might be caused by glomerulomegaly. As for glomerulosclerosis, the percentage of segmental sclerosis (OR = 1.03, 95%CI: 0.85 to 1.24, P = .79; I2 = 0.0%) were similar in 2 groups by the fixed pooling model. There were also no observably difference in the percentage of mesangial cell proliferation (OR = 0.97, 95%CI: 0.79 to 1.18, P = .74; I2 = 35.80%), endocapillary hypercellularity (OR = 0.79, 95%CI: 0.54 to 1.16, P = .23; I2 = 59.60%), crescents (OR = 0.54, 95%CI: 0.26 to 1.13, P = .10; I2 = 82.00%) and tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis (OR = 1.00, 95%CI: 0.81 to 1.25, P = .97; I2 = 49.70%); between the 2 group. Regarding the vasculopathy, we observed significantly higher rate in overweight/obesity patients when compared to no-overweight/obesity patients (OR = 1.73, 95%CI: 1.35 to 2.22, P = .000; I2 = 21.90%). In line with previous study[40] that global optical score (GOS) (WMD = 1.22, 95%CI: 0.70 to 1.74, P = .000; I2 = 0.00%) in patients of group O was significantly higher than patients in group N.

3.4. Therapeutic management

According to the KDIGO clinical practice guideline for glomerular diseases, therapeutic management contain supportive treatment, such as edema controlling, proteinuria reducing, and hypertension administration, and disease-specific therapy, like glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive. In our study, it involves dietary management, tonsillectomy, renin–angiotensin system inhibitor, glucocorticoid/immunosuppressor, and calcium channel blocker. And therapy strategies were compared in 5 studies (Fig. 4). As to glucocorticoid or immunosuppressive therapy, covering steroid pulse therapy, steroid oral prescription, cyclophosphamide and so on, the treated proportion of patients in 2 group was comparable with high heterogeneity, and the random-effect model was applied (OR = 0.54, 95%CI: 0.26 to 1.11, P = .09; I2 = 84.2%). The result of meta-analysis indicated that there were more patients in the overweight/obesity group treated with angiotensin-converting-enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker (OR = 1.96, 95%CI: 1.51 to 2.54, P = .000; I2 = 0.00%). It might be explained by a significantly higher prevalence of hypertension in group O. Although management with angiotensin-converting-enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker, there were more patients progressed to arterial hypertension in group O at last examination.[39]

3.5. Outcomes at the end of follow-up

There were 10 studies that reported the outcomes at the end of follow-up. Given varied follow-up outcomes, kidney composite outcomes were used, which were defined as ESRD/ESRF or CKD5, eGFR decreased ≥ 30%, doubling of Scr, renal replacement therapy (RRT), or mortality (Fig. 5). The meta-analysis of 10 studies showed that the incidence of outcomes between the 2 groups (OR = 0.96, 95%CI: 0.77 to 1.19, P = .69; I2 = 0.00%). As showed in our study, the baseline characteristics in patients with overweight/obesity were worse than those with non-obesity. Considering the poor baseline characteristics might contribute to adverse outcomes, subgroup analysis based on baseline eGFR and proteinuria were hold (Fig. S3, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N656). It had no statistical difference in the incidence of kidney composite outcomes between 2 group whether the baseline eGFR were comparable (OR = 0.92, 95%CI: 0.65 to 1.30, P = .64; I2 = 0.00%) or significant different (OR = 0.98, 95%CI: 0.74 to 1.30, P = .91; I2 = 39.7%). In the same way, it was similar in 2 baseline proteinuria subgroup (baseline proteinuria comparable subgroup: OR = 0.97, 95%CI: 0.48 to 1.96, P = .93; I2 = 0.00%; baseline proteinuria poor in group O subgroup: OR = 0.98, 95%CI: 0.74 to 1.29, P = .86; I2 = 50.6%). A further subgroup analysis was conducted for kidney composite outcomes by nation and follow-up duration (Fig. S4, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N656). The results showed that there was no difference in Asia (OR = 0.82, 95%CI: 0.61 to 1.12, P = .215; I2 = 0.00%) and non-Asia (OR = 1.11, 95%CI: 0.82 to 1.52, P = .49; I2 = 3.00%) subgroup for composite outcomes in 2 groups. Again, the incidence of composite outcomes in 2 groups was similar in follow-up duration ≥5 years (OR = 1.09, 95%CI: 0.80 to 1.47, P = .59; I2 = 3.00%) and <5 years (OR = 0.84, 95%CI: 0.62 to 1.14, P = .27; I2 = 0.00%). To explore the different renal outcomes, a meta-analysis was performed to analyze the incidence of ESRD (contain ESRF and CKD5) and RRT. The results showed that there was no difference in incidence of ESRD (OR = 0.95, 95%CI: 0.75 to 1.21, P = 0. 68; I2 = 0.00%) and RRT (OR = 0.89, 95%CI: 0.64 to 1.24, P = .50; I2 = 6.80%). Similarly, a meta-analysis of 3 studies showed that there was no difference in the rate of doubling of Scr (OR = 2.18, 95%CI: 0.74 to 6.64, P = .16; I2 = 0.00%) and mortality (OR = 0.98, 95%CI: 0.46 to 2.07, P = .06; I2 = 54.3%) between 2 groups. The eGFR level of patients at the end of follow-up in group O was significantly lower than group N (WMD = ‐12.76, 95%CI: ‐18.49 to ‐7.03, P = .000; I2 = 0.00%; Egger test, P = .76) with mean levels of 58.18 mL/min/1.73 m2 and 72.53 mL/min/1.73 m2, respectively. But the meta-analysis of HRs from Cox regressions indicated that overweight/obesity was greatly associated with poor kidney outcomes in primary glomerulonephritis (HR = 1.17, 95%CI: 1.04 to 1.32, P = .01; I2 = 42.70%).

3.6. Publication bias

The funnel plots for the eGFR, serum creatinine, 24-h proteinuria and adverse kidney outcomes were showed in Figure S5, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N656 with the low risk of publication biases. And sensitivity-analysis indicated the result of meta-analysis were stable.

4. Discussion and conclusion

With the rising number of overweight/obesity, it is not clear how kidney outcomes in primary glomerulonephritis relates to BMI. Therefore, this study aims to systematically review and meta-analyze the relationship between BMI and kidney outcomes in primary glomerulonephritis. In our meta-analysis, we discovered that overweight/obesity in primary glomerulonephritis has been associated with exceptionally poor baseline clinicopathological features, but there was no difference in kidney composite outcomes at the end of follow-up. Nevertheless, the meta-analysis of HRs from Cox regressions revealed a association between overweight/obesity and poor renal outcomes in primary glomerulonephritis.

We found that the baseline clinical parameters of overweight/obesity patients with primary glomerulonephritis were remarkably poor, characterized by a lower eGFR, elevated Scr, uric acid, and lipid levels, more severe proteinuria, and higher incidence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus, which is consistent with a previously published study.[40,41] Multiple metabolic abnormalities were interwoven with CKD. This may explain why renin–angiotensin system inhibitor antihypertension therapy is prescribed to a greater number of overweight/obesity patients. Excessive deposition of perirenal fat, renal sinus fat and visceral adipose tissue around the kidneys, activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, and stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system increased renal sodium reabsorption, which led to renal injury by glomerular hyperfiltration.[42] Increased renal sodium reabsorption, which elicited elevated blood pressure, in combination with compensatory renal vasodilation, played an essential role in kidney injury. Hypertension and hyperfiltration promoted distension of the basement membrane, glomerulomegaly, podocyte hypertrophy, and apoptosis, leading to FSGS and ORG that aggravated renal injury.[43] Just as Berthoux et al reported, overweight and obesity were associated with more dialysis and death events, not independently but by translating into more risk factors like hypertension and more severe proteinuria.[33] In the meantime, Bonnet F et al also indicated that overweight was an independent risk factor for the development of hypertension.[39] Furthermore, metabolic abnormalities in obese patients, such as dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia, and insulin resistance, might promote the progression of CKD via a variety of pathways. Insulin resistance can also damage the kidneys through glomerular hyperfiltration, hypertension, and excessive sodium reabsorption.[44] On the other hand, obesity was thought to be a cause of chronic inflammation via releasing adipokines by adipose cells that led to the renal structural and functional injury. Furthermore, a decrease in eGFR could contribute to a high serum uric acid level, which could account for chronic inflammation, cells injury, and glomerular hemodynamic changes, eventually resulting in renal function deterioration.[45]

But the mechanisms how obesity promoted the progression of primary glomerulonephritis still comprehended incompletely. ORG was featured by glomerulomegaly with or without FSGS lesions. The abnormal accumulation of lipid in mesangial cells, proximal tubule cells and podocytes induced both structural and functional damages of the kidneys. Simultaneously, visceral adiposity contributed endothelial cell dysfunction, vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, perivascular adipose tissue stiffness, extracellular matrix expansion, and ultimately tubulointerstitial fibrosis.[46] And a study from Japan verified that the glomerular was enlarged and the thickness of glomerular basement membrane was increased significantly for obese IgAN patients, which aggravated the proteinuria.[34] The meta-analysis was also made at the histopathologic level. Our study showed that total glomerular number was reduced significantly which could be explained by glomerulomegaly. It was considered that maximal glomerular diameter could be a reliable indicator for the progression of IgAN.[47] Interestingly, our study showed no difference in mesangial cell proliferation, endocapillary hypercellularity, crescents, segmental sclerosis, global sclerosis, and tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis between 2 groups. On the one hand, meta-analysis could be conducted in a few studies because that pathological reports in enrolled studies were multifarious. On the other hand, the renal biopsies were evaluated by different renal pathologists which was a degree of subjective. To explore the severity of the kidney lesions, the GOS was also analyzed which was consisted of sum of glomerular (0–6), tubular (0–4), interstitial (0–5), and vascular (0–5) indices. Our study manifested that GOS was significantly higher in overweight/obesity patients. In general, the kidney lesions in overweight/obesity patients were more serious.

To figure out what role obesity played on the progression of primary glomerulonephritis, we defined the composite kidney outcomes as ESRD or CKD5, eGFR decreased ≥ 30%, doubling of Scr, RRT or mortality. In contrast to the previous study,[40] our result indicated that the adverse renal outcomes between overweight/obesity and no-overweight/obesity patients were similar, and the meta-analysis of HRs from Cox regressions indicated that overweight/obesity was related to the poor prognosis of primary glomerulonephritis significantly. We dissected the inconsistency for the following reasons. First, the data that Wang Q et al extracted was incomplete for that only parameters from normal weight and overweight patients with IgAN. Second, the outcomes in the previous study included CRF (Scr > 1.5 mg/dL and 1.2 mg/dL in man and women, respectively, with a creatinine clearance below 80 mL/min/1.73 m2) and CKD-3+, which just were advanced stage of nephropathy but not kidney outcomes.[40] And the follow-up period was not long enough in some studies to investigate the composite kidney outcomes. Most included researches in our study are retrospective generating baseline characteristics imbalance with higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus, worse renal function. Wu et al conducted a propensity-score matching analysis to eliminate interference of confounding factors with comparable baseline characteristics, including follow-up time, eGFR, proteinuria and treatment. And their research illustrated that BMI did not yet play as the independent risk factor for the disease progression but probably relate with worse renal function.[31] Overweight/obesity might accelerate the progression of glomerulonephritis via inducing metabolic syndrome. Shimamoto et al reported that the time to achieved urinary protein remission was prolonged for patients of IgAN with high BMI, but the differences was disappeared when aggressive therapy was given.[32] And aggressive therapy in this study included steroid oral, steroid pulse, tonsillectomy, or a combination of them. Zhang et al also indicated that the rate of complete remission or partial remission for ORG + IgAN patients was higher in those with glucocorticoid/immunosuppressor therapy.[11] But the risk of infection was increased and there was no long-term benefit. In other words, overweight/obesity was a risk factor of the adverse kidney outcomes for glomerulonephritis patients which might be improved by aggressive therapy.

The concept of metabolically healthy obesity (MHO) has been proposed in recent years.[48] And it was thought that MHO had a lower risk to develop to CVD, compared to metabolically unhealthy obesity but higher than metabolically healthy lean people. And MHO was considered a transient phenotype that almost half of them were unstable and would advance to metabolic syndrome.[49] As expected, the risk of CVD for unstable MHO was higher than stable MHO. So that it was necessary for obese people to lose weight, even for stable MHO. This might explain why clinicopathological characteristics were comparable for IgAN patients between obese and nonobese group.[10] And weight reduction by low-calorie diet for 6 months could lighten proteinuria for overweight IgAN patients.[50] In summary, losing weight should be recommended to obesity glomerulonephritis patients to decrease the risk to adverse kidney outcomes.

BMI was most widely used for its convenience, however, it was not greatest to apply BMI value to assess body fat for that body composition, like fluid status, gender, and age were overlooked. The potential impact of BMI on renal disease might be overestimated. And waist circumference, percentage body fat and hip to waist ratio were proposed to be more proper assessment tools. In our study, cutoff point BMI for obesity was not defined because of varied cutoff value in different studies, instead participants were divided into overweight/obesity group (group O) and no-overweight/obesity group (group N) according to the BMI value. Simultaneously, overweight/obesity patients might have other unhealthy lifestyle, like smoking, high fat diet and lack of exercise, which had an effect on the progression of renal disease. BMI might have been overestimated in those patients with nephrotic syndrome or fluid overload. Just as Elyan BMP et al showed that weight loss was greater in nephrotic than non-nephrotic glomerulonephritis patients after 6 months of follow-up.[8]

There were several limitations in our meta-analysis. First, all most included studies are retrospective leading to imbalance of baseline characteristics. And further high-quality prospective studies are still needed. Second, the most included articles were about IgAN, and only a few were about other pathological types of primary glomerular nephropathy, such as FSGS, MN, MCD. So that subgroup analysis by pathological types was restricted. Third, there was probably a risk of bias because most of the enrolled research was conducted in Asia. Fourth, the pathological reports in enrolled studies were multifarious, so meta-analysis could be conducted in a few studies. Fifth, the BMI cutoff point for obesity was not defined because of the varied cutoff value in different studies, instead, participants were divided into an overweight/obesity group and non-overweight/obesity group according to the BMI value. Last, the changes in BMI value at baseline and at the end of follow-up were not analyzed, which might also be related to the progression of renal function deterioration.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that overweight/obesity primary glomerulonephritis patients had remarkably poor baseline clinicopathological characteristics. Being overweight or obese would likely impact the progression of primary glomerulonephritis but not serious enough to contribute to significant poor renal outcomes. And the aggressive therapy, like hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia and high fasting glucose controlling, could prevent disease progression. And further high-quality prospective clinical trials should be led to investigate the relationship of obesity and adverse renal outcomes.
