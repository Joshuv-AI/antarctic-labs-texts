Use of reverse dot blot hybridization DNA-array and targeted next-generation sequencing for the detection of drug resistance in patients with tuberculosis: a prospective diagnostic accuracy study
Authors: Andrii Dudnyk, Nadiia Tytarenko, Iris Romero Andrada, Oksana Lytvyniuk, Olena Tolstova, Joan-Pau Millet, Xavier Casas, Adrian Antuori, Nelly Ciobanu, Valeriu Crudu, José Domínguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposePhenotypic drug susceptibility testing (pDST) for Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTC) is slow, and routine molecular assays detect only a subset of resistance-associated mutations. We aimed to assess the diagnostic accuracy of the TB Resistance DNA-Array (GenID GmbH, Germany) for detecting isoniazid (INH) and rifampicin (RIF) resistance, and to evaluate the added value of Deeplex® Myc-TB, a culture free, targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) from GenoScreen, France, for identifying drug resistance and resolving discrepancies with pDST.MethodsClinical samples from 234 individuals with bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) were analyzed: 98 (41.9%) from Ukraine, 25 (10.7%) from Moldova, and 111 (47.4%) from Spain. TB Resistance DNA-Array and tNGS results were compared with pDST and cross-evaluated to identify resistance-conferring mutations.ResultsAmong 196 sputum specimens tested with the TB Resistance DNA-Array, 98 (50.0%) produced conclusive RIF results and 102 (52.0%) conclusive INH results. Among conclusive outputs, the assay showed high sensitivity and specificity for RIF (94.3% and 98.4%) and INH (95.7% and 94.6%). Deeplex® Myc-TB generated conclusive results for 24/30 (80.0%) sputum samples and 21/23 (91.3%) culture isolates from 53 individuals. tNGS demonstrated high sensitivity for INH and RIF (both > 97.0%) but misclassified 2 of 8 INH-susceptible cases and 2 of 9 RIF-susceptible cases as resistant, suggesting a potential risk of overtreatment with second-line drugs. For linezolid, tNGS showed perfect specificity (100%); however, 1 of 3 cases was falsely categorised as resistant. For fluoroquinolones, sensitivity and specificity were 90.0% and 83.3%, respectively.ConclusionThe TB Resistance DNA-Array performed reliably for detecting RIF and INH resistance in sputum samples with conclusive results. Integration of tNGS can improve interpretative algorithms tailored to clinically relevant and region-specific resistance mutations.

Background

The pathway to detecting drug resistance in patients with tuberculosis (TB) requires systemic changes that bring molecular technologies closer to people seeking care and provide accurate information to support personalized treatment options [1]. In line with updated World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations, the rapid identification of drug resistance in TB patients is structured into initial diagnostic tests and follow-on tools, with additional consideration given to the complexity of each technology [2]. Over the past decade, moderate complexity nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) for detection of resistance to rifampicin (RIF) and isoniazid (INH) have demonstrated multiple advantages over platforms identifying only RIF resistance or phenotypic drug susceptibility testing (pDST) [3, 4]. A systematic evaluation of 147 studies on line probe assays (LPA) for the diagnosis of drug-resistant tuberculosis confirmed their high diagnostic performance. Overall sensitivity for RIF-resistant TB (RR-TB) was 0.96 (95% CI 0.95–0.97) and specificity 0.99 (95% CI 0.98–0.99), while for INH-resistant TB (Hr-TB) sensitivity and specificity were 0.91 (95% CI 0.89–0.93) and 0.99 (95% CI 0.98–0.99), respectively [5].

The reverse dot blot hybridization DNA-array (DNA-array) is highly similar to LPA technology but is based on a 96-well microplate format, offering a flexible approach within a single reaction run and aligning with routine laboratory workflows [6]. Moreover, this microtiter well format allows incorporation of a greater number of sequence-specific oligonucleotide probes printed in small, discrete spots [7]. These probes can be duplicated or triplicated to increase the probability of detecting target DNA regions and to improve accuracy during test result interpretation, unlike LPA, which are constrained by the physical length of the paper strip and typically rely on a single probe per target [8]. A previous multicentre evaluation of a line blot assay using 315 culture isolates from high multidrug-resistant TB burden countries reported a sensitivity of 85.2% for RIF-resistance detection and only 16.9% for INH [9]. In contrast, a study from Cameroon testing 59 sputum samples using a dot blot assay reported 70.6% sensitivity and 95.2% specificity for RR-TB detection [10]. Subsequent simplifying of the microarray system, evaluated in 153 culture isolates, demonstrated improved sensitivity for first-line drugs—100% for RIF and 90.0% for INH—while specificity was 95.0% and 98.2%, respectively [11]. Although a cartridge-based microarray test was prototyped nearly 10 years ago, with the possibility of adapting spotted probes to future diagnostic requirements [12], there are currently no authomated molecular assays available that can identify resistance to new and repurposed anti-TB drugs within a single test format.

In 2024, the WHO recommended application of the targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) at the diagnostic algorithm using respiratory samples from patients with pulmonary TB [2]. Expanding access to tNGS under routine programmatic conditions offers a culture-free alternative to conventional methods, combining genotyping of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTC) with amplification of selected loci in genes associated with resistance to most anti-TB medicines [13]. A recent multicentre clinical evaluation of 720 samples reported significantly lower sensitivity of LPA (the GenoType MTBDRplus, Hain Lifescience, Nehren, Germany) for both RIF and INH in direct comparison with tNGS; however, the test failure rate was approximately half that of tNGS Deeplex® Myc‑TB (GenoScreen, Lille, France) [14]. Due to the high costs of sequencing, there are limited data from resource-restricted, high drug-resistant-TB-burden countries on the implementation of tNGS in clinical practice.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of the TB Resistance DNA-Array (GenID GmbH, Strassberg, Germany) for detecting resistance to INH and RIF, and to assess the added value of tNGS using Deeplex® Myc-TB for identifying resistance to anti-tuberculosis drugs and for resolving discordant results between molecular and culture-based drug DST.

Methods

Study design and settings

This was a prospective cohort study designed to determine the diagnostic accuracy of the TB Resistance DNA-Array and the operational feasibility of tNGS Deeplex® Myc-TB in sputum samples and culture isolates from bacteriologically confirmed patients with TB. We consecutively enrolled patients at the Regional Phthisiopulmonology Center (Vinnytsia, Ukraine), Regional Center of Socially Significant Diseases (Odesa, Ukraine) and PMSI Institute of Pneumology “Chiril Draganiuc” (Chișinău, Moldova) from May 2019 to June 2020. As most of these samples came from patients with drug-resistant TB, we also selected sputum samples from TB patients hospitalised at the Serveis Clinics (Barcelona, Spain). Sputum samples and culture isolates were tested by TB Resistance DNA-Array and Deeplex® Myc-TB at the Germans Trias i Pujol Research Institute, Badalona, Barcelona, Spain.

Study populations

Only patients with bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB who provided informed consent were included into the study. From September 2021 to September 2024 we consecutively tested sputum samples and culture isolates from 234 patients with bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB from Ukraine (n = 98, 41.9%), Moldova (n = 25, 10.7%) and Spain (n = 111, 47.4%). Drug resistant cases were classified according to the updated WHO definitions [15, 16].

Ethical approval

The study was approved by independent ethics committees at each clinical site where patients were recruited (National Pirogov Memorial Medical University, protocol №5 dated 03.07.2018; Regional Center of Socially Significant Diseases – N1232 dated 11.07.2018; Germans Trias i Pujol Hospital – PI19/01408; PMSI Institute of Pneumology “Chiril Draganiuc” – Protocol NºI4TB dated 24.06.2019). All patients provided written informed consent before submitting clinical samples.

Study procedures

All samples were collected during the first two weeks after TB treatment initiation. Only a single sample per patient was tested. Early morning spontaneusly expectorated sputum specimen minimum 1 mL in volume was obtained from each patient. Before being tested, all of the samples were decontaminated by the Kubica N-acetyl-L-cysteine NaOH method. After decontamination, the concentrated pellet was resuspended in sterile phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). Smear microscopy and XpertMTB/RIF tests were performed for routine patient-care purposes using standard procedures of sample processing. All samples were cultured in liquid medium using BACTEC MGIT (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, USA). pDST in liquid medium was performed using the proportion method, evaluating the percentage of colonies growing at the critical drug concentration. An exception was 17 samples from Vinnytsia, Ukraine, in which solid Löwenstein–Jensen medium was used to assess resistance to KMN, CAP, AMK, LFX, MFX, and ETH, due to the absence of consumables at the local laboratory for testing these drugs in MGIT at that time. Culture-based DST (either on solid or liquid medium) was done according with international guidelines and national laboratory standards [17, 18].

DNA isolation

Mycobacterial DNA extraction for the TB Resistance DNA-Array was performed from a 500 µL aliquot of each specimen using a commercial DNA extraction kit and Bacteria Lysis Buffer Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with heat lysis at 95 °C for 20 min using a heat block. Subsequently, 5 µL of supernatant was used to perform the test directly following lysis. For Deeplex® Myc-TB, DNA extraction, purification and quantification was performed as described in the user manual [19]. The extracted DNA was stored at -20 °C.

TB Resistance DNA-Array

The TB Resistance DNA-Array is a commercially available test intended for the identification of INH and RIF resistance for research use only. Detection of resistance to other anti-TB drugs is possible through the addition of specific probes and is currently under development. The technology is based on reverse dot blot hybridization using a 96-well microtiter plate format and was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions by a qualified laboratory technician. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene was used by manufacturer as an internal amplification control and DNA-quantity and -quality controls. PCR grade water was consider as a negative control while M. tuberculosis strain H37Rv was added as a positive control.

Ten µL of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplicon was used to detect mutations or wild-type sequences in the genome of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTC) associated with resistance to INH and RIF. An equal volume of denaturing reagent was added to the amplicon and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Following amplification, the test involves four steps: hybridization, washing with Stringent solution, conjugation, and evaluation of result with ELISpot reader and AIDot Software (Strassberg, GenID). A total of 200 µL of hybridization buffer was added to each well and incubated for 30 min at 47 °C in a shaking water bath, followed by the addition of 200 µL of stringent wash solution to each well, with incubation for 15 min at 47 °C under the same conditions. We added 200 µL of conjugate solution and 200 µL of substrate to all wells. Prior to evaluation, the reaction was stopped by washing with distilled water.

TB Resistance DNA-Array results were compared with reference tests - pDST and Xpert MTB/RIF molecular assay. Each array well contains 22 triplicated probes including conjugation, amplification, Mycobacterium universal, MTC controls, and INH- as well as RIF-specific mutation and wild probes (Fig. 1).

Interpretation of TB Resistance DNA-Array results

Resistance to INH is based on detection of triplet in katG gene reaction zone (codon S315T) and inhA regulatory region (promoter region − 15, -16) while detection of triplets in rpoB gene reaction zone (codons 516, 526, 531) indicates RIF resistance. Categorization of the test results was performed either as a sensitive if only wild type triplets were present and no mutations detected or resistant if any triplets is developed in the mutations reaction zone. In case of absence of both triplets in wild type and mutation reaction zones, the result was classified as indeterminate. The test was considered invalid if any of control triplets (Conjugation, GAPDH amplification, Mycobacterium universal,) was not developed.

Deeplex® Myc-TB

Starting from a processed clinical sample, tNGS includes following steps: DNA extraction, target amplification, library preparation, sequencing, data analysis [20], and interpretation based on standard catalogue of mutations [21]. The assay relies on tNGS technology of a single 24-plex amplicon mix targeting mycobacterial species identification, MTC strain genotyping and 18 MTC drug resistance-associated genes for RIF, INH, pyrazinamide (PZA), ethambutol (EMB), fluoroquinolones (FQN), amikacin (AMK), streptomycin (STM), ethionamide (ETH), bedaquiline (BDQ), clofazimine (CFZ), linezolid (LZD), and previously recommended anti-TB drugs kanamycin (KMN) and capreomycin (CAP).

Nine µL of DNA suspension from culture isolates and sputum samples were used for Deeplex® Myc-TB PCR. Following amplification, the test consists of amplicons clean-up, DNA quantification by Qubit® dsDNA HS assay, library preparation for sequencing (Illumina DNA Prep) and sequencing by MiSeq (Illumina, USA). Mycobacterium bovis BCG and sterile water were used as positive and negative controls, respectively, for each run of the tNGS test. Results analysis and interpretation was performed using Deeplex® Myc-TB web app.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with R Statistical Software and IBM SPSS Statistics. The threshold for statistical significance was set to p < 0.05. Intended sample size estimation was adjusted for RIF and INH resistance rate in the participating countries with expected test sensitivity and specificity of 0.90 and calculated with R Statistical Software, n = 174 patients with bacteriologically confirmed TB diagnosis. Due to the different rates of resistance to anti-TB drugs in the participating countries impacting accuracy calculations, we used the term observed accuracy. Concordance between the evaluated test and the reference standard was calculated by the Cohen’s Kappa coefficient. Kappa < 0: no agreement; Kappa between 0.00 and 0.20: slight agreement; Kappa between 0.21 and 0.40: fair agreemet; Kappa between 0.41 and 0.60: moderate agreement; Kappa between 0.61 and 0.80: substantial agreement; Kappa between 0.81 and 1.00: almost perfect agreement.

Clinical implications of Deeplex® Myc-TB for the prediction of anti-TB drug resistance

A total of 24 of 30 (80.0%) sputum specimens and 21 of 23 (91.3%) culture isolates from 53 patients with bacteriologically confirmed pulmonary TB tested by Deeplex® Myc-TB yielded conclusive results with definitive interpretations of resistance and genotyping. Deeplex® Myc-TB classified most identified MTC species as single-nucleotide-polymorphism (SNP)-based phylogenetic Lineage 2 (Beijing clade), representing 33 of 45 isolates (73.3%). Within this group, spoligotype international types (SIT) included SIT-265 (9 of 33, 27.3%) and SIT-1 (23 of 33, 69.7%), and one isolate was assigned to the L2.2.1 sublineage with an undescribed SIT. Lineage 4 accounted for 10 of 45 isolates (22.2%), including sublineages LAM9-SIT1176/SIT803 (3 of 10, 30.0%), H1-SIT47, T4-CE41-SIT39, and T1-SIT172/SIT53, as well as sublineage 4.3 (3 of 10, 30.0%) comprising LAM9-SIT161 and T1-SIT251. One isolate within this group had an undescribed SIT. Additionally, two isolates (2 of 45, 4.4%) were classified as lineages other than H37Rv, including T1-SIT52 and SIT154.

In one sputum sample and one culture isolate, tNGS of the gene encoding the 65-kDa heat shock protein (hsp65) revealed a simultaneous combination of two strain variants, suggesting a potential mixed infection. SNP-based phylogenetic lineage combinations were also identified in three additional culture isolates and one sputum sample.

The calculated sensitivity, specificity, and agreement values between tNGS and pDST are shown in Table 3. Deeplex® Myc-TB demonstrated high sensitivity for the first-line drugs INH (97.2%) and RIF (97.1%), indicating strong potential for use in the rapid detection of MDR-TB. However, calculating specificity for these drugs was challenging due to the low number of susceptible samples with valid results: overall, 2 of 8 susceptible patients were misclassified as resistant for INH, and 2 of 9 for RIF.

Among 36 TB patients with INH resistance detected by pDST, tNGS identified mutations in the katG gene in 34 out of 36 cases (94.4%). Specifically, mutations were found in codon S315T in 31/35 cases (86.1%), and in other codons including A109T (n = 1), Y113C, G121D, G268D (n = 1), and S315N (n = 1). Mutations in the fabG1-inhA operon were detected in 10/36 cases (27.8%): fabG1 promoter mutations c-15t (n = 7), t-8c (n = 1) and inhA coding mutations P99L (n = 1), Q66, H121R, and A154T (n = 1). Additionally, 2/36 (5.6%) cases harbored mutations in the ahpC gene: G21D (n = 1), and a combination of V60M and D113N (n = 1).

Regarding RIF resistance, tNGS identified mutations located within the RIF resistance-determining region of the rpoB gene in 21 out of 35 cases (60.0%), with the most frequent being the canonical S450L mutation. Less frequent mutations included S450W (n = 1), S450F (n = 1), as well as S171N, S195C, G492S (n = 1), L452P (n = 1), and H445D (n = 2).

FQN resistance was associated with mutations in the gyrA gene, most commonly at codons D94G (4/10), A90V (3/10), S91P (2/10), D94N (1/10), and D94Y (1/10). Additionally, one mutation was identified in the gyrB gene at codon D461A. Among three patients with confirmed resistance to LZD by culture-based DST, a mutation in the rrl gene at position G2814T was identified in only one case. No mutations were identified in TB patients with available pDST results for BDQ. However, in two cases without available pDST results, amino acid substitutions of uncertain significance in the Rv0678 gene—E113K and M10I—were detected, with the latter occurring in combination with an uncharacterized substitution at D116N.

Deeplex® Myc-TB correctly detected 7 of 8 TB cases susceptible to AMK and 2 of 5 susceptible cases to STM. It also identified all 7 cases with documented phenotypic resistance to STM and all 3 of 3 resistant cases to CAP, while no resistant cases to AMK were tested. For ETH, tNGS detected resistance in 4 of 7 resistant cases and in none of the 3 susceptible cases.

Comparison of results between tNGS and DNA-array

Among the 16 TB patients tested using both technologies, 9 had uncertain resistance results with the TB Resistance DNA-Array because MTC was not detected, whereas 2 patients had invalid results using tNGS. Thus, only 7 patients had conclusive results available for both Deeplex® Myc-TB and the TB Resistance DNA-Array, allowing direct comparison (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we report the first clinical evaluation of the TB Resistance DNA-Array applied directly to sputum samples from TB patients in Ukraine, Moldova, and Spain, and offer additional insights into the utility of Deeplex® Myc-TB for drug-resistance determination. Given that a considerable proportion of TB Resistance DNA-Array results yielded uncertain resistance interpretations, the observed high sensitivity and specificity for RIF (94.3% and 98.4%) and INH (95.7% and 94.6%) among interpretable cases should be viewed cautiously and may not fully reflect assay performance in routine practice. Focusing on sputum samples with at least scanty bacterial load markedly increased the likelihood of conclusive results (97.4% of smear-positive vs. 2.6% of smear-negative samples; OR 17.9 (95% CI 4.1–77.6), highlighting the influence of bacterial load on DNA-array performance in routine practice. Our findings revealed a higher proportion of inconclusive results compared with a retrospective study from China, in which over 16% of results from another commercial DNA microarray (CapitalBio Corp., Beijing, China) were excluded from resistance analysis [22]. Nevertheless, in our study, the TB Resistance DNA-Array demonstrated approximately 10% higher sensitivity for RIF and more than 20% higher sensitivity for INH, while maintaining similar specificity for both drugs. Compared with a study from Japan evaluating an in-house DNA microarray, the diagnostic accuracy for RIF resistance was comparable between the two technologies, whereas the TB Resistance DNA-Array exhibited nearly 35% higher specificity for INH resistance detection in sputum samples [23].

In a clinical evaluation of an LPA manufactured by GenID in patients with suspected TB (n = 87) and RR-TB (n = 67) from Namibia [24], 10.4% of results were invalid, which was significantly lower than observed with the DNA-array in our study. However, the TB Resistance DNA-Array outperformed the LPA in most measures of diagnostic accuracy. For INH, sensitivity and specificity were 95.7% (95% CI 85.5–99.5) versus 89.7% (95% CI 72.6–97.8) and 94.6% (95% CI 84.9–98.9) versus 91.7% (95% CI 73.0–99.0), respectively. Sensitivity for RIF was 94.3% (95% CI 80.8–99.3) versus 84.2% (95% CI 68.7–94.0), while specificity was slightly higher for the LPA at 100% (95% CI 82.4–100.0) compared with 98.4% (95% CI 91.5–100.0) for the DNA-array.

It is important to note that even WHO-endorsed diagnostic tools, such as the Truenat assay— which employs a two-step approach to detect RIF resistance—may yield a substantial proportion of invalid results, with rates reaching up to 20%. Specifically, approximately 5% of Truenat MTB tests and about 15% of reflex RIF resistance tests have been reported as indeterminate [25]. As demonstrated in our previous study conducted in Ukraine, the initial use of a multiplex molecular test may offer therapeutic advantages over a RIF-focused approach. Resistance to second-line drugs was detected in 6 of 17 (35.3%) clinical samples from patients with TB who had documented RIF susceptibility, potentially reducing unnecessary exposure to empirical regimens among patients not eligible for such treatment [26].

Several existing assays based on automated NAATs (aNAATs) have been designed for the detection of INH and RIF resistance, providing information similar to that obtained with the TB Resistance DNA-Array. aNAATs are typically cartridge-based or closed systems with standardized processing, reduced hands-on time, and lower infrastructure requirements, making them suitable for decentralized or near-patient settings [27]. In contrast, the TB Resistance DNA microarray is based on a microplate format that enables multiplex probe customisation, offering flexibility for the development of expanded resistance panels. This higher multiplexing capacity is advantageous for detecting resistance beyond INH and RIF through the addition of specific probes, providing a low-cost and robust solution for resource-limited countries—an unmet need that remains insufficiently addressed by conventional molecular options [28].

Discrepancies between molecular and phenotypic resistance assessments are well recognized, reflecting their fundamentally different methodological principles. For example, in our previous study evaluating resistance to second-line drugs and EMB, 55 of 104 culture isolate samples showed discordant results between pyrosequencing and pDST [29]. Although the high overall accuracy of tNGS was reported in a meta-analysis [30], other studies have shown considerably lower specificity for RIF resistance detection, ranging from 56.6% [31] to 75.0% [32] when compared to pDST as the standard of care. In the current study, we identified five MTC strains with false MDR-TB detection. Four of these belonged to the Beijing clade (Lineage 2) and carried high-confidence resistance mutations to INH and RIF located in the katG S315T and rpoB S450L gene loci. The remaining sample showed a mixture of Lineage 4 (98.8%) and Lineage 4.8 (1.2%) and harbored mutations in the fabG1 and rpoB S450L gene loci.Given that all four TB patients responded successfully to first-line treatment, the discrepancy observed between tNGS and pDST results may be attributed to heteroresistance where both susceptible and resistant bacterial subpopulations coexist within the same sample or cross-contamination during sample handling [33].

Although the tNGS showed better performance for FQN, with 90.0% sensitivity and 83.3% specificity, the overall reliable detection of pre-XDR and XDR-TB cases may be limited when relying on this technology alone. A recent study performed at Reference TB Laboratory in Ukraine demonstrated that a lower bacterial load in the sample can directly reduce the likelihood of obtaining a valid result by up to 30% when using tNGS, despite the method generally achieving over 90% sensitivity and specificity for most tested drugs [34]. A mutation in the rrl gene at position G2814T was detected in only one of three patients with phenotypic LZD resistance, and no specific mutations were identified in the single patient with phenotypic BDQ resistance. This highlights a key limitation of Deeplex® Myc-TB: while it targets known genomic regions, it may not detect all resistance markers for LZD and BDQ, particularly those that are not yet fully characterized. Accurate molecular detection of resistance to these drugs therefore remains challenging, underscoring the need to integrate clinical context, pretest probability [35], and determination of minimum inhibitory concentrations to guide informed treatment decisions [36]. Intensified research is required to fully characterise molecular determinants for BDQ and LZD resistance. Moreover, assessing the value of timely genomic sequencing is critical for detecting drug-resistance mutations missed by standard molecular methods, by such improving treatment guidance; particularly in regions with high prevalence of extensive drug resistance pattern.

The European Region accounts for one-fifth of the global burden of MDR-TB cases (21%) and over one-third of pre-XDR-TB cases (37%), according to the WHO 2024 report [37]. Notably, by 2023, individuals of Ukrainian origin accounted for 49.5% of all RR/MDR-TB cases among foreign-born populations in Europe [38]. These figures highlight the importance of our findings, which reflect the performance of rapid diagnostic tests in detecting drug resistance among patients in high- and low TB burden countries. In the context of the ongoing war in Ukraine, culture-free molecular technologies for drug resistance detection offer also operational benefits by reducing reliance on fuel-based power generation required for long-lasting culture-based DST during electricity outages.

Among the 196 sputum samples with valid TB Resistance DNA-Array results, 52 (26.5%) showed mutations conferring resistance to both RIF and INH. Of these, 32 (61.5%) carried a combination of rpoB S531L or S531W and katG S315T mutated codons. Additionally, we identified three cases in which the katG S315T mutation was combined with less common rpoB mutations, specifically H526D, H526Y, or D516V. These findings are consistent with a previous study from India and China, which demonstrated that over half of the mutations in the rpoB and katG genes were located at specific codons, S531L and S315T [39, 40].

Several approaches may be considered when handling uncertain TB Resistance DNA-Array results. We observed inferred rpoB mutations in 22/196 samples (11.2%), most commonly associated (17/22; 77.3%) with absent wild-type bands across all three rpoB regions (codons 513–516, 522–526, and 529–533). Classifying these inferred mutations as RIF-resistance—against the pDST reference standard—would have reduced specificity from 98.4% (95% CI 91.5–100.0) to 78.5% (95% CI 67.8–86.9), owing to a substantial increase in false-positive results, likely attributable to neutral mutations. Conversely, classifying inferred mutations as “resistance not detected” would have reduced sensitivity from 94.3% (95% CI 80.8–99.3) to 80.5% (95% CI 65.1–91.2).

A different pattern was observed for inferred katG and inhA mutations. Overall, 18 inferred results were identified among 196 cases, the majority of which (13/18; 72.2%) showed absence of the wild-type band within the inhA − 16, −15, and − 8 reaction zones. Nearly all of these patients were phenotypically susceptible to INH, with the exception of one individual. Among three additional patients with phenotypically confirmed INH resistance, we observed either isolated absence of reactivity in the katG codon 315 region (n = 1) or concurrent absence of wild-type bands in both katG codon 315 and inhA − 16 to − 8 regions (n = 2). Interpreting such isolated inferred katG or inhA mutations as indicative of INH resistance—relative to culture-based DST—would result in an approximate 20% reduction in sensitivity, from 95.7% (95% CI 85.5–99.5) to 75.4% (95% CI 63.5–85.0). In contrast, classifying these inferred mutations as “resistance not detected” would lead to a more modest decline in sensitivity, from 94.6% (95% CI 84.9–98.9) to 88.2% (95% CI 76.1–95.6) within our cohort.

Deeplex® Myc-TB enabled simultaneous genotypic prediction of drug resistance and phylogenetic characterization. The assay identified the majority (73.3%) of MTC species as belonging to the SNP-based phylogenetic Lineage 2 (Beijing clade), corroborating findings from a previous whole-genome sequencing (WGS) study of MDR-TB patient samples from the same regions of Ukraine [41]. In contrast, WGS analysis of 268 MTC isolates from Moldova demonstrated a lower prevalence of Lineage 2 (43.3%), with Lineage 4 predominating (56.7%) among MDR/RR-TB patients [42]. Similarly, population-based WGS data from the Valencia province in Spain revealed a marked predominance of Lineage 4, accounting for 92.1% of circulating strains in the region [43]. Notably, the detection of more than one distinct MTC strain in several patients (6/53–11.3%) highlights the complexity of drug resistance diagnostics and the possibility of superinfection with a secondary strain during ongoing TB treatment. Among the patients with multiple MTC strains, four out of six were MDR-TB cases, including two retreatment patients and one co-infected with HIV. Similar observations have been reported in Uganda, where standard 24-loci MIRU-VNTR typing identified multiple MTC strains in 5 (6.4%) of 78 patients, all of whom were newly diagnosed, and two-thirds co-infected with HIV [44]. These findings suggest that mixed infections may occur not only during treatment but also at the time of diagnosis, particularly in settings with a high burden of TB and HIV. Our findings of mixed MTC infections are consistent with a recent study that applied Deeplex® Myc-TB to stool specimens from patients treated for TB in Eswatini. In that cohort, 12% (6/51) of patients with detectable MTC showed evidence of mixed infections, which are unlikely to be identified by liquid culture due to the overgrowth of the predominant strain [45].

To our knowledge, this study is the first to provide a direct comparative evaluation of a TB Resistance DNA-array alongside tNGS in a high MDR-TB burden setting. Our comparison of results between tNGS and DNA-array clearly demonstrates that incorporating specific probes targeting the fabG1 - inhA operon - particularly promoter mutations at the c-15t position - and the rpoB gene S531L mutation can reduce the number of uncertain interpretations and enhance the sensitivity of moderate-complexity molecular assays such as reverse hybridization dot blot DNA-arrays. The potential implementation of tNGS on sputum samples as a follow-up test demonstrated a 64.0% absolute risk reduction (ARR) (95%CI 42.1–85.9, p = 0.00016) in incorrect treatment initiation compared to Xpert MTB/RIF-based decision-making for the 9-month BDQ-FQN-ETH-EMB-INHhd-PZA regimen. In contrast, no significant differences were observed for the 6BPaLM regimen (ARR 0.08; 95% CI: − 0.19 to 0.34; p = 0.845) or for the 6BDQ-DLM-LZD-LFX-CFZ and 9BDQ-LZD-MFX-PZA treatment scenario assumptions (both ARR − 0.14; 95% CI: − 0.41 to 0.16; p = 0.57) in our cohort of patients.

Our study presents several strengths. This study presents the first published dataset analyzing the application of the DNA-array directly on sputum samples from 196 TB patients in Ukraine, Moldova, and Spain. This is particularly relevant given the strong potential of this technology to be adapted for high-throughput detection of genetic determinants of resistance to new and repurposed anti-TB drugs. Through parallel analysis using tNGS, we not only evaluated the accuracy of resistance detection but also provided detailed genotypic characterization of circulating strains, together with a comparative assessment of both technologies for detecting resistance to RIF and INH.

However, our study has certain limitations. Due to the substantial number of inconclusive results observed with both technologies, the number of samples available for direct comparison was relatively limited. The samples in our study were properly stored since collected until tested, but it could not be discarded that long storage may have affect the integrity of DNA, and consequently the results of the tests. We were also unable to conduct a more detailed analysis of data from Xpert MTB/RIF, as specific RIF resistance-conferring mutations were not routinely reported and were therefore not documented in medical records. Discrepancies between phenotypic and genotypic DST may arise due to several factors [46]. These include differences in detection limits, which can cause minor resistant subpopulations to be missed by culture-based methods if they fail to grow; the presence of silent mutations, heteroresistance, technical and random errors, etc [47].

In conclusion, despite challenges related to uncertain resistance interpretation, the TB Resistance DNA-Array demonstrated reliable performance in detecting RIF and INH resistance in sputum samples with conclusive results. Further development and optimization of the TB Resistance DNA-Array would benefit from large-scale evaluation on well-characterized strains and smear-positive sputum samples, alongside comparison with tNGS, to further enhance overall assay performance. In our cohort, the Lineage 2 Beijing clade remains the most prevalent MTC strain circulating among patients from Ukraine. Surveillance data collected during the implementation of tNGS could inform the development of more accessible, higher-plex molecular assays and corresponding interpretation algorithms, tailored to the clinical significance and regional prevalence of resistance-associated mutations. Deeplex® Myc-TB may provide a time-to-action advantage over the conventional Xpert MTB/RIF assay, potentially reducing inappropriate treatment in tuberculosis patients eligible for the 9-month BDQ-FQN-ETH-EMB-INHhd-PZA regimen.
