Staging lung cancer: total-body PET/CT or whole-body PET/CT?
Authors: Xiaoxiang Zhang, Jiahao Xie, Yuzhao Zheng, Ying Tian, Xiaohui Chen, Li Chen, Kemin Zhou, Penghui Sun, Wenlan Zhou, Hubing Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTotal Body PET/CT is increasingly used in clinical practice, but its benefits for lung cancer staging are not fully established. Background

Lung cancer is the most common malignant tumor worldwide and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Its prognosis is closely tied to clinical staging, with survival rates declining as the disease progresses (5-year survival rates for stages I to IV are 43%−50%, 25%−36%, 7%−9%, and 2%, respectively) [2, 3]. Accurate staging is crucial for predicting prognosis and guiding formulate appropriate treatment strategies [4]. Whole-body 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging combined with brain MRI has become the standard protocol for lung cancer staging, as recommended by international guidelines such as those from the NCCN [5, 6]. Up to 30–40% of lung cancer patients experience a change in staging following PET/CT imaging, leading to alterations in treatment management in up to 30% of cases [7, 8].

Traditional Whole-Body PET/CT (WB PET/CT) covers the area from the top of the head to the mid-thigh and is the most commonly used imaging modality [9]. It typically involves PET/CT with a short axial field of view (15–30 cm), acquired through multi-bed scanning [10, 11]. Since the introduction of the ultra-long field-of-view (194 cm) PET/CT uEXPLORER in 2018, single-bed imaging now enables full-body coverage (from head to feet), giving rise to Total Body PET/CT (TB PET/CT) [12, 13], Owing to its operational simplicity, single-bed total body scanning has become a routine procedure for long-axis PET/CT scanners on a daily basis. The expanded imaging range allows TB PET/CT to detect distal limb metastases, enhancing comprehensive and accurate systemic detection of malignant tumors [10, 14, 15]. However, the impact of TB PET/CT on lung cancer staging and treatment management remains unclear.

This study retrospectively analyzed TB PET/CT data from 763 lung cancer patients, reconstructed corresponding WB PET/CT images, and compared metastasis detection and staging between the two modalities. It aimed to evaluate whether TB PET/CT offers clinical benefits for lung cancer staging and treatment.

Methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study was conducted on 1,350 patients who underwent 18F -FDG PET/CT imaging for suspected or confirmed lung cancer at our institution from July 2022 to July 2023. After excluding 367 patients with incomplete follow-up data or those treated elsewhere, and 220 patients with prior lung cancer treatment, 763 newly diagnosed lung cancer patients with pathological diagnoses and follow-up information were included (505 males and 258 females). Primary lung cancer was confirmed via biopsy and histopathological examination. Metastasis was diagnosed using imaging modalities such as brain MRI, chest CT, abdominal ultrasound, whole-body bone scan, and PET/CT. For patients with advanced-stage disease, only a select few suspected metastatic lesions underwent biopsy to inform a more precise treatment plan. The majority of metastatic lesions were confirmed through imaging modalities during clinical follow-up over a period of more than six months.

The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature.

Radiotracer synthesis

The positron-emitting radionuclide 18F was produced using a PET trace cyclotron (General Electric, USA). 18F-FDG was synthesized using a TRACELab F-FDG module (General Electric, USA), with radiochemical purity exceeding 95% after purification [16].

PET/CT image acquisition

Patients were instructed to fast for 4–6 h before the scan. Blood glucose levels were measured before radiotracer injection to ensure levels were below 11.1 mmol/L. 18F-FDG (3.7–5.4 MBq/kg) was injected via the hand or elbow vein. After a 1-hour rest, PET/CT imaging was performed using a uEXPLORER scanner (Shanghai United Imaging Healthcare Co., China). The scan covered the total body, including the head, torso, and the four limbs, encompassing the lower limbs (legs) or also the upper limbs (arms). PET acquisition time was 5 min, while CT parameters were set at 120 kV, automatic milliamperage to minimize patient radiation dose, pitch 1.0125, rotation time 0.5 s, and slice thickness 3 mm. All data were reconstructed using list-mode ordered-subset expectation maximization with point spread function and time-of-flight (OSEM-PSF-TOF) algorithms. CT images were reconstructed with soft tissue window thickness at 3 mm and lung window thickness at 1.5 mm. Bone window analysis was used for bone lesions. PET and CT images were transferred to an Image Workstation for registration and fusion.

Whole-body PET/CT images were reconstructed from the original data based on the Whole-body PET/CT FOV, covering from the head to mid-thigh [17], while other image parameters remained unchanged.

Image analysis

Two nuclear medicine physicians with over 5 years of PET/CT diagnostic experience independently analyzed the images, focusing on distal limb metastases and benign lesions detected by TB PET/CT but not by WB PET/CT. They were only aware of the primary tumor diagnosis [18]. Any disagreement was resolved by consensus between the reviewers. Any 18F-FDG uptake above background was considered positive. The diagnosis of lesions (whether benign or malignant) and the specific diagnosis for each lesion were determined by integrating PET and CT imaging features. For example, lesions with PET-positive uptake but benign CT features (e.g., inflammation) were classified as benign. Conversely, lesions presenting as enlarged lymph nodes, soft tissue masses, bone destruction, or other signs indicative of malignancy on CT, when accompanied by PET-positive uptake, were considered metastatic. For semi-quantitative analysis, the region of interest (ROI) was outlined on the lesion on PET axial slices using a 41% isocontour to define the volume of interest (VOI). Maximum (SUVmax) and mean standardized uptake values (SUVmean) were automatically measured. The primary lung cancer lesion’s maximum diameter, SUVmax, and SUVmean were recorded. For organs with > 20 positive lesions, the count was capped at 20, while < 20 lesions were counted individually. The SUVmax and SUVmean of the most avid lesion were used as representative values.

In comparison to WB PET/CT, when TB PET/CT identifies additional distant metastases and consequently results in the patient’s clinical stage being upgraded from stage I - III to stage IV, this is defined as a stage - changing event.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM). Quantitative variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were presented as frequency (percentage). Univariate analysis was conducted using chi-square or independent t-tests. Multivariate analysis was performed using logistic regression (forward method). The presence of distal limb metastases was the dependent variable (0 = absent, 1 = present), with independent variables including both numerical measures, such as age, primary lesion diameter, SUVmax, and SUVmean, and categorical factors, including gender, lymphatic metastasis, visceral metastasis, and histopathological findings (0 = absent, 1 = present). A P-value of < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the patients

This study included 763 patients with pathologically confirmed lung cancer. The median age was 62 years (range: 54–68 years), with 505 males (66.2%) and 258 females (33.8%). The most common histological subtype was adenocarcinoma (581 cases, 76.1%), followed by squamous cell carcinoma (100 cases, 13.1%), small cell lung cancer (49 cases, 6.4%), and rare types such as SMARCA4-deficient non-small cell lung cancer (12 cases), sarcomatoid carcinoma (6 cases), lymphoepithelioma-like carcinoma (5 cases), large cell carcinoma (5 cases) and undefined histopathologic type (5 cases, 0.7%). According to the 8th edition of the TNM staging system [19], 291 patients (38.1%) were in stage I, 58 (7.6%) in stage II, 125 (16.4%) in stage III, and 289 (37.9%) in stage IV (Table 1).

Comparison of TB PET/CT and WB PET/CT in detecting metastatic lesions and staging

Among 289 stage IV patients, 96 (33.2%) exhibited limb metastases, with 31 (32.3%) having isolated metastases and 65 (67.7%) having multiple metastases. Concurrent metastases included bone outside the limb (95.8%), muscle outside the limb (21.9%), lymph nodes (95.8%), brain (21.9%), pleura (27.1%), adrenal glands (32.3%), and liver (36.5%).

Compared to WB PET/CT, TB PET/CT detected additional distal limb metastases in 60.4% (58/96) of patients with limb metastases, representing 20.1% (58/289) of stage IV patients and 7.6% (58/763) of the entire cohort. Specifically, TB PET/CT identified 96 additional bone metastases and 43 additional muscle metastases compared to WB PET/CT. These lesions were predominantly located in the distal femur (52.1%), tibia (34.4%), and lower thigh muscles (44.2%) (Fig. 1).

Distribution of distal limb metastases in 58 patients

In patients with isolated distal limb metastasis, TB PET/CT detected more lesions in 13 (41.9%) compared to WB PET/CT and upstaged one patient from stage III to stage IV. The treatment was not changed for this patient (Fig. 2). Among those with multiple metastases, TB PET/CT detected more lesions in 45 (69.2%) (Fig. 3), but no upstaging occurred. Overall, TB PET/CT changed staging in only 0.3% of stage IV patients compared to WB PET/CT.

Factors associated with distal Llimb metastasis in stage IV lung cancer patients

Univariate analysis showed that distal limb metastasis was significantly associated with liver metastasis (P = 0.013), adrenal metastasis (P = 0.021), bone metastasis outside the limbs (P < 0.001), and muscle metastasis outside the limbs(P = 0.004). No significant associations were found with gender, age, primary lesion imaging features, pathological type, lymph node metastasis, or brain metastasis (Table 2). Multivariate analysis revealed that bone metastasis outside the limbs (OR = 8.288, 95% CI: 3.642–18.861) and muscle metastasis outside the limbs (OR = 3.911, 95% CI: 1.624–9.417) were independent predictors of distal limb metastasis (both P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Detection of distal limb benign lesions using TB PET/CT

Compared to WB PET/CT, TB PET/CT identified additional distal limb benign lesions in 261 patients (34.2%). Acute arthritis was most common, affecting 193 patients (25.3%), including knee (87.0%), ankle (26.4%), and foot (11.4%) arthritis (Fig. 4). Other benign lesions included lower limb varicose veins (3.5%), neurogenic tumors (1.3%), lipomas (0.5%), fractures (1.8%), and degenerative changes (2.1%) (Table 4). Notably, one patient with a tibial fracture underwent surgery (Fig. 5), and 40 patients with arthritis received anti-inflammatory treatment.

Discussion

Lung cancer is a malignancy that frequently metastasizes to distant organs, with most patients presenting at an advanced stage [20]. The skeleton is a common site of metastasis [21], with the majority of bone metastases occurring in the axial skeleton (approximately 73.1%) and proximal limbs (such as the humerus and femur, 22.5%). In contrast, distal limbs (forearms and lower legs) and extremities (hands and feet) are relatively rare sites of metastasis, accounting for only 2.0% and 2.4%, respectively [22–24]. This pattern is closely related to the distribution of red bone marrow [25]. Muscle metastases are even rarer, typically seen in advanced stages [26]. Alastair reported that the most commonly affected muscle is the gluteus (12.3%), while distal limb muscles (e.g., hand, forearm, and lower leg) are less frequently involved (1.4%, 1.2%, and 2.6%, respectively) [27]. This may be due to the greater exposure of disseminated tumor cells to shear stress [27, 28].

Due to the rarity of distal limb metastases, conventional 18F-FDG WB PET/CT imaging typically covers only from head to the mid-thigh area [22, 29], with the distal limbs often excluded to save time and increase throughput. Extending the scan range to include the entire lower limbs would add 60–80 cm and increase imaging time by 8–10 min using WB PET/CT [30]. In routine practice, distal limb metastases are firstly assessed through patient history. If distant metastasis is suspected, the scan field is expanded; otherwise, it stops at the mid-thigh. However, the advent of long-axis PET/CT technologies (e.g., uEXPLORER [13], Biograph Vision Quadra [31], and Discovery MI Gen 2 [32]) allows full-body coverage in 1–2 bed positions, significantly reducing imaging time. This makes TB PET/CT a model for comprehensive whole-body scans [10].

Our study demonstrates that TB PET/CT, with its single-bed full-body coverage, can completely visualize systemic metastases in lung cancer, avoiding missed diagnoses of distal limb metastases. Among 96 stage IV patients with limb metastasis, 58 (60.4%) had distal limb metastases (hands, forearms, distal femur, knees, lower legs, and feet). These patients accounted for 20.1% (58/289) of stage IV patients and 7.6% (58/763) of all patients. Of the 139 distal limb metastases, 96 (69.0%) were bone metastases and 43 (31.0%) were muscle metastases. These would have been missed with conventional WB PET/CT. TB PET/CT thus provides a more thorough assessment of systemic metastases.

The incidence of distal limb metastases in our study (7.6%) was higher than the previously reported rates of 4.4–5.2% [22, 27]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the potential bias in case selection, given that patients referred to PET/CT centers often present with more advanced or severe disease, as well as the higher sensitivity of PET/CT compared to conventional X-rays or CT scans [33, 34]. Another possible reason stems from the variations in diagnostic approaches. In some institutions, additional PET/CT scans are generally avoided for patients who have already been diagnosed with stage IV disease based on diagnostic CT scans. In contrast, at our center, PET/CT scans are still performed in some stage IV patients if a more comprehensive assessment of systemic metastases is deemed beneficial for disease evaluation or for guiding treatment decisions.

Our study shows that although TB PET/CT identified distal limb metastases in 20.1% (58/289) of stage IV patients, only one patient (0.3%) experienced a change in staging, which did not lead to any significant alterations in treatment strategies. Additionally, WB PET/CT also helps to minimize patients’ radiation exposure and enhances patient throughput for short-axis scanners [30]. Therefore, WB PET/CT should still be recommended as the standard practice for staging lung cancer when using short-axis scanners. However, “true whole-body” 18F-FDG PET/CT may have added benefit in some patients. For example, in Fig. 2 patient, an osteolytic metastatic lesion in the lower limb was detected by TB PET/CT, which led to the implementation of radiotherapy specifically targeting the lesion. As a result, the patient experienced improved local control and significant pain relief. Some researches also reported that although lower limbs additional PET/CT acquisition seems to offer poor additional benefit, however, but it may affect clinical management in some patients [35–38].

Our study found that distal limb metastases were often associated with bone metastases outside the limbs and muscle metastases outside the limbs (both P < 0.001). This suggests that when using traditional short-axial PET/CT for staging, expanding scan range to cover the distal limbs is recommended to reduce missed detection of distal limb metastases if bone or muscle metastases are detected on WB PET/CT.

In the present study, TB PET/CT was found to have an additional advantage: it detected numerous benign lesions in the distal limbs. For instance, acute arthritis was identified in 193 patients (25.3%), with 20.7% of these patients subsequently receiving anti-arthritis treatment. Other benign lesions such as varicose veins, neurogenic tumors, lipomas, fractures, and degenerative changes were also detected. The ability to identify these benign conditions in a timely manner allows for prompt treatment and management.

This study has some limitations: (1) It is retrospective, and case selection may be biased, especially since patients at PET/CT centers often present with difficult diagnoses or severe conditions. (2) Due to ethical constraints, pathological confirmation of each metastatic lesion was not possible, potentially leading to misinterpretation of inflammatory lesions as metastases.

Conclusion

TB PET/CT identifies additional distal bone and muscle metastases in approximately 20% of stage IV lung cancer patients compared to WB PET/CT, thereby offering a more comprehensive evaluation of systemic metastasis. Nevertheless, it led to stage shift in only one patient and 0% change in oncologic treatment. In this context, WB PET/CT continues to be adequate for staging lung cancer and should remain the standard practice when utilizing short-axis scanners.
