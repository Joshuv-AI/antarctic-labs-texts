Positive affect and inflammation in women with breast cancer: A systematic review
Authors: Michalina Frankowska, Mateusz Gliwinski, Magdalena Blazek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPositive affect has been implicated in psychobiological processes relevant to cancer adaptation and survivorship. However, evidence linking positive affect to objective inflammatory and immune-related biomarkers in women with breast cancer remains limited, fragmented, and methodologically heterogeneous. This systematic review was conducted to provide a focused and transparent synthesis of studies examining associations between positive affect and inflammatory or immune-related outcomes in this population.MethodsFive electronic databases were systematically searched. The review protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261304159). Studies were included if they assessed positive affect using validated measures and reported at least one objective inflammatory or immune-related biomarker in women with breast cancer. Observational studies and randomized controlled trials were eligible. Six studies met predefined PICO criteria.ResultsSix studies examined associations between positive affect and inflammatory or immune-related outcomes across treatment and survivorship phases. Higher low-arousal positive affect was associated with lower C-reactive protein (CRP) in longitudinal analyses. Randomized controlled trials of Mindful Awareness Practices increased positive affect and meaning-related well-being; effects on circulating interleukin-6 (IL-6) and CRP were inconsistent, whereas transcriptional analyses indicated reduced or buffered pro-inflammatory gene expression. Increases in eudaimonic well-being were linked to reductions in conserved transcriptional response to adversity (CTRA) profiles. Affiliative positive emotions were associated with enhanced T helper type 1 (Th1)-type cytokine production.ConclusionsCurrent evidence suggests that positive affect may be associated with reduced inflammatory activity or more adaptive immune functioning in women with breast cancer. However, heterogeneity in affect conceptualization and biomarker assessment underscores the need for larger, methodologically harmonized longitudinal and intervention studies.Keywords: Breast cancer, Positive affect, Eudaimonic wellbeing, Inflammatory markers, Inflammation, Conserved transcriptional response to adversity

Introduction

The vast majority of research in health psychology and psychoneuroimmunology has traditionally focused on the detrimental effects of stress and its biological correlates. In parallel, however, a relatively newer line of research has emerged, providing accumulating evidence for an association between positive affect and improved somatic health outcomes (Seligman and Csikszentmihalyi, 2000; Pressman and Cohen, 2005). Despite growing interest in this area, the physiological mechanisms underlying this relationship remain incompletely understood. Moreover, relatively few studies have examined this association in clinical populations, in which improving physical health and quality of life constitutes a central therapeutic goal. Addressing the nature of the relationship between affective states and somatic health—and evaluating this relationship using available biological data—appears to be a necessary step toward a more holistic understanding of human functioning.

The term positive affect refers to an individual's subjective experience of positive emotional states, such as joy, happiness, enthusiasm, excitement, and contentment (Clark et al., 1989). In the literature, two primary conceptual perspectives on positive affect are typically distinguished: hedonic and eudaimonic. The hedonic tradition emphasizes pleasure, enjoyment, and satisfaction, whereas the eudaimonic perspective focuses on meaning, purpose in life, and self-realization (Czapiński, 2012). Empirical evidence suggests that positive affect is associated with lower incidence of somatic diseases and reduced all-cause mortality in the general population (Cohen and Pressman, 2006; Chida and Steptoe, 2008).

In oncological populations, positive affect has also been linked to clinically meaningful outcomes. Earlier studies demonstrated an association between positive affect and reduced mortality among women with recurrent breast cancer (Levy et al., 1988), as well as among patients with renal cell carcinoma (Prinsloo et al., 2015). Subsequent research has shown that higher levels of positive affect are related to better psychological adjustment to cancer, particularly among individuals who have successfully completed treatment (cancer survivors). Specifically, positive affect has been associated with lower anxiety, fewer depressive symptoms, reduced pain and fatigue, and consequently, higher quality of life (Guadagnoli and Mor, 1989; Schroevers et al., 2000; Baker et al., 2002).

More recent discoveries point to a biological signature underlying these associations, offering insight into potential psychoneuroimmunological mechanisms. Advances in genomic research have identified a conserved transcriptional response to adversity (CTRA), a gene expression profile regulated primarily by activation of the sympathetic nervous system and the “fight-or-flight” response. The CTRA pattern is characterized by upregulated expression of pro-inflammatory genes and downregulated expression of genes involved in Type I interferon antiviral responses (Cole SW, 2019) Emerging empirical studies suggest positive affect may be associated with a downregulation of the CTRA profile, indicating a potential biological pathway linking affective states to immune and inflammatory processes (Frankowska and Błażek, 2022).

Together, these findings highlight the relevance of investigating positive affect not only as a psychological construct but also as a factor with measurable biological correlates, particularly in populations facing substantial inflammatory and immune challenges, such as patients with breast cancer. However, despite increasing interest, the evidence linking positive affect to objective inflammatory biomarkers in this population has not yet been systematically synthesized. Addressing this gap may provide important insights into the psychobiological mechanisms that support resilience, recovery, and long-term health outcomes in breast cancer patients.

Therefore, the aim of this systematic review is to synthesize and critically evaluate the available evidence on the association between positive affect - conceptualized within both hedonic and eudaimonic frameworks - and objective inflammatory markers in women diagnosed with breast cancer, in order to clarify the potential psychobiological pathways linking affective well-being with inflammatory processes in this clinical population.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420261304159).

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted across five electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, EBSCO, and Ovid. These databases were selected to capture peer-reviewed literature across oncology, psychoneuroimmunology, behavioral medicine, and health psychology, thereby maximizing coverage of studies relevant to the review question. The total number of records identified across all databases was 592, including 91 from PubMed, 194 from Scopus, 133 from Web of Science, 105 from EBSCO, and 69 from Ovid. The search strategy was developed based on the PICO framework and aimed to identify studies examining the association between positive affect and inflammatory or immune markers in women diagnosed with breast cancer.

Search terms included combinations of keywords related to positive affect and well-being (e.g.: “positive affect”, “well-being”, “hedonic affect”, “eudamonic affect”), breast cancer (e.g.: “breast cancer”, “breast carcinoma”, “breast neoplasm”), and inflammatory outcomes (e.g.: “inflammation”, “inflammatory markers”, “CRP”, “C-reactive protein”, “IL-6”, “TNF-alpha”, “cytokines”, “immune markers”). These terms were combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR) and adapted as necessary to the syntax of each database. Searches were limited to peer-reviewed articles published in English or Polish. No restrictions were imposed on publication year.

Studies were excluded if they involved mixed cancer populations without separate analyses for breast cancer, lacked objective biological measures, focused exclusively on negative affect without assessment of positive affect or hedonic/eudemonic well-being, used non-validated psychological measures, or were case reports, theoretical papers, reviews, commentaries, or animal studies.

Study selection

All records retrieved from the database searches were imported into Mendeley reference management software. Duplicate records were identified and removed using automated tools within Mendeley, followed by manual verification. Titles and abstracts were screened against the predefined eligibility criteria. Full texts were retrieved for records that appeared eligible or for which eligibility could not be determined from the abstract alone. Final inclusion decisions were made after full-text assessment. In unclear cases, eligibility decisions were discussed among the reviewers until consensus was reached. After duplicate removal, 260 records were screened by title and abstract against the predefined eligibility criteria. At this stage, 192 records were excluded because they were clearly outside the scope of the review question, most commonly because they did not involve women with breast cancer, did not assess positive affect or related well-being constructs, did not include objective inflammatory or immune-related biomarkers, were not empirical peer-reviewed studies, or otherwise did not match the predefined PICO framework. A total of 68 reports were sought for retrieval, of which 39 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility. After full-text assessment, 28 reports were excluded: 27 did not meet the predefined PICO criteria, and 1 was excluded because the full text was not available in English or Polish. Of the studies identified through the systematic search, six studies met all predefined PICO-based eligibility criteria and were therefore included in the main systematic review synthesis. These studies explicitly assessed positive affect using validated psychological instruments and examined its association with objective inflammatory or immune-related biomarkers in women diagnosed with breast cancer.

In addition, five studies identified through the same systematic search process were retained for contextual narrative discussion only. Although these studies did not fully meet all predefined PICO criteria, they provided relevant psychoneuroimmunological evidence within this clinical population. Specifically, the studies involved women with breast cancer and reported objective inflammatory or immune-related outcomes, but examined psychosocial constructs related to positive affect (e.g., general well-being, quality of life, coping, or social support) rather than directly assessing positive affect using validated affective measures. These studies were not included in the primary systematic evidence base and were instead used to support interpretation of potential biological and psychosocial mechanisms relevant to the observed associations. To maintain conceptual and methodological clarity, contextual studies were identified through the same search strategy, reported transparently, and discussed separately from studies meeting the formal eligibility criteria.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted using predefined and standardized extraction categories consistent with the registered review protocol. Data were extracted by one reviewer and checked by at least one additional reviewer. Extracted information included study characteristics (authors, year, study design), sample characteristics (sample size, treatment status), measures of positive affect, type of inflammatory or immune-related biomarkers, and key findings. Any discrepancies or uncertainties identified during the checking process were resolved through discussion and consensus. Extracted data were synthesized and summarized in structured tables presented in the Results section.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality was assessed using a structured narrative appraisal tailored to the designs of the included studies. Particular attention was paid to study design, sample characteristics, validity of psychological and biological measurements, control of potential confounding variables, and clarity of outcome reporting. Quality appraisal, eligibility assessment, and data extraction were conducted by one reviewer and checked by at least one additional reviewer. In cases of uncertainty or disagreement, final judgments were reached through discussion and consensus.

To support data quality, consistency, and reduce potential bias in the review process, several procedural safeguards were implemented, including prospective protocol registration in PROSPERO, adherence to PRISMA 2020 reporting guidance, use of predefined PICO-based eligibility criteria, reviewer checking procedures, and duplicate removal and record tracking using Mendeley reference management software. These methodological considerations were taken into account during the narrative synthesis, and studies with greater limitations were interpreted more cautiously when evaluating the overall strength and consistency of the evidence, particularly in light of study heterogeneity and potential sources of bias.

Strategy of data synthesis

Given the heterogeneity of study designs, psychological constructs, biomarker types, and analytical approaches, a narrative synthesis was employed. Quantitative meta-analysis was not conducted due to variability in outcome measures and insufficient homogeneity across studies.

Results

The six studies included in the main review comprised observational and randomized controlled designs conducted in women diagnosed with breast cancer at various stages of treatment and survivorship. Positive affect was operationalized using several conceptually related, but not identical, self-report measures across the included studies. The most commonly used instrument was the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS), which primarily assesses the frequency and intensity of positive emotional states such as enthusiasm, alertness, and inspiration (Watson et al., 1988). Other studies employed broader measures of psychological well-being, including the Mental Health Continuum–Short Form (MHC-SF), which encompasses emotional, psychological, and social well-being dimensions and is more closely aligned with eudaimonic well-being (Keyes, 2002; Keyes et al., 2008). As is Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy (FACIT), which includes dimensions related to meaning, purpose, and peace, reflecting broader existential and eudaimonic aspects of well-being (Peterman et al., 2002). Additionally, one study utilized the Affective Balance Scale (ABS), to measure positive emotions of affection, contentment, vigor, and joy, what is conceptually more consistent with hedonic models of well-being (Derogatis, 1996). Inflammatory outcomes included circulating biomarkers such as C-reactive protein and pro-inflammatory cytokines, as well as gene expression profiles related to inflammatory signalling. Across the six studies included in the main synthesis, positive affect was examined in relation to multiple indicators of inflammatory and immune activity in women with breast cancer at different stages of the disease trajectory (pre-treatment and survivorship). Overall, findings suggested a heterogeneous but conceptually coherent pattern in which positive affect—particularly low-arousal and eudaimonic forms—was associated with more adaptive inflammatory or immune profiles, although effects varied depending on the biological outcome and measurement level.

In a prospective longitudinal study, Moreno et al. (2016) observed that higher levels of low-arousal positive affect (e.g., calmness, relaxation, contentment), as opposed to high-arousal positive affect (e.g., excitement, enthusiasm), were associated with lower circulating CRP concentrations over time among breast cancer survivors. In contrast, Manigault et al. (2022), examining patients prior to adjuvant treatment, found no significant association between positive affect and CRP levels. In this study, positive affect was conceptualized as one of the components of psychological resilience. The authors demonstrated that individuals reporting a greater number of psychosocial resources buffering against stress exhibited weaker associations between stress and depressive symptoms, including those linked to inflammation. These findings further support the protective role of psychosocial resilience factors and suggest that the combined presence of multiple psychosocial resources may contribute synergistically to resilience processes.

Randomized controlled trials evaluating Mindful Awareness Practices (MAPs) provided partially convergent evidence. In Bower et al. (2015), the intervention increased positive affect and meaning/peace and resulted in lower IL-6 concentrations at post-intervention, controlling for baseline levels. In a larger three-arm trial (Bower et al., 2023), MAPs again significantly increased positive affect from pre-to post-intervention and at 3-month follow-up. Although circulating IL-6 and CRP did not differ between groups, pro-inflammatory gene expression increased in the wait-list control relative to MAPs, suggesting effects detectable at the transcriptional rather than protein level.

Consistent with these genomic findings, Boyle et al. (2019) demonstrated that increases in eudaimonic well-being were associated with decreases in the conserved transcriptional response to adversity (CTRA) pro-inflammatory gene expression profile. Participants reporting larger gains in eudaimonic well-being exhibited greater reductions in overall CTRA scores and increased expression of antiviral/antibody-related gene transcripts, although changes in eudaimonic well-being were not significantly correlated with the pro-inflammatory subcomponent when examined separately.

In contrast to inflammatory markers, Blomberg et al. (2009) found that greater affiliative positive emotions were associated with increased production of Th1-type cytokines (IFN-γ and IL-12), suggesting enhancement of cellular immune competence rather than suppression of immune activity.

Taken together, the findings indicate that positive affect is not uniformly linked to reduced immune activity but rather to a pattern consistent with improved immunoregulation: lower chronic inflammatory signaling, altered pro-inflammatory gene expression, and, in some contexts, enhanced cellular immune responses. The included studies operationalized positive affect using conceptually distinct measures, ranging from instruments primarily assessing hedonic positive emotional states (e.g., PANAS and ABS) to broader measures encompassing eudaimonic dimensions of well-being (e.g., MHC-SF and FACIT-Sp). Importantly, associations appeared to be more consistent for low-arousal or eudaimonic forms of positive affect, as well as at the transcriptional level, than for circulating inflammatory proteins, which demonstrated more variable associations across disease phases.

Contextual evidence

Five additional studies identified through the same search process were considered only as contextual evidence and are discussed separately from the six studies included in the primary systematic synthesis. These investigations examined psychosocial variables closely related to positive affect, such as well-being, quality-of-life, resilience and reported objective inflammatory or immune-related outcomes. Although these studies did not fully meet the predefined PICO criteria — due to the absence of a validated positive affect measure — they consistently demonstrated associations between psychosocial functioning and immune or inflammatory processes in women with breast cancer. Several studies indicated that broader psychosocial resources are linked to inflammatory regulation. Notably, these psychosocial resources are conceptually related to positive affect, which constitutes one of their components, thereby warranting their consideration as relevant contextual evidence. Oliveira et al. (2025) reported that the psychosocial contribution to the observed inflammatory profile is largely influenced by coping mechanisms, particularly venting and the use of emotional and/or instrumental support. Similarly, Reis et al. (2022) found that lower social and family well-being was associated with higher levels of S100A8/A9 and IL-1β, suggesting that interpersonal well-being may relate to inflammatory activation.

Evidence also suggested that emotional processing capacities may moderate inflammatory effects. Reed et al. (2016) demonstrated that the association between elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines and sickness symptoms was attenuated in individuals with greater emotional acceptance, indicating a buffering role of adaptive emotion regulation. In contrast, Sakki et al. (2022) observed improvements in resilience, quality of life, and depressive symptoms after completion of the 8-week Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) program, without corresponding changes in CRP, cortisol, or ACTH.

Finally, Boivin and Webb (2020) found that higher CD8+ T-cell percentages were associated with poorer quality of life and greater depressive symptoms, suggesting that immune activation may correspond to worse psychological functioning rather than psychological well-being enhancing immunity under certain treatment conditions.

Taken together, although these studies did not directly measure positive affect, they consistently indicate that psychosocial functioning—including coping resources, emotional regulation, and interpersonal well-being—is associated with immune and inflammatory processes. This body of contextual evidence supports the plausibility of the associations identified in the main synthesis and suggests that positive affect may represent one component within a broader network of psychosocial resilience factors influencing psychoneuroimmunological pathways.

Across both the primary synthesis and contextual evidence, the majority of studies demonstrated associations between more adaptive psychosocial functioning and more favorable immune or inflammatory profiles, although the consistency of findings varied according to biomarker type and psychosocial construct.

Discussion

This systematic review examined associations between positive affect and immune and inflammatory processes in women with breast cancer. Across the main synthesis, positive affect was consistently linked with patterns indicative of more adaptive immunoregulation, although the direction and magnitude of associations depended on the biological level assessed (Moreno et al., 2016; Bower et al., 2015, 2023; Blomberg et al., 2009; Boyle et al., 2019; Manigault et al., 2022). In general, positive affect was more reliably related to inflammatory gene expression and functional immune responses than to circulating inflammatory proteins (Bower et al., 2023; Boyle et al., 2019). This distinction is important because the included biomarkers reflect different levels of biological organization and are therefore not directly interchangeable. Gene expression profiles may capture upstream regulatory activity, circulating inflammatory proteins reflect downstream systemic processes. Consequently, comparability across studies is inherently limited, and the robustness of cross-study integration must be interpreted with caution.

A notable pattern emerging from the included studies concerns the differentiation between forms of positive affect. Low-arousal and eudaimonic positive affect demonstrated more consistent associations with biological outcomes than high-arousal positive affect (Moreno et al., 2016; Boyle et al., 2019). Calmness, contentment, and meaning-related well-being were associated with lower inflammatory signaling and more favorable transcriptional profiles, whereas markers such as CRP and IL-6 showed less consistent relationships (Bower et al., 2015, 2023; Manigault et al., 2022). This distinction aligns with theoretical models proposing that restorative, parasympathetic-linked affective states may be more relevant for immunoregulation than activated positive states characterized by heightened physiological arousal.

Another consistent observation was the level-specific nature of associations. Effects were most robust at the transcriptional level, including conserved transcriptional response to adversity (CTRA) – related gene expression, while circulating cytokines displayed greater variability (Bower et al., 2023; Boyle et al., 2019). This discrepancy likely reflects the temporal dynamics of immune regulation: gene expression changes may occur earlier and more sensitively in response to psychological processes, whereas circulating proteins are influenced by multiple physiological sources and slower systemic processes. The absence of associations with CRP in the pre-treatment phase further suggests that baseline inflammatory activity and disease stage may moderate detectable psychobiological relationships (Manigault et al., 2022). For this reason, the present narrative synthesis should be understood as identifying broad patterns across related but biologically distinct outcomes, rather than as demonstrating uniform effects across fully comparable inflammatory endpoints.

The strength of the evidence should also be interpreted in light of potential confounding factors common in psycho-oncology research. Most studies included in the primary synthesis involved women in the survivorship or post-treatment phase, whereas one study assessed patients before treatment. Although this relative consistency in treatment phase may reduce some heterogeneity, differences in disease severity, comorbid depressive symptoms, fatigue, sleep disturbance, comorbid medical conditions, and medication use may still influence immune and inflammatory markers.

Importantly, positive affect did not uniformly predict reduced immune activity. In some cases, higher affiliative positive emotions were associated with greater Th1 cytokine production, indicating enhanced cellular immune competence rather than suppression (Blomberg et al., 2009). Taken together, the evidence supports a regulatory rather than strictly anti-inflammatory interpretation - positive affect appears linked to a more efficient and context-appropriate immune response, characterized by reduced chronic inflammatory signaling alongside preserved or enhanced antiviral and cellular immunity.

Contextual studies were interpreted cautiously and were not considered equivalent to evidence derived from the primary systematic synthesis. Instead, they were used as complementary, hypothesis-supporting evidence that may aid in the interpretations.

Studies examining coping, emotional acceptance, social well-being, and broader psychosocial resilience demonstrated similar associations with inflammatory and immune markers despite not directly measuring positive affect. Emotional regulation capacities attenuated relationships between inflammation and sickness symptoms (Reed et al., 2016), interpersonal well-being corresponded with lower inflammatory markers (Reis et al., 2022), and coping resources showed immunological associations particularly after treatment (Oliveira et al., 2025). The convergence of these findings across related constructs supports the plausibility of shared underlying mechanisms.

Several psychobiological pathways may account for these observations. Positive affect and related resilience factors are associated with reduced threat appraisal, improved emotion regulation, and increased parasympathetic activity, which may modulate inflammatory signaling through neuroendocrine and autonomic pathways. In parallel, meaning-related well-being may influence immune gene transcription via conserved stress-responsive signaling pathways (Boyle et al., 2019; Bower et al., 2023). Rather than acting as a direct suppressor of immune activation, positive affect may facilitate adaptive calibration of immune responses to environmental demands.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this systematic review indicates that positive affect and related dimensions of psychological well-being are associated with inflammatory and immune-related processes in women with breast cancer. Although the number of studies meeting strict PICO criteria remains limited, converging findings from contextual evidence support the biological relevance of affective states and reinforce the importance of psychosocial well-being in comprehensive cancer care.

Limitations

The conclusions of this review should be interpreted in light of several limitations. The number of eligible studies was small and heterogeneous in design, disease stage, and biomarker selection. Biomarkers ranged from gene expression to circulating proteins and immune cell subsets, further contributing to heterogeneity. Additionally, several studies were observational, precluding causal inference, and intervention studies often included multiple psychosocial components beyond positive affect alone.

Implications and future directions

Despite these limitations, the findings suggest that positive affect represents a relevant psychobiological factor associated with immune regulation in cancer survivorship. Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs integrating multi-level immune assessment (gene expression, cellular function, and circulating markers) and distinguish between affective dimensions. Greater standardization of immune and inflammatory outcome measures may additionally improve cross-study comparability and facilitate future meta-analytic synthesis. In particular, combining commonly assessed circulating inflammatory markers (e.g., IL-6, CRP, TNF-α) with transcriptional indicators such as the CTRA gene expression profile may provide a more integrative framework for examining psychoneuroimmunological associations in breast cancer populations. However, given the current methodological heterogeneity of the field, additional research is needed before establishing standardized biomarker panels. Clarifying temporal dynamics between psychological changes and biological responses will be particularly important. Moreover, interventions designed to enhance meaning-related and low-arousal positive affect may represent promising candidates for mechanistic trials evaluating psychoneuroimmunological pathways.

Clinical and translational implications

From a clinical perspective, the findings of this review suggest that positive affect and well-being may represent modifiable targets in psycho-oncological care. Interventions aimed at enhancing positive affect, meaning, or overall psychological well-being—such as mindfulness-based programs or meaning-centered therapies—have demonstrated favorable effects on immune and inflammatory processes in breast cancer patients. While current evidence does not support the routine use of inflammatory biomarkers as clinical endpoints for psychological interventions, these findings underscore the potential value of integrated care models that address both psychological and biological dimensions of cancer survivorship.
