Anxiety and neurophysiological correlates of stuttering severity in adults who stutter
Authors: Agit Şimşek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStuttering has traditionally been conceptualized as a motor speech disorder; however, accumulating evidence suggests that auditory processing differences and emotional factors may also contribute to its clinical presentation. Despite increasing interest in these domains, the interplay between auditory cortical processing, stuttering severity, and anxiety in adults remains incompletely understood.ObjectiveThis study aimed to examine auditory evoked cortical responses (P1, N1, P2, and mismatch negativity [MMN]) in adults who stutter compared with fluent-speaking controls, and to explore how neurophysiological measures vary in relation to stuttering severity and state–trait anxiety levels.MethodsAuditory event-related potentials were recorded using pure-tone stimuli in adults who stutter and age-matched control participants. Latency and amplitude measures of the P1, N1, P2, and MMN components were analyzed. Anxiety was assessed using the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). Group comparisons and correlational analyses were performed to characterize neurophysiological, clinical, and psychological patterns.ResultsCompared with controls, adults who stutter exhibited significant alterations in both early (P1, N1) and later (P2, MMN) stages of auditory cortical processing. Increasing stuttering severity appeared to be associated with a pattern of variation in ERP latencies and amplitudes, accompanied by parallel changes in state, trait, and total anxiety scores.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that stuttering in adulthood is associated with widespread differences in auditory cortical processing that co-vary with both anxiety levels and clinical severity. The results support a multidimensional framework in which sensory, emotional, and speech-related processes interact, highlighting the importance of integrated neurophysiological and psychological assessment approaches in stuttering research and clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04837-3.

Introductıon

Stuttering is a neurodevelopmental communication disorder characterized by involuntary disruptions in the flow of speech [15]. Although it has traditionally been classified as a motor speech disorder, growing evidence suggests that it may also involve alterations in sensory and auditory processing mechanisms [5]. Developmental stuttering refers to a fluency disorder with onset in early childhood, typically emerging during language development and persisting into adulthood in some individuals.

Auditory evoked cortical responses (event-related potentials; ERPs) provide a powerful, non-invasive approach for investigating such neurophysiological differences. In individuals who stutter, several studies have reported prolonged P2 latency and reduced P2 and mismatch negativity (MMN) amplitudes, indicating atypical auditory cortical processing [14]. However, findings across studies are not entirely consistent, with some variability observed depending on methodological approaches and sample characteristics. More recent studies have further demonstrated that P2 responses are related to stuttering severity and may normalize following therapeutic intervention, highlighting their potential clinical relevance [8, 12].

Beyond sensory processing, the psychological dimension of stuttering has received increasing attention. Adults who stutter have been shown to exhibit significantly higher state and trait anxiety levels, as measured by the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), compared with fluent-speaking controls [3, 16]. These findings underscore the importance of emotional factors in the clinical manifestation of stuttering.

Despite advances in neurophysiological and psychological research, relatively few studies have concurrently examined auditory cortical processing measures in relation to anxiety levels. Moreover, to date, no controlled study conducted within a Turkish adult population has systematically investigated the combined contribution of auditory cortical responses and anxiety to stuttering severity.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to compare auditory evoked cortical responses (P1, N1, P2, and MMN) between adults who stutter and fluent-speaking controls, and to examine how these neurophysiological measures vary in relation to state and trait anxiety levels. By integrating sensory and emotional dimensions, this study seeks to contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the multidimensional nature of stuttering in adulthood.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional, controlled, quantitative design to compare auditory evoked cortical responses (P1, N1, P2, and mismatch negativity [MMN]) and state–trait anxiety levels between adults who stutter and fluent-speaking control participants.

Participants

Stuttering group

A total of 29 adults with developmental stuttering were initially recruited. Inclusion criteria were: (i) age between 18 and 45 years; (ii) a diagnosis of developmental stuttering supported by a certified speech and language therapist; (iii) onset of stuttering during childhood; (iv) absence of any diagnosed neurological or psychiatric disorder; (v) no current use of psychoactive medication (e.g., antidepressants, anxiolytics); and (vi) provision of written informed consent.

Exclusion of psychiatric disorders was based on self-report and medical history obtained during participant screening. Participants were specifically asked about any prior diagnosis of psychiatric conditions and current use of psychotropic medication, and those reporting a diagnosed psychiatric disorder or ongoing psychiatric treatment were excluded.

A total of 29 adults with developmental stuttering were included in the final analyses.

Control group

The control group consisted of 30 age-matched adults with no history of stuttering or other speech or language disorders. Additional exclusion criteria included any self-reported neurological, psychiatric, or communication disorder. Psychiatric exclusion in the control group was also based on self-report and medical history.

Hearing status was verified using auditory brainstem response (ABR) testing in both groups. Only participants demonstrating a clearly identifiable wave V at 20 dB nHL were included. This criterion was used to ensure that peripheral auditory function did not confound the ERP findings.

Ethical approval

The study protocol was approved by the İnönü University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (session date: 24 February 2026; session number: 4; decision number: 2026/9479). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants received written and verbal information about the study procedures and provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Assessment ınstruments

Handedness

Hand dominance was assessed using the 10-item Edinburgh Handedness Inventory, a standardized and widely used measure of handedness [10]. Participants who were not right-handed were excluded from the study.

Assessment of stuttering severity

Stuttering severity was assessed using the Stuttering Severity Instrument–Fourth Edition (SSI-4). Speech samples were evaluated by a certified speech and language therapist, and severity levels (mild, moderate, severe) were determined based on standardized SSI-4 scoring criteria.

State–trait anxiety ınventory (STAI)

State and trait anxiety levels were assessed using the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), originally developed by Spielberger et al. [13] and validated for Turkish populations by Öner and Le Compte [9].

Recording of auditory evoked cortical responses

EEG recording and preprocessing

EEG data were recorded using a 32-channel EEG system (Neurosoft, Russia) with Ag/AgCl sintered electrodes positioned according to the international 10–20 system. Signals were continuously sampled at 1000 Hz and band-pass filtered online between 0.1 and 100 Hz. All recordings were referenced online to the linked mastoid electrodes, with electrode impedances maintained below 5 kΩ throughout data acquisition. Additional offline preprocessing steps were applied prior to ERP averaging.

Ocular and movement-related artifacts were identified and removed using a combination of automatic amplitude-based artifact rejection procedures and visual inspection. EEG epochs were segmented from − 100 ms to 500 ms relative to stimulus onset and baseline-corrected using the pre-stimulus interval (− 100 to 0 ms). Trials exceeding ± 100 μV at any electrode were excluded from further analysis.

For the P1, N1, and P2 components, artifact-free epochs were averaged separately for each participant, yielding approximately 200 accepted trials per condition. For the MMN paradigm, difference waveforms were computed by subtracting the averaged standard-stimulus waveform from the averaged deviant-stimulus waveform.

ERP components were analyzed at the Cz and Fz electrode sites, selected based on their established sensitivity to auditory cortical responses and consistency with prior ERP studies in stuttering and auditory processing. Peak latency (ms) and peak amplitude (μV) were extracted using a semi-automated peak detection algorithm and supported by visual inspection.

Because raw EEG time-series data were not retained following feature extraction, analyses were limited to peak-based latency and amplitude measures. However, peak-based ERP analyses are widely used in the literature and provide robust indices of cortical auditory processing.

P1, N1, and P2 paradigm

Auditory stimuli consisted of 1000 Hz tone bursts with a duration of 100 ms (10 ms rise/fall time, 80 ms plateau) presented binaurally via headphones at an intensity of 65 dB HL. Stimuli were delivered at a rate of 1 Hz (interstimulus interval: 1000 ms). A total of 250 stimuli were presented, with at least 200 artifact-free epochs included in the averaging process.

MMN paradigm (Oddball)

The MMN paradigm employed a passive auditory oddball design. Standard stimuli consisted of 1000 Hz tone bursts (80%, 400 trials), while deviant stimuli were 1200 Hz tone bursts (20%, 100 trials). Stimulus duration was 100 ms, intensity 65 dB HL, and interstimulus interval 500 ms. Stimuli were presented binaurally via headphones. MMN responses were obtained by computing difference waveforms (deviant ERP minus standard ERP).

ERP component ıdentification

ERP components were identified within predefined time windows based on established literature: P1 (50–80 ms) as the most positive peak, N1 (80–120 ms) as the most negative peak, P2 (150–250 ms) as the most positive peak, and MMN (100–250 ms) as the most negative peak in the difference waveform. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages.

Normality of distributions was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, with skewness and kurtosis values between − 2 and + 2 considered indicative of approximate normality [6]. Group comparisons were conducted using independent-samples t-tests for normally distributed variables and Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed variables.

Comparisons of ERP parameters and STAI scores across stuttering severity levels were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc tests when appropriate. Associations among stuttering severity, ERP parameters (latencies and amplitudes of P1, N1, P2, and MMN), and STAI scores were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) for normally distributed variables and Spearman’s rank-order correlation coefficient (ρ) for non-normally distributed variables.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In the context of multiple correlation analyses, findings were interpreted not solely on the basis of statistical significance but also with consideration of effect size magnitude, directional consistency, and pattern-level coherence.

No formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, considering the potential for type I error.

Dıscussıon

This study examined auditory evoked cortical responses (P1, N1, P2, and mismatch negativity [MMN]) in adults who stutter compared with fluent-speaking controls, and explored the relationships between these neurophysiological measures, stuttering severity, and state–trait anxiety levels. The findings suggest that adults who stutter may exhibit alterations in both early and later stages of auditory cortical processing, characterized by delayed latencies and reduced response amplitudes. In parallel, neurophysiological measures showed systematic associations across clinical severity and anxiety levels, rather than reflecting isolated or independent effects. Given the cross-sectional design, these associations should be interpreted within a correlational, rather than causal, framework.

Early auditory processing: P1 and N1 components

Prolonged P1 latency and reduced P1 amplitude in adults who stutter suggest slowed early cortical auditory processing and attenuated neural responsiveness. The P1 component is considered to reflect early cortical activation associated with the arrival of auditory input at the primary auditory cortex [4]. The present findings extend prior observations in pediatric samples by indicating that P1 latency delays may persist into adulthood [8], supporting the notion of developmental continuity in early auditory processing differences associated with stuttering.

For the N1 component, prolonged latency accompanied by preserved amplitude suggests that basic sensory encoding of acoustic features remains largely intact, whereas the temporal efficiency of auditory processing is reduced. This pattern aligns with emerging perspectives that stuttering may be more closely related to timing and temporal processing mechanisms than to deficits in primary sensory perception per se [11].

P2 component: auditory ıntegration and sensorimotor processing

The most pronounced group differences were observed for the P2 component. In adults who stutter, prolonged P2 latency and reduced amplitude point to alterations in auditory integration, auditory memory, and sensorimotor processing. These findings are consistent with previous reports of P2 abnormalities in both adult and pediatric stuttering populations [8, 14].

The P2 component has been linked to speech preparation processes and auditory feedback integration. Notably, Rocha et al. [12] demonstrated normalization of P2 responses following therapeutic intervention, in parallel with gains in speech fluency. In this context, the graded relationship observed between P2 parameters and stuttering severity in the present study raises the possibility that P2 may represent a neurophysiological marker sensitive to clinical variation. Nevertheless, longitudinal and intervention-based studies are required before such associations can be translated into clinical applications. This gradient was visually apparent in the scalp topographic maps (Figs. 3A, 4A), where P2 amplitude showed the most marked reduction over central electrode sites in the stuttering group.

Mismatch negativity: automatic auditory change detection and anxiety

MMN findings suggest that automatic auditory change detection responses are reduced at the group level in adults who stutter. However, the observation that increasing stuttering severity was associated with shorter MMN latencies and greater MMN amplitudes (in absolute terms) indicates that this relationship is not straightforward. Rather than reflecting a simple linear deterioration, these findings may arise from the interaction of multiple mechanisms.

One possible explanation is that anxiety-related factors may modulate MMN responses, leading to increased neural reactivity in more severe cases. Alternatively, these patterns may reflect compensatory neural processes or heightened sensitivity to auditory change. The MMN difference waveform (Fig. 2, right panel) further supports this interpretation, demonstrating a substantially attenuated negativity in the stuttering group relative to controls.

Consistent with this perspective, Ioakeimidis et al. [7] reported enhanced MMN amplitudes in individuals with high trait anxiety, reflecting a more reactive mode of auditory change detection. In the present study, higher STAI scores in individuals with severe stuttering may indicate that the observed MMN pattern is partially associated with increased auditory sensitivity or hypervigilance. Taken together, these findings suggest that the relationship between stuttering severity, auditory cortical processing, and anxiety is best conceptualized as interactive and multidimensional rather than unidirectional.

Stuttering and anxiety: ınterpretation of psychometric findings

Elevated state and trait anxiety levels in adults who stutter, relative to controls, are consistent with a substantial body of literature [2, 3, 16]. The strong positive associations between stuttering severity and STAI scores further suggest that anxiety may vary along a severity continuum in stuttering. At the same time, existing evidence suggests that anxiety levels in individuals who stutter are heterogeneous. Bauerly et al. [1] reported that lower self-reported anxiety in some individuals may reflect enhanced self-regulatory capacities. Such findings underscore the importance of considering individual variability and caution against interpreting anxiety as a uniform or unidirectional feature of stuttering.

Although individuals with diagnosed psychiatric disorders were excluded, variability in subclinical anxiety levels was expected and captured using the STAI, which is designed to assess anxiety in non-clinical populations. The very strong correlations observed between ERP amplitudes and anxiety scores should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect shared underlying variance, measurement overlap, or sample-specific characteristics rather than purely independent associations. Additionally, the magnitude of these correlations is higher than typically reported in the literature and therefore warrants cautious interpretation and replication in larger samples.

Toward an ıntegrated ınterpretation

Collectively, the findings of this study point to a pattern of co-variation across auditory cortical processing measures, anxiety levels, and clinical severity in adults who stutter. ERP parameters appeared to exhibit a pattern of gradation across severity levels, supporting a dimensional interpretation in which neurophysiological and psychological measures co-vary along a continuum of stuttering severity. Rather than reflecting independent effects, these measures appear to change in a pattern of co-variation.

Nevertheless, due to the cross-sectional design, the directionality of these relationships cannot be established. Whether auditory cortical processing differences are associated with stuttering severity, reflect long-term speech dysfluency, or relate to anxiety-related processes remains an open question. Addressing these issues will require longitudinal and intervention-based studies capable of disentangling temporal and causal mechanisms.

Lımıtatıons and future dırectıons

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample consisted exclusively of adults aged 18–45 years, which limits the generalizability of the findings to childhood and older age periods. Future studies should examine auditory cortical processing profiles and their relationship with anxiety across different developmental stages of stuttering.

Second, only pure-tone stimuli were used in the auditory paradigms. Given the relevance of speech-specific sensory–motor integration processes in stuttering, future research employing phoneme- or syllable-based stimulation paradigms may provide more detailed and ecologically valid insights into auditory processing differences.

Third, individuals with a clinical diagnosis of anxiety disorders were not included. Consequently, it remains unclear whether auditory cortical responses differ in subgroups of individuals who stutter with comorbid clinical anxiety. This represents an important direction for future investigation.

Fourth, psychiatric screening was based on self-report and medical history rather than structured clinical interviews, which may limit the precision of psychiatric exclusion.

Fifth, raw EEG data were not retained following feature extraction, which restricts the ability to reanalyze signal-level characteristics and assess variability in greater detail. Future studies should aim to retain full EEG datasets to enhance reproducibility and allow more advanced signal-level analyses.

Sixth, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied, which may increase the risk of type I error and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Finally, the unusually large effect sizes and correlation coefficients observed in this study should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect sample-specific characteristics or shared variance between measures.

Independent replication studies using larger and more heterogeneous samples, together with retention of full signal-level EEG data and greater emphasis on individual variability patterns, would further strengthen the robustness and generalizability of the present findings.

In addition, due to the cross-sectional design of the study, the observed associations among auditory cortical processing, anxiety levels, and stuttering severity cannot be interpreted causally. Longitudinal and intervention-based studies are needed to clarify the directionality and temporal dynamics of these relationships. Further exploration of the interaction between sensory processing mechanisms and anxiety in stuttering also represents a promising avenue for future research.

Conclusıons

This study suggests that adults who stutter may exhibit differences in both early (P1, N1) and later (P2, MMN) stages of auditory cortical processing. These neurophysiological characteristics appeared to vary across stuttering severity levels and were accompanied by patterns of change in anxiety measures. Although group differences were associated with large standardized effects, these findings should be interpreted in the context of a carefully defined sample and limited overlap across clinical severity levels.

Overall, the results suggest that stuttering may not be fully explained as a disorder restricted to motor speech production alone, but may also involve auditory processing mechanisms and emotional factors. From this perspective, motor, sensory, and emotional processes may function as interacting components rather than entirely independent contributors to stuttering.

In conclusion, the present findings highlight the potential importance of jointly considering neurophysiological and psychological dimensions in the assessment of stuttering and may support the need for individualized, integrative approaches in both evaluation and intervention. Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional nature of the study, further longitudinal and intervention-based research is required to clarify the direction and underlying mechanisms of the observed patterns.
