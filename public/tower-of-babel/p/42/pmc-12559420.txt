Assembly of dynamic helical β-peptides with switchable handedness into multiple types of metal-peptide frameworks
Authors: Ingyu Han, Ha-Jin Lee, Ilia A Guzei, Soo Hyuk Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetal-directed assemblies of helical peptides are emerging chiral biomimetic materials, yet dynamic helical peptides remain underexplored. Here we report the coordination-driven assembly of dynamic 12/10-helical β-pentapeptides into distinct metal–peptide frameworks (MPFs) with silver ions. Ligand 1, consisting of cis-2-aminocycloheptanecarboxylic acid with alternating chirality, exhibits solvent-dependent screw-sense preference: (P)-helix in protic and (M)-helix in aprotic solvents. The two helical conformers are not enantiomers and assemble into two distinct MPFs (Ag-1M and Ag-1P), while a racemic ligand mixture (1 and ent-1) yields a third MPF (Ag-rac-1PM). A series of modified ligands (2-5), incorporating various central residues, form MPFs analogous to Ag-1M or Ag-1P, with pore environments modulated by side chains. Particularly, an MPF incorporating polar N-acetyl azepane moieties (Ag-5M) selectively recognizes a chiral guest through post-crystallization. These results highlight dynamic helical β-peptides as versatile building blocks for diverse chiral MPFs, with tunable pore environments through rational peptide design.Subject terms: Organic molecules in materials science, Self-assembly, Peptides

Introduction

Helices are the most abundant secondary structures in both natural proteins and synthetic peptides1–3. Helical peptides have been popular building blocks for designing hierarchical assemblies4. The atomic-resolution crystal structures are available for several types of helical backbones, enabling the precise arrangement of side chain groups along the helical axis. The self-assembly of helical peptides can be engineered by disposing functional groups for several noncovalent interactions5. Additionally, incorporation of metal-binding moieties could result in metal-coordination-driven helical peptide assemblies6,7.

Constructing high-order structures of metal-peptide assemblies8–17, also known as metal-peptide networks18 and metal-peptide frameworks19 is a topic of significant interest for potential applications, including chiral recognition and separation20,21, proteomimetics22, stimuli-responsive materials23, and catalysis24. Helical peptides have drawn particular attention as building blocks for porous or channel-like metal-peptide frameworks25–31 that are alternatively termed metal-helix frameworks (MHFs)32. In contrast to conventional approaches with organic ligands to construct porous architectures such as metal-organic frameworks (MOFs), covalent-organic frameworks (COFs), or hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks (HOFs), helical peptide ligands offer highly chiral environments within MHFs through chiral residues and helical handedness. Furthermore, the construction of a mirror-image MHF can be achieved by incorporating enantiomeric peptide ligands.

Helical peptide ligands used in known MHFs commonly consist of homochiral residues and adopt a single handedness governed by the chirality of the backbone carbon atoms. Interactions with external species such as neighboring peptides, solvent molecules and metal ions in the self-assembly process could change the helical propensity of the peptides, but their handedness generally would not switch. In contrast, certain types of peptides in nature consist of both L- and D-α-amino acid residues with alternating chirality, termed DL-peptides, and can adopt several types of helical conformations with either handedness33–40. Gramicidin A is a representative DL-peptide displaying various conformations, such as a double-stranded, antiparallel β-helix in organic solvents and a single-stranded helical dimer as a transmembrane channel33. Model DL-peptides containing more than seven residues show similar conformational behaviors including interchangeable handedness38,39. Likewise, unnatural β-peptides that consist of the alternation of two enantiomeric β-amino acid residues could adopt both right- and left-handed 12/10-helical conformations41–43.

These dynamic helical peptides containing heterochiral residues could serve as versatile building blocks to generate diverse self-assembled structures, as exemplified in the case of natural gramicidin A dimers36. Promoting a specific helical conformation of a dynamic helical peptide can lead to the formation of a distinct MHF, generating a variety of MHF structures from the accessible helical conformers of a single peptide sequence. However, most heterochiral peptide helices have not been explored in the area of peptide-based structures, presumably because crystallization of a specific helix out of several equilibrated helices is deemed extremely difficult. In this regard, we envisioned that the 12/10-helical β-peptides with switchable handedness are strong candidates for overcoming the issue of conformational control and for assembling into various peptide-based structures. The crystal structures for both right- and left-handed 12/10-helices are available for the design41,43, highlighting that the two opposite-handed 12/10-helices are not enantiomeric unlike conventional achiral peptide helices44. Therefore, dynamic conformational control of 12/10-helical β-peptides may result in two distinct assembled structures. Construction of MHFs from these dynamic helical peptides is desirable for creating “handedness-tunable” pore environments and understanding the dynamic conformational behaviors of the peptide ligands during the metal-driven assembly.

Additionally, the rigid 12/10-helical backbone enables the rational design of MHFs, a strategy that has rarely been realized to date. The design and the synthesis 12/10-helical peptide ligands with diverse side chains can be relatively straightforward. A key challenge remains in understanding how ordered architectures emerge from these modified helical ligands, particularly in elucidating the relationship between helical handedness and the resulting supramolecular structures. The ability to reproducibly construct well-defined architectures from diverse 12/10-helical ligands provides a solid foundation for establishing general design principles for MHFs and advancing their functional applications.

Here we report the construction of two distinct MHFs from the same 12/10-helical β-pentapeptide by adjusting the solvent conditions to control its helical handedness. The non-enantiomeric, (M)- and (P)-helical conformers of the β-pentapeptide ligand exhibit different nonbonding interactions and were assembled into the two distinct MHFs with silver(I) ions, respectively. A mirror-image structure for one of the MHFs was achieved by employing the enantiomeric β-peptide ligand. A racemic mixture of the β-peptide ligand assembled into a third MHF containing both right- and left-handed helical ligands. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the assembled frameworks can serve as tunable platforms for specific functions thorugh rational modification of the peptide ligand. In this regard, we show that the porous MHF bearing a polar side chain inside chiral pores can perform chiral recognition by selectively capturing one enantiomeric guest from a racemic mixture during post-crystallization.

Results

Design and conformational analysis of peptide ligand

12/10-Helical β-peptides consisting of cyclic β-amino acid residues are potential ligand candidates for MHF formation because of the accessible crystal structures and dynamic folding propensity. We have previously reported the crystal structures of 12/10-helical β-peptides and demonstrated that oligomers consisting of cis-2-aminocyclohexanecarboxylic acid (cis-ACHC) and cis-2-aminocyloheptanecarboxylic acid (cis-ACHpC) with alternating chirality display both right- and left-handed 12/10-helices that equilibrate in solution41,43. The ratio between the two helical conformers varies depending on solvent conditions and the terminal capping groups, suggesting that proper combination of solvent conditions and metal-binding capping groups enables dynamic control of the helical handedness. In addition, a longer peptide ligand containing more than three residues is desirable, considering that the majority of the precedent MHFs are composed of silver ions and tripeptide ligands spanning one helical turn13,25–30. Employing longer helical peptides into MHFs could provide higher opportunity of constructing larger and tunable pores decorated with chiral side chain groups. We designed 12/10-helical β-pentapeptide ligand 1, reflecting these desirable factors (Fig. 1). The β-pentapeptide consists of the alternation of the two enantiomers of cis-ACHpC, which can undergo rapid helix inversion by ring flip of the cycloheptane moiety43. The 4-pyridyl moieties are introduced at both termini as metal-binding sites. The right- (P)- and left- (M)−12/10-helices of 1 encompass two helical turns involving four intramolecular hydrogen bonds (Fig. 1a). Two pairs of the NH and CO groups in each helix remain non-hydrogen-bonded (highlighted in green shades) and could join intermolecular interactions with external entities. Therefore, the two opposite-handed helices are not enantiomeric because the positions of those non-hydrogen-bonded groups differ.

Before attempting metal coordination, we analyzed the conformational behavior of 1 in solution using circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy (Fig. 1b). The CD signatures are dependent on the solvent type: a positive maximum at about 203–205 nm in protic alcohols (methanol and n-propanol) and a negative maximum at 205 nm in aprotic acetonitrile. These results suggest that 1 prefers right-handed, (P)−12/10-helical conformations in protic solvents, whereas left-handed, (M)−12/10-helices are predominant in aprotic solvents. Additional CD spectra in mixtures of protic and aprotic solvents imply that the two opposite-handed helices are in equilibrium with various ratios (Supplementary Fig. 22). We further investigated the solid-state conformation of the β-peptide using single-crystal X-ray crystallography. Crystals of 1 were grown from 1,2-dichloroethane via slow cooling. The crystal structure of 1 displays an (M)−12/10-helical conformation, consistent with the preferred handedness in aprotic solvent conditions for crystallization (Fig. 1c). Given the solvent-dependent helical preference and non-enantiomeric nature of the two opposite-handed helical conformers, we anticipated that 1 can behave as an ambidextrous ligand, inducing two distinct metal-coordinated architectures by controlling the favored handedness in crystallizing solvent conditions45.

Metal-helix framework assembled from the (M)-helical conformer of 1

We then attempted the triple-layering method to crystallize metal ion-coordinated 12/10-helical β-pentapeptide25,26. The crystal growth involves three solution layers in a microtube: the solution of a peptide ligand at the top, a solution containing metal ions at the bottom, and a buffer solution in the middle. Diverse solvent combinations were screened for the three solutions.

The Ag(I)-coordinated MHF from (M)-helical 1 (Ag-1M) was crystallized by layering the solution of 1 in methanol over AgBF4 in nitromethane for a week. The asymmetric unit contains one molecule of 1, which adopts a (M)−12/10-helical conformation fully encompassing two helical turns (Fig. 2a). Considering that the (M)−12/10-helix is not favored in protic solvent conditions for crystal growth, this result is attributed to the adaptive binding of the dynamic helical ligand to silver ions during metal-directed self-assembly. The two 4-pyridyl groups at both termini of 1 are aligned to face the opposite directions along the a axis and linked to silver atoms with the 4-pyr-Ag-4-pyr angle of 175°. Interestingly, the helical axes of all the helical ligands are on the ab plane. Therefore, all the one-dimensional coordination polymer chains are arranged on the ab plane and propagate along the a axis (Fig. 2b). Two adjacent coordination chains are interlocked near silver atoms by several nonbonding interactions: argentophilic interaction, π–π interaction, and intermolecular hydrogen bonding (Fig. 2c). The distance between the two adjacent silver atoms is 3.22 Å. The two 4-pyridyl groups bound to one silver atom are arranged in parallel to those linked to the adjoining silver atom by the distances of 3.47 Å and 3.69 Å, respectively. These results strongly suggest the existence of argentophilic interactions supported by the π-π interactions46. An intermolecular hydrogen bond between the groups near the N-termini of helical ligands in two coordination polymer chains further strengthens interlocking adjacent chains. These interactions at all the silver ion-coordination sites result in a two-dimensional layer with hexagonal pores on the ab plane, in which the two adjacent coordination polymers can be overlapped by flipping one chain with respect to the a axis. The longest distance in a hexagonal pore is about 26 Å and the shortest distance is about 10 Å. Hydrophobic contact between the cycloheptane moieties in adjacent helices contributes to the crystal packing along the c axis, displaying the honeycomb-like channels along the c axis (Fig. 2d). The counterions (BF4−) are located close to silver ions and do not block the channels. There are another channel-like pores along the b axis with a diameter of about 7 Å (Fig. 2e). As the bc plane is tightly packed by 1 with no solvent accessible pore, the structure of Ag-1M displays porous channels oriented along both the b and the c axes.

a Molecular conformation of 1 viewed perpendicular (top) and along the helical axis (bottom). b Two adjacent coordination polymer chains. c Coordination layer with hexagonal pores. d, e Crystal packing displaying porous channels viewed along the c axis and the b axis.

Additionally, we investigated the effect of the counter-anion on the assembled structure by using AgPF6 instead of AgBF4 during crystallization. The resulting MHF containing PF6- (Ag-1M-PF6, Supplementary Figs. 28-29) was obtained under the identical solvent conditions as Ag-1M. No structural difference between the two MHFs were observed. Tetrafluoroborate ions in Ag-1M were simply replaced by the hexafluorophosphate ions in Ag-1M-PF6. These results indicate that the formation of Ag-1M is primarily governed by the conformation of 1, with minimal impact from the counter-anion.

Metal-helix framework assembled from the (P)-Helical conformer of 1

After the discovery of Ag-1M, assembled from the (M)-helical conformer of 1, we hypothesized that an alternative MHF could be assembled from the (P)-helical conformer of 1 by modifying crystallization conditions. Through screening different combinations of protic solvents and counterions, we successfully characterized the crystal structure of the second Ag(I)-coordinated MHF, formed from the (P)-helical conformer of 1 (Ag-1P) (Fig. 3a). Crystals of Ag-1P were grown by layering the solution of 1 in tert-butanol over aqueous AgBF4 and AgPF6 for three days. The buffer solution was a mixture of nitromethane and tert-butanol. The solution layers were used in a 1:0.5:1 volume ratio for the ligand solution, buffer solution, and aqueous silver ions, respectively.

Crystal structure of MHF assembled from the (P)-helical conformer of 1 (Ag-1P).

a Molecular conformation of 1 viewed perpendicular (top) and along the helical axis (bottom). b Two intertwined coordination polymer chains displaying the right-handed, antiparallel double helix. The yellow line indicates the imaginary double helical axis. c Double-helical coordination polymer pair viewed along the a, b and c axes. d Porous coordination layer on the bc plane. e, f Crystal packing viewed along the a axis and the b axis.

The crystal structure of Ag-1P displays a (P)−12/10-helical conformation of 1, with the two 4-pyridyl groups facing the opposite directions similar to (M)−12/10-helical 1 (Fig. 3a). However, non-enantiomeric nature of (P)−12/10-helical 1 appears to induce a strikingly different metal-directed assembly of Ag-1P compared with Ag-1M, through the incorporation of different nonbonding interactions. The helical axes of the peptide ligands in Ag-1P are not in the same plane. There are no argentophilic interactions between silver ions, nor any intermolecular hydrogen bonds with other peptides between the terminal groups of the ligands. We speculated that water molecules between the pairs of non-hydrogen bonded amides prevented nonbonding interactions around the adjacent silver ions, unlike those observed for Ag-1M. The two adjacent Ag(I)-coordinated polymer chains of (P)−12/10-helical 1 intertwine along the c axis to adopt right-handed, antiparallel double helices (Fig. 3b), resulting in two types of porous structures when viewed along the a and b axes (Fig. 3c). These double helices are then linked through hexafluorophosphate ion bridges between two silver ions along the b axis, generating a two-dimensional porous layer on the bc plane (Fig. 3d). Adjacent porous layers are tightly packed along the a axis, assembling into the formation of channel-like structures with circular pores (Fig. 3e). The pore diameter is about 16 Å. The crystal packing generates additional channel-like structures along the b axis (Fig. 3f). The corresponding pore size (13 Å) is slightly smaller than that along the a axis. This highly porous architecture of Ag-1P is attributed to the rigidity of the double-helical coordination polymer chains and their tight packing via hydrophobic interactions.

Metal-helix framework with the enantiomer of 1 adopting a (M)-helical conformer

Constructing the mirror image of an assembled structure using enantiomeric molecules is a generally accepted strategy as exemplied in chiral metal-organic frameworks and D-proteins47,48. Enantiomers of chiral organic ligands and D-α-amino acids are typically used for those purposes. Similarly, we synthesized the enantiomeric 12/10-helical β-pentapeptide ligand (ent-1) to construct the mirror image structure of the MHF with enantiomeric pore environments. CD spectroscopic analysis revealed that ent-1 exhibited exactly opposite conformational behaviors compared to 1, displaying the solvent-dependent preference for the opposite handedness (Supplementary Fig. 23). We chose Ag-1P as the candidate structure for the MHF from ent-1 because Ag-1P exhibits higher reproducibility during crystallization compared to Ag-1M. Using the same crystallization condition as Ag-1P, we obtained the expected mirror image structure (Ag-ent-1M), where ent-1 adopts the enantiomeric (M)-helical conformation (Supplementary Figs. 34–36). This result confirms the high reproducibility and tunable chirality of these enantiomeric MHFs.

Metal-helix framework assembled from racemic mixtures of 1 and ent-1

Racemic crystallography is an efficient approach for crystallizing peptides, proteins and certain molecules with lower crystallinity49–51. However, employing a racemic mixture in metal-peptide assemblies is rare and remains underexplored. In this regard, we further investigated the construction of a hybrid MHF using a racemic mixture of 1 and ent-1 (rac-1). Using the same solvent conditions as Ag-1P, rac-1 successfully crystallized with silver ions to form another distinct MHF (Ag-rac-1PM). However, the concentration of each enantiomeric ligand and silver ion solution had to be reduced by half, as the improved crystal packing led to rapid crystal growth unsuitable for crystallographic analysis. The space group changed from enantiomorphic I222 to centrosymmetric Pnnm, which is a common consequence of racemic crystallography. Two enantiomeric β-pentapeptide ligands 1 and ent-1 adopt (P)−12/10- and (M)−12/10-helical conformations in the crystal state, respectively (Fig. 4a). These ligands alternate within one-dimensional coordiantion polymer chains. Pairs of adjacent coordination polymer chains are cross-linked via hexafluorophophate bridges between silver atoms, forming a porous layer along the c axis (Fig. 4b). Two distinct spatial arrangements exist for these porous layers (Fig. 4c). One layer (purple) is tilted clockwise from the ac plane by about 30°, while the other layer (violet) is tilted counterclockwise by about 30° (Fig. 4c). These adjacent layers are cross-linked via additional hexafluorophosphate bridges between silver atoms. This unit of the two cross-linked layers create two distinct porous structures when viewed along the a and the b axes (Fig. 4d). Crystal packing of these units along the a and the b axes eventually results in a three-dimensional porous architecture with two types of channel-like structures (Fig. 4e).

a Molecular conformations of 1 and ent-1 viewed perpendicular (top) and along the helical axis (bottom). b Coordination layer formed from two coordination chains along the c axis. c, d Three-dimensional arrangement of the two interlocking coordination layers and the corresponding porous structures viewed along the a and b axes. e Crystal packing viewed along the a axis and the b axis. f Comparison of the three pore types: Ag-1P (cyan), Ag-ent-1M (green) and Ag-rac-1PM (magenta). The imaginary mirror plane and rotation axes are shown.

Interestingly, the assembled structure of Ag-rac-1PM closely resembles those of Ag-1P and Ag-ent-1M, although the former involves racemates and the latter two contain only single-enantiomeric ligands (Fig. 4f). In Ag-1P, (P)-helical ligand 1 assembles into one-dimensional, (P)-helical coordination polymers, two of which intertwine to form an ordered, (P)-double-helix, facilitated by their matching handedness. A similar (M)-double-helix is observed for Ag-ent-1M, formed from the two (M)-helical coordination polymers of ent-1. However, in racemic Ag-rac-1PM, coordination polymer chains of (P)-helical 1 and (M)-helical ent-1 cannot intertwine in the same manner because of their opposite handedness, which leads to structural incompatibility in three-dimensional assembly. Consequently, the formation of homochiral coordination chains of 1 or ent-1 is disfavored, and instead, heterochiral coordination chains consisting of alternating 1 and ent-1 are formed from the racemate. These heterochiral chains assemble into a framework structurally similar to those constructed from homochiral coordination chains.

Nevertheless, the pore environments in the three MPFs differ depending on ligand chirality. The two enantiomeric MHFs (Ag-1P and Ag-ent-1M) display C2-symmetric arrangements of homochiral ligands with a single handedness about the center of each pore. In contrast, each pore of Ag-rac-1PM possesses a plane of symmetry because of the symmetric alignment of enantiomeric ligands. This symmetric arrangement results in a slightly larger pore size viewed along the a axis in Ag-rac-1 (17 Å) compared to Ag-1P and Ag-ent-1M (16 Å). These results suggest that the chiral environment of pore surfaces in a given MHF architecture can be modulated using the 12/10-helical β-peptide ligands, providing a platform for chiral and porous materials with tunable surfaces.

Selective reconstitution of predefined MHF architectures via handedness control

To enable functional applications of the MHFs described above, it is essential to reproducibly construct their architectures using modified 12/10-helical β-peptide ligands capable of incorporating functional side chains. The type of the MHF can be directed by the helical handedness of the ligand. To this end, we strategically modified 1 by replacing the central residue with a series of cyclohexane-based β-amino acids. The crystal structures of Ag-1M and Ag-1P indicate that replacing the central residue of 1 serves as an ideal site for pore surface design, as it does not engage in hydrophobic interactions with adjacent ligand molecules and may interact with solvent molecules within the pore, as supported by the highly disordered cycloheptane moiety.

We first replaced the central cis-ACHpC residue in 1 with cis-ACHC, which also supports 12/10-helical folding in both handedness41. The resulting ligand 2 displays 12/10-helical folding with solvent-dependent screw-sense preference similar to 1 (Fig. 5a). To achieve residue-dependent screw-sense control, we further synthesized ligands 3 and 4 containing 4-methyl-substituted cis-ACHC analogs, cis,cis-mACHC and cis,trans-mACHC, respectively43. The 4-methyl substituent locks the cyclohexane moiety, preventing ring flipping and promoting a defined helical handedness. The CD spectra of 3 and 4 are consistent with single-handed 12/10-helices irrespective of solvent: the (M)-helix for 3 and the (P)-helix for 4 (Fig. 5b-c).

a–c Structures and CD spectra of 2-4. d Molecular conformations of (P)-helical 2 (left) and porous coordination layers of Ag-2P (right); the cyclohexane moiety in 2 is highlighted in blue. e Molecular conformations of (M)-helical 3 (left) and a coordination layer of Ag-3M (right). f Superposition of coordination layers for Ag-3M (orange) and Ag-1M (yellow).

All three modified ligands (2-4) were coordinated with silver ions under the same crystallization conditions as for Ag-1M or Ag-1P. Dynamic helical ligand 2 assembled into Ag-2P, which is structurally analogous to Ag-1P, with cis-ACHC replacing the central cis-ACHpC residue of 1 (Fig. 5d). The MHF formed from (M)−12/10-helical ligand 3 (Ag-3M) closely resembles Ag-1M, with nearly identical noncovalent interactions (Fig. 5e). Structural overlay of Ag-3M and Ag-1M confirms that the central cis-ACHpC of 1 can be replaced with cis,cis-mACHC of 3 without alteration of the overall framework (Fig. 5f). Similarly, the MHF from (P)−12/10-helical 4 (Ag-4P) is comparable to Ag-1P and Ag-2P, although weak diffraction limited detailed structural analysis (Supplementary Figs. 43-45).

These results demonstrate that the two MHF types (Ag-1M and Ag-1P) can be reproducibly reconstructed through ligand modification and controlled by helical handedness of the ligand. Incorporation of single-handed ligands 3 and 4 not only enables the introduction of functional side chains, but also allows controlled assembly of MHFs, establishing a foundation for tuning both MHF type and pore environment.

MHFs as porous materials for chiral recognition

To explore the utility of our MHFs as porous materials, we assessed their capacity to accommodate guest molecules. However, we encountered key limitations when attempting to apply the MHFs as conventional MOF-type porous hosts. Ag-1M showed insufficient reproducibility in crystallization, while Ag-1P exhibited low chemical and structural stability upon solvent removal (Supplementary Figs. 55–57). We therefore turned to the crystalline sponge (CS) approach, which does not rely on permanent porosity but instead utilizes pre-formed crystalline hosts for guest encapsulation and crystallographic characterization52–55. Given the intrinsic chirality, structural flexibility, and accessible pore dimensions of our MHFs, we identified them as promising CS platforms, particularly for chiral guest recognition, where chirality transfer and host-guest complementarity are crucial56. This strategy is inspired by the pioneering work of Fujita and coworkers52, and allows us to leverage the unique features of our system beyond conventional porous material under dry conditions.

Because 1 contains only hydrophobic residues and lacks specific side chain functionalities, the cavities of Ag-1M and Ag-1P do not feature hydrophilic or electron-deficient moieties, which are key elements for organizing guest molecules into periodic arrays within the framework. To address this, we designed ligand 5 by replacing the central cis-ACHpC of 1 with an N-acetyl analog of cis-5-aminoazepane-4-carboxylic acid (cis-AAzC), which enhances hydrophilicity of the ligands and enables additional functionalization at the azepane moiety without disrupting 12/10-helical folding (Fig. 6a)57. CD spectra suggest that (M)-12/10-helical conformations prevail for 5 in both aprotic and protic solvents. The metal-directed assembly of 5 with silver ions (Ag-5M) aligns with this preferred handedness, resulting in the same MHF type as Ag-1M (Fig. 6b). All noncovalent interactions observed in Ag-1M are retained in Ag-5M, whose hydrophobic pore surfaces are now decorated with polar N-acetyl groups. These results reinforce that the MHF architectures can be reliably directed by the helical handedness of β-pentapeptide ligands following functional modification.

To evaluate Ag-5M as a crystalline sponge for post-crystallization guest uptake, we removed the mother liquor from the vial containing Ag-5M crystals using syringe and then added several drops of racemic 3-methylcyclohexanone (3MCH) as liquid guest molecules. After three hours, the crystals were analyzed by single-crystal X-ray crystallography. Remarkably, only (3R)-methylcyclohexanone (3RMCH) was encapsulated within the pores (Ag-5M-3RMCH, Fig. 6c). This selective post-crystallization of 3RMCH occurred without conventional activation procedures for the CS method and allowed the refinement of the chiral guest molecule with 70% chemical occupancy. To understand this chiral recognition, we examined the position of the (3R)-methyl group, which resides within the helical grooves of the pore surface (Fig. 6d). These grooves provide a well-matched environment for 3RMCH, allowing selective inclusion and periodic arrangement within the pore, whereas the enantiomeric (3S)-methylcyclohexanone (3SMCH) was excluded.

To further validate the chiral recognition capability of the MHF, we synthesized the enantiomeric ligand ent-5 and successfully obtained the mirror-image MHF (Ag-ent-5P) under the same crystallization condition (Fig. 6e). Upon post-crystallization with racemic 3MCH, only 3SMCH was encapsulated within the MHF (Ag-ent-5P-3SMCH, Fig. 6f). As in the previous case, the helical grooves of ent-5 formed a well-matched array compatible exclusively with 3SMCH (Fig. 6g). Although these experiments are at a fundamental stage, the results strongly support the guest-selective nature of the peptide-based porous materials and establish MHFs as viable platforms for chirality-recognizing crystalline sponges.

Discussion

We have demonstrated that 12/10-helical β-pentapeptide 1 can be assembled into two porous MHFs with silver ions by controlling the helical handedness of the dynamic peptide ligand. The two MHFs (Ag-1M and Ag-1P) from 1 support our expectation that dynamic helical peptides are versatile blocks inducing diverse assembled structures. In addition, we have shown that racemic crystallography serves as an efficient methodology for achieving structural diversity in the formation of MHFs, leading to the formation of a third MHF (Ag-rac-1PM).

The structural difference between Ag-1P and Ag-1M is attributed to the non-enantiomeric nature of the two opposite-handed 12/10-helical conformers of 1. Most previous examples of dynamic helical peptides with switchable handedness commonly consist of achiral building blocks, such as α-aminoisobutyric acid and α,β-dehydroamino acids44. The (P)- and (M)-helical conformations in an achiral sequence are enantiomeric and often lead to racemic crystals containing two enantiomeric metal-peptide assemblies58. In contrast, the two non-enantiomeric 12/10-helical conformers of 1 exhibit different noncovalent interactions at both termini, as described above, resulting in two distinct MHFs, each composed of a single-handed conformer of 1.

Some short and flexible peptide ligands are known to assemble into multiple metal-peptide architectures that can be modulated by altering counter-anions during crystallization58. However, the β-pentapeptide ligands in this study are relatively rigid and occupy a limited conformational space, adopting only two opposite-handed 12/10-helices spanning two helical turns. Therefore, variations in counter-anions do not affect the overall MHF architecture, as exemplified by Ag-1M, where substituting BF4- with PF6- in crystallization condition leads no structural change. Modification of the helical ligand by substituting the central residue preserves the helical propensity but changes the screw-sense preference, which is the determinant to the resulting MHF types, as shown in Ag-2P, Ag-3M, and Ag-4P. These results strongly suggest that both types of MHFs originally formed from dynamic ligand 1 can be reliably reconstituted using modified ligands, enabling the rational design of functional MHFs. Substitution at non-central residues in 1 may likewise alter the screw-sense preference59, potentially yielding either MHF architecture with more diverse pore environments.

As a potential application of these MHFs, we demonstrate that a modified MHF (Ag-5M) functions as a crystalline sponge, selectively recognizing a chiral guest through post-crystallization with a racemic mixture without conventional activation steps. The MHF structures reported in this study not only serve as porous scaffolds for functional applications but also contribute to a deeper understanding of metal-directed peptide assembly, which remains challenging to predict because of the complexity of noncovalent interactions. Ongoing works focus on incorporating diverse functional side chains into these MHFs to achieve precisely controlled and tunable properties for chiral recognition.

Other reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Samchun Chemical and TCI. All the materials were used as purchased. Analytical thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was performed on a precoated silica gel glass plate (Merck silica gel 60, F254, 0.25 mm). Silica gel Si 60 (230–400 mesh, Intertec) was used for the flash column chromatography. β-Amino acids (1S,2R)-ACHpC, (1R,2S)-ACHpC, (1R, 2S)-ACHC, (1R,2S,4R)-mACHC, (1R,2S,4S)-mACHC, (4S,5R)-AAzC, and (4R,5S)-AAzC were prepared by previously reported methods43,57,60. All the 12/10-Helical β-peptide ligands were prepared by a standard EDCI- or HBTU-mediated coupling method in solution. Mass spectra (MALDI-TOF/TOF) were acquired using an autoflex maX (Bruker). NMR spectra were recorded on a 400 MHz FT-NMR Spectrometer (Bruker Biospin Avance III HD 400).

Peptide synthesis

β-Peptide ligands 1 and ent-1 were synthesized from the two enantiomers of cis-ACHpC (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 4)43. To a solution of cycloheptene (1.0 eq) and anhydrous Na2CO3 (0.15 eq) in dichloromethane, chlorosulfonyl isocyanate (CSI, 1.0 eq) was added dropwise at 0 °C with stirring. The mixture was stirred at 45 °C for 2 d, then quenched with water and diluted with CH2Cl2. Aqueous phosphate buffer containing 25% Na2SO3 was added with vigorous stirring. The mixture was extracted with EtOAc. The combined organic layers were washed with brine, dried over MgSO4, filtered, concentrated in vacuo, and purified by flash column chromatography (hexanes: EtOAc = 1:1). The resulting racemic β-lactam (1.0 eq) in water was added to a flask charged with diisopropyl ether and CAL-B (lipase from Candida Antarctica). The mixture was stirred at 60 °C for 2 d, then filtered and rinsed with hot diisopropyl ether. The filtrate was concentrated in vacuo and recrystallized from EtOAc and hexanes to give the chiral β-lactam, which was treated with aqueous 18% HCl and concentrated in vacuo to afford H-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-OH. The filtrate from recrystallization was concentrated in vacuo and recrystallized from water and acetone to yield H-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-OH. Each enantiomer of H-cis-ACHpC-OH (1.0 eq) in a 1:1 mixture of water and methanol was treated with (Boc)2O (1.01 eq) and Na2CO3 (1.2 eq). The mixture was stirred for 8 h, diluted with EtOAc, washed with brine, dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo to give Boc-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-OH or Boc-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-OH.

To a solution of Boc-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-OH or Boc-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-OH (1.0 eq) in DMF were added HBTU (1.3 eq), HOBt (1.3 eq), DIPEA (2.0 eq), and DMAP (2.0 eq). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 d. Upon completion, the mixture was diluted with EtOAc and washed with 30% aqueous K2CO3. The organic layer was dried over Na2SO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo to give a crude product, which was purified by flash column chromatography (CH2Cl2: EtOAc = 70:30 → 30:70). The resulting compound, capped with the 4-pyridyl group at the C-terminus, was then coupled with the enantiomeric cis-ACHpC using the following procedure. The N-Boc protected peptide (1.0 eq) was treated with 4 M HCl in 1,4-dioxane (ca. 10 eq) at room temperature for 3 h with stirring. The mixture was concentrated under a nitrogen gas stream to yield the corresponding HCl salt, which was dissolved in CH2Cl2 and combined with Boc-ACHpC-OH (1.3 eq), EDCI (1.5 eq), HOBt (1.3 eq), and TEA (2.5 eq). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 d, diluted with EtOAc, and washed with 30% aqueous K2CO3. The organic layer was dried over Na2SO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatography (CH2Cl2: EtOAc = 70:30 → 30:70 → 10:90) to give Boc-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-NH(4-pyr) or Boc-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-NH(4-pyr). This coupling procedure was repeated three additional times with enantiomeric Boc-cis-ACHpC-OH. The resulting N-Boc protected β-pentapeptide was treated with 4 M HCl in 1,4-dioxane (ca. 10 eq) at room temperature for 3 h with stirring. The mixture was concentrated under a nitrogen gas stream to give the corresponding HCl salt, which is dissolved in CH2Cl2 and combined with TEA (2.5 eq), isonicotinic acid (1.3 eq), and EDCI (1.5 eq). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 d, diluted with EtOAc, and washed with 30% aqueous K2CO3. The organic layer was dried over Na2SO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatography using a gradient of CH2Cl2/EtOAc (50:50 → 10:90), followed by elution with 5% methanol in EtOAc. The combined fractions were concentrated in vacuo to give the desired β-pentapeptide (overall yield: 40% in six steps for 1 and 35% in six steps for ent-1).

β-peptide 2 was synthesized from Boc-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-NH(4-pyr) and Boc-(1R,2S)-ACHC-OH (Supplementary Fig. 7)60. A mixture of ethyl 2-oxocyclohexanecarboxylate (1.0 eq), (S)-1-phenylethylamine (1.1 eq), and glacial acetic acid (1.1 eq) in toluene was refluxed for 6 h with continuous removal of water using a Dean-stark trap. Half of toluene was then distilled off. The mixture was diluted with acetic acid and was added with NaBH4 (2.0 eq) portionwise over 2 h at 0 °C with stirring. The mixture was stirred for 2 h at room temperature and diluted with excess water in an ice bath. The cooled mixture was basified with 10 M aqueous NaOH to pH 9.0 and extracted with EtOAc. The combined organic layers were dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo. The crude aminoester was pooled in ether and treated dropwise with HBr in acetic acid (1.0 eq) with stirring. The mixture was stored at room temperature overnight. The precipitate was recrystallized from acetonitrile-ethanol to yield needle-like crystals. A two-necked round-bottom flask was charged with the crystals (1.0 eq), followed by the addition of 10% Pd/C (20 wt%), HCO2NH4 (5.0 eq), and MeOH under a nitrogen gas stream. The mixture was refluxed for 8 h, cooled, filtered through Celite pad, and concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was pooled in aqueous NaHCO3 and extracted with EtOAc. The combined organic layers were dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo to give H-(1R,2S)-ACHC-OEt, which was subsequently converted to Boc-(1R,2S)-ACHC-OEt using a procedure analogous to that employed for Boc-cis-ACHpC-OH, involving treatment with (Boc)2O and triethylamine. To a solution of Boc-(1R,2S)-ACHC-OEt (1.0 eq) in methanol, a solution of LiOH (5.0 eq) in water was added with stirring at 0 °C. The mixture was stirred at 0 °C overnight. After solvent removal by evaporation, the mixture was acidified to pH 1 with 1 M aqueous HCl and extracted with EtOAc. The combined organic layers were washed with brine, dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo to afford Boc-(1R,2S)-ACHC-OH. Boc-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-NH(4-pyr) was sequentially coupled with Boc-(1R,2S)-ACHC-OH, Boc-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-OH, Boc-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-OH, and isonicotinic acid using a standard EDCI-mediated coupling protocol to give 2 (overall yield: 27% in six steps).

Two β-peptides containing a cis-mACHC residue (3 and 4) were synthesized from (1R,2S,4S)-mACHC and (1R,2S,4R)-mACHC, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 16)43. The synthesis began with the reductive amination of racemic ethyl 4-methyl-2-oxocyclohexanecarboxylate (1.0 eq) and (S)-1-phenylethylamine (1.0 eq) following the same procedure used for the synthesis of cis-ACHC. The reaction yielded a mixture of two epimers, which were separated by flash column chromatography using 10% EtOAc in hexane as the eluent. The two isolated epimers were then converted to Boc-(1R,2S,4S)-mACHC-OH and Boc-(1R,2S,4R)-mACHC-OH, respectively, through a sequence of transformations analogous to those used in the synthesis of Boc-cis-ACHC-OH. These steps included hydrogenolysis with HCO2NH4 and 10% Pd/C, Boc protection using (Boc)2O and triethylamine, and saponification with LiOH in methanol and water. Boc-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-NH(4-pyr) was sequentially coupled with Boc-(1R,2S,4S)-mACHC-OH, Boc-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-OH, Boc-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-OH, and isonicotinic acid to give 3 (overall yield: 40% in six steps). Similarly, Boc-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-NH(4-pyr) was coupled with Boc-(1R,2S,4R)-mACHC-OH, Boc-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-OH, Boc-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-OH, and isonicotinic acid to give 4 (overall yield: 28% in six steps).

β-peptide 5 was synthesized from Boc-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-NH(4-pyr) and Cbz-(4R,5S)-AAzC(N-Boc)-OH (Supplementary Fig. 16)57. To a solution of 1-Boc-4-piperidone (1.0 eq) in ether at −78 °C, ethyl diazoacetate (1.5 eq) and boron trifluoride etherate (1.1 eq) were added dropwise. The mixture was stirred for 2 h at −78 °C, then neutralized with 30% aqueous K2CO3, extracted with EtOAc, dried over MgSO4, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo. The resulting β-ketoester (1.0 eq) in 50 mL vial was added with (S)-1-phenylethylamine (1.0 eq) and acetic acid (0.1 eq). The mixture was sonicated at 70 °C for 30 min. A mixture of NaBH4 (2.5 eq) and glacial acetic acid (12.0 eq) was prepared at 0 °C and then added slowly to the reaction vial. The sealed mixture was subject to a second sonication at 70 °C for 30 min. After dilution with EtOAc, the mixture was basified to the pH 10 using 10 M aqueous NaOH and extracted with EtOAc. The combined organic layers were washed with brine, dried over Na2SO4, filtered, concentrated in vacuo, and purified by column chromatography. The resulting aminoester (1.0 eq) in ether was treated with 33% HBr in acetic acid (1.0 eq) at 0 °C with stirring. The mixture was kept at room temperature for 12 h. The yellowish precipitate was filtered and washed with ether to yield the corresponding HBr salt as a white solid. Cbz-(4 R,5S)-AAzC(N-Boc)-OH was prepared via a three-step procedure analogous to that used for Boc-cis-ACHC-OH. Following hydrogenolysis with HCO2NH4 and 10% Pd/C, the debenzylated HBr salt in dichloromethane was added with benzyl chloroformate (1.1 eq) and triethylamine (2.0 eq). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 1 d, diluted with EtOAc, and was washed successively with 10% citric acid, brine, saturated aqueous Na2CO3 and brine. The organic layer was dried over MgSO4, filtered, concentrated in vacuo, and purified by flash column chromatography. Saponification with LiOH in methanol and water afforded Cbz-(4 R,5S)-AAzC(N-Boc)-OH. Boc-(1S,2 R)-ACHpC-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-NH(4-pyr) was coupled sequentially with Cbz-(4R,5S)-AAzC(N-Boc)-OH, Cbz-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-OH, and isonicotinic acid. The resulting β-pentapeptide was treated with 4 M HCl in 1,4-dioxane (ca. 10 eq) for 3 h at room temperature with stirring, then concentrated under a nitrogen gas stream. The resulting HCl salt in dichloromethane was treated with acetic anhydride (2.0 eq) and triethylamine (5.0 eq) with stirring for 8 h, followed by a standard work-up procedure to give 5 (overall yield: 16%). Enantiomeric ent-5 was synthesized using the same procedure from Boc-(1R,2S)-ACHpC-(1S,2R)-ACHpC-NH(4-pyr) and Cbz-(4S,5R)-AAzC(N-Boc)-OH (overall yield: 16%).

Circular dichroism spectroscopy

Circular dichroism spectra were measured using a JASCO-815 spectrometer at 293 K. The 1 mm path length cell was used for the acquisition, and the wavelength range was 190–330 nm. The data interval was 0.1 nm and the bandwidth was 1.0 nm. The acquisition speed was set at 200 nm/min. CD data were acquired from the background of the sample spectrum and smoothed out over 25 data points. The final spectra were normalized to the path length and concentration. The sample concentrations were 0.3 mM for all the solvent conditions.

Crystal growth of 1

Crystals of 1 were grown by slow cooling. Ligand 1 was dissolved in 1,2-dichlorehtane. Subsequently, the mixture was heated. After 1 h of slow cooling, colorless plate-shaped crystals were obtained.

Crystal growth of Ag-1M, Ag-1M-PF6, and Ag-3M

A ligand solution was prepared by dissolving 6.2 mg of 1 or 3 in methanol (50 mM, 0.14 mL). Equal volumes of a mixture of methanol and nitromethane were used as the buffer solution. A silver ion-containing solution was prepared by dissolving AgBF4 in CH3NO2 (50 mM). In a microtube (φ = 5 mm), 0.14 mL of the silver ion-containing solution, 0.14 mL of the buffer solution and 0.14 mL of the ligand solution were layered carefully. The tube was sealed with a Teflon film and covered with aluminum foil. Colorless and block-shaped crystals were obtained after one week. AgPF6 instead of AgBF4 was used for the crystal growth of Ag-1M-PF6.

Crystal growth of Ag-1P and Ag-ent-1M, Ag-2P, and Ag-4P

A ligand solution was prepared by dissolving 2.7 mg of a ligand (1, ent-1, 2, or 4) in tert-butanol (30 mM, 0.10 mL). Equal volumes of a mixture of t-butanol and nitromethane were used as the buffer solution. The silver ion-containing solution was prepared by mixing equal volumes of aqueous AgBF4 and AgPF6 (30 mM each). In a microtube (φ = 5 mm), 0.10 mL of the silver ion-containing solution, 0.05 mL of the buffer solution and 0.10 mL of the ligand solution were layered carefully. The tube was sealed with a Teflon film and covered with aluminum foil. After three days of crystallization, colorless block-shaped crystals were obtained.

Crystal growth of Ag-rac-1PM

The ligand solution was prepared by mixing equal volumes (0.05 mL each) of 1 and ent-1 (15 mM in t-BuOH). The buffer solution was the same as that used for Ag-1P. The silver ion-containing solution was prepared by mixing equal volumes of aqueous AgBF4 and AgPF6 (30 mM each). Crystals of Ag-rac-1PM were grown using the same triple-layering method as that used for Ag-1P and Ag-ent-1M. After two days of crystallization, colorless block-shaped crystals were obtained.

Crystal growth of Ag-5M and Ag-ent-5P

A ligand solution was prepared by dissolving 2.9 mg of 5 or ent-5 in methanol (30 mM, 0.10 mL). Equal volumes of a mixture of methanol and nitromethane were used as the buffer solution. A silver ion-containing solution was prepared by dissolving AgBF4 in water (30 mM). In a 2 mL vial, 0.10 mL of the silver ion-containing solution, 0.10 mL of the buffer solution and 0.10 mL of the ligand solution were layered carefully. The vial was tightly capped and placed inside the dark. Colorless and plate-shaped crystals were obtained after two days.

Post-crystallization of guest molecules inside Ag-5M and Ag-ent-5P

Post-crystallization of the guest molecules within the MHFs was carried out immediately after single-crystal X-ray diffraction data collection. To mount the crystals, the mother liquor of Ag-5M or Ag-ent-5P was removed using a syringe. Several crystals were selected and inspected for data collection under synchrotron radiation. After confirming the collected data (approximately 30 min after data collection), 0.10 mL of racemic 3-methylcyclohexanone was added to the original crystallization vial. After 3 h, the 3-methylcyclohexanone was removed using a syringe, and the crystals were coated with silicon oil for further inspection, without any additional pre-cleaning steps.

Crystallographic data acquisition and structure refinement

The crystal evaluation and data collection of 1 were performed on a Bruker D8 VENTURE Photon III four-circle diffractometer with CuKa (l = 1.54178 Å) radiation at the Molecular Structure Laboratory, University of Wisconsin-Madison. The data for the other crystals (Ag-1M, Ag-1M-PF6, Ag-1P, Ag-ent-1M, Ag-rac-1PM, Ag-1P, Ag-2P, Ag-3M, Ag-5M, Ag-ent-5P, Ag-5M-3RMCH, Ag-ent-5P-3SMCH) were collected at 100(2) K using a synchrotron radiation equipped with PLSII 2D bending magnet and a Rayonix MX225HS CCD area detector in a Pohang Accelerator Laboratory, Korea. Data collection was performed with a radiation wavelength of l = 0.700000 Å, detector distance of 48 mm, omega scan with ∆w = 1°, and the exposure time was 0.1 s per frame. All the non-hydrogen atoms were refined anisotropically, and hydrogen atoms were added to their geometrically ideal positions. The disordered solvent molecules and counter-anions were treated using the SQUEEZE program for better structure refinement. Numerous geometrical constraints and restraints and atomic displacement parameter restraints were used to ensure stable refinement. Backbone torsion angles of the β-pentapeptide ligands along with the summaries of crystal data and structure refinement for each structure are provided in Supplementary Tables 1–28.
