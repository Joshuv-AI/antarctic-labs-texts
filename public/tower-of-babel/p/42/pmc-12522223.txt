Influencing factors for childbirth readiness among pregnant women based on the reciprocal determinism theory and backpropagation neural network: a cross-sectional study in China
Authors: Yuan Mengmei, Zhao Meizhen, Zeng Tieying, Wu Meiliyang, Zhang Ke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildbirth readiness is essential for improving maternal health outcomes and reducing mortality, yet preparedness remains low among pregnant women globally. This study aims to identify key factors influencing childbirth readiness among Chinese women, using reciprocal determinism theory and a backpropagation neural network.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 816 pregnant women from three hospitals in Hubei, China. A structured questionnaire based on reciprocal determinism theory was designed to collect data on individual and environmental factors. Backpropagation neural network modeling and multiple linear regression were used to identify and rank the importance of the factors.ResultsBackpropagation neural network analysis revealed that behavioral intention in childbirth preparation, family care, information support, and fear of birth were the strong predictors of childbirth readiness, with behavioral intention in childbirth preparation being the most significant. Additional influential factors included travel time to the nearest hospital and satisfaction with antenatal care. The backpropagation neural network showed good predictive accuracy.ConclusionThis study innovatively used reciprocal determinism and backpropagation neural network modeling to identify key childbirth readiness factors, highlighting behavioral intention in childbirth preparation, family care, fear of birth, and information support. Integrating intrinsic motivation with extrinsic support enhances maternal readiness and provides strategies for improved antenatal care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08141-7.Keywords: Childbirth readiness, Pregnant women, Neural networks, Prenatal care, Maternal health services, Cross-Sectional studies

Background

Pregnancy and childbirth represent a significant life event for women. However, approximately 289,000 women die annually due to childbirth-related complications, often linked to insufficient preparation [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified childbirth preparedness as a crucial aspect of antenatal care(ANC), emphasizing its significant role in reducing maternal mortality rates [3]. Research has shown that women who are unprepared face higher risks of preterm labour, prolonged labour, obstetric complications, and postpartum haemorrhage [4]. These complications can also affect the foetus, resulting in low birth weight, neonatal asphyxia, and even miscarriage or stillbirth [5, 6]. Beyond physical risks, inadequate preparation contributes to negative birth experiences, increasing the likelihood of postpartum depression, birth trauma, and postpartum traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [7], which can affect subsequent fertility intentions [8].

However, childbirth preparedness remains at a low level worldwide. A study in India found that only 35% of women were well-prepared for labour [9], while in Ethiopia, that number dropped to just 17% [10]. Research from Ghana indicated that even though women understand the importance of being prepared, many still arrive at hospitals unready for childbirth [11]. Studies have shown that women from rural areas, with lower education levels, unemployed, or with lower incomes tend to be less prepared [1, 12]. Conversely, those with a history of negative pregnancy or childbirth, or with pregnancy complications, tend to be more prepared [13]. Most research has focused on these sociodemographic factors, but there has been limited exploration of the psychological aspects that might affect preparedness. Findings also suggested that factors such as a partner’s education level and family relationships were critical in determining childbirth readiness [14, 15]. Furthermore, a study in Iran highlighted that poor quality health services and lack of respect from healthcare providers were also significant barriers to adequate birth preparation [16].

The factors influencing childbirth readiness were complex and multidimensional. Many of the existing studies lacked the guidance of theoretical frameworks, which may result in overlooking some of the critical influences. Reciprocal determinism theory (RDT), developed by psychologist Albert Bandura, provided a valuable model for addressing this gap [17]. RDT suggested that individual behaviour, environmental factors, and personal characteristics interact and influence each other. It also expanded the idea of personal factors to include psychological aspects like beliefs, motivations, and attitudes, not just sociodemographic traits [17]. This theoretical approach benefited the current study in two main ways: First, it allowed for a comprehensive examination of childbirth preparedness by considering both individual and environmental influences. Second, it broadened the understanding of personal factors by highlighting the importance of attitudes and motivations, alongside demographic characteristics.

Guided by the RDT framework, this study systematically incorporated a broad range of factors influencing childbirth readiness by considering both personal and environmental components. At the personal level, in addition to commonly examined socio-demographic and obstetric variables, we introduced behavioral intention in childbirth preparation and fear of birth as key psychological factors. These reflect the cognitive and affective domains emphasized in RDT, where beliefs, motivations, and emotions are understood to shape behavioral outcomes directly. It has been shown that stronger behavioral intention is associated with greater engagement in antenatal preparation [18], while fear of childbirth is a barrier to childbirth preparation [19]. On the environmental side, variables such as family care, information support, and availability of healthcare services were included to reflect the external conditions that can either facilitate or hinder preparedness. Supportive family environments and adequate childbirth information have been found to enhance maternal confidence and planning, whereas limited service accessibility or poor health education may severely undermine women’s childbirth readiness [19, 20].

Therefore, this study aimed to comprehensively explore factors influencing childbirth readiness based on the RDT framework and to identify key predictors, providing a scientific foundation for improving maternal preparedness and strengthening antenatal care services.

Methods

Theoretical framework

This study used Albert Bandura’s reciprocal determinism theory as a theoretical basis for identifying factors influencing childbirth readiness. The rationale for using this theoretical framework was to provide a structural platform for logically understanding the influence and association of multiple factors associated with childbirth readiness levels and to identify potential knowledge gaps and future research directions for policy planners and stakeholders. Based on existing research, we hypothesized that factors influencing childbirth preparation behaviour could be operating at the personal level (socio-demographic factors, obstetric factors, fear of birth, and behavioral intention in childbirth preparation) and the environmental level (family, healthcare services, and social-related factors). Design and participants

This was a descriptive cross-sectional study. The study was conducted from November 2020 to February 2021. Pregnant women were recruited from the Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology of three tertiary hospitals in Hubei province, China, during this period. The study included pregnant women who: (1) were aged 18 years or older; (2) had a gestational age of 32 weeks or more; (3) intended to have a natural birth. Exclusion criteria included women with severe chronic or mental illnesses that may affect their participation in the study, such as cardiovascular diseases, psychiatric disorders, or other severe medical conditions.

Data collection

Data were collected in person at the obstetrics clinic or ward by six investigators, all of whom were postgraduate students from our hospital. Before the survey, a training program was carried out to reduce the variability of investigators’ effects on information gathering. For women who fulfilled the criteria for inclusion, the researchers informed them of the purpose and method of the study and invited them to participate. A total of 903 pregnant women expressed interest in the study. They were given an informed consent form that included details of the study and were invited to complete questionnaires on the spot. In the end, 853 pregnant women participated in the survey.

All data was collected face-to-face. The electronic questionnaires were created on the “questionnaire star” platform, and platform technology was employed to ensure the quality of the collected data, including the identification of invalid responses, mandatory questions, break-off responses, and time spent on the questionnaire. Uniform instructions, filling methods, and requirements were provided. In addition, this study set all the questions as mandatory. Therefore, participants will not be able to submit the questionnaire when there are missing questions, which ensures the completeness of the recovered questionnaire data.

Variables and instruments

In this study, the structured and pretested questionnaire was made up of two parts. Part A collected the dependent variable: childbirth readiness. Part B was designed based on the reciprocal determinism framework to collect information on independent variables: personal factors and environmental factors.

Dependent variable: childbirth readiness

A Childbirth Readiness Scale (CRS), developed by Chinese scholar Yuan et al. in 2022 [21], was used to assess the preparedness of every respondent. The CRS consisted of four dimensions and 18 items: Self-management (4 items), Information literacy (6 items), Birth confidence (4 items), and Birth plan (4 items). Pregnant women were asked to indicate their level of agreement towards the statements using a 5-point Likert scale (5 = strongly agree, 1 = strongly disagree). A higher score indicated a better preparation for birth. The total Cronbach alpha coefficient of the total scale and four dimensions was 0.935 and 0.853–0.914. The split-half reliability is 0.880.

Independent variables: personal factors

Personal influencing factors comprised four aspects, including maternal socio-demographic factors, obstetric factors, fear of birth, and behavioral intention in childbirth preparation.

The socio-demographic factors included age, nationality, marital status, type of residence, household registration location, education level, employment status, payment of medical expenses, household income, and living status.

The obstetric factors contained gestational age, number of pregnancies, parity, number of foetuses, planned pregnancy, negative pregnancy history, high-risk pregnancy, pregnancy complications, abnormal inspection results, and use of labour analgesia.

Fear of birth was assessed by the Fear of Birth Scale (FOBS) screening tool [22]. The FOBS assessed fear of birth using a visual simulation. Subjects were asked the question “How do you feel about your upcoming labour? And responded in two dimensions: anxiety and fear. Two horizontal lines from 0 to 10 centimetres were drawn, with the number 0 indicating no anxiety and no fear, and the number 10 indicating extreme anxiety and extreme fear. The average of the two values was the fear of birth score, and a score greater than 5.4 was considered to be an intense fear of birth. The scale had good internal consistency, with a mean correlation of 0.84. Because of its simplicity of use, it was considered to be the most appropriate instrument for measuring fear of birth in a clinical setting [22].

Four questions measured behavioral intention in childbirth preparation: “I am willing to be proactive in preparing for childbirth”, “I think preparing for childbirth is very important”, “I try to be prepared for childbirth”, and “I am willing to encourage other pregnant women to prepare for birth”. Women were asked to respond to the question with a 5-point Likert scale (5 = strongly agree, 1 = strongly disagree). The level of behavioral intention in childbirth preparation was assessed by a total score of the four questions, ranging from 4 to 20. The higher the score, the stronger women’s behavioral intention in childbirth preparation. The total Cronbach alpha coefficient was 0.914, and the split-half reliability was 0.906 in this study.

Independent variables: environmental factors

Environmental factors included family-related factors, healthcare services-related factors, and social-related factors.

Family-related factors. The education level and occupation of the partner were collected. We also assessed women’s satisfaction with couple and family relationships by asking women questions, “How satisfied are you with the couple relationships?” and “How satisfied are you with the family relationships?”

Family readiness was assessed by asking questions about family accompanying antenatal check-up, family participation in antenatal classes, family knowledge of pregnancy and childbirth, family knowledge of obstetric danger signs, family involvement in women’s childbirth preparation, and partner role adaptation, all with a “yes” or “no” answer.

Family care was assessed by the Family Adaptation Partnership Growth Affection and Resolve Index (Family APGAR) [23], which has been widely used in Chinese pregnant women and proven to be valid and reliable. Family APGAR consisted of five items and reflects five aspects: adaptation, partnership, growth, affection, and resolve. Responses were rated on a 3-point Likert scale, and each item was assigned 0–2 points (almost never = 0, sometimes = 1, usually = 2). The level of family care obtained by pregnant women was assessed with the total score of all items. The total scores ranged from 0 to 10, with 7 to 10 indicating good family function, 4 to 6 indicating moderate family dysfunction, and 0 to 3 indicating severe family dysfunction. The retest reliability of the scale was 0.80 ~ 0.83. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in this study was 0.819.

Healthcare services factors. For healthcare services, we collected information including gestational age at first antenatal visit, timely antenatal check-up, the travel time to the nearest hospital, participation in antenatal classes, and health education during antenatal check-up. We also measured satisfaction with antenatal care provided by asking women a question, “How satisfied are you with the antenatal care services provided?”.

Social related factors. We assessed participants’ social support and information support through questions: “How much social support have you got?” and “How much information support have you got?”. The answers were scored from 1 (inadequate) to 3 (adequate). Besides, we set a multiple-choice question to assess the source of pregnancy-related knowledge.

Data analysis

The collected data were entered into Epidata version 3.1 and analysed using SPSS version 25.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of participants and outcome variables. For continuous variables following a normal distribution, data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For continuous variables not following a normal distribution, data were presented as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. The total childbirth readiness score was a continuous variable with an approximately normal distribution. The variable age in this study was converted from a continuous variable into a categorical variable according to the research purpose. The cut-off age is 35 years, as pregnancies above 35 years are considered to be of advanced maternal age.

Based on the type and distribution of independent variables, the following statistical methods were applied for univariate analysis: (1) For continuous variables (e.g., gestational age, behavioral intention in childbirth preparation, fear of birth, family care), Spearman correlation analysis was used, as these variables exhibited non-linear relationships with the childbirth readiness score; (2) For binary categorical variables (e.g., age, nationality, marital status, type of residency), the childbirth readiness scores were not normally distributed across categories; therefore, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied; (3)For multi-categorical variables (e.g., education level, household income, number of pregnancies, parity), since the childbirth readiness scores did not follow a normal distribution across groups, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test. These non-parametric tests were chosen to ensure appropriate statistical analysis given the nature and distribution of the data. In all analyses, the significance level was set to p < 0.05.

In order to determine the predictors of childbirth readiness, this study employed a backpropagation neural network model, which is a type of feedforward neural network known as a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) based on the error backpropagation algorithm. The purpose of using backpropagation neural network in this study is to complete the extraction of influencing factors’ features in a data-driven manner and autonomously through machine learning, to avoid the interference of human factors to the maximum extent possible, to improve the accuracy and precision of the analysis, and to identify the key influencing factors of childbirth readiness accurately.

A multilayer perceptron with a single hidden layer was implemented based on theoretical evidence that a single hidden layer is sufficient to approximate any continuous function with adequate accuracy, while maintaining simplicity and reducing the risk of overfitting. The model consists of three layers: an input layer, a hidden layer, and an output layer, with each layer containing multiple neurons. Neurons within the same layer are not interconnected, but neurons between adjacent layers are fully connected [24]. The number of neurons in the hidden layer was determined through empirical testing by evaluating multiple configurations ranging from 5 to 15 neurons. The lowest prediction error was observed when using 10 hidden neurons (error = 4.47), which was therefore selected to balance predictive accuracy and model simplicity. Only variables with statistical significance in univariate analysis (p < 0.05) were selected as input features for the neural network, resulting in a total of 23 input variables. This selection was based on the assumption that statistically significant variables are more likely to have meaningful predictive value and improve model efficiency and interpretability.

The childbirth readiness score was set as the dependent variable. A hold-out validation strategy was applied, where the dataset was randomly split into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%). The test set was not involved in training but served to evaluate model performance and generalization accuracy after training. During training, early stopping was applied to reduce the risk of overfitting based on the training loss curve. The basic structure of the backpropagation neural network used in this study is illustrated in Fig. 2 [25, 26]. The weight matrices between the input layer and hidden layer, and between the hidden layer and output layer, are denoted as V and W, respectively.

Backpropagation neural network structure diagram

Before training, all input data were normalized to the [0, 1] range using the mapminmax function in MATLAB to improve convergence efficiency. The backpropagation neural network was configured with a single hidden layer, based on theoretical evidence that one hidden layer is sufficient to approximate continuous functions while maintaining model simplicity. The trainrp (resilient backpropagation) algorithm was used as the training method to address issues of slow convergence and local minima. A log-sigmoid activation function was applied in both the hidden and output layers, given its effectiveness in handling nonlinear relationships in small-to-medium-sized health datasets. The learning rate was set to 0.01, a standard default value in MATLAB and neural network training literature. This rate provided a good balance between convergence speed and training stability without requiring further tuning. Network weights and biases were initialized using MATLAB’s default settings.

To evaluate the relative influence of each input variable on the prediction outcome, we calculated importance scores using the connection weight method. This method estimates variable importance by analyzing the absolute values of the weights connecting each input neuron to the output neuron, via the hidden layer. The resulting raw scores were then normalized so that the most influential variable was assigned a value of 100%, and all others were scaled proportionally. This approach provides an interpretable estimate of each variable’s contribution to the model’s predictions and has been widely applied in backpropagation neural network studies for feature ranking.

In addition to the neural network analysis, this study also examined the influencing factors of childbirth readiness using traditional multiple linear regression analysis. The total childbirth readiness score was set as a dependent variable. Significant variables identified in the univariate analysis (p < 0.05) were included as independent variables. A stepwise method was applied to select the final model. The results of the multiple linear regression analysis were then compared with those obtained from the backpropagation neural network model to enhance the robustness of the findings.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the ethics committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No. TJ-IRB20190614). Informed consent was also obtained from all respondents. They were informed that their anonymity would be guaranteed and that the results obtained would be used only for this study. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Of the 903 women approached, 50 declined to participate, resulting in 853 women signing consent forms and completing the questionnaires - a response rate of 94.5%. A total of 37 questionnaires were excluded: 25 were completed in under six minutes (at least six min to complete according to the pre-experiment), and 12 questionnaires showed an evident pattern in item selection (for example, consistently selecting the same alternative option for all scales), suggesting invalid responses. This left 816 valid questionnaires for analysis.

Participant characteristics and comparative analysis

In terms of personal factors, the participants ranged in age from 18 to 44 years, with a mean of 30.31 years. Most women (83.7%) had attained an undergraduate degree or above. Approximately 80% of participants reported a monthly household income above 5000 RMB, and women from higher-income households demonstrated significantly greater childbirth readiness (p < 0.01). Household registration location was also related; participants with rural registration exhibited lower readiness than their urban counterparts (p < 0.05). Regarding obstetric characteristics, 58.6% of women were primiparous. Multiparity was strongly associated with higher childbirth readiness (p < 0.001). Similarly, the number of pregnancies had a significant relationship with readiness (p < 0.001). The use of labour analgesia was positively associated with better readiness outcomes (p < 0.01). Abnormal inspection results were linked to lower readiness scores (p < 0.05). Moreover, 40.8% of participants reported significant fear of birth, which was inversely correlated with childbirth readiness (p < 0.001). Behavioral intention in childbirth preparation showed a positive association with childbirth readiness (p < 0.001).

Turning to environmental factors, higher satisfaction with couple and family relationships, greater family involvement in antenatal preparation, better partner role adaptation, and stronger family care were all significantly linked to increased childbirth readiness (p < 0.001). In addition, family knowledge of pregnancy and childbirth and family knowledge of obstetric danger signs were associated with a high level of childbirth readiness (p < 0.001). Regarding healthcare services-related factors, participants who could reach the nearest hospital within 20 min demonstrated higher childbirth readiness compared to those who needed longer travel times (p < 0.01). Participation in antenatal classes (p < 0.001), receiving health education during antenatal check-ups (p < 0.001), and higher satisfaction with antenatal care services (p < 0.001) were also positively associated with readiness. For social-related factors, women who perceived higher levels of social support and information support showed significantly greater childbirth readiness scores (p < 0.001).

Multiple linear regression analysis of childbirth readiness

A multiple linear regression analysis identified nine variables that significantly influenced childbirth readiness, together explaining 58.7% of the total variance in the equation (F = 51.284, p < 0.001), as shown in Table 3. Stronger behavioral intention in childbirth preparation, better family care, more information support, higher numbers of pregnancies and parity, better partner role adaptation, higher satisfaction with ANC provided, lower fear of birth, and better family knowledge about pregnancy and childbirth were all positively associated with childbirth readiness.

In this study, a multilayer perceptron neural network algorithm was used to run the algorithm and analyse the importance and predictive accuracy of each influential factor. Significant variables in the univariate analysis were used as input neurons, with the childbirth readiness score serving as the dependent variable. The dataset was randomly assigned to the training set (70.0%, 571 cases) and the test set (30.0%, 245 cases). The relative error of the model for the training set was 0.488, and for the test set, it was 0.388, indicating good predictive performance. The neural network model identified the top five factors influencing childbirth readiness as behavioral intention in childbirth preparation, family care, fear of birth, information support, and travel time to the nearest hospital. The results of multiple linear regression showed that the variables of behavioral intention in childbirth preparation, family care, information support, number of pregnancies, parity, partner role adaptation, satisfaction with ANC provided, fear of birth, and family knowledge about pregnancy and childbirth had a statistically significant effect on readiness for childbirth (F = 51.284, p < 0.001). In addition, the backpropagation neural network model emphasized the importance of travel time to the nearest hospital and social support. The fitting results of the multiple linear regression model and the backpropagation neural network model were close to each other.

The multiple linear regression model showed a good fit with an adjusted R² of 0.587. Meanwhile, the backpropagation neural network model was able to handle nonlinear relationships effectively and provided highly accurate predictions, with factor rankings aligning well with real-world observations. On the whole, both models are suitable for the study of factors affecting childbirth readiness in this study.

Discussion

This study employed reciprocal determinism theory in combination with backpropagation neural network modeling to systematically explore the factors influencing pregnant women’s readiness for childbirth. Within the framework of reciprocal determinism, the backpropagation neural network model identified behavioral intention in childbirth preparation, family care, information support, and fear of birth as the key predictors of childbirth readiness. Among these, behavioral intention and fear of birth represent personal factors, whereas information support and family care are classified as environmental factors.

The results of the study revealed that Chinese pregnant women exhibited notable variations across different dimensions of childbirth readiness. Among these dimensions, self-management scored the highest, likely reflecting the growing public awareness of healthy lifestyles driven by socio-economic development and the widespread implementation of health education campaigns. In contrast, information literacy was comparatively lower, suggesting insufficient preparedness regarding childbirth-specific knowledge. Despite the increasing availability of diverse information channels, pregnant women demonstrated limited proactive engagement in acquiring detailed knowledge—particularly regarding active participation during delivery. This phenomenon may be partly attributed to the hospital-based delivery model in China, where medical professionals typically dominate the birthing process, potentially reducing women’s perceived need to independently seek childbirth-related information. These findings highlight the necessity of targeted interventions aimed at addressing these specific gaps in childbirth education and supporting more comprehensive maternal preparedness.

This study further identified behavioral intention in childbirth preparation as the most significant predictor of childbirth readiness, underscoring its importance in both multiple linear regression and backpropagation neural network analyses. This finding is consistent with evidence from other regions. For example, a community-based cross-sectional study in Southwest Ethiopia by Letose et al. [13] reported that women’s attitudes toward childbirth readiness significantly influenced their preparedness levels. Similarly, a randomized controlled trial conducted in the Netherlands by Friederichs et al. [18] demonstrated that women who received motivational interviewing participated more actively in childbirth preparation classes and exhibited higher levels of readiness compared with those in the control group. Collectively, these findings suggest that healthcare professionals should encourage pregnant women to actively engage in childbirth preparation activities by providing personalized motivational reinforcement, which may lead to greater levels of childbirth readiness.

Conversely, fear of childbirth emerged as a significant negative predictor of childbirth readiness. The backpropagation neural network model further confirmed the detrimental impact of childbirth-related fear. A study by Hassanzadeh et al. [27] indicated that anxiety, depression, and fear during pregnancy can discourage women from participating in childbirth preparation classes, ultimately leading to lower readiness levels. Similarly, Duncan et al. [28] reported that lower antenatal fears were associated with greater childbirth readiness. In the Chinese context, however, discussions of psychological well-being during pregnancy remain relatively limited due to cultural stigmas, which may result in under-recognition of childbirth-related fears and insufficient psychological support for pregnant women [29]. These findings underscore the need to prioritize the psychological well-being of pregnant women in future interventions, with particular emphasis on alleviating childbirth-related fears. Providing emotional support and implementing psychological interventions could enhance mental preparedness, boost confidence, and encourage active engagement in childbirth preparation.

The results of the study indicated that family factors played a crucial role in influencing pregnant women’s birth readiness. Specifically, aspects of family care, adaptation in partner roles, family knowledge of pregnancy and childbirth, and satisfaction with couple and family relationships were significantly associated with higher levels of women’s childbirth readiness. This finding is consistent with previous research across diverse cultural contexts. A cross-sectional study in Pakistan by Ghani [15] showed that women with good family relationships were more likely to attend regular antenatal checkups. Numerous studies have demonstrated that positive family involvement helps reduce maternal stress, promotes healthier behaviors, and contributes to emotional stability during pregnancy [30, 31].

In the context of Chinese culture, where family-centered values are deeply rooted, the involvement of family members, particularly partners and elders, plays an even more prominent role in shaping maternal behaviors and attitudes towards childbirth [32]. However, despite these cultural differences in family dynamics, the beneficial role of family and partner support appears to be universal, likely because it addresses fundamental emotional and practical needs during pregnancy. Therefore, antenatal education programs should not only focus on pregnant women individually but also involve family members, especially partners, in health promotion efforts. Enhancing the awareness and participation of family members can foster a supportive environment that empowers women, improves self-management, builds confidence, and ultimately increases preparedness for childbirth.

Beyond family factors, social support and information support also play a crucial role in preparing for childbirth. Adequate social support can help mothers adapt to new roles, equip them with essential knowledge, enhance self-efficacy, and reduce negative emotions [33, 34]. The importance of information support was also emphasized in the analysis of a backpropagation neural network model. This finding aligns with Guo’s research [35], showing that information support improves maternal self-management and health-promoting behaviors, thereby contributing to higher levels of childbirth readiness. In particular, this underscores the potential of leveraging online resources and social support networks to provide pregnant women with professional, easily accessible information that boosts their confidence in the childbirth process. Notably, in China, the rapid growth of digital platforms and social media has led many pregnant women to rely heavily on online information about childbirth. While this trend has improved access to resources, the variability in information quality presents significant challenges for effective childbirth preparedness. Therefore, integrating reliable online educational resources with formal healthcare guidance should be prioritized to ensure that pregnant women receive accurate, evidence-based information to support their readiness for childbirth.

Our findings indicated that personal and environmental factors were closely interrelated in shaping pregnant women’s readiness for childbirth. Based on the RDT framework, our findings suggest the existence of potential reciprocal influences between personal and environmental factors affecting childbirth readiness. Strong family care may enhance behavioral intention for childbirth preparation by providing emotional reassurance, practical assistance, and positive role modeling, thereby boosting women’s confidence and motivation to prepare for childbirth. Conversely, women with stronger behavioral intentions are more likely to actively engage family members, share childbirth-related information, and encourage supportive behaviors from partners and relatives. Although this study did not directly test such interaction effects, the potential bidirectional relationship is theoretically consistent with Bandura’s reciprocal determinism theory [17]. It is also supported by previous research, which demonstrates that family support enhances self-efficacy and proactive preparation [15], while stronger behavioral intentions are associated with greater utilization of family resources [18]. Future studies should further explore these reciprocal pathways to deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying childbirth readiness.

This study employed both backpropagation neural networks and multiple linear regression analyses to identify the key factors influencing childbirth readiness. Both models highlighted behavioral intention in childbirth preparation, family care, and information support as the primary determinants, while the backpropagation neural network additionally emphasized the importance of childbirth fear, a finding consistent with previous research [28]. Both models demonstrated high predictive accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. While multiple linear regression is commonly applied in nursing research, it relies on strict linear assumptions that may limit its ability to capture complex, nonlinear relationships. In contrast, the backpropagation neural network does not require specific data distribution assumptions and is particularly well-suited for modeling nonlinear dynamics and multifactorial interactions within healthcare data. In this study, the backpropagation neural network achieved notably higher predictive accuracy [25, 26, 36, 37], and its ranking of influencing factors closely aligned with real-world conditions, reinforcing its practical value in healthcare research.

This study contributes to the existing literature by integrating reciprocal determinism theory with backpropagation neural network modeling to comprehensively identify the key predictors of childbirth readiness. While previous studies have examined the factors influencing childbirth readiness, few have adopted a well-developed theoretical framework. By applying RDT, this study systematically accounted for the dynamic interactions between individual, behavioral, and environmental factors, providing a multidimensional perspective on the determinants of childbirth readiness. Moreover, the use of backpropagation neural network represents a methodological advancement, leveraging machine learning to identify key predictors with improved robustness and accuracy. This innovative integration of theory-driven and data-driven approaches yields valuable insights, particularly by highlighting the pivotal roles of behavioral intention, family care, information support, and fear of birth within the Chinese cultural context. From a practical standpoint, the findings underscore the need to integrate intrinsic motivation with extrinsic support in maternal healthcare strategies. Enhancing mothers’ internal drive for childbirth preparation, combined with stronger family involvement and access to reliable information, can optimize childbirth readiness. Additionally, this study provides a methodological reference for future maternal health research by demonstrating the effective integration of theoretical frameworks and machine learning techniques.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, due to its cross-sectional design, the temporal order of variables could not be established, which limits the ability to infer causal relationships between influencing factors and childbirth readiness. Future studies employing longitudinal or interventional designs are warranted to confirm and extend these findings. Second, the study relied on self-reported data, which may introduce recall bias and social desirability bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of the responses. Although face-to-face data collection and mandatory responses improved data completeness, the inherent limitations of self-reporting remain unavoidable. Third, the generalizability of the findings is limited. The participants were recruited from three tertiary hospitals in urban China, and the majority were highly educated women with relatively high socioeconomic status. Consequently, the results may not fully represent the experiences of women from rural areas or those from underprivileged backgrounds. Key influencing factors, such as behavioral intention and information support, may operate differently in rural or low-resource settings. Future research should therefore examine these contextual differences to enhance the external validity and generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

This study innovatively combined reciprocal determinism with backpropagation neural network modeling to systematically explore the influencing factors of childbirth readiness among Chinese pregnant women. Based on the reciprocal determinism framework, this study comprehensively explored the individual and environmental influences on birth readiness and accurately identified the key influences of behavioral intention in childbirth preparation, family care, fear of birth, and information support through backpropagation neural network analysis. Notably, this study introduced behavioral intention in childbirth preparation as a cognitive factor among the individual factors, and the results suggest that behavioral intention in childbirth preparation is strongly associated with higher levels of birth readiness. This study provided valuable strategies for health professionals and policymakers to recommend the use of a combination of intrinsic motivation and extrinsic support in maternal health care: intrinsic motivation focuses on motivating mothers to prepare for childbirth. In contrast, extrinsic support emphasizes strengthening information and family support to promote maternal readiness for childbirth synergistically.
