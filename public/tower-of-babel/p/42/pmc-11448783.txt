Understanding the Adsorption Kinetics of Acetone in Humid Activated Carbons: Perspectives from Adsorption-Breakthrough Experiments and Molecular Simulations
Authors: Shanshan Wang, Jianggen Hu, Licheng Li, Songlin Zuo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe presence of water vapor influences the adsorption equilibrium and kinetics of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in porous materials. By combination of breakthrough experiments and molecular simulations, the competitive adsorption mechanisms of water vapor and acetone on activated carbon with different textures and surface chemical properties at different humidity levels were investigated. Adsorption capacity decreases with increasing relative humidity owing to the formation of preferential adsorption sites between water and the activated carbon surface, while the adsorption rate initially increases and then decreases with increasing relative humidity. Experimental and simulation results revealed that the existence of a small amount of water changed the pore size distributions of activated carbon, thereby promoting the diffusion of acetone molecules. As the relative humidity increased, a portion of the acetone dissolved in water, resulting in a reduction in the adsorption rate. The response of different functional groups to relative humidity was further clarified by molecular simulation. Activated carbons with a high electrostatic interaction with acetone were less affected by humidity and thus exhibit greater potential as adsorbents.

Introduction

Following the rapid industrial development of the past century, the release of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) into the atmosphere has become a threat to the global ecological environment. Several technologies have been used to reduce VOC emissions, including condensation,1 adsorption,2 catalytic oxidation,3 and biodegradation.4 Among these technologies, adsorption is the optimal method for removing VOCs from polluted air. To date, many adsorbents have been studied to identify cost-effective adsorbents for the treatment of VOCs. Activated carbon (AC) is widely used for the storage and separation of various gases because of its large adsorption capacity, fast absorption rate, and easy regeneration.5 However, AC is a flammable material and the adsorption process is mostly exothermic. An excessively high temperature increase may lead to a bed combustion and fire hazards. In the past decade of engineering practice, AC has been used to adsorb volatile organic gases, especially ketone adsorbates, and many fire accidents have occurred.6 Compared with the high-cost method of nitrogen purging bed cooling, the watering cooling method is more commonly used in the industry.7 Therefore, when the adsorption performance (adsorption capacity and rate) of VOCs is evaluated, the influence of water vapor must be considered.

As is known to all, the adsorption of water/VOCs on ACs is determined by the surface chemical properties.8 Different types and concentrations of functional groups can be introduced onto the surface of the ACs by utilizing different heat or chemical treatment in the preparation process.9 The contribution of such functional groups is usually considered to be two aspects: the pore texture, such as the surface area or the pore volume,10 and the surface chemical property,11 which are important factors that affect the adsorbent–adsorbate interactions. Surface functional groups on AC change its polarity and hydrophobicity,12 thus affecting the adsorbent–adsorbate intermolecular interactions, including the electrostatic and nonelectrostatic (van der Waals, VDW) interactions.13 Understanding the different contributions for the VOCs adsorption equilibrium and rate is key to the design of high-performance AC.

Numerous works have been performed for the adsorption equilibrium of organic vapors on different adsorbents in the existence of water vapor. Owing to its competitive adsorption, omnipresent water vapor usually has a negative impact on the VOC adsorption capacity of the adsorbent, which decreases with increasing relative humidity.14 The water vapor quickly adheres and occupies a majority of the adsorption sites on the adsorbents, reducing the adsorption capacity for VOCs.15 For example, AC with a high content of hydrophilic sites (metal impurities and surface oxygen) significantly decreased the adsorption of benzene under wet conditions.16 Other studies emphasize that the influence of humidity depends on the hydrophilicity of the VOCs used. Competitive adsorption is observed between hydrophobic compounds (such as toluene and dichloromethane) and water at all humidity levels,12 while cooperative adsorption occurs between a hydrophilic VOC (such as ethanol) and water vapor at low humidity levels, and competitive adsorption occurs at high humidity levels.17

The adsorption equilibrium of water vapor on ACs would influence the adsorption kinetics of water vapor accordingly,18 thereby affecting the adsorption kinetics of VOCs. The mass transfer rate of pure water vapor varies significantly with the shape of the adsorption isotherms. The relationship between the mass transfer coefficient and the water loading can be described by the breakthrough curve. The maximum value of the rate coefficient is observed at low pressure. As the bulk adsorption and sharp rise in adsorption capacity occur in the isotherm, the rate coefficient decreased to a minimum value. Then, the rate coefficient starts to increase due to the pore-filling mechanism as the pressure further increases.12 Noteworthily, the adsorption kinetics of VOCs is rarely addressed when competitive adsorption occurs between water vapor and VOCs. Experiments for studying competitive adsorption of water vapor and organics are usually carried out in three ways:19 (1) transferring the organics to a bed of preloaded water, (2) transferring the mixture of organics and water vapor to the dry bed, and (3) transferring the mixture of organics and water vapor to the bed of preloaded water. Due to the strong affinity between water and the nitrogen/oxygen-containing surface of ACs, the interaction environment between VOCs and ACs will be changed when water molecules are preloaded.20 Furthermore, the structural changes after water adsorption are difficult to detect with existing experimental methods, as surface area characterization requires dehydration of the adsorbents, which brings a great challenge to the explanation of the influence of the surface structure and surface chemical properties of ACs on the adsorption kinetics of VOCs.

With the continuous growth of computing resources, numerous molecular simulation studies have provided insights into the microscopic properties of gas adsorption in various porous materials. As a supplementary tool for experimental methods, molecular simulation eliminates the interference of multiple factors in experiments and conducts a single-factor analysis. From the perspective of gas-adsorbent interaction, it is considered that the electrostatic interaction between water and the adsorbents is the main factor affecting the adsorption performance. Grand Canonical Monte Carlo simulations provide a sufficient explanation for the impact of water on the capture of CO2 by porous adsorbents at the molecular level and may guide the future application of target adsorbents.21 Molecular dynamics studied were performed to explore the influence of structural properties22 and surface chemical properties23 on the diffusion of water molecules, as a supplement to the experimental results, to determine the adsorption rate of water molecules in porous carbon materials and the role of surface chemistry in the adsorption.

The objective of this work is to determine the influence of water vapor on the adsorption equilibrium and adsorption kinetics of the VOCs. ACs with different nitrogen and oxygen contents were prepared as comparable adsorbents. Using acetone as the targeted pollutant VOC and combining breakthrough experiments and molecular simulation, the equilibrium and kinetics of acetone adsorption on ACs under different relative humidity were investigated to reveal the influence of the characteristics of AC and the influence of humidity.

Details of Breakthrough Experiments

Experimental Materials

Details of Reagents Used in the Breakthrough Experiments

Coconut-shell ACs were collected from Jiangsu Youhuada Environmental Materials Technology Co., Ltd. N2 gas (>99.999%) and NH3 gas (>99.0%) were purchased from Nanjing Baohe Gas Co., Ltd. Acetone (≥99.5%), nitric acid (65.0–68.0 wt %), and hydrochloric acid (36.0–38.0 wt %) were purchased from Nanjing Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Modification of Activated Carbons

To investigate the effect of texture and surface chemical properties, three types of ACs (p-AC, HNO3-AC, and NH3-AC) were prepared from coconut-shell ACs. First, pristine coconut-shell ACs were fully washed with 0.1 mol/L hydrochloric acid solution and water to remove the influence of ash, until the conductivity of the filtrate was less than 10 μS/cm. The washed ACs were subsequently dried in an oven, and the dried ACs were labeled as p-AC. Second, a certain portion of the p-AC was soaked in nitric acid solution, heated at 90 °C for 2 h, washed with water, filtered until the conductivity of the filtrate was less than 10 μS/cm, dried in an oven, and labeled as HNO3-AC. Third, another portion of p-AC was activated under a NH3 atmosphere (50 mL/min) at 800 °C for 3 h, cooled to room temperature under a N2 atmosphere, and denoted by NH3-AC. To investigate the effect of humidity, wet ACs were prepared using dry p-AC, HNO3-AC, and NH3-AC. Dry ACs were first soaked in deionized water, removed after no bubbles were observed, and then placed in a drying device for quantitative drying. The water content of wet ACs was monitored in real time using an electronic balance during drying, and the wet ACs were removed after their water content decreased to the specified content. The moisture contents of the wet ACs used in this test were 10, 20, 30, and 40 wt %.

Characterizations of Activated Carbons

N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms were measured at −196 °C to determine the specific surface area (SBET), pore distribution, and pore volume of dry ACs. SBET was determined using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller equation. Pore size distribution was determined by the method based on quenched solid density functional theory. Total pore volume (Vtotal) was estimated by converting the adsorption capacity of adsorbed nitrogen to the liquid volume at a relative pressure of 0.99. The micropore volume (Vmicro) was calculated using the Dubinin–Radushkevich equation.

The surface chemistry of the ACs was characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) with an Al Kα (1486.6 eV) line excitation source. The spectra were analyzed by the XPSPEAK 4.1 software.

Procedure of the Breakthrough Measurements

A schematic of the breakthrough experiment is shown in Figure 1. The acetone feed gas was generated by bubbling the acetone solution at 4 °C at a flow rate of 200 mL/min. The gas flow was then diluted by a flow of nitrogen gas (400 mL/min) controlled at (25 ± 3) °C as the acetone mixture required for the breakthrough test. The adsorption column was filled with ACs. The tail gas of the breakthrough test was analyzed and detected by gas chromatography (SP6890, Shandong Lunan) under the following conditions: column temperature, 150 °C; vaporization chamber temperature, 180 °C; temperature of the detection room, 180 °C; and carrier gas, nitrogen. A six-way valve was used for sampling, and a hydrogen flame ionization detector was employed.

Methods for Grand Canonical Monte Carlo Simulation

The adsorption of acetone on pristine AC models or AC models preadsorbed with water molecules was simulated using the GCMC simulation method that performed with the HD-CADSS software package.31 In each simulation, the AC models were fixed, and the preadsorbed water molecules and the acetone molecules were moving randomly, with 25% probability for the translation, rotation, generation, and deletion of the molecules, respectively. Other details for the simulation are the same as our previous work.26

Results and Discussion

Pore Texture and Surface Chemical Properties of Activated Carbon

Several studies have indicated that adsorption capacity is closely related to the micropore volume. ACs with large micropore volumes possess high pore utilization and adsorption capacity, which are suitable for acetone adsorption.32 In addition, mesopores are important channels for the entrance of acetone molecules and for determining the adsorption rate.33 Therefore, the proportions of micro-, meso-, and macropores have an important impact on the acetone adsorption capacity and adsorption rate. First, the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms and pore size distributions of three dry AC materials, namely, p-AC, HNO3-AC, and NH3-AC, were studied. The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms in Figure 3a show that the adsorption isotherms of the three ACs are Type I, indicating the presence of numerous micropores in the three types of ACs. Moreover, the pore size distributions in Figure 3b show that the pore sizes are mainly concentrated in the micropores. Although the specific surface areas and micropore volumes of the two modified ACs (HNO3-AC and NH3-AC) are less than those in the p-AC (Table 2), the proportion of the micropore volume of the modified ACs increases to a certain extent. In p-AC, HNO3-AC, and NH3-AC, the micropore volumes accounted for 60.5%, 65.9%, and 70.4% of the total pore volume, respectively.

Effect of Relative Humidity on Acetone Adsorption Equilibrium

The breakthrough curves of acetone in p-AC, HNO3-AC, and NH3-AC at different water contents (0, 10, 20, 30, and 40 wt %) were plotted in Figure 5. It was clearly visible that the preadsorbed water in ACs had a negative effect on the breakthrough curve of acetone. Taking C/C0 = 2% as the breakthrough point and C/C0 = 98% as the saturation point, the breakthrough, saturation time, and working adsorption capacity of acetone on three ACs were calculated as shown in Table 4. It shows that the breakthrough times and the working capacity of acetone became smaller with an increase in the water content. These results were consistent with the influence of water vapor on the adsorption properties of AC as reported in the literature.38 When the water content raised from 0% to 40 wt %, the breakthrough time of acetone on p-AC, HNO3-AC, and NH3-AC decreased by about 42, 50, and 52%, respectively, and the working adsorption capacity decreased by about 61, 57, and 50%, respectively. The smaller breakthrough time and working capacity could be attributed to competitive adsorption between water vapor and acetone on the AC surface. Owing to its strong competitive adsorption, omnipresent water vapor may lead to reduced capacity and slow adsorption kinetics for target VOCs.39 Surface chemistry plays a significant role on competitive adsorption between VOCs and water vapor.40 Water vapors quickly adhere to the hydrophilic sites (e.g., oxygen/nitrogen-containing functional groups) on ACs and occupy a majority of the adsorption sites.41 The preadsorbed water molecules then bind more water molecules with hydrogen bond,8 thereby reducing the adsorption capacity for acetone.

Effect of Water Content on the Acetone Adsorption in Activated Carbons

The three ACs have different specific surface areas and different types and concentrations of oxygen- and nitrogen-containing functional groups. Thus, it is difficult to evaluate the influence of surface chemical properties on the competitive adsorption characteristics of acetone and water vapor separately through experimental data.42 In addition, due to the complex interaction between adsorbent and adsorbate, the competitive adsorption mechanism has not been elaborated accurately, so molecular simulation methods are urgently needed here.

To further study the effect of different surface chemical properties with different water contents through molecular simulation, atomic models for ACs containing different functional groups were constructed. According to the XPS analysis results, it was found that there were carboxyl, hydroxyl, carbonyl, pyridine-N, and amino N groups on the surface of the prepared activated carbon. In order to investigate the influencing factors of these functional groups, the carboxyl, hydroxyl, carbonyl, pyridine-N, and amino-N groups were selected for modification at the edges of the coronal graphite BSU to generate the modified AC models for GCMC simulations, as shown in Figure 6a. Furthermore, to study the effect of water content and interpret the key control factors of the adsorption process, all AC models studied were preadsorbed with 10, 20, 30, and 40 wt % water, as shown for the model example in Figure 2e. The water molecules occupy the pores of the AC when they are preadsorbed in the ACs, thereby changing the texture property (surface area or pore volume) of the adsorbent.20 The surface area and pore volume of ACs preadsorbed with water molecules can be determined through molecular simulation. This functionality is an advantage of the molecular simulation method over the experimental method, as measuring the pore properties through experiments requires drying of the activated carbon material. Figure 6b shows the pore topological physical characteristics of AC modified by different functional groups at different water contents. Consistent with the experimental results, the surface area and pore volume of the modified ACs decreased. When water molecules were preadsorbed, the surface area and pore volume are significantly reduced, which gives us meaningful information that cannot be characterized by experiments.26 The characterization of ACs preadsorbed with water molecules were achieved through molecular simulation models. Compared with dry ACs, when the water content is 40 wt %, the surface area and pore volume of ACs decreased by approximately 60% and 14%, respectively (Figure 6b).

Essentially, the contribution of adsorbent materials to the competitive adsorption behavior can be divided into two aspects, namely, the nonelectrostatic and electrostatic contributions. The nonelectrostatic contribution originates from AC topology and VDW interaction, while the electrostatic contribution is mainly attributed to the electrostatic affinity between the surface functional groups and water/acetone molecules. Finding the key influencing factors is significant for analyzing the competitive adsorption mechanism and providing a theoretical basis for material preparation. Technically, the electrostatic interactions can be turned off by molecular simulation calculation, which is a commonly used and effective method to assess the nonelectrostatic and electrostatic contributions separately.43

In order to quantitatively evaluate the contribution of nonelectrostatic and electrostatic interaction, we define two parameters named Cnon–E and CE. Cnon–E refers to the contribution of nonelectrostatic interaction and is defined as the difference of acetone adsorption capacity between pristine and modified ACs when the electrostatic interaction in turn off. Also, CE refers to the contribution of electrostatic interaction and is defined as the difference between the acetone adsorption capacity in the presence and absence of electrostatic interaction in each AC models. As shown in Figure 7, in the modified ACs, the contribution of electrostatic interactions to the adsorption capacity was always greater than that of nonelectrostatic interactions, regardless of the dry or wet state of the ACs. For the nonelectrostatic contribution, AC–COOH has the largest nonelectrostatic contribution, while AC-pyridine-N has the smallest nonelectrostatic contribution. As we have analyzed above that the contribution of nonelectrostatic interaction mainly depends on the physical structure (surface area and pore volume) of the adsorbents and VDW interactions between the adsorbents and acetone. The AC–COOH and AC-pyridine-N models had similar surface areas and pore volumes (Figure 6b), whereas AC-pyridine-N had less VDW interaction than AC–COOH (Table 2). Therefore, it could be concluded that functional groups with higher VDW interaction are more favorable for acetone adsorption than those with lower VDW interaction. For the nonelectrostatic contribution, it can be clearly seen that the electrostatic contribution of oxygen containing functional groups is greater than that of nitrogen-containing functional groups in both dry and wet ACs.26 The electrostatic contributions of oxygen-containing functional groups in dry and wet ACs are 0.49–0.75 and 0.38–0.61 mmol/g, respectively, while those of nitrogen containing functional groups in dry and wet ACs are 0.10–0.29 and 0.18–0.21 mmol/g, respectively, demonstrating that oxygen containing groups on the AC surface are more conducive to acetone adsorption than nitrogen-containing groups. Among them, the electrostatic contribution of −OH and −COOH groups is the largest because they are more hydrophilic than other functional groups, and their influence on water molecules is superior to other functional groups.

Furthermore, the nonelectrostatic and electrostatic contributions respond differently to water content. The nonelectrostatic contribution values for dry OH-AC, COOH-AC, CO-AC, pyridine-N AC, and amino-N AC are 0.3, 0.6, 0.15, −0.10, and 0.4 mmol/g, respectively; while the values are 0.2, 0.3, −0.05, −0.12, and 0.08 mmol/g for 40 wt % water content counterparts. The electrostatic contribution values for dry OH-AC, COOH-AC, CO-AC, pyridine-N AC, and amino-N AC are 0.5, 0.68, 0.6, 0.3, and 0.25 mmol/g, respectively; while the values are 0.1, 0.06, 0.15, 0.17, and 0.13 mmol/g for 40 wt % water content counterparts. The decrease of electrostatic contribution is much less than that of the nonelectrostatic contribution. Increasing water content reduced the contributions of nonelectrostatic and electrostatic effects, which further demonstrates the detrimental effect of water on acetone adsorption equilibrium. However, it is obvious that the detrimental effect of water on the contribution of nonelectrostatic interaction is greater than that on the contribution of electrostatic interaction, showing that AC with high electrostatic affinity with the adsorbates is less affected by water vapor and is more conducive to acetone adsorption.

Figure 8 shows that the adsorption rate constant kTh of acetone first increases and then decreases with increasing water content, demonstrating that the existence in water is conducive to the improvement of the acetone adsorption rate within an appropriate water content range. Among the dry p-AC, HNO3-AC, and NH3-AC, the kTh of acetone is about 0.5–1.0 L/(min mmol). When the water content reaches 20 wt %, the kTh value reaches a maximum value around 1.5–2.0 L/(min mmol). As the water content further increases to 30 and 40 wt % %, the kTh value decreases to about 1.0 L/(min mmol). This could be attributed to the change of the micro/medium/large pore ratio of AC in the presence of preadsorbed water. Therefore, we conclude that the diffusibility of acetone molecule is enhanced by the preadsorbed water. At the same time, we cannot ignore that as a soluble organic solvent, acetone may be dissolved in water that was preadsorbed in ACs, and the rate of acetone dissolution from the gas phase to the water is lower than the adsorption rate of acetone molecules on the activated carbon. When the water content in wet ACs reaches 30% or 40 wt %, acetone may be dissolved in water in the wet carbon. The high water content significantly reduces the bed capacity of organic adsorbent, and the adsorbent surface is no longer suitable for organic substances. In this case, the organic substances dissolved in the condensed water in the pores will not diffuse.12

Adsorption being an exothermic process, the bed temperature can rise by up to 2K during the competitive adsorption process of acetone and water vapor.45 The water in the AC pores absorbs a portion of heat and vaporizes, thereby alleviating the local temperature rise of the AC adsorption bed to a certain extent and improving the adsorption rate of acetone on the 20 wt % % wet carbon. The liquid drops found at the outlet of the adsorption bed during the breakthrough experiment further proved this point. When the water content reaches 30% or 40 wt %, the adsorption heat of acetone is insufficient to vaporize the excess water, causing it to block the pores of the activated carbon, thus inhibiting the acetone adsorption performance of the activated carbon and reducing the adsorption rate.

Conclusions

The effects of the surface groups and water content of AC on the acetone adsorption equilibrium and kinetics were studied by combining breakthrough experiments and molecular simulation theory calculations; the influence of water content on the adsorption behavior of acetone molecules was analyzed in order to provide guidance on whether to add a water spray cooling process and the setting of relevant operating parameters in the adsorption bed process. The results show that

I.The increasing water content in ACs leads to a decrease in acetone breakthrough time and a reduction in acetone working adsorption capacity. The influence of the electrostatic interaction of the functional groups was discounted by the molecular simulation method, and the nonelectrostatic and electrostatic contributions to the acetone adsorption capacity were elaborated. A comparison between wet and dry ACs showed that the presence of water molecules significantly reduced the adsorption performance. AC with a high electrostatic contribution is less affected by the water content and is thus more conducive to adsorption.

II.The adsorption rate of acetone first increased and then decreased with increasing water content in ACs. The highest acetone adsorption rate was obtained when the water content in ACs was 20 wt %. The structural characteristics of the aqueous AC models were characterized by using molecular simulations. The results showed that the presence of a certain amount of water changed the pore size distribution of AC, thereby promoting the diffusion of acetone molecules. Water in the ACs absorbs part of the heat generated by the bed heating and improves the adsorption rate of acetone on 20 wt % wet ACs. When the higher water content reaches 30 or 40 wt %, acetone may be dissolved in the water in the wet ACs, and acetone dissolved in the condensed water in the pores will not diffuse, thus possessing a low adsorption rate.

The topic has strong implications because, by identifying the adsorption equilibrium and kinetics using a representative volatile organic compound (acetone), this study provides a safety guideline for the use of activated carbon subjected to modification with different functional groups to reduce VOCs in gaseous emissions under different water environments while optimizing its adsorption rate and capacity, as well as a reference for further studies on the adsorption of other VOCs with more complex structures.
