Dietary modulation of antimicrobial peptides (HAMP and LEAP2) in Chinook salmon ( O ncorhynchus tshawytscha) fed a phytobiotic functional diet: Integrated genomic characterisation and tissue‐specific expression
Authors: Roberta Marcoli, Jane E Symonds, Seumas P Walker, Cesar Lopez, Mark A Preece, Christopher Battershill, Steve Bird
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are key components of the innate immune system in fish, providing rapid protection against microbial pathogens. Among these, the liver‐expressed antimicrobial peptides (LEAPs), comprising LEAP‐1 (hepcidin; HAMP) and LEAP‐2, play central roles in host defence and iron regulation. In this study, we present an integrated characterisation of the genomic organisation, tissue expression and dietary responsiveness of HAMP and LEAP2 paralogues in Chinook salmon (Oncorhynchus tshawytscha), and assess their modulation by a phytobiotic functional feed under commercial farming conditions. Genome mining identified three functional HAMP and three LEAP2 paralogues in Chinook salmon, all retaining conserved features typical of their respective peptide families and arising from salmonid‐specific genome duplication events. Constitutive expression analysis revealed liver‐dominant expression of HAMP, whereas LEAP2 paralogues exhibited distinct tissue‐specific profiles, with LEAP2.1 and LEAP2.2 expressed predominantly in the liver and skin, and LEAP2.3 largely restricted to gills and skin. A six‐month feeding trial using a functional diet supplemented with the phytobiotic NatControl™ resulted in marked tissue‐ and paralogue‐specific upregulation of AMP gene expression. HAMP expression was upregulated across multiple immune‐relevant tissues, including head kidney, skin, spleen, liver and gills. LEAP2 paralogues also showed clear dietary responsiveness, with LEAP2.1 and LEAP2.2 upregulated in the liver and kidney, and LEAP2.3 showing strong upregulation in gills. Despite these molecular responses, no significant differences in growth or survival were observed between diet groups. Together, these findings provide the first integrated characterisation of HAMP and LEAP2 systems in Chinook salmon, demonstrate their sensitivity to dietary immunomodulation and support their use as molecular biomarkers of functional immune priming. The results further indicate that NatControl™ acts as an effective immunostimulant, enhancing innate immune readiness without compromising growth performance under non‐challenging conditions.Keywords: antimicrobial peptide, Chinook salmon, immune system, natcontrol, phytobiotic diet

INTRODUCTION

Aquaculture is one of the fastest‐growing food production sectors, but its expansion is constrained by infectious diseases that cause significant mortality, reduced growth and substantial economic losses (Magnadottir, 2010). Chinook salmon (Oncorhynchus tshawytscha) is a species of high commercial value in New Zealand and North America, where it supports aquaculture and recreational fisheries. However, Chinook salmon, as other salmonids, are vulnerable to infectious agents, which can spread rapidly under farming conditions (Heath et al., 2000). Conventional disease control strategies, including vaccines, antibiotics and chemotherapeutics, have had success but remain limited: vaccines do not cover all pathogens, antibiotics can contribute to antimicrobial resistance and chemotherapeutics raise environmental, consumer and social licensing concerns (Alderman & Hastings, 1998; Muziasari et al., 2017; Sørum, 2008). Consequently, there is strong interest in novel approaches to disease management, including the use of functional feeds enriched with compounds that promote health and immunity (Encarnação, 2016; Li et al., 2009; Tacchi et al., 2011). These feeds may include probiotics, prebiotics, vitamins, trace minerals, nucleotides, and increasingly, phytobiotics derived from plants and seaweeds (Hernández et al., 2016; Reverter et al., 2014). Phytobiotics such as fucoidans (sulphated polysaccharides from brown algae) and labdane diterpenes (diterpenoid lactones from higher plants) have demonstrated immunomodulatory effects in fish, including enhanced macrophage activity, stimulation of phagocytosis, modulation of cytokine production and regulation of oxidative stress (Caipang et al., 2011; Chiou et al., 1998; Deachamag et al., 2006). These compounds interact with innate immune pathways, potentially enhancing resistance to pathogens and reducing reliance on antibiotics. NatControl™ (HP Ingredients, Bradenton, FL, USA) is a seaweed‐based feed additive containing a standardised labdane diterpenoid fraction that has been associated with improved survival and immune gene upregulation during P. salmonis challenge in Atlantic salmon (Romero et al., 2021). Manufacturer materials outline a proposed immune‐support and antioxidant mechanism (HP Ingredients technical materials).

Central to innate immunity in fish are antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), small, cationic molecules with broad‐spectrum activity against bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites (Reddy et al., 2004; Tennessen, 2005). AMPs act by disrupting microbial membranes, sequestering nutrients, such as iron, or modulating host immune signalling (Masso‐Silva & Diamond, 2014). In vertebrates, AMPs include defensins, cathelicidins, histone‐derived peptides and the liver‐expressed antimicrobial peptides (LEAPs) (Krause et al., 2000; Park et al., 2001). LEAPs are cysteine‐rich, β‐sheet peptides with strong antimicrobial activity, subdivided into two major families: LEAP1 (also known as hepcidin antimicrobial peptide; HAMP) and LEAP2 (Krause et al., 2003).

HAMP was first identified in humans as an antimicrobial peptide and regulator of iron homeostasis, with a mature peptide containing eight conserved cysteine residues (Krause et al., 2000; Park et al., 2001). In mammals, HAMP binds to the iron exporter ferroportin, inducing its degradation and thereby reducing iron availability to invading pathogens (Zhang & Enns, 2004). In fish, multiple HAMP paralogues have been reported, reflecting gene duplication events, with expression not only in liver but also in gills, skin, spleen and kidney, suggesting broader immunological roles (Álvarez et al., 2013; Casadei et al., 2013; Douglas et al., 2003).

LEAP2, by contrast, is a four‐cysteine peptide lacking the iron‐regulatory functions of HAMP but showing strong antimicrobial activity and modulation of host defence (Krause et al., 2003; Liu et al., 2014). In salmonids, LEAP2 paralogues are often found in multiple copies due to the salmonid‐specific whole‐genome duplication (Berthelot et al., 2014; Guyomard et al., 2012) and both basal and induced expression of LEAP2 paralogues vary by tissue and environmental stimulus, suggesting functional diversification (Martin‐Antonio et al., 2009; Masso‐Silva & Diamond, 2014).

Salmonids are of particular interest for AMP research because they have undergone an additional (fourth) round of whole‐genome duplication (WGD), resulting in multiple paralogues of immune genes (Berthelot et al., 2014). Studies in rainbow trout and Atlantic salmon have reported three HAMP paralogues and three LEAP2 paralogues with divergent expression (Álvarez et al., 2013; Casadei et al., 2015; Douglas et al., 2003). However, despite the species' importance for aquaculture, little is known about these gene families in Chinook salmon.

In this study, we isolated and characterised the HAMP and LEAP2 paralogues present in the Chinook salmon genome through comparative genomic and phylogenetic analyses, establishing their evolutionary relationships within salmonids. We then quantified their baseline tissue distribution across a suite of organs to define constitutive expression patterns. Finally, we examined gene expression responses to long‐term feeding with a phytobiotic‐enriched functional diet, revealing how sustained immunostimulant supplementation modulates HAMP and LEAP2 gene regulation in Chinook salmon. By integrating genomic characterisation with a six‐month feeding trial, this work provides the first comprehensive description of HAMP and LEAP2 in Chinook salmon and demonstrates their potential as biomarkers for assessing the efficacy of functional feeds in aquaculture.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Genome mining and identification of AMP paralogues

To perform genome mining and identification of AMP paralogues we utilised the methodology described by Marcoli et al., 2023 and Wen et al., 2025. Chinook salmon HAMP and LEAP2 gene sequences were first identified within the available genome assembly (Christensen et al., 2018), and conservation of synteny was examined between Chinook salmon and selected vertebrate genomes. Initial searches of the Chinook salmon genome database (Otsh_v1.0; GenBank assembly accession: GCA_002163495.1) were performed using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST; Altschul et al., 1990) with known rainbow trout HAMP and LEAP2 gene sequences. Homologues of each gene were identified on multiple genomic contigs.

Gene annotation was subsequently performed using GENSCAN (Burge & Karlin, 1998), Fgenesh (Salamov & Solovyev, 2000), BLAST (Altschul et al., 1990), FASTA (Pearson & Lipman, 1988) and the translate tool in ExPASy (Gasteiger et al., 2005) to characterise the genes of interest and neighbouring loci. Using the same approach, genomes of selected vertebrate species (Table 1) available in Ensembl (Yates et al., 2016) were analysed to assess synteny conservation surrounding the HAMP and LEAP2 regions. Gene organisation and predicted amino acid sequences derived from the Chinook salmon genome were then compared across species.

Phylogenetic and synteny analyses

To analyse the phylogenetic and synteny we utilised the methodology described by Marcoli et al., 2023. In short, selected vertebrate HAMP and LEAP2 sequences were retrieved from the NCBI database. Multiple sequence alignments of selected amino acid and nucleotide sequences were generated using ClustalX v1.81 (Larkin et al., 2007) and phylogenetic relationships constructed from the alignments of the full‐length amino acid sequences of known vertebrate HAMP and LEAP2 molecules using the neighbour‐joining method (Saitou & Nei, 1987). The tree was edited using Dendroscope v3.7.2 (Huson & Scornavacca, 2012) or TreeView (Page, 1996) and confidence limits added (Felsenstein, 1985). Finally, the predicted amino acid sequences were analysed using SignalP v4.0 (Petersen et al., 2011) and important protein structures predicted using PredictProtein (Bernhofer et al., 2021).

Fish husbandry and feeding trial

Chinook salmon (initial weight ~ 320–350 g), with the same genetic, nutritional and environmental background, were maintained for a 6 month period (July to December 2018), in semi‐commercial trial pens at Ruakaka farm, Queen Charlotte Sounds, under commercial growing conditions. Six pens of 6 × 6 m2 with a grower net of 6 m depth and a central ring for the extraction of mortalities were used in this trial. Each pen was stocked with 600 Chinook salmon. Pens were cleaned periodically and mortalities recorded daily. For each diet (control and the immunostimulant enriched diet), three pens were used, giving a total of 1800 fish per diet, and therefore 3600 fish in total. The schedule and feeding frequency followed the management and operation protocols of the New Zealand King Salmon (NZKS) company.

Three tanks of fish (n = 1800) were fed either a control (CON) or a natcontrol (NatControl HPIngredients—USA) (NED) enriched diet (Table 2). The natcontrol enriched diet was supplemented with NatControl™ at 0.1% inclusion, corresponding to 1 g kg−1 of feed.

Tissue sampling

At the end of the trial, 5 fish per pen (15 fish per diet) were randomly sampled as representatives of the population. Fish were anaesthetised using AQUI‐S® (AQUI‐S New Zealand Ltd., NZ) and killed by spinal severance. Immediately following death, the head kidney, liver, spleen and skin were sampled, immediately placed in RNAlater® (Thermofisher, USA) and stored at −80°C prior to RNA extraction. In addition, before the organs were sampled, all 30 fish underwent a standard physical external and internal assessment to determine their overall health.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was isolated from ~100 mg of tissue taken from the liver, spleen and gills using the easy spin [DNA free] Total RNA Extraction Kit (iNTrON Biotechnologies, Republic of Korea), following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a Nanodrop DeNovix DS‐11 Series Spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA), and the integrity of the RNA was checked using electrophoresis and imaged using the iBright™ CL750 Imaging System (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA). cDNA was produced from 3 μg of previously extracted total RNA, using the HiSenScriptTM RH(−) RT PreMix kit (iNTrON Biotechnologies, Gyeonggi‐do, Republic of Korea), following the manufacturer's instructions. After incubation, each cDNA sample was diluted three times using Dnase/Rnase‐Free water and stored at −20°C prior to use in quantitative PCR (qPCR). First‐strand cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 μg RNA with SuperScript III reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen) and oligo(dT) primers in a 20 μL reaction. Negative controls without reverse transcriptase were included to verify the absence of genomic DNA.

Primer design and validation

Primers were designed using Primer3 software to amplify fragments spanning exon–exon junctions when possible (Table 3). Separate primer sets were developed for LEAP2 paralogue (LEAP2.1, LEAP2.2, LEAP2.3). The primers were intentionally designed to amplify a highly conserved region shared among all identified HAMP paralogues, as the high sequence similarity between paralogues prevented the design of reliable paralogue‐specific primers. Two housekeeping genes, ribosomal protein L18 (RPL18) and RNA polymerase II subunit F (POLR2F), were also targeted to normalise expression; the primers for those were designed by Marcoli et al., 2023.

Primer specificity was validated by agarose gel electrophoresis of qPCR products and by sequencing amplicons. Amplification efficiency (E) was determined from standard curves of serially diluted cDNA and only primers with efficiencies between 90% and 110% were used.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using a 48‐well Magnetic Induction Cycler (MIC; BMS, Australia). Reactions (20 μL) contained 5 μL cDNA, gene‐specific forward and reverse primers (1 μL each), 10 μL SsoAdvanced™ Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio‐Rad) and nuclease‐free water. All samples were run in duplicate with double‐negative controls for each primer pair. Cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 98°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 98°C for 15 s and primer‐specific annealing for 30 s. Fluorescence was measured at 60°C each cycle, and melt curve analysis (65–95°C) was performed to confirm single amplicon formation. Amplification specificity was further verified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis, with selected amplicons sequenced to confirm target identity.

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed using R (v4.0.3). Growth performance was compared between diets using t‐tests. Relative gene expression was calculated using the Pfaffl method, normalised against geometric means of RPL18 and POLR2F and expressed relative to the control diet group. Constitutive expression of HAMP and LEAP2 genes was assessed in fish receiving the control diet only. Expression values were normalised against the reference gene RPL18 and POLRF2 and calculated using the 2−ΔCt method (Schmittgen & Livak, 2008), providing a measure of baseline tissue‐specific gene expression.

Statistical significance was determined using the F test, and depending on the p‐value result, either a t‐test assuming unequal variance or a t‐test assuming equal variances was performed, and the p‐value of the t‐test was analysed, where values p < 0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS

Growth performance and survival

Over the six‐month feeding trial, no significant differences were observed in growth performance or mortality between Chinook salmon fed the control or NatControl™ diets. Specific growth rate (SGR), feed conversion ratio (FCR) and specific feeding rate (SFR) were comparable across treatments (Figure 1 and Supplementary material S9). These findings indicate that dietary inclusion of NatControl™ did not affect overall performance under non‐challenging winter farming conditions.

Characterisation of Chinook salmon HAMP

Coding cDNA of Chinook salmon HAMPa, HAMPb and HAMPc sequences and genes was predicted from the Chinook salmon genome (Supplementary material S1). All three predicted cDNA sequences were 267 nucleotides (nt) in length and had a gene organisation of three exons and two introns, all of which had the intron splicing consensus (GT/CAG) conserved at the 5′ and 3′ ends.

Determining the positions of the HAMP genes within the Chinook salmon genome showed that there was a high degree of synteny around the genomic regions where HAMP1 is found within other salmonids (Supplementary material S2). The genes lens intrinsic membrane protein 2 (LIM2), V‐Set and immunoglobulin domain containing 10 like (VSIG10L), electron transfer flavoprotein subunit beta (ETFB), upstream transcription factor 2 (USF2), IgLON family member 5 (IGLON5), argonaute RISC catalytic component 3 (AGO3), argonaute RISC catalytic component 1 (AGO1) and free fatty acid receptor 2 (FFAR2) were found either side of the HAMP1 genes in all the salmonid genomes analysed. HAMPa and HAMPb of each salmonid are found on one chromosome, whereas HAMPc of each salmonid is found on a different chromosome and also contains an additional HAMP gene, which appears to be a pseudogene. Limited synteny is shared between the salmonid genomes and human, amphibian and selected fish genomes, where only USF2 and FFAR2 are conserved. However, on comparison with selected fish genomes, all salmonid HAMPs characterised are more related to fish HAMP1 and that multiple forms exist due to genome duplication and tandem gene duplication events. No synteny can be seen between the regions where the salmonid HAMP1 genes, and the Amazon molly, stickleback, Fugu and Tetraodon HAMP2 genes are found. These are found on a completely different region of the genome to the fish HAMP1 gene; however, good synteny is seen between the HAMP2 genome regions of these fish species (Supplementary material S2).

All three Chinook salmon HAMP cDNA sequences encode an 88 amino acid preprohepcidin sequence. The typical HAMP domains (predomain signal peptide, prodomain and mature peptide) are identified in all Chinook salmon HAMP amino acid sequences. The position of a predomain signal peptide cleavage site, predicted using SignalP, was detected between Ala24 and Val25. A typical RX(R/K)R motif for propeptide convertases was identified and the HAMP mature peptide, shown to contain eight conserved cysteine residues, was also shown to be present. Prepropeptide sequence alignment with different species (Figure 2) shows that the cysteine active region is well conserved in all vertebrates, as is the RX(R/K)R motif for propeptide convertases. The relationship of the Chinook salmon HAMP sequences with the other known fish HAMP sequences, especially the other salmonid HAMP sequences, is apparent in the phylogenetic tree obtained using amino acid data from selected vertebrate HAMP amino acid sequences (Figure 2). Within the tree, it can be seen that the HAMP sequences from the Acanthomorphata clade of bony fish separate into two distinct groups, HAMP1 and HAMP2 (Supplementary material S3). HAMP sequences from fish that are part of the superorders Ostariophysi Anguilliformes, Clupeiformes and Salmoniformes are part of separate clades. These groups only have HAMP1 represented, but multiple copies of this gene exist in a number of the species represented, particularly the Salmoniformes. Due to the high similarity of the Chinook salmon HAMP sequences, primers were designed that would amplify all three expressed forms of the gene for qPCR (Supplementary material S4).

Characterisation of Chinook salmon LEAP2

Coding cDNA of Chinook salmon LEAP2.1, LEAP2.2 and LEAP2.3 (Supplementary material S5) sequences and genes were predicted from the Chinook salmon genome. LEAP2.1 was 291 nucleotides (nt) in length, LEAP2.2 was 276 nucleotides (nt) in length and LEAP2.3 was 237 nucleotides (nt) in length. All three predicted cDNA sequences had a gene organisation of three exons and two introns, all of which had the intron splicing consensus (GT/CAG) conserved at the 5′ and 3′ ends.

Determining the positions of the LEAP2 genes within the Chinook salmon genome showed that there was some conservation of genes around the genomic regions where each salmonid LEAP2 gene is found. This was not always the exact same gene, but a gene related to the same family (Supplementary material S6). For LEAP2.1, Shroom Family Member 4 (SHROOM4), which is closely related to Shroom Family Member 3 (SHROOM3), and bone morphogenetic protein 15 (BMP15), which is closely related to growth differentiation factor 9 (GDF9), were found. For LEAP3, cyclin I family member 2 (CCNI2), sosondowah ankyrin repeat domain family member B (SOWAHB), SHROOM3, AF4/FMR2 family member 4 (AFF4) and GDP9 were found. For LEAP2.2, although there is no direct synteny with any of the non‐fish species genomes investigated, there are genes close by that can be found around the other salmonid LEAP2 genes.

The Chinook salmon LEAP2.1 cDNA sequence encoded a 96 amino acid preproLEAP2.1 sequence, the LEAP2.2 cDNA sequence encoded a 91 amino acid preproLEAP2.2 sequence and the LEAP2.3 cDNA sequence encoded a 78 amino acid preproLEAP2.3 sequence. The typical LEAP2 domains (predomain signal peptide, prodomain and mature peptide) were identified in all the Chinook salmon LEAP2 amino acid sequences. The position of a predomain signal peptide cleavage site, predicted using SignalP, was detected in all three sequences. A typical RX(R/K)R motif for propeptide convertases was identified and the LEAP2 mature peptide was shown to contain four conserved cysteine residues. Prepropeptide sequence alignment with different species (Supplementary material S7) shows that the cysteine active region is well conserved in all vertebrates, as is the RX(R/K)R motif for propeptide convertases. The relationship of the Chinook salmon LEAP2 sequences with the other known fish LEAP2 sequences, especially the other salmonid LEAP2 sequences, is apparent in the phylogenetic tree obtained using amino acid data from selected vertebrate LEAP2 amino acid sequences (Figure 3). The formation of two groups of fish LEAP2 genes can clearly be seen within the tree, with Chinook salmon LEAP2.1 and 2.2 falling into one group (Type I) and LEAP2.3 falling into the other (Type II). Primers for each gene were designed (Supplementary material S8).

DISCUSSION

This study provides the first integrated characterisation of HAMP and LEAP2 paralogues in Chinook salmon, combining genomic analyses with a six‐month functional feeding trial. Three HAMP and three LEAP2 paralogues were identified, each retaining conserved structural motifs characteristic of their respective families, yet displaying divergent phylogenetic relationships and tissue‐specific expression patterns. Importantly, dietary supplementation with the phytobiotic NatControl™ resulted in significant, tissue‐specific modulation of both HAMP and LEAP2 paralogue expression.

The discovery of three functional HAMP paralogues in Chinook salmon aligns with reports from Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout, where multiple paralogues have been identified as a consequence of the salmonid‐specific whole‐genome duplication (Berthelot et al., 2014; Hilton & Lambert, 2008). Each Chinook HAMP encoded the canonical prepropeptide, including the signal sequence, propeptide convertase site and mature peptide with eight conserved cysteine residues. These features mirror the mammalian HAMP described in humans (Krause et al., 2000; Park et al., 2001), reinforcing the evolutionary conservation of this gene family. Phylogenetic analyses placed all Chinook HAMP genes within the HAMP1 clade, consistent with earlier work showing that salmonids generally lack HAMP2 homologues (Douglas et al., 2003; Muncaster et al., 2018). This restriction to HAMP1 paralogues suggests that functional diversification in salmonids has arisen through duplication and divergence within HAMP1 rather than maintenance of HAMP2. Constitutive expression of HAMP was highest in liver, the primary site of hepcidin production across vertebrates, but was also evident in spleen, kidney, gills and skin. This broad tissue distribution supports roles in both systemic iron regulation and local innate immunity, consistent with findings in other teleosts (Álvarez et al., 2013; Casadei et al., 2013).

In addition to HAMP, we identified three LEAP2 paralogues: LEAP2.1, LEAP2.2 and LEAP2.3. LEAP2.1 and LEAP2.2 clustered with Type I teleost LEAP2, while LEAP2.3 grouped within Type II. This duplication pattern matches that described in other salmonids (Liu et al., 2014), reflecting retention of paralogues after genome duplication and potential divergence in function. Constitutive expression revealed clear paralogue‐specific profiles: LEAP2.1 and LEAP2.2 were most abundant in liver and skin, while LEAP2.3 was primarily expressed in gills and skin. Such divergence implies subfunctionalisation, where duplicated genes partition ancestral functions across tissues (Force et al., 1999). LEAP2.1/2.2 may contribute predominantly to systemic and epithelial defence, while LEAP2.3 appears adapted for mucosal immunity at the gill surface, a key portal of pathogen entry.

Dietary supplementation with NatControl™ resulted in upregulation of HAMP and LEAP2 paralogue expression in a tissue‐ and paralogue‐specific manner. HAMP expression was upregulated across all tissues analysed, with the greatest increases observed in skin, head kidney and spleen. LEAP2.2 showed upregulation in multiple tissues, including head kidney, liver and skin. LEAP2.1 exhibited upregulation primarily in systemic tissues, with more variable responses in mucosal tissues. In contrast, LEAP2.3 showed upregulation restricted to the gills, where expression increased by up to 8‐fold relative to control fish. NatControl™ contains labdane diterpene compounds, which have been shown in mammalian and in vitro models to modulate immune pathways such as NF‐κB signalling, nitric oxide production and pro‐inflammatory cytokine release (Chiou et al., 1998; Xia et al., 2004). In fish, diterpene‐containing plant extracts have been reported to enhance macrophage activity, phagocytosis and expression of immune genes (Deachamag et al., 2006). The strong induction of HAMP and LEAP2 paralogues observed here suggests that these compounds may act upstream on pathogen recognition receptors or transcription factors that control AMP gene expression.

Study limitations and future directions

Although no significant differences in growth performance or survival were detected between fish fed the control and NatControl™ diets during the winter trial, the molecular responses observed remain biologically meaningful. The absence of growth impairment suggests that immune activation did not impose a substantial energetic cost, allowing normal somatic growth to be maintained. This is consistent with previous studies showing that dietary immunostimulants do not necessarily enhance growth under non‐challenging conditions, but may prime the immune system and improve disease resistance and survival when animals are exposed to pathogenic stress (Sakai, 1999; Vallejos‐Vidal et al., 2016). Although these results show an increase in innate immune system gene upregulation, this study was conducted under winter farming conditions with low baseline mortality and no disease outbreaks; therefore, the effects of NatControl™ supplementation on disease resistance could not be directly assessed. Future pathogen challenge trials are required to determine whether the observed upregulation of antimicrobial peptides translates into improved survival and reduced pathogen load. The NatControl™ diet elicited a structured AMP response that becomes more interpretable when considered alongside the distinct systemic versus local roles of hepcidin (HAMP) and the paralogue‐specific divergence of LEAP2 genes. The results suggest NatControl™ influences both central immune compartments and epithelial/mucosal barriers, with each AMP gene family contributing differently across tissues.

In conclusion, this study provides the first integrated characterisation of HAMP (LEAP‐1) and LEAP2 paralogues in Chinook salmon, revealing distinct tissue‐specific expression patterns consistent with subfunctionalisation. Dietary supplementation with the phytobiotic NatControl™ resulted in tissue‐specific upregulation of these antimicrobial peptides without compromising growth or survival under winter farming conditions. Together, these findings support the use of HAMP and LEAP2 as molecular biomarkers of nutritional immunomodulation and highlight the potential of functional feeds to enhance immune readiness in aquaculture.
