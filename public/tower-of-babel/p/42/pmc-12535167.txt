The pomegranate (Punica granatum L. cv. ‘Wonderful’) genome and P. protopunica shed light on pomegranate domestication. Is Daru a wild stock?
Authors: Alexandra Slonimsky, Oleg Lovky, Rotem Harel-Beja, Irit Bar-Ya’akov, Ravit Eshed, Amir Sherman, Doron Holland, Ron Ophir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe pomegranate is cultivated across a wide range of geographical regions worldwide for ornamental purposes, fruit aril for the food industry, and juice production. The pomegranate genome is reported to consist of 2n = 16 chromosomes in some cultivars and 2n = 18 in others. Clustering assembled contigs using Hi-C technology resulted in eight pseudochromosomes, alternatively, anchoring these contigs onto a genetic map produced nine pseudochromosomes. The origin of the domesticated pomegranate is unknown. Previous studies have suggested that the variant Daru, which is grown in the region of India and Nepal, is the origin of the wild stock.ResultsHere we explored the phylogeny, genetic structure, and substitution pattern of P. granatum. Phylogenetic analysis of Punica granatum accessions revealed that Daru seedlings constitute a distinct clade, which is a sister to the clade of Asian accessions, rather than an outgroup. No evidence of introgression was observed between Daru and cultivated accessions. Analysis of the non-synonymous to synonymous substitutions ratio in genic regions outlines a similar profile of the Daru and the domesticated pomegranates with overall positive selection, while the wild type P. protopunica illustrated a different profile with positive selection only in some regions. Domesticated pomegranate variants show low heterozygosity and nucleotide diversity, particularly on chromosome 1.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that differences in chromosome numbers may be associated with the formation of chromosome quadrivalents and their impact on the arrangement of contigs into pseudochromosomes. The profile of Kn to Ks ratio in domesticated pomegranates likely reflects positive selection. Genetic profiling of Daru, domesticated P. granatum, and wild-type P. protopunica revealed that Daru more closely resembles domesticated plants, challenging the notion that it represents the wild pomegranate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11993-0.

Introduction

The pomegranate (Punica granatum L.) is a member of the Myrtales order, together with Eucalyptus grandis, and is a clade in the Punicacea family. The fruit tree is endemic to hot regions in Afghanistan and Baluchistan but has been dispersed in a wide range of climate regions, from tropical to subtropical areas [1, 2]. Pomegranate heights ranging from 1.5 m to 7 m are classified as shrubs [1]. The pomegranate is considered a deciduous tree. However, some subspecies, mostly of Indian origin, are evergreen. A subset of the latter subspecies turns into fall when they grow under colder climates and, therefore, can be considered conditionally deciduous trees.

The Punica genus contains two species, P. granatum L. and Punica protopunica Balf. P. protopunica is endemic to the Socotra archipelago (located south of Yemen; [3]). It has larger, narrower leaves, different foliage, continuous flowering, smaller pink (not red) flowers, and small greenish-yellow fruits when ripens. Its distribution is limited only to this region. No breeding program is known for P. protopunica. In contrast, P. granatum is cultivated worldwide. Pomegranate is considered to be growing wild and generates landraces in Asia from northern Iran and Turkmenistan to northern India, Nepal, and eastern Europe. Cultivated pomegranate has been introduced to Europe, America, and the Middle East, making it adaptable to a wide range of climate conditions. Daru, which grows in north India and Nepal, is considered to be the wild stock of P. granatum [4, 5]. However, wild pomegranates are difficult to distinguish from cultivated ones [6]. Clonal propagation of pomegranate can be done by cutting or grafting [7]. Cutting is a common practice in many pomegranate-growing regions of the world [7]. Usually, it is done with Indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) treatment [7]. Adventitious root formation in cuttings is suggested to be the domestication trait [8]. Therefore, the wide range of usage (ornamental and as edible fruit), the wide range of geographical distribution makes it difficult to pinpoint the potential wild type, which wild type was originally picked for propagating by cuttings.

Several traits characterize the uniqueness of the pomegranate tree, such as the high content of antioxidants, punicalagin [9], and anthocyanin in the aril but mainly in the peel [10]. This high content of antioxidants makes the pomegranate a candidate for remedy, as presented in several studies [11, 12]. The ovary contains tens of ovules developed into coated seeds with arils in a unique structure of lower axial and upper parietal placentas [13, 14]. Hard-seeded are genetically differentiated from soft-seeded pomegranate variants in several regions, of which the largest region is 26.2 Mb on chromosome 1 [15]. Previously, the assembly of another pomegranate cultivar was reported to have different numbers of chromosomes. The de novo genome assemblies of ‘Dabenzi’ cultivar reported 2n = 18 [9], while the de novo genome assemblies of ‘Tunisia’ and ‘Moshiliu’ indicated genomic structure of 2n = 16 [15, 16]. The first reference genome, ‘Dabenzi’, sequenced with short reads platform (Illumina Hi-Seq 2000) and mate-pairs sequencing. The chromosomal level assembly was based on genetic map anchoring. ‘Taishanhong’ genome used the same platforms of short reads but not assembled to the chromosome level. Genome sequencing of ‘Tunisia’ was the first to use the single-molecule sequencing technology (SMRT) generating log reads on Pacific Bioscience Sequel platform. Likewise, for sequencing the ‘Moshiliu’ genome the same technologies were used [16].

Domesticated species are characterized by a trait that enables humans to control the crop lineage and a beneficial trait in which humans are interested. The beneficial trait, for example, could be what makes the fruit more edible, such as the reduction in cyanide in the Prunus dulcis kernel [8]. The “control” trait of fruit tree species exists in wild species, which is the ability for grafting, and therefore, no selection is required for the control trait. Thus, fruit trees are propagated by grafting once the beneficial trait is unraveled. Others, such as citrus and mango, are polyembryonic and can be cloned by sowing the nucellar seeds. Therefore, if fruit trees are domesticated solely on the basis of their beneficial trait, it is sometimes difficult to pinpoint the beneficial trait that is associated with domestication among all traits that have been accumulated during the proceeding crop cultivation.

In this work, we describe the assembly and the genomic structure of the ‘Wonderful’ cultivar and also examined the dispute on the number of chromosomes. We challenge the notation of Daru as a wild progenitor of domesticated pomegranate by mapping 23 accessions from the ARO breeding programs and two Daru seedlings to explore the genetic structure and diversity of the P. granatum. We also examine the substitutions in the coding sequences to estimate the whole genome selection and use the Punica protopunica as a true wild type relative to the domesticated.

Methods

Plant materials, DNA extraction and sequencing

P. granatum tree var. ‘Wonderful’ (PG-100-1) trees were grown in the nursery at the Newe-Yaar Center of the Agricultural Research Organization (ARO). We covered young leaves to obtain etiolated leaves and immediately froze them in liquid nitrogen. To remove residual polysaccharides and salts and to purify high-molecular-weight (HMW) genomic DNA, a salt: chloroform-based cleanup was performed following PacBio SampleNet protocols. DNA was adjusted to 500 µl with elution buffer with 1 M NaCl, and 2 mM EDTA, then extracted with phenol: chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1). After phase separation, the aqueous layer underwent a second salt wash using 1 M NaCl and 2 mM EDTA, followed by extraction with chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (24:1). Polysaccharides were selectively precipitated with 0.3 volumes of 99.99% ethanol, and the supernatant containing gDNA was transferred and precipitated with 1.7 volumes of ethanol. The DNA pellet was washed twice with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in 100 µl of TE buffer. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C to ensure complete solubilization.DNA was sequenced by preparing a 20 Kb library according to the PacBio manufacturer’s protocols and was run on nine cells of the Pacific Bioscience (PacBio) Sequel II platform.

Short reads were obtained by extracting DNA from 0.5 mg of young leaves that were picked from a P. protopunica tree and 25 P. granatum trees as part of the ARO germplasm collection (Table S1). The leaves were submitted to DNALink LTD. for DNA extraction. Genomic DNA was extracted from ~ 0.2 g of flash-frozen plant tissue using a modified CTAB protocol with a sorbitol pre-wash and chloroform-based purification. The entire process of DNA extraction was performed by JS Link (JS Link Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea). Ground tissue was incubated in sorbitol buffer (0.35 M sorbitol, 0.1 M Tris-HCl pH 7.6, 5 mM EDTA, 10 mM 2-mercaptoethanol) for 15 min to remove polyphenolic compounds, followed by centrifugation (14,000 x g, 8 min). The pellet was lysed in CTAB buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 20 mM EDTA, 1.4 M NaCl, 2% CTAB, 1% PVP) at 65 °C for 30 min. DNA was extracted with phenol: chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1), re-extracted with chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (24:1), and precipitated with 2/3 volume of isopropanol. After centrifugation (12,000 x g, 15 min), the pellet was washed twice with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in 200 µl of elution buffer. TruSeq library preparation, and sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform using the standard platform’s protocols to yield approximately 50 Gbp of 150 bp paired-end reads.

SNP calling and filtering

Adapter trimming was performed with trimmomatic-0.36 [17] followed by alignment to the pomegranate `Wonderful` cv. reference genome using Bowtie2 [18]. To assess genotype, we sorted reads, removed duplicates, and called genotype with the GenotypeCaller application as part of the GATK v. 4.2.5 tools kit [19]. The 27 P. granatum and P. protopunica sequencing files were run separately through that pipeline and combined using the `CombineGVCFs` application. A variant call format (VCF) file of all P. granatum variants and a VCF file with additional P. protopunica variant calls were created. Genomic sites with at least one missing call were removed. To retain high-quality variant calls a VCF file was imported to R with vcfR [20]. The genotype quality (GQ) and read depth (DP) were centered on their median (Fig. S1). Polymorphic sites with a minimum of less than two minimum absolute deviation (MAD) units from the median were filtered out. To avoid sites with possible paralogous variants, an upper bound of five MAD units was set. Hence, the lower bound was set to two MAD units less than the median were, and the upper bound set to five MAD units above the median. Sites with DP values in the range between the lower and upper bounds were retained.

Observed allele frequencies was fitted to the expected allele frequencies under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) using chi-square test. Sites were considered under HWE if the p-values > 0.1.

Genome assembly, scaffolding, and polishing

We assembled 65 Gbp of PacBio Sequel II (185X base pair depth) by running `Canu` software [21] as ‘canu useGrid = true gridOptions=”--partition = qh --get-user-env --export = ALL” gridEngineArrayMaxJobs = 99 java=/usr/bin/java -d run1 -p pomegranate genomeSize = 350 m -pacbio RO_69870.fasta’ with default parameters [22]. The assembly statistics were N50 = 2.9 Mbp and the L50 is 31 (Table S2). Two approaches were used to order the assembly scaffolds. One approach was using Hi-C and related protocols that use proximity ligation and massively parallel sequencing to detect the three-dimensional structure of the chromatin at the chromosome level [23]. We used the Hi-C Proximo-kit, which is based on DPNII enzyme-restriction cut by PhaseGenomics service. Ordering was performed using the LACHESIS (ligating adjacent chromatin enables scaffolding in situ) algorithm, which runs as follows [24]. First, we ran clustering to create groups that are equivalent to LG groups [25]. Then the minimum spanning tree (MST) algorithm was subsequently applied to order the scaffolds within each group. In the third step, the ordered scaffolds were oriented with respect to one another by precisely taking into account where the Hi-C reads were mapped on each scaffold. Finally, the Phase Genomics proprietary polishing step was applied to correct the orders within a pseudo-chromosome. The second approach was based on the published ‘Moti’ x ‘Wonderful’ segregating population map [25]. Markers of the genetic map were aligned to the 580 assembly contigs and running AllMaps [26]. We sequenced the ‘Wonderful’ cultivar with Illumina Nova-seq to get high-confidence coverage. Genome polishing was applied by alignment of 155,175,264 short-reads (60X) followed by the application of Pilon [27].

Genome annotation

Nine pseudochromosomes were submitted into the Maker2 pipeline [28]. Maker is a genome annotation pipeline that integrates a set of programs to predict the locations of the genes (exons and introns), repeats, tRNAs, pseudogenes, etc. The result is a general feature format (GFF) file, which specifies the positions of all predicted genome features. Our strategy for a list of true-positive genes is oulined in Fig. S5. We applied our published transcriptome and published pomegranate proteins as a training set for both ab initio gene prediction tools, Augustus [29] and SNAP [30]. The BUSCO set of proteins was used as a training set for Augustus to expand the prediction horizon and allow Augustus to learn from other angiosperm plants [31]. Optimization was performed by running three iterations of the algorithm. The predicted proteins were selected as proteins with a hit in the InterPro database [32] by running InterProScan with default parameters or proteins with Maker’s prediction value, and an annotation edit distance (AED) < = 0.25.

The GFF file was imported into a ‘GRanges’ object in the ‘rtracklayer’ R-package [33]. The non-redundant region of repetitive sequence was calculated by applying the ‘reduce’ function.

Genome size estimation

Adapter-cleaned short reads were aligned to the mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of Eucalyptus grandis, and the aligned reads were filtered out. The histogram generated using the Jellyfish [34]with m = 21 (k-mer of 21 bp) was loaded into findGSE R-package [35] to fit the count distribution and estimate the genome size (Fig. S2).

Genetic structure and diversity

The genetic structure of the 25 P. granatum accessions was analyzed using the ‘LEA’ R-package [36, 37]. The dataset for the structure analysis was the polymorphic sites subset that was under HWE. The sparse non-negative matrix factorization approach was applied for K values ranging from 2 to 10, with 20 replicates for each K and the entropy of each Q-matrix for each K/repetition was calculated. Q-probabilities were aligned across different K values by reordering them using the algorithms implemented in CLUMPP [38] and CLAMPAK [39] in an R script.

The average nucleotide diversity was calculated using vcftools with a window of 100 Kbp. The vcftools command was ‘vcftools --gzvcf pg.snp.vcf.gz --window-pi 100000 --out pg_pi’ (https://vcftools.sourceforge.net/).

Phylogenetic analysis

For the phylogenetic tree, we used the ape R-package [40]. The distance between accessions was defined as 1-proportion of shared allele (PSA) [41] We set the root and draw the tree using the ‘ggtree’ R-package.

Admixture analysis

The ABBA-BABA analysis was performed following the tutorial provided on the program’s GitHub repository [42, 43]. The Freq.py script ran using the western and eastern accessions as the test group and the Daru seedling as the contributor for the ancestral allele A or the derived allele B. The definition of the groups was according to the clades in the phylogenetic tree. P. protopunica was used as an outgroup to define the ancestral allele. The output file was imported into R, and the proportion of ABBA and BABA sites was calculated. The proportion of alleles in the groups was designated as p1, p2, and p3 for eastern accessions, western accessions, and Daru seedlings, respectively, whereas 1-pi is the ancestral allele. The proportion of ABBA sites was calculated as (1-p1)*p2*p3, and the proportion of BABA sites was calculated as p1*(1-p2)*p3. The D-statistic was calculated [Σ(ABBA) - Σ(BABA)]/[Σ(ABBA) + Σ(BABA)]. As the expected D is 0, the standrad D was calculated as Z = mean(D)/standard error(D). To test the statistical significance, we used the functions provided on the GitHub site to calculate the Block Jackknife method.

Results

Genome assembly and annotation

We sequenced the genome of the ‘Wonderful’ cultivar, an elite Israeli pomegranate (P. granatum L.), using single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing of the PacBio-Sequel II platform. A total of 314 Mb was assembled into 580 contigs with an N50 of 2.9 Mb. The assembly covers more than 90% of the estimated genome size (351 Mb) as estimated by the k-mer method (Fig. S2), which supports a previously reported estimation [9]. We applied Hi-C technology and genetic map anchoring to assemble the contigs into pseudo-chromosome level. The sequence reads of Hi-C fragments were clustered into eight pseudo-chromosomes ranging from 29.8 Mb to 57.8 Mb. The ‘Tunisia’ genome study used the same techniques and assembled eight pseudo-chromosomes as well [15]. Mapping the ‘Wonderful’ pseudo-chromosomes to the ‘Tunisia’ with at least 99% identity hits, covered 97% of the ‘Tunisia’ genome (Fig. S3). In addition, the genome assembly contigs were ordered into pseudo-chromosomes by anchoring to a genetic map [25]. The outcome of anchoring the pseudo-chromosomes to nine linkage groups of the pomegranate genetic map was a chromosome split into two, corresponding to LG8 and LG9 (Fig. S4). The completeness of the genome was estimated to be 96% (1329 of 1375) by running benchmarking universal single-copy orthologs (BUSCO v 3.02; [31]). Only 1.5% of the assembly was estimated to be fragmented, and 1.3% was predicted as duplicated. Assembly polishing corrected 12,499 single-base sequencing errors, 295,482 assembly errors that turned out to be small insertions, and 6,041 that led to small deletions covering a total of 452,240 bps 0.15% of total assembly).

Genome annotation included gene prediction, repetitive sequences, tRNAs, and their genomic coverage delineated in Fig. 1A. We used the previously published whole-transcriptome [41] and previously published pomegranate proteins [9] as a training set in the Maker2 pipeline. A total of 33,895 genes were predicted, of which 29,055 are full-length proteins. The median number of exons per gene was four, whereas the most frequent number of exons per gene was two (Fig. 1B). The proportion of repetitive regions of the pomegranate genome was 33.1%. The majority of the repeats consisted of transposable elements (TEs), which covered 31.6% of the pomegranate genome, with a range from 20.9% (Chr9) to 35.6% (Chr1). The abundant TEs are long-terminal repeats (LTR) members of the Copia and Gypsy families. Those TEs were an order of magnitude more frequent than LINEs, DNA transposons, and the rolling-circle TE, Helitron (Table 1; Table S3).

SNP calling and diversity

To explore the diversity of P. granatum, we sequenced 23 accessions from the Agricultural Research Organization (ARO) collection, two Daru seedlings, and P. protopunica as an outgroup (Table S1). A total of 1,960,014 polymorphic sites were called. We removed sites with no calls and retained 1,465,903 sites with biallelic single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). A clean set of 410,255 SNPs after quality filtering of reads’ depth (DP) and genotype quality (GQ) parameters. A subset of 333,393 sites was estimated to be under HWE.

We explored the expected heterozygosity under HWE (Fig. 2A) and the average nucleotide differences per site between the accessions, i.e., nucleotide diversity (π; Fig. 2B). The expected heterozygosity (He) ranged from 0.039 to 0.5 with an average of 0.17 ± 0.14. The nucleotide diversity ranged from 7.7 × 10−7 to 2.99 × 10−3 with an average of 1.67 × 10−4 ± 1.54 × 10−4. The average He values were 0.15 ± 0.11, 0.17 ± 0.14, 0.17 ± 0.14, 0.17 ± 0.14, 0.18 ± 0.15, 0.18 ± 0.14, 0.16 ± 0.14, 0.2 ± 0.16, and 0.18 ± 0.16 for chromosomes Chr1 to Chr9, respectively. The longest (~ 20 Mb) region of low heterozygosity and nucleotide diversity was estimated to be on Chr1. These findings indicated high selective constraints in this region. Although recurrent selection during the cultivation of domesticated pomegranate might be a possible explanation, a comparison to wild type data requires supporting that. An increase in genetic variability was detected in chromosomes Chr8 and Chr9 relative to the other chromosomes. Interestingly, a discrepancy between high He and low π was observed on Chr2 and Chr5, which might indicate balancing selection.

Genetic structure of pomegranate

To examine the genetic relationships among Punica accessions, we constructed a phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3A) with P. protopunica as an outgroup. The accessions of western cultivation, i.e., from Israel, Spain, and California, and the accession of eastern cultivation, i.e., Iran, China, and India, were arranged in two clades. Interestingly, Daru seedlings, although separately clustered, diverged from the common ancestor of the eastern cultivars. This result does not support the previous suggestion that Daru is the wild-type ancestor of pomegranate domestication [1, 44]. Moreover, we explored the genetic structure of the germplasm collection in a range from K = 2 to K = 5 populations. Insignificant residuals (less than 1%) of the Daru genome can be found in western cultivars in only two accessions, the Mollar, and the Rachel cultivars (Fig. 3B, cluster 2). Whereas six accessions contain minor traces of ‘Daru’. This finding reinforces the suggestion that ‘Daru’ is not the wild-type stock of domesticated pomegranate. The minimum entropy (Fig S4) is K = 3, which is congruent with the phylogenetic tree and substantiates the division of two cultivated groups and the ‘Daru’.

Genetic variation in genic regions

Detection of substitutions in genic regions can elucidate the changes in functionality that have occurred during the evolutionary history, the domestication event, and the cultivation. The ratio of transition to transversion substitutions (Ti/Tv) is 2.04. Such value was previously observed in plants [45]. The Ti/Tv ratio for synonymous substitutions was only 3.40. Both values are indications of natural polymorphisms [46], excluding the possibility that we observed sequencing errors. Among P. granatum accessions, most of the genes (71%) had no change, and the remaining genes (29%) had at least one substitution in any of the accessions. Hereafter, we focused on homozygous substitutions of the derived allele to ensure that we estimated the substitutions’ functionality. As expected, the proportions of the homozygous genes to the derived allele (HGDAs) corresponded with their distance from the western cultivar, ‘Wonderful’, which is the reference genome. In the western accessions, 32% of the mutated genes were HGDAs, 44% of the genes in the eastern cultivars were HGDAs, and 60% in the ‘Daru’ accessions. However, all HGDAs retained either synonymous changes or minor missense substitutions, e.g., a replacement of a hydrophobic amino acid with another hydrophobic amino acid. In some accessions, HGDAs accumulated nonsense (NS) substitutions. An accession with HDGAs for NS substitution might have no functional copy of those genes.

Kni, j is the number of nonsynonymous substitutions corresponding to accession (j) and gene (i), whereas Ksi, j is the number of synonymous substitutions corresponding to accession (j) and gene (i). The rate of Kn to Ks ranges from − 1 for deleterious selection to + 1 for advantageous selection, and 0 is the neutral selection effect. The median Kn/Ks was above zero in the two breeding clades (the western and the eastern) and for the Daru accessions, but slightly lower. The median Kn/Ks of P. protopunica was close to zero (Fig. 4A). P. protopunica, which has no known breeding program, can be depicted as a wild-type reference to compare the Kn/Ks ratios in the wild-type species. It seems that domesticated species were prone to positive selection, and this observation withdraws the claim of Daru as a genuine wild type (Fig. 4C). The Kn/Ks profile of western cultivars resembles the profile of eastern cultivars, but it differs from the Daru and the P. protopunica (Fig. 4B). For simplicity in the graph we designated the clade in phylogenetic tree as G1 for the eastern accessions, G2 for the western accessions, and G3 for the Daru seedlings. Clearly, the three groups, G1-3, accumulated more non-sense substitutions than did the P. protopunica, especially at chromosomes 2, 4, and 5 (Fig. 4D). Once more, it puts the ‘Daru’ with domesticated accessions.

Discussion

Genome assembly and chromosome number

The bivalent number of P. granatum chromosomes varies in the literature. An early study of cytological analysis suggested that the number of 2n chromosomes is 16 [47]. Later, a research group from India examined chromosome behavior during meiosis and suggested that the number of chromosomes should be 2n = 18. They explained that the formation of the quadrivalent can lead to the misinterpretation of chromosome number [48]. The quadrivalent is a result of translocation, which occurs in some proportion, and the gametes with quadrivalent chromosomes create inviable zygotes [49]. In the next generation sequencing epoch, the estimation of chromosome number can be indicated by ordering sequencing contigs into pseudochromosome. Three pomegranate genomes have been published with assemblies at the pseudochromosomes level. The genome assembly of ‘Dabenzi’ [9] anchored the scaffold to the genetic map, whereas the genome assembly of ‘Tunisia’ [15] and the genome assembly of ‘Moshiliu’ [16] used Hi-C technology to order the scaffolds into pseudochromosomes. The number of chromosomes varies from 2n = 18 for the ‘Dabenzi’ genome to 2n = 16 for the ‘Tunisia’ genomes. We clustered the ‘Wonderful’ genome contigs once via Hi-C technology, resulting in n = 8 chromosomes, and anchored those contigs on a genetic map [25] to n = 9 linkage groups. We mapped the nine chromosomes of the ‘Wonderful’ genome with the eight publicly available chromosomes of ‘Tunisia’ (Fig. S3). Similar to our study, the hits of chromosomes 8 and 9 aligned with one chromosome defined by Hi-C clustering of the ‘Tunisia’ pomegranate genome. The usage of Hi-C technology for chromosome-level assembly as alternative to genetic map anchoring is a new methodology. The artifacts and errors associated with the methodology require more investigation to improve the outcomes. For instance, incorrect alignment of Hi-C reads to repeat regions can lead to long-range contacts [50]. Most of the applications that were developed assume prior knowledge about the expected number of chromosomes. Moreover, the emergence of new sequencing technology capable of producing telomere-to-telomere genome assembly may corroborate that determination [51]. Therefore, we support the conclusion of Raman et al., (1971) that the impression of 2n = 16 caused by technology artifacts [48], although the technology has been changed.

The proportion of repetitive regions (RRs) in the pomegranate genome was reported to be 40.5% for ‘Dabenzi’ genome [9], 51.2% for ‘Taishanhong’ [13], and 50.93% for ‘Tunisia’ genome [15]. None of those studies outlined how they calculated the RRs. Notably, two research groups reported two substantially different RRs proportions, 26.74% and 52% for the genome of the same cultivar, ‘Bhagawa’ [52, 53]. This discrepancy suggests that the choice of calculation approach can significantly influence repeat proportion estimates. Likewise, the proportion without reducing the overlaps in ‘Wonderful’ genome resulted in 61.4%. Since there are overlaps among the RRs that are counted, we surmise that the aforementioned proportions are an overestimation.

Punica granatum diversity

P. granatum diversity was estimated using genome-wide biallelic SNPs. The average He was 0.17, and the average π was 1.67 × 10−4. The nucleotide diversity of pomegranate is lower than that of other domesticated perennial crops such as peach (1.5 × 10−3) [54], cassava (2.6 × 10−3) [55], and date palm (9.2 × 10−3) [56]. Previous studies that assessed the diversity of pomegranate germplasm used microsatellites, which estimated higher heterozygosity. Comparative studies showed that SNPs are more accurate in estimating diversity than microsatellites [44, 57, 58]. Nonetheless, previous studies on pomegranate diversity included more wild-type accessions and significantly fewer polymorphic sites. Our genome-wide study showed that the average He was around 0.17 for most of the chromosomes except Chr1, which on average had heterozygosity, whereas Chr8 and Chr9 were the most heterozygous. Interestingly, chromosome 1 contains a large genomic region (~ 20 Mb) with a reduction in both heterozygosity and nucleotide diversity (π). The reduction over a large region implies that selective constraints sweep, probably due to low recombination. Other interesting regions are those with high He and low π estimation, such as those on Chr2 and Chr5. A possible explanation for that discrepancy can be that in a case where one breeding program (western accessions) selects one allele and the other program (eastern accessions) selects the other allele, the diversity (nucleotide difference) within each breeding system is low. However, the estimation of overall He increases when the selective constraints are relaxed.

Domestication and genetic structure

The genetic structure of the cultivated pomegranate of worldwide germplasm has been previously studied based on 480 SNPs [41]. The present work, based on genome-wide (hundreds of thousands of SNPs), included the ‘Daru’ seedlings and P. protopunica as an outgroup. The reconstructed phylogenetic tree supports the split into two major clades, a western accession from Israel, Spain, and California, and the accession of eastern cultivation, i.e., Iran, China, and India. Moreover, the ‘Daru’ generated a distinct clade, which was found to be sister to the eastern accessions clade. We expected that if ‘Daru’ had been a genuine wild type of all pomegranate, it would have diverged earlier to domestication rather than split from the eastern clade during the crop breeding. The genomic structure depicted by the Q-proportion barplot corroborates the three distinct genetic clusters. The western and eastern accessions presented common traces in each other’s genomes, suggesting that they have a shared origin. Moreover, this analysis and the introgression test using the ABBA-BABA method revealed no traces of the ‘Daru’ genome in either the eastern or western accessions as D was not statistically different from zero.

A few wild pomegranate species have been suggested in the literature. Gregory M. Levin [59], who investigated the Turkmenistan collection, suggested that the wild pomegranate spread from the Balkan. However, the largest wild-type collection was reported in Kopetdagh [59]. Turkmenistan is the midpoint of the two breeding clades [41]. Wild pomegranates grow in Albania [60], Transcaucasia, Central Asia [2, 6], North India [4, 44, 61], and China [62]. However assessing the origin of domesticated P. granatum is difficult, as the wild stock has not been identified. We used the P. protopunica, which has no breeding program to the best of our knowledge, and the diversity in the genic regions to illustrate the differences between wild and domesticated species. Two phenomena were observed in the clades of domesticated accessions relative to the P. protopunica species. The number of nonsense substitutions markedly increased as if a set of genes were dispensable under cultivation conditions. The overall nonsynonymous-to-synonymous ratio, i.e., the positive selection, also increased. This is probably due to the process of selecting new functions during breeding programs. These changes in domesticated species relative to the wild protopunica were observed in ‘Daru’ seedlings, which makes the ‘Daru’ more resemble the domesticated crop than the wild species. This might raise the question of Daru’s origin, which might be a feral escape.

Conclusion

Two key points were outlined in this study. First, the Hi-C method for chromosome-level assembly is based on DNA structure. Although it is efficient and cost-effective, it is important to consider that chromosomal aberrations may influence the results. We suggest that inconsistency in chromosome numbers among cultivars is method-dependent. Second, regarding the question of what the wild stock in pomegranate is, further study is required. Our findings corroborate the exclusion of ‘Daru’ from the list. The domesticated P. granatum split into two groups: the eastern cultivars and the western cultivars. That separation is reflected in the genetic structure while the ‘Daru’ seems to retain different alleles from both groups. For all domesticated accessions and ‘Daru’, a positive selection was detected in the genic region by Kn/Ks ratio. In contrast, the a wild species P. protopunica showed nearly neutral evolution with average Kn/Ks ratio close to zero.
