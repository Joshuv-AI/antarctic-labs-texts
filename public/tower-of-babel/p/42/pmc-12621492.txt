Chlorogenic acid carbon dots inhibit NDV proliferation by suppressing virus-induced mitochondrial autophagy and MAVS degradation in host cells
Authors: Qian Yang, Luqing Zhang, Shuyao Yang, Hangyu Li, Yanwen Yang, Yi Liao, Yao Wang, Haibo Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chlorogenic acid (CGA) is widely used to inhibit virus replication and transmission. However, it lacks targeting capabilities and sustained-release properties. In contrast, carbon quantum dots can not only inhibit virus replication directly but can also serve as drug carriers to enhance the targeting capacity and efficacy of antiviral drugs. In this study, CGA carbon dots (CGA-CDs) were prepared using a microwave-assisted synthesis method. The physicochemical characteristics and antiviral activities of CGA-CDs against Newcastle disease virus (NDV) were explored through a series of experiments. The average particle size of CGA-CDs was 5.675 ± 0.15 nm. The CGA-CDs were non-ordered carbon structures composed of C and O, with uniform size, good dispersion properties, and a surface rich in –COOH, –OH, and other hydrophilic groups. The optimal excitation wavelength of CGA-CDs was 390 nm, the emission wavelength was 450 nm, and the CGA-CDs thus emitted blue fluorescence. Notably, CGA-CDs produced significantly stronger antiviral effects than equivalent concentrations of CGA and could successfully inhibit virus adsorption and replication. Furthermore, CGA-CDs significantly inhibited virus-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, mitochondrial autophagy, and mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) degradation, providing superior antiviral efficacy. In vivo experiments further demonstrated the good biocompatibility of CGA-CDs. Moreover, in NDV-infected chickens, CGA-CDs significantly reduced the viral load in the spleen and proventriculus, alleviated pathological damage to major organs, enhanced antibody responses, and markedly lowered the mortality rate. In summary, CGA-CDs showed significant anti-NDV activity by inhibiting NDV adsorption and replication, likely through the inhibition of virus-induced mitophagy and MAVS degradation. These findings highlight the potential of CGA-CDs as potent antiviral agents.

Introduction

Chlorogenic acid (CGA) is a natural polyphenolic compound that is present in coffee, tea, and various fruits and is commonly used in Traditional Chinese Medicine. Research indicates that CGA not only possesses anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties but can also inhibit virus replication and transmission through multiple mechanisms [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. For instance, Abaidullah et al. demonstrated that CGA positively regulates the MDA5, TLR7, and NF-κB signaling pathways, enhancing host antiviral responses against γ-coronaviruses [5]. Meanwhile, Li et al. discovered that the combined use of CGA and baicalein significantly inhibits the replication of infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV) in chicken embryos and mitigates inflammatory responses [6]. Furthermore, Ding et al. showed that CGA inhibits neuraminidase activity in influenza viruses (H1N1/H3N2), thereby preventing the release and spread of viral particles [7]. In addition, evidence also indicates that CGA can inhibit virus replication by inducing apoptosis. In particular, Shi et al. demonstrated that CGA inhibits the release of porcine δ-coronavirus (PDCoV) via apoptotic induction [8]. However, pure CGA is not widely used in medicine owing to its poor solubility and low in vivo bioavailability. To overcome these limitations, CGA is commonly encapsulated in inorganic carriers. However, the synthesis of such nanoparticles requires relatively complex processes and is time-consuming. Therefore, simple, safe, and effective methods to enhance the bioavailability, solubility, and antiviral activity of CGA are currently needed.

Carbon quantum dots are carbon-based nanoparticles that are smaller than 10 nm in size. These nanoparticles possess excellent photoluminescence properties, a high specific surface area, and an abundance of surface functional groups [9]. Owing to these characteristics, carbon quantum dots show broad application prospects in the field of biomedicine, especially in antiviral research [10]. Carbon quantum dots can not only inhibit virus replication directly but can also serve as drug carriers to enhance the targeting capacity and efficacy of antiviral drugs [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Papi et al. highlighted the broad application prospects of carbon-based nanomaterials in the COVID-19 era, especially for the development of antiviral drugs [15].

The functional groups present on the surface of carbon quantum dots can interact with the proteins and nucleic acids on the surface of viral particles, thereby preventing viruses from binding to host cells. Hsieh et al. showed that functionalized graphene quantum dots can effectively inhibit the replication of coronaviruses and enteroviruses by interacting with viral surface proteins [16]. Additionally, Ting et al. demonstrated that curcumin-based cationic carbon quantum dots can act as multisite inhibitors against enterocoronavirus, effectively inhibiting viral replication [17]. Finally, Lin et al. found that loading curcumin onto carbon quantum dots can significantly enhance its antiviral activity [18].

Newcastle disease virus (NDV) is a highly contagious avian virus responsible for serious economic losses in the global poultry industry [[19], [20], [21]]. Despite the widespread use of vaccines and the adoption of effective prevention and control strategies, NDV remains a serious threat due to its evolution dynamics and the use of non-standard farming practices. In recent years, advances in nanotechnology and natural medicine have led to significant progress in the treatment of NDV infections. Fan et al. found that icariin polysaccharides and propolis flavonoids can synergistically inhibit the infectivity of NDV and improve treatment success rates in cases of Newcastle disease [22]. In addition, Zhang et al. found that flavonoids can synergistically inhibit NDV infection in host cells and enhance the protection rate offered by Newcastle disease vaccines [23]. In their latest study, Wang et al. demonstrated that Canthin-6-one analogs can effectively block the proliferation of NDV by inhibiting the Akt and ERK pathways [24]. Meanwhile, Alkhalefa et al. demonstrated the significant antiviral activity of propolis and chitosan nanoparticles against genotype VII NDV in vitro [25], and Nazaktabar et al. showed that poly (rhodanine) nanoparticles exhibit good anti-NDV activity and low toxicity in chicken embryos [26].

In this study, we explored the potential antiviral effects of CGA carbon quantum dots (CGA-CDs) against NDV and explored the mechanisms underlying these effects. First, carbon quantum dots were prepared using CGA as a precursor. Subsequently, a series of experiments revealed that CGA-CDs exhibit good antiviral effects and can prevent NDV adsorption, replication, and proliferation by inhibiting virus-induced mitochondrial autophagy and mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) degradation in host cells.

Materials and methods

Materials

CGA was purchased from MACKLIN. Chloroquine (CQ), the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) detection kit, the anti-NDV-HN polyclonal antibody, and the reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection kit were purchased from Solarbio. The anti-MAVS monoclonal antibody, anti-LC3B monoclonal antibody, and anti-SQSTM1 (P62) monoclonal antibody were purchased from HUABIO. Mito Tracker Green, LysoTracker Red, and Hoechst 33342 live cell staining solution were purchased from Beyotime.

DF-1 cells, the NDV Mukteswar strain, and the NDV-F plasmid were provided by the Tibetan Plateau Ethnoveterinary Medicine Laboratory of Southwest Minzu University.

Preparation of CGA-CDs

First, 0.5 g of CGA was dissolved in 10 mL of pure water. Then, 10 mL of the CGA aqueous solution was transferred to a 200 mL crucible. The crucible was placed in a microwave oven at 700 W for 10 min for the microwave-assisted synthesis of CGA-CDs. The crucible was removed and cooled to room temperature, and the brown precipitate formed at the bottom of the crucible was dissolved in 10 mL of pure water to obtain unpurified CGA-CDs. The mixture was passed through a 0.22-μm microporous membrane. Then, it was transferred to a 2000 Da dialysis bag activated with pure water (100 °C for 10 min). The dialysis bag containing the filtered mixture was placed overnight in pure water, which was changed every 6 h, to obtain an aqueous mixture of purified CGA-CDs. After low-temperature vacuum freeze-drying, the final CGA-CDs were obtained, sealed, and stored at room temperature.

The yield was calculated based on the ratio of the solid mass of CGA-CDs obtained via freeze-drying (mproduct) to the input mass of CGA (mCGA = 0.50 g), as follows:Yield(%)=mproductmCGA×100%

Characterization of CGA-CDs

Characterization experiments were conducted using pure water obtained from a Millipore water purification system (MilliQ, Millipore, 18.2 M resistivity). The morphology of CGA-CDs was measured using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEOL, Japan) at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. CGA-CDs were also examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. Subsequently, the ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) absorption spectra of the nanoparticles were recorded using the Nicolet Evolution 300 Ultraviolet–Visible spectrometer (Thermo, USA), and the fluorescence characteristics were analyzed based on their fluorescence spectra. The chemical composition and functional groups of CGA-CDs were identified using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Avatar 300, USA), where FTIR samples were prepared by pressing a mixture of CGA-CDs and potassium bromide into thin tablets. Finally, the functional groups on the surface of CGA-CDs were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) (ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher).

Cell culture and toxicity assay

Frozen chicken embryo fibroblasts (DF-1) were thawed in a 37 °C water bath, transferred to a centrifuge tube containing 2 mL Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM), and centrifuged for 5 min at 1000 rpm and 26 °C. The supernatant was removed, and the cells were suspended in 1 mL of fresh complete DMEM containing serum (10 % fetal bovine serum [FBS]) and penicillin–streptomycin (1 %). The cells were then inoculated in a cell culture flask and cultured in a 5 % CO2 incubator at 37 °C. After the DF-1 cells formed a monolayer, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. An appropriate amount of 0.25 % pancreatic enzyme (including EDTA) was added to digest the cells for 30 s. The cells were then gently washed with DMEM, resuspended in the same medium, and transferred to a new cell culture flask (1:2 dilution). Finally, DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS was added, and the cells were incubated under standard culture conditions.

When these cells reached 80–90 % confluence, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. The cells were digested, washed, resuspended in DMEM, and transferred again to a new cell culture flask, as described above. After incubation in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS under standard culture conditions, 100 μL of the cell suspension was seeded in each well of a 96-well plate. When cell confluence reached 80–90 %, the medium was discarded, and 100 μL of PBS was added to wash the cells twice. The cells were incubated in culture medium containing 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, 500, and 1000 μg/L CGA-CDs or equivalent amounts of CGA. Meanwhile, a negative control group and a blank group were also established. The cells were cultured at 37 °C for 24 h in a 5 % CO2 cell incubator. The medium was subsequently discarded, and 100 μL DMEM and 10 μL CCK-8 were added to each well. The absorbance (OD) of each well was measured at 450 nm using an enzyme-labeler. Cell viability was calculated as follows:Cellviability(%)=(ODexperimentalwell−ODblankwell)/(ODnegativecontrolwell−ODblankwell)

Virus culture and titer measurements

DF-1 cells were cultured in a monolayer. When optimal confluence was reached, the medium was discarded, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. Then, an appropriate amount of the viral suspension was added. After 90 min of adsorption, the viral suspension was discarded, and maintenance medium was added. The cells were cultured for 48 h. The culture flask was sealed and stored at-80 °C. After freezing and thawing twice, the culture medium was collected and centrifuged (4 °C and 3000 rpm for 10 min) to obtain the supernatant, which was stored at −80 °C.

A 100 μL suspension of DF-1 cells was added to each well of a 96-well cell culture plate and incubated in a 5 % CO2 incubator at 37 °C until the cells formed a monolayer. The virus suspension was serially diluted using DMEM (10−1 to 10−10), with 900 μL of each dilution being prepared. After discarding the medium from each well, the cells were washed three times with PBS. The corresponding virus dilutions were then added to each column of the plate (six wells per column), and 100 μL of virus suspension was also added per well. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 90 min to allow viral adsorption. After incubation, the supernatant was discarded, and 100 μL of maintenance medium (containing 2 % FBS) was added to each well. The cells were cultured for 72 h, and the number of wells with more than 50 % cells showing cytopathic effects was recorded. The 50 % tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) was then calculated using the Reed–Muench method.

Evaluation of antiviral effects

DF-1 cells were cultured in a six-well cell culture plate until they reached 80–90 % confluence. The medium was discarded, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. The experiment included three groups: the CGA group, the CGA-CDs group, and a non-drug treatment control group. The virus suspension was diluted in maintenance medium containing different drugs, and 500 TCID50 of the virus suspension was added to each well. After 90 min of adsorption in an incubator, 125 μg/mL of CGA and 62.5 μg/mL, 125 μg/mL, and 250 μg/mL of CGA-CDs were added to the respective wells. The cells were incubated at 37 °C under 5 % CO2 for 24 and 48 h, respectively. After this, the cell culture plates were subjected to two freeze–thaw cycles at −80 °C. Finally, samples were collected for TCID50 detection, and RNA was extracted for RT-qPCR analysis.

In another set of experiments, DF-1 cells were cultured in a six-well plate until they reached 80–90 % confluence. The culture medium was then discarded, and the wells were washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, 500 TCID50 of the virus suspension was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 90 min to allow viral adsorption. After incubation, CGA (125 μg/mL) and CGA-CDs (62.5 μg/mL, 125 μg/mL, and 250 μg/mL) were added, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 incubator. Following 2 h, 4 h, and 6 h of culture, the plates were subjected to two freeze–thaw cycles at −80 °C. Finally, samples were collected for RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis.

Changes in virus adsorption and replication and the release of progeny viruses following treatment

Dilute the viral solution using 2 % maintenance medium containing different concentrations of CGA-CDs.Then, 500 TCID50 of each treated virus suspension was added to six-well cell culture plates with cells showing 80–90 % confluence. After 90 min of adsorption, the cell culture plates were subjected to two freeze–thaw cycles at −80 °C. Samples were collected for hemagglutination testing, and RNA was extracted for RT-qPCR analysis.

In another experiment, 500 TCID50 of the virus suspension diluted with maintenance medium was added to six-well cell culture plates containing cells with 80–90 % confluence. After 90 min of adsorption, maintenance medium containing 125 μg/mL CGA-CDs was added. A control group without drug treatment was also set up. The cells were incubated for 12, 24, and 36 h. Indirect immunofluorescence staining, western blot analysis, and TCID50 detection were subsequently performed.

For the final experiment, 500 TCID50 of the virus suspension was added to each well of a six-well cell culture plate and allowed to adsorb for 90 min. Maintenance medium containing different concentrations of CGA-CDs was then added. The culture medium from each well was collected at 12, 24, 36, and 48 h of incubation and centrifuged at 4 °C and 3000 rpm for 10 min. Finally, the supernatant was collected for RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis.

Targeted energy metabolism analysis

DF-1 cells were cultured to 80–90 % confluence in a 100-mm cell culture dish. The culture medium was discarded, and the dish was washed twice with PBS. The virus was diluted with maintenance medium containing CGA-CDs to a final concentration of 500 TCID50. The experiment included an untreated virus group and an unvaccinated control group, with six replicates established per group. The virus was adsorbed onto the cells in the culture dish for 90 min. After adsorption, the unadsorbed virus particles were removed, and maintenance medium containing CGA-CDs or without CGA-CDs was added. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Following incubation, DMEM was discarded, and pre-cooled PBS (4 °C) was added. The cells were gently shaken for 1 min, and the washing step was repeated twice to remove the culture medium. The culture dishes were placed on ice, and more pre-cooled PBS (4 °C) was added. The cells were quickly scraped onto one side of the dish using a scraper, and the dish was tilted to collect the cell suspension into a pre-cooled centrifuge tube. The cells were centrifuged at 800 rpm to remove the supernatant. The cell pellets were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 1 min, stored at −80 °C, and then sent to Shanghai Baipu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for targeted cell energy metabolism analysis.

Assessment of MMP and ROS

DF-1 cells were cultured in six-well cell culture plates until they reached 80–90 % confluence. The culture medium was discarded, and the plates were washed twice with PBS. The experiment included an untreated virus group (control) as well as a negative control group and a CCCP injury group. After 90 min of viral adsorption in the cell incubator, the unadsorbed virus particles were removed, and the plates were washed once with PBS. Maintenance medium containing CGA and CGA-CDs was then added, and the cells were cultured for 24 h.

In the MMP experiment, the cells were treated with maintenance medium diluted 1:1000 with CCCP and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. The plates were washed twice with PBS, and then 2 mL of maintenance medium containing JC-1 (1 × concentration) was added to each well. The cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. After incubation, the plates were washed three times with pre-cooled ultrapure water containing buffer (1 × ), and finally, 1 mL of maintenance medium was added. The cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

For ROS detection, DCFH-DA was diluted in serum-free medium (1:1000) to achieve a final concentration of 10 μmol/L. After removing the culture medium, an appropriate volume of the diluted DCFH-DA solution (usually no less than 1 mL per well) was added to ensure that the cells were adequately covered. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with serum-free culture medium to remove any unincorporated DCFH-DA. Finally, 1 mL of maintenance medium was added to each well, and the fluorescence was observed and recorded using a fluorescence microscope.

Assessment of mitochondrial and lysosomal co-localization

A 1 mM Mito-Tracker Green stock solution was diluted at a ratio of 1:500, while Lyso-Tracker Red was mixed with maintenance medium at a 1:13,333 ratio. Meanwhile, the culture medium was removed from the cell culture plates, and the plates were washed twice with PBS. Next, 2 mL of the staining medium was added to each well, and the cells were placed in an incubator for 45 min. After staining, an appropriate amount of Hoechst 33342 live cell staining solution (100 × ) was added evenly to the culture medium to achieve a final concentration of 1 × , and the mixture was gently agitated. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Following incubation, the dye-containing culture medium was discarded, and the cells were washed thrice with PBS. Finally, the cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

RNA extraction and fluorescence quantitative RT-PCR

A 300 μL sample was added to an enzyme-free tube, followed by 700 μL of TRIzol reagent. The samples were homogenized and left at room temperature for 5 min to allow the complete separation of the nucleic acid–protein complex. Then, 200 μL of chloroform was added to each tube. The tubes were shaken for 15 s and incubated at room temperature for 3 min. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min. The upper, colorless aqueous phase was carefully transferred to a new enzyme-free tube. An equal volume of isopropanol was added to each tube, and the tube was mixed thoroughly and left to stand at 25 °C for 10 min to allow RNA precipitation. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the RNA pellet was washed with 1 mL of 75 % ethanol (prepared with RNase-free ddH2O). The samples were centrifuged again at 10,000 rpm and 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the RNA pellet was air-dried at 25 °C for 3–5 min. Finally, 30 μL of RNase-free ddH2O was added to dissolve the RNA. The solution was gently pipetted to ensure complete dissolution, and the RNA was stored at −80 °C.

The virus F gene copy number was determined using Takara's PrimeScript™ FAST RT reagent kit with gDNA Eraser and TB Green Premix Ex TaqII FAST qPCR. Indirect immunofluorescence assay

Cells were seeded in a six-well culture plate. After discarding the culture medium and washing twice with PBS, the cells in each well were fixed with 1 mL of pre-cooled methanol at 4 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with 5 % bovine serum albumin at 25 °C for 45 min. Next, 1 mL of primary antibody was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Following primary antibody incubation, 500 μL of the secondary antibody was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 1 h. Finally, 100 μL of DAPI staining solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 20 min. Each step was followed by three washes with PBST (PBS containing 0.5 % Tween-20), with each wash lasting 5 min. After the final wash, the cells were observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Western blot

The protein sample was mixed with loading buffer at a ratio of 4:1 and incubated at 100 °C for 10 min. After cooling, western blot analysis was performed to quantitatively detect the relevant proteins.

Following the manufacturer's instructions, protein samples were separated using both stacking and separating gels. Electrophoresis was carried out under a constant voltage of 80 V, and protein separation was performed at 120 V until the protein bands reached the bottom of the gel. The gel was cut to isolate the target protein bands, and nitrocellulose (NC) membranes were cut in similar shapes. The protein gels and NC membranes were placed together and positioned between filter paper or sponges. This assembly was placed in an electroblotting device and fully immersed in pre-cooled transfer buffer. The device was kept on ice, and a constant current of 200 mA was applied for 1.5 h to transfer the proteins onto the NC membranes.

After the transfer, the rubber block was removed, and the NC membranes were soaked in blocking solution (5 % skimmed milk powder in TBS) with gentle shaking at 60 rpm for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were washed three times using TBST (TBS containing 0.5 % Tween-20), with each wash lasting 10 min. The membranes were then incubated with a diluted primary antibody at 4 °C overnight with gentle shaking. After another round of washes, the membranes were incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature, with gentle shaking in the dark. Washes were repeated, and the protein bands were finally visualized using a high-sensitivity chemiluminescence detection system.

Determination of the median embryo lethal dose (ELD50)

The NDV suspension was serially diluted using saline (10-fold; 10−1 to 10−10). Ten-day-old SPF chicken embryos were treated with 0.1 mL of each dilution, with six replicates tested per dilution. Mortality was monitored daily, and the ELD50 values of NDV were calculated using the Reed–Muench method.

Embryo neutralization assay

For this assay, 10-day-old SPF chicken embryos were treated with 100 ELD50 of NDV and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The model group was treated with 0.2 mL saline, while the experimental group received 0.2 mL of 125 μg/mL CGA-CDs. Embryo survival was monitored every 12 h. After 48 h, allantoic fluid was collected for HA assays, viral titration assays, and viral copy number detection.

In vivo safety assessment

Seven-day-old yellow-feathered chickens were randomly assigned to a blank control group or CGA-CDs group (n = 6 per group). The control group received intramuscular injections of 0.2 mL saline, while the CGA-CDs group received intramuscular injections of 10 mg/kg CGA-CDs. After treatment administration for 14 consecutive days, the chickens were euthanized, and their hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys were collected to observe histopathological changes via hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of CGA-CDs

The morphology of CGA-CDs was observed using TEM and high-resolution TEM (Fig. 1A, B, and C). CGA-CDs had a sphere-like shape, with no obvious lattice fringes. The nanoparticles were well dispersed and evenly distributed, with an average particle size of 5.675 ± 0.15 nm. On XRD pattern analysis (Fig. 1D), the CGA-CDs showed a wide peak at 2θ = 21°. These results showed that CGA-CDs are composed of non-ordered carbon and have an amorphous structure, consistent with the lack of lattice fringes observed on high-resolution TEM [27].Fig. 1Characterization of CGA-CDs. The elemental composition of CGA-CDs and the distribution of surface functional groups were analyzed using XPS, as shown in Fig. 1E and F. Analysis of the full XPS spectrum revealed that CGA-CDs exhibit two peaks with binding energies of 532.9 eV and 285.28 eV, corresponding to the photoelectron energies of C 1s and O 1s, respectively. The proportion of the C and O elements was found to be 68.45 % and 31.55 %, respectively. The separate analysis of the C 1s spectrum showed that the C 1s peak could be split into three additional peaks with binding energies of 284.8 eV, 286.5 eV, and 289.2 eV. This indicated the presence of C–C, C–O–C, and C=O bonds, respectively, demonstrating that a large number of hydrophilic groups were distributed on the surface of CGA-CDs.

The surface components of CGA-CDs were characterized further. As shown in Fig. 1G, the infrared spectrum of CGA-CDs contained an absorption peak at 3409 cm−1 (–OH bonds), 1724 cm−1 (C=O bonds), and 1268 cm−1 and 1161 cm−1 (C–O–C bonds). The peaks corresponding to C=C bonds at 1617 cm−1, 1524 cm−1, and 1449 cm−1 indicated that the surface of CGA-CDs was composed of –OH, –COOH, and other functional groups [28], in line with the results of XPS analysis.

Carbon shows an obvious absorption peak in the ultraviolet region, and most of its absorption peaks are distributed around 260–320 nm. The fluorescence characteristics of CGA-CDs were characterized based on their ultraviolet and fluorescence spectra. As shown in Fig. 1H and I, acromion was detected at 296 nm and 324 nm, indicating C=O bond n-π∗ and C=C bond π-π∗ electronic transitions. When the excitation wavelength was 390 nm, the maximum emission wavelength was 450 nm. When the excitation wavelength changed from 350 nm to 410 nm, the fluorescence intensity at 390 nm first increased before decreasing thereafter. The maximum emission wavelength was dependent on the excitation wavelength because nanoparticles of different sizes and surface energies coexisted in a given sample.

Antiviral effect of CGA-CDs

The TCID50 of the Mukteswar strain was calculated to be 10−8.84/0.1 mL using the Reed–Muench method. DF-1 cells were treated with CGA-CD suspensions and CGA solutions with concentrations of 31.25–1000 μg/mL. After 24 h of culture, cell viability was detected using the CCK-8 method. As shown in Fig. 2A, the cell viability of DF-1 cells treated with 31.25–500 μg/mL CGA-CDs was greater than 90 %. In contrast, only 31.25–125 μg/mL CGA treatment provided >90 % cell viability, indicating that CGA-CDs had lower cytotoxicity than CGA itself. Chicken embryos serve as an important model for early-stage drug screening, providing an effective system for evaluating a drug's ability to inhibit viral infection. Both antiviral activity and neutralizing efficacy can be quantitatively assessed using this model. In this study, 100 ELD50 of NDV was inoculated into 10-day-old SPF chicken embryos and incubated for 48 h. Subsequently, embryos from the CGA-CDs treatment group exhibited markedly better development than those from the model control group, which showed severely inhibited embryo growth (Fig. 6C–E). Hemagglutination assays showed that the HA value in the model group (28) was much higher than that in the CGA-CDs group (26). Additionally, CGA-CDs treatment was found to significantly reduce viral titers and NDV-F gene copy numbers in the allantoic fluid, demonstrating strong antiviral effects in vivo.

In the challenge experiment, pathological examination on day 7 post-infection revealed that chicks in the model group exhibited severe lesions, including darkened and atrophied livers, cardiac congestion, and extensive pulmonary hemorrhage. However, the chicks in the CGA-CDs group showed markedly alleviated pathological changes (Fig. 6F). Viral load analysis of the proventriculus and spleen demonstrated that CGA-CDs significantly reduced NDV replication on both day 3 and day 7 post-infection (P < 0.05 versus the model group, Fig. 6G). The mortality rate in the model group reached 100 % by day 6. However, the CGA-CDs group showed the lowest mortality rate (30 %) on day 7, exhibiting significant protection against NDV infection. Furthermore, HI assays of serum samples collected on days 2 and 7 post-infection revealed that the antibody titers in the CGA-CDs group were approximately two-fold higher than those in the model group (Fig. 6I and J).

Histopathological assessment is vital for examining the therapeutic effects of drugs in virus-infected animal models. In the current study, after NDV infection, the model group exhibited serious histopathological damage, including congestion in the hepatic sinusoids and red pulp, hemorrhage in the myocardial matrix, inflammatory cell infiltration and congestion in the alveolar walls leading to wall thickening, and increased basophilia and dilation of the renal tubules. While the histopathological damage was significantly attenuated after treatment with CGA-CDs, only a slight protective effect was achieved following CGA treatment. Together, these findings demonstrated that CGA-CDs effectively suppress NDV replication, mitigate tissue damage(Fig. 6K).

Targeted energy metabolism

Mitochondria, the energy factories of cells, are responsible not only for producing ATP but also for regulating various biological processes, including apoptosis, immune responses, and signal transduction. Viruses leverage mitochondrial energy metabolism in various ways to support their replication needs and evade host immune defenses. Previous studies have demonstrated that NDV can alter host metabolic pathways and inhibit the mitochondrial TCA cycle to promote its own replication [31].

Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) is a supervised statistical method used for discriminant analysis. It employs partial least squares regression to establish a relationship between sample categories and metabolite expression levels, enabling the prediction of sample classifications. In a PLS-DA score plot, R2 and Q2 values closer to 1 indicate a more stable and reliable model. A Q2 value greater than 0.5 suggests that the model has good predictive capability. As shown in Fig. 7C, the R2 of the PLS-DA score plot was 0.981, and the Q2 was 0.91, indicating that the model was stable, reliable, and had good predictive ability. Hierarchical clustering analysis demonstrated that the metabolites in the same cluster showed similar expression patterns and could be involved in related metabolic reactions. As illustrated in Fig. 7A, the invasion of NDV into host cells appeared to cause metabolic abnormalities, especially in the TCA cycle. It affected the levels of intermediates such as citric acid, malic acid, fumaric acid, and succinic acid, all of which showed lower levels after NDV invasion. However, after treatment with CGA-CDs, these metabolites were significantly upregulated. The absolute quantitative analysis of targeted energy metabolism indicated that after treatment with CGA-CDs, malic acid, fumaric acid, and succinic acid showed significant accumulation (Fig. 7B). As shown in Fig. 7D, some highly important metabolites were detected, and the experimental results were consistent with those of metabolite hierarchical clustering analysis. After treatment with CGA-CDs, a significant upregulation of TCA cycle intermediates was also identified. KEGG metabolic pathway analysis revealed that CGA-CDs significantly upregulated metabolites like malic acid, fumaric acid, succinic acid, alpha-ketoglutarate, isocitrate, etc. within the TCA cycle (Fig. 7E). The activity of the mitochondrial respiratory chain leads to ROS production. Excessive ROS accumulation can lead to the oxidation of key biomolecules, such as mitochondrial DNA, causing oxidative stress and accelerating mitochondrial damage. This, in turn, can further increase ROS levels, producing a vicious cycle [34]. In the current study, green fluorescence was markedly decreased in the CGA-CDs group versus the untreated group (Fig. 8D), indicating that CGA-CDs can effectively inhibit virus-induced mitochondrial damage.

MMP refers to the potential difference across the inner mitochondrial membrane and is an important indicator of mitochondrial function. A stable MMP is essential for maintaining normal physiological functions, energy metabolism, and cell survival [31]. To verify the effect of virus infection on mitochondrial function, JC-1 staining was performed, and MMP was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 8E, the CCCP treatment group showed significant green fluorescence, while the untreated group had significantly higher green fluorescence than the CGA-CDs group. These findings demonstrated that NDV could disrupt the MMP of host cells, causing mitochondrial depolarization. However, CGA-CDs could inhibit virus-induced mitochondrial depolarization and attenuate the decrease in MMP.

Morphological analysis of mitochondria

TEM is a key method for observing mitochondrial autophagy. Previous studies have shown that viral invasion triggers mitochondrial autophagy, disrupting metabolic functions in host cells and promoting virus proliferation [35]. Using TEM, Zhang et al. observed that mitochondria are surrounded by a double membrane structure following H1N1 virus invasion, leading to significant mitochondrial autophagy [36].

To investigate whether NDV infection induces mitochondrial damage and autophagy in host cells, and to understand whether CGA-CDs can mitigate virus-induced mitochondrial damage, cells were infected with 500 TCID50 of NDV and treated with CGA-CDs. TEM revealed significant differences in mitochondrial morphology and mitophagy among different treatment groups (Fig. 9). In the CGA-CDs group (upper panels, marked with red circles), the mitochondria had an intact overall structure, with continuous outer membranes, well-organized cristae, and no obvious swelling or rupture. This indicated that mitochondria remained in a relatively healthy state in this group. However, in the NDV group (lower panels, marked with green and blue circles), some mitochondria showed structural damage, including loosened inner membranes, cristae loss, and decreased matrix density. Notably, several mitochondria were partially or completely engulfed by double-membrane structures, typical of autophagosomes targeting damaged mitochondria, suggesting that mitophagy had been activated to eliminate dysfunctional mitochondria. Effect of CGA-CDs on virus-induced mitochondrial autophagy and MAVS protein degradation

During autophagosome formation, LC3I in the cytoplasm binds to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and is integrated into the bilayer membrane of autophagosomes to form LC3II. Thus, the accumulation of LC3II is a hallmark of autophagosome formation. P62 binds to LC3, and when lysosomes fuse with autophagosomes, P62 also dissolves [37]. To determine whether NDV triggers mitochondrial autophagy and whether CGA-CDs can inhibit this process, western blotting analysis was performed to detect autophagy-related proteins in this study. A CQ-treated group was included to observe changes in mitochondrial autophagy, as shown in Fig. 10A. CQ inhibited lysosomal function, leading to the accumulation of the P62 and LC3II proteins. In the CGA-CDs group, the expression levels of P62 and LC3II were comparable to those in the blank group. In contrast, the untreated group showed significantly higher LC3II expression and notably lower P62 expression than the CGA-CDs group. Additionally, the mitochondrial matrix protein COXIV was dramatically downregulated in the untreated group versus the other groups. MAVS is expressed on mitochondria and functions as an adaptor protein, modulating innate immune signaling pathways in host cells. When the TLR and RLR proteins recognize pathogens, signals are transmitted to MAVS, promoting the activation of downstream NF-κB, which stimulates interferon production and antiviral immunity [38]. In this study, western blot analysis was used to assess whether CGA-CDs can prevent the degradation of MAVS during mitochondrial autophagy. As illustrated in Fig. 10C and D, the expression levels of MAVS and COXⅣ in the untreated group were significantly lower than those in the CGA-CDs group. These findings suggested that CGA-CDs can inhibit the virus-induced degradation of MAVS, thereby preserving antiviral innate immunity and suppressing viral replication.

Compared to previously reported antiviral CDs, CGA-CDs show wider mechanistic effects and broader application prospects. The greatest advantage of CGA-CDs over previously reported carbon dots lies in their unique “mitochondrial protective mechanism”, which not only inhibits multiple stages of the viral life cycle but also preserves the host immune structure (MAVS), representing an antiviral strategy with stronger support from intrinsic defense mechanisms [[39], [40], [41], [42]].

Conclusion

CGA-CDs, synthesized in this study via a microwave-assisted method, exhibit uniform size, good dispersibility, and surface hydrophilicity. In vitro, CGA-CDs effectively inhibit NDV adsorption, replication, and progeny release, while mechanistically protecting mitochondria from virus-induced autophagy and preventing MAVS protein degradation. In vivo, CGA-CDs reduce viral loads, alleviate pathological damage, and mitigate clinical symptoms in NDV-infected chicken embryos and young chickens. Together, these findings highlight that CGA-CDs can serve as promising antiviral nanomaterials. Their dual effects, through direct viral inhibition and mitochondrial protection, support their further development as antiviral agents against NDV and potentially even other RNA viruses. However, this study has some limitations, including the lack of long-term safety assessments, pharmacokinetic analyses, and validation of antiviral effects against other viruses. Future studies should thus focus on dose optimization, in-depth mechanistic analysis, and broad-spectrum antiviral efficacy assessment, providing a foundation for the development of CGA-CDs as multifunctional antiviral nanomaterials.
