Streptomyces morookaense spinal suppurative infection: a case report
Authors: Liyang Wu, Jiahao Pu, Xinhua Xi, Yongzhen Bao, Lincong Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeStreptomyces species are ubiquitous soil actinomycetes and a major source of antibiotics, but invasive human infection with spinal involvement is exceedingly rare and may mimic tuberculous or fungal spondylodiscitis. We report a thoracic suppurative vertebral infection caused by Streptomyces morookaense and highlight an integrated diagnostic approach.MethodsA 66-year-old woman with no known immunodeficiency developed progressive thoracic back pain one month after severe trauma with open wounds. CT/MRI showed osteolytic endplate destruction at T3–T4 with paravertebral abscess formation. Fluoroscopy-guided percutaneous biopsy of the T4 vertebral body was performed for histopathology, culture, and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS).ResultsHistopathology demonstrated fibrinous exudate, necrosis, and inflammatory granulation tissue with fragmented trabeculae, without granuloma or caseous necrosis; acid-fast staining was negative. Vertebral tissue culture grew Streptomyces spp, and mNGS identified high-abundance sequences matching S. morookaense. Intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam led to rapid pain relief and normalization of inflammatory markers within one week, and no recurrence was observed during follow-up.ConclusionThis case suggests that Streptomyces morookaense has the potential to involve the thoracic spine in immunocompetent individuals. For unexplained spinal infections with negative routine tests, percutaneous vertebral sampling with integrated interpretation of pathology, culture, and mNGS can improve detection of rare pathogens and help avoid inappropriate empirical therapy.

Introduction

The genus Streptomyces, classified within the order Actinomycetales, comprises Gram-positive, aerobic, branched filamentous bacteria that are widely distributed in soil and natural environments. These organisms are the natural producers of various clinical antibiotics, including gentamicin, erythromycin, and tetracycline. However, Streptomyces are exceedingly rare as confirmed human pathogens [1]. A review of the extant literature reveals numerous case reports and systematic reviews that indicate the pathogenicity of the bacterium is often associated with open wounds, environmental exposure, or invasive procedures. The infection sites primarily involve the skin and soft tissues, lungs, or the central nervous system [2]. Invasive deep bone and spinal infections are even rarer, predominantly occurring in immunocompromised patients or those with severe underlying conditions [3]. However, invasive Streptomyces infections in immunocompetent individuals have also been described, particularly in pulmonary infections [4–6]. To the best of our knowledge, reports of invasive Streptomyces infection in immunocompetent individuals remain extremely limited.

Vertebral infections caused by Streptomyces have been reported only rarely worldwide, and the limited published cases were not always characterized to the species level [7]. The most common pathogens responsible for spinal infections are Staphylococcus aureus, followed by Mycobacterium tuberculosis and fungi, whereas less frequently encountered organisms such as Actinomyces, Nocardia, and Streptomyces may be overlooked in routine clinical practice [8]. To the best of our knowledge, spinal suppurative infection specifically caused by Streptomyces morookaense has not been previously reported. Spinal infections caused by Streptomyces is particularly difficult to diagnose because both the clinical presentation and imaging findings are non-specific. Vertebral osteolytic destruction and paravertebral abscess formation may closely resemble tuberculous spondylitis, fungal infection, or even neoplastic disease, resulting in a substantial risk of misdiagnosis [9, 10]. In addition, Streptomyces grows slowly and may be overlooked or dismissed as environmental contamination in routine microbiological culture [11]. In this context, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS), which enables hypothesis-free pathogen detection, may provide important complementary value in unexplained or culture-negative spinal infections, particularly when tissue sampling is limited and rare pathogens are suspected [12]. Nevertheless, mNGS findings should be interpreted cautiously in conjunction with clinical, pathological, and microbiological evidence, as background microbial reads may reduce specificity.

This case report documents a rare instance of Streptomyces morookaense pyogenic vertebral osteomyelitis of the thoracic spine in an elderly woman with essentially normal immune function following severe trauma. The pathogen was identified through vertebral puncture biopsy combined with histopathology, bacterial culture, and mNGS. Although invasive Streptomyces infection has been reported in immunocompetent individuals, spinal suppurative infection caused specifically by Streptomyces morookaense appears to be exceptionally rare and, to the best of our knowledge, has not been previously reported. This case not only expands the clinical spectrum of invasive Streptomyces infections but more importantly illustrates a diagnostic workflow for unexplained spinal infections. The text emphasizes the critical importance of standardized specimen collection and comprehensive pathogen assessment in identifying rare pathogens. This approach is crucial for avoiding empirical anti-tuberculosis therapy and inappropriate broad-spectrum treatment, thereby ensuring optimal patient care (Fig. 1).

Case presentation

The patient is a 66-year-old female with no history of diabetes, malignancy, long-term immunosuppressive medication use, or tuberculosis exposure. Two months prior to the present date, the patient sustained a traffic accident resulting in multiple bilateral rib fractures, multiple facial and cranial bone fractures, and open facial trauma. She underwent a debridement procedure, followed by suturing, and received conservative treatment at another medical facility prior to being discharged. One month prior to the onset of symptoms, the patient began to experience persistent chest and back pain, which worsened with physical activity and at night. The patient exhibited no symptoms of fever, fatigue, or neurological deficits, such as limb numbness. Local pain management proved ineffective. As the symptoms worsened, she was referred to our hospital.

Admission Physical Examination: The patient’s vital signs remain stable, with a temperature of 37.8 °C. The patient is alert and oriented, indicating a positive response to treatment. The patient exhibited significant tenderness and percussion pain over the spinous processes of the upper thoracic spine (approximately T3–T4 level), without local redness, swelling, or sinus tract formation. The patient exhibited normal muscle strength in both lower extremities, with a grade of V according to the standard scale. Sensory function remained intact, and no pathological signs were detected. Additionally, no palpable lymph node enlargement was observed in the superficial regions. No other significant findings were identified during the systemic examination.

Laboratory findings: The patient’s white blood cell count was 6.33 × 10⁹/L (normal range: 4–10 × 10⁹/L), with a neutrophil percentage of 68%. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated to 41 mm/1 h (normal range: 0–20), and the C-reactive protein (CRP) level was 0.68 mg/dL (normal range: 0–0.6). The T-SPOT.TB assay (Fosun Diagnostics Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for tuberculosis was negative, as was the Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) antibody test (Autobio Diagnostics Co., Ltd., Zhengzhou, China). The X-ray findings indicated the presence of vertebral bone destruction at T3; however, the resolution of these findings was found to be limited. A subsequent thoracic spine CT scan revealed osteolytic destruction of the endplates at T3–T4 and paravertebral soft tissue swelling (Fig. 2). Baseline MRI demonstrated abnormal signal intensity involving the T2–T4 vertebral bodies, with destructive changes at T3–T4 accompanied by paravertebral abscess formation. Enhancing inflammatory soft tissue partially extended into the spinal canal, resulting in localized stenosis (Fig. 3). These findings were considered consistent with active spinal infection at presentation. These findings were considered consistent with active spinal infection at presentation. The collective imaging findings indicated the presence of infectious vertebral body inflammation; however, distinguishing between bacterial infection, tuberculosis, and fungal lesions proved challenging.

To identify the pathogen, a percutaneous biopsy of the T4 vertebral body was performed via the left pedicle under fluoroscopic guidance, yielding vertebral bone tissue specimens. Histopathology of the T4 vertebral bone showed fibrinous exudation, necrosis, and inflammatory granulation tissue with scattered fragmented trabeculae. No granulomas, caseous necrosis, or malignancy were found. Immunohistochemistry revealed activated macrophages (CD68 +) and neovascular proliferation (CD34 +, CD31 +). Acid-fast staining was negative, excluding mycobacterial infection (Fig. 4A). Vertebral blood and tissue specimens were cultured on 5% sheep blood agar at 37 °C. After 3 days of incubation, gray, chain-like colonies were observed. Gram staining demonstrated Gram-positive filamentous organisms, consistent with Streptomyces at the genus level (Fig. 4B). Concurrent mNGS analysis of the biopsy specimen identified Streptomyces at the genus level, with a relative abundance of 37.8% and 145 unique reads. At the species level, Streptomyces morookaense was identified with 65 unique reads and an identification confidence of 99%. This organism was considered clinically significant, as the mNGS findings were consistent with genus-level culture results supporting Streptomyces spp. After a thorough consideration of the anatomical location, clinical context, pathological findings, and the high concordance between both diagnostic approaches, the case was definitively diagnosed as Streptomyces morookaense-induced pyogenic vertebral osteomyelitis of the thoracic spine.

Drug susceptibility testing was not performed because our laboratory currently lacks a standardized broth microdilution susceptibility platform for actinomycetes and other infrequently isolated organisms. Therefore, isolate-specific susceptibility testing could not be completed in this case. After multidisciplinary consultation, piperacillin/tazobactam (4.5 g intravenously every 8 h) was selected as an empiric antibacterial regimen. This decision was based on the presence of radiologically confirmed pyogenic spinal infection requiring prompt parenteral treatment, the absence of evidence supporting mycobacterial or fungal infection, and available clinical experience with reference to the CLSI M45-A3 framework for infrequently isolated or fastidious bacteria. We therefore interpret the favorable clinical response in this case as supportive, but not definitive, evidence of antimicrobial appropriateness. Following a week of treatment, the patient exhibited a substantial improvement in the severity of pain in the chest and back regions (as measured by the visual analogue scale (VAS)score, which decreased by more than 50% from the initial admission). Concurrently, the levels of ESR and CRP returned to within normal ranges, and there was no observed deterioration in neurological function. These short-term laboratory and clinical improvements occurred after MRI had documented active destructive and paravertebral inflammatory lesions at presentation. The patient requested discharge after marked symptomatic improvement and continued intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam therapy at a community hospital for an additional 4 weeks. No oral step-down antimicrobial regimen was used. During 1 month of outpatient and telephone follow-up, the patient reported no recurrence of fever or progressive back pain, and inflammatory markers remained within normal limits. No clinical evidence of relapse was observed. However, because isolate-specific susceptibility testing and complete radiologic follow-up were unavailable, treatment success should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Discussion

This case exemplifies the capacity of Streptomyces morookaense to induce profound vertebral infections in hosts exhibiting essentially normal immune function under specific high-risk conditions, including severe trauma, compromised skin/mucosal barriers, and environmental exposure. The clinical manifestations of such infections are often insidious, and imaging lacks specificity, which can result in these infections being overlooked or misdiagnosed as spinal infections caused by other common pathogens. Standardized vertebral puncture biopsy and multimodal pathogen detection were employed to definitively identify Streptomyces morookaense as the causative agent. This finding adds to rare existing reports of invasive streptomycete infection involving the spine and may offer additional insight into the diagnostic evaluation of unexplained spinal infections. According to previous studies, Streptomyces has generally been regarded as an opportunistic pathogen or environmental contaminant, and invasive infection remains extremely rare. Although such infections have also been reported in immunocompetent hosts, particularly in the lung [13], the novelty of the present case lies in the rare presentation of thoracic spinal suppurative infection caused by Streptomyces morookaense. Reports of bone and joint involvement are already uncommon, and vertebral involvement is exceptionally rare [7]. The patient had no typical immunosuppressive background, whereas severe trauma with open wounds represented the major predisposing factor. Spinal infection is generally considered to occur through three major routes, including hematogenous spread, direct inoculation, and contiguous extension from adjacent infected tissue. In the present case, the exact route of infection cannot be definitively established. Given the history of severe trauma, open soft-tissue injury, and environmental exposure, trauma-associated local inoculation or colonization with subsequent local extension is considered a clinically plausible explanation. This finding suggests that the pathogenic potential of Streptomyces may be underestimated. It is imperative to exercise vigilance for the occurrence of deep infections in analogous high-risk exposed populations, rather than attributing them exclusively to “culture contamination.”

From both clinical and radiological viewpoints, this case is illustrative. The patient exhibited only progressive thoracic and back pain, devoid of any characteristic systemic toxic manifestations. Radiological findings revealed destruction of the vertebral endplates and paravertebral abscesses, indicative of bacterial suppurative vertebral osteomyelitis. However, these findings were highly suggestive of tuberculous or fungal spondylitis. Preliminary research suggests that the diagnosis of primary vertebral osteomyelitis should be based on a comprehensive evaluation of imaging findings, pathological characteristics, and the identification of the causative pathogen. Empirical long-term use of anti-tuberculosis or broad-spectrum antibiotics should be avoided in the absence of histological or culture evidence [14, 15]. In this particular instance, the team exercised caution and prudence by refraining from the initiation of empirical anti-tuberculosis therapy in the absence of substantial evidence. Instead, the attending medical professionals promptly performed a percutaneous vertebral puncture to obtain tissue specimens. The pathology report ruled out the presence of tuberculosis and malignancy, while the culture and mNGS tests identified a rare pathogen. This “evidence-chain diagnostic pathway” aligns closely with international consensus on spinal infection management and offers practical guidance for reducing misdiagnosis.

With respect to the confirmation of pathogens, this case underscores the complementary roles of conventional culture and mNGS. The genus Streptomyces is characterized by its sluggish growth patterns and heightened sensitivity to environmental factors. Relying exclusively on conventional culture methods may result in diagnostic errors due to inadequate incubation times or operational oversights, or may be misinterpreted as environmental contamination. mNGS facilitates expeditious and comprehensive detection of nucleic acid sequences in specimens, providing benefits for rare, difficult-to-culture, or mixed-infection pathogens [9]. However, mNGS carries the risk of detecting environmental microorganisms or low-abundance background bacteria, which may lead to overdiagnosis without clinical and pathological support. In this case, culture from the lesion supported Streptomyces at the genus level, whereas mNGS of the vertebral biopsy specimen supported species-level assignment as Streptomyces morookaense. Although isolate-based species confirmation by MALDI-TOF MS or 16S rRNA sequencing was not available, the concordance of lesion-site culture, vertebral-specimen mNGS, imaging findings, histopathology, and clinical response to antibacterial therapy supports the interpretation that this organism represented a true pathogen rather than incidental environmental contamination. This serves as a classic demonstration of the combined application and parallel validation of mNGS and traditional culture.

In regard to the process of differential diagnosis, the present case offers a valuable educational opportunity. The spectrum of pathogens that can cause spinal infections includes Mycobacterium tuberculosis, actinomycetes, Nocardia species, and various fungi. Some actinomycetes have been observed to manifest filamentous or branched structures on a histological level, thereby readily mimicking fungal or granulomatous lesions. Tuberculous spondylitis is frequently associated with caseous necrosis and granuloma formation; actinomycete infections commonly exhibit “sulfur granules” and anaerobic characteristics; Nocardia species are typically weakly acid-fast positive and preferentially affect immunocompromised hosts; whereas streptomycetes are generally aerobic and acid-fast negative. The histological examination of this case revealed the absence of tuberculoid granulomas, an acid-fast negative result, and a T-SPOT.TB test result that was also negative. When considered in conjunction with culture and mNGS results that repeatedly indicate Streptomyces, this observation suggests that in cases with radiographic evidence of chronic vertebral destruction but negative conventional tests, the inclusion of Actinobacteria and other rare bacteria in the differential diagnosis should be considered.

With regard to treatment, there is currently no uniform standard for the management of Streptomyces spinal infections. The preponderance of experience in this domain derives from case reports and published accounts of invasive Streptomyces or actinomycete infections [16, 17]. A body of extant research suggests that certain Streptomyces may demonstrate susceptibility to aminoglycosides, certain β-lactams, and carbapenems. However, significant inter-strain variability underscores the importance of antimicrobial susceptibility testing [18]. Due to the scarcity of bacterial material, it was not feasible to conduct a systematic antimicrobial susceptibility test for this particular case. The selection of piperacillin/tazobactam as the empirical treatment was made following a multidisciplinary consultation and a thorough review of the existing literature and pharmacological characteristics. The symptoms exhibited a rapid resolution, and the inflammatory markers returned to normal levels. These findings indicate that the treatment regimen was effective in this particular case. However, the absence of substantial evidence and systematic susceptibility data hinders the recommendation of this regimen as a fixed treatment. Future research endeavors must entail the accumulation of additional cases and the execution of in vitro experiments to substantiate these findings.

It must be acknowledged that this case report is not without its limitations. First, the follow-up period was relatively short, and formal follow-up MRI was not completed. Therefore, although short-term therapeutic response was supported by symptom improvement and normalization of ESR and CRP within 1 week, a quantitative radiologic-laboratory correlation over time could not be established. Long-term imaging follow-up is still required to assess structural recovery and recurrence risk. Secondly, the incomplete nature of the antimicrobial susceptibility testing results constrained the evidence level for the treatment regimen. Thirdly, a single-case report is not sufficient to determine the true incidence of Streptomyces spinal infections or the optimal treatment duration. However, these limitations underscore the importance of standardized pathogen collection, specimen preservation, and multicenter case compilation.

In summary, the clinical implications of this case primarily include the following: First, within the context of severe trauma and potential environmental exposure, rare environmental bacteria such as streptomycetes may be the true causative agents and should not be uniformly regarded as contamination. Secondly, for cases of spinal infections of unknown etiology, timely vertebral biopsies should be performed based on imaging evaluations. Pathogen diagnosis should be made by integrating pathology, culture, and mNGS results to avoid the misuse of empirical anti-tuberculosis or prolonged broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy. Thirdly, mNGS serves as a crucial supplementary tool. Its integration with traditional methods has been shown to significantly improve the detection rate of rare pathogens, aiding in the optimization of individualized antimicrobial strategies. As molecular diagnostic technologies become more widespread, striking a balance between preventing missed diagnoses and avoiding overdiagnosis will become a critical challenge in the field of spinal infections. This case offers a paradigmatic illustration of the aforementioned efforts. Given the inherent limitations of a single case report, causal relationships, diagnostic generalizability, and therapeutic implications should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Additional cases and susceptibility data are required to better define pathogenic potential, diagnostic strategies, and optimal antimicrobial management.

Conclusions

It has been observed that Streptomyces morookaense can lead to the development of thoracic vertebral pyogenic spondylitis in hosts exhibiting no substantial immune compromise, but rather, a history of severe trauma and environmental exposure. The utilization of a multi-evidence chain, substantiated by percutaneous vertebral biopsy, histopathology, culture, and mNGS, facilitates the identification of this rare pathogen in cases of unexplained spinal infection. This diagnostic approach may help reduce misclassification as a contaminant and may help avoid inappropriate empirical anti-tuberculosis therapy. This case underscores the integration of standardized specimen collection and molecular diagnostics into the routine management pathway for challenging spinal infections, which bears significant clinical implications.
