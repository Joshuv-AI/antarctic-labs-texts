Functional characteristics of endosymbiotic picornia-like virus in Diaphorina citri
Authors: Shuai Wang, Muhammad Younas, Chengtao Bao, Yuxi Xiao, Inzamam Ul Haq, Jia Lü, Yu Bin, Taiyun Wei, You Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Widespread prevalence of DcPLV detected in Diaphorina citri across China.•Both vertical and plant-mediated horizontal transmission modes were confirmed.•High viral accumulation in salivary glands and midgut of infected psyllids.•DcPLV infection significantly increased the acquisition and accumulation of CLas.•No adverse effects observed on fecundity, development, or adult longevity.Keywords: Insect-specific viruses, Vertical transmission, Viral symbionts, Citrus greening

Introduction

Huanglongbing (HLB), commonly known as citrus greening disease, is one of the most economically damaging diseases, caused by the phloem-limited bacterium Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (CLas) and vectored by Asian citrus psyllid, Diaphorina citri, in a persistent and propagative manner (Bové, 2006). Its continued spread across Asia, the Americas, and Africa has devastated citrus industries and prompted emergency control measures (Zhang, 2019; Zhou, 2020). Currently, several management strategies have been developed to mitigate HLB, such as the plant defense inducers (Canales et al., 2016), antimicrobial application (Yang et al., 2018), removal of infected citrus trees (Yuan et al., 2021), and supplementation with macro and micro-nutrients to improve plant health and resistance (Mattos-Jr et al., 2020). However, these strategies showed limited results in field application. Disease management highly relies on controlling the insect vector through the application of insecticides, but it is environmentally unfriendly and causes insecticide resistance (Pardo et al., 2018). These circumstances have urged the scientist to look for alternatives to control the pest; utilization of insect specific or endosymbiotic viruses in D. citri is one of those alternatives, which could serve as an abundant source for HLB mitigation. These viruses may be used for manipulating the insect vector or the carrying pathogen (Nouri et al., 2018). Recent studies have revealed the presence of diverse insect-specific viruses, or endosymbiotic viruses, in D. citri (Vasilakis and Tesh, 2015). These insect-associated viruses can significantly influence host physiology, including adaptation and reproduction (Vasilakis and Tesh, 2015). It has been reported that Aphis glycines virus 1 (ABV-1) has shortened nymphal development time and enhanced the reproductive output in aphids (An et al., 2022), while the aphid-lethal paralysis virus impaired the movement and reduced longevity in Rhopalosiphum padi (Williamson et al., 1988).

Nouri et al. (2016) performed the metagenomic analysis and discovered five new viruses, including Diaphorina citri-associated C virus (DcACV), Diaphorina citri flavi-like virus (DcFLV), Diaphorina citri densovirus (DcDNV), Diaphorina citri reovirus (DcRV), and Diaphorina citri picorna-like virus (DcPLV) in D. citri. Britt et al. (2020) used PCR-based methods and High Throughput Sequencing (HTS) techniques to detect these previously discovered D. citri-associated viruses in nymphs and adults of D. citri populations in Florida. Furthermore, a comprehensive virome survey of natural ACP populations was conducted across 21 major citrus-producing counties in Florida to detect and monitor five D. citri-associated viruses DcACV, DcFLV, DcDNV, DcRV, and DcPLV, across geographically and temporally diverse populations. DcACV was identified as the most prevalent virus in Florida D. citri populations, occurring in over 60 % of all tested samples, followed by DcPLV and DcFLV (Britt et al., 2020). Liu et al. (2024) also recorded that DcPLV was most prevalent and approximately 60 % of all adult insect samples were co-infected with more than one insect pathogen, including the five D. citri-associated viruses and CLas. DcPLV was demonstrated to potential propagate vertically and horizontally and establishes a persistent, asymptomatic infection in D. citri (Liu et al., 2024). Insect viruses can profoundly affect host biology, often through persistent, non-lethal infections. Studies in mosquitoes, aphids, and planthoppers have shown that endosymbiotic viruses may regulate immunity, modify development, or affect vector competence (Goenaga et al., 2015; Jia et al., 2021). These findings provided a critical foundation for the functional characterization of DcPLV in modulating host reproductive dynamics, warranting further investigation into the mechanistic basis of DcPLV’s functional interactions within D. citri.

Therefore, the present study aimed to comprehensively investigate the biology, transmission, and host impact of DcPLV in D. citri. Previously Chen et al. (2020) also studied on the prevalence of the DcRV and found an incidence of 58–100 % in Fuzhou, China but the DcPLV have remained unexplored. Thus, we examined the prevalence of DcPLV and CLas in 17 geographically diverse D. citri populations in China from 2023 to 2024, characterized seasonal viral infection dynamics in the orchard at Gutian. Furthermore, we explored vertical and horizontal transmission modes, quantified the effects of viral infection on fecundity, development, and lifespan, and measured viral localization and accumulation in host tissues.

Materials and methods

Survey of D. citri in the field

Populations of D. citri were collected from 17 citrus-growing regions in southern China between March 2023 and February 2024 (Table 1). Sampling locations were selected to represent a wide geographic and climatic range, including provinces such as Fujian, Guangdong, Jiangxi, Guangxi, and Yunnan. At each location, adults of D. citri were collected from citrus plants using aspirators and transferred to insect-proof boxes for transport to the laboratory. In addition to regional sampling, a long-term survey was conducted in Gutian County, Fujian Province. Monthly collections were performed between March 2023 and February 2024 to monitor seasonal changes in pathogen prevalence. The population with 100 % DcPLV-infected or non-infected D. citri was selected to create a population in the lab at Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University, Fuzhou, China.

Detection of DcPLV and CLas

DNA and RNA were extracted from D. citri using previous method (Chen et al., 2020). DcPLV and CLas infections were determined using conventional PCR. Virus-specific primers targeting the DcPLV-VP were designed based on published sequences (Nouri et al., 2016; Britt et al., 2020). CLas detection was conducted using primers targeting the 16S rRNA gene (Jagoueix et al., 1996). Each PCR reaction (25 µL) included 12.5 µL 2 × Taq PCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China), 1 µL of each primer (10 µM), 2 µL of template cDNA or DNA, and 8.5 µL of nuclease-free water. The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 40 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR products were resolved at 1.5 % agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide. Primer sequences used for DcPLV are provided in Table S1. The standard curve in real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) for DcPLV was created with previous method and the curve is 3.4068x+35.422 (R2=0.9996) (Gregorova et al., 2022). The polyclonal antibodies against RdRp of DcPLV were prepared as previously described (Li et al., 2025). To avoid the insect honeydew contaminated plant tissue, all plant tissues were clean with ultrapure water before sampling.

Transmission assays

Virus-infected and virus-free colonies of D. citri were established using field populations from Gutian County (high DcPLV-positive rate) and Yuexiu Village (low DcPLV-positive rate). Individuals were screened and confirmed using RT-PCR, and then colonies were reared separately for two generations to maintain virus status under lab conditions. Each group was maintained on healthy citrus seedlings in isolated cages.

Horizontal transmission was tested by two experiments. (1) Examine the virus in the leaf which fed by the virus-infected D. citri for 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 days; (2) D. citri cross-feeding on virus-free citrus plants. 30 individuals of DcPLV-infected adults were confined to virus-free leaves for 8 days, then removed, and replaced with 30 uninfected individuals. After 1, 3, 5 and 7 days of feeding, the second group was tested for virus acquisition.

To determine vertical transmission efficiencies of DcPLV, controlled mating experiments were conducted under insectary conditions (25 ± 1 °C, 70 % RH, 16:8 L: D photoperiod). Adult D. citri were grouped into combinations of infected and uninfected individuals: (i) infected female × uninfected male, (ii) infected male × uninfected female, (iii) both infected, and (iv) both uninfected. Combinations were allowed to mate and oviposit on healthy citrus seedlings enclosed in ventilated cages. After adult removal, F1 offspring were collected at the 5th instar stage and analyzed for virus presence using PCR. Transmission efficiency was calculated as the percentage of infected offspring. To rule out potential horizontal transmission via citrus leaf, we discarded all nymphs hatched in the 6 days post infection. Besides, we also carefully clean the leaves with water sprayer. This minimized the possibility of virus acquisition from contaminated plant surfaces.

Biological effects of viral infection

To evaluate the effects of viral infection on D. citri biology, laboratory colonies were established from virus-infected and virus-free populations. Parameters including adult longevity, fecundity (number of eggs per female), and eclosion period (egg to adult) were assessed. Newly emerged adults were monitored daily for survival. Fecundity was recorded by counting eggs laid over 7 d for eclosion timing, 50 eggs per group were observed until adult emergence. Each experiment was replicated three times, with at least 30 individuals per replicate.

Viral tissue distribution

To assess tissue-specific localization and developmental dynamics of DcPLV, both quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) and Western blot analysis were employed to evaluate viral loads. The tissues of midgut, salivary glands, hemolymph, and reproductive organs (ovaries and testes) were dissected from adult DcPLV-infected D. citri. RNA was extracted from each tissue, and virus were quantified using RT-qPCR assays and Western blot targeting the DcPLV-RdRp.

Correlation between DcPLV and CLas

To investigate the relationship between DcPLV and CLas infection in D. citri, field-collected D. citri infected with CLas was analyzed. CLas accumulation in DcPLV-positive and DcPLV-negative D. citri populations was then quantified using RT-qPCR. In the subsequent experiment, laboratory-reared newly emerged D. citri from virus-infected and virus-free colonies were allowed to feed on CLas-infected citrus leaves using the leaf-cage method. Samples were collected at 4, 8, and 12-day post-access to diseased plants (padp), and CLas accumulation were evaluated by RT-qPCR and Western blot to assess the influence of DcPLV. Sampling time points (4, 8, and 12 days’ post-exposure) were chosen based on previous reports demonstrating that adult D. citri can acquire CLas within a few days of feeding on infected citrus (George et al., 2018). However, future studies extending beyond 12 days may further clarify the dynamics of acquisition.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and SPSS 26.0. Comparisons between treatment groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test or unpaired two-tailed t-tests, as appropriate. The correlation analysis of DcPLV and CLas was processed with Pearson correlation coefficient. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All experiments included a minimum of three biological replicates.

Results

Survey the DcPLV and CLas in D. citri

A total of 307 D. citri individuals from 17 regions across China were screened for DcPLV and CLas infection. DcPLV was detected in 108 individuals (35.18 %), with infection observed in all 17 locations at varying frequencies (Table 1). The highest DcPLV infection rate was recorded in Zhangzhou, Fujian Province (62.50 %). However, CLas was only detected in 5 regions with much lower frequency and the samples from Sibao Village (Gutian, Ningde) which showed a CLas infection rate of 50 %, while other regions including Jiangxi, Hainan, Zhejiang Provinces, exhibited lower or zero detection levels (Table 1). Results revealed the co-infection of DcPLV and CLas in 7 individuals, with the highest co-infection rate in Gutian (29.7 %). However, statistical analysis suggested no direct correlation between the presence of DcPLV and CLas infection, indicating independent infection dynamics (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Dynamics of DcPLV and CLas in D. citri

A year-long survey was conducted in Gutian, Fujian Province, to determine the seasonal infection dynamics. DcPLV displayed greater temporal variability. The infection rate of DcPLV ranged from 20 % in January to a peak of 57 % in May. A clear upward trend was observed from March to May, which coincides with the period of active citrus flushing and increased psyllid reproductive activity. A gradual decline in DcPLV prevalence was observed after May, reaching the lowest point during June–July. CLas infection rates ranged from 30.67 % to 73.33 %, showing an overall increasing trend during periods of citrus flush growth (April to July) (Fig. 1A). The time of highest CLas infection rate was close with the DcPLV prevalence (Fig. 1A). Correlation analysis revealed that the field infection rates of DcPLV and CLas were positively correlated (R2=0.104, P < 0.05; Fig. 1B).

Vertical transmission mode of DcPLV

Controlled mating assays revealed that vertical transmission of DcPLV in D. citri is partial biparental transmission. The highest transmission rate (58.33 %) was observed when both parents were infected, while maternal-only and paternal-only crosses resulted in 38.89 % and 16.67 % infected offspring, respectively (Table 2). To rule out potential horizontal transmission, we employed a revised vertical transmission assay. After oviposition, adult insects were removed, and citrus seedlings containing the eggs were discarded. Newly hatched nymphs were carefully transferred within 12 h of emergence to fresh virus-free citrus seedlings and reared in clean cages. This minimized any possibility of virus acquisition from contaminated plant surfaces. Virus status of F1 progeny was confirmed using RT-PCR at the 4th instar stage. These findings suggest that although DcPLV can be transmitted through both parental lines, maternal transmission is more efficient, possibly due to differential viral loads in reproductive tissues. No viral detection occurred in progeny from virus-free crosses, confirming that vertical transmission is essential for viral persistence.

Horizontal transmission and plant-mediated exposure

Initially, the RT-qPCR assays and western blot analysis showed that the DcPLV accumulation in the citrus leaves after DcPLV-infested D. citri fed for 2–10 d (Fig. 1C-D), indicating that DcPLV can be transmitted to plants through the saliva of D. citri. Then, D. citri could acquire DcPLV from those leaves and the infection rate of psyllids exhibited a time-dependent increase (Fig. 1E). We also found that DcPLV was detectable in D. citri after 24 h feeding (Fig. 1E). It confirmed that DcPLV can be horizontally transmitted among D. citri populations via infected citrus plants (Fig. 1E). Further, to determine whether DcPLV can proliferate in citrus leaves, RT-qPCR was employed to detect virus accumulation in the citrus leaves. The results demonstrated a gradual decline of DcPLV in the citrus leaves over time after removing D. citri (Fig. 1F), providing preliminary evidence that DcPLV could not replicate or proliferate within citrus tissue.

Assessment of the biological impact of DcPLV on fecundity, development, and longevity of D. citri

The biological impact of DcPLV was assessed to evaluate its potential as a target for biological control or as a mutualistic agent. Therefore, we investigated the impact of DcPLV, on three key biological parameters, including fecundity (number of offspring/female), developmental time (duration from egg deposition to peak adult emergence), and adult lifespan (adult longevity) of D. citri. DcPLV-infected and non-infected D. citri populations were reared under controlled laboratory conditions to compare reproductive output and survival. DcPLV-infected females laid an average of 54.3 ± 3.1 eggs, while uninfected controls laid 51.9 ± 2.6 eggs (Fig. 2A). These results suggest that DcPLV did not affect female reproductive capacity. Developmental timing of experimental groups and control individuals also showed no significant differences observed across treatments (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2B). Similarly, DcPLV infection had no effect on the lifespan of both male and female adults, with both infected and uninfected individuals living approximately 75–77 days (Fig. 2C).

Tissue-specific localization and quantification of DcPLV

The localization and quantitative expression of DcPLV were assessed across different tissues of adult D. citri and developmental stages. We found that DcPLV is mainly distributed in the midgut and salivary glands, with significantly higher viral accumulation compared to other tissues (P < 0.01). DcPLV-infected D. citri showed the highest viral copy number in the midgut and salivary glands (Fig. 2D-E).

DcPLV accumulation in different developmental stages and gender of D. citri

The titer of DcPLV were quantified across different developmental stages in D. citri, including eggs, nymphal instars, and adults. It was found that the viral titers increased progressively throughout the nymphal instars, peaking at 7 days’ post-emergence in adults, and then became stable (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, the distribution of DcPLV in male and female adult D. citri was examined to assess potential sex-based differences in viral accumulation. The results revealed that the average DcPLV titer in female D. citri was significantly higher than in males (Fig. 3B-C). These results suggest a sex-specific disparity in DcPLV accumulation within D. citri populations.

Effect of DcPLV on CLas acquisition and accumulation in D. citri

The influence of DcPLV on CLas acquisition in D. citri was further investigated following the "leaf-cage" feeding method. DcPLV-positive and DcPLV-negative newly emerged adult D. citri were used to assess CLas acquisition at 4, 8, and 12 days’ post-exposure. RT-qPCR analysis revealed significantly elevated CLas titers in DcPLV-infected D. citri at 8 and 12 days post-acquisition, whereas no significant difference was observed at 4 days post-acquisition compared to non-viruliferous D. citri (Fig. 3D). Further validation was confirmed using Western blot assay and the accumulation of CLas-OMP in DcPLV-infected D. citri was significantly increased compared with that in non-viruliferous D. citri (Fig. 3E). Therefore, it was preliminarily concluded that DcPLV-infected D. citri could be more efficient in spreading CLas.

Discussion

Insect-specific viruses have the potential to influence vector mortality and transmission capacity. However, the biological interactions between these viruses and D. citri hosts remain poorly understood, and mutualistic associations involving insect viruses have remained unexplored. This study provides a comprehensive investigation into the biology, transmission dynamics, and host interactions of DcPLV in the D. citri. It combines field surveys, biological characterization, and physiological assays. Our extensive field screening revealed that DcPLV is ubiquitously present in D. citri populations across 17 citrus-growing regions in China, with infection rates frequently exceeding and reaching 62 % in multiple sites (Table 1). This is consistent with previous studies (Liu et al., 2024). Besides, multiple studies found picorna-like virus from D. citri, potato psyllid and wild lime psyllid (Britt et al., 2020; Stuehler Jr et al., 2023). Seasonal investigation in Gutian confirmed the persistence of DcPLV in D. citri all over the year, regardless of fluctuations in climate or citrus phenology. Recently, Britt et al. (2020) found five D. citri-associated viruses (DcACV, DcFLV, DcDNV, DcRV, and DcPLV) in D. citri populations from Florida citrus groves.

Additionally, our findings showed that high prevalence of DcPLV is coupled with 38.89 % vertical transmission efficiency through infected females, and it supports the classification of DcPLV as a stably maintained, maternally inherited symbiont. However, the absence of viral replication in plants, confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blot, further indicates that citrus acts only as a mechanical vector, not a biological host, for DcPLV. These findings align with previous reports on insect-specific viruses in hemipteran vectors, such as aphids and planthoppers, where vertical transmission is the primary mechanism for persistence (Van Munster et al., 2003). Vertical transmission of arboviruses in mosquito populations has been well-documented, highlighting its significance in the spread of both pathogenic and nonpathogenic viruses. Vertical transmission of yellow fever virus and Zika virus has been confirmed in Aedes aegypti, while Aedes species have also been shown to vertically transmit other medically important arboviruses, including Dengue virus, Chikungunya virus, West Nile virus, Ross River virus, Sindbis virus, and Western Equine Encephalitis virus (Anderson et al., 2014; Li et al., 2017). These findings align with our results and reinforce the notion that vertical transmission represents a common and evolutionarily conserved strategy for virus persistence in insect populations, including both pathogenic and symbiotic viruses. Horizontal transmission of bacterial symbionts such as Rickettsia and Wolbachia from Bemisia tabaci, Cardinium from the leafhopper Scaphoideus titanus, and other endosymbionts from Euscelidius variegatus to host plants has been also reported, which further supports the potential for such transmission (Chrostek et al., 2017).

Tissue-specific analyses revealed that DcPLV was predominantly localized in the midgut and salivary glands of D. citri, with minimal presence in the hemolymph and reproductive organs. This distribution is biologically meaningful, as the midgut represents the initial entry barrier for CLas acquisition (Ammar et al., 2011), while the salivary glands are the final conduit for pathogen egress and subsequent transmission to host plants. The co-localization of DcPLV in these key tissues raises the possibility of indirect interactions with CLas, potentially through mechanisms such as immune modulation, competition for cellular machinery, or alteration of epithelial barrier permeability. Our findings slightly differ with Liu et al. (2024), who demonstrated that DcPLV was highly accumulated in salivary glands compared to the midgut. There may be variation in virus detection sensitivity, viral strain heterogeneity, or age and physiological state of the insects. Additionally, environmental or host-plant factors may also influence virus localization dynamics. Similar patterns have been reported for other insect-symbiont systems, where symbiotic bacteria colonize the midgut, salivary glands, and reproductive tissues, enabling both horizontal and vertical transmission routes (Bright and Bulgheresi, 2010; Ammar et al., 2011; Ghanim et al., 2017). Our findings on the vertical and horizontal transmission modes, as well as tissue tropism of DcPLV, are in line with previous observations for other D. citri-associated viruses such as (Chen et al., 2020) reported that DcRVs exhibited a similarly high prevalence and vertical transmission rate, along with localization in salivary glands and midgut tissues. Moreover, (Rashidi et al., 2022) demonstrated that D. citri flavi-like virus is not only transmitted vertically and horizontally but also associated with increased acquisition efficiency of CLas. Besides, it is conceivable that DcPLV may also be excreted through honeydew, a known route for other insect-specific viruses. It is important to acknowledge that the potential for honeydew-mediated contamination cannot be fully excluded. Although we took careful measures to clean plant surfaces before sampling, our current experimental results do not definitively resolve the ‘honeydew contamination’ hypothesis. Future studies specifically designed to differentiate between these transmission pathways will be essential to clarify the exact mechanisms of DcPLV presence in plant tissues.

Furthermore, the developmental profiling of DcPLV revealed early presence in eggs and a progressive accumulation across nymphal stages, peaking in adults (Fig. 3A). These findings are consistent with maternally inherited viruses that establish replication early and maintain systemic infections throughout the host’s life. Higher DcPLV titers in females further suggest sex-specific replication or immune regulation, potentially tied to ovarian tissue tropism or reproductive physiology (Fig. 2D). Such patterns have also been reported for insect specific viruses in aphids, where they have recorded the mutualistic relationship among aphids and their viruses (Ryabov et al., 2009). A conditional mutualism is recorded between the rosy apple aphid, Dysaphis plantaginea, and its densovirus (DplDV), where infection promotes the development of winged morphs under poor dietary conditions. Although these winged aphids are smaller and less fecund, they can disperse to new hosts, unlike DplDV-free individuals (Ryabov et al., 2009). This suggests that similar adaptive roles may exist for viruses like DcPLV in D. citri populations. Picorna-like viruses exhibit diverse biological interactions with their insect hosts, such as the deformed wing virus (DWV), caused wing deformities and developmental defects in honeybees (McMenamin and Flenniken, 2018). However, unlike DWV, DcPLV appears nonpathogenic, as it does not significantly impair D. citri reproduction, eclosion, or adult longevity. This is characteristic of persistent symbiotic viruses, which often evolve low-virulence relationships with their hosts to maximize vertical transmission opportunities (Nouri et al., 2016). Our findings unveiled that DcPLV did not cause any detectable change in survival or reproductive parameters, suggesting a more mutualistic or neutral interaction. It is plausible DcPLV contributes to immune modulation or microbiome homeostasis. Future studies integrating transcriptomics and microbiome profiling may help to unravel their hidden roles.

Conclusion

The present study provides an integrated analysis of DcPLV and their biological and ecological roles within populations of D. citri. Through field-based surveillance, molecular diagnostics, and physiological assays, this study found that DcPLV is highly prevalent across all surveyed citrus-growing regions with strong vertical transmission and ecological persistence. The virus was shown to be maternally inherited with 58 % efficiency, and its tissue-specific accumulation in the midgut and salivary glands suggests potential involvement in vector-pathogen interactions. It did not show a significant effect on D. citri reproduction, development and lifespan. These findings enhance the understanding of the endosymbiotic virus of D. citri to develop D. citri management options following biocontrol strategies. These results suggest that DcPLV infection enhances CLas acquisition and accumulation in D. citri, which may contribute to increased vector competence. However, further transmission assays are needed to confirm whether this also results in higher CLas transmission rates to citrus hosts. Future work may also explore the relationship between DcPLV and the host immune system to elucidate potential mechanisms for pathogen control.
