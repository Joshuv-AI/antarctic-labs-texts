Endometrial thickness and pathology in postmenopausal women with bleeding on transdermal 17β-estradiol plus body-identical progesterone
Authors: Sarah Glynne, Aini Kamal, Amy Neville, Lynsey McColl, Osama Naji, Louise Newson, Daniel Reisel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe primary objective was to explore the relationship between endometrial thickness and transdermal 17β-estradiol/micronised progesterone dose in postmenopausal women with unscheduled bleeding on menopausal hormone therapy (MHT). The prevalence of endometrial pathology was also assessed.MethodsRetrospective analysis of a consecutive case series. Postmenopausal women attending a private menopause clinic were included if they presented with unscheduled bleeding on transdermal 17β-estradiol plus micronised progesterone between 1st June 2022 and 31st May 2024, and attended for an in-house ultrasound scan.Results235 women were included (mean age 57 years, 49.37% overweight or obese). 173 women (73.62%) received on-label transdermal estradiol doses. Most women (n = 220 women, 93.62%) used continuous progesterone. On ultrasound examination, 173 women (73.62%) had a normal endometrium, 48 (20.43%) had a thickened endometrium, and 14 (5.96%) had an inadequately visualised endometrium. High BMI (> 25 kg/m2) was significantly associated with increased endometrial thickness (ET) (mean ET normal BMI vs overweight: 3.84 mm vs 4.52 mm, p = 0.07; mean ET normal BMI vs obese: 3.84 mm vs 4.50 mm, p = 0.04). There was no evidence that ET differed according to transdermal estradiol dose (on- vs off-label, p = 0.53), or by progesterone dose (low vs normal vs high, p = 0.61) or route (oral vs vaginal, p = 0.26). In multivariable analyses, there was evidence of an association between ET and MHT regimen (continuous vs sequential, p = 0.03). Amongst women with a measured serum estradiol concentration (n = 92), there was no evidence of an association between ET and serum estradiol level (p = 0.21). There were no cases of endometrial hyperplasia or cancer.ConclusionsIn the study cohort, endometrial thickness in women with unscheduled bleeding on transdermal 17β-estradiol plus micronised progesterone was not associated with MHT dose. The prevalence of endometrial pathology was low, including in women using off-label estradiol doses. Our findings suggest that progesterone dose should be clinically guided for optimal efficacy and to minimise risks. More research is needed to confirm our findings and prospectively evaluate endometrial outcomes in different patient populations over longer time periods, and to enable a more personalised approach to menopause care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00404-025-08161-w.Keywords: Menopause, Transdermal estradiol, Micronised progesterone, Body-identical MHT, Unscheduled bleeding, Endometrial pathology, Endometrial cancer

Problem statement

In high income countries, approximately 5–15% of eligible women are prescribed menopausal hormone therapy (MHT). Transdermal estradiol formulations are usually preferred, but dose must be individualised to account for differences in absorption. The lowest effective dose might be an off-label dose for up to 1 in 4 women. Around 40% of postmenopausal MHT users experience unscheduled bleeding, which may signal underlying endometrial pathology. There are limited data to inform the optimal progesterone dose for women using high and/or off-label transdermal estradiol doses, to ensure adequate endometrial protection.

What does this study add ?

No association between endometrial thickness and transdermal estradiol or progesterone dose was observed in postmenopausal women with unscheduled bleeding on menopausal hormone therapy. A patient-centred approach, in which progesterone dose is tailored to the individual, is likely to be the optimal strategy to ensure adequate endometrial protection and avoid unnecessary progesterone exposure.

Introduction

Unscheduled bleeding (USB) on menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) is defined as irregular bleeding which occurs after initiating or changing an MHT preparation that should be ‘bleed-free’ - continuous combined menopausal hormone therapy, (ccMHT), or which occurs in addition to the scheduled monthly withdrawal bleed in women using sequential preparations (sMHT) [1]. It occurs in approximately 40% of postmenopausal MHT users [2], and is a common cause of repeat attendance and premature discontinuation of MHT [2, 3]. Bleeding more than 6 months after initiating MHT, or more than 3 months after a change in dose or formulation, signals the need for further assessment to investigate for the presence of pathology, such as polyps, fibroids, endometrial hyperplasia and cancer [1].

Compared with synthetic hormone formulations, transdermal body-identical (also known as ‘bioidentical’) 17β-estradiol plus oral body- or (bio-)identical micronised progesterone (MP) is usually better tolerated and has fewer contra-indications. Consequently, transdermal 17β-estradiol combined with MP is usually considered the optimal MHT regimen [4–7], but patient preference and individualised care are key [8–10]. There is considerable inter-individual variation in transdermal estradiol absorption and pharmacokinetics, and up to 1 in 4 women may not achieve therapeutic estradiol levels (usually 200-600 pmol/L, or 55–163 pg/ml) using the highest licenced dose (4 pumps of 0.06% gel daily, or equivalent) [11]. ‘Poor absorbers’ can be offered a higher off-label dose (or a change in formulation) to achieve optimal blood levels for menopausal symptom relief, bone and cardiovascular protection [6]. However, a recent UK safety alert warned against prescribing higher than licenced oestrogen doses due to a lack of long-term safety data [12]. If in ‘exceptional circumstances’ an off-label oestrogen dose is deemed necessary, a recent guideline has recommended that the progestogen dose is increased proportionately to ensure adequate endometrial protection [1].

Available data support the endometrial efficacy of MP at 100 mg/day (ccMHT) or 200 mg/day (sMHT) in postmenopausal women using regulator-approved doses of transdermal 17β-estradiol [13–15]. There are no data supporting the endometrial safety of MP when combined with high, off-label transdermal estradiol doses. However, ‘poor absorbers’ using off-label doses to achieve normal serum estradiol levels are unlikely to be at greater risk of harm than ‘good absorbers’ who achieve normal levels using licenced doses. Further, high and/or off-label progestogen doses can cause side effects that limit adherence (e.g. low mood, hypersomnolence, bloating, constipation, and mastalgia) [16], and may increase breast cancer risk [17].

Advising doctors not to prescribe off-label transdermal estradiol doses other than in ‘exceptional circumstances’ [12], and/or recommending a ‘proportionate’ increase in progestogen dose for all women using high doses of transdermal estradiol [1], is inconsistent with international menopause guidelines which recommend that MHT dose, formulation and route should be tailored to the individual [8, 9, 18]. Many UK prescribing committees no longer support prescribing of estradiol doses above the licenced dose range in primary care, and many general practitioners (GPs) are unable or unwilling to prescribe off-label estradiol doses. Insisting that women using high estradiol doses either reduce their dose and/or increase their progestogen dose has caused considerable anxiety for women using high estradiol doses for clinical efficacy, and for the 10–20% of women who are progestogen-sensitive [16] and unable to tolerate a higher progestogen dose.

The aims of this study were two-fold: 1) to measure endometrial thickness (ET) and the prevalence of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer in a real-world cohort of postmenopausal women with USB whilst using on- and off-label doses of transdermal estradiol plus micronised progesterone, and 2) to explore the relationship between ET and body mass index (BMI), estradiol dose, serum estradiol concentration, progesterone dose, progesterone regimen (sequential vs continuous), and route of progesterone administration (oral vs vaginal). It is hoped that our paper will contribute to a better understanding of transdermal estradiol and micronised progesterone pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (endometrial effects), and facilitate more personalised, high quality menopause care.

Methods

This was a retrospective analysis of a consecutive case series. Women attending a UK-based independent menopause clinic between 1st June 2022 and 30th June 2024 were included if they had been using transdermal 17β-estradiol plus MP for ≥ 6 months, experienced USB that met the referral criteria for ultrasound assessment [19], and attended for an in-house transvaginal ultrasound scan (TVUS) during the study period. Cases were identified from the clinic database (Semble Ltd, UK) [20]. Clinical information including demographical data, prescription data, serum estradiol concentration, ultrasound outcomes, and gynaecology referral rates, were extracted from the medical records. Hysteroscopy findings and endometrial biopsy results were obtained by searching patient letters and/or contacting patients directly to verify outcomes.

Equivalent dose estimation

The British Menopause Society ‘Women’s Health Concern HT Types, Doses and Regimens’ factsheet was used to calculate equivalent doses of different estradiol formulations [21]. Estradiol dose was categorised using the ‘number of pumps equivalent’ or ‘PE’. For example, women using four pumps of 0.06% gel daily (0.75 mg estradiol per actuation), or a 100-mcg patch twice weekly, were both included in the “four pumps equivalent” (4 PE) dose category. Doses exceeding 4 pumps/day 0.06% Estrogel (> 3.0 mg estradiol daily) [22], a 100 mcg/24 hour estradiol patch twice weekly [23], or equivalent (> 4 ‘PE’), were classified as off-label.

The usual starting dose of micronised progesterone in combined MHT regimens is 100 mg daily (ccMHT), or 200 mg for 12–14 days per month (sMHT) [13]. Accordingly, oral or vaginal MP at 100 mg daily or 200 mg for 12–14 days per month was categorised as ‘normal’ dose. In ccMHT regimens, < 100 mg daily was categorised as low dose, and > 100 mg daily was categorised as high dose. In sMHT regimens, < 200 mg for 12–14 days per month and/or shorter durations (< 12 days per month), were categorised as low dose; > 200 mg for 12-14 days per month and/or longer durations (> 14 days per month but less than daily), were categorised as high dose.

Serum estradiol concentration

Serum estradiol concentration within 3 months of the ultrasound scan was recorded, if available.

Serum estradiol levels are not routinely monitored in women prescribed MHT. In the study cohort, blood tests were undertaken for the following reasons: to determine if estradiol levels were in the therapeutic range in women with a suboptimal clinical response; when there were menopausal symptoms that were difficult to distinguish from side effects such as bleeding or headaches; and/or at the patient’s request.

Blood samples were processed by Nationwide Pathology using the Atelica IM Enhanced Estradiol (eE2) assay, as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Detectable concentrations ranged from 40.95 to 10,410.00 pmol/L. The intra-assay coefficient of variation was 2.7% to 7% and the uncertainty of measurement was 5.58%.

Ultrasound assessments

All transvaginal ultrasound scans (TVUS) were performed by a single experienced sonographer (AB) using a Konica Minolta HS1 ultrasound system.

Endometrial thickness (ET) was measured as the maximum measurement in the sagittal plane with inclusion of both endometrial layers. Where there was fluid in the uterine cavity, a double layer measurement was taken (the sum of the thickness of both single layers).

Following the TVUS, women were managed as per national guidance [19]. For example, women with focal lesions, a thickened endometrium (ET of ≥ 5 mm for ccMHT, or ≥ 7 mm for sMHT), or an incompletely visualised endometrium, were referred for further assessment (endometrial biopsy +/- hysteroscopy). In the absence of pathology, women were referred back to their usual clinician for ongoing management that usually entailed adjustment of the progestogen dose/ regimen.

Statistical tests

All analyses were performed in the statistical software package R version 4.4.1 [24]. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient demographics and outcome variables. Continuous variables were summarised using the mean (standard deviation, SD), median (interquartile range, IQR) and range, and categorical variables as frequency (%).

Univariate analyses were performed to test for differences in the distribution of endometrial thickness (mm) according to BMI (normal/underweight vs overweight vs obese), MHT regimen (continuous vs sequential), progesterone route (oral vs vaginal), and estradiol/ progesterone dose, using the Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis hypothesis tests. Where statistically significant differences were found with the Kruskal–Wallis test, post hoc pairwise comparisons were carried out using Dunn’s test and p values were adjusted for multiple testing using Holm’s method.

In multivariable analyses, a linear model was fitted to the log-transformed endometrial thickness to test for an association with BMI, estradiol dose, progesterone dose, MHT regimen and progesterone route. No interactions were explored because the subgroup sample sizes were small. The ANOVA test was used to carry out tests of significance for each explanatory variable. Results are reported using a coefficient table and analysis of variance (ANOVA), and comparisons between different variable combinations were calculated using the R package model-based [25].

The proportion of patients using on- vs off-label estradiol doses was compared using the statistical hypothesis test of equal proportions. Differences in the distributions of serum estradiol concentration by on- vs off-label estradiol dose were compared using the Mann–Whitney hypothesis tests.

Research ethics and consent

Informed consent was obtained from all patients for their data to be used for the purpose of research and audit. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the UCL Research Ethics Committee (UCL REC ID: 9093.008).

Results

235 women were eligible for study inclusion. The mean age of the study cohort was 56.84 years (SD 4.56 years, range 47–75 years). Half of the cohort were overweight or obese (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, n = 116, 49.37%). 173 women (73.62%) were prescribed on-label transdermal estradiol doses (1–4 PE), and 62 (26.38%) were prescribed off-label doses (≥ 5 PE). Most (n = 220, 93.62%) were prescribed continuous (daily) progesterone, and most (n = 194, 82.55%) were using a normal progesterone dose when they reported unscheduled bleeding (MP dose range: 100 mg for 12 days every 3 months to 200 mg/day). 16 of 18 women using low-dose MP used vaginal MP.

Amongst women who initiated MHT at our clinic (n = 142, 60.4%), the mean number of months from date of initiation to ultrasound scan was 22.54 months (SD 14.22 months, range 4–66 months). 93 women (39.6%) initiated MHT elsewhere and attended to have their dose/regimen optimised (duration of MHT use not known).

173 women (73.62%) had a normal endometrial thickness and appearance (sMHT: 13/15, 86.67%; ccMHT: 160/220, 72.73%). 48 women (20.43%) had a thickened endometrium (sMHT: 2/15, 13.33%; ccMHT 46/220, 20.91%). 14 women (5.96%) had a poorly visualised endometrium. 54 women (22.98%) were referred to gynaecology for further assessment; 50 of 54 women referred to gynaecology had an endometrial biopsy and all revealed normal histology.

Multivariable modelling (Supplemental Material, Tables S3 and S4) confirmed evidence of an association between endometrial thickness and BMI (p = 0.027). Endometrial thickness was on average 14.6% higher in women who were overweight vs normal/ underweight (95% CI 1.1%–29.8%), and 17.4% greater in women who were obese vs normal/ underweight (95% CI 1.8%–35.5%). Multivariable modelling also confirmed that there was no evidence of an association between endometrial thickness and estradiol dose (p = 0.74), progesterone dose (p = 0.82), or progesterone route (p = 0.37). However, in contrast to univariate analyses, there was evidence of an association between endometrial thickness and MHT regimen at the 5% level (p = 0.03). Endometrial thickness was estimated to be on average 29.0% greater in women using sequential vs continuous progesterone, but the confidence interval was wide (95% CI 3.3%–60.9%).

The distribution of serum estradiol concentration was positively skewed with a median concentration of 348.30 pmol/L (IQR 198.60–613.60 pmol/L). The median serum estradiol concentration in women using on- vs off-label doses was 254.8 pmol/L vs 507.6 pmol/L (p <0.001). Discussion

In this observational study, we retrospectively explored the association between endometrial thickness and BMI, serum estradiol concentration, and MHT dose, route and regimen, in a cohort of postmenopausal women with unscheduled bleeding on body-identical MHT (transdermal 17β-estradiol plus micronised progesterone). In the study cohort, ET was positively associated with BMI. In univariate analyses, there was no evidence of an association between ET and transdermal estradiol dose, progesterone dose, progesterone route, or MHT regimen. Multivariable modelling revealed an association between ET and MHT regimen, but with a high level of uncertainty (likely because the subgroup sample size was small, n = 15). In women with a measured serum estradiol concentration, there was no evidence of an association between ET and estradiol concentration. There were no cases of endometrial hyperplasia or cancer.

Endometrial thickness and BMI

In the study cohort, ET was significantly greater in overweight and obese women compared with women with a normal BMI.

Our findings are consistent with previous data demonstrating a positive association between BMI and ET [26]. Obesity is a proinflammatory, hyper-estrogenic state. Aromatisation of adrenal androgens in adipose tissue leads to elevated circulating levels of both estradiol (the major premenopausal oestrogen, anti-inflammatory), and estrone (the dominant postmenopausal oestrogen, pro-inflammatory) in overweight and obese postmenopausal women [27–29]. Excess estradiol and estrone stimulate endometrial proliferation and thickening, but evidence suggests that estrone is the principal oestrogen driving development and progression to endometrial cancer [27, 28]. Endometrial cancer risk is higher in postmenopausal obese women who have never been exposed to MHT [30], suggesting that MHT has a protective effect.

Given that obesity is a major risk factor for endometrial hyperplasia and cancer [31], and MHT seems to mitigate risk in obese women, MHT use should be encouraged for obese women with bothersome menopausal symptoms. MHT also reduces risk of diabetes and metabolic syndrome [32], which are more prevalent in obese women and independently increase endometrial cancer risk [31]. Transdermal estradiol is the optimal formulation for women with an elevated BMI because it has not been shown to increase the risk of venous thromboembolic events in observational studies [33–35]. Further, oral administration of estradiol results in markedly elevated serum estrone levels but transdermal administration does not [36]. Closer monitoring of serum levels +/- dose adjustments may be required because obesity impacts all four phases of drug pharmacokinetics [37]. If obese women develop USB on transdermal estradiol plus MP and endometrial pathology has been excluded, the progesterone dose should be increased to arrest bleeding and enable continued use of MHT. The LNG-IUS can alternatively be used but is associated with a small increased risk of breast cancer [38, 39]. Obesity increases breast cancer risk, and obese women should be counselled about both the potential benefits of the LNG-IUS (e.g. convenience, superior bleeding control, reduced endometrial cancer risk, contraception) and risks (e.g. progestin-related side effects, expulsion, possible small increased risk of breast cancer) when choosing a progestogen.

Endometrial thickness and estradiol dose

There was no evidence of an association between mean ET and transdermal estradiol dose.

The lack of an association between ET and transdermal estradiol dose is not surprising considering that transdermal absorption is highly variable and around 1 in 3 women do not achieve therapeutic estradiol levels (usually 200–600 pmol/L) using licenced doses [11, 36]. Consequently, in clinical practice off-label transdermal estradiol doses are mainly prescribed to overcome poor absorption and achieve normal blood levels.

In the study cohort, 92 women had measurement of serum estradiol concentration, including 38 women (41.30%) using off-label doses. The median estradiol concentration was within the therapeutic range, including in women using off-label doses, confirming that off-label doses were mainly used to overcome poor transdermal absorption and did not result in excessive systemic oestrogen exposure. We did not find evidence of a correlation between estradiol level and ET, likely because the pharmacodynamic (endometrial) effects of estradiol also vary between individuals due to differences in tissue sensitivity. Overall, our findings suggest that if women without risk factors for endometrial cancer use high transdermal estradiol doses to achieve normal blood levels, a proportionate increase in progestogen dose is unlikely to be necessary. Women using off-label transdermal estradiol doses to achieve high levels for clinical effect, around 3% of women based on very limited data [40], are likely to be at greater risk of USB and endometrial hyperplasia and may benefit from a higher progesterone dose and/or more frequent endometrial monitoring.

Tailoring the progestogen type and dose to the individual reduces the risk of side effects and may have other health benefits. In randomised clinical trials, use of synthetic progestins in combined MHT regimens is associated with a small increase in breast cancer risk. In the combined arm of the Women’s Health Initiative trial (n = 16,608), breast cancer incidence was significantly higher in postmenopausal women treated with conjugated equine oestrogen plus medroxyprogesterone acetate for 5.6 years although there was no significant difference in breast cancer mortality after 20 years of cumulative follow-up (annualised breast cancer incidence 0.45% vs 0.36%, HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.13–1.45; annualised breast cancer mortality 0.045% vs 0.035%, HR 1.35, 95% CI 0.94–1.95) [41]. The synthetic progestin was likely to account for the increase in breast cancer risk in combined MHT users, because oestrogen only MHT has breast protective effects [41, 42]. There haven’t been any RCTs comparing breast cancer incidence in women treated with body-identical hormones vs placebo, but oestrogen combined with body-identical progesterone for up to 5 years has not been shown to increase breast cancer risk in observational studies [43, 44]. Until further data are available, the decision to prescribe an off-label progestogen dose should be clinically guided, to ensure that ‘poor absorbers’ with normal estradiol levels are not exposed to high +/- off-label progestogen doses unnecessarily.

Endometrial thickness and progestogen dose, route and regimen.

In the study cohort, there was no evidence of an association between ET and MP dose or route. None of the study participants were diagnosed with endometrial hyperplasia or cancer during the study interval.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have demonstrated endometrial protection when oral MP in combined MHT regimens is applied sequentially in doses of 50–200 mg/ day, or continuously in doses of 100–400 mg/day [15, 45]. However, none of the previous studies included women using off-label transdermal estradiol doses. Our findings additionally suggest that MP doses ranging from 100 mg for 12 days every 3 months to 200 mg daily may provide adequate endometrial protection in women using off-label transdermal estradiol doses, although the number of women using low-dose MP plus high dose transdermal estradiol was very small (n = 3).

In our study, most women in the low-dose MP subgroup used vaginal MP. Low-dose MP is often used per vaginum by women with progesterone intolerance, because vaginal administration avoids first pass hepatic metabolism and results in lower serum levels of pregnanolones and metabolites that are associated with side effects [36]. Few studies have examined the effect of vaginal MP on the endometrium when combined with transdermal estradiol. Those that have indicate that low and normal dose vaginal MP can provide effective endometrial protection for up to 3 years, but only women using low-to-moderate estradiol doses (1–2 PE) were included [45].

In the ELITE study (n = 526), low dose, vaginal MP (45 mg/day for 10 days per month) was insufficient to completely oppose the endometrial effects of low-dose oral estradiol (1 mg/day) [46]. Over 80 months of follow-up, participants in the active treatment group had progressive and statistically significant increases in endometrial thickness (p < 0.001), underwent more endometrial biopsies, and had higher rates of endometrial hyperplasia compared with the placebo group (hyperplasia with atypia: 3.7% vs 0%; hyperplasia without atypia 8.2% vs 0%). There was 1 case of endometrial cancer in the active treatment group (0.7%) vs 2 cases in the placebo group (3.1%) [46]. Consequently, it is recommended that if used vaginally, MP should be given in similar doses and durations as suggested for oral MP [13].

In contrast to the ELITE study, we did not observe an increase in ET in women using low dose, vaginal MP. Further, the prevalence of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer in our study was 0%, which is lower than that reported in similar studies (endometrial hyperplasia: 0.5–2.0%, endometrial cancer: 1.3%) [47, 48]. Possible reasons include lower estradiol and/or estrone levels in women using on- and off-label doses of transdermal estradiol vs. women treated with oral estrogens in other studies, and/or use of body-identical hormones vs synthetic hormones in other studies. However, the prevalence of endometrial pathology in our study may have been underestimated because the sample size was small (n = 235 including just 18 women using low-dose MP), the mean duration of MHT use was short (22.54 months) or unknown, and we did not have histological outcomes for all women (biopsies were only done if clinically indicated). 173 women had a normal endometrial thickness/ appearance and were not referred for further investigation, in line with national guidance [1]. Of 62 women with a thickened or poorly visualised endometrium, 50 (80.65%) subsequently underwent biopsy +/- hysteroscopy. Eight women were not referred for further investigations mainly because the bleeding was managed medically and resolved (Supplemental material, Table S1), and 4 women were referred but did not have a biopsy. Therefore, until further data are available, women using low-dose oral or vaginal MP should continue to have regular endometrial surveillance as per current guidelines [1].

There was some evidence of an association between ET and MHT regimen. Mean ET was greater in women using sMHT vs ccMHT (5.07 mm vs 4.08 mm) and, in multivariable analyses, the difference was statistically significant. The confidence interval was wide indicating a high level of uncertainty, likely because the number of sMHT users in the study cohort was small (n = 15) and the overall prevalence of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer in postmenopausal women with USB on MHT is low. Our findings are consistent with previous data showing that continuous combined MHT regimens provide more effective endometrial protection than sequential MHT regimens (endometrial hyperplasia: 1–2% of ccMHT users and 2.5–16% of sMHT users; endometrial cancer: 1.3–2% of ccMHT users and 5% of sMHT users) [1, 13].

Strengths and limitations

Transdermal estradiol and micronised progesterone are usually considered the optimal or ‘gold-standard’ MHT formulations [4–7], and are the most frequently prescribed MHT items in the UK [49]. However, evidence on the endometrial safety of transdermal estradiol combined with micronised progesterone is limited [15, 45]. Our study adds to the available literature and is the first to include women using both on and off-label transdermal estradiol doses in a real-world setting. Use of real-world data enhances the generalisability of our findings because it accounts for factors that influence MHT pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics in the real world, e.g. differences in the site and/or surface area of transdermal estradiol application, the timing of oral progesterone administration relative to food ingestion, and suboptimal compliance. All transvaginal scans were conducted in a single centre by a single sonographer, which enhances the reliability of our findings because there was no inter-operator variability.

This was an observational study and data were analysed retrospectively. As such, it is subject to the usual limitations that are inherent in retrospective, non-randomised studies. First, 95% of our clinic attendees are White British, and women who access private healthcare are generally less deprived and therefore less likely to experience chronic ill-health. Ethnicity, healthy lifestyle behaviours, comorbidities and polypharmacy influence estradiol and progesterone pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. This limits the generalisability of our findings. Second, some of the subgroup sample sizes were small which increases the risk of Type II errors (false negatives). For example, just 18 women were included in the low-dose MP subgroup. We did not find evidence of an association between endometrial thickness and progesterone dose, but this may simply be due to a lack of power (i.e. small sample size) rather than because an association doesn’t exist. Third, other than BMI we did not collect data concerning other factors that influence ET and cancer risk (e.g. PCOS, diabetes, and a family history of endometrial cancer) [31]. Fourth, serum estradiol concentration can fluctuate considerably in postmenopausal MHT users [11]. A single estradiol level measured up to 3 months before the ultrasound scan may differ substantially from the serum estradiol concentration at the time of bleeding/ scan, and does not reflect cumulative endometrial oestrogen exposure. Fifth, the study duration was short and we were not able to measure the duration of MHT use for the whole cohort. Finally, this was an observational study and endometrial biopsies were only performed where clinically indicated. It is therefore possible that the prevalence of endometrial pathology was higher than reported.

Despite the study limitations, our findings are of interest and signal the need for larger studies of longer duration to prospectively evaluate endometrial outcomes in women using on- and off-label doses of transdermal estradiol plus body-identical progesterone in the real-world, especially perimenopausal women who are frequently prescribed MHT but have been excluded from most studies [15].

Conclusion

In this cohort of postmenopausal women using on- and off-label transdermal estradiol doses in combination with oral or vaginal micronised progesterone, most women had a normal endometrial thickness and appearance and the prevalence of endometrial pathology was low. High BMI and MHT regimen were the only measured variables associated with significant increases in ET. Notably, there was no evidence of an association between ET and transdermal estradiol dose (on- vs off-label), likely because most women using off-label doses absorb estradiol poorly across the skin and therefore use high doses to achieve normal blood levels.

Both transdermal estradiol and progestogen pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics are highly variable [11, 36, 50]. The same duration, dose and type of progestogen in combined MHT regimens may cause endometrial atrophy in some women but hyperplasia in others [16, 50]. Therefore, rather than recommending a progesterone dose that is ‘proportionate’ to the transdermal estradiol dose, we suggest that progesterone dose decisions should be clinically guided according to: the high transdermal estradiol dose indication - poor absorption (low or normal estradiol levels) vs therapeutic efficacy (high levels); the bleeding pattern - light vs heavy flow and/or the presence of unscheduled bleeding; the presence of risk factors for endometrial hyperplasia/cancer, such as obesity; the likelihood of progestogenic side effects; and patient views and preferences. A ‘proportionate’ increase in progesterone dose might be appropriate if women are using high dose oral estradiol because high oral doses are more likely to correlate with high blood levels of estradiol and estradiol metabolites (estrone and estrone sulphate) [36], and blood tests are less informative in women treated with oral estradiol. If a high progestogen dose is indicated but not tolerated or desired, the endometrium should be monitored at regular intervals. Tailoring the progesterone dose to the individual is consistent with international menopause guidelines that recommend an ‘adequate’ or ‘appropriate’ progestogen dose, because all women are different [8, 9, 18]. Ensuring that all MHT users achieve optimal estradiol and progesterone levels is essential to reduce the risk of side effects including USB and endometrial pathology, and to avoid the harms of under-treatment which include persistent menopausal symptoms, an increased risk of osteoporosis [51], and increased cardiovascular and all-cause mortality [52].

Summary sentence

There was no evidence of an association between endometrial thickness and transdermal estradiol or progesterone dose in postmenopausal women with unscheduled bleeding on body-identical MHT. Progesterone dose should be tailored to the individual to ensure adequate endometrial protection and reduce risk of side effects.
