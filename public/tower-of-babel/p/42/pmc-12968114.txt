Remote Ischemic Preconditioning Exerts Neuroprotective Effects Via the PGC-1α/FNDC5/BDNF Pathway in Focal Brain Ischemia of Rats
Authors: Teruki Matsuoka, Ryoma Matsuzaki, Kazuki Nakanishi, Shogo Kakimoto, Yuki Kato, Shotaro Otsuka, Kiyoshi Kikuchi, Harutoshi Sakakima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRemote ischemic preconditioning (RIpreC) is a strategy for remotely protecting target organs such as the brain by applying brief ischemia and reperfusion to the limb. However, the mechanisms underlying RIpreC-induced neuroprotection remain unclear. We aimed to investigate the neuroprotective effects of RIpreC on the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-gamma coactivator 1 alpha (PGC-1α)/ fibronectin type III domain-containing protein 5 (FNDC5)/ brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) pathway in rat models of ischemic stroke. Rats were assigned to three groups: ischemia-reperfusion injury (IR, n = 11), RIpreC and IR (RIpreC, n = 11), and intact control (control, n = 5). RIpreC was performed approximately 24 h before IR injury. IR injury induced by middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) for 60 min. At 2 days after MCAO, brain infarction, sensorimotor functions, and the expression of PGC-1α, FNDC5/irisin, BDNF, B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2), Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax), caspase-3, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated biotinylated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL)-positive cells, and the p-Akt/Akt ratio were examined. In addition, SR-18,292, a PGC-1α inhibitor, was administered 1 h before MCAO. The RIpreC group had a significantly reduced infarct volume and ameliorated sensorimotor function compared with the IR group. RIpreC increased the expression of PGC-1α, FNDC5, BDNF, and the p-Akt/Akt ratio in the ischemic brain. Bax/Bcl-2 ratios, as well as perilesional caspase-3- and TUNEL-positive neuronal cells, decreased following RIpreC. Inhibition of PGC-1α blunted the RIpreC-induced neuroprotective and neurorestorative effects. This study suggests that RIpreC induced BDNF expression through a PGC-1α/FNDC5 pathway after ischemic stroke, thereby exerting neuroprotection and ameliorating post-stroke neurobehavioral dysfunctions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12975-026-01422-z.Keywords: RIpreC, PGC-1α, Irisin, BDNF, Neuroprotection, Ischemic stroke

Introduction

The application of remote ischemic conditioning (RIC) has recently gained increasing attention as a therapeutic approach to promote recovery after stroke [1]. RIC activates endogenous protective pathways in distant organs, such as the brain and heart, and serves as a strategy for remotely protecting a target organ by applying brief cycles of ischemia and reperfusion to the limb [2, 3]. As a therapy based on endogenous mechanisms of protection, RIC shows potential for several diseases, such as stroke, dementia, and heart attack [2, 4]. In experimental stroke models, RIC decreases ischemic lesions and improves neurological outcomes. It can be applied before ischemia (remote ischemic preconditioning, RIpreC), during ischemia (remote ischemic perconditioning, RIperC), and after reperfusion (remote ischemic postconditioning, RIpostC) [1, 5, 6].

Ischemic stroke is the leading cause of serious long-term physical disability and mortality worldwide. Advances in acute treatments, such as thrombolysis with recombinant tissue plasminogen activator and mechanical thrombectomy, have improved survival rates; however, the number of ischemic stroke survivors living with disabilities has significantly increased [7]. The use of recombinant tissue plasminogen activator remains limited to small proportion of patients because of its narrow therapeutic time window [8, 9]. Recent treatments for acute stroke have mainly focused on restoring blood flow, but they do not directly repair damaged brain tissue. Preventive interventions, such as RIpreC, may reduce the risk of ischemic injury prior to stroke onset and contribute to subsequent functional recovery. Therefore, we investigated the effects and underlying mechanisms of RIpreC as a preventive approach within RIC therapies. RIpreC allows the study of mechanisms associated with RIC-induced neuroprotection.

Although the onset of ischemic stroke is unpredictable, RIpreC has minimal adverse effects and potential as a preventive measure against diseases, such as cerebral small vessel disease, recurrent ischemic stroke, and post-stroke cognitive decline [4, 10, 11]. In addition, it has been adopted to protect the brain from subsequent serious ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury [12]. The pathophysiology of acute stroke involves multiple mechanisms, including oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, neuronal cell death, and blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption. In preclinical studies, RIpreC reduces cerebral infarction, alleviates brain edema, and reduces neuronal apoptosis compared with conditions in which RIpreC is not applied prior to ischemic stroke [1, 13, 14]. One underling mechanism involves the activation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α pathway, a transcriptional factor that regulates inflammation, apoptosis, and energy metabolism, and may play a critical in RIpreC-mediated neuroprotection against cerebral ischemia [13]. RIpreC increases in the levels of brain proteins associated with ischemic tolerance, including brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and heat shock protein 70 [15]. RIpreC also significantly downregulates 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine, a marker of oxidative DNA damage, after ischemic stroke, indicating an inhibitory effect on oxidative stress [16]. Moreover, RIpreC inhibits apoptotic pathways, such as the apoptosis-inducing factor/poly ADP ribose polymerase-1 pathway [16]. It may attenuate excessive mitophagy, which enhances mitochondrial structure and function, thereby promoting cell survival while reducing infarct volume after ischemic injury [17]. Several studies have demonstrated that RIpreC-mediated neuroprotection involves anti-inflammatory activity, increased cerebral blood flow (CBF), suppression of neuronal death, stimulation of neurogenesis, and activation of antioxidant responses [4]. Potential signaling mechanisms associated with communication between the limbs and brain have been identified in animal models and include autonomic nervous system pathways and biochemical mediators [4, 18]. Therefore, multiple mechanisms may be involved in RIpreC-induced neuroprotection [12]. However, the mechanisms underlying RIpreC-induced neuroprotection remain unclear.

Irisin is a myokine cleaved from fibronectin type III domain-containing protein 5 (FNDC5) and functions as a novel hormone primarily released by skeletal muscles after physical exercise [19, 20]. Recent studies have reported that the membrane-bound precursor protein FNDC5/irisin contributes to neuroprotection against cerebral ischemia through various mechanisms, such as reduced neuronal apoptosis, decreased neuroinflammation, and increased BDNF protein secretion [21, 22]. Physical exercise-induced secretion of muscle-derived peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-gamma coactivator 1 alpha (PGC-1α), a transcriptional coactivator and metabolic regulator, drives the expression of FNDC5 [22, 23]. FNDC5 mRNA is expressed not only in muscle cells but also in human brain tissue [24], and irisin present in several brain regions, such as the hippocampus and cerebrum [22]. In the mouse hippocampus, FNDC5 expression increases with physical exercise, and PGC-1α and FNDC5 regulate BDNF expression in the brain [25]. Recently, several studies have reported that FNDC5/irisin plays a key role in regulating metabolism, reducing inflammation and apoptosis, and exerting antioxidant effects in brain injury [21, 22, 26, 27]. Irisin administration upregulates B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) and BDNF while downregulating Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax) and caspase-3 activity in the ischemic brain [21]. Irisin treatment also ameliorates neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis in intracerebral hemorrhage of mice [28]. In addition, it improves neurobehavioral function, alleviates oxidative stress, and reduces neuronal apoptosis in early brain injury after experimental subarachnoid hemorrhage [29]. Activation of the ERK1/2 and Akt pathways mediates irisin-induced protection against ischemia, which contributes to the neuroprotective effects of exercise [26].

Hess et al. [2] reported evidence suggesting that RIC and physical exercise share common mechanisms. Exercise preconditioning, a prophylactic intervention, protects the brain from subsequent injury by preserving the BBB, modulating inflammatory responses, promoting angiogenesis, and inhibiting apoptosis and glutamate overactivation [30–33],. Exercise preconditioning provides neuroprotection against acute stroke by reducing neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, while enhancing BDNF expression in the penumbra area [34]. Taken together, preclinical evidence suggests that RIpreC and exercise preconditioning share a common mechanism. Therefore, RIpreC may regulate brain irisin expression and provide neuroprotection in acute ischemic stroke. However, whether FNDC5/irisin is involved in the neuroprotective mechanisms of RIpreC after IR injury is unclear. In this study, we aimed to determine whether the PGC-1α/FNDC5/BDNF pathway is involved in the neuroprotective mechanisms of RIpreC against ischemic stroke.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Experimental Protocol

Seventy-three male Sprague-Dawley rats (body weight, 232.3 ± 28.0 g; CLEA Japan, Inc., Shizuoka, Japan) were used in this study. They were trained for 6 days before the experiment to familiarize then with the rotarod and beam walking tasks. After familiarization, motor behaviors and sensorimotor functions were assessed. The rats were divided into three groups to ensure comparable motor function across groups: IR injury group (IR, n = 11), IR and RIpreC group (RIpreC, n = 11), and normal intact control group (control, n = 5). Fifteen rats were excluded due to death during surgery (n = 10) or the absence of infarction confirmed by 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining (n = 5). All rats were housed in pairs in a temperature-controlled room (23.0 ± 1.0℃) under a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with water and food available ad libitum. This study was approved by the Ethics Board of the Institute of Laboratory Animal Sciences of Kagoshima University (M21007) and conformed to the Animal Research Reporting In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines. RIpreC Protocol

RIpreC treatment was performed approximately 24 h before IR injury. For RIpreC, the rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% induction, 1.5% maintenance) administered via face mask. RIpreC was delivered using a custom-developed automatic pump controller (DMH-01; KOBATA GAUGE MFG. Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) with pediatric cuffs (ASWAN 8–9016-01, size: 25 mm × 90 mm; Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). The cuff was placed above both thighs, and the automatic pump delivered three cycles of 10-min inflation and deflation at 170–180 mmHg to occlude blood flow. This protocol was based on a previous study [35]. Furthermore, blood flow was examined in six animals using laser Doppler flowmetry (fiber-optic laser blood flow meter FLO-C1; Omega Wave Co., Ltd., Japan) with a standard probe (diameter: 3.0 mm, depth: 1.0 mm). Dorsalis pedis blood flow was measured to confirm ischemia and reperfusion in the left hindlimb (Supplementary Fig. 1B). CBF was recorded in the left hemisphere, 2 mm lateral and posterior to the bregma, from the skull surface without craniotomy (Supplementary Fig. 1 C). Blood flow was monitored before and at 2, 5, and 10 min during each cycle (10 min ischemia and reperfusion), and the percentage change relative to baseline was calculated.

Ischemic Stroke Induction

Rats were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of medetomidine (0.3 mg/kg), midazolam (2.0 mg/kg), and butorphanol (2.5 mg/kg). IR injury was induced by 60 min of left middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO), followed by reperfusion using an intraluminal filament, as described in our previous studies [34, 36]. Rectal temperature was monitored throughout the surgery and maintained at 37.0 ℃ ± 0.5 ℃ using a heating blanket (KN-474; NATSUME, Tokyo, Japan). Two days after IR injury, the rats were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital and perfused transcranially with physiological saline. The brain, including ischemic regions, was then collected for analysis.

Evaluation of Ischemic Infarct

The brain was carefully cut into seven 2-mm-thick coronal sections from the frontal tip (IR and RIpreC, n = 11, control, n = 5). These sections were immersed in a 1% solution of TTC in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) at 37.0 ℃ for 10–15 min. After TTC staining, the sections were scanned into a computer to examine ischemic infarct volume. Infarct size was measured using Image J software 1.52v (NIH, USA), and the total infarct volume (mm3) was calculated.

Assessment of Neurological Scores and Sensorimotor Functions

Rats were assessed 2 days after IR injury using neurological scoring, the beam walking task, the rotarod task, and the sticky tape reaction task (IR and RIpreC, n = 11, control, n = 5). Neurological function was graded on a 5-point scale (0–4), as described in our previous study [34, 37]. The rotarod task (MK-670; MUROMACHI KIKAI CO, LTD, Japan) examined motor coordination and balance following the method described in our previous study [34]. Briefly, the rotation speed increased from 0 to 25 rpm in increments of 2.5 rpm every 6 s. The trail ended if the animal fell off the cylinder. Each animal was given 3 trails. The average value was used for analysis. Motor behavior was further evaluated with the beam walking task using an elevated narrow beam (50 cm long × 2.5 cm wide), scored on a 6-point scale (0–5), as described in our previous study [34]. Sensorimotor function was assessed with the adhesive sticky tape reaction task, which measured the time taken to respond to tape applied to the forelimbs. Each forepaw was tested in two trials, and the average reaction time (s) was recorded. The cutoff for tape reaction was set at 120 s. Two individuals performed these assessments.

Histology and Immunohistochemistry

Brain sections (#2, 3, 5 of seven consecutive TTC sections) were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4 ℃ overnight and embedded in paraffin for histological and immunohistochemical analyses (IR and RIpreC, n = 11, control, n = 5, Fig. 2A). Coronal sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with the following antibodies: rabbit anti-PGC-1α (1:500; ab191838; Abcam plc, USA), rabbit anti-FNDC5 (1:400; a marker of irisin; 23995-1-AP; Proteintech, USA), rabbit anti-BDNF (1:500; bs-4989R; Bioss Inc., Woburn, USA), rabbit anti-Bcl-2 (1:400; a marker of anti-apoptotic protein; bs-4563R; Bioss Inc., Woburn, USA), rabbit anti-Bax (1:400; a marker of pro-apoptotic protein; ab32503; Abcam Plc, USA), and rabbit anti-caspase-3 (1:500; a marker of apoptotic activity; 19677-1-AP; Proteintech, USA). Subsequently, the sections were washed three times in PBS for 10 min each and incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to a peroxidase-labeled dextran polymer (EnVision; Dako, CA, USA) for 60 min. Immunoreactivity was visualized using diaminobenzidine staining. Immunohistochemical procedures were described in our previous study [34].

Co-localization of mouse anti-neuronal nuclei (NeuN) antibody (1:200; a marker of neurons; ab104224; Abcam plc, Cambridge, UK) with rabbit anti-caspase-3 antibody (1:200; 19677-1-AP; Proteintech, USA) immunoreactivities was examined using immunofluorescence staining. Immunofluorescence staining was observed using a fluorescence microscope (EVOS M5000 imaging system; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.).

Semi-Quantitative Analyses of Immunostained Sections

Two areas in the motor cortex of the ischemic penumbra in each immunolabeled section were photographed at 20× magnification with a camera-equipped microscope, ensuring no overlap of the visual fields (Fig. 2A, B, rectangle areas). Expression of PGC-1α, FNDC5, BDNF, Bcl-2, Bax, and caspase-3 was quantitatively measured in coronal sections (#2 of 7 consecutive sections) using Image J software (1.52v). Semi-quantitative analyses were performed by two or three blinded investigators, and mean values were analyzed. In addition, immunofluorescent-stained sections showing co-localization of caspase-3 and NeuN in the ischemic penumbra were photographed at 20× magnification. The ratio of caspase-3- and NeuN-positive neurons to the total NeuN-positive neurons was determined, and quantitative analyses were conducted by two or three blinded investigators.

Apoptotic Neuronal Cell Detection

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated biotinylated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay was performed in the ischemic penumbra using an apoptosis in situ detection kit (293–71501; Wako Chemicals, Japan, Inc.), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sections were counterstained with methyl green. Two areas in the penumbra were photographed at 20× magnification using the same system as described above (Fig. 2A, B, rectangle areas). The number of TUNEL-positive neurons was counted in the sections (#2 of seven consecutive sections). Analyses were performed by two blinded investigators, and mean values were analyzed.

Western Blotting

After TTC staining, protein levels in ipsilateral brain sections (#1, 4, and 6 of seven consecutive sections, IR and RIpreC, n = 5, control, n = 4) were investigated by western blotting, which was performed as previously described [34]. A previous report showed that TTC staining did not constrict quantitative gene and protein analyses [38]. The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit anti-PGC-1α (1:1000; ab191838; Abcam plc, Cambridge, UK), rabbit anti-FNDC5 (1:1000; 23995-1-AP; Proteintech), rabbit anti-BDNF (1:500; ab108319; Abcam plc, Cambridge, UK), anti-rabbit-phospho-Akt (1:1000; #9271; Cell Signaling), anti-rabbit-Akt (1:1000; #9272; Cell Signaling), rabbit anti-Bcl-2 antibody (1:1000; bs-4563R; Bioss Inc., Woburn, USA), rabbit anti-Bax antibody (bs-0127R; Bioss Inc., Woburn, USA), rabbit anti-caspase-3 (1:1000; 19677-1-AP; Proteintech), and anti-mouse alpha-tubulin (1:8000; 66031-lg; Proteintech Group, Inc., USA). Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence device (WSE-6100 LuminoGraph I; ATTO, Tokyo, Japan) and measured using ImageJ software (1.52v). Intensities were normalized to the corresponding α-tubulin levels in the same samples.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Plasma irisin levels were measured using a commercially available rat irisin ELISA kit (FineTest, Wuhan Fine Biotech Co., Ltd., China; ER1486) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Blood samples were collected within 1 h after RIpreC (RIpreC 1 h, n = 6), 24 h after RIpreC (RIpreC 24 h, n = 6), and normal intact control (control, n = 6). Plasma was isolated by centrifugation and used for the quantification of circulating irisin. Data were processed and analyzed using the ELISA Calculator (Arigo Biolaboratories, Taiwan).

Inhibition of PGC-1α by SR-18,292

To examine the effect of RIpreC on PGC-1α signaling after stroke, another seven rats received SR-18,292 (45 mg/kg), a PGC-1α inhibitor, by intraperitoneal injection (RIpreC + SR group). The inhibitor was administered 1 h before MCAO induction (Fig. 1A). SR-18,292 was obtained from Selleck Chemicals (USA) and diluted in dimethyl sulfoxide (046–21981; Wako, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Two days after MCAO, the rats were perfused with physiological saline under deep anesthesia using sodium pentobarbital. The brains, including the ischemic regions, were excised for analysis. TTC staining, neurological scoring, sensorimotor testing, and immunohistochemistry were performed using the same methods as described. Data were compared with those of the IR group. In addition, semi-quantitative analysis of immunolabeled sections was performed in two areas of the ischemic penumbra using the same methods (Fig. 2A, B, rectangle areas).

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis involved parametric or non-parametric testing, as appropriate. Dorsalis pedis blood flow was analyzed using a Friedman test, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc tests for multiple comparisons. For comparisons among three or more groups, the infarct volume, neurological score, beam walking score, tape reaction time, and plasma irisin levels were analyzed using a Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc tests for multiple comparisons. The immunopositive area and protein levels for PGC-1α, FENDC5, BDNF, p-Akt, Akt, Bcl-2, Bax, caspase-3, and TUNEL were analyzed using the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc tests for multiple comparisons. Furthermore, to explore the effects of RIpreC, we also compared the infarct volume and neurological scores between the two groups using the Student’s t-test and Mann–Whitney U test, respectively. For comparisons between groups, the percentage of caspase 3 and NeuN positive cells, infarct volume walking time, percent area which was positive for PGC1-1, FNDC5, BDNF, caspase-3 were analyzed using the Student’s t-test. The neurological score, beam walking score, and tape reaction time were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.2.3 (GraphPad Holdings, LLC, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

RIpreC Altered the Blood Flow of the Dorsalis Pedis and CBF

Blood flow changes in the dorsalis pedis were observed during the ischemic and reperfusion phases of the first cycle, as the three cycles showed similar patterns (Fig. 1C). Blood flow in the ischemic phase decreased (approximately 29%) due to thigh compression of the hindlimb, compared with pre-ischemic levels (100%). After reperfusion, blood flow immediately increased compared with the ischemic phase, then gradually decreased toward pre-reperfusion values (Fig. 1C), indicating ischemia and reperfusion of the femoral artery induced by RIpreC treatment. By contrast, blood flow in the early reperfusion phase exceeded pre-ischemic levels.

No significant change was observed in the proportion of CBF during the three RIpreC cycles. However, CBF responses differed between the ischemia and reperfusion phases. CBF fluctuated during ischemia, whereas CBF gradually increased during reperfusion until the end of the phase (Fig. 1D).

RIpreC Reduced Ischemic Infarction and Attenuated Neurological Scores and Sensorimotor Functions

TTC and HE staining revealed the extent of brain infarction in the left hemisphere (Fig. 2A, B). The RIpreC group exhibited a significantly reduced infarct volume compared with the IR group (p < 0.01, Fig. 2C). The IR group showed decreased neurological scores and impaired sensorimotor function compared with the control group. Neurological and beam walking scores were significantly improved in the RIpreC group compared with the IR group (p < 0.05, Fig. 2D, E). Walking time in the rotarod task did not significantly differ between the RIpreC and IR groups (Fig. 2F). The sticky tape reaction task was performed on the right (affected side) and left (unaffected side) limbs. Both groups showed worse latency in the right limb than in the left (Fig. 2G). The affected limb significantly exhibited worse latency in the IR group compared with the control group (p < 0.001, Fig. 2G).

RIpreC Increased the Expression of PGC-1α, FNDC5, BDNF, and Akt Activity in the Ischemic Brain

FNDC5/irisin secretion is promoted by increased PGC-1α expression, and BDNF secretion is associated with PGC-1α and FNDC5/irisin expression in the brain [25, 39]. Therefore, PGC-1α, FNDC5, and BDNF expression levels were examined by immunohistochemistry or western blotting (Fig. 3). PGC-1α and FNDC5 immunoreactivities in the perilesional cortex were significantly decreased by ischemic stroke compared with those in the control group (p < 0.05, Fig. 3A-C). By contrast, perilesional PGC-1α and FNDC5 immunoreactivities were significantly increased in the RIpreC group compared with the IR group (p < 0.05, Fig. 3A-C). Similarly, protein levels of PGC-1α and FNDC5 were significantly increased in the RIpreC group compared with the IR group (p < 0.05, Fig. 3F-H). However, plasma irisin levels were not elevated at 1–24 h after RIpreC compared with the control group (Fig. 3E). Perilesional BDNF immunoreactivity was significantly decreased in the ischemic stroke group compared with the control group (p < 0.01, Fig. 3A, D). However, the RIpreC group showed significantly increased BDNF immunoreactivity surrounding the lesion compared with the IR group (p < 0.001, Fig. 3A, D). In addition, BDNF protein levels followed a trend similar to that of immunostaining (Fig. 3F, I). These results suggest that the increase in BDNF expression induced by RIpreC is associated with elevated PGC-1α and FNDC5 expression in the ischemic brain. FNDC5/irisin may alleviate oxidative stress by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway [40]. In addition, BDNF binds to its receptor, activates intracellular signals, including the PI3K/AKT pathway, and promotes cell survival, differentiation, and proliferation [41]. Therefore, p-Akt and Akt levels were investigated in the ischemic brain (Fig. 3J). p-Akt and Akt levels were significantly lower in the IR group than in the control group (p < 0.05, Fig. 3K, L). However, p-Akt and Akt levels were significantly higher in the RIpreC group than in the IR group (p < 0.05, Fig. 3K, L). The p-Akt/Akt ratio was also significantly lower in the IR group than in the control group (Fig. 3M).

RIpreC Decreased Bax/Bcl-2 Ratios, Caspase-3 Expression, and TUNEL-Positive Neuronal Cells

To investigate the mitochondrial anti-apoptotic pathway after ischemic stroke, we examined the expression of Bcl-2, Bax, and caspase-3 by immunohistochemistry and western blotting (Fig. 4). Neuronal apoptosis was examined using the TUNEL assay and immunofluorescence staining. Bcl-2 immunoreactivity in the RIpreC group was significantly increased in the perilesional cortex compared with the IR group (p < 0.01, Fig. 4A, B). By contrast, Bax immunoreactivity in the perilesional cortex was significantly decreased in the RIpreC group compared with the IR group (p < 0.05, Fig. 4A, C). Caspase-3 immunoreactivity surrounding the lesion was also significantly decreased in the RIpreC group compared with the IR group (p < 0.01, Fig. 4A, D). Similarly, the number of TUNEL-positive neurons surrounding the lesion was significantly decreased in the RIpreC group compared with the IR group (p < 0.01, Fig. 4A, E). At the protein level, Bcl-2 expression increased in the RIpreC group than in the IR group (Fig. 4F, G), but the difference was not statistically significant. Bax protein expression was significantly reduced in the RIpreC group compared with the IR group (p < 0.01, Fig. 4F, H). Consequently, the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio was significantly reduced in the RIpreC group compared with the IR group, suggesting that RIpreC exerted mitochondrial anti-apoptotic activity (p < 0.05, Fig. 4F, I). Caspase-3 expression was also significantly reduced in the RIpreC group compared with the IR group (p < 0.05, Fig. 4F, J). In addition, caspase-3-positive cells co-localized with the neuronal marker NeuN, suggesting neuronal apoptosis surrounding the lesion (Fig. 4K). The ratio of caspase-3/NeuN-positive neurons was significantly reduced in the RIpreC group compared with the IR group, suggesting that RIpreC reduced neuronal apoptosis (p < 0.05, Fig. 4L).

Inhibition of PGC-1α Blunted the Neuroprotective and Neurorestorative Effects of RIpreC

To investigate the PGC-1α-mediated neuroprotective and neurorestorative effects of RIpreC, we administered the PGC-1α inhibitor SR-18,292 before MCAO. Administration of PGC-1α inhibitor blocked the therapeutic effect of RIpreC (Fig. 5). No changes in brain infarctions, neurological scores, or sensorimotor functions were observed in the RIpreC-treated SR-18,292 group compared with the IR group (Fig. 5A-F). Immunohistochemical findings showed that PGC-1α, FNDC5, BDNF, and caspase-3 immunoreactivities surrounding the lesion in the RIpreC-treated SR-18,292 group were similar to those in the IR group (Fig. 5G-K).

Discussion

Our findings showed that RIpreC induced BDNF expression surrounding the lesion through the PGC-1α/FNDC5 pathway after ischemic stroke, thereby exerting neuroprotection and ameliorating neurobehavioral dysfunctions. In addition, the PGC-1α/FNDC5/BDNF axis may alleviate neuronal apoptosis by activating the Akt signaling pathway. These results suggest that BDNF expression via the PGC-1α/FNDC5 pathway may represent a key neuroprotective mechanism of RIpreC therapy.

Irisin is a polypeptide hormone stimulated by exercise that exerts neuroprotective effects in ischemic stroke [20, 39, 42]. In human and experimental studies, acute exercise and cold exposure induce the release of irisin into the blood [43–45]. Irisin, or other factors induced by irisin, may cross the BBB and affect gene expression in the brain [4]. Furthermore, irisin has been detected in cerebrospinal fluid and several brain regions, suggesting the possibility of protein transport into the brain [27]. One potential mechanism of RIpreC is the transmission of humoral factors to the brain, thereby providing neuroprotection against ischemia [2, 46]. However, our results showed that plasma irisin levels were not elevated at 1 h and 24 h after RIpreC. Pang et al. [47] reported that the plasma irisin levels peaked at 6 h after treadmill exercise and decreased to pre-exercise levels by 24 h in mice, suggesting that the timing of blood analysis may have contributed to the detection of elevated plasma irisin concentrations. Therefore, it may be necessary to examine the concentration of circulating irisin in the blood earlier than 24 h after RIpreC. Further studies may be needed to investigate the blood irisin concentrations after RIpreC.

Recently, some potential signaling factors involved in communication between the limbs and brain have been identified, such as biochemical factors, cells, exosomes, and the autonomic nervous system [48, 49]. Further studies are needed to clarify how RIpreC stimulates the release of irisin into the blood and how this hormone is transmitted to the brain. Irisin levels are also affected by a large number of stressors such as brain ischemia [42]. Li et al. [26] reported that plasma irisin levels decreased after stroke, and FNDC5 levels also decreased in skeletal muscle during stroke, suggesting that irisin release from muscle into the blood is inhibited after stroke. Our results indicated that brain PGC-1α and FNDC5 expression decreased after brain ischemia. However, these expressions increased in the ischemic brain following RIpreC. FNDC5 is identified as a PGC-1α-dependent myokine in the brain [22, 23]. PGC-1α is a regulator of neuronal FNDC5 gene expression in the brain [25], suggesting that RIpreC may be increased the expression of irisin in the brain. Therefore, RIpreC-induced neuroprotection may involve not only blood-mediated mechanisms but also different mechanisms.

RIpreC increases CBF and protects mitochondrial activity to reduce oxidative stress, which may be important factors that may contribute to its neuroprotective effects [2]. In addition, protective factor release following RIpreC is associated with humoral and neuronal pathways, such as vagal signaling, in animal models of myocardial infarction [50]. RIpostC, applied after brain ischemia, increased CBF at the end of the intervention and enhanced collateral circulation in the peri-infarct area after MCAO [51]. Our findings showed that CBF gradually increased during the reperfusion phase, unlike peripheral blood flow, suggesting that RIpreC promotes CBF restoration. These results suggest that RIpreC may stimulate the brain through activation of peripheral sensory fibers and increased CBF. Therefore, this study suggests that increased brain PGC-1α/FNDC5 expression by RIpreC may be mediated not only by circulating irisin but also by neuronal factors and CBF.

The PI3K/Akt pathway is activated by various signals containing growth factors and extracellular matrix components, and contributes to the reduction of inflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis [36, 40, 52]. BDNF binds to tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB), activating several intracellular signals, including the PI3K/AKT pathway, and promoting cell survival, differentiation, and proliferation [41]. The mechanisms of action of irisin include the reduction of apoptotic activity through the prevention of mitochondrial fission and fusion, as well as the alleviation of oxidative stress by activating the PI3K/Akt pathway [40]. Irisin also reduces the expression of glucose-induced apoptotic markers such as Bax, Bad, and caspase-3, while increasing anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2 [53]. Our findings showed that RIpreC decreased neuronal apoptosis through the mitochondrial anti-apoptotic pathway, which may be associated with improved infarct volume and sensorimotor function. Microglial PGC1-α, a master coregulator of gene expression in mitochondrial biogenesis, contributes to protection against ischemic brain injury through the suppression of neuroinflammation [54]. The deacetylation of PGC-1α play as foundation for activation of PGC-1α [55], but SR-18,292 inhibits the deacetylation of PGC-1α [56]. Administration of a PGC-1α inhibitor reversed the RIpreC-induced beneficial effects, such as reduced infarct volume and ameliorated sensorimotor functions. These results suggest that RIpreC treatment may provide neuroprotective effects and improve sensorimotor functions by stimulating the PGC-1α/FNDC5/BDNF axis and activating Akt pathways in ischemic stroke. However, administration of PGC-1α inhibitor (SR-18292) could promote BBB permeability, infarct volume, and neurological deficits in the brain ischemic reperfusion model [57]. This study did not examine the effects of PGC-1α inhibitor in normal animals and stroke models without RIpreC intervention. Further studies are needed to clarify the potential non-specific toxic effects of SR-18,292 in normal or ischemic brains.

Preconditioning protects the brain either almost immediately after stimulation or after a delay of 1 to 3 days, which induces protein synthesis-dependent protection [58]. The neuroprotective effect observed in this study may be attributed to these delayed effects, as RIpreC was performed 1 day before brain injury. However, in clinical studies, RIpreC has been applied in a more chronic manner to examine its protective effects against several diseases such as stroke and ischemic vascular dementia [10, 59]. RIpreC treatment also improves cognitive function and enhances motor learning in healthy adults [60, 61]. Therefore, RIpreC has therapeutic applications not only in disease prevention but also in the promotion of brain health in healthy individuals. Both irisin and BDNF play crucial neuroprotective roles in the brain. A preclinical study demonstrated that elevated BDNF-TrkB signaling negatively regulates FNDC5 gene expression, forming a homeostatic FNDC5/BDNF feedback loop [25, 62]. This suggests that irisin and BDNF interact to maintain homeostasis in various organs, including the brain. Sustained upregulation of irisin and BDNF for brain protection may require continuous intervention. Further studies are required to examine the mechanisms and long-term effects of RIpreC interventions in both disease prevention and brain health promotion.

A limitation of this study is the use of young adult animals. Mollet et al. [49] reported that the beneficial effects of RIpreC vary with age in a clinical study. Further studies are required to clarify the impact of age on the effects of RIpreC. This study did not examine the effects of SR-18,292 in the IR group. In addition, we used immunohistochemical analyses to compare the effects of SR-18,292. Further studies may be needed to examine the effectiveness of SR-18,292 using other methods, such as western blotting. Furthermore, long-term functional recovery may better reflect the long-term benefits of neuroprotection. Therefore, long-term observation may be necessary to verify the long-term benefits of neuroprotection after RIpreC. Another limitation is the small sample size in each group, as the number of available animals was limited. Despite these limitations, this study showed that RIpreC treatment may increase BDNF expression surrounding the lesion through the PGC-1α/FNDC5 pathway in the ischemic brain, which could exert neuroprotection and improve neurobehavioral dysfunction after stroke. These findings may provide valuable insights for elucidating the mechanisms of RIpreC in preventing ischemic stroke.

Conclusions

RIpreC is a potential therapy that utilizes endogenous protective mechanisms by applying brief ischemia to a limb to protect target organs such as the brain. This study demonstrated that one of the mechanisms of RIpreC may involve reducing mitochondrial apoptosis via the PGC-1α/FNDC5/BDNF axis and activating the Akt pathway after cerebral ischemia, which may contribute to neuroprotection and the amelioration of neurobehavioral dysfunction after stroke.
