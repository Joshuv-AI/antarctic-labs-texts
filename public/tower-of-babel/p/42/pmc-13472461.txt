Rubia cordifolia as an antimicrobial agent against periodontal pathogens – An in vitro microbiological study
Authors: Swapna Shivasharan Gore, Pranjali Girish Mandurkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Extensive research on the antimicrobial activity of medicinal plants for periodontal therapy has been conducted over the past decades. The gradual increase in antibiotic resistance necessitates research for new antimicrobial compounds. Rubia cordifolia has exhibited reassuring antimicrobial potential against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. However, no study has yet evaluated its therapeutic potential in periodontal diseases. Therefore, we aimed to design, formulate, and evaluate the antimicrobial activity of R. cordifolia root extract against periodontal pathogens.Materials and Methods:Extract of the roots of R. cordifolia was prepared using cold maceration and methanol extraction. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), and time-kill curve (TKC) analysis of R. cordifolia extract were assessed against Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg), Tannerella forsythia, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Fusobacterium nucleatum (Fn), and Prevotella intermedia, where chlorhexidine was utilized as a control drug.Results:The MIC value for Fn was 0.8 μg/mL, representing the lowest value observed, whereas Pg exhibited the highest MIC value at 6.25 μg/mL. The MBC values ranged from 6.25 μg/mL to 12.5 μg/mL. The TKC revealed a kill time of 2 h for all tested organisms except Pg.Conclusion:The methanolic extract derived from the roots of R. cordifolia demonstrated bactericidal effects against keystone pathogens associated with periodontitis. Consequently, there is a critical requirement of additional clinical studies to assess its potential therapeutic application in the treatment of periodontal disease.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontitis is one of the most prevalent pathologies affecting the oral cavity and constitutes a crucial public health concern.[1] A complex interplay between the subgingival biofilm and the host immune-inflammatory events within the gingival and periodontal tissues leads to periodontal diseases. The breakdown of the periodontal ligament fibers, resulting in clinical attachment loss, together with alveolar bone resorption and eventual tooth loss, is the consequence of the inflammatory response of the host tissue against the bacterial load.[23] More than 700 distinct species of bacteria inhabit the oral cavity and are responsible for various oral diseases.[4] A large number of bacterial species are identifiable in the periodontal pocket, but the red-complex bacteria are most commonly associated with periodontitis. Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (Aa), Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg), Fusobacterium nucleatum (Fn), Prevotella intermedia (Pi), and Tannerella forsythia (Tf) are considered to be the main causative agents.[56]

The withdrawal of dental plaque, as well as the plaque-retentive factors, is the main purpose of periodontal therapy. Nonsurgical periodontal therapy removes plaque and calculus on the diseased tooth in order to heal the tissue and reduce the probing pocket depth. Scaling and root planing (SRP) restores diseased tissues to a healthy condition. However, even after SRP, some bacterial species persist in the treated sites.[7] Hence, diffusion of antibiotics into biofilms smoothens the way for penetration and destruction of the biofilm and eventually destroys bacteria.[8] This is done as an adjunct to nonsurgical therapy, which includes the removal of bacterial biofilm, calculus, and toxins from the periodontally involved root surfaces. The primary goal is to control the periodontal infection.[9]

The patient outcomes have been severely affected by escalating numbers of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, which in turn leads to higher universal mortality rates. With the gradual rise in antibiotic resistance, Phyto drugs are assumed to play a significant role as a possible alternative therapy in dentistry.[10] There is growing interest among researchers in medicinal plants with inherent antimicrobial efficacy. Therefore, research on new antimicrobial compounds derived from herbal products seems to be the highest priority.[1112131415]

The family Rubiaceae contains around 70 species of plants, out of which Rubia cordifolia belongs to the genus Rubia. R. cordifolia, popularly known as “Manjistha,” is native to the tropical regions of India and China. In Sanskrit, Kannada, Marathi, and Bengali, it is referred to as “manjistha,” while in Hindi and Gujarati, it is known as “majith.” In Telugu, it goes by “tamaralli,” and in Tamil, it is called “manditti.”[16]

It is a traditional medicinal plant in Indian and Chinese medicine and was listed in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia 6 in 2015. Tropical Australia and America, Western and Northern Europe, the Mediterranean, and a few parts of Asia and Africa are the hubs for R. cordifolia.[17]

R. cordifolia is a perennial plant with numerous medicinal properties. Root extracts are used as anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, antiseptic, antipyretic, anthelminthic, anticancer, antiacne, anti-arthritic, antihyperglycemic, anticonvulsant, antioxidant, anti-stress, nootropic, wound healing, and ulcer healing agents, among others. Due to its wide range of pharmacological properties, the root extract of R. cordifolia is utilized to treat conditions such as arthralgia, arthritis, cough, diabetes, intermittent fever, slow healing of broken bones, and tubercular conditions of the skin and mucous tissues.[161718]

In addition, it has been proven to increase osteoblastic activity in diseases like osteoporosis and periodontitis.[19] The scanty research in the field of periodontology on the effects of R. cordifolia extract on the management of periodontal diseases makes it mandatory to evaluate its antimicrobial activity against periodontal pathogens.

As R. cordifolia is available at a low cost and possesses antimicrobial and antioxidant effects, this study was designed to assess the antimicrobial effect of R. cordifolia root extract against periodontal pathogens.

Several studies highlighted the promising potential of R. cordifolia against a few Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.[20] To date, no studies have evaluated the role of R. cordifolia against periodontal pathogens. Therefore, we aimed to analyze the antimicrobial activity of R. cordifolia root extract against Aa, Pg, Fn, Pi, and Tf.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The Institutional Ethics Committee issued ethical clearance for the conduct of this in vitro study. The present study was conducted with the intent to assess the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC), and time-kill curve (TKC) analysis of extracts of R. cordifolia root against Pg, Fn, Aa, Pi, and Tf, keeping Chlorhexidine (CHX) as a control.

Naturally collected roots of R. cordifolia were procured from a commercial vendor, and the product was free of extraneous matter.

The extract powder preparation involved R. cordifolia roots air-drying and grinding. A methanol extract was prepared by macerating the ground roots in methanol (1:30) and heating to 50°C for 24 h on a rotary shaker. Then, the filtration of the solution was carried out through Whatman No. 1 filter paper using a suction pump, followed by centrifugation for 10 min at 6000 rpm. A rotavapor was utilized to evaporate the supernatant of the final methanolic extract. The dry crude extract was then collected and stored in an airtight bottle at −20°C until use [Figure 1].[21]

Preparation of Rubia cordifolia extract (a) Air-dried roots; (b) Ground powder; (c) Powder maceration; (d) Filtration with Whatman no. 1 paper using a suction pump; (e) Final methanolic extract

The prepared methanolic extract was then used for microbial culture. The microbial variants cultured for this antimicrobial activity were Pg (ATCC 33277), Fn (ATCC 25586), Tf ATCC 43037D-5), Pi (ATCC 25611D-5), and Aa (ATCC 43718) (ATCC: American type culture collection, USA).

The MIC assay was carried out to assess the bacteriostatic activity of the test agents, defined as the lowest concentration showing no visible microbial growth after incubation. Nine serial dilutions of the R. cordifolia extract and 0.2% CHX were prepared in brain–heart infusion broth. An initial mixture of 20 μL extract with 380 μL broth (50 μL/mL) was prepared, followed by successive twofold dilutions (25 → 12.5 → 6.25 → 3.125 → 1.56 → 0.78 → 0.39 → 0.195 μL/mL). To each dilution, 200 μL of bacterial suspension containing Pg, Fn, Pi, Tf, or Aa was added. The tubes were incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 48 h, and turbidity was compared with a positive control (broth alone) to determine the MIC value for each organism [Figure 2].[22]

Serial dilution method to detect minimum inhibitory concentration

Following the MIC test, the MBC assay was conducted to evaluate the bactericidal potential of the extract. Serial dilutions (50–0.195 μL/mL) were inoculated onto brain–heart infusion agar plates divided into sections for each concentration. The plates were incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 24 h, and colony formation was examined microscopically. The lowest concentration showing no visible bacterial growth was recorded as the MBC value of the test agent.[22]

The TKC assay was conducted to evaluate the rate of bacterial killing by the R. cordifolia extract compared with 0.2% CHX. Equal volumes of brain–heart infusion broth and the test agent were combined to obtain final concentrations of 25 μL/mL for the extract and 0.1% for CHX. Each tube was inoculated with 0.5 μL of bacterial suspension and incubated in a CO2 jar at 35°C. Aliquots were withdrawn at 5, 10, 30 min, and 2 h, plated on agar, and incubated for 48–72 h to determine viable counts (CFU/mL). A ≥3 log10 reduction in CFU/mL indicated bactericidal activity, while a lower reduction denoted bacteriostatic action. The kinetic curve of CFU/mL over time illustrated the antimicrobial efficacy of the extract.[23]

RESULTS

The results showed that Pg, Fn, Tf, Pi, and Aa showed prominent sensitivity to pure root extract with MIC parameters of 12.5 μL/mL, 0.8 μL/mL, 6.25 μL/mL, 1.6 μL/mL, and 6.25 μL/mL, correspondingly [Table 1 and Figures 3 and 4], MBC parameters of 12.5 μL/mL, 12.5 μL/mL, 12.5 μL/mL, 6.25 μL/mL, and 12.5 μL/mL, respectively [Table 2 and Figures 5 and 6], and TKC values were 2 h for all organisms except Pg [Table 3 and Figure 7].

DISCUSSION

Periodontal disease results from the host’s response to a procession of microbial complexes that destroy the periodontium.[24] Mechanical periodontal therapy, followed by chemical plaque control as an anti-infective therapy, is used to treat periodontal disease in the etiologic phase.[25] Various herbal derivatives have been used not only for prevention but also for treating oral infections in recent times. Research has depicted that herbal products have upgraded periodontal clinical parameters such as inflammation, bleeding on provocation, and clinical attachment.[26]

R. cordifolia demonstrates numerous pharmacological actions such as anti-inflammatory, antitumor, antioxidant, antidiabetic activity, and wound healing activity.[27] Methanolic extracts of R. cordifolia exhibited activity against Gram-positive bacteria in a concentration-dependent manner, with many being highly susceptible to inhibition. Some are significantly inhibited by penicillin G.[20] The study demonstrates the excellent antibacterial ability of R. cordifolia in methanolic and water extracts.[28] As there is an increasing demand for effective antibacterial products against drug-resistant microorganisms,[29] the current study was conducted to evaluate the MIC, MBC, and time-kill analysis curve of the methanolic root extract of R. cordifolia against Pg, Aa, Pi, Tf, and Fn.

Over 700 bacterial species are present in subgingival plaque. Several studies have reported that Pg, Aa, Pi, Tf, and Fn are the primary pathological agents in the progression of periodontal disease and are aptly termed keystone pathogens.[3031] The first objective of our study was to evaluate the MIC against Pg, Aa, Pi, Tf, and Fn, followed by the determination of MBC and TKC. This study is the first to assess the antibacterial effectiveness of the methanolic root extract of R. cordifolia against Pg, Aa, Pi, Tf, and Fn.

The methanolic crude root extract was prepared from air-dried roots using a chain of procedures.[21] MIC values were determined using the serial dilution method. To ascertain MBC values against Pg, Aa, Fn, Tf, and Pi, all serially diluted tubes were incubated in anaerobic media for 24 h, followed by colony counting.[22] Finally, TKC was measured for the aforementioned organisms.[23] CHX was used as the control drug throughout the experiments.

The TKC assay was performed to validate the antibacterial activity of R. cordifolia observed in the MIC and MBC tests. It provided a dynamic assessment of bacterial killing over time, confirming the extract’s bactericidal nature through a ≥3-log10 CFU/mL reduction within 2 h for all pathogens except Pg.[23] The kill pattern closely corresponded with MIC and MBC values, ensuring result consistency. CHX (0.1%) served as a positive control and verified assay accuracy. Similar time-dependent antibacterial activity of R. cordifolia has been reported by Li et al., supporting the present findings.[32]

The results showed that the MIC was lowest for Fn (0.8 μg/mL) and highest for Pg (6.25 μg/mL). MBC values were consistent across all organisms (12.5 μg/mL) except for Pi, which was 6.25 μg/mL. TKC values indicated a time kill of 2 h for all organisms except Pg.

The longer TKC duration observed for Pg may be attributed to its structural and enzymatic defense mechanisms that enhance antimicrobial resistance. Its lipid-rich outer membrane and capsular polysaccharides limit the penetration of phytochemicals, while proteolytic enzymes and antioxidant systems neutralize reactive oxygen species generated by R. cordifolia constituents. These features collectively delay bacterial lysis, resulting in a prolonged kill time compared with other pathogens.[3233]

Li et al. conducted an in vitro study in 2019 against specific Gram-positive and Gram-negative foodborne pathogens, with gentamycin used as a control, utilizing essential oil derived from the roots of R. cordifolia. In their study, the MIC values were superior to those in the current study, whereas the MBC values were similar to those observed in our study.[32]

The antimicrobial efficacy observed in the present study may be attributed to the presence of bioactive phytoconstituents such as anthraquinones and naphthoquinones in R. cordifolia, which have been reported by Li et al. to exert antibacterial effects through disruption of bacterial cell walls, interference with metabolic enzymes, and induction of oxidative stress via reactive oxygen species generation.[32]

The MIC, MBC, and TKC values obtained in the current study for Pg and Aa differed from those reported in the in vitro study by Shetty et al. in 2013, which utilized aqueous and ethanolic garlic extracts.[33]

The antibacterial effectiveness of the methanolic root extract of R. cordifolia demonstrated comparable results to the ethanolic extracts of Sphaeranthus indicus tested against periodontal pathogens such as Pg, Fn, Tf, and Aa in an in vitro study by Gawade et al.[34]

The MIC and TKC values achieved in the present study were more effective than those reported by Souza et al. for Pg using Copaifera langsdorffii Oleoresin extract.[35]

Khot et al. evaluated the antimicrobial activity of aqueous and ethanolic extracts of Ocimum basilicum against periodontal pathogens and reported findings comparable to the results obtained in the present study.[36]

In conclusion, periodontitis is a multifactorial disease, causing destruction of the periodontium by the host immune response. Mechanical debridement has long been considered the treatment of choice.[3738] CHX is contemplated as the gold standard and is universally used for chemical plaque control. However, its long-term use is associated with various adverse effects. Therefore, there is a growing need to develop alternatives that offer high efficacy with minimal adverse effects.[3940] Herbal medicine is progressively gaining attention among researchers for investigating the therapeutic potential of plants.[41]

The current in vitro study provides pilot evidence of the antibacterial effectiveness of the methanolic root extract of R. cordifolia. Periodontitis is a complex condition associated with numerous pathogenic bacteria, yet only five bacteria were evaluated in this study, which may be considered a limitation. Nevertheless, the study focused on the most predominant periodontal microorganisms. Future clinical trials are needed to establish the therapeutic benefits of R. cordifolia methanolic root extract in treating periodontal disease.

CONCLUSION

In the present in vitro study, the methanolic extract of R. cordifolia has demonstrated effective antimicrobial activity against periodontal pathogens (Pg, Aa, Fn, Pi, and Tf). Considering the MIC, MBC, and TKC values, the methanolic extract of R. cordifolia could potentially serve as a supervised adjunct to mechanical periodontal therapy, given its safety and cost-effectiveness. Therefore, further research is validated to verify the in vivo efficacy of R. cordifolia as a therapeutic agent for preventing periodontal diseases.
