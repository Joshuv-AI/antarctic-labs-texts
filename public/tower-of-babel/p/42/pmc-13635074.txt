Evaluation of targeted Massively Parallel Sequencing methods for forensic metagenomics
Authors: Kamila Marszałek, Michał B. Kowalski, Agata Jagiełło, Anna Woźniak, Kinga Herda, Rafał Płoski, Andrzej Ossowski, Manuela Oliveira, Renata Zbieć-Piekarska, Paweł P. Łabaj, Wojciech Branicki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMassively Parallel Sequencing (MPS) is effective for monitoring the microbial composition of environmental samples. Soil microbial signatures are critical for pinpointing the geographic location of forensic evidence, but standard 16S rRNA methods lack species-level resolution. Targeted sequencing panels, consisting of informative DNA fragments, can overcome this shortcoming and are highly desirable for forensic investigations. To address this, we evaluated three target enrichment methods for metagenomic analysis. First, we used Whole Metagenome Sequencing (WMS) data from 134 soil samples across 46 locations in Poland to extract a set of 200 markers. Using these markers, we created prototype targeted sequencing panels to compare two amplicon capture-based methods (Thermo Fisher AmpliSeq™ and Integrated DNA Technologies xGen™) and one hybridization capture-based method (Roche KAPA HyperPlus). The comparison of the technologies was guided by the results of classification of sample origin by machine learning classifier trained on feature profiles from WMS. The methods were assessed on technical parameters including data quality, reproducibility, sensitivity, and practical implementation for forensic laboratories. The performance and precision varied depending on technology and DNA concentration. The Roche KAPA HyperPlus hybridization capture-based method consistently demonstrated superior performance. Across various DNA input quantities, it showed the highest correlation with WMS data and achieved an exceptional F1 score of 0.94 at 5 ng, significantly outperforming the amplicon-based methods. This indicates that hybridization capture is a more robust and accurate approach for forensic soil microbiome profiling, particularly for low-template evidence, providing a highly reliable tool for predicting geographic origin.Key points • Targeted Massively Parallel Sequencing methods for forensic soil microbial analysis • Targeted sequencing allowed the determination of the place of origin of soil samples • Roche KAPA HyperPlus: the most accurate classification of the soil samples origin. Introduction

Microbial forensics has emerged as a powerful discipline that leverages the analysis of microbial communities to provide valuable intelligence in criminal investigations (Arenas et al. 2017). The recent advent of Massively Parallel Sequencing (MPS) has expanded the field's applications, which now include determining the cause and time of death (Gouello et al. 2021; Metcalf 2019), identifying individuals from trace biological evidence (Bell et al. 2018; Javan et al. 2016; Meadow et al. 2015; Sherier et al. 2022) and attributing samples to a source in cases of biocrime or bioterrorism (Clarke et al. 2017; Metcalf et al. 2017; Oliveira et al. 2024; Wilkins et al. 2017).The diverse applications and the underlying technological advancements in the topics of microbial forensics were extensively covered and discussed in the literature, for example in the works of (Oliveira et al. 2024; Oliveira and Amorim 2018). Among these applications, the analysis of the environmental microbiome to determine the geographic origin of forensic evidence allows to link a suspect or an object to a specific location, such as a crime scene.

The field of forensic microbiology and especially the potential of the environmental microbiome gained significant traction and recognition as a subject of interest for criminal investigations in the early 2000 s, primarily in response to the anthrax attacks in the United States (Keim et al. 2011). Since then, it has been argued that the microbiome present in environmental samples from sources such as water, air, soil or various surfaces provides valuable information about the crime scene and narrows down the area of the perpetrator search (Fitzpatrick and Donnelly 2021; Young and Linacre 2021). It is worth noting that microbial communities have different compositions and functions across geographical locations (Clarke et al. 2017). One of the most significant initiatives aimed at characterizing microbial diversity and functions across various ecosystems is the Earth Microbiome Project (EMP) (Thompson et al. 2017). In this project, microbial DNA sequences obtained from 40 different biomes were analyzed. During the initial analysis of 10 000 samples, nearly 6 million bacterial taxonomic units were identified showing microbial richness, useful for predicting soil samples origin (Gilbert et al. 2014). In a MetaSUB consortium study almost 5000 environmental samples from 60 cities around the world were analyzed demonstrating that distinct microbial signatures are driven by factors such as elevation, coastal proximity, climate among others allowing for geographical differentiation based on the detected microbial profiles (Danko et al. 2021). Based on those data identification of continent-specific, and city-specific microbial fingerprints was possible (Bhattacharya et al. 2022). These fundamental studies increased the interest of forensic science in the environmental microbiome as a potential source of information in criminal investigations (Kumari et al. 2022). One potential source of microbiome enabling forensic DNA intelligence is the soil microbiome.

Traditional forensic soil analysis, which relies on physical and chemical characteristics (e.g. color, mineralogy, and elemental composition) lacks the resolution needed to distinguish samples, particularly when they come from geologically homogenous areas or when only trace amounts of material are available (Allwood et al. 2020; Bhattacharya et al. 2022; Gouello et al. 2021; Guo et al. 2022; Trevors 2010). Several methods have been used to analyze the microbial composition of these complex samples, but traditional forensic techniques have been limited by sensitivity, specificity and efficiency (Fitzpatrick 2004). Metagenomic analysis of the highly site-specific microbial communities within soil offers a high-resolution alternative (Schmedes and Budowle 2019). However, current metagenomic strategies present a practical dilemma for forensic laboratories. The more cost-effective method of targeted sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene is often limited by its inability to resolve closely related taxa and its reliance on the completeness of reference databases, which may not contain the uncultured, location-specific microorganisms that act as the most reliable forensic markers (Delgado-Baquerizo et al. 2018; Dixit et al. 2021). Using 16S rRNA for the sample's origin prediction allows only for the differentiation between continents, so the resolution of the prediction accuracy is low (Walker and Datta 2019). WMS provides the necessary species- and strain-level resolution to differentiate closely related locations but is often prohibitively expensive and computationally intensive for routine casework (Chaudhari et al. 2023). Hence the rising popularity of the targeted sequencing methods which generate data only from areas of interest, requiring less resources for computing and storage (Mertes et al. 2011). Within targeted sequencing PCR amplicon and hybridization capture-based sequencing can be distinguished. While PCR amplicon capture is used to target specific genes or regions by amplifying them using specifically designed primers, the hybridization capture method enables sequencing with the selectively enriched abundance of target genes, regions or taxonomic groups of interest within the microbial community. Customized capture probes are specifically designed to bind to the target's sequences of interest. Hybridization with highly specific probes reduces off-target sequencing. Detailed comparison between these two targeted sequencing methods can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

To address the limitations of reference-based marker selection, the deBruijn graph-based framework for sequence search and discovery—MetaGraph (Karasikov et al. 2020)—was used to extract high-fidelity contigs (assembled from k-mers), which were quantified using kallisto and underwent a process of selection of 200 most informative features, using the Boruta algorithm (Kursa and Rudnicki 2010). K-mers are nucleotides of length k (in the SMAFT project, we chose k equal to 31 as it was recommended by the authors of the MetaGraph framework). They represent unique genomic signatures for differentiating microorganisms and identifying location-specific signals, even from unclassified organisms. This topic is extensively described in (Moeckel et al. 2024). Implementation of the k-mer-based methods of data analysis allows for utilization of location-specific microorganisms even without the necessity of using extensive databases that are typically needed for taxonomic or functional classification (Kawulok et al. 2019). Implementation of k-mer-based method allows to extract location-specific sequences from both known and unknown microorganisms, increasing the accuracy of the prediction of the origin of the tested soil samples without the need to refer to reference databases. Those sequences can be then used to design targets, on which the probes and primers for the targeted sequencing technology are based.

The primary aim of this study was to leverage these markers to design a prototype targeted MPS panel and to conduct a rigorous, parallel technical evaluation of three distinct target enrichment sequencing methods for its implementation. Two PCR amplicon-based methods—Thermo Fisher Scientific Ion AmpliSeq™ (TFS) and Integrated DNA Technologies xGen™ Amplicon (IDT) and one hybridization capture-based method (Roche KAPA HyperPlus) were assessed based on key forensic validation metrics, including on-target efficiency, data quality, sensitivity across a range of DNA inputs (1–100 ng), and reproducibility. By comparing their performance with the original WMS data, this work aims to identify the most robust and accurate method for forensic soil microbiome analysis, thereby providing a validated workflow for the geographic prediction of soil evidence. Furthermore, the practical considerations and challenges associated with the implementation of the best working hybridization capture-based Roche KAPA HyperPlus (Roche) method in forensic laboratories were explored, emphasizing its potential impact on the field of forensic microbiome analysis.

Material and methods

Collection points design

The test area of this study was Poland, which due to its compact, relatively regular shape, characterized mainly by flat terrain and relatively low climatic differentiation makes an interesting use case based on features identified as relevant in (Danko et al. 2021). Places for sample collection were selected based on climatic, meteorological, and geographic data obtained from the OpenWeatherMap, over the last 20 years. Based on the above-mentioned data, 12 collection areas were determined, which represent all types of environments excluding the edges of the environments (field edges, roadside ditches, molehills). At each collection area, specific collection points were determined, resulting in the designation of 80 collection points across Poland (Fig. 1A). At each collection point, four sampling points (three main and one reserve point) have been set within five meters of the designated collection point. At each point the metadata was collected using KoBoToolbox software (https://www.kobotoolbox.org/) with custom, pre-made questionnaire template. The metadata was not used in this technical validation of the MPS methods, but was necessary for the collection points design in the whole study. The selection of diverse testing sites to challenge the targeted sequencing methods under varying environmental context was done with random sampling method on sampling areas and collection areas level. Soil samples collection

Samples were collected from the organic layer of soil (approximate depth: 5 cm) at the designated collection points by screwing the sterile tubes into the soil, which was deprived of plants, grass, leaves lying on the surface, using a cleaned spade. If the soil was hard or compacted, it was crushed using a disposable, sterile plastic spatula. Soil samples were placed into 1,5 ml Eppendorf™ tubes, and immediately frozen at −20ºC; after being delivered to the designated laboratory, DNA from the main 1,5 ml tubes with soil was immediately extracted. For the purpose of the evaluation of the Massively Parallel Sequencing methods, we have used soil collected at six representative collection points.

DNA extraction and quantification

DNA was extracted according to the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro (Qiagen) protocol using 250 mg of soil. 10 µl of spike-in control I High Microbial Load D6320 (ZymoBIOMICS) was added to each sample. Homogenization of the material was performed using Vortex Genie II (Scientific Industries) with a Vortex Adapter for 24 1.5–2.0 ml tubes (Qiagen) for 10–20 min depending on the number of samples homogenized at once. Extracted DNA was quantified using NanoDrop™ 8000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Isolates were frozen in −80º C. For each batch of DNA isolation performed, positive control in the form of Microbial Community Standard D6300 (ZymoBIOMICS) and a negative control in the form of a water sample were also isolated.

Whole Metagenome Sequencing

WMS was performed in two parts. The first part was a test sequencing on Illumina MiSeq FGx. After determining the correctness of the obtained data, the second, main sequencing was performed on Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Shallow sequencing assesses DNA quality, quantity, and library preparation success, identifying issues like contamination or degradation that could affect deeper sequencing results. Before library preparation, the concentration of the DNA in tested samples was remeasured by Qubit™ dsDNA Broad Range Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and, for samples with lower DNA concentrations, with Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Libraries for technical WMS were prepared with Illumina Library Prep Kit along with Illumina DNA/RNA UD Indexes Sets A, B, C, D following the user guide provided by the manufacturer. The use of the Illumina Library Prep Kit allowed for the preparation of libraries from the tested soil microbiome isolates. DNA concentration in all prepared libraries was measured with a Qubit™ Flex fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and a Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Additionally, using the Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent) and the High Sensitivity DNA Kit (Agilent) and DNA 1000 Kit (Agilent) reagents, the size and fragment distribution in the prepared DNA libraries was determined. The library construction procedure was optimized by adjusting the number of PCR cycles to the amount of DNA present in each reaction according to the manufacturer's recommendations. The selected sets of Illumina DNA/RNA UD Indexes with 384 unique double indexes enabled the multiplexing of more than one hundred samples in one sequencing, which reduced the cost of this step. Library pools were normalized to a concentration of 4 nM and sequenced in 2 × 151 bp technical runs on an Illumina MiSeq FGx instrument (Illumina) using the Illumina Reagent Kit V2 300–cycles, in the presence of 5% PhiX. The results of each technical sequencing were analyzed with the SAV Illumina software. The presence of samples in the pool, the level of readings, the presence of indexes, and the number of duplicates obtained for each library were checked. The main sequencing was carried out using the NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina) in the standard operating mode using NovaSeq 6000 S4 Reagent Kit v1.5 300–cycles (Illumina) with reading the sequence of double indexes (10 bp per index). Raw data from sequencing was demultiplexed using the bcl2fastq program, version v2.20.0.422. Demultiplexing was performed with no tolerance for index errors.

Custom DNA test panel design

The WMS FASTQ files were processed with MetaGraph (0.1.0b) to construct massive deBruijn graph, consisting of nodes coming from all samples and all locations sampled. The high-fidelity contigs were extracted from the deBruijn graphs using basic assembly method implemented in the framework. The contigs have been quantified with the use of kallisto (0.46.0) program for quantification by pseudoalignment. The obtained counts were by default normalized with TPM normalization technique, which is also the technique of choice for the extracted high-fidelity contigs as it avoids bias by oligonucleotide length. The set of 200 most informative features was engineered with the use of the Boruta feature selection algorithm (Kursa and Rudnicki 2010). Implemented as part of the Python scikit-learn-contrib ecosystem as boruta-py (0.3). The lengths of targets used in the final panel varied from 230 to 934 bp. For each targeted MPS method chosen for validation, a corresponding custom test panel was designed. The test panels were based on 200 selected targets, with a total size of 64,626 bp (Supplementary File F1). The main criteria considered while designing panels for the purpose of this research were that panels must be small enough to accommodate the use of medium-throughput devices that are available in forensic laboratories and must show high sensitivity demonstrated by developmental validation for forensic purposes while using less than or equal to 100 ng of DNA as an input.

Validation of the targeted MPS methods

To select the optimal method of targeted MPS analysis, three high-throughput DNA analysis methods were tested, enabling qualitative and quantitative analysis of the soil microbiome composition. PCR-based target enrichment methods included TFS and IDT as well as hybridization capture-based target enrichment method Roche. For each method, analyses of samples containing different quantities of DNA were performed. The methods were tested using DNA isolated from soil originating from six collection points (marked as 1.1—6.3 in Supplementary Table S2 and S3) and from Microbial Community Standard D6300 (ZymoBIOMICS) (named as 7.1–7.3 in Supplementary Table S2 and S3) as a positive control. In case of TFS and Roche six, and in the case of IDT five, different amounts of DNA were used as an input for each evaluated sample to determine the best sensitivity and specificity of the method. For each DNA input tested, a different number of PCR amplification cycles was used (Table 1). Libraries were prepared in triplicate. For each of the tested methods, the parameters were assessed: compliance of the obtained results of the microbiome composition analysis between the WMS data and the data obtained with the tested targeted method, the quality of the data received, sensitivity of the tested methods, repeatability of the obtained results, simplicity and economy of method application (Table 2). Roche KAPA HyperPlus

Using the hyperdesign.com tool, a custom DNA panel targeting selected regions was designed, comprising 255 probes with a coverage rate of 99.26%. One hundred and twenty-six libraries (21 libraries per each DNA input amount) were prepared using the KAPA HyperPlus Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions and considering optimization of the procedure by adjusting the Pre-Capture PCR amplification cycles to the user’s guide (Table 1). Before the hybridization step, the libraries were combined into 12-plexes. Libraries were quantified by Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Bioanalyzer™ HS DNA Assay Kit (Agilent). Final libraries at a concentration of 12 pM were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq sequencer using the MiSeq Reagent Kit V3 600-cycles (Illumina) with the reference PhiX library concentration of 20 pM to obtain 1 million reads per sample.

Thermo Fisher Scientific Ion AmpliSeq™

Ion AmpliSeq™ On-Demand Panel was designed and ordered at AmpliSeq.com. It consisted of one DNA primer pool with 253 amplicons, whose sizes varied from 125 to 275 bp with 96.37% coverage. One hundred and twenty-six libraries (21 libraries per each DNA input amount) were prepared manually using Ion AmpliSeq™ Library Kit Plus along with Ion Xpress™ Barcode Adapters 1–96 Kit following user guide provided by the manufacturer. The library construction procedure was optimized by adjusting the number of PCR cycles to the amount of DNA present in each reaction according to the manufacturer's recommendations (Table 1). Libraries were quantified by Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Bioanalyzer™ HS DNA Assay Kit (Agilent), then library concentrations were normalized to 100 pM. Ion 540™ Kit was used for template preparation and chip loading was carried out by Ion Chef™ System; samples were sequenced on Ion GeneStudio™ S5 System with run flows set on 650. The libraries were sequenced in a sufficient quantity to yield a total of 1 million reads per sample.

Integrated DNA Technologies xGen™ Amplicon

A custom-designed DNA panel was ordered through customer service; it contained one DNA primer pool with 590 amplicons, whose size varied from 101 to 150 bp with 80.43% coverage. One hundred and five libraries (21 libraries per each DNA input amount) were manually prepared using xGen™ Amplicon Core Kit. The library construction procedure was optimized by adjusting the number of PCR cycles to the amount of DNA present in each reaction, following the user's guide (Table 1). Libraries were quantified by Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Bioanalyzer™ HS DNA Assay Kit (Agilent). The supplied SNAP reagents were used to enzymatically normalize the pooled libraries to a concentration of 4 nM. The final pools were quantified by Qubit™ ssDNA Assay Kit. Sequencing was carried out on Illumina MiSeq sequencer using the MiSeq Reagent Kit V2 300–cycles (Illumina) without the reference PhiX library in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations; final libraries pool concentration on flow cell was 20 pM. The libraries were sequenced in a quantity sufficient to obtain 1 million reads per sample.

Statistical analysis

To compare the efficiency and accuracy of the three sequencing methods in recovering target sequences, the on-target rate was calculated from the quantification reports. Since the comparison involves three distinct data distributions from different sources, metrics capable of handling data sparsity and other distribution properties were selected, specifically, the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was used to account for data sparsity, cosine similarity to compare metagenomic profiles as vectors (including zero values), and the coefficient of determination (R2) to assess the correlation between variables. To evaluate the specificity and sensitivity of each technology and input, the F1 score, defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, was calculated. This makes it especially useful when dealing with imbalanced datasets, where one class is significantly underrepresented, as it considers both false positives and false negatives. Additionally, to visually represent differences in the quantified microbial profiles obtained with each method relative to WMS, heatmaps of Transcripts Per Million (TPM) values were generated, comparing targeted sequencing samples to the original WMS samples.

Results

Custom panels were designed as detailed in the Materials and Methods section and synthesized by specialists from Roche, Thermo Fisher Scientific and Integrated DNA Technologies. The targets coverage differed between the tested methods (Table 2), with the amplicon-based solutions having the lowest coverage in this benchmark. Libraries were successfully prepared, and the sequencing was conducted using two different MPS methods, i.e. IonTorrent and Illumina. In Supplementary Table S2 the initial DNA amount used for the library preparation and sample purity based on absorbance, while in Supplementary Table S3 the final library concentration for each tested soil sample in each tested method is presented. Supplementary Table S2 shows that the initial DNA concentrations extracted from the six soil samples were generally high, ranging from 198.5 ng/µl to 367.03 ng/µl, while the positive control sample had a concentration of 57.84 ng/µl. The A260/280 ratios were consistently between 1.87 and 1.95, indicating high purity with minimal protein contamination. The A230/260 ratios, however, showed significant variability (ranging from 0.37 to 2.07), which is characteristic of soil-derived DNA and suggests varying levels of residual contaminants like humic acids. As detailed in Supplementary Table S3, the final library concentrations varied considerably across the three target enrichment methods. The Roche method consistently yielded the highest library concentrations across all DNA inputs, often producing yields well above 35 ng/µl. In contrast, the TFS method resulted in significantly lower concentrations, typically in the range of 2–15 ng/µl. The IDT method produced intermediate library yields, generally between 20 and 40 ng/µl.

Comparative analysis of the data acquisition methods was used to determine the most efficient method for the soil sample origin classification (see Statistical analysis section). This evaluation was performed on a set of DNA inputs (1 ng, 5 ng, 10 ng and 25 ng), to determine the best-performing method.

The on-target mapping values analysis (Fig. 2) highlights significant differences in the efficiency of the targeted sequencing methods. The Roche method consistently achieved the highest mapping values across all DNA input amounts, ranging from approximately 70% to 90%, indicating strong target specificity. The TFS and IDT methods showed substantially lower mapping values (generally below 20%) and greater variability across different DNA inputs. Therefore, the Roche hybridization capture-based method demonstrated superior performance over the tested amplicon-based methods, TFS and IDT, across all evaluated metrics. It exhibited the highest reproducibility, sensitivity, and accuracy in predicting the geographic origin of soil samples, achieving an F1 score of 0.94 with just 5 ng of DNA input. This method also showed the greatest similarity to WMS data across all comparative analyses, including Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, Cosine similarity, and R2 metrics. Specifically, Roche achieved the lowest dissimilarity values (approximately 0.1–0.2), the highest cosine similarity scores (close to 1), and R2 values ranging from 0.87 to 0.98, reflecting its robust and consistent performance across various DNA input levels.

Additionally, the Roche method excelled in on-target mapping efficiency, maintaining percentages between 70 and 90% across all DNA inputs, significantly outperforming the TFS and IDT methods, which generally exhibited mapping efficiencies below 20%. Heatmap analyses of TPM values further underscored Roche’s alignment with WMS profiles, displaying the most consistent feature representation. In contrast, TFS and IDT showed greater deviations and underrepresentation of metagenomic features. The Roche method emerged as the most effective for soil microbiome analysis, and its robust methodology suggests promising applicability in diverse geographic regions, potentially offering even higher discriminatory power in forensic contexts.

Taxonomical classification of the selected markers has been performed with megablast, using the entire NCBI Nucleotide database (including environmental sequences). Classified microbial contigs accounted for 47.5% of the marker sequences, and unclassified reads accounted for the remaining 52.5% of the marker sequences constituting the test panel. Discussion

The aim of this work was to evaluate three targeted MPS methods which combine advantages of WMS and 16S rRNA-based methods for their usefulness in forensic microbiome differentiation. Additionally, it was preliminarily determined whether specific microbial community patterns could be utilized to distinguish the geographical origin of soil samples—as described in (The MetaSUB International Consortium 2016) and (Khodakova et al. 2014)—within a Polish environment. To achieve this objective, a novel solution leveraging metagenomic data and addressing the need for standardized protocols tailored to forensic requirements was developed. The present research introduces three wet-lab protocols designed to enable soil sample origin prediction using targeted high-throughput sequencing. A comprehensive overview of previously employed methods for forensic sample analysis is provided in (Jordan and Mills 2021).

Custom DNA panels were designed for each method tested based on unique sequences extracted from WMS data using the MetaGraph framework. The results of the taxonomic analysis of those sequences showed us that more than 50% of them aren’t present in the NCBI Nucleotide database. It is an additional advantage of our solution that we don’t have to rely on databases containing only known organisms to create a DNA panel, which enables correct classification of soil sample origin location. Among the classified microbial reads (47.5%), most belong to the domain Bacteria, predominantly represented by the phyla Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota, Acidobacteriota, and Gemmatimonadota. The heavy representation of these specific taxa aligns with expected ecological distributions, given that the targets were sourced from soil samples (Kalam et al. 2020). These phyla are universally recognized as the most abundant and ubiquitous lineages in terrestrial soil microbiomes globally, playing foundational roles in essential ecosystem services such as carbon cycling, organic matter decomposition, and symbiotic nitrogen fixation (Delgado-Baquerizo et al. 2018; Fierer 2017). For instance, the high prevalence of the order Hyphomicrobiales (phylum Pseudomonadota) reflects their critical function as primary drivers of the soil nitrogen cycle and root nodulation (Kuypers et al. 2018). Furthermore, the substantial proportion of unclassified sequences (52.5%) is a well-documented hallmark of soil environmental sequencing, reflecting the vast, uncultured microbial "dark matter" that still comprises a majority of complex soil ecosystems (Lloyd et al. 2018). This conclusion allows to mine for interactions between features on the feature engineering and feature selection stages. Furthermore, the almost perfect 50/50 split of sequences coming from known and unknown organisms lays the groundwork for explainable AI efforts (such as SHapley Additive exPlanations—SHAP) for the final SMAFT panel, to understand how much of predictive power relies on unclassified sequences.

The minimal depth threshold of the WMS analysis was set at 100 million reads per sample, which allowed us to catch single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in metagenomes. Such depth is of great importance for the purpose of the primers/probes design, especially in the targeted sequencing methods, for example for the 16S rRNA gene-based method successful primers/probes design plays a major role in defining the accuracy of the reconstructed bacterial profiles (Mancabelli et al. 2020). The hybridization capture-based method provided by Roche – KAPA HyperPlus – along with the designed probes and 5 ng of the input DNA allowed us to predict soil samples origins with an F1 score of 0.94. amplicon-based methods were less successful in their performance with the highest F1 score of 0.38 for TFS method and 0.38 for IDT method for all evaluated DNA input quantities. A central finding of this study was the remarkable consistency of the Roche method's performance across the entire range of tested DNA input quantities, from 1 to 100 ng. The method consistently achieved high on-target mapping efficiency, maintaining values between 70 and 90% regardless of the initial DNA concentration. This performance stands in stark contrast to the amplicon-based methods, which exhibited much lower and more variable mapping rates, generally below 20%. This high degree of consistency was also reflected in the comparative metrics, where the Roche method maintained a consistently low Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and high Cosine similarity and R2 values when compared to WMS data across all input levels. The explanation for this robust performance is rooted in the fundamental differences in the underlying technical strategies of the methods.

The probable cause for the decreased performance of the amplicon-based methods lies within higher difficulty in the development of primer sets due to the need for a repetitive process of design, optimization, and validation to cover entire targets of interest. If the target region is too large, it could be difficult to design primers that can efficiently amplify the entire region, leading to incomplete coverage, and the PCR amplification step can introduce bias, leading to over- or under-representation of some sequences. Furthermore, regions with extreme GC content can pose difficulties for primer design and amplification, which can lead to inefficient amplification or bias in the results. In contrast, hybridization capture-based methods are well-suited for capturing and sequencing larger genomic regions and can handle regions with extreme GC content as they rely on specific probe hybridization rather than PCR amplification (Alteio et al. 2021; Kalle et al. 2014), as we also have shown in (Gong et al. 2024). Moreover, the success of amplicon-based MPS depends on the quality and integrity of the DNA sample; DNA extracted from soil samples differs significantly in quality depending on the soil type. Our data suggest that these quality variations, specifically the presence of co-extracted inhibitors, directly drove the performance anomalies observed in the amplicon-based assays. For the IDT method, which was tested strictly within the manufacturer’s recommended 10–25 ng range, we observed a decrease in cosine similarity at the upper input limit (25 ng). This decline is likely attributable to the co-concentration of PCR inhibitors, such as humic acids, which are common in soil extracts. As shown in Supplementary Table S2, our samples exhibited significant variability in purity, with A260/230 ratios ranging from 0.37 to 2.07. Increasing the DNA input volume to 25 ng inherently increases the load of these contaminants, thereby disrupting PCR efficiency and inducing stochastic bias—a known vulnerability of amplicon-based chemistries in complex environmental samples. Similarly, this variability in DNA purity offers a mechanistic explanation for the inconsistent on-target mapping rates observed with the TFS method. Unlike hybridization-based workflows, the TFS protocol relies strictly on multiplex PCR, rendering it highly susceptible to polymerase inhibition. In samples with high organic contamination (low A260/230 ratios), stochastic PCR failure likely reduced specific target amplification, allowing non-specific products to dominate the sequencing library. In contrast, the Roche method remained robust against these impurities, likely because the hybridization step effectively purifies the target DNA prior to amplification, mitigating the impact of inhibitors. The Roche method utilizes a two-step process: an initial hybridization capture phase followed by a separate PCR amplification step. The hybridization step, which involves the binding of specific probes to target sequences, is not a stochastic amplification event and is less prone to the biases that plague amplicon-based PCR. This initial enrichment effectively "cleans up" the sample, concentrating the target sequences before the PCR step begins. The subsequent amplification is then performed on a pre-enriched library, which significantly reduces the impact of PCR bias on the final microbial community profile. The study further corroborates this by detailing how the number of PCR cycles was intentionally adjusted for each DNA input (e.g., 17 cycles for 1 ng or 6 cycles for 100 ng) to ensure sufficient material without compromising the integrity of the enriched library. Additionally, the higher estimated target coverage of the Roche panel (99.26% with 255 probes) compared to the amplicon panels (e.g., Integrated DNA Technologies’ 80.43% coverage with 590 amplicons) contributes to a more comprehensive and accurate representation of the microbial features, further explaining its superior and consistent performance.

From the perspective of practical forensic implementation, the choice between amplicon-based workflows (TFS and IDT) and hybridization capture (Roche) presents a trade-off between operational simplicity and critical performance. While the amplicon-based methods offer a faster, more automatable workflow and lower implementation costs (approximately 18 000–20 000 € per 96 samples in 2022 compared to 25 000 € for Roche), this study demonstrates that they lack the robustness required for forensic soil analysis. Despite the Roche manufacturer's standard recommendation of 100 ng input, our validation revealed that the Roche KAPA HyperPlus method maintains superior performance down to 1 ng of input DNA. It consistently achieved high on-target mapping efficiency (70–90%) and superior data accuracy (F1 score of 0.94 at 5 ng), whereas the cheaper and simpler amplicon-based methods failed to produce reliable data, exhibiting mapping rates below 20% and F1 scores below 0.4. Consequently, for forensic casework involving low-template or degraded evidence, the higher cost and longer hands-on time of the Roche hybridization protocol are justified by its unique ability to generate valid, high-fidelity profiles where simpler methods fail.

The ability to recover and analyze genetic information from minute quantities of DNA is a foundational requirement for forensic science, where samples are often limited or degraded. While our evaluation demonstrated that the Roche method maintains high performance at 1 ng of DNA input (achieving ~ 70–90% on-target efficiency), extrapolating these results to sub-nanogram levels requires caution. Theoretical considerations suggest that the hybridization capture mechanism—which enriches targets prior to amplification—may offer distinct advantages over amplicon-based methods in preventing stochastic dropout at trace levels. However, at sub-1 ng inputs, the risk of stochastic effects, such as the complete absence of low-abundance microbial signatures in the initial aliquot, becomes a primary concern regardless of the library preparation chemistry. While an increase in the number of post-capture PCR cycles could be used to generate sufficient sequencing reads, there is a point of diminishing returns where excessive cycles could introduce artifacts and duplicate reads. Therefore, while we hypothesize that the Roche protocol offers the most promising avenue for trace soil analysis among the methods tested, rigorous empirical validation using sub-1 ng inputs (e.g., 0.1–0.5 ng) is a necessary next step to confirm its forensic utility for trace evidence.

Beyond the technical validation of these targeted sequencing workflows across different manufacturer-specified conditions, achieving reliable results in routine forensic casework requires broader operational consistency. Therefore, it must be taken into consideration that standardization of the whole system between laboratories has to be performed, and the procedures must be followed, especially those regarding DNA input, number of amplification cycles, or hybridization time, to achieve comparable results. The use of a mock community as an internal standard allows the control extraction process and robustness of the workflow during soil microbiome metagenomic analysis. Additionally, samples spike-in enrichment facilitates the comparison of results obtained in different series of analysis or in distinct laboratories and may enable the quantification of microbial targets in metagenomes or validation/verification of metagenomic analysis methods. It is crucial to follow instructions regarding soil sample collection and storage as these pre-laboratory steps also significantly impact the reliability of downstream analysis. Ensuring stringent quality checks at every stage of the wet-lab procedure, as facilitated by the spike-in control, is paramount for the successful generation of high-quality, trustworthy data for subsequent analysis.

It is also important to note that the custom DNA panels evaluated in this study were designed using WMS data derived from soil samples collected during a single season (autumn). While seasonal variation will undoubtedly influence the taxonomic composition of soil microbiomes—and the ecological stability of geographic prediction models across different seasons is the subject of our ongoing research—it does not alter the primary technical conclusions of this validation study. The performance disparities observed between the evaluated methods stem from their underlying chemistries, specifically the inherent robustness of hybridization capture against the susceptibility of amplicon-based multiplexing to PCR bias and inhibitors. From a technical implementation standpoint, the most pronounced effect of seasonality will likely be fluctuations in total extractable microbial biomass. Soil evidence collected during metabolically restrictive seasons may yield minimal DNA. Therefore, the demonstrated ability of the Roche method to maintain high consistency and prediction accuracy at low DNA inputs underscores its essential utility for forensic casework, where seasonal factors may severely limit the available DNA template. This robustness is particularly promising when taking into account Poland's environmental structure (Rdzany 2014)—its low climatic and microclimatic variability resulting from the country’s relatively small size. Despite this limited diversity, we were still able to accurately estimate the origin of a soil sample based solely on its unique microbiome profiles.

Targeted sequencing using MPS has significantly advanced forensic investigations by allowing the analysis of a broad spectrum of genetic markers. This method is especially useful when dealing with difficult forensic samples, such as those that are degraded or available in limited amounts (Bruijns et al. 2018; McCord et al. 2019). By focusing on specific, highly informative loci, targeted sequencing can be used for human identification and kinship analysis. Furthermore, panels designed with ancestry-informative markers can predict an individual's biogeographic origin, providing crucial investigative intelligence in cases involving unknown individuals (Bardan et al. 2023; Eduardoff et al. 2016). Through the targeted sequencing of relevant markers, it is also possible to predict externally visible characteristics, including hair and eye color, offering additional descriptive information to aid investigations (Bardan et al. 2023). The effectiveness of targeted sequencing in recovering and analyzing fragmented DNA from degraded samples is largely due to advanced targeted capture methods, such as hybridization capture enrichment. MPS further helps in the analysis of DNA mixtures by generating high read depth data, which allows for accurate deconvolution and quantification of contributions from multiple individuals (Bose et al. 2018; Wu et al. 2019). Beyond identification, targeted sequencing is increasingly applied to forensic molecular autopsy to investigate the genetic basis for sudden unexplained deaths, analyzing genes associated with inherited cardiac conditions (Kling et al. 2025). Shih and coworkers (Shih et al. 2018) have shown that hybridization capture enrichment can be used to analyze both whole mitochondrial genomes and nuclear SNPs in difficult forensic samples. Furthermore, it has shown promise for obtaining valuable genetic information from severely degraded skeletal remains, critical for identification in historical cases or mass fatalities (Haarkötter et al. 2024). The successful developmental validation of commercial targeted MPS systems, such as the MiSeq FGx® Forensic Genomics System, for use in forensic casework and database laboratories underscores the readiness of this technology for widespread implementation, demonstrating robust performance essential for operational forensic DNA analysis (Jäger et al. 2017).

Although WMS is quite expensive and computational resources demanding, it allows for obtaining additional information which enables further exploration of the complicated relationships within microbial communities and their environment. WMS facilitates the annotation of functional genes within the tested sample (Robinson et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021), enabling identification of the genes associated with antibiotic resistance (Brizola Toscan et al. 2025), which in turn contributes to the opportunity of epidemiological monitoring (Afshinnekoo et al. 2015; Gunn and Pitt 2012) and the prediction of possible epidemics that may occur, e.g., as a result of microbiological threats related to the circulation of water in the soil (Zheng et al. 2022). Furthermore, identifying the composition of microbes found in the tested soil samples may facilitate more precise monitoring of local environmental or climate shifts by examining how organisms adapt to altering environmental conditions. Moreover, identification of the genes associated with metabolic pathways or nutrient cycling can be valuable for agriculture, especially in the context of the growing problem of soil depletion and degradation (Coban, De Deyn, and van der Ploeg 2022). Additionally, the application of metagenomics is proving instrumental in urban planning; the study of the built environment microbiomes has demonstrated that urban infrastructure significantly shapes microbial communities, influencing human health and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) patterns. WMS also contributes to microbiome-informed green infrastructure by characterizing microbial diversity in urban green spaces and understanding their role in immune system regulation. Monitoring of the public transportation system, as exemplified by the MetaSUB project (Danko et al. 2021) helped us understand how microbial communities interact with human mobility patterns in mass transit environments and thus allowed for data-driven policy decisions to improve sanitation, ventilation and AMR containment (Bruno et al. 2022).

An additional advantage of the presented system is the capability to compare samples against each other, enabling forensic investigators to identify similarities or distinctions among soil samples from diverse locations. This comparative capability is valuable in forensic investigations, where sample matching can serve as critical evidence. Furthermore, with the rapid advancements in sequencing methods and computational approaches, data analysis is becoming more effective and less expensive, which makes it easier to use the system in more laboratories and reduce the barriers to its widespread use.

Conclusion

The three protocols evaluated—Roche KAPA HyperPlus, Thermo Fisher Scientific Ion AmpliSeq™, and Integrated DNA Technologies xGen™ Amplicon—are all capable of predicting soil sample origin using targeted MPS techniques. However, the hybridization capture-based Roche method was unequivocally superior in this research, demonstrating the highest accuracy, sensitivity, and reproducibility across all metrics tested. It achieved the closest resemblance to WMS data, with the lowest Bray–Curtis dissimilarity values (~ 0.1–0.2), the highest Cosine similarity scores (close to 1), and R2 values (0.87–0.98) consistently across a range of DNA inputs. This superior performance was also supported by an on-target mapping efficiency of 70% to 90%, significantly outperforming the amplicon-based methods, which had mapping rates below 20% and greater variability. The Roche method’s robust two-step methodology, which mitigates biases and the disruptive effects of co-extracted soil inhibitors, allowing it to achieve a high F1 score of 0.94 with just 5 ng of input DNA, while the amplicon-based methods peaked at only 0.38. These results highlight the distinct advantages of hybridization capture-based sequencing for complex environmental samples, establishing the Roche KAPA HyperPlus as the most efficient method for forensic soil microbiome profiling. Its ability to generate high-fidelity microbial signatures from minimal DNA amounts makes it particularly well-suited for forensic casework, though empirical validation on sub-nanogram trace evidence remains a necessary step.

Finally, to further enhance the method's discriminatory power, future work will involve expanding the microbial reference database to include soil samples from diverse European climatic zones and varying soil types. Testing the custom panel's resolution on a continental scale will assess the ecological stability of these markers and establish the method's broader applicability and robustness for international geographic origin prediction.
