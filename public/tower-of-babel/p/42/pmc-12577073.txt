Optimal placement of the temporary fixation pin in tibial plateau leveling osteotomy: a canine ex vivo study
Authors: Jeong-Woon Kim, Jung-Moon Kim, Hwi-Yool Kim, Jun-Sik Cho, Keuntae Lee, Jung-Hyun Kim, Junsuk Jo, Sangyul Lee, Sang-Kun Jang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTibial Plateau Leveling Osteotomy (TPLO) is widely accepted for stabilizing the stifle joint in dogs with cranial cruciate ligament disease. However, postoperative tibial tuberosity fractures remain a significant complication, particularly in small-breed dogs. Recent anatomical findings suggest that Sharpey’s fibers (SF) contribute to local structural reinforcement within the tibial tuberosity, but the biomechanical impact of temporary fixation pin positioning relative to these fibers has not been experimentally quantified.ResultsEighteen pelvic limbs from nine small-breed canine cadavers (mean body weight 5.98 kg) were randomized to three groups (n = 6) based on temporary fixation pin positioning. Group 1 had the pin inserted perpendicular to the tibial mechanical axis at the level of SF. Group 2 received pin placement 3 mm distal, and Group 3 received placement 6 mm distal and inclined from cranial to caudal. All specimens underwent a standardized TPLO, followed by mounting at a standing angle of 135°, and vertical tensile force was applied until failure. Pre- and postoperative tibial plateau angle (TPA) and absolute tibial tuberosity width (ATTW) confirmed comparable anatomy across groups. Group 1 exhibited significantly higher maximum failure loads compared to Groups 2 and 3 (p < 0.017), with no significant difference between those two groups. Fracture configuration differed notably: Group 1 showed complex, comminuted fractures of the distal tibial crest, while Groups 2 and 3 demonstrated simple linear transverse fractures through pin tract at the mid-crest.ConclusionsPositioning the temporary fixation pin at the level of the SF markedly enhances the biomechanical resistance of the tibial tuberosity under tensile loading in ex vivo TPLO models. These findings endorse precise proximal pin placement as a modifiable surgical parameter to mitigate fracture risk in small-breed dogs. Future investigations employing dynamic loading protocols and evaluating breed-specific anatomical variations are warranted to validate these results in vivo.Keywords: Small breed dogs, Tibial plateau leveling osteotomy, Tibial tuberosity fracture, Temporary fixation pin, Sharpey’s fibers, Tensile test

Background

Cranial cruciate ligament disease (CCLD) is one of the most commonly encountered orthopedic conditions in dogs and constitutes a primary etiology of hindlimb lameness [1, 2]. The etiology of CCLD remains unclear, though degenerative, biomechanical, genetic, and immune-mediated factors have been implicated [3–5]. Various surgical techniques have been developed to address stifle instability caused by CCLD, including intracapsular stabilization, extracapsular stabilization, and radial osteotomy procedures [2, 6–8]. Among these, Tibial Plateau Leveling Osteotomy (TPLO) is a widely accepted technique, as it restores stifle stability by neutralizing tibial thrust through modification of the tibial plateau angle during weight-bearing [2, 9, 10]. Despite its proven effectiveness, TPLO is associated with complications ranging from 11.4% to 34% [11, 12], with tibial tuberosity fractures being a significant concern.

Fracture of the tibial tuberosity accounts for 0.4-9% [12–14]. The complication is clinically significant, often necessitating revision surgery [12]. Proposed risk factors for tibial tuberosity fractures following TPLO include cranialized osteotomy, overcorrection of TPA, poor osteotomy reduction, oversized saw blade use, mal-positioned temporary fixation pin, and concurrent bilateral TPLO [13–15]. The absolute tibial tuberosity width (ATTW), defined as the narrowest mediolateral width of the tibial tuberosity, measured cranial to the osteotomy and aligned with the patellar ligament (PL) insertion site, is considered a key factor influencing the occurrence of tibial tuberosity fractures following TPLO. Several studies have identified ATTW as an important measure associated with fracture risk [13–16].

According to numerous textbooks and peer-reviewed studies, placing the temporary fixation pin at the level of the Sharpey’s fibers(SF), corresponding to the most cranial part of the tibial tuberosity, is an effective preventive measure against tibial tuberosity fractures following TPLO [1, 12, 14, 15, 17–21]. However, one study has raised doubts about the efficacy of this approach [13]. All available studies are retrospective, and no mechanical testing has quantitatively assessed the relationship between the maximum tensile strength of the quadriceps mechanism, specifically at the tibial tuberosity, and the position of temporary fixation pin insertion.

The purpose of this study is to evaluate how proximal positioning of the temporary fixation pin, particularly at the level of the SF, affects the biomechanical stability of the tibial tuberosity during TPLO. Specifically, three different techniques of temporary fixation pin insertion were compared, focusing on how the proximal position of the pin relative to the SF affects tensile resistance at the tibial tuberosity.

It was hypothesized that pinning at the level of the SF would result in a significantly higher tensile force to failure compared to more distal pin placements. This biomechanical advantage could potentially reduce the incidence of tibial tuberosity fractures and improve surgical outcomes in clinical settings.

Materials and methods

Specimen preparation

Pelvic limbs (n = 18) were harvested from nine skeletally mature small-breed dogs (4.0–7.9 kg), including Maltese (n = 2), Poodles (n = 3), and mixed-breed dogs (n = 4), that had been euthanized for reasons unrelated to the present study (Incheon Veterinary Medical Association, Korea). Sample size was based on availability, reflecting the number of cadaveric limbs accessible and constraints of specimen preparation. Standardized craniocaudal and mediolateral radiographs were obtained using a 25 mm calibration marker. Samples were excluded if an open proximal tibial physis was present radiographically or there was evidence of deformity or orthopedic disease involving the stifle. Only eligible limbs were included, and no samples were excluded after group allocation. The limbs were disarticulated at the hip joint. Then, limbs were clipped, labeled, enveloped in gauze moistened with 0.9% saline and subsequently frozen at − 70 °C. Specimens were allowed to equilibrate at room temperature for 24 h prior to surgical planning. Specimens were randomly assigned to one of the three groups (n = 6) using an online random list generator [22]. Each cadaver contributed two limbs, but each limb was treated independently.

Osteotomy planning

Surgical planning for TPLO was completed preoperatively using vPOP Pro software v3.0.10 (VetSOS Education Ltd.®, Shrewsbury, UK). Mediolateral and craniocaudal radiographs were obtained to assess the osteotomy site and identify any anatomical deformities. The tibial plateau angle (TPA) was assessed in the mediolateral view, following the method described by Slocum and Devine [23, 24]. The radial osteotomy was centered over the intercondylar tubercles at the geometrically optimal location for TPLO [25]. The caudal exit of the osteotomy was planned to be perpendicular to the caudal tibial cortex [16]. Tibia width and absolute tibial tuberosity width (ATTW) were also measured in the mediolateral view using the approach described by Hamilton et al. [13, 16]. Specific measurements denoted as D1, D2, and D3 were taken to determine the osteotomy site using the standard method previously described by Jeong et al. [26].

The osteotomy was planned to ensure that the relative tibial tuberosity width remained near 25% of the craniocaudal tibial width [13, 27]. If necessary, the center of rotation was adjusted cranially and distally along a 45° trajectory relative to the tibial long axis, following previously described methods [16], to preserve the overall morphology of the tibial tuberosity.

Surgical procedure

All soft tissues were meticulously dissected from each limb, preserving the patella, patellar tendon, stifle ligaments, and the distal 4 cm of the quadriceps musculature. A proximal radial osteotomy was performed using an oscillating saw (Zaguar, IMEDICOM®, Gunpo, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea), and 12-mm or 15-mm TPLO blades (IMEDICOM) were selected according to tibial width, following the original technique described by Slocum [10]. After completion of the tibial plateau osteotomy, the proximal segment was rotated.

Group 1: pin at the SF

A 1.1-mm Kirschner wire was placed perpendicular to the tibial mechanical axis, adjacent to the patellar tendon attachment line (SF) at the most cranial point of the tibial tuberosity (Fig. 1A) until it engaged the caudal tibial cortex [11, 28].

Group 2: pin 3-mm distal to the SF

A 1.1-mm Kirschner wire was placed perpendicular to the tibial mechanical axis, 3 mm distal to the patellar tendon attachment line (Fig. 1B) until it engaged the caudal tibial cortex.

Group 3: pin 6-mm distal to the SF with Cranial-to-Caudal inclination

A 1.1-mm Kirschner wire was inserted in an inclined cranial-to-caudal direction (Fig. 1C), entering the tibial crest 6 mm distal to the patellar tendon attachment line. The wire was advanced until it engaged the caudal tibial cortex, following a technique similar to that described by Talaat et al. [29].

After temporary reduction of the proximal tibial segment, a 2.0-mm six-hole conventional TPLO plate (Jeil TPLO Plates; Jeil Medical Corp.®, Seoul, Republic of Korea) was positioned, and the Kirschner wire was removed prior to definitive screw fixation. All subsequent procedures were performed by a single surgeon (J.W.K.) based on preoperative planning. The surgeon aimed to achieve a postoperative TPA of 5° [27, 30] and selected either a 12-mm or 15-mm TPLO saw blade. Pin placement was visually aligned relative to the tibial mechanical axis during surgery. In Groups 1 and 2, the pin was intended to be placed perpendicular to the tibial mechanical axis, whereas in Group 3, it was placed in an inclined cranial-to-caudal direction. However, no postoperative radiographic or angular measurements were performed to objectively verify the pin orientation.

Postoperative radiography

Immediate postoperative radiographs were obtained for every limb (Fig. 2). On these images, ATTW, TPA, and rotation distance were measured. A sample was accepted for further analysis only if it met al.l three radiographic criteria: (1) a postoperative TPA of 0°–14° [30, 31]; (2) no discernible osteotomy gap; and (3) secure bicortical screw purchase. Specimens that failed to satisfy any of these thresholds were discarded and replaced with a newly prepared sample. All measurements were obtained in triplicate, in a single session, by the same investigator (J.W.K.).

Biomechanical evaluation

For biomechanical testing, the hock and stifle joints were disarticulated, retaining only the tibia with the TPLO construct, the patella, the PL, and the distal 4 cm of the quadriceps muscle. Each specimen was secured to a wooden board angled at 135° to the tibial axis to simulate the mid-stance phase of gait [16, 32, 33]. A 1.8 mm Steinmann pin was inserted through the tibial shaft into the board for fixation, and three additional 2.0 mm cortical screws were placed in the proximal and distal tibia to counteract rotational forces. Incremental tensile force was then applied to the quadriceps muscle via a custom gripping jig (Fig. 3). Vertical distraction was performed at a constant displacement rate of 10 mm/min using a mechanical testing machine (Instron 5585; Instron Corp.®, Norwood, MA, USA) until tibial tuberosity failure occurred. Failure was defined as the first abrupt drop in the load-displacement curve. The primary outcome measure was the maximum failure load (N) of the tibial tuberosity under vertical tensile force. Radiographs were obtained post-failure to document the fracture configuration and location. Parameters recorded for each specimen included pre-osteotomy tibial width (mm), post-osteotomy ATTW (mm), TPA (°), maximum failure load (N), and mode of failure.

A photograph of the axial tensile testing model. Each specimen was secured at 135° relative to the tibial axis and subjected to vertical distraction (indicated by the arrow) at a constant rate of 10 mm/min until tibial tuberosity failure occurred

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software v.30.0.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Due to the small sample size in each of the three groups, non-parametric tests were employed. Group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by post hoc pairwise analysis using the Mann–Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction. A p-value < 0.017 was considered statistically significant. No blinding was applied in this study. All procedures, including specimen preparation, group allocation, TPLO, and outcome measurement, were jointly conducted by two investigators (J.W.K. and J.M.K.).

Results

Eighteen hindlimbs were harvested from nine skeletally mature small-breed canine cadavers between March 2024 and January 2025. The mean body weights were similar across groups (G1: 6.04 ± 1.19 kg, G2: 5.93 ± 1.08 kg, G3: 5.98 ± 1.37 kg) (Table 1), with no significant difference (p = 0.975).

Pre- and postoperative ATTW and TPA

There were no significant differences among the groups in either ATTW or TPA at both the preoperative (ATTW: p = 0.874; TPA: p = 0.717) or postoperative (ATTW: p = 0.352; TPA: p = 0.874) time points. The mean postoperative ATTW values were 5.40 ± 0.40 mm in Group 1, 5.62 ± 0.96 mm in Group 2, and 5.93 ± 0.35 mm in Group 3, while the mean postoperative TPA values were 4.33 ± 2.16°, 4.70 ± 3.28°, and 4.48 ± 2.52°, respectively (Table 1).

Types of failure

Failure configurations varied by group and reflected the position of the temporary fixation pin (Table 2). In Group 1, half of specimens (n = 3; Fig. 5A) exhibited comminuted distal tibial crest fractures and half (n = 3; Fig. 5B) had irregular mid-crest transverse fractures. In Groups 2 and 3, all specimens sustained simple linear transverse fractures through the pin tract— mid-crest in Group 2 (n = 6; Fig. 5C), and distal crest in Group 3 (n = 6; Fig. 5D).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that the position of the temporary fixation pin substantially affects the tensile strength of the tibial tuberosity following TPLO (Fig. 4). The pin placed at the most cranial part of the tibial tuberosity demonstrated significantly higher resistance to failure, confirming the mechanical advantage of engaging the region reinforced by the SF. These findings identify pin location as a modifiable surgical parameter that can minimize stress concentrations and potentially reduce tibial tuberosity fractures.

Tibial tuberosity fractures following TPLO have been associated with multiple modifiable risk factors in previous studies [12, 14–18], including a shallow residual tibial tuberosity width, excessive TPA correction, greater body weight loading, and mal-position of the temporary anti rotation pin. In the present study, radiographic assessment confirmed that pre- and postoperative TPA, ATTW, and body weight were equivalent across groups (Table 1). Thus, differences in maximum tensile failure load are attributable primarily to pin placement relative to SF.

The superior biomechanical performance of Group 1 reflects two complementary anatomical factors: first, the robust anchorage provided by the SF at the cranial tibial tuberosity, and second, the inherently greater bone stock available at proximal sites compared with distal locations.

Sharpey’s fibers are specialized collagenous structures that anchor muscle, tendon, ligament, and periosteum to bone, playing a critical role in stability by distributing forces across the bone–soft tissue interface [1, 34]. Recent human studies suggest these fibers participate in bone remodeling and repair, suggesting a broader biological relevance in musculoskeletal integrity [35, 36]. Anatomically, the SF concentrate at high stress entheses such as alveolar sockets, cranial sutures, and tendon–bone insertion sites [1]. In human orthopedic practice, preserving SF integrity is emphasized to avoid iatrogenic weakening at tendon-to-bone interfaces [35]. In veterinary medicine, specifically during TPLO, clinical observations similarly indicate increased tibial tuberosity fracture risk when pins are placed distal to the SF insertion [1, 15, 17, 21].

In the present study, distal pin placement where the tibial tuberosity is inherently thinner, was associated with a substantial reduction in maximum failure load. In contrast, placement near the SF, where bone stock and tendon-bone anchorage are more robust, provides superior mechanical support and is associated with reduced fracture risk (Fig. 6). These observations suggest that placing the pin near the SF likely reduces the risk of tibial tuberosity fractures.

Several studies have highlighted the role of tibial tuberosity morphology in fracture prevention, noting considerable individual variation in the location of the PL insertion [14, 16, 29, 37, 38]. According to Mehrkens et al. [14]., when the narrowest point of the tibial tuberosity is located distal to the PL insertion, the fracture risk increases compared to cases where it tapers at or proximal to the PL insertion. These findings emphasize the importance of both bone stock and tuberosity geometry in preventing postoperative complications during TPLO.

A retrospective clinical study by Bergh et al. [13] found no significant association between proximal pin placement and the fracture incidence. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in patient populations, as that study primarily involved medium- to large-breed dogs. In such breeds, the tibial crest typically provides more robust bone stock, potentially mitigating the mechanical consequences of distal pin placement. By contrast, the current study exclusively involved small-breed dogs, in which limited bone stock in the tibial crest has been identified as a significant surgical challenge [39]. These anatomical differences may help explain the inconsistent findings, though further validation is needed.

The comparable failure loads between Group 2 and 3 may be attributed to the fact that both pin placements remained within the tibial crest. Although they differed in distance from SF, both positions were within a region where bone stock is relatively uniform from its mid to distal crest, resulting in minimal variation in biomechanical resistance (Fig. 6).

Fracture configurations observed in this study exhibited distinct patterns based on the pin’s proximal positioning relative to SF (Table 2; Fig. 5). Group 1 presented two types, including comminuted and irregular transverse fractures. In contrast, Groups 2 and 3 consistently exhibited linear transverse fractures passing through the pin tract, indicating that distal pin placement compromises structural integrity and creates predictable failure lines.

A previous study by Shahar and Banks-Sills utilizing three-dimensional biomechanical modeling indicated that quadriceps muscle force during a slow walk can range from 16.5% to 94.8% of body weight, depending on stifle flexion angle [32, 33]. Based on the body weight of dogs in the present study (4.0–7.9 kg), the estimated quadriceps force would range from 37 to 74 N, well below the lowest failure load observed (194 N). However, this model does not account for high-impact activities such as running or jumping, necessitating further dynamic biomechanical evaluation.

This study has several limitations. First, the exclusive use of small-breed canine limbs limits the applicability of these results to larger breeds with different anatomical characteristics. Second, the small sample size, while appropriate for ex vivo testing, limits statistical power. Third, static axial loading failed to replicate dynamic mechanical stress experienced during ambulation, which may be critical for understanding tibial tuberosity failure over time. Clinical fractures typically occur between two-six weeks postoperatively [13, 21], highlighting the need for dynamic loading models to enhance translational relevance. Fourth, the cadaveric model precluded biological responses such as bone remodeling and SF development, which are essential for long-term fixation stability. Histologic evidence indicates that the SF begin forming around eight weeks postoperatively [40], with their morphology influenced by age, hormonal status, and bone pathology [34]. Fifth, pin placement was visually aligned but not radiographically confirmed, limiting reproducibility. Lastly, removal of periarticular soft tissues and the lack of donor medical histories precluded assessment of native stabilizers and pathological influences on bone quality.

These findings suggest that precise positioning of the temporary fixation pin adjacent to the SF likely reduces the risk of postoperative tibial tuberosity fractures in small-breed dogs undergoing TPLO. Further in vivo studies incorporating dynamic loading conditions and breed-specific anatomical variations are warranted to validate these biomechanical outcomes.

Conclusion

Optimal placement of the temporary fixation pin at the level of Sharpey’s fibers insertion enhances biomechanical resistance and may reduce tibial tuberosity fracture risk following TPLO in small-breed dogs. These findings support incorporating this anatomical reinforcement into surgical planning for safer outcomes.
