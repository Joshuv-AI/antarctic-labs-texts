Strain and External Electric Field Engineering of S-Terminated MXene on Selective and Sensitive Detection of N-Containing Compound Gases: A Computational Study
Authors: Sirisak Singsen, Panupol Untarabut, Wongsathorn Kaewraung, Lappawat Ngamwongwan, Jariyanee Prasongkit, Suwit Suthirakun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGas sensors are used for monitoring hazardous gases and vapors. With the emergence of S-terminated MXene synthesis, herein, we explore the sensing ability of Ti3C2S2 toward N-containing gases, including NH3, NO, NO2, trimethylamine (TMA), and nicotine (NT), using first-principles calculations and a statistical thermodynamic model. We find that Ti3C2S2 exhibits high selectivity to TMA, NO, and NT with moderate adsorption energies of −0.610, −0.490, and −0.476 eV, respectively, minimizing environmental noise from ambient gases. The electronic structure of Ti3C2S2 subtly alters upon adsorption of TMA, NO, and NT, facilitating detectable signals in the sensing device. However, the adsorption of NO2 and NH3 is less pronounced due to weak physisorption (<0.3 eV). Employing engineering strategies including biaxial strain and an external electric field greatly enhances the selectivity and sensitivity of NO2 (NH3) detection by boosting adsorption strength up to −0.351 eV with ε = 5% (−0.370 eV with |E⃗| = 0.6 V/ Å). In addition, the moderate adsorption energies of the gases result in a suitable recovery time in the range of milliseconds, leading to high reusability of the sensing device. The estimated adsorption densities suggest potential coverage of these N-containing molecules even at low concentrations and room temperature. Computational analysis of the sensing capability of Ti3C2S2 using the nonequilibrium Green’s function method indicates that it is a promising gas-sensing material. In addition, mechanical modifications, electric field adjustments, and gate voltage alterations could be used to obtain effective sensing materials for N-containing gas detection.

Introduction

Numerous gaseous pollutants have been released into the environment due to the ongoing human and industrial development. The emission of N-containing gases, such as ammonia (NH3), nitric oxide (NO), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), trimethylamine (TMA), and nicotine (NT), leads to a toxic atmosphere, driven by industrial waste gases and cigarette smoke.1,2 Air pollution is a significant factor affecting human health, causing irritation and severe damage to the skin, throat, lungs, and eyes.1,3 Therefore, developing highly sensitive and selective gas sensors to detect traces of these gases upon exposure is needed.

Two-dimensional (2D) materials have gained significant attention for sensing applications due to their superior surface interaction, lower operating temperatures, and larger surface areas.4 Recent studies, including those on stanene nanosheets,5−7 MoS2,8,9 and other 2D nanomaterials,10−12 have shown notable advancements in their adsorption and sensing properties for gases and metal ions. For instance, the silicene monolayer is an effective sensor for detecting NH3, NO, and NO2,13 while the S-doped green phosphorene sensor exhibits high sensitivity toward NO2.14 Recent computational studies have demonstrated that 2D covalent triazine frameworks15 and Al2C monolayers16 are effective in detecting NO and NH3, respectively, due to sufficient changes in their electronic properties upon gas adsorption. Additionally, metal-functionalized tunable MoS2 responds strongly to NT molecules.17 Furthermore, 2D-covalent organic frameworks have been shown to be effective sensors for TMA molecules.18 These findings showcase the versatility of 2D materials in sensing applications, driven by their tunable surface and electronic properties.

2D MXene materials have attracted great attention for several applications.19 A range of these materials have been synthesized with their chemical formula being denoted as Mn+1XnTx, where M, X, and T stand for transition metals, C or N elements, and the surface terminal groups, respectively.20,21 Among MXene materials, titanium carbide MXene (Ti3C2Tx, T = O, OH, F) shows high sensing ability, high response, short recovery time, and greater selectivity.22−24 Notably, previous experiments demonstrated that Ti3C2O2 was regarded as a susceptible sensing material for NH3 at room temperature with an ultrahigh signal-to-noise ratio.25,26 In addition, computational studies suggested that Ti3C2(OH)2 should exhibit high selectivity and sensitivity toward N-containing gases (i.e., NO, NO2, N2O, and NF3).27

Recently, S-terminated titanium carbide, Ti3C2S2, has been successfully fabricated through an etching process in acidic molten salts, followed by S group functionalization.21 The 2D Ti3C2S2 material has not, experimentally nor computationally, been studied as a chemical sensing material of N-containing gases. However, 2D-MXene with S termination on titanium carbide is likely to be an attractive candidate for sensing several gases. For instance, the S-terminated Hf2C exhibits superior sensing performance for detecting NO and NO2.28 Previous theoretical studies showed that Sc2CS2 and Y2CS2 are remarkable materials with high sensitivity as NO, NO2, NH3, NT, ethanol, and formaldehyde sensors.29 Moreover, experimental work demonstrated that the S-doping of Ti3C2Tx displays good selectivity to toluene gas with a high response of up to 312% at 50 ppm.30 Motivated by the impressive properties of S-termination on MXene, it is vital to investigate the sensing properties of Ti3C2S2 as a promising material for detecting N-containing gases.

In this work, we employed a combination of the first-principles method based on density functional theory (DFT) and statistical thermodynamics to assess the gas sensing properties of Ti3C2S2 for detecting N-containing gases, including NH3, NO, NO2, TMA, and NT. To do so, we first explored the thermal stability of the MXene materials at room and high temperatures using ab initio molecular dynamic (AIMD) simulations. Next, to elucidate the selectivity, adsorption energies of gas molecules on the MXene monolayer were calculated, while changes in electronic properties and work functions were analyzed to explore the sensitivity of the MXene toward the adsorbed gases. Moreover, the recovery time and the adsorption density were studied to determine the gas desorption and the possibility of gas attraction on the sensing surface, respectively. Lastly, using the nonequilibrium Green’s function method (NEGF), the electron transport properties were examined to assess the sensitivity and selectivity of the sensing device in detecting the adsorbed gases. This work provides a computational perspective on the promising properties of S-terminated MXene materials for gas detection.

Computational Methods

Density Functional Theory

All computations were carried out using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP 5.3)31,32 within the framework of the spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT) approach. The frozen-core ion and electron interactions were described using the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method.33 The DFT-D3 approach was used to correct the weak van der Waals (vdW) interactions between the gas molecules and the adsorbates.34 It should be noted that DFT-D3 is better for describing the vdW correction than DFT-D2 for 2D MXenes as mentioned in several works.28,35,36 The electron exchange-correlation functional was estimated using the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional.37 Previous DFT computations with PBE+U method (U = 4.2 V at Ti atoms) suggested that the electronic structure of Ti3C2T2 (T = O, S) exhibits metallic character;35 thus, the PBE functional is sufficient to describe the electronic properties of MXene materials.

The monolayer Ti3C2T2 system was modeled using 4 × 4 supercells with an added vacuum gap of 20 Å. Its cell size is large enough to avoid the interaction between its periodic images, especially for the adsorption of large molecules (i.e., TMA, NT).29,38 The valence-electron wave functions of H 1s1, C 2s22p2, O 2s22p4, S 3s23p4, N 2s22p3, and Ti 3d24s2 were expanded in a plane-wave basis with a kinetic energy cutoff of 520 eV. The Brillouin zone integration was sampled by 5 × 5 × 1 and 11 × 11 × 1 Monkhort-Pack scheme39 for structure optimizations and electronic property calculations, respectively (see Figure S1). All structures were optimized using a conjugate gradient algorithm, with the energy and force convergence criteria of 10–6 eV and 0.02 eV/Å, respectively. The atomic structures were visualized using VESTA software.40 The degree of charge transfer between the adsorbates and the adsorbent was analyzed via Bader charge calculations.41,42

Ab Initio Molecular Dynamic Simulation

To elucidate the thermal stability of the sensing materials, we carried out DFT-based ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulation for Ti3C2T2 (T = O, S) substrates at normal (T = 300 K) and desorption (T = 500 K) temperatures. The dynamics were run using the NVT ensemble of Nose-Hoover thermostat43,44 with a time step of 2 fs for 10 ps. The energy cutoff and the number of k-points sampled were decreased to 450 eV and 2 × 2 × 1, respectively, to reduce computational resources.

Nonequilibrium Green’s Function Method

Using the nonequilibrium Green’s function (NEGF)45 based on DFT as implemented in QuantumATK package,46 we investigated the electron transport properties of Ti3C2S2. The system was divided into three parts: left (L) and right (R) electrodes and a scattering region in between them. The GGA-PBE and double-ζ polarized (DZP) basis sets were employed for the calculations. To account for long-range van der Waals interactions, the Grimme DFT-D3 method was used. Periodic boundary conditions were applied along the x-direction, with semi-infinite electrodes along the z-direction. A real-space grid with an energy cutoff of 150 Ry was used. The k-points grids were set up as 4 × 1 × 1 and 4 × 1 × 100 k-points for the device and electrodes, respectively.

Results and Discussion

Structural Properties and Thermal Stability of Ti3C2S2

The pristine Ti3C2S2 structure consists of a sandwich of stacked Ti and C layers, with the surface terminated by S layers. The monolayer Ti3C2S2 unit cell exhibits a hexagonal symmetry with a P63/mmc space group. Its calculated lattice parameters are a = b = 3.137 Å, which agree well with previous experimental (∼3.17 Å)21 and computational values (3.123 Å).35 We chose the most stable type-III configuration as a model in this work which is more stable than the other two configurations by at least 0.93 eV (type-I and type-II).35

Then, from the optimized unit cell, we constructed a 4 × 4 supercell monolayer for the adsorption study. As shown in Figure 1a, this monolayer contains three different adsorption sites, including atop S (ST), atop Ti (TiT), and atop C (CT) sites.

(c) The evolution of the calculated total energy of the monolayer Ti3C2S2 using AIMD simulation at 300 K (blue line) and 500 K (red line). The inserted pictures display the dynamic configurations at the final simulation time. Color codes: blue, Ti; brown, C; and yellow, S.

The calculated electronic density of states (DOS) of Ti3C2S2 reveals a metallic character with nonferromagnetic properties, as shown in Figure 1b. The Ti 3d states dominate the Fermi level, suggesting that the Ti 3d states play an important role in the conductance and sensitivity of the MXene sensor upon gas adsorption. This result is consistent with previous DFT-PBE reports.47

The thermal stability of sensing materials is a key factor in determining their durability. Here, we carried out AIMD simulations to evaluate the thermal stability of the sensing material at sensing (300 K) and gas desorption temperatures (500 K),48 as depicted in Figure 1c. We find that the Ti3C2S2 monolayer remains dynamically stable in both temperatures, with their total energies fluctuating around its original equilibrium values. Although the outer S layers slightly oscillate over the simulation time, the Ti3C2S2 experienced no decomposition or distortion, indicating high stability for practical applications. This is due to the strong chemical binding of the S functional group on bare Ti3C2, with a formation energy of −5.521 eV, corresponding to eq S1. This result is consistent with another DFT study, which reported a strong binding energy of −9.345 eV.35

Adsorptions of N-Containing Gases on MXene

As shown in Figure 2c, the adsorption energies of TMA, NO, and NT on Ti3C2S2 are moderate. The TMA molecule is horizontally adsorbed on the substrate at a distance of 2.961 Å with the strongest adsorption energy of −0.610 eV while gaining a charge of 0.366 e– upon adsorption. The adsorption is slightly stronger than that on WS2 (−0.594 eV),38 but weaker than that on borophene nanotubes (−0.780 eV).50

NO and NT exhibit slightly weaker adsorption energies of −0.490 and −0.476 eV with slightly smaller charge transfers of 0.282 e– and 0.238 e–, respectively. The adsorbed NO points its N atom toward the MXene with a tilted orientation, with an N–S distance of 2.399 Å, while NT shows a vertical orientation at 2.759 Å above the substrate. The adsorption of NO on Ti3C2S2 is stronger, compared with that on green phosphorene (−0.25 eV)14 and Zr3C2O2 (−0.217 eV).51

In contrast, the adsorptions of NH3 and NO2 are rather weak, at most −0.196 eV which is weaker than the suggested value of −0.3 eV. Such weak interactions are consistent with their adsorbed configuration in which they are quite far from the substrate at 3.09 Å for NH3 and 3.15 Å for NO2, suggesting weak van der Waals characteristics. This weak interaction also correlates well with a small amount of charge transfer of 0.184 e for NH3 and negligible for NO2.

To better understand the diffusion behavior of the adsorbed molecules on the Ti3C2S2 MXene surface, we selected the NO and NO2 molecules as representative of strong and weak adsorption, respectively. Their diffusion barriers were computed using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method.52 The results show that the weakly adsorbed NO2 has no diffusion barrier, while the strongly adsorbed NO displays a barrier of 0.788 eV, as shown in Figure S2. The calculated results align with their adsorption energies, where the weaker the adsorption, the lower the diffusion barrier. Such a low barrier enhances the diffusivity of the adsorbed molecules, resulting in a reduced signal in the sensing device. Overall, the adsorption energies of TMA, NO, and NT lie in the appropriate range, suggesting that Ti3C2S2 may be a promising material for sensing applications.

Next, we further investigated how sensitive Ti3C2S2 is to ambient gases, including CO2, H2O, N2, and O2. Their adsorption energies and configurations are depicted in Figure 2b and Figure S3, respectively. We find that these ambient gases weakly adsorb on the substrate with adsorption energies less negative than −0.3 eV and adsorption distances ranging from 2.697 to 3.349 Å. Such weak interactions suggest negligible interference in the presence of the N-containing gas molecules (e.g., TMA, NO, and NT), resulting in high selectivity. Our calculated results are in good agreement with previous DFT-PBE calculations that the S-terminated MXene is inactive to normal atmospheric gases.28,29

To evaluate the sensing performance of Ti3C2S2 relative to other 2D MXenes, we calculated the adsorption energies of the target gases on conventional O-terminated Ti3C2 materials. It should be noted that metallic Ti3C2O2 has been successfully utilized in well-known sensing devices to effectively detect chemical gas molecules.22,53−55 As illustrated in Figures S4 and S5, the adsorption configurations of gas molecules on Ti3C2O2 are similar to those on Ti3C2S2. However, for the NH3 adsorption, the nitrogen atom of NH3 reorients itself downward to the substrates, in contrast to its adsorption on the Ti3C2S2 system. Notably, almost all adsorption energies in the Ti3C2O2 system are more negative than those in the Ti3C2S2 system, with larger charge transfer (0.030 to 0.366 e–) and shorter binding distance (2.06 to 2.91 Å), as summarized in Table S1. Our calculated gas adsorptions on Ti3C2O2 are mostly consistent with previous DFT calculations.27,56 This indicates that the different functional groups on Ti3C2 affect the sensing performance.27 In the presence of O surface termination, the Lewis-acid Ti layers with vacant 3d-orbitals are more prone to interact with the electron-rich N atom due to the short Ti–O bond (1.971 Å) that exposes Ti atoms to the surface. On the contrary, Ti3C2S2 exhibits a longer Ti–S bonds (2.382 Å), resulting in the Ti layer being further from the surface. It is seen in the NO adsorbed system that the bond distance between the nearest Ti atom and the N atom in the Ti3C2O2 system (3.021 Å) is shorter than that in Ti3C2S2 system (3.524 Å), leading to stronger adsorption. This suggests that the sensing performance of the conventional Ti3C2O2 sensor is superior to that of the proposed Ti3C2S2 sensor in binding N-containing gas molecules. Hence, further improvements will be required to achieve high-sensing performance in Ti3C2S2.

Effect of Strain and External Electric Field on the Selectivity of Ti3C2S2

To enhance the adsorptivity of the sensing material, appropriate modifications can be employed through various means, including strain engineering and applying an external electric field.57−59 Such engineering techniques can effectively modulate the molecule adsorption behaviors. Previous DFT studies suggested stronger binding of NH3 on the Zr3C2O2 by applying strains,51 which may offer better control over adsorption capabilities than metal-doping strategies.60−62 In addition, an experimental study demonstrated that the amount of charge transferred between the adsorbent and the substrate depends on the magnitude of the electric field, resulting in increased device sensitivity.58 Employing an electric field measurement setup remains feasible for low-cost applications, particularly when combined with affordable sensing materials.63,64 Thus, it is worthwhile to study the change in the gas responses to the monolayer Ti3C2S2 under a biaxial tensile strain and an external electric field.

For the NO adsorption, its adsorption energy slightly changes upon applying strain, with the Eads reaching −0.719 eV at ε = 5%. A strain-induced Ti3C2S2 possesses a high selectivity toward NOx species with moderate adsorption strength. Moreover, at a strain of ε = 4%, Ti3C2S2 could differentiate between NO2 and NH3 due to its specific selectivity toward NO2.

Next, we examined the role of a normal direction electric field (|E⃗|) at a magnitude of −0.6 to 0.6 V/Å, where the positive (negative) value represents its upward (downward) direction with respect to the substrate. As illustrated in Figure 3c, the adsorption energies depend on the magnitude and direction of the applied electric field. Almost all adsorbed molecules exhibit stronger interactions when subjected to the field in the +z direction. Remarkably, a strong electric field of 0.6 V/Å facilitates NH3 adsorption with Eads = −0.370 eV, however, it leads to excessively strong binding of NT and TMA with adsorption energies more negative than −1 eV.

In contrast, an electric field in the +z direction weakens NO2 adsorption with Eads = −0.177 eV at |E⃗| = 0.6 V/Å. This could be due to its negligible charge transfer upon adsorption. We notice that the direction of the charge migration is opposite of the direction of the applied electric field, hence, the external electric field should be applied in a direction opposite to the preferential direction of charge transfer.

Electronic Structures of Ti3C2S2 upon Gas Adsorption

Besides selectivity, effective sensing materials must possess high sensitivity. The electrical conductance of the sensing materials should be significantly altered to generate a measurable signal upon gas adsorption, where the conductance could be either increased or decreased depending on the detected gas species and the material’s intrinsic properties.66 Herein, we analyzed the change in electronic properties of Ti3C2S2 via DOS analysis, as depicted in Figure 4a–h. The integrated number of electrons (ne) near the EF in the range of thermal energy (EF ± 0.026 eV) was also calculated to approximate the alteration of the number of electrons, as shown in Figure 4g.

The blue regions and gray lines represent the total DOS of the substrate before and after gas exposure. (i) The values of the corresponding integrated DOS. (j) The work functions upon gas adsorption on a monolayer of Ti3C2S2. The red dash lines in (i) and (j) represent the integrated DOS and work function of the bare substrates, respectively.

Our computation reveals that gas adsorption on pristine Ti3C2S2 results in a significant change in the DOS for NT, NO, and TMA molecules, with an integrated ne of 2.353, 2.126, and 2.428, respectively. Such values are slightly different from the integrated DOS of the bare substrate (2.216 e–) with Δne in the order of 4.06–9.57%. On the other hand, the DOSs of the substrate do not significantly alter upon adsorption of NH3 and NO, with a small Δne within 0.27%. This is consistent with their weaker adsorption energies. In addition, due to the intrinsic metallic character of Ti3C2S2, the change in DOS of the substrate is not obvious after gas adsorption. This is because there is weak orbital hybridization of the electronic states between the adsorbent and the adsorbate although their adsorption strength can be moderate.51,67

The change in electronic structures is dramatically improved by introducing an in-plane strain and an external electric field. As shown in Figure 4f,g, at a strain of 5%, the total DOS is increased (decreased) around the Fermi level when binding with NO (NO2), yielding an integrated DOS difference of 12.67% (5.33%). This suggests that the sensitivity of the induced-strain sensor substantially increased to 212.1% and 31.2% for NO and NO2, respectively, compared with their sensitivities on pristine Ti3C2S2 (Figure 4i). In the presence of an electric field, the total DOS of the adsorbed NH3 system is changed where the difference of the integrated DOS reaches 5.31% which is larger than that of its adsorption on pristine Ti3C2S2 (0.04%).

We further examined the sensitivity by analyzing the work function of the substrate. The change in work function represents the probability of electron emission from the Fermi level to the vacuum state, generating a detectable signal after gas exposure.68 In this work, we calculated the average electrostatic potentials of Ti3C2S2 with and without gas adsorption as detailed in Figure S6. Then, the corresponding work functions were calculated by Φ = Evac - EF, where Evac and EF are the energy of a stationary electron at the vacuum level and the highest occupancy level of a ground state electron at the Fermi level, respectively.

As shown in Figure 4j, the trend of the work function is similar to the integrated DOS. There is an obvious alteration for NT, NO, and TMA adsorption on pristine Ti3C2S2, whereas those for NO and NO2 are barely different, suggesting weak sensitivity. However, their change in work function is improved with the help of applied strain, enhancing its sensitivity. Note that the calculated work function with the effect of an external electric field was not observed as we cannot define the vacuum level of the system. Nevertheless, an experimental study reported that the introduction of an electric field can enhance the sensitivity of the sensing materials.58

The reusability of a sensing material can be determined by its recovery time (τ) - the duration required for the sensor to return to its baseline state after being exposed to a target gas. Too short a recovery time corresponds with poor adsorptivity, making it difficult to recognize the detectable response, whereas too long a recovery time could be attributed to challenging desorption due to strong adsorption. Note that we could not calculate the adsorption density of a gas when an external electric field is present, as the obtained Morse potential and the eigenvalues are different. However, we suggest that the stronger the adsorption, the higher the value of adsorption density which affects the large electrical response.

Electronic Transmission and Sensing Performance

The electronic transport properties of the Ti3C2S2 nanodevices, as shown in Figure 6a, were investigated. We present the transmission spectra in Figure 6b–d, demonstrating insights into the probability for an incoming electron being transferred from the left to the right electrode through the scattering region. The transmission spectra were analyzed for both pristine and gas-exposed Ti3C2S2 under exposure to TMA, NT, and NO gases. These gases were selected based on their favorable adsorptive interactions as discussed in the previous section. The transmission spectra demonstrate how gas adsorption alters the electronic transport properties, offering valuable information about the material’s potential as a gas sensor.

(b) Transmission spectra of Ti3C2S2 with adsorbed TMA, (c) NT, (d) NO, and (e) NO at a strain ε = 5%, compared to pristine Ti3C2S2. (f) Corresponding sensitivity as a function of energy. (g) Sensitivity histogram for two gate voltages: Vg = −0.04 V and Vg = +0.60 V.

As seen in Figure 6b and 6c, the transmission within the range −0.5 eV < E-EF < 0.5 eV shows only slight changes for TMA and NT compared to the pristine system. In contrast, NO exhibits a more pronounced drop across a broader energy range from −2 to 2 eV, as shown in Figure 6d. This distinct transmission profile allows for clear differentiation between NO and TMA or NT. Despite the strong binding energies of NO, NT and TMA, the transmission drop for NO reflects its different influence on the electronic transport properties.

When a strain of ε = 5% is applied to the nanodevices, remarkable changes in the electronic properties become apparent, including a consistent drop in transmission throughout the energy range from −2 to 2 eV compared to the nonstrained case [see Figure 6e]. As a result, the conductance decreases as strain is applied. For instance, when measured at the Fermi level, the conductance of the unstrained nanodevice measures 9.75G0 (where G0 is the conductance quantum), but in the strained device, it drops to 6.25G0 representing a reduction in conductance of approximately 35.90%. Additionally, when NO gas is adsorbed, the transmission decreases, indicating that NO adsorption reduces conductance in both strained and unstrained nanodevices.

We compare the transmission sensitivity, influenced by external gate voltages for TMA, NT, and NO in Figure 6f. The variations in sensitivity indicate the capability to differentiate among these gases. Particularly, the sensitivity to NO is higher than for TMA and NT, indicating its potential for selective detection. Although TMA and NT can bind to the substrate, this does not significantly affect the changes in sensitivity. Previous computational studies have indicated that adsorption energy may not directly correlate with the sensitivity of the sensor.75 This implies that the higher sensitivity observed for NO detection cannot be solely attributed to its adsorption energy on the MXene. Instead, the sensitivity is likely influenced by the specific electronic interactions between the adsorbed gas molecules and the MXene, which modulate the charge transport characteristics.

Figure 6g presents a sensitivity histogram for two gate voltages: Vg = −0.04 V and Vg = +0.60 V (as indicated by dashed lines in Figure 6f), showing the Ti3C2S2 MXene device’s ability to detect NO effectively. At Vg = −0.04 V, sensitivity values are 0.18% for TMA, 0.001% for NT, 2.34% for NO without strain, and 12.20% for NO with strain. At Vg = +0.60 V, sensitivity ranges from 4.75% for TMA to 18.73% for NO, with strain slightly reducing sensitivity to 16.59%. These results demonstrate the device’s capacity to distinguish between gases at different gate voltages. At Vg = −0.04 V, the device can differentiate NO from TMA and NT, though the sensitivity to NO is relatively low. However, applying strain significantly enhances sensitivity. At Vg = +0.60 V, NO is easily distinguishable without strain, as its sensitivity is sufficiently high. This suggests that, depending on the gate voltage, strain may not always be necessary for effective NO detection, offering flexibility in practical applications.

Conclusions

In this work, we used first-principles computations to study the gas-sensing ability of Ti3C2S2 MXene toward the targeted N-containing gases including NH3, NO, NO2, trimethylamine (TMA), and nicotine (NT). Through a combination of DFT calculations, and statistical thermodynamic modeling, we find that TMA, NO, and NT exhibit selective adsorption on the Ti3C2S2 monolayer, with moderately strong adsorption energies ranging from −0.49 to −0.61 eV. Notably, these adsorption strengths surpass those of ambient gases, thus reducing environmental noise interference. Our analysis of the calculated DOS, charge distribution, and work function reveals strong sensitivity to TMA, NO, and NT, while NO2 and NH3 adsorption has minimal impact on the material’s electronic structure due to weak physisorption. Employing strain engineering and electric field on Ti3C2S2 enhances the selectivity and sensitivity of NO2 and NH3 detection. These modifications induce significant changes in the DOS and work function, resulting in detectable signals in the sensing device. Moreover, the calculated recovery times for TMA, NO, and NT fall within milliseconds, indicating high reusability, while those for NO2 and NH3 are promising upon applying strain and electric field. The estimated adsorption densities of these N-containing gases display high gas coverage even at low concentrations and room temperature. Using the NEGF approach, we evaluated the sensitivities of a prototypical Ti3C2S2 MXene device toward TMA, NO, and NT molecules, demonstrating that it is indeed possible to reliably detect NO. Overall, our study underscores the promising sensing properties of S-terminated MXene and proposes that strategies such as mechanical strain, electric field application, and gate voltage tuning could be used to obtain effective sensing materials for the detection of these N-containing gas molecules.
