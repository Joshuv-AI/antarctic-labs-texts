Public and health care provider attitudes, understanding, and behaviors regarding emergency use authorizations: a scoping literature review
Authors: Caroline Rains, Kathryn J. Aikin, Helen W. Sullivan, Bridget J. Kelly, Shirley Liu, Camara Wooten, Jenna Brophy, Susana Peinado, Lauren McCormack, Sandra Crouse Quinn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnder Sect. 564 of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, when the US Department of Health and Human Services Secretary issues a declaration based on one of four types of determinations, FDA may authorize an unapproved product or unapproved uses of an approved product for emergency use. It is unclear how much the public and health care providers understand about emergency use authorizations and how that understanding influences perceptions or behavioral intentions. The current study aimed to explore consumer and health care provider perceptions of emergency use authorizations and how promotion of emergency use authorizations has been investigated in the literature.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review via PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and CINAHL for original research published in English from January 1, 2012, through November 10, 2022. We conducted surveillance of the literature through March 2023.ResultsWe identified 13 studies reported in 14 publications that addressed public and health care provider attitudes, understanding, and behaviors with regard to products authorized under an emergency use authorization. We did not identify any articles describing research regarding the promotion of emergency use authorization products. Findings suggest gaps in understanding of emergency use authorizations, both for the public and health care providers. Findings also suggest the public and health care providers have concerns about safety or the lack of full FDA approval that may contribute to decreased willingness to receive treatments and vaccines under an emergency use authorization.ConclusionsFuture research should explore how emergency use authorizations have been promoted and how that promotion impacts public and health care provider perceptions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20234-0.

Background

For a prescription drug to receive US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval, FDA must determine, among other things, whether clinical data submitted by the sponsor of an investigational drug product demonstrates that there is substantial evidence of effectiveness and that the product’s benefits outweigh its known and potential risks. The prescription drug development process typically involves several phases of pre-clinical and clinical research [1]. Vaccines and some medical devices typically follow similarly thorough processes of testing and review before being approved [2, 3]. However, during times of emergency, and when the appropriate determination and declaration under Sect. 564 of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act have been issued, FDA may authorize the emergency use of unapproved medical products and unapproved uses of approved products through “emergency use authorization” (EUA) [4]. Importantly, an EUA still requires a careful consideration of the balance of potential risks and benefits based on the data available [5, 6]. Under Sect. 564 of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act [5], EUAs apply specifically to “unapproved medical products or unapproved uses of approved medical products to be used in an emergency to diagnose, treat, or prevent serious or life-threatening disease or conditions caused by [chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear] threat agents when certain criteria are met, including there are not adequate, approved, and available alternatives” [7].

The EUA authority has been used in response to multiple public health crises, including Ebola virus disease, Enterovirus D68, avian influenza A, Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus, nerve agents, mpox (formerly known as monkeypox), Zika virus, and most recently, SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19 [8]. Despite this long history, little is known about how the public and health care providers perceive the EUA process or products authorized under EUAs.

One of the few studies conducted on the topic prior to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) examined the public’s willingness to accept an unapproved vaccine and a drug authorized under EUA during the H1N1 pandemic in 2009 [9]. The nationally representative survey conducted by Quinn et al. (n = 1,543) revealed that only 9% of respondents were willing to accept a hypothetical vaccine that was “new but not yet approved,” and 49% of respondents would be very or extremely worried if offered a vaccine that was not yet approved by FDA. When asked about taking a drug or a vaccine for influenza that was offered under the emergency use authorization rule, more than half of respondents indicated they would be willing to take the drug or vaccine authorized under EUA if it were accompanied by a fact sheet or dispensed by their healthcare provider (HCP) or a public health professional. Less than 15% of participants reported they would take the drug or vaccine if they did not receive a fact sheet or if it were dispensed by a non-health professional, suggesting that the source of information about the EUA may be an important influence on attitudes toward the product. FDA has generally required recipients of EUA products and HCPs be provided with a fact sheet that contains information about the product prior to administration, where practicable [6, 10], though in certain circumstances the context of the emergency may warrant administration of the product first. Research suggests health literacy may be an important factor to consider in the development of these fact sheets [9].

In addition to fact sheets and information from HCPs, consumers and providers may be influenced by promotional communications about EUA products. Promotion of EUA products is allowed, but according to FDA guidance, “conditions (e.g., limitations) may be placed on advertisements and other promotional descriptive printed matter (e.g., press releases issued by the EUA sponsor) relating to the use of an EUA product, such as requirements applicable to prescription drugs under Sect. 502(n) and requirements applicable to restricted devices under Sect. 502(r)”; for example, that “the major statement relating to side effects and contraindications shall be presented in a clear, conspicuous and neutral manner” [6]. It is essential for FDA to understand what is known about the promotion of EUA products to date to help inform limitations or conditions on the promotion of these products. This literature review aims to understand the research on public and HCP attitudes, understanding, and behaviors regarding EUA products and any research examining the promotion of EUA products.

Methods

We conducted a scoping literature review of published studies. Scoping reviews use systematic methods to determine the coverage and focus of a body of literature and are particularly useful for examining emerging evidence [11]. The protocol for this scoping review was registered on AsPredicted.org (registration #109329).

We developed three research questions (RQs) to guide the review:RQ 1: What are the public attitudes, understanding, and behaviors with regard to products authorized under an EUA (including drugs, medical devices, medical tests, and vaccines)?RQ 2: What are health care provider attitudes, understanding, and behaviors with regard to products authorized under an EUA (including drugs, medical devices, medical tests, and vaccines)?RQ 3: To what extent has the promotion of EUA products been empirically investigated with the public and health care providers?

Literature search strategy

We searched MEDLINE via PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and CINAHL for original research published in English from January 1, 2012, through November 10, 2022. We conducted surveillance of the literature through March 2023. We restricted our search to research with a US-based sample, published from 2012 to capture EUAs issued for Zika virus, Ebola virus, and COVID-19. We used search terms related to EUA products, promotion, knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors. The complete search strategy is available in Appendix 1, Additional file 1. We supplemented this literature search with a grey literature search by entering each category of search terms into Google and reviewing the first 10 pages of results. We also reviewed reference lists of all included articles.

Study selection

We selected studies conducted among HCPs or the public that included outcomes on understanding, perceptions, and behaviors related to EUA product risks and benefits. We used a broad definition of HCPs to capture a wider range of literature. Eligible study designs were experimental (controlled or uncontrolled), surveys, and qualitative research.

We reviewed references using DistillerSR, [12] a web-based systematic review software that allows review teams to upload database records, create screening and data abstraction forms, and track the flow of references through the review phases. Before initiating the screening, we conducted a pilot review of a subset of articles to ensure a shared understanding of screening criteria and consistency in decisions. We then independently reviewed titles/abstracts and full-text articles. Each reference was independently screened by two reviewers. A senior reviewer resolved all disagreements.

Data abstraction

For articles meeting the inclusion criteria, one reviewer abstracted data into a structured form in DistillerSR and a second reviewer performed a quality control review to ensure all data were accurate and complete. We abstracted information on study characteristics, methods, and outcomes relevant to the RQs. Because this is a scoping review, we did not conduct risk of bias assessments, evaluate strength of evidence, or quantitatively synthesize results.

Results

Search results

We screened 1270 unique citations and identified 13 studies reported in 14 publications for inclusion in this review. One study reported results in two separate publications. Seven studies addressed RQ 1, six studies addressed RQ 2, and no studies addressed RQ 3 (Fig. 1). Study characteristics

We identified seven studies reported in eight publications that addressed RQ 1 [13–20]. An overview of the study characteristics is presented in Table 1. Five studies focused on COVID-19 vaccines, [13–18] one focused on H1N1 and the drug peramivir, [20] and one developed two hypothetical scenarios (one on respiratory virus and one on radiation exposure) [19]. Six of the studies sampled a general population of adults [13, 15–20] and one surveyed female guardians of children aged 1 to 4 years [14]. Articles included a range of racial and ethnic groups and ages. Six studies used online surveys through panel vendors [13–18, 20] and one used intercept interviews in the Washington, DC, metropolitan area [19]. Only one study was nationally representative, [20] whereas the other six used convenience sampling [13–19]. Sample size ranged from 148 to 2,042. Public understanding

One study reported on understanding of EUAs among the public. Liu et al. [19] examined 148 respondents’ understanding of regulatory terms used to communicate about medical countermeasures in public health emergencies through hypothetical scenarios (respiratory virus and radiation exposure) in 2015–2016. Although respondents reported that they understood the messages provided in fact sheets, they demonstrated poor understanding of the terminology used in the messages for terms such as “unapproved.” On average, respondents did not know the meaning of a word more than 10% of the time. When asked to say what came to mind after hearing the term “emergency use authorization,” participants had mixed responses reflecting a range of emotions. Words and phrases mentioned in their responses included “concerned,” “desperate,” “fast-track approval,” “fear,” “good,” “helpful,” “important,” “last resort,” “over-controlling,” “risky,” “rushing,” “scary,” “sneaky,” “soothing,” “suspicious,” “unapproved,” and “unsure.”

Public attitudes

Three studies reported on the public’s attitudes about products under an EUA or described as “experimental” [13, 14, 20]. Across all three studies, respondents’ responses reflected concerns about safety. Fisher et al. [14] conducted a survey in April–May 2022 with 411 parents of children aged 1 to 4 years and examined reasons for their intention not to vaccinate their child against COVID-19. FDA authorized COVID-19 vaccines under EUA in children aged 6 months to 4 years in June 2022. Fisher et al. found that the most frequent concern among parents who would definitely not, probably not, or were unsure about vaccinating their child under “emergency approval” was vaccine safety [14]. One resistant parent noted, “This vaccine has been approved for emergency purposes only. It’s the only vaccine in history who’s [sic] been used without long term testing. I wouldn’t subject my children to this kind of thing” [14]. Even among parents who indicated they probably or definitely would vaccinate their child, 5.5% mentioned safety concerns and indicated worry about the rapid development and “emergency authorization of the vaccine” [14]. A nationally representative survey of 2,042 respondents in 2010 by Quinn et al. [20] examined responses to a statement taken from an FDA fact sheet about peramivir, a drug that was prescribed for those infected with severe cases of H1N1 influenza in 2009. The statement described peramivir as “experimental because it is still being studied” and explained that “it is not approved by FDA for use in the United States.” The researchers examined respondents’ attitudes about peramivir’s safety, effectiveness, side effects, and experimental nature. White participants had greater concerns about the safety of peramivir than both Black and Hispanic participants, but no other significant group differences were observed.

The third study on attitudes, by Chu et al., [13] explored how respondents’ ideology and support for free markets influenced assessment of risks and mitigation policies. Participants viewed either a video that emphasized a government-regulatory solution to the pandemic or a market-driven solution. They found that respondents who favored free-market policies indicated more support for EUA of COVID-19 treatment and vaccines, regardless of which video they watched [13]. Respondents who reported being liberal on political, social, and economic issues and those who favored free-market policies less were less likely to support EUAs after watching a video that highlighted market-oriented solutions, as compared with the group that watched the video highlighting government-regulatory solutions to COVID-19. Video messaging did not affect support for an EUA for respondents who reported being conservative [13].

In addition to the three studies that reported on the public’s attitudes about products under an EUA, one study reported on trust in the government and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) when it comes to “emergency medications/vaccinations.” The Liu et al. study [19] of hypothetical scenarios, conducted with intercept interviews in 2015–2016 (n = 148), found that on a scale of 1 to 10, White respondents had higher levels of trust in the government (6.47) than Hispanic (5.85) and African American (5.06) respondents (P = 0.047). Similarly, White respondents had higher levels of trust in CDC (7.65) than Hispanic (6.18) and African American (6.17) respondents (P = 0.017).

Public behavioral intentions and willingness

Several studies examined willingness to take an EUA product. Three studies examined willingness to receive an authorized COVID-19 vaccine, [15–18] one examined willingness to take peramivir, [20] and one examined intention to vaccinate a child against COVID-19 [14]. Across studies, having an EUA reduced intention to use or take a vaccine or drug, with some differences by subgroups. Guidry et al. [15] surveyed 788 individuals in July 2020, prior to the approval or authorization of any COVID-19 vaccine, and found that although 60% of respondents expressed willingness to receive a future COVID-19 vaccine, only 25% of respondents said they would be willing or definitely willing to receive the vaccine under an EUA. Kreps et al. [18] found that an EUA decreased the likelihood of vaccination by 7% compared with a full FDA approval among a convenience sample of 1,971 respondents in an October 2020 survey. Further, among those who believed vaccines generally are extremely safe (on a scale from not at all safe to extremely safe), the EUA decreased willingness to vaccinate by 11%, with all other characteristics being held equal [18]. In the second study about COVID-19 vaccines, Kreps et al. [16–18]conducted a choice-based conjoint analysis with 1,096 respondents in July 2020 and found that an EUA was associated with a lower probability of choosing a vaccine compared with a full FDA approval. Similarly, an EUA was associated with a decrease in the marginal mean willingness to receive a vaccine from 0.57 under full FDA approval to 0.55 under an EUA, meaning that when comparing the EUA and full FDA approval, respondent willingness to receive a vaccine was slightly lower when the vaccine was under an EUA versus when it had full FDA approval, averaged across the combinations of other vaccine attribute values (e.g., efficacy, adverse effects, origin country) [16, 17].

Kreps et al. [16, 17] and Guidry et al. [15] also examined willingness to vaccinate by subgroups [15–17]. Kreps et al. [16, 17] found that an EUA had a stronger adverse effect on willingness to vaccinate among women and older respondents (reduced acceptance by ~ 4% in both subgroups) [16, 17]. Guidry et al. [15] also found that older respondents were less willing to get a vaccine under EUA (P < 0.05) and that Black respondents were less willing to get a vaccine under EUA than their White counterparts (P < 0.05) [15].

Quinn et al. [20] examined adults’ willingness to take peramivir when it was described as “experimental” in a 2010 nationally representative survey of 2,042 individuals. They found that 48% of respondents indicated they would probably or definitely take the drug [20]. The use of the term “experimental” to describe the drug decreased willingness to take the drug among Black participants and White participants when presented with different conditions (e.g., if hospitalized, if severely ill) [20]. Further, trust in government was a predictor of willingness for Black and Hispanic participants, and trust in FDA was a predictor of willingness for all groups [20]. The Fisher et al. [14] survey of 411 female guardians of children aged 1 to 4 years examined intention to vaccinate their child against COVID-19 under “emergency approval” in April–May 2022. They found that about half of respondents (46%) would definitely not or probably not vaccinate, 23% were unsure, and 31% would definitely or probably vaccinate their child against COVID-19 under “emergency approval” [14].

RQ 2:

What are health care provider attitudes, understanding, and behaviors with regard to products authorized under an EUA (including drugs, medical devices, medical tests, and vaccines)?

Study characteristics

We identified six studies that addressed RQ 2 [21–26]. An overview of the study characteristics is presented in Table 3. All studies focused on the COVID-19 vaccine. Five used surveys and employed convenience sampling, [21–25] while one conducted qualitative interviews and used purposeful sampling of a variety of HCPs in different practice settings from states with the highest COVID-19 infection rates at the time of the study (interviews conducted in July 2021) [26]. Study samples included occupational therapy professionals and students [21]; health care personnel, such as clinical and nonclinical staff, researchers, and trainees [25]; health care professionals and students [23]; emergency department physicians [22]; emergency department residents and medical students [24]; and physicians and nurse practitioners involved in referring and prescribing treatment to COVID-19 outpatients [26]. Three studies were published only in conference abstracts or short papers and did not report detailed demographic characteristics of their samples [22–24]. In two of the three studies that reported sample demographic characteristics, [21, 25] the samples skewed female and the average age was between 41 and 45 years. In the other study, the study recruited half female and half male respondents [26]. For the one study that reported race/ethnicity data, respondents were primarily White (85%) and not of Latinx/Hispanic origin (97%) [25]. Sample sizes ranged from 20 to 4,537. Health care provider understanding

One study explored understanding. Gonzales et al. [24] examined knowledge of 119 health care professionals and students within a health sciences university and found that most respondents (81.5%) were knowledgeable about the EUA status of the COVID-19 vaccines [24]. The study did not provide information on dates of data collection or any further details on this result.

Health care provider attitudes

Three studies examined provider attitudes and found that some respondents had concerns about the authorization status of the COVID-19 vaccines [21, 25, 26]. Cage et al. [21] conducted a survey of 114 occupational therapy professionals and students and found that most participants (79%) agreed they felt comfortable with the current level of authorization available for COVID-19 vaccines in early 2021. The Shaw et al. [25] study of 4,537 health care personnel (conducted in February–March 2021) found that among the 1,334 respondents who expressed concern about COVID-19 vaccines, a small number of respondents [15] expressed concern that the vaccines were under EUA and not fully approved by FDA. A greater percentage of respondents who chose not to get the COVID-19 vaccine expressed concern about EUA status (3.9%) than participants who had not yet decided whether to get the vaccine (1.1%) or those who had or planned to receive the vaccine (0.7%) [25]. Williams et al. [26] conducted qualitative interviews with 20 HCPs (date of data collection not reported) and found that nearly half of participants had questions about the safety and efficacy of treatments for COVID-19 because they were new and not fully approved [26]. Several participants noted that they did not have confidence in the efficacy of the treatments or have enough time to become familiar with the clinical evidence of the treatments, which influenced how they represented the treatment to patients and contributed to hesitancy in referring patients for treatment [26]. A few participants noted that the EUA status itself was a barrier [26]. However, many participants said that their initial skepticism was addressed by knowledge exchange with expert colleagues [26].

Health care provider behavioral intentions, willingness, and behaviors

Three studies examined providers’ behavioral intentions and actual behaviors for receiving a COVID-19 vaccine [21–23, 25]. A survey of 187 emergency department physicians by Cronin et al. [22] reported that 68% of respondents would be willing to receive a COVID-19 vaccine under an EUA, whereas 23% would not (date of data collection not reported). Another survey by Cronin et al. [23] of 156 emergency department residents and medical students reported that 33% of respondents somewhat agreed that they would receive a COVID-19 vaccine if one became available in the next 6 months under EUA. The study did not report how the remaining 67% of medical residents and students responded or provide dates of data collection. Shaw et al. [25] conducted a survey of 4,537 health care personnel in February–March 2021 and found that most respondents had received the COVID-19 vaccine (80%) or intended to get vaccinated (8%). This was higher than the percentage of respondents who reported that they intended to get vaccinated when assessed in December 2020 before the vaccine was available (57%). Study authors suggest that this difference may be because hesitant health care personnel became more receptive to vaccination as efficacy and safety data became available [25]. They also note that workplace requirements can help increase vaccine uptake [25].

RQ 3:

To what extent has the promotion of EUA products been empirically investigated with the public and health care providers?

We did not identify any studies that address RQ 3.

Discussion

This scoping review investigated the evidence on public and HCP attitudes, understanding, and behaviors with regard to products authorized under an EUA, as well as the extent to which the promotion of EUA products has been empirically investigated. Except for two studies investigating H1N1 and hypothetical conditions, [19, 20] the remaining evidence was published since 2020 and focuses on COVID-19.

This review found that members of the public expressed variable levels of concern regarding EUA products, particularly about the safety of drugs or vaccines under an EUA for themselves or for their children. Members of the public also demonstrated limited understanding about the meaning of an EUA as well as terminology used in messages to communicate information about EUAs, with some respondents responding negatively to and expressing confusion about the meaning of certain words. Further, EUA status tended to decrease behavioral intentions to receive a vaccine or take a drug, with participants across several studies reporting they would be less likely to receive or take a product under an EUA compared to a product without mention of status or a product with full FDA approval (e.g., future COVID-19 vaccine, vaccination with full FDA authorization). In addition, our review found that trust in the government was an important factor influencing attitudes toward EUAs in a couple studies, with greater trust in the government predicting willingness to take an EUA product for Black and Hispanic participants and greater trust in FDA predicting willingness to take an EUA product for Black, Hispanic, and White participants. We also identified some differences by race and ethnicity in level of trust in the government when it comes to emergency medications/vaccinations.

Among HCPs, most respondents were knowledgeable about the authorization status of the COVID-19 vaccines. In addition, most respondents reported being comfortable with the level of authorization available for COVID-19 vaccines in early 2021. Some respondents, however, expressed concerns about the COVID-19 vaccine, including that the vaccine was new, under EUA, or not fully approved by FDA. Further, some HCPs reported not having enough time to become familiar with the clinical evidence of COVID-19 monoclonal antibodies which affected how they represented the treatment to patients. However, exchanging knowledge with expert colleagues helped to address concerns for many of those respondents. HCPs generally reported willingness to receive a COVID-19 vaccine under an EUA, and one study showed that the number of respondents who were vaccinated in February–March 2021 was higher than the number of respondents who reported they intended to get vaccinated when surveyed in December 2020.

Limitations of body of evidence

Eligible studies conducted with providers were heterogenous in terms of the types of providers surveyed, making it difficult to synthesize findings. The measures reported by the studies also varied widely, highlighting the complexity of factors that influence perceptions and further limiting our ability to synthesize results across studies. In addition, three of the five studies included for RQ 2 were conference abstracts or short papers [22–24] and were limited in describing the population demographics, methods, and findings. Further, most studies were conducted with convenience samples and thus do not provide representative results. The body of evidence available on this topic is also limited in part because of the lack of studies that reported outcomes directly related to EUA product risks and benefits. For example, studies more commonly reported reasons for COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy, but they did not explicitly measure elements related to EUAs. In studies of barriers and facilitators to COVID-19 vaccination, the speed with which the vaccine was developed is often mentioned, while the type of marketing authorization is not explicitly mentioned in those studies [27–29].

Another limitation is that some of the researchers used the terms “approval” and “authorization” interchangeably, which may have implications for participant understanding of the distinction. In one study [14], a product authorized under an EUA was described as under “emergency approval,” although this is not a regulatory term used by FDA. To truly understand consumer and HCP perceptions of terminology describing EUA, it is important to understand the perceptions and connotations of the specific terms used in communications and to ensure they are defined in accordance with FDA guidance.

Implications for practice

Although this review identified some evidence about providers’ attitudes, understanding, and behaviors related to EUA products, there was limited information from one study on their perceptions as it related to communication and interactions with patients. A few study participants indicated that their skepticism about safety and efficacy of treatments for COVID-19 with an EUA product likely contributes to hesitancy in referring patients for treatment [26]. Among consumers, using EUA to describe the vaccine generally decreased respondents’ willingness to receive a vaccine compared with a vaccine not under an EUA. Outside of their own skepticism, providers may face challenges during conversations with patients about treatment options and vaccines available under an EUA. One possible recommendation to address challenging conversations with patients is to consider re-organizing or adding to the information included in required fact sheets distributed to HCPs prescribing products issued under EUAs. For example, those fact sheets might include FAQs to help HCPs better answer patient questions about the EUA mechanism and the differences between an EUA product and a product with full FDA approval. The literature also identified differences in vaccine behaviors based on provider role, so it may be useful to target educational materials to different types of providers [25].

In addition, the findings highlight the persistent need for attention to health literacy and building trust with audiences. Public health leaders should ensure they are using clear communication [30] and plain language principles [31] in health communication. While best practices in communication may also suggest testing terminology and messages with audiences before disseminating products like fact sheets [32], this may not be practicable during a public health emergency. Quinn et al. [32] note that active anti-vaccine misinformation, in addition to historical research abuses and racism in health care, contribute significantly to reluctance among Americans to receive vaccines. Therefore, health communications from the government, public health agencies, and manufacturers must reflect the social context in which they will be delivered. Ultimately, EUA messages should be immediate, culturally competent, and transparent to create trust in government, in providers, and in the authorization process itself.

Future research needs

Overall, this is a small body of literature, and these questions may deserve exploration with larger, more representative samples of both consumers and HCPs. In most cases, the question about a product’s EUA status was just one among various other topics asked about in the study. Most surveys about vaccine preferences use generic wording about vaccination and do not provide sufficient context or information about the specific attributes of the vaccine [17]. Questions should instead focus on vaccination when it is conditional on specific elements of the vaccine (e.g., efficacy, side effects, EUA status, exposure to social media or other information) to provide a better understanding of the factors that contribute to hesitancy [17]. Future research could explore understanding and perceptions of EUAs with more depth and breadth, including specific beliefs driving attitudes, current provider-patient communication practices regarding EUAs, outstanding questions about EUAs, and information needs. Exploration of information needs might include assessing awareness and utilization of existing materials developed for HCPs, such as NIH guidelines or EUA-specific FAQs developed by FDA. This would provide valuable insight into critical levers and approaches to help address public reluctance or refusal as well as the kind of information that could be used in future communications to address lack of knowledge about the meaning of an EUA.

Research is also needed to investigate how EUA products are marketed and promoted and how their promotion contributes to public and provider awareness, understanding, and perception of the EUA status of products.

Conclusions

Findings suggest gaps in understanding of EUAs, both for the public and HCPs. Findings also suggest that people have concerns about EUA status, particularly regarding safety or the lack of full FDA approval, which may contribute to decreased behavioral intentions to receive treatments and vaccines under an EUA. Future research could examine in more detail how EUAs and the promotion of EUA products are understood and perceived to better address reluctance, negative attitudes, and gaps in understanding.
