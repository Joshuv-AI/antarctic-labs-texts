Advanced Imaging Preparation for Endoscopic Mitral Valve Surgery—Analysis of Circumflex Anatomy and Associated Procedural Risks
Authors: Jacqueline Kruse, Marwan Hamiko, Ömür Akhavuz, Jonathan Hobbach, Eissa Alaj, Kaveh Eghbalzadeh, Sebastian Zimmer, Marcel Weber, Daniel Kütting, Ali El-Sayed Ahmad, Farhad Bakhtiary, Miriam Silaschi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIatrogenic injury to the circumflex artery (CX) during mitral valve (MV) surgery occurs in 0.3%-1.8% of cases. This study analysed the CX’s anatomical relationship to the mitral annulus using advanced imaging to predict peri-operative risk in patients undergoing endoscopic MV surgery.MethodsA CT analysis using 3mensio software was performed in 315 patients (2019-2023) undergoing minimally invasive MV surgery. A prediction index (PI) for CX injury was calculated to identify at-risk individuals.ResultsCX anatomy was assessable in all patients (n = 315). The mean age was 62.84 ± 11.34 years; median annulus area was 12.97 ± 5.69 cm2. The mean distance between the left CX and mitral annulus was 6.97 ± 3.85 mm. A critical distance <4 mm was observed in 23.5% (74/315), among whom circumflex obstruction occurred in 2.71% (2/74), compared to 0% in those with ≥4 mm distance (P = .05). Percutaneous coronary intervention was required in 0.32% (1/315). A low injury PI (≤0.2) was found in 27.31% (86/315), correlating with male sex, larger annular dimensions, and elevated injury risk.ConclusionsPreoperative CT-based measurement of the CX’s distance from the mitral annulus is feasible and may identify patients at elevated risk for iatrogenic injury. A distance ≤4 mm or injury PI ≤0.2 indicates increased risk. Routine CT imaging, combined with coronary angiography, is recommended for risk stratification before MV surgery.Keywords: circumflex artery, endoscopic mitral valve surgery, coronary artery injury, CT imaging

INTRODUCTION

Iatrogenic injuries to the circumflex artery (CX) during mitral valve (MV) surgery are severe and occur in 0.3%-1.8%. CX injury after MV surgery is a recognized but infrequent complication. Nevertheless, it can lead to severely impaired outcomes such as myocardial infarction, ventricular arrhythmias, heart failure, and death. Proximity of the CX to the mitral annulus, especially at the level of the anterolateral commissure, increases the risk of injury during surgical procedures. Despite advancements in surgical techniques, risk of obstruction has not changed which calls for improved preoperative planning.

Risk of injury is even increased when rigid annuloplasty rings are used.1,2

Preoperative coronary CT angiography (CCTA) can help identify high-risk zones where the CX is <3 mm from the mitral annulus, with a 25% incidence of this critical distance.3,4

The risk of CX injury is higher in patients with left dominant or codominant coronary circulation, as these configurations result in a shorter distance between the CX and the mitral annulus.3

The variability in clinical presentation, from asymptomatic to cardiogenic shock, underscores the need for comprehensive preoperative assessment and intraoperative monitoring (echocardiographic signs of impaired wall motion). The aim of this study was to perform a comprehensive analysis of circumflex anatomy using advanced CT imaging software prior to minimally invasive MV surgery and describe anatomical patterns and configurations at risk for injury.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design and patient population

This was a retrospective, single-centre observational study. This study included 315 patients over a 4.5-year period, which had an evaluable, ECG-gated CT-scan and were able to receive a minimally invasive MV procedure. Criteria for minimally invasive surgery are: feasible excess site for cannulation strategy for the heart lung machine, no vessel kinking, and minimally arterial vessel calcifications (to prevent risk of stroke).

Advanced CT imaging assessment using the 3mensio software (3mensio Medical Imaging BN, Euclideslaan 57, 3584 BM Utrecht, Netherlands) was used for detailed assessment of mitral annular and circumflex anatomy.

Feasibility of CX assessment was evaluated and mitral annular dimensions with the minimum distance from CX to mitral annulus were described. Protocol for measurement was as follows: First the mitral annulus was defined and measured using the multi-point function on the MV module in 3mensio. These are performed in diastole. Second, the manual vessel function is activated and the corresponding vessel was marked (in this case CX). Third, in 3D mode, the automated “Distance from Annulus” function is activated, where along the vessel line all distances to the mitral valve annulus (MVA) are reported. Last, we use the minimum vessel distance to annulus as the correct value.

Overall cohort

Three hundred and fifteen patients underwent minimally invasive MV surgery during a 4.5-year period. The 30-day mortality rate was 1.9%. Acute renal injury occurred in 6.4% of patients. The mean age at surgery was 62.8 years, with a mean BMI of 26.0 ± 5.1 kg/m2. Arterial hypertension was prevalent in 57.8% of patients, while 7.3% had diabetes mellitus. The mean ICU stay was 4.9 days, with 21.9% experiencing prolonged ICU stays of more than 3 days.

Cardiac conditions were common, with 60% of patients having a history of previous PCI and 4.1% having a history of stroke. Respiratory complications including respiratory insufficiency in 14.9% and pneumonia in 7.0%. The most frequent type of valve lesion was Carpentier Type II (excessive leaflet movement) at 54.0%, followed by Type I (normal leaflet movement) at 44.8%. CX distance was on mean 7.0 mm ± 3.9 and a CX distance <4 mm was seen in 23.5% (74/315) patients (Figure 1). The mean length of hospital stay was 15 days. Further values are listed in Table 1.

Surgical technique

For preoperative planning, our department routinely conducts contrast-enhanced ECG-gated CT scans as part of standard care to anticipate potential intraoperative complications. The scan covers the area from the supra-aortic vessels to the lower inguinal vessels. This allows us to prepare for any challenges during the procedure.

We then utilize specialized CT software for cardiac interventions (3Mensio Medical Imaging BV). This enables us to accurately determine valve sizes, assess the distance between the mitral annulus and the CX, evaluate annular calcifications, measure the anterior mitral leaflet length, create a digital neo-LVOT, and compare various prosthesis options (Figures 1 and 2). The details of our standard endoscopic MV procedure have been previously described by our team.5 Of note, we use 2.0 Polyphene non-resorbable sutures with a V5 or V6 needle as valve/ring sutures.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected and analysed retrospectively. A CT analysis was subsequently performed on all patients who had received an evaluable ECG-gated CT-scan.

Categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers and percentages, with the denominator specified for each value. Continuous variables are reported as mean values with 95% confidence intervals (lower and upper) unless stated otherwise.

Statistical analyses were carried out using MedCalc software (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium). Graphs and diagrams were created with graphpad Prism (Version 10.4.1.).

Circumflex injury prediction index

We developed a dedicated index to predict risk of CX injury. The CX prediction index (CXPI) gives a quantitative assessment of the distance between the RCX and the MVA in relation to the area of the MVA, and is additionally normalized by the patient’s body surface area (BSA). A low CXPI indicates a risk for obstruction. While the absolute values of CX distance are important, the CXPI gives more information about CX distance in relation to annulus and body size.

RESULTS

Overall rate of CX injury was 0.6% (2/315), 0% was treated via PCI and 0.6% (2/315) treated conservatively. CX injury was not fatal in any case, overall 30-day mortality in the cohort was 1.9% (6/315). Mean CX distance was 7.0 ± 3.9, lowest value was 0.9 mm and the highest was 18.6 mm. Comparison of patients CX distance ≤4 mm vs ≥4 mm

The first comparison was made with a CX distance of <4 mm (n = 74) and ≥4 mm (n = 241) in terms of various clinical parameters and outcomes. However, there was a significant difference in the annulus area, which was larger in the group with a CX distance >4 mm (14.6 cm2 (14.1-15.0) vs 7.9 cm2 (6.1-9.6), P < .01). Cross-clamp time was significantly shorter in the group with a larger CX distance (82.08 minutes vs 96.35 minutes, P = .01). CX distance was independent from body size. ICU stay and 30-day mortality rates showed no significant differences (P = .57).

The rate of acute renal injury requiring dialysis was significantly lower in the group with a shorter CX distance (P < .01). The group with a shorter CX distance had a higher rate of pneumonia (P = .05) and postoperative myocardial infarction (P = .05), which was caused by CX injury in both cases. There were no significant differences in other postoperative complications. The mentioned values are listed in Table 2.

Low vs physiological CXPI

Patients with a low CXPI (≤0.2) were significantly more often male (73.3% vs 52.4%, P < .01) and had larger cardiac anatomical dimensions, including annulus area (17.56 cm2 (16.8-18.3) vs 11.2 cm2 (10.5-12.0)), anteroposterior (AP) diameter (42.9 mm vs 32.2 mm), craniocaudal (CC) diameter (50.2 mm vs 35.4 mm), and trigone to trigone (TT) (50.6 mm vs 35.4 mm), all with P < .01. These patients also had a significantly larger BSA (2.0 m2 vs 1.9 m2, P = .01) and more frequently underwent MV repair (55.8% vs 44.5%, P = .07).

Comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus (2.3% vs 9.2%, P = .03) and prior cardiac surgeries (1.2% vs 8.3%, P = .02) were less common in the low CXPI group. ICU and hospital stays were shorter in the low CXPI group (ICU: 3.1 (2.0-4.2) vs 5.5 (2.7-8.4) days; hospital: 11.6 vs 16.2 days) without reaching significance. Postoperative troponin values were increased in patients with a low CXPI but not significant (770.9 (626.9-914.8) vs 569.5 (440.0-698.9), P = .07) on the first postoperative day and 875.6 (731.6-1019.5) vs 751.5 (502.4-1000.6), P = .36 on the second postoperative day. Overall mortality and complication rates remained low in both the groups. Further values are listed in Table 3.

Two patients experienced postoperative injury to the circumflex artery (RCx) following MV surgery (50% repair), with critically short CX distances to the mitral annulus of 3.8 and 3.6 mm. Both patients were classified according to the CXPI into the low group (0.11 and 0.14), one developed notable postoperative complications. Both patients survived and were discharged home eventually.

DISCUSSION

Evaluation of CX anatomy is feasible in all patients using dedicated CT software and offers value in preoperative risk assessment of injury. Based on our findings, it seems that male patients with larger BSA and shorter CX distance are at higher risk for CX injury. We were able to develop a CXPI, which may predict risk of CX injury even in numerically larger CX distances.

There is anatomical variance to be noted when it comes to the CX artery. Normal anatomical course is with a closer proximation to the mitral anulus in P1 region but in a few cases the anatomical constitution is sub- or supra-annular which influences the distance and is only measured in 3D. McCarthy et al analysed 95 patients who underwent CCTA. He measured the distance from the CX to the posterior mitral annulus, dividing the area into 5 zones. The results indicated that the closest distance was found between the anterolateral commissure and segment P1, with a concerning 25% of patients having a distance of less than 3 mm, and a mean distance of only 1.94 ± 0.8 mm in this high-risk zone (3).

When measuring the CT in 3Mensio, a 3D reconstruction is done from which the RCx distance to the mitral annulus can be done from all angles.

The study emphasizes the importance of raising awareness about the CX’s proximity to the MV. It recommends using imaging to guide surgical planning and highlights the need for careful preoperative assessment and possible technique adjustments to reduce the risk of artery injury.

An interesting finding is that patients with larger CX distances have bigger annulus areas (P < .01), which may impact surgical strategies, especially in valve procedures. This supports existing research linking larger annulus sizes to more complex surgeries and longer recovery.6

The study found notable differences between patients with low and normal CXPI, suggesting CXPI may indicate more than just risk of CX injury. Its predictive value is still uncertain, but the findings are hypothesis-generating. A multicentre study is planned, inviting global centres to share CT images of patients with CX injury for further analysis.

Patients with a low CXPI were predominantly male (P < .01) and had larger cardiac dimensions across all measured axes. It would be interesting, to identify a certain “phenotype” at risk for CX injury through a multi-centric evaluation. Our findings are consistent with previous studies, which show gender-based differences in cardiovascular outcomes.7,8

MV repair was more common in the low CXPI group, likely due to anatomical factors. Similar BMI and BSA across groups suggest CX distance is not tied to body or heart size but shows high anatomical variability, emphasizing the need for baseline CT planning.

CX injury was rare in this study, highlighting the need for multicentre CT-based evaluation and outcome correlation. Assessing CXPI’s value in clinical decisions is key. In practice, low CXPI with dominant CX arteries <4 mm may favour using open rings or adjusting suture placement to reduce injury risk. Gender-related differences in CX anatomy also merit further study.

If CX artery injury occurs, a clear protocol is essential. Dumps et al noted that it can cause severe ventricular dysfunction or arrhythmias, complicating weaning from bypass and potentially leading to chronic heart failure. Treatment options include reopening sutures, bypass grafting, or PCI (which is currently preferred).

CONCLUSION

CX injury itself occurs in around 2% of patients undergoing MV surgery. Assessment of RCX distance from mitral annulus using advanced CT imaging software was feasible in all patients prior to minimally invasive MV surgery in this analysis. Close location of RCX to the MV with risk for injury is associated with a distance <4 mm and was observed in 23.49%. A dedicated index (CXPI) may help surgeons assess risk for injury but needs to be further validated in larger patient cohorts.
