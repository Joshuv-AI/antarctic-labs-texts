Estimation of the α/β ratio for metastatic melanomas in a patient based on actual responses to radiation therapy delivered with various fractionation schedules
Authors: Yuto Imai, Yuta Shibamoto, Seiya Takano, Minori Niwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRadiation response of 41 metastatic melanomas treated with various fractionation schedules in a single patient was analyzed to identify an α/β ratio that fits the clinical data.Materials and methodsOf the 41 tumors, 37 were located within 5 cm from the body surface, while 4 were deep-seated. The patient received radiation doses between 32 and 52.5 Gy in 4–18 fractions over 7 series of radiotherapy during 5 months. Since no tumors developed recurrence between 4 and 7 months after radiation, local status at 4 months was used as an endpoint. Local control was analyzed in relation to gross tumor volume (GTV) and biological effective dose (BED)2 Gy, 6 Gy, 10 Gy, 15 Gy, 20 Gy, and 25 Gy to identify a boundary threshold to classify controlled from uncontrolled tumors.ResultsAll tumors exhibited partial or complete responses; however, 11 developed local recurrence. In figures plotting GTV vs. BED for all 41 tumors, upward-sloping lines to discriminate controlled from uncontrolled tumors could be drawn for all BEDs between 2 and 25 Gy, but 34–68% of the tumors failed to meet the threshold. When non-deep-seated and deep-seated tumors were analyzed separately, upward-sloping lines could be drawn using α/β ratios of 10 and 15 Gy; no tumors recurred above the lines whereas some of the tumors recurred below the lines. In contrast, it was impossible to draw such lines using lower or higher α/β ratios.ConclusionsAn α/β ratio of 10–15 Gy appeared to more accurately reflect the actual radiation response of metastatic melanomas in this patient.

Introduction

Surgery is the standard treatment for operable malignant melanoma, but radiation therapy is often indicated for inoperable and metastatic lesions [1]. While photon and electron therapy has played a role in the management of cutaneous melanomas [2, 3], particle therapy has recently been increasingly utilized for the definitive treatment of melanomas in the head and neck region, with favorable treatment outcomes reported, particularly regarding local control [4, 5]. Traditionally, the radiosensitivity of melanomas has not been considered to be high [1]. The α/β ratio, derived from the α and β values of the linear-quadratic dose-response curve, has been reported to be relatively low for melanomas; studies from the 1980s suggested the ratios of less than 5 Gy [1, 6, 7]. The average α/β ratios of squamous cell carcinomas and adenocarcinomas are typically around 10 Gy, whereas the ratio is as low as 2 Gy for slow-growing prostate cancer [7–9]. Conventionally fractionated radiotherapy is effective for tumors with a high α/β ratio (most cancers), while hypofractionated radiation delivering high dose per fraction is considered more effective for tumors with a low α/β ratio [10]. Consequently, hypofractionated radiotherapy was deemed more effective than conventional fractionation for melanomas, providing favorable local control and symptomatic relief with limited toxicity [11, 12]. However, the α/β ratio is reported to vary among individual tumors, tumor sites, and host conditions [1, 8, 13], making accurate estimation of average α/β ratios quite challenging.

Recently, we had the opportunity to treat a patient with multiple metastatic lesions resulting from melanoma. The metastatic lesions developed sequentially in various locations. Since most of the lesions were symptomatic and shrank soon after radiation rendering sufficient pain relief, we treated over 40 lesions in total. The patient resided in a region that was not in close proximity to our hospital, which necessitated the use of various fractionation schedules, particularly hypofractionated ones. By analyzing the data from this patient, we were able to assess local control rates according to the tumor site, size, and fractionation schedules. Based on the local control data, we attempted to estimate an α/β ratio for these tumors. In evaluating α/β ratios clinically, usually data from many patients are analyzed to obtain an average α/β ratio. In contrast, this study analyzed data from 41 tumors within a single patient. In this study, we assessed a methodology for estimating the α/β ratio from multiple metastatic tumors in a single patient, and then compared the obtained values to those previously reported.

Materials and methods

Patient and metastatic tumors

This study examined 41 metastatic tumors in a 50-year-old female patient with melanoma (primary site: right temporal region of the head), all of which were treated with 6-MV X-rays under various fractionation schedules. Six months after surgery for the primary lesion, the patient was referred to our hospital for the treatment of metastatic tumors. The tumor had no BRAF mutation. The patient had received intravenous pembrolizumab treatment soon after surgery, but due to the severe hepatic toxicity, she could not continue the treatment. Thus, the patient received pembrolizumab only once, 6 months before the initiation of radiation therapy. The patient never underwent systemic chemotherapy throughout her life. Initially, we administered 15 fractions of 3.5 Gy. However, as additional metastatic tumors developed successively, we opted to implement more hypofractionated regimens. Consequently, subsequent fractionation schedules were determined based on tumor size, tumor site, and patient convenience; namely, small and superficial tumors tended to be treated with more hypofractionated regimens. Also, hypofractionated regimens were more often used at the later period of the treatment. The total dose was determined, taking the responses of previously treated tumors into consideration and assuming an α/β ratio of 2–6 Gy.

A total of 48 metastatic tumors were treated across 9 treatment sessions during an 8-month period. However, for 7 tumors treated in the last 2 series of radiation therapy, the follow-up periods were 2 months or less, which did not allow for adequate evaluation of local control; therefore, these tumors were excluded from the analysis. Consequently, a total of 41 tumors treated within 5 months of the initial radiotherapy were analyzed. Of these 41 tumors, 32 were subcutaneous metastases, 2 were located just beneath the peritoneum, 2 were superficial lymph node metastases, and 1 was in the parotid gland. These 37 tumors were relatively small, with a gross tumor volume (GTV) obtained during treatment planning ranging from 0.6 to 15.5 cm3, and were categorized as non-deep-seated tumors located within 5 cm of the body surface. In contrast, 4 tumors were located in the pancreas, adrenal gland, thorax, and paraesophageal region, respectively. These tumors were relatively large, with GTVs ranging from 34.5 to 45.3 cm3, and were classified as deep-seated (visceral) tumors.

Radiation therapy

For treatment planning, the patient was positioned in a commercial fixation system and underwent 64-row multi-detector computed tomography with a 5-mm slice thickness while maintaining shallow breathing. Contouring of the GTV and organs at risk (OAR) was performed using the Pinnacle3 planning system, version 9 (Philips Medical Systems, Eindhoven, the Netherlands) and the data were subsequently transferred to the tomotherapy planning station. The GTV plus a 5-mm margin [= planning target volume (PTV)] was treated. Depending on the tumor site, the OAR varied; however, the dose-volume constraints for OAR generally aligned with those outlined in a recent comprehensive review [14].

All treatments were administered using the helical mode of the TomoTherapy HDA system (Accuray Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA). The treatment was conducted over 7 sessions, during which 2 sites were treated simultaneously in 3 of the sessions. Each treatment field encompassed 1 to 15 tumors. The prescribed doses to achieve 50% coverage of the PTV (D50%) ranged from 32 to 52.5 Gy across 4 to 18 fractions. The D50% prescription has been traditionally used in helical tomotherapy [15]; in all treatments, the D95% and D98% were ≥ 94% and ≥ 92% of the D50%, respectively. The actual treatment schedules included: 32 Gy in 4 fractions for 20 tumors, 34 Gy in 4 fractions for 8 tumors, 36 Gy in 6 fractions for 3 tumors, 40 Gy in 8 fractions for 3 tumors, 40 Gy in 10 fractions for 2 tumors, 40 Gy in 8 fractions for 2 tumors, 52.5 Gy in 15 fractions for 2 tumors, and 48.6 Gy in 18 fractions for 1 tumor. Biological effective doses (BED)1–40 Gy were calculated using the linear-quadratic model {BED = D[1 + d/(α/β)], where D represents the total dose and d denotes the fractional dose} [16]. The data were initially analyzed for α/β ratios of 1 to 40 Gy, but the ratios below 2 Gy and above 25 Gy did not seem to better fit the data, so α/β ratios of 2, 6, 10, 15, 20, and 25 Gy were used for detailed analyses. The α/β ratios of around 2 Gy have been reported for melanomas and appeared to represent those of slow-growing tumors [7–10]. The ratio of 10 Gy appeared to represent most cancers, and the ratios of 15 Gy or higher have also been reported for melanomas [7, 8, 10, 17].

Follow-up evaluation and statistical analysis

The patient was monitored for 9 months following the initial treatment, which continued until 1 month before her death. During this period, CT scans, including those for treatment planning, were conducted at least once a month for all treated tumors, allowing for the evaluation of local control for each tumor. Since CT was performed frequently and most of the tumors were visible on unenhanced CT, contrast media were used only when necessary to mainly evaluate deep-seated tumors. The local status was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST), version 1.1 criteria [18], with local recurrence defined as a greater than 20% increase in the long axis of the tumor compared to the shortest long axis measured after radiotherapy. Since all recurrences developed between 2 and 4 months after radiation and no tumors exhibited recurrence between 4 and 7 months post-radiotherapy, the 4-month local failure rate was selected as a study endpoint. Statistical analyses were conducted using open-source software, R version 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Scikit-learn in Python (https://scikit-learn.org/stable/) was used for support-vector machine analysis. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare two groups, since the sample sizes were small.

An informed consent to use the patient’s data for research purposes was obtained from the patient and her family. Approval for this study was granted by an institutional review board (NMPC No. 24-02).

Results

All 41 tumors responded to radiation therapy, achieving either a partial or complete response (CR) within 3 months. Pain relief was obtained in all symptomatic lesions. However, 11 of the 41 tumors developed local recurrence within 4 months. The other 30 tumors did not exhibit any recurrence within this timeframe, and no tumors recurred between 4 and 7 months after radiotherapy. No adverse events of Grade 2 or higher were observed. Figure 1 and Supplementary File — Figure S1 illustrate the responses to radiotherapy of a pancreatic metastasis and a subcutaneous tumor, respectively. Both tumors achieved a CR within 2 months; while the subcutaneous tumor maintained CR until 7 months after radiotherapy, the pancreatic tumor developed local recurrence at 4 months.

Table 1 presents the local failure rates at 4 months according to the tumor site, size, timing of radiotherapy (< vs. ≥ 2 or 3 months from the initial radiotherapy), and radiation doses expressed in terms of the BED. These variables were available from the medical record. The World Health Organization performance status was recorded as 1 at the time of the first radiotherapy and 2 at the time of the last radiotherapy. The serum total protein levels were 7.4 g/dL and 6.4 g/dL, respectively, while the hemoglobin levels were 12.2 g/dL and 8.7 g/dL, respectively. Consequently, we analyzed the timing of radiotherapy. GTV ≥ 2.0 cm3 tended to be associated with a higher recurrence rate, although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.09). Deep-seated tumors were larger than non-deep-seated tumors, with 2 out of 4 deep-seated tumors (50%) experiencing local failure, compared to 9 out of 37 non-deep-seated tumors (24%) that developed recurrence; however, this difference was also not significant (p = 0.29). Overall, no clear distinction was observed in the 4-month local failure rate according to the analyzed factors.

The local control status in relation to GTV size and BED2 Gy, 6 Gy, 10 Gy, 15 Gy, 20 Gy, and 25 Gy is shown in Figure 2 and Supplementary File — Figure S2. Open symbols represent controlled tumors, while closed symbols indicate tumors that experienced recurrence. To identify an appropriate boundary to distinguish controlled from uncontrolled tumors, we first analyzed all 41 tumors together using linear discriminant analysis for α/β ratios ranging from 2 to 25 Gy. The discriminant line was expected to be upward-sloping, as higher radiation doses are generally required to control larger tumors; indeed, upward-sloping lines were drawn for all α/β ratios (Supplementary File — Fig. S2). However, the separation between controlled and uncontrolled tumors was apparently poor, with 34–68% of the tumors not meeting the threshold. Therefore, we subsequently attempted to analyze deep-seated and non-deep-seated tumors separately, since these two groups of tumors had different sizes and tumor beds.

In Figures 2A–2F, the bold lines represent the boundaries separating controlled and uncontrolled tumors obtained by linear discriminant analysis for deep-seated and non-deep-seated tumors. In 4 deep-seated tumors, clear upward-sloping lines were drawn between controlled and uncontrolled tumors using α/β ratios of 10, 15, 20, and 25 Gy, whereas the separation was less distinct with α/β ratios 2 and 6 Gy. However, for 37 non-deep-seated tumors, reasonable upward-sloping lines could not be established for any α/β ratio using linear discriminant analysis. To identify more suitable upward-sloping lines for the non-deep-seated tumors, a support-vector machine method was employed under the following two conditions: 1) all uncontrolled tumors must lie below the line and 2) as many controlled tumors as possible should lie above the line. The dotted lines in Figure 2 indicate these boundaries. For α/β ratios of 2, 6, 20, and 25 Gy, only downward-sloping lines were suggested. Conversely, for an α/β ratio of 10 Gy, both upward- and downward-sloping lines were proposed, each with 4 controlled tumors lying above the lines. For an α/β ratio of 15 Gy, a downward-sloping line was suggested, but an upward-sloping line was also proposed as an alternative. Taken together, these results indicate that α/β ratios of 10 and 15 Gy appeared to better fit the data than the lower and higher α/β ratios.

Discussion

Although melanomas are generally considered to be relatively radioresistant, particularly to photons with conventional fractionation, all tumors in our patient responded remarkably well to radiotherapy, resulting in significant pain relief. This suggests that the tumors may not be as radioresistant as previously thought. A total of 9 treatment sessions were conducted in this patient, but the PTVs did not overlap, so management of cumulative doses was not difficult. Although no tumors were biopsied to confirm the melanoma metastases, it seemed reasonable to assume that all were indeed metastases from melanoma, as no other type of malignancy was detected in any CT examination during the entire period, and all tumors initially responded similarly to radiation. Given that we administered daily fractions ranging from 3.5 to 9 Gy, it remains uncertain how the tumors would have responded, had a 2-Gy daily fractionation been employed.

Generally, larger tumors are more challenging to control than smaller ones [19, 20]. However, in this study, the influence of tumor size was ambiguous; tumors with a GTV greater than 2 cm3 tended to develop recurrences (p = 0.09). The lack of statistical significance may be attributed to the relatively small sample size in a single patient, as well as the minor size difference, with mean diameters ranging from approximately 2 cm to 4 cm. Also, larger tumors tended to be treated with higher doses, which may be another reason for the lack of differences in local control due to tumor size and BED. Tumor location can also affect radiosensitivity [21], but in this study, the distinction between deep-seated and non-deep-seated tumors was unclear. If we assume that the oxygenation status did not significantly differ between the two tumor sites, the impact of tumor location may not be substantial. Host factors, including hemoglobin levels and performance status, may also be associated with radiosensitivity [22–25]. In this patient, both hemoglobin levels and performance status declined during the later stages of treatment along with disease progression; however, the influence of these factors remained unclear. This may partly be due to the fact that relatively small superficial tumors were treated during the latter period. The overall response of tumors is determined by a combination of these factors, along with the intrinsic radiosensitivity of tumor cells in each case. Therefore, the effects of tumor size, location, timing of radiotherapy, and BED may have been indeterminate in this study.

Various values of estimated α/β ratios have been reported for melanoma. Previous studies indicated relatively low α/β ratios, while others reported a wide range of ratios from 0.57 to 48.7 Gy [7, 8, 11–13]. A few research groups have attempted to estimate α/β ratios based on the actual radiation responses of melanomas; however, these studies analyzed tumors from different patients. Since the α/β ratio varies with individual tumors, these analyses simply estimated the average values of various tumors in many patients. To our knowledge, no investigators have attempted to estimate the ratios using the actual radiation responses of metastatic tumors in the same patient with the same histological class across the multiple metastases, as we did in this study. Since higher doses are necessary to control larger tumors, the slopes in Figure 2 should be upward; the slopes should not be too steep with the slope coefficients possibly between 0.2 and 0.5, as suggested in Figure 2. Using linear discriminant analysis, these conditions were met for 4 deep-seated tumors (where BED10–25 Gy provided a better fit than BED2 Gy and BED6 Gy) but not for 37 non-deep-seated tumors. With the support vector machine method, these conditions were partially met for α/β ratios of 10 and 15 Gy in the non-deep-seated tumors. Therefore, a definitive suitable α/β ratio was not suggested, likely due to data scatter; however, our method for estimating the α/β ratio appeared feasible, and it was considered that the ratio between 10 and 15 Gy fits the data better than lower and higher ratios. Bentzen et al. [11] analyzed 239 melanoma lesions in 121 patients and reported that the most suitable α/β ratio was 0.57 Gy. In contrast, Zubatkina et al. [17] analyzed 274 tumors metastatic to the brain that were treated with single-fraction Gamma Knife radiosurgery, and found that α/β ratios of 10 and 15 Gy best fit the actual responses. Interestingly, their results align well with our findings, although the linear-quadratic model may not fit single-fraction radiotherapy well [16]. To find an appropriate model to fit single-fraction radiotherapy should be a future topic in radiation biology. Nevertheless, higher α/β ratios have also been reported by other investigators [1, 13]. Therefore, it may not be reasonable to always employ hypofractionation schedules, as conventional fractionation may be sufficiently effective for some melanomas. However, estimating the α/β ratio before the initiation of radiotherapy in individual tumors remains challenging. Thus, we suggest using a relatively high dose per fraction when employing high-precision radiotherapy techniques, such as stereotactic body radiotherapy.

This study has several limitations. First, the tumors needed to be categorized into deep-seated and non-deep-seated groups, as separation of controlled and uncontrolled tumors was poor when all tumors were analyzed together. Second, the appropriateness of the statistical methods could not be assessed due to the small sample size (especially for deep-seated tumors) and data variability. A linear model was assumed to differentiate controlled from uncontrolled tumors, following previous investigators [11]; however, alternative approaches, including nonlinear regression should be explored if a larger sample size becomes available. Third, the end point of a 4-month local failure rate may not have been optimal; a longer follow-up period could have provided more comprehensive insights. However, given that the patient survived only 10 months after the initial treatment at our institution, extending the follow-up time was not feasible. Fourth, the pattern of metastases was atypical, with no brain lesions, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Fifth, non-contrast CT was primarily used for follow-up; however, since all recurrences were diagnosed within 4 months post-radiation, it seemed that the risk of misclassifying post-radiation changes as recurrence or progression appeared to be low. Despite these limitations, we believe that the results suggest that the metastatic melanomas had a relatively high α/β ratio.

Conclusions

Our method for estimating α/β ratios from the actual radiation responses of metastatic tumors in a single patient appeared feasible. Although we could not definitely determine the precise α/β ratio due to data variability, a relatively high α/β ratio appeared to correspond more closely with the tumors’ observed responses. In the analyzed case study, radiotherapy provided a favorable and effective local treatment approach.
