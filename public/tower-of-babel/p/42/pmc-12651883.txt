Epididymal dynamics and preimplantation roles of a sperm-enriched 5′ fragment of tRNA-valine
Authors: Simeiyun Liu, Andrew D Holmes, Alka Gupta, Sol Katzman, Upasna Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYSperm small RNAs are implicated in non-genetic transmission of paternal environmental effects; however, their biogenesis and functions remain poorly understood. We previously identified a 5′ fragment of tRNA-valine-CAC-2 (tRFValCAC) as one of the most abundant tRNA fragments (tRFs) in mature mouse sperm. tRFValCAC is enriched in sperm during epididymal maturation, and extracellular vesicles (EVs) secreted by epididymal epithelial cells can deliver tRFValCAC to sperm. Here, we investigate the mechanistic basis of tRFValCAC delivery to sperm and its potential functions in the preimplantation embryo. We demonstrate that heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A/B (hnRNPAB) binds tRFValCAC in the epididymis and regulates its abundance in EVs, thereby modulating its levels in sperm. Inhibition of tRFValCAC in preimplantation embryos alters the transcript abundance of genes involved in RNA splicing and mRNA processing and dysregulates alternative splicing. Phenotypically, tRFValCAC inhibition results in fewer embryos reaching the blastocyst stage. These findings illuminate the mechanism underlying sperm tRF-mediated intergenerational inheritance.

INTRODUCTION

The most fundamental process for the perpetuation of a species is the transfer of information from parent to offspring via their gametes (sperm and egg). Although genetic information contributes to the majority of heritability in biology, epigenetic information—information beyond the underlying DNA sequence—is also passed on to the offspring, a phenomenon defined as intergenerational epigenetic inheritance.1,2 The most well-characterized epigenetic information carriers include DNA methylation, histone modifications, and small RNAs. How epigenetic information is established in gametes and influences offspring development remains poorly understood.

Small RNAs play key roles in multiple, well-established epigenetic inheritance paradigms, such as RNA interference in C. elegans and paramutation in maize.3,4 Among them, tRNA fragments (tRFs), which are cleavage products of full-length tRNAs, are an emerging class found across diverse organisms.5–8 In this study, we use the term tRFs to be consistent with our previous studies,9,10 though tRNA-derived RNA (tDR) has been proposed as a uniform naming system for these small RNAs.11 tRFs can originate from the 5′, 3′, or internal regions of tRNAs and have been implicated in translation inhibition,12–15 ribosome biogenesis,16–19 mRNA silencing,20–26 chromatin regulation,9,27,28 apoptosis,29 cell proliferation,20 and retrotransposon control.28,30

tRFs are one of the most abundant classes of small RNAs in mature mouse sperm.9,31 During post-testicular maturation in the epididymis—a tubular organ essential for sperm motility and fertility—sperm travel from the caput through the corpus, and mature sperm are stored in the cauda epididymis (Figure 1A). Epididymis epithelial cells secrete extracellular vesicles (EVs) known as epididymosomes that have been shown to deliver their protein cargo to sperm32,33 and thus play important roles in regulating sperm function.34 We reported that while testicular spermatozoa are highly enriched in gamete-specific piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), the mature sperm in the epididymis are dominated by tRFs.9,10 tRFs are also highly abundant in the epididymis epithelial cells, and a subset of these tRFs can be delivered to sperm via epididymosomes.9,10 Importantly, sperm tRFs are modulated by environmental conditions and can influence offspring phenotypes. For instance, exposure to dietary alterations (such as a high-fat, low-protein, Western, or high-sugar diet), stress, inflammation, gut-microbiota depletion, aging, ethanol, and other toxicants (dichloro-diphenyl-trichloroethane, phthalates, methotrexate, and opioids) resulted in altered levels of tRFs in sperm of mice,9,35–42 fish,43 and men.44–47 Moreover, the injection of bulk tRFs purified from the sperm of exposed males or environment-sensitive synthetic tRFs into control zygotes generated offspring with altered metabolic phenotypes.35,38,39,48 However, the specific roles sperm-enriched tRFs play in regulating offspring phenotypes remain to be elucidated. Understanding the biogenesis and functions of sperm tRFs is crucial to clarify their role in intergenerational epigenetic inheritance.

We previously reported that a 5′ fragment of tRNA-valine-CAC-2 (tRFValCAC) is among the most abundant tRFs in mature mouse sperm, and its abundance increases dramatically as sperm mature in the epididymis.9 We showed that tRFValCAC is also highly abundant in the epididymis epithelial cells, and epididymosomes can deliver tRFValCAC to sperm.9 Here, we set out to investigate the mechanism of tRFValCAC delivery from the epididymis to sperm and the functional roles of tRFValCAC in preimplantation embryos. We show that the RNA-binding protein heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A/B (hnRNPAB) interacts with tRFValCAC in the epididymis and regulates its levels in epididymal epithelial cell-derived EVs and sperm. Inhibiting tRFValCAC in zygotes altered the 2-cell-stage transcript abundance of genes involved in RNA splicing and mRNA processing and led to changes in alternative splicing. This inhibition also disrupted preimplantation development, suggesting a potential mechanism for tRFValCAC-mediated epigenetic inheritance.

RESULTS

hnRNPAB binds tRFValCAC in the epididymis and regulates its EV-mediated delivery to sperm

To understand how tRFValCAC becomes enriched in sperm during epididymal maturation, we aimed to identify its interacting proteins in the epididymis, as RNA-binding proteins play critical roles in every stage of RNA life, including intercellular transport.49 We isolated proteins that bind to a biotinylated tRFValCAC oligonucleotide (oligo) in the epididymal cellular extract and identified them using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (Table S1). Among the top candidates, we focused on hnRNPAB (Figure 1B), which belongs to the hnRNP family of proteins that have been implicated in gene regulation and microRNA (miRNA) sorting into EVs.50–53 Notably, related proteins hnRNPF and hnRNPH have been shown to bind a 5′ fragment of tRNA-glycine-GCC (tRFGlyGCC).28 We found that hnRNPAB is expressed in all three segments of the epididymis (Figure 1C). Consistent with LC-MS/MS data, western blot analysis revealed a significant enrichment of hnRNPAB in proteins pulled down with tRFValCAC compared to the scramble control (Figures 1D and S1A). Additionally, immunoprecipitation of hnRNPAB-associated RNAs revealed the specific binding of tRFValCAC with hnRNPAB (Figure S1B). These findings collectively imply that hnRNPAB interacts with tRFValCAC in the epididymis.

We also detected hnRNPAB in epididymosomes and sperm (Figures S1C and S1D), but limited material prevented direct testing of its interaction with tRFValCAC in these samples. Nonetheless, considering that hnRNPAB interacts with tRFValCAC in the epididymis, is present in epididymosomes and sperm, and other members of this family have been implicated in miRNA sorting into EVs,50 we hypothesized that it regulates the sorting of tRFValCAC into epididymosomes and its subsequent delivery to sperm. To model tRFValCAC epididymal dynamics, we used a distal caput epididymis epithelial cell line (DC2),54 known to secrete EVs that can target sperm, thus exhibiting properties similar to epididymosomes.55,56 tRFValCAC pull-downs from DC2 extracts showed significant hnRNPAB enrichment over scramble controls (Figures 1E and S2A). DC2-derived EVs were 100–150 nm in size (Figure S2B), expressed tetraspanins CD81, CD63, and CD9 (Figures S2C and S2D), and, mimicking the selective sorting of tRFValCAC into epididymosomes in vivo,7,8 were enriched for tRFValCAC relative to cells (Figure S2E). As epididymosomes are a heterogeneous mix of vesicles and isolating epithelial cell-derived EVs from epididymal fluid is challenging, we established DC2 cells as a tractable in vitro model to study tRFValCAC dynamics in the epididymis. Next, to test whether hnRNPAB regulates tRFValCAC levels in EVs, we knocked down hnRNPAB in DC2 cells (Figure 1F) and analyzed tRFValCAC abundance in both cells and their EVs. Knockdown (KD) of hnRNPAB significantly reduced tRFValCAC levels in EVs but not in cells (Figures 1G and 1H). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed a modest but significant increase in mean size with no change in the particle number of hnRNPAB KD EVs compared to the control EVs (Figures 1I–1K). To assess delivery to sperm, we incubated caput sperm with EVs from hnRNPAB KD cells and observed reduced levels of tRFValCAC in sperm compared to those treated with control EVs (Figure 1L). These findings suggest that hnRNPAB regulates tRFValCAC levels in epididymal epithelial EVs and, thereby, its delivery to sperm.

tRFValCAC regulates transcript abundance in preimplantation embryos

Sperm small RNAs are transmitted to the embryo during fertilization, contributing to paternal epigenetic inheritance.57–60 To investigate the regulatory role of tRFValCAC in early preimplantation embryos, we inhibited tRFValCAC using an antisense locked nucleic acid (LNA) oligo targeting tRFValCAC (Val_LNA).61 Val_LNA successfully inhibited tRFValCAC (Figure S3A) without altering the levels of the full-length mature tRNA-Val-CAC-2 (Figure S3B). Embryos were generated via in vitro fertilization (IVF) and microinjected with either Val_LNA or a control LNA oligo (Ctrl_LNA) (Figure 2A). Late-2-cell-stage embryos, post-zygotic genome activation, were collected and subjected to single-embryo mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq). Principal-component analysis (PCA) of transcriptomic data revealed distinct clustering of embryos from the Val_LNA and Ctrl_LNA groups (Figure 2B). Differential gene expression analysis identified 505 significantly altered genes (adjusted p value [padj] < 0.05, log2 fold change ≥ 1.0), with 311 downregulated and 194 upregulated (Figure 2C; Table S2). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the differentially expressed genes showed an enrichment of genes involved in chromosome organization, cell cycle, and mRNA processing and splicing (Figure 2D). Dysregulated genes included the cell cycle regulator Ccne1 (cyclin E1), which was downregulated, and chromatin regulators, such as the histone demethylases Kdm2b (down) and Kdm4a (up) and the histone H2bc4 (up) (Figure 2E). Histone genes, in particular, showed broad upregulation in Val_LNA embryos (Figure 2F). Because tRFs carry modified residues crucial for stability and function15,62,63 and the modification status of tRFValCAC is unknown, we could not generate a synthetic mimic for gain-of-function studies in embryos. Instead, we used retinoic acid (RA) in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), a physiologically relevant model, to induce endogenous tRFValCAC.64 RA treatment increased tRFValCAC levels and downregulated target genes H2bc4 and Kdm4a (Figures S3C and S3D). Together, gain- and loss-of-function studies demonstrate that tRFValCAC regulates genes linked to the cell cycle and chromatin organization, processes critical for early embryonic development.

tRFValCAC regulates RNA processing and splicing in preimplantation embryos

In addition to the cell cycle and chromatin organization, GO analysis revealed that genes involved in RNA processing, RNA splicing, and the spliceosome were significantly overrepresented among the differentially expressed genes in Val_LNA embryos (Figures 2D and S4A). Key splicing factors, including Srsf10 and Srrm2, were downregulated in Val_LNA embryos (Figure 3A) and upregulated upon RA-mediated induction of tRFValCAC in mESCs (Figure S4B). To investigate whether these changes affected global splicing, we conducted an alternative splicing analysis. As single-embryo mRNA-seq data lack sufficient read depth, we pooled reads from 4–5 embryos to generate pseudo-replicates for junctionCounts-based analysis (STAR Methods). We identified 155 significant differential alternative splicing events (ΔPSI [percent spliced in] > 0.1, padj ≤ 0.25), with approximately one-third being skipped exons (Figure 3B; Table S3). Notably, splicing regulators Srsf10, Ptbp1, and Rbm39 showed altered splicing. In Val_LNA embryos, Srsf10 transcripts exhibited increased inclusion of an alternate last exon (Figures 3C and S4C), and Ptbp1 showed reduced inclusion of exon 8 (Figures 3D and S4C). As both of these genes were downregulated (Figure 3A), these changes in splicing potentially reduce transcript stability. In contrast, Rbm39 was upregulated and showed increased inclusion of exon 2b (Figures 3E, 3F, and S4C). We also observed altered 3′ splice-site usage, notably in Mus81, a gene crucial for initiating compensatory DNA synthesis during mitosis and resolving mitotic interlinks to ensure accurate chromosome segregation.65–67 Mus81 showed reduced inclusion of an alternate 3′ splice site despite unchanged expression (Figures 3G, 3H, and S4C). These findings indicate widespread splicing disruption in Val_LNA embryos, affecting key splicing regulators and functional targets.

tRFValCAC potentially regulates alternative splicing through interactions with RNA-binding proteins

To investigate the role of tRFValCAC in regulating alternative splicing, we focused on the splicing regulator hnRNPA1. hnRNPA1 is a potent splicing factor that regulates multiple splicing events, including its own. It collaborates with other splicing factors, either cooperatively or antagonistically, to control splicing outcomes.68 One such transcript is the pyruvate kinase gene (Pkm), which produces embryonic (Pkm2) and adult (Pkm1) isoforms (Figure 4A).69,70 hnRNPA1 promotes Pkm2 production by facilitating exon 10 inclusion, while RBMX (RNA Binding Motif Protein X-linked, also called hnRNPG) counteracts this by inhibiting hnRNPA1 binding and favoring Pkm1 expression.69,71,72 In Val_LNA embryos, hnrnpa1 transcript levels decreased and Rbmx increased (Figure 4B), lowering the hnrnpa1/Rbmx ratio. Splicing analysis confirmed that Pkm2 is the predominant isoform in embryos (Figure 4C), with Val_LNA embryos showing modestly increased exon 9 inclusion (Figure 4D). Single-embryo RT-qPCRs on control embryos also detected a higher abundance of hnrnpa1 than Rbmx and confirmed that Pkm2 is the major embryonic isoform (Figure 4E). In line with mRNA-seq data, Val_LNA embryos displayed a significant decrease in the hnrnpa1/Rbmx transcript ratio relative to Ctrl_LNA embryos (Figure 4F). While the Pkm2/Pkm1 ratio trended lower in Val_LNA embryos, the change was not statistically significant (Figure 4G), potentially due to instability of Pkm1, which may explain the overall reduction in Pkm transcript levels (Figure 4H). To further examine the role of tRFValCAC in regulating the hnrnpa1/Rbmx ratio, we utilized mESCs and RA treatment. As previously reported,64 tRFValCAC levels were downregulated after 48 hour (h) of RA treatment and increased after 120 h (Figure 4I), allowing bidirectional modulation of endogenous tRFValCAC. At 48 h, reduced tRFValCAC was associated with a lower hnrnpa1/Rbmx ratio (Figure 4J), consistent with Val_LNA embryos. At 120 h, elevated tRFValCAC levels increased the hnrnpa1/Rbmx ratio (Figure 4K). These findings from both embryos and mESCs support a model in which tRFValCAC modulates the hnrnpa1/Rbmx axis, potentially impacting the alternative splicing of downstream targets.

To uncover how tRFValCAC regulates gene expression and splicing in early embryos, we profiled its interacting proteins via LC-MS/MS in mESCs (Table S4). Several ribosomal proteins and hnRNPs, including hnRNPA1, hnRNPAB, hnRNPA2B1, and hnRNPM, were enriched in the tRFValCAC pull-down compared to a scrambled control. Notably, the splicing factor hnRNPA1 was identified as an interacting protein (Figure 4L), a finding confirmed by western blot (Figures 4M and S4D). Given that hnRNPA1 negatively autoregulates its own mRNA levels73 and its transcript abundance is reduced in Val_LNA embryos, we hypothesized that tRFValCAC binding to hnRNPA1 relieves this repression and supports proper splicing. Consistently, tRFValCAC inhibition increased hnRNPA1 binding to its own transcript (Figure 4N), likely reducing hnrnpa1 levels (Figure 4B).74 Moreover, we found that 49 out of 505 differentially expressed genes and 21 out of 130 differentially alternatively spliced genes in Val_LNA embryos are known hnRNPA1 targets (Figure 4O).75 tRFValCAC may regulate embryonic gene expression and splicing by modulating hnRNPA1, relieving its autorepression to support proper splicing—critical in early embryos with high RNA-processing demands due to elevated proliferation and metabolic activity.

Inhibition of tRFValCAC disrupts preimplantation embryonic development

To assess the role of tRFValCAC in preimplantation development, zygotes were injected with Val_LNA or Ctrl_LNA at 100, 200, or 400 ng/μL (Table S5). At 24 h post-IVF, ~15% of embryos injected with 200 ng/μL Val_LNA failed to complete the first cleavage and were stuck in the 1-cell dividing stage, compared to only ~2% of Ctrl_LNA embryos (Figure 5A). This defect was dose dependent: 400 ng/μL Val_LNA caused ~20% arrest at the 1-cell dividing stage and reduced 2-cell progression to ~53% vs. ~85% in controls (Figures 5A and 5B). No significant effect was observed at 100 ng/μL (Figure S5A). Furthermore, inhibition of a 3′ fragment of tRNA-Val-CAC-2 (using 3′Val_LNA oligo) did not cause the 1-cell division arrest nor the downregulation of hnrnpa1 transcripts as observed in Val_LNA embryos (Figures S5C and S5E), indicating that the molecular and developmental phenotypes are specific to inhibition of tRFValCAC. At 96 h, Val_LNA significantly reduced blastocyst formation: ~42% of embryos reached this stage at 200 ng/μL and ~35% at 400 ng/μL vs. ~65% and ~62% for Ctrl_LNA, respectively (Figures 5C and 5D). A subset of embryos in both groups remained at the 2-cell or 4-cell stage at 96 h, likely due to injection or culture stress.76,77 Nonetheless, as both groups were subjected to the same conditions, the effects observed in Val_LNA embryos are specific to the treatment. Additionally, previous studies have reported that paternal dietary challenges can result in 10%–30% fewer embryos reaching the blastocyst stage, with metabolic disruption in adult offspring.78–81 Thus, 23%–27% fewer Val_LNA embryos reaching the blastocyst stage is within the range of biologically relevant developmental effects, reinforcing the significance of the observed phenotype. No significant delay in reaching the blastocyst stage was observed in embryos injected with 100 ng/μL of Val_LNA or 3′Val_LNA compared to the control (Figures S5B and S5D). Together, these data demonstrate a stage-specific and dose-dependent effect of Val_LNA on preimplantation embryonic development.

DISCUSSION

The small RNA payload of sperm is reprogrammed during epididymal maturation55,82–87; however, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. tRFValCAC can be delivered to sperm via epididymosomes9,10; our data suggest that this process is regulated by the RNA-binding protein hnRNPAB. hnRNPAB binds tRFValCAC in the epididymis and is detected in epididymosomes and sperm. The KD of hnRNPAB in epididymis epithelial cells reduced tRFValCAC levels in secreted EVs, and sperm exposed to these EVs contained significantly less tRFValCAC. hnRNPAB potentially controls tRFValCAC sorting into EVs and/or regulates the biogenesis of specific subtypes of EVs. Future studies analyzing small RNA content in distinct EV subclasses (e.g., exosomes vs. microvesicles) will clarify which vesicle type is enriched in tRFValCAC. Akin to other hnRNP family proteins that regulate miRNA sorting into EVs, such as hnRNPA2B1,50,88 hnRNPC1,89 and SYNCRIP,90 hnRNPAB may sort tRFValCAC via specific sequences or structural features. Our pull-down experiments revealed a selective interaction between hnRNPAB and tRFValCAC (Figure S1B; no other tRFs were detected in the pull-down, except for a fragment of tRNA-GlyGCC), suggesting that this specificity is driven by unique sequences, secondary structures, or modifications of tRFValCAC.

Functionally, Val_LNA-injected embryos exhibited alterations in the transcript abundance of genes involved in the cell cycle, chromatin organization, and RNA processing, as well as differential alternative splicing events. Our data suggest that tRFValCAC modulates transcript abundance and splicing via interactions with hnRNPA1. We found that ~9% of differentially expressed and ~16% of differentially alternatively spliced genes in Val_LNA embryos were previously reported to be bound by hnRNPA1, and tRFValCAC can modulate the interaction of hnRNPA1 with its own transcript. Moreover, as hnRNPAB was also pulled down with tRFValCAC in mESCs, sperm tRFValCAC is potentially deposited in the embryo in complex with hnRNPAB, where it can complex with additional RNA-binding proteins such as hnRNPA1 to regulate gene expression. Notably, depending on the tRF being studied and the cellular context, tRFs can regulate transcript abundance at the level of transcription,20,21,27,91 mRNA stability,17,26 and translation.12,13,16 For instance, a prior study reported that a 5′ fragment of tRNA-valine-CAC binds to ribosomes and inhibits global translation during stress.16 Thus, the transcript abundance changes observed upon inhibition of tRFValCAC could be a result of the combinatorial alteration of mRNA processing and translational efficiency. Additionally, the interaction of another tRF, tRFGlyGCC, with hnRNP family members hnRNPF and hnRNPH in mESCs28 suggests that hnRNPs could serve as key effectors of tRF-mediated functions in embryonic cells.

Sperm tRFs are altered in response to various environmental perturbations in mice9,35,36,38,39,42,84,92,93 and humans,44,94 and injection of tRFs isolated from the sperm of exposed mice into control zygotes can disrupt offspring metabolism.35,38,93 However, the mechanism of such inheritance remains unclear. Here, we identify tRFValCAC as a key regulator of preimplantation embryonic gene expression and development. Its inhibition delayed the first cleavage and reduced blastocyst formation. Environmental challenges, such as exposure to a low-protein diet,9 a high-fat diet,35,81,95 and elevated temperature,96 modulate the levels of specific tRFs in sperm and alter preimplantation development in resulting embryos. Moreover, paternal low-protein and high-fat diets lead to metabolic disruption in adult offspring.35,97 Furthermore, it is known that defective preimplantation development can lead to metabolic diseases in adulthood, likely due to alterations in the fetus-to-placenta ratio.98,99 Taken together, our results support a model wherein the paternal environment can alter the levels of sperm-deposited tRFs in the embryo, altering preimplantation embryonic gene expression and development, ultimately influencing metabolic outcomes in adulthood. This work provides new insights into the potential mechanisms underlying sperm tRF-mediated intergenerational epigenetic inheritance.

Limitations of the study

A limitation of the current study is the lack of in vivo manipulation of hnRNPAB in epididymal epithelial cells to assess its effect on tRFValCAC levels in epididymosomes and sperm. Secondly, although transcriptomic changes were consistent in both Val_LNA-injected embryos and RA-treated mESCs, RA may also independently affect gene expression. Finally, the precise relationship between transcriptomic alterations and developmental defects in embryos following tRFValCAC inhibition remains to be clarified.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Mice used in the study

Wild-type FVB/NJ mice were used in the study. All animal care and use procedures were approved by the University of California, Santa Cruz Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were group-housed (maximum of 5 per cage) with a 12-h light-dark cycle (lights off at 6 p.m.) and free access to food and water ad libitum. Male and female mice aged 6–12 weeks were used for different experiments.

Cell lines used in the study

E14 mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) were used in the study. These cells are derived from 129/Ola male blastocyst. E14 mESCs were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, cat# 11965118), 10% FBS (Gibco, cat# A5256701), 1% non-essential amino acids (Gibco, cat# 11140076), 1% glutamine (Gibco, cat# 35050061), 0.1mM beta-mercaptoethanol (Sigma, cat# M6250), and LIF (Sigma, cat# ESG1107). For tRFValCAC induction, mESCs were treated with 0.5 μM Retinoic acid (RA) or DMSO for 48 h or 120 h, according to a previous report.64 Distal caput epididymis-derived immortalized cell line (DC2) was a gift from Marie-Claire Orgebin-Crist. This cell line is derived from a C57BL/6 SV40-tsA58 male.54 DC2 cells were seeded in 75 cm2 Nunc EasYFlasks (Thermo Fisher, cat # 156499) coated in collagen type 1, rat tail (Gibco, cat # A1048301). Cells were cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM) supplemented with 10% exosome-free fetal bovine serum (Gibco, cat# A2720801), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, cat# 15140122) and 0.3ul/ml dihydrotestosterone (Millipore, cat# 521–18-6).

METHOD DETAILS

Preparation of single-cell suspension of epididymides

Epididymides were dissected and made into single-cell suspension following a published protocol with modifications.100 Epididymides from a 10- to 12-week-old FVB mouse were dissected and sliced into 1–5 mm fragments, then incubated in prewarmed 1X PBS. Once the tissues were cleared of any visible sperm, they were transferred to a glass flask containing freshly prepared tissue dissociation media (DMEM, Collagenase IV 4 mg/mL, DNAse I 0.05 mg/mL) and placed on a shaker rotating at 200 rpm at 35°C for 30 to 45 min. Next, samples were allowed to settle, and 5 mL of supernatant was removed. 10 mL 0.25% trypsin-EDTA and 0.05 mg/mL DNAse I solution were added to the flask, and the flask was shaken for another 50 min until there were no observable tissue pieces. The resulting solution was sequentially filtered through 100 μm, 70 μm, and 40 μm cell strainers, followed by centrifugation and washing of the pellet with 1XPBS. The cell pellets were subjected to lysing using cell lysis buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1.8 mM MgCl2, 0.5% NP40, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail for mammalian cells, 1 mM DTT, 80 U/ml RNase inhibitor). The resulting epididymal cellular extract was used as the input for the pull-down of tRFValCAC-interacting proteins using a biotinylated RNA oligo, as described below.

tRFValCAC-interacting proteins pull-down

Protein pull-downs with 3′-biotinylated RNA mimics were conducted using a previously published protocol.64 Briefly, 1ug of biotinylated tRFValCAC synthetic oligo or a scrambled control oligo in 250 μl of binding buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.0, 0.1 M KCl, 10 mM MgCl2) was incubated with 500 μg of epididymal cellular extract or mESCs extract. Next, RNA-bound proteins were eluted in Laemmli buffer using streptavidin agarose beads. 2× Laemmli buffer was added to the protein samples, boiled at 95°C for 10 min, and loaded on an SDS-PAGE gel. The gel was run for a few minutes to allow proteins to migrate out of the wells when protein-containing gel pieces were cut out and submitted for mass spectrometry analysis at the Vincent J. Coates Proteomics/Mass Spectrometry Laboratory at the University of California, Berkeley. A similar procedure was used to validate protein interaction through Western blot, but the gel was allowed to run until the dye front reached the end of the gel. The gel was transferred onto a PVDF membrane and subsequently probed with antibodies against hnRNPAB (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat # sc-376411) or Anti-hnRNP A1 antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat # sc-32301).

LC-MS/MS data analysis

The EmPAI101 method was used to analyze the MS data from tRFValCAC-interacting proteins pull-down from the epididymis. PAI (protein abundance index) represents the number of peptides per protein normalized by protein size. PAI was then converted to exponentially modified PAI (emPAI), equal to 10PAI minus one, proportional to protein content in a protein mixture. Zero EmPAI values were substituted with the lowest detected values (0.01). Then, scramble control emPAI values were subtracted from those of tRFValCAC pull-down. Any candidates exhibiting higher emPAI scores in scramble controls compared to the tRFValCAC pulldown were excluded, and the tRFValCAC-interacting proteins ranked with high emPAI to low. Proteins were considered enriched in the sample at a t-test p-value <0.05. The mESC pull-down LC-MS/MS data was analyzed using PEAKS Studio 11 software. Briefly, raw MS data were processed through PEAKS Studio 11 for high-confidence identification of proteins. Proteins that passed the filter for significant peptides (FDR< 1%) were included in downstream analysis. Next, spectral counts were used to compare protein abundance in tRFValCAC pulldown and scramble control samples. Proteins that showed higher spectral counts in the control compared to the tRFValCAC pull-down were excluded from further analysis.

Epididymides, sperm, and epididymosome collection

Epididymides and sperm were collected from male mice (10–12 weeks old) as previously described.9 Caput and cauda epididymides were dissected out and transferred to a 35mm dish containing 1mL of pre-warmed Whitten’s Media (100 mM NaCl, 4.7 mM KCl, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 1.2 mM MgSO4, 5.5 mM Glucose, 1 mM Pyruvic acid, 4.8 mM Lactic acid (hemicalcium), and HEPES 20 mM). Two incisions were made in the tissue, and the tissue was gently squeezed to release fluid. After a 15-min incubation at 37°C, media were transferred to a new tube for an additional 15-min incubation, and epididymis tissues were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. After incubating for 30 min at 37°C, the media was spun at 2000 × g for 2 min. The supernatant that contained epididymosomes was transferred to a fresh 1.5 mL tube, and the sperm pellet was washed with 1X PBS, followed by incubation in lysis buffer for 10 min on ice to remove somatic cell contamination. The sperm sample was finally washed with 1X PBS and pelleted after centrifuged at 2000 × g. The supernatant with epididymosome was centrifuged at 10000 × g for 30 min to remove cellular debris, followed by ultracentrifugation at 120,000 × g at 4°C for 2 h (Beckman, TLA120.4 rotor). The resulting pellets were washed in cold PBS and underwent a second ultracentrifugation at 120,000 × g at 4°C for 2 h. These epididymosome pellets were then resuspended in RIPA lysis solution for protein extraction.

Western blot analysis

Protein lysates were obtained by mixing a suspension of epididymosomes or EVs in 2x RIPA buffer (2% NP40. 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.2% SDS in PBS, supplemented with protease inhibitors) in a 1:1 vol/vol ratio. For sperm samples, sperm pellets were resuspended in 100–500 μL of lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 1% Triton X-100, 150 mM NaCl, and 1% protease inhibitor mixture. The suspension was kept on ice for 20 min, then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. For epididymis tissue, the tissue was minced and resuspended in 1× RIPA buffer (1% NP-40, 0.05% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS in PBS, supplemented with protease inhibitors). The mixture was then sonicated for 10 s, repeated 4–5 times, until most of the tissue was dissociated. The lysate was kept on ice for 20–30 min, followed by centrifugation at 500 × g to remove debris. Protein concentrations were determined by the Bradford Protein Assay (Bio-Rad), and equal amounts of protein lysates, with Li-Core Chameleon Duo Pre-stained protein ladder, were resolved by SDS-PAGE on 4–20% precast Bio-Rad mini gels, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane and processed with a standard immunoblotting protocol (anti-hnRNPAB was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat # sc-376411; Anti-beta Actin antibody from Abcam, cat # 8226; Anti-COX IV antibody from Abcam, cat # EPR9442; Anti-hnRNP A1 antibody from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat # 32301) with Li-cor Intercept Blocking Buffer and TBS-based buffers. The proteins of interest were detected with secondary antibodies conjugated to infrared dyes, and the membranes were scanned in an Odyssey Infrared imaging system (Li-Cor, Inc.). The secondary antibodies used were IRDye 800CW goat anti-mouse IgG and IRDye 680RD goat anti-rabbit IgG (Li-Cor, Inc.).

RNA immunoprecipitation assay

The immunoprecipitation was conducted following the methods outlined in a previous study.64 Cells were UV-crosslinked at 450 mJ/cm2 and following crosslinking cell extracts were prepared using a lysis buffer containing 100 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP40, RNase inhibitor, protease inhibitor cocktail, and 1 mM DTT. The cell extract was then pre-cleared with protein A/G resin (Thermo Scientific, cat # 53132) for 1 h at 4°C, followed by incubation with either hnRNPAB antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat # sc-376411), hnRNPA1 antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, cat # 32301 ) or IgG control (Cell Signaling Technologies, cat # 3900S) overnight at 4°C. After four washes with lysis buffer, RNA from the protein complex was released by proteinase K treatment at 37°C for 10 min. The RNA was subsequently extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, cat # 15596026). Small RNA libraries were constructed using the OTTR-seq method, described below,102 or RNA was used for RT-qPCR.

DC2 cells-secreted extracellular vesicles isolation

DC2 cell culturing and extracellular vesicles (EVs) isolation were processed as previously described.55 EVs were isolated from DC2 cell-culture media using sequential ultracentrifugation. Briefly, cellular debris were removed from the media by centrifugation at 200 × g for 10 min, 2000 × g for 10 min, and 10,000 × g for 30 min. Next, EVs were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 55,000 × rpm for 2 h using the TLA120.4 rotor (Beckman). The EV pellet was resuspended in 1XPBS and frozen at −80°C until further analysis. NTA analysis using Nanosight instrument for EV size measurements and Exoview analysis for EV markers was performed at Extracellular Vesicle Core at The Saban Research Institute, Children’s Hospital Los Angeles.

Caput sperm incubation with DC2 EVs

The experiment was performed as previously reported.55 Caput epididymal sperm from 3 mice were collected by making incisions at both ends of the caput and puncturing the tissue in BWW buffer. The tissues were then left to allow sperm to ooze out for 90 min at 37°C. The sperm solution (1 mL) was centrifuged at 400 × g for 15 min at room temperature, and sperm in the pellet were washed in BWW and pelleted again. Microscopy confirmed minimal tissue cell contamination. Individual sperm samples were divided and incubated with 100 μL of either control or hnRNPAB-KD DC2 EVs in a 96-well plate at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 3 h. Following incubation, sperm were pelleted, washed three times with PBS, lysed in sperm lysis buffer, and prepared for RNA extraction and small RNA sequencing.

Reverse transcription quantitative Real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

RNA extraction followed the method described above. Each sample utilized a minimum of two technical replicates. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, reverse transcription was performed using the SuperScript III Kit (Invitrogen, cat # 18080093). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was carried out using the KAPA SYBR FAST qPCR mix (KAPA Biosystems). The primer details for qPCRs can be found in Table S6.

TaqMan small RNA assays

tRFValCAC and U6 levels were examined using custom-designed TaqMan small RNA Assays, following the manufacturer’s protocol (ThermoFisher). Briefly, 10 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed using the TaqMan reverse transcription kit (ThermoFisher, Cat # 43–665-96). Following this, qPCR was performed in 15 μL reactions employing TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (ThermoFisher, cat # 43–643-38), according to the manufacturer’s PCR program.

Single embryo RT-qPCR

Single embryos were harvested at the 2-cell stage for gene expression analysis using the Single Cell-to-CT kit (Invitrogen, Cat # 4458237). Briefly, a Single Cell Lysis mix was prepared by combining Single Cell Lysis Solution with DNase I. mRNA was then converted into cDNA using a reverse transcription mix. For preamplification, 14 cycles were performed in the thermal cycler. For qPCR, the preamplified product was diluted 1:20 with TE Buffer, and 50 μL per well was used in a 96-well plate format. The 20× TaqMan Gene Expression Assay (FAM) used in this study include hnRNPA1 (Thermo Fisher, Cat # Mm01303205_g1), Gapdh (Thermo Fisher, Cat # Mm99999915_g1) and Eukaryotic 18S rRNA Endogenous Control (Applied Biosystems, Cat # 4333760F).

Small RNA sequencing and data analysis

OTTR-seq small RNA sequencing was performed as previously described.102 Briefly, input RNA was labeled at the 3′ end by incubation in a buffer containing ddATP for 90 min at 30°C, followed by the addition of ddGTP and another incubation at 30°C for 30 min. The reaction was stopped by incubating at 65°C for 5 min, followed by adding 5 mM MgCl2 and 0.5 units of shrimp alkaline phosphatase (NEB, cat #M0371S) at 37°C for 15 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 5 mM EGTA and incubating at 65°C for 5 min. Samples were then incubated in templated cDNA synthesis buffer, adaptors, and dNTPs at 37°C for 20 min, followed by heat inactivation at 65°C for 5 min and RNase A/H treatment. cDNA was size selected on 10% PAGE-Urea gel to minimize adaptor dimers. Size selected cDNA was PCR amplified for 12 cycles with Q5 high fidelity polymerase (NEB, cat #M0491S). The final PCR product was cleaned with AMPure XP beads (Beckman, cat # A63881) and separated by 6% PAGE gel to remove adaptor dimers. The desired product was excised from the gel and eluted in 400ul elution buffer overnight at −20°C, followed by isopropanol precipitation and resuspension in 9μL of RNase-free H2O. The final libraries were sequenced on a HiSeq or NextSeq instrument.

Small RNA sequencing data analysis was done using the tRAX analytical analysis tool103 with default parameters except for a minimum non-tRNA read size of 16 and using the Ensembl gene set,104 the mm10 piRNA gene set from piPipes,105 and ribosomal RNA repeats from UCSC repeatmasker106 as the gene set. In tRAX, reads were mapped to the mouse mm10 genome combined with tRNA sequences taken from gtRNAdb.107 tRNA reads were defined as any reads whose best mapping includes a mature tRNA sequence. The tRAX pipeline uses bowtie2108 with options “-k 100 –very-sensitive –ignore-quals –np 5 –very-sensitive,” extracts all best mappings from those results, and categorizes all tRNA mappings to acceptor type-specific, decoder type-specific, or unique tRNA transcript-specific, and only reads specific to acceptor type and anticodon were used for corresponding tRNA counts. Reads that mapped to mature tRNAs were further classified into four fragment types based on the read alignment and the ends of the tRNAs. Reads where the 5′ end lies within 3 nts and 3′ end lies with 5 nts of the respective ends on the mature tRNAs are categorized as “whole” full-length tRNAs (referred to as “tRNAs”), and reads that overlap or closely align to either the 5′ ends or 3’ end of the tRNAs are classified as “tRF_5” and “tRF_3”, respectively. Mature tRNA reads that do not display these features are classified as “tRF_other”. Adjusted p-values and log2-fold change were calculated using DESeq2109 with default parameters as a component of the tRAX pipeline, and plots were generated with ggplot2 and Prism.

Transfection of mESCs and DC2 cells

Cell transfections were conducted in OptiMEM using 6-well plates.9 Briefly, mESCs were transfected with 1ug of psiCHECK-2 plasmid with either 1ng of Val_LNA oligo or Ctrl_LNA oligo using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, cat # 11668019). DC2 cells were transfected with 400 ng of siRNA targeting hnRNPAB using Lipofectamine 2000. After 16 h, media was changed, and cells were allowed to grow for a total of 48 h when RNA extraction was performed using the standard Trizol protocol.

Dual reporter assay in mESCs

The Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay (Promega, cat #E1910) was conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions. tRFValCAC sequence was inserted into the multiple-cloning site at the 3′ end of the hRluc gene within the psiCHECK-2 Vector (Promega, cat #C8021). mESCs were transfected using the method described above, wherein LNA_Val or Ctrl_LNA oligo was co-transfected with the tRFValCAC containing psiCHECK2 vector. The transfected mESCs were then employed for the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay to evaluate Renilla luciferase activity, while Firefly luciferase activity was utilized as an internal control for plasmid expression.

Northern blot analysis

Northern blot was performed following the previous protocol.110 Briefly, total RNA from mESCs was mixed with an equal amount of Gel loading buffer II (Invitrogen, cat # AM8546G) and heated to 65°C for 5 min to denature the RNA. The RNA was then run on a 10% PAGE 7M urea denaturing gel, followed by transfer to a positively charged nylon membrane and then crosslinked with EDC (1-Ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide) at 60°C for 1–2 h and prehybridized with ULTRAhyb Ultrasensitive Hybridization Buffer (Invitrogen, cat # AM8670). The membrane was then hybridized with 50 pmol mL–1 Biotin-labeled Locked Nucleic Acid-modified DNA probes (designed and synthesized by Qiagen) specific to the 5′ end of tRNA-ValCAC and 5S rRNA. After washing, the blots were then processed and developed using a Chemiluminescent Nucleic Acid Detection Module Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, cat # 89880). The Sequences of oligos used are: tRNA-ValCAC 5′ CGT GAT AAC CAC TAC ACT ACG G/3Bio/3′ and 5S rRNA 5′ GAT CGG GCG CGT TCA GGG TGG TAT G/3Bio/3’.

In vitro fertilization (IVF)

The protocol was performed following a previously reported method with some modifications.9 FVB/NJ mice were used as egg and sperm donors. Cauda epididymis and vas deferens sperm were collected from 8 to 12 weeks old males. The tissue was placed in 1mL prewarmed EmbryoMax Human Tubal Fluid (HTF) (Sigma, cat # MR-070-D) media containing 0.75mM Methyl-beta-cyclodextrin, and sperm were allowed to swim out by incubating the tissue at 37°C for 20–30 min. Sperm were counted, and 1×106 sperm was then pre-incubated for 1 h at 37°C and 5% CO2 in 200 μL drops of fertilization medium KSOM (EmbryoMax Advanced KSOM Embryo Medium, Sigma, cat # MR-101-D) + 1mM Glutathione covered with sterile mineral oil. Superovulation was induced in female mice using PMSG and hCG as in.9 Cumulus-oocyte complexes were collected from the oviducts of females at 13–15 h after hCG injection. These were then transferred to the 200uL fertilization drop. The oocytes were then co-cultured with sperm at 37°C under 5% CO2 for 4 h to allow fertilization. Fertilized zygotes were washed to get rid of excess sperm and cumulus cells and cultured to later stages of development at 37°C under 5% CO2 and 5% O2.

Embryo RNA microinjection experiments

Protocol was adopted from.9 Zygotes for microinjection studies were generated by IVF as described above. After four hours of IVF, the zygotes were washed three times in KSOM medium and placed in a drop of KSOM medium at 37°C in 5% CO2 and 5% O2 for 2 h. Embryos were then transferred to a 10ul KSOM drop covered by oil and subjected to micromanipulation. Embryos were microinjected with either H3.3-GFP mRNA with LNA-containing scramble oligo (Ctrl_LNA) (control group) or H3.3-GFP mRNA plus tRFValCAC antisense LNA oligo (Val_LNA) (experimental group). RNA injections were carried out using a Femtojet (Eppendorf) microinjector at 100 hPa pressure for 0.2 s, with 7 hPa compensation pressure. RNAs used for microinjections and their concentrations were 100 ng/μL of H3.3-GFP mRNA, 100 ng/μL, 200 ng/μL or 400 ng/μL of control oligo (5′ AACACGTCTATACGC 3′), 100 ng/μL, 200 ng/μL or 400 ng/μL of 5′tRFValCAC-2 antisense LNA oligo (5’ CGAACGTGATAACC 3′) and 200 ng/μL of 3′tRFValCAC-2 antisense LNA (5′ GGGGACCTTTCGCGT 3). After the microinjections, embryos were placed back into the incubator, and H3.3-GFP fluorescence was verified at the 2-cell stage. GFP-positive embryos were cultured until the late 2-cell stage for mRNA-seq (28 h post-IVF) or until the blastocyst stage for developmental analysis.

It should be noted that as 5′ sequence of tRNA-Val-CAC-2, tRNA-Val-CAC-4, and tRNA-Val-AAC-1 are highly similar, the 5′ fragments derived from these tRNAs are indistinguishable for the most part and, therefore, this LNA oligo is predicted to also target 5′ fragments derived from tRNA-Val-CAC-4 and tRNA-Val-AAC-1. Given that the 5′ fragments generated from these tRNAs will be of identical sequence, it is likely that they are a single entity in the cell. Alternatively, the 5′tRFs derived from these three tRNAs could differ in their RNA modification status and have distinct functions. A BLAST search revealed that Val_LNA shows sequence complementarity with the Apoa2 gene. However, as the expression of Apoa2 remains unchanged in Val_LNA embryos, off-target effects with this oligo are unlikely.

Single embryo mRNA-Seq

Single embryo mRNA-Seq libraries were generated using the SMART-Seq protocol,111 as described previously.9 Embryos with fewer than 10,000 sequenced reads were excluded from the dataset. The 151×151bp paired-end sequencing reads were trimmed at the 3′ end to 120×120bp. The trimmed reads that mapped to the mouse genome repeat elements (mostly rRNA) were removed from further processing. The repeat-filtered reads were aligned to the mouse (mm10) genome (augmented by a splice junction database derived from GENCODE transcriptome version VM18) with STAR,112 version 2.5.3a, with soft-clipping disabled (using the parameter alignEndsType EndToEnd). Duplicate alignments were removed, leaving about 6.5 million mapped reads per sample. Differential gene expression from these mapped reads was assessed using DESeq2, version 1.38.3, comparing 16 replicates each of the “Val_LNA” (experimental group) and “Ctrl_LNA” (control group) conditions. 2 embryos (one from each condition) were excluded from downstream analyses as those embryos were identified as outliers in the PCA analysis (did not cluster with the remaining 30 embryos). Data from the remaining 15 embryos per condition are included in Figures 3 and 4. The GO analyses for significantly altered genes were done using PANTHER and Broad Institute’s GSEA tools.

Alternative splicing analysis of RNA-seq data

For splicing analysis, the mapped reads were combined into 3 pseudo-replicates for each of the “Val_LNA” and “Ctrl_LNA” conditions, comprising 5 or 4 replicates per pseudo-replicate, chosen to maximize the difference between pseudo-replicates within each condition as determined from a PCA analysis of the underlying replicates. Splicing variations were assessed using the junctionCounts tool,113 version 0.1.2 (available at https://github.com/ajw2329/junctionCounts), against a set of splicing events that junctionCounts derived from GENCODE transcriptome version VM18. The Percent Spliced In (PSI) for each event was calculated by junctionCounts for each pseudo-replicate. To find significant changes, we used DEXSeq114 as included in the “DEXSeq_comparison.R” script in junctionCounts, with the minpsi set to 0.1, minimum junction reads set to 10, and the ri_span at 0.1, and the p-values of events passing these filters were adjusted using a Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Our analysis focused on splicing events with a less conservative padj value cutoff of 0.25 to allow for the lower read count in our dataset (155 significant events identified at this cutoff). Even at a more stringent padj value cutoff of <0.1, we identified 73 significantly differentially spliced events.

To visualize the junctionCounts PSI calls for each pseudo-replicate, we also generated UCSC Genome Browser tracks in which each event is represented by two isoforms corresponding to the included and excluded forms. For each isoform, the BED “score” field was set proportional to the estimated splicing level: 1000 × PSI for the included isoform and 1000 × (1–PSI) for the excluded isoform. When spanPSI values were available, both scores were further scaled by (1–spanPSI) to account for uncertainty in the PSI estimate.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1) was used for statistical analysis unless otherwise noted. To assess the difference between two means, an unpaired or paired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. Normalized read counts from RNA sequencing data were determined using DESeq2, and significant changes in transcript abundance were identified using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s correction or Benjamini-Hochberg correction to generate padj values. Significantly differentially spliced events were determined using DEXseq with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. To assess differences between the development of control and Val_LNA embryos, two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s correction and chi-square test with Bonferroni’s correction were used. Data is presented as mean values ±standard error of the mean (SEM), and statistically significant results are denoted with asterisks to indicate the level of significance unless otherwise noted: * = p or padj <0.05; ** = p or padj <0.01; *** = p or padj <0.001 and **** = p or padj <0.0001; ns = not significant. Number of replicates are denoted by ‘n’. At least 3 independent biological replicates were examined in all experiments, unless otherwise noted. Information about the statistical test used, number of replicates, and p or padj values is included in the figure legends.
