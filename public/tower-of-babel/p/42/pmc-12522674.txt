Optimizing resin bonding to saliva-contaminated polyetheretherketone: comparative efficacy of cleaning methods
Authors: Zhi-cen Lu, Meng-hui Yang, Wei-yi Zhong, Ya-xin Zheng, Guang Hong, Hao Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigated the effects of saliva contamination and different cleaning protocols on the surface characteristics and bonding performance of polyetheretherketone (PEEK).MethodsA total of 120 PEEK specimens were divided into six groups: uncontaminated (UN), contaminated without cleaning (S), and contaminated followed by cleaning with Ivoclean (IC), Katana Cleaner (KC), ethanol (ALC), or sandblasting (BLA). Resin composite cylinders were bonded to the specimens, and shear bond strength (SBS) was evaluated before and after 10,000 thermal cycles. Surface properties (water contact angle, roughness) were analyzed.ResultsSaliva contamination significantly reduced SBS (P < 0.05), irrespective of aging. The IC and ALC groups showed significantly lower SBS than the UN group. In contrast, cleaning with KC or BLA effectively restored SBS to levels comparable with uncontaminated PEEK, under both aged and unaged conditions. Adhesive failure was the predominant failure mode. While contamination and cleaning reduced hydrophobicity (lower contact angles), surface roughness remained unchanged across groups.ConclusionsSalivary contamination impairs PEEK-resin bonding, but sandblasting or Katana Cleaner effectively restores bond strength. These protocols offer clinically viable decontamination strategies for PEEK-based restorations.

Background

Polyetheretherketone (PEEK), a synthetic polymer, has gained widespread use in dentistry due to its exceptional biocompatibility and mechanical properties [1]. Compared to conventional materials such as polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) and composite resins, PEEK exhibits lower solubility and reduced water absorption, making it highly suitable for oral environments [2]. With an elastic modulus of 3–4 GPa, PEEK closely matches the biomechanical properties of human dentin, offering a significant advantage over metal alloys and ceramics, which are excessively rigid (110–130 GPa) and may damage opposing teeth or implants, particularly in compromised clinical situations [3]. Today, PEEK is used in various dental applications, including post/core systems, crowns/bridges, abutments, and dental implants [4–6]. Clinical studies have reported high patient satisfaction with PEEK restorations, establishing it as a viable and increasingly preferred alternative to traditional dental materials [7].

Despite its advantages, PEEK faces clinical challenges, particularly during try-in procedures when it is exposed to saliva [8]. Salivary contamination can compromise bonding performance by interfering with surface interactions [9]. Previous studies have demonstrated the detrimental impacts of saliva on the bonding performance of various dental materials, including zirconia, resin composites, and lithium disilicate [10–13]. For instance, salivary residues inhibit the chemical reactions between 10-methacryloyloxydecyl dihydrogen phosphate (10-MDP) and zirconia surface, reducing shear bond strength (SBS) by approximately 14 MPa [14]. Similarly, SBS decreases by about 17 MPa for resin composites and 20 MPa for lithium disilicates [10, 15]. These reductions in bond strengths often fall below clinical standards, leading to restoration failures. However, it remains unclear whether saliva similarly affects the bond strength of PEEK.

To address salivary contamination, various cleaning methods have been employed, broadly categorized into mechanical (e.g., water rinsing, sandblasting, ultrasonication) and chemical (e.g., ethanol, hydrogen peroxide, phosphoric acid) approaches. Among mechanical techniques, sandblasting has proven effective in restoring SBS in contaminated zirconia, increasing it by approximately 10 MPa to its baseline levels [16, 17]. However, sandblasting requires specialized equipment that may not be readily available in all dental practices. Chemical approaches, such as ethanol cleaning, offer an alternative by denaturing proteins [18]. Ethanol has shown efficacy in removing salivary contaminants from materials such as glass fiber posts and lithium disilicate, restoring SBS by approximately 10 MPa and 15 MPa, respectively [10, 18]. Commercial cleaning agents, which utilize nonselective oxidation or surfactants for contaminant removal, simplify the cleaning process and have demonstrated efficacy in restoring SBS to baseline levels for zirconia, resin composite, and lithium disilicate [15, 19–22]. However, despite these advancements, there remains a significant gap in research on cleaning methods specifically optimized for PEEK.

Therefore, this in vitro study aimed to evaluate the effects of human saliva and different cleaning methods on the bonding performance of PEEK. The null hypotheses tested were: (1) salivary contamination does not affect the bonding performances of PEEK; and (2) none of the cleaning methods employed demonstrated an improvement in the bonding performance of saliva-contaminated PEEK.

Materials and methods

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the School and Hospital of Stomatology, Fujian Medical University (Approval No. 2022-56) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Chinese Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects (2016). Unstimulated saliva was collected immediately before application, from one healthy author who had refrained from consuming food and beverages for 1.5 h prior to the collection process [19, 23]. All data were anonymized to protect participant privacy, and the co-author participant was excluded from data analysis to avoid conflicts of interest.

Specimen Preparation

The sample size was calculated using PASS v21.0.3 software (NCSS LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA) with the following parameters: effect size f = 0.40, α error probability = 0.05, and Power (1-β error probability) = 0.85 [24]. A total of 120 blocks (6 × 6 × 2 mm) were meticulously cut from PEEK discs (BioHPP, Bredent GmbH, Senden, Germany) using a low-speed diamond saw (Isomet, Buehler Ltd., Lake Bluff, IL, USA) under water cooling. These blocks were subsequently embedded in PMMA denture base material (Acron, Nissin Dental Products Inc, Tokyo, Japan) to achieve a diameter of 15 mm and thickness of 5 mm. The bonding surfaces of all specimens were polished with water-cooled carborundum discs (600, 800, and 1200 grit, Matador, VSM, Heidenheim, Germany). Following polishing, the specimens were sandblasted (Basic master, Renfert, Hilzingen, Germany) with a 50 μm alumina abrasive (Cobra, Renfert, Hilzingen, Germany) at a pressure of 0.4 MPa [25]. The nozzle was placed perpendicular to the surface at a distance of 10 mm for 15 s. After sandblasting, the specimens were cleaned in an ultrasound bath for 10 min and thoroughly dried with oil-free air [17].

All specimens, except for those in the control group (UN), underwent salivary contamination by immersion in human saliva for 1 min [14]. Subsequently, the specimens were rinsed with deionized water for 15 s and carefully dried with oil-free air to remove residual saliva while ensuring surface uniformity. The specimens were then subjected to different cleaning methods as follows:

No surface cleaning (S): No additional cleaning procedures were performed.

Ivoclean (IC): Ivoclean (Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) was applied for 20 s, followed by rinsing with deionized water for 15 s to remove any residue, then drying with oil-free air for 10 s.

Katana cleaner (KC): Katana cleaner (Kuraray Noritake Dental, Tokyo, Japan) was applied for 20 s, followed by rinsing with deionized water for 15 s and drying with oil-free air for 10 s [15].

Ethanol (ALC): 75% ethanol was applied using absorbent cotton balls for 20 s, followed by drying with oil-free air for 10 s.

Sandblasting (BLA): The contaminated surface was sandblasted using 50 μm alumina at a pressure of 0.4 MPa for 15 s, with the nozzle placed 10 mm perpendicular to the surface. The specimens were then cleaned in an ultrasound bath for 10 min and dried with oil-free air for 10 s [17].

Shear bond strength test

The bonding surface of each specimen was coated with a bonding primer (Visio.link, Bredent GmbH, Senden, Germany) using a microbrush. The primer was then polymerized with a dental curing light (Bluephase 20i, Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) for 90 s at 1200 mW/cm2 following the manufacturer’s instructions [8]. Pre-made resin composite cylinders (4 mm in diameter, 2 mm in thickness) were bonded to the specimens with a luting composite (combo.lign, Bredent GmbH, Senden, Germany). Excess cement was carefully removed before further curing for 180 s. After polymerization, all specimens were stored in deionized water at 37 °C for 24 h [17].

Each group was further subdivided into 2 subgroups (n = 10): an unaged subgroup and an aged subgroup. The unaged subgroups were left untreated, while the specimens in the aged subgroups underwent 10,000 thermocycles to simulate approximately 1 year of use [26]. Each thermocycle was conducted between 2 water baths of 5 °C and 55 °C with a dwell time of 30 s by an automated thermocycling machine (TC-501 F (III), Weier, Nanjing, China) [27].

The SBS test was performed using a universal testing machine (AGS-X, Shimazu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) at a crosshead speed of 1.0 mm/min [28, 29]. A chisel-shaped rod was aligned parallel to the bonded surface, positioned at a distance of 0.5 mm from the surface. The SBS value (in MPa) was calculated by dividing the fracture load (in N) by the bonded area (in mm2) [30]. The fractured samples were examined with a stereoscopic microscope (Stemi2000-C, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) to identify the types of failure at 40× magnification. Specimens exhibiting unclear failure patterns were coated with gold via a sputter coater (GVC-1000, Micro Hezao, Beijing, China) and analyzed at a field emission-scanning electron microscope (EM8000, KYKY Technology, Beijing, China) to precisely determine the failure modes at 500× magnification under 10 kV. The failure modes were categorized as follows: [31]

Water contact angle

Thirty additional specimens were prepared to evaluate the surface wettability of the six groups (n = 5). The surface wettabilities of different groups with deionized water were assessed with a static contact angle goniometer (SDC-200 S, Sindin, Dongguan, China). A 0.5 µL droplet of deionized water was gently placed onto the testing surface via a syringe tip positioned just above the specimen. For each specimen, 3 different water droplets were applied and the contact angles were measured from both the right and left sides of the droplets. An average contact angle (ø) was determined by calculating the mean of measurements taken from each specimen, following a 5-second duration of water application to the surface.

Surface roughness measurement

Surface roughness measurements were conducted with a contact profilometer (SEF650, Kosaka Laboratory Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) after water contact angle testing. The measurement was conducted over a 4.8-mm length, with roughness profiles acquired at 0.8-mm intervals. The contact stylus gauge was set to move at a speed of 0.1 mm/s. For each specimen, 5 measurements were recorded along parallel tracks, spaced 0.8 mm apart and average roughness (Ra) was calculated.

Statistical analyses

The SPSS statistical software package (SPSS 25.0 for Windows, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used. The data were assessed to determine if the normality (Shapiro-Wilk test) and equal variance (Levene’s test) assumptions were violated. Statistical analyses of the SBS values were conducted with two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Tukey’s post-hoc test was used to identify statistical differences respective to the cleaning procedures within each aging method. Finally, a student’s t test was used to investigate statistical differences within the same clean procedure comparing aging method [28]. While the analyses of water contact angles and surface roughnesses were conducted with one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc test. The level of significance was set to 0.05.

Discussion

Previous studies have extensively investigated surface treatments to enhance the bond strength of PEEK. However, the impact of salivary contamination and the efficacy of various decontamination methods remain unclear. In this study, conventional cleaning methods (ethanol and sandblasting) [11, 31], as well as commercial products with distinct mechanisms—Ivoclean (a highly alkaline solution, pH 13–13.5) and Katana Cleaner (containing 10-MDP triethanolamine salt, pH 4.5)—were evaluated. To our knowledge, this is the first study to confirm that salivary contamination significantly reduces the bond strength between PEEK and resin composite. Both cleaning methods and aging independently affected bond strength, leading to the rejection of both null hypotheses.

Salivary contamination caused a 43% reduction in SBS values in unaged specimens and a 67% reduction after aging, consistent with findings in other dental materials [16, 19, 22]. Notably, both sandblasting and Katana cleaner effectively restored SBS values to levels comparable to uncontaminated specimens, reduction rates of 3.7% and 18.1%, respectively, after 10,000 thermocycles. Sandblasting is regarded as a simple yet effective method to improve the bonding performance for polymer-based dental substrates [32], especially when combined with bonding agents [33]. It mechanically removed the contaminants and enlarged the contact area, allowing more functional groups from the primer (Visio.link) to adhere, contributing to better polymer crosslinking and SBS values, in line with previous studies [15, 19, 29]. While all groups exhibited similar surface roughness, group BLA showed slightly higher values. Katana cleaner also effectively restored SBS, performing comparably to groups UN and BLA. It functions as a surfactant, using water as a solvent to target fatty acids, dissolve oils within the contaminated interface, and neutralizes or removes both hydrophilic and hydrophobic contaminants from saliva [9]. Previous study has shown that salivary proteins involve salivary micelles, which perform unique and complex interactions in a liquid environment [9, 13]. Meanwhile, the hydrophobic groups of 10-MDP triethanolamine salt in Katana cleaner can aggregate and form micelles in water, which would combine with salivary micelles and eventually achieve the preferred results [15]. In contrast, Ivoclean exhibited limited effectiveness before aging. It contains metal oxide particles that bond to phosphate contaminants in saliva based on the chemical gradient principles [19]. Ivoclean utilizes zirconium oxide particles to bind with phosphate groups in salivary contaminants, facilitating their removal from treated surfaces [34]. This approach is effective for zirconia ceramics, where phosphate contamination can block reactive sites needed for bonding with phosphoric functional monomers. In contrast, PEEK lacks a reactive oxide layer and does not chemically interact with artificial saliva, resulting in Ivoclean having limited impact on its bonding performance [35].

Ethanol was also ineffective at removing salivary contamination from PEEK. Mechanical cleaning (e.g., cotton wiping) failed due to saliva’s sticky, hydrophobic nature [21]. As highlighted by Burkhardt et al.., treating saliva-contaminated surfaces with ethanol can cause proteins to become fixed to roughened surfaces, making them less likely to be removed. Similar findings were reported in previous studies on ethanol decontamination of zirconia [20, 23].

Thermocycling is a well-established method for artificial aging in in vitro tests assessing the bonding durability of veneering composites to PEEK surfaces [36]. Lai reported a 23% decrease in bonding strength after 5,000 thermocycles when bonding PEEK to a light-curing cement [37], whereas only an 11% decline was observed in another study [32]. To better simulate aging conditions and amplify the degradation effect, a longer thermocycling period was applied in the present study. The reduced bonding strength in group KC after aging indicates that 10-MDP’s effectiveness may decrease over time, while Ivoclean helped prevent hydrolysis and debonding in luting composite-to-PEEK bonding. Failure mode pattern indicated that groups UN, KC, and BLA exhibited mixed failure regardless of aging, whereas group IC displayed it only after aging, aligning with the SBS values. This phenomenon may be attributed to the enhanced SBS values changing the failure pattern [30].

The bonding performance is also influenced by surface wettability [27]. In this study, all cleaning methods resulted in lower contact angles compare to the non-contamination group, indicating improved surface hydrophilicity. However, sandblasting yielded higher contact angles than other cleaning methods, suggesting limited improvement in wettability, which is consistent with previous studies [37, 38]. However, it still provided superior bonding affinity and long-term durability. This suggests that wettability alone does not fully determine bond strength [31]. Sukcheep et al.. pointed out that the compositions of resin cements and the sensitivity of material handling techniques also influenced the bonding performance [31].

Overall, the findings support the use of sandblasting or Katana Cleaner to restore bonding efficacy after salivary contamination, with Ivoclean demonstrating better aging resistance. These methods are straightforward to implement in clinical practice and demonstrate low technique sensitivity. Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, the contamination protocol did not fully simulate clinical conditions, as restorations during try-in are often exposed not only to saliva but also to blood. Blood contamination can form a persistent proteinaceous film on the adhesive substrate, which interferes with the adhesive interface and compromises bond integrity. Additionally, the use of saliva from a single donor may limit the generalizability of the results. Second, the evaluation was limited to one PEEK material (Bio-HPP), leaving unanswered questions about how other PEEK materials with different filler compositions (e.g., CF/PEEK) respond to contamination and cleaning. Therefore, further research is warranted to investigate the effects of blood contamination on the bonding performance of diverse PEEK-based materials.
