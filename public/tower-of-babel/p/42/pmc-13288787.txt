Comparing Outcomes of Heartmate 3 and Heart Transplantation in Older Children With Dilated Cardiomyopathy—Can Transplantation be Delayed or Avoided?
Authors: Waldemar F. Carlo, Luz A. Padilla, James K. Kirklin, Ryan S. Cantor, Devin Koehl, Jeffrey P. Jacobs, Shahnawaz Amdani, Kathryn Chatfield, Manan Desai, Gabrielle Vaughn, Catherine M. Montgomery, Katerina Boucek, Katheryn Gambetta, David L. S. Morales
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChildren with end‐stage dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) usually undergo heart transplantation or a short‐term bridge with a ventricular assist device. Heartmate 3 (HM3) may represent an appealing longer‐term option, though pediatric data are limited. We analyzed two clinical registries and hypothesized that adolescents with DCM treated with either HM3 or transplant would present similar survival.MethodsThe Pedimacs and Pediatric Heart Transplant Society registries were queried for DCM patients ages 10–18 years treated with HM3 or transplant, between 2017 and 2022. Mortality and adverse events were reported.ResultsNinety HM3 and 306 transplant patients were reported within the respective registries. There was a higher proportion of Black patients in the HM3 cohort (40% vs. 30% in the transplant cohort, p = 0.03). Twelve‐ and 36‐month survival rates were both 94.4% for HM3 patients and were 99.3% and 95.6% for transplant patients. In the HM3 group, there were 66 transplants (median time of 2.8 months), 3 late explants for recovery, and 3 early deaths. Conditional 1‐year survival for HM3 and transplant was 100% and 96.3%, respectively, at 3 years. In the HM3 group, bleeding, stroke, and device malfunction rates were low at ≤ 0.1 events per patient per year.ConclusionsAdolescents with DCM treated with either HM3 or transplant had excellent 3‐year outcomes, though many HM3 pediatric patients are transplanted early after device placement. Safely supporting this group of patients for an extended duration with HM3 has implications for delaying/avoiding transplantation, longer‐term patient survival, and donor organ availability.

Introduction

Adolescents with end‐stage heart failure secondary to dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) traditionally undergo primary cardiac transplantation or a short‐term bridge to transplantation with a ventricular assist device (VAD). VADs have evolved with the newer generation Heartmate 3 (HM3, Abbott), approved in 2017, demonstrating short‐term outcomes comparable to transplantation in adults [1, 2].

Adolescents and young adult heart transplant recipients have higher rates of rejection and graft loss and worse survival compared to younger children and older adults [3, 4, 5]. These outcomes are perhaps related to the biological risk of rejection, medication nonadherence, and vulnerability during the pediatric‐to‐adult care transition. At the same time, extrapolating data from adult HM3 studies, adolescents and young adults may have the best outcomes with this device [2, 6, 7].

While VADs in children have primarily been used as a bridge to transplant or decision, a paradigm shift may be occurring where longer‐term VAD support is viewed as a reasonable or even standard treatment alternative to transplantation or as the first step of a planned sequential therapy resulting in a net prolongation of life by delaying transplantation. This shift assumes comparable HM3 and transplant outcomes in children, an assumption difficult to confirm because multicenter pediatric trials do not exist, individual center volumes are small, and no single registry houses both VAD and transplantation outcomes.

To explore HM3 and transplantation outcomes in a similar pediatric cohort, we analyzed two separate clinical registries. We hypothesized that adolescents with DCM treated with the HM3 would present similar three‐year survival compared to a contemporaneous group of adolescents with DCM treated with transplantation. Additionally, we explored secondary outcomes of rehospitalization and adverse events unique to each therapy.

Methods

We analyzed two distinct registries after approval of a combined study proposal submitted to each organization. We chose this approach because each registry contained unique information not available in the other registry. The transplant registry does not contain outcomes after HM3 placement. The VAD registry does not contain outcomes after transplant. We did not perform a linkage analysis, as this would only add to how HM3 patients did after transplant rather than comparing HM3 and transplant outcomes. Data from transplant recipients were obtained from the Pediatric Heart Transplant Society (PHTS) database, a multicenter event‐driven registry that captures most pediatric heart transplants in North America. Data from HM3 recipients were obtained from the Pediatric Interagency Registry for Mechanical Circulatory Support (Pedimacs), a Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) supported registry on mechanical circulatory support in the pediatric population. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval for registry participation is maintained at each center. Both the PHTS and Pedimacs registries are maintained by Kirklin Solutions.

Using the PHTS registry, we included patients aged 10–18 years with a diagnosis of DCM who underwent cardiac transplantation from 2017 to 2022. Follow‐up data collection continued through December 31, 2022. We excluded multi‐organ transplant recipients. To prevent crossover of patients (the same patient being in the Pedimacs HM3 cohort and subsequently in the PHTS Transplant cohort) for the primary analysis, we excluded patients from the PHTS cohort if they had an HM3 at transplant (n = 73). In a separate secondary analysis, we analyzed post‐transplant outcomes for this excluded HM3 group of patients in PHTS.

Using the Pedimacs registry, we included 10–18‐year‐old DCM patients who underwent HM3 left VAD placement within the same time period. We excluded patients with a right VAD, biventricular assist devices, and any non‐HM3 type of left VAD.

Our primary outcomes were mortality after transplantation in the PHTS registry and mortality after HM3 placement in the Pedimacs registry. Patients were followed for up to three years. Censoring is described in the statistical analysis below. Secondary outcomes after transplantation included retransplantation as well as common adverse events (infection, rejection, malignancy, and coronary allograft vasculopathy) as defined by PHTS and presented as estimated freedom from each first adverse event at three years. Secondary outcomes after HM3 placement included rehospitalization and common adverse events (stroke, bleeding, infection, and device malfunction) as defined by Pedimacs. Adverse event rates from Pedimacs were calculated within 2 weeks (early) and after 2 weeks (late) from implantation. We additionally analyzed survival after transplantation for HM3 patients within the PHTS registry.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed at Kirklin Solutions. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical outcome variables in HM3 and transplant cohorts. Where possible, chi‐square or Fisher's exact test or student t‐test were used to compare variables present in both cohorts. Kaplan–Meier curves were used to estimate survival rates in both cohorts as well as common adverse events in the transplant cohort. The HM3 group was censored at the time of transplantation or explant for recovery or the last entry in the registry. The transplant group was censored at the time of the last entry in the registry. Adverse event rates for the HM3 group were calculated within 2 weeks (early) and after 2 weeks (late) from implantation.

Results

We included 306 transplant recipients from the PHTS registry and 90 HM3 patients from the Pedimacs registry. Race breakdown was different between groups, with the HM3 group having 32% White and 40% Black patients, while the transplant group had 48% White and 30% Black patients. Markers of acuity were increased in the HM3 group, as evidenced by higher rates of intubation, inotrope use, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), as well as higher bilirubin. Using the Pedimacs registry, 12‐ and 36‐month survival rates were both 94.4% for HM3 patients (Figure 2). There were three deaths in the HM3 group by nine months after device placement, and three explants for recovery after nine months. No patient died between 9 and 36 months of support; thus, conditional one‐year survival was 100%. Sixty‐six (73%) of the HM3 patients were transplanted at a median time of 2.8 months after device placement. Of these sixty‐six patients, 38% were White and 39% were Black. For patients undergoing HM3 placement in 2018–2022, their rate of undergoing transplant by one year post‐implant was 85% with no significant difference between 2018 to 2020 and 2021–2022 (86% vs. 84%, respectively, p = 0.9). Post‐transplant survival of the HM3 patients is not tracked in the Pedimacs registry.

Three‐year survival curve comparing the primary outcome in each group.

In the transplant group in the PHTS registry, 12‐ and 36‐month post‐transplant survival were 99.3% and 95.6%, respectively (Figure 2). Conditional 1‐year survival for the transplant cohort was 96.3% at 3 years. Using only PHTS registry data, 56% (170 patients) of the transplant group had any VAD at the time of transplant. VAD types were as follows: Heartware HVAD (n = 125), Berlin Heart Excor [8], Centrimag [9], Heartmate II [2], Impella CP [4], Syncardia Total Artificial Heart [2], other unspecified VAD [4], and one each of Impella 2.5, Heartmate IP, and Tandem Heart. Three‐year post‐transplant survival was 97.2% and 93.5% for patients without and with a VAD, respectively (p = 0.34). Additionally, there was a single retransplant.

Not included in the primary analysis (to avoid crossover of patients in both primary cohorts) were an additional 73 patients in the PHTS registry who had an HM3 at the time of transplant. There were three deaths in this group of patients with a 94.3% estimated three‐year post‐transplant survival.

Adverse events in the HM3 group are reported in Table 2 as adverse event rates occurring early (less than 2 weeks) or late (2 weeks or greater) after implant. Rates of bleeding (both gastrointestinal and non‐gastrointestinal), device malfunction, ischemic stroke, and hemorrhagic stroke were uncommon, with an adverse event rate of 0.1 per patient year or less. All non‐GI bleeding complications occurred within the first month of device placement, while only a single stroke occurred beyond that first month. Infection (0.8 per patient year) and rehospitalization (0.9 per patient year) were more common adverse events.

Three years after transplant, freedom from rejection and rejection with hemodynamic compromise was 64% and 84%, respectively. Freedom from infection was 79%. Malignancy and cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV) were uncommon. Rehospitalization data were unavailable for the transplant group.

Discussion

The primary objective of this retrospective review of two distinct clinical registries was to compare 3‐year outcomes of older children with DCM treated with either HM3 or transplantation. The high rate of early transplantation in the HM3 cohort limited the analysis of HM3 outcomes. Despite this limitation, our analysis does offer important findings. We found excellent survival in both groups. Additionally, we found a low hazard for mortality and adverse events in a small number of patients remaining with the HM3 after 9 months. Despite increasing HM3 utilization during this study period, its use as a long‐term support device does not appear to be changing, as evidenced by unchanged rates of first‐year transplantation. The results of this analysis support the use of HM3 in adolescents with DCM as an initial alternative to primary transplantation. While the insufficient data and current HM3 utilization strategy do not allow for a true comparison of HM3 vs. transplant outcomes, this analysis does suggest that the strategy of delaying transplant in HM3 adolescents appears to be a safe practice and, speculatively, may result in a net prolongation of life.

Our data are consistent with the limited pediatric data available on HM3. The ACTION network multicenter registry found similar rates of mortality and adverse events, with overall survival remaining excellent despite higher illness acuity at implant [6]. With a median time to transplant of under 3 months in two‐thirds of the HM3 cohort in that study, duration of support continues to be short [6, 10]. We likewise found that the HM3 cohort was disproportionately male but also noted racial differences that had not been previously reported, and this finding is discussed below. Regarding adult HM3 data, we cannot meaningfully compare our outcomes due to our low number of long‐term support patients [1, 2, 11].

Choosing between HM3 placement and primary transplantation in adolescents with DCM can occur at the initial traditional timepoint—i.e., advanced heart failure symptomatology not sufficiently managed by medical therapies. HM3 placement has historically been closely followed by waitlisting and transplantation, as evidenced by the short median time to transplantation in this and other studies [6, 10]. This paradigm differs from the adult VAD community, where dramatically fewer patients are transplanted in the first year after device placement [12] and chronic outpatient medical care of the VAD recipient is common, which may also be a result of lower transplantation rates in adults with stable outpatient VAD support. Higher pediatric listing priority (status 1A) afforded to VAD patients likely contributes to shorter time on HM3 in this analysis. Data showing favorable long‐term results of the adult HM3 and transplant sequential strategy could lead to greater acceptance of this strategy in the pediatric community.

As Thangappan and Morales suggest in their 2019 commentary “the predetermination of the patient's destiny before implantation of an intracorporeal continuous‐flow VAD should no longer be necessary.” [13], Once implanted with an HM3, the child then presents their care team with an ongoing reassessment of transplantation vs. continued HM3 support. Remaining with an HM3 may represent a more appealing option in select patients. Data from this analysis, as well as adult HM3 studies, suggest a low ongoing hazard for mortality and stroke in the medium term in adolescents and young adults living with an HM3 device with transplantation as a “rescue” option if needed [2]. Thus, conditional on already having achieved uncomplicated HM3 support, select adolescents may actually face higher risks undergoing transplantation compared to remaining on their device. On the other hand, patient and family preferences need to be considered, as some adolescents may prefer transplant over remaining on HM3 support. Future generations of VADs that improve outcomes and patient tolerability may influence these preferences in favor of longer‐term VAD use.

Utilization of HM3 as part of a planned sequential pathway in select patients would be a paradigm shift from the current use of VADs as primarily a bridge to transplant or as destination therapy for patients felt to be non‐candidates for transplantation [14]. Such an approach may offer benefits as noted by experts in the field [13, 15]. Allograft life expectancy is limited, and delaying initial transplantation will delay the onset of that limited graft life. At the end of the graft life, patients with coronary vasculopathy needing re‐transplantation are underprioritized in the current adult allocation system and face substantially higher waitlist mortality [9]. On the other hand, VAD recipients with accrued time on the device may actually be prioritized (incentivization) in future allocation schema. Some HM3 patients receiving longer‐term optimized guideline‐directed medical therapy may experience myocardial recovery and explant, as seen in three patients in our HM3 cohort. Finally, adolescents and young adults with an HM3 may be bridged to a more favorable age or stage of life for optimizing post‐transplant success.

Elaborating on this final point about reaching a more favorable age or stage of life, post‐transplant risk of rejection and death is increased in the late teenage years and young adulthood [3, 4] and during transition to an adult center [8, 16]. Unexpectedly, we found racial differences in the make‐up of the cohorts, with White patients more common in the transplantation group and Black patients more common in the HM3 group. While some of this disparity could be attributable to the specific centers found in each registry, differential application of the two therapies may be ongoing based on perceived post‐transplant risk differences, with medical, socioeconomic, and obesity contraindications potentially explaining racial differences. The authors support equitably evaluating all candidates for transplantation and HM3 therapies based on medical factors and utilizing a multidisciplinary approach to overcome socioeconomic challenges that may exist with either therapy. One could speculate that medication nonadherence in the immunosuppressed allograft recipient is more consequential than in the anticoagulated HM3 patient.

Adverse events represent an ongoing concern in both groups. Unplanned rehospitalization was common in the HM3 group and comparable to adult data, estimating a 67% readmission rate by three years in both the HM3 and transplant groups [2]. Bleeding and stroke were uncommon after the initial recovery from HM3 placement.

This analysis has several limitations. First, unlike in large single‐center adult studies, we are unable to compare HM3 and transplant outcomes in a single pediatric study database or registry. Pedimacs and PHTS registries are both rigorously maintained by the same data center and have considerable institutional overlap. To prevent the same patients from being present in both cohorts, we excluded from the primary analysis the PHTS registry patients who had an HM3 at transplant. Second, as mentioned above, the high rate of early transplantation in the HM3 group limits what can be concluded about long‐term support outcomes. Many HM3 patients were transplanted within months, and we do not know the reasons for transplantation nor how they would have done if remaining on the device. To address the question of whether VAD or transplantation is the best initial approach for a patient with end‐stage DCM, we would ideally compare a cohort of HM3 patients intended for long‐term VAD support with a cohort of non‐VAD transplant recipients, but the available data in these registries is inadequate for this approach. Third, the transplantation group does not account for potential waitlist mortality of patients awaiting transplantation as primary treatment. Fourth, registry data were limited by the lack of rehospitalization data in the PHTS registry and potential incompleteness of data in both registries from patients transitioning to adult centers. Fifth, PHTS registry data do not clarify why the proportion of transplant patients with status 1A greatly exceeded the proportion of patients on VAD or ECMO at transplant. Sixth, this was not a randomized trial, and baseline differences between groups likely influenced whether patients underwent transplantation or HM3 placement. Finding good outcomes with both strategies does not imply that every individual patient has an equivalent likelihood of survival with both therapies. Finally, the true incidence of adverse events and mortality may be underestimated, especially in the transplant cohort, because some patients remain within the three‐year window at the time of data collection, and data is no longer collected if a patient transitions to an adult center.

Despite these limitations, our data suggest that HM3 use in adolescents with DCM is associated with excellent survival but a high rate of early transplantation in the current era. Additional studies should have larger patient volumes, longer duration HM3 support, and complete follow‐up. Learning about longer‐term HM3 support in adolescents is paramount because an extended duration of support that can safely delay or avoid transplant could unlock important benefits for overall long‐term patient survival and donor organ availability.
