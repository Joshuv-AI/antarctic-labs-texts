Characterization of suspicious prostate lesions at multiparametric MRI with dynamic 68 Ga-Gozetotide PET/CT
Authors: Daniel Parrott, Qing Yuan, Daniella Pinho, Yin Xi, Daniel N Costa, Alberto Diaz De Leon, Marianna Dakanali, Xiankai Sun, Orhan K Oz, Spencer Bowen, Neil M Rofsky, Dana Mathews, Ivan Pedrosa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProstate cancer (PCa) will affect approximately 12% of US men in their lifetime and remains the 2nd most common cause of cancer-specific death. However, there is a broad spectrum of severity of PCa; some require aggressive treatment and others can “watch-and-wait”. Distinction of clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa) from indolent PCA with current imaging methods remains challenging.ObjectiveThis study aims to evaluate dynamic prostate specific membrane antigen (PSMA) positron emission tomography (PET/CT) in the characterization of suspicious prostate lesions identified on multiparametric MRI (mpMRI).MethodsProspective study of biopsy-naïve patients with at least one Prostate Imaging and Reporting Data System (PI-RADS) score 5 or one PI-RADS score 4 plus a prostate specific antigen (PSA) ≥ 10 ng/mL. Dynamic PET/CT was performed for 30 min after administration of 68Ga Gozetide. Time activity curves (TAC; kBq/cc) of suspicious prostate lesions were generated. TAC logarithmic slope was calculated for each GG at 5-, 10-, 15-, and 30 min. Apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) at mpMRI for each lesion was tabulated.Results19 PI-RADS v2.1 ≥ 4 and 6 secondary PI-RADS v2.1 ≥ 3 lesions in 19 patients were analyzed: 4 (16.0%) grade group (GG) 0 (benign), 3 (12.0%) GG 1, 6 (24.0%) GG 2, 6 (24.0%) GG 3, and 6 (24.0%) GG ≥ 4. Significant differences in slope values were observed between clinically insignificant disease (GG 0/1) and GG 2 or higher as early as 5 min post-injection (p < 0.05). At 30 min, GG 1 had the lowest mean slope (− 0.18 ± 0.57) and GG ≥ 4 exhibited the highest mean slope (2.35 ± 2.03). Slope value/ADC ratio further improved discrimination between different GGs.ConclusionsDynamic 68 Ga Gozetotide time activity curves could distinguish GG0/1 from GG3 and GG4/5 PCa. The addition of mpMRI characteristics could further distinguish GG2 from GG4/5. GG2 could not be distinguished from GG3, probably due to small sample size.Clinical Implications.Dynamic 68 Ga Gozetotide PET/CT imaging may offer enhanced discrimination of MRI-visible prostate cancer lesions and assist in management decisions.Study registered with the FDA (IND 144,177) and clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04179968).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41824-025-00268-2.

Introduction

Approximately 12% of men will be diagnosed with prostate cancer (PCa) during their lifetime in the U.S (American Cancer Society 2024; Rawla 2019). Despite a 98% overall 5-year survival rate, PCa represents the 2nd most common cause of cancer-specific death in men (Rawla 2019). Increasing evidence indicates that indolent tumors have excellent prognosis when left untreated (Wilt et al. 2012; Hamdy et al. 2016). Distinguishing patients with indolent disease, who do not benefit from treatment, from those with life-threatening disease is crucial. The International Society of Urological Pathology [ISUP] classification provides a five-tier histopathologic framework for differentiation of indolent PCa from clinically significant PCa (csPCa), with grade group (GG) 1 PCa considered clinically insignificant and GG ≥ 2 considered csPCa. (Epstein et al. 2016; Humphrey 2017) Recently, intermediate favorable risk lesions (GG 2) have been recognized as having outcomes like GG 1 and being potential candidates for active surveillance (AS) whereas intermediate unfavorable risk (GG 3) PCa patients are generally not considered for AS (Serrano and Anscher 2016).

Multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) is the most accurate imaging technique for initial PCa detection and staging (Weinreb et al. 2016; Turkbey et al. 2019). The multiparametric approach integrates different features into the Prostate Imaging and Reporting Data System (PI-RADS) v2.1, which reports lesions on a 1 (very low likelihood of csPCa) to 5 (high likelihood of csPCa) scale (Costa et al. 2014). Data supports targeted biopsies of suspicious abnormalities seen on mpMRI (PI-RADS ≥ 3) (Siddiqui et al. 2015; Valerio et al. 2015). Apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values derived from diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) sequences have been linked to cellular density and correlated with cancer aggressiveness (Costa et al. 2019; Berman et al. 2016). Other studies have illustrated a potential contribution of dynamic contrast enhancement (DCE) MRI to differentiate indolent PCa from csPCa (Berman et al. 2016). However, mpMRI cannot reliably differentiate favorable [GG 2] from unfavorable intermediate [GG 3] risk or even high [GG 4] and very high [GG 5] risk cancers. (Costa et al. 2019; Yamasaki et al. 2015)

Although primarily utilized for detection of radiographically occult metastatic prostate cancer in patients with biochemical recurrence (Fendler et al. 2019), PSMA PET/CT was reported as more effective at identifying suspicious lesions than mpMRI, and combined PSMA PET/CT and MRI was more effective than either modality alone (Emmett et al. 2022; Emmett et al. 2021; Gallium 68 PSMA-11 PET, 2022; Wang et al. 2020). Commonly, static PET/CT whole body imaging is obtained 60 min post radiotracer injection. Given the potential contribution of DCE MR to help stratify prostate cancer (Weinreb et al. 2016; Turkbey et al. 2019; Costa et al. 2014; Berman et al. 2016; Mazaheri et al. 2017), we hypothesize that PSMA PET with dynamic data acquisition may offer additional value to differentiate different PCa histologic GGs. While several studies have reported dynamic PSMA data (Sachpekidis et al. November 2016; Perveen et al. 2018; Heuvel et al. 2021; Schmuck et al. 2017), prospective data using dynamic PSMA PET/CT is lacking. Furthermore, the usefulness of combined mpMRI and dynamic PSMA PET to differentiate PCa histologic risk is unknown. Our study aims to evaluate dynamic PSMA PET/CT in the characterization of suspicious prostate lesions at mpMRI.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This was a prospective, single center, HIPAA-compliant study, registered with the FDA (IND 144,177) and on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04179968, November 27, 2019) and approved through the University of Texas Southwestern’s institutional review board (IRB) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Funding was provided internally through the University of Texas Southwestern. All participants signed an informed consent prior to enrollment. Inclusion criteria were: (1) biopsy-naïve patients with suspected PCa as determined by referring physician; (2) mpMRI of the prostate demonstrating at least one prostate lesion with a PI-RADS v2.1 score 5 or at least one PI-RADS v2.1 score 4 plus a prostate specific antigen (PSA) ≥ 10 ng/mL; and (3) scheduled for biopsy after mpMRI. Exclusion criteria were inability to complete PET (e.g., body habitus, claustrophobia), known allergy to 68 Ga Gozetotide, or lack of histopathology.

PET/CT acquisition

Participants underwent both a dynamic and static 64 slice 68 Ga Gozetotide PET/CT (GE Discovery system with 5 detector ring configuration). Prior to each scan, subjects emptied their bladder and at the discretion of the nuclear medicine physician were injected with 20 mg furosemide for additional urinary clearance. For the dynamic scan, a single field of view (FOV 25 cm) low-dose CT of the pelvis was acquired (mA 15–515, 140 kV, 3.75 mm slice thickness). Dynamic list mode PET data were acquired using the same pelvic FOV as for the CT scan at intervals of 10 × 30 s (300 s), 5 × 60 s (300 s), 5 × 120 s (600 s), and 2 × 300 s (600 s), for a total exam time of 30 min. Imaging began immediately prior to each subject being intravenously injected with 1.8–2.2 MBq (0.049–0.059 mCi)/kg body weight of 68 Ga Gozetotide. List mode images were used to construct time activity curves (TACs) as well as summed to generate a single image (‘summed dynamic images’) for visual interpretation. Multiplanar reformats of the summed images were reconstructed at 5 mm slice thickness.

After the dynamic PET acquisition, subjects were allowed to void as needed. At 60 min post 68 Ga Gozetide injection, patients were repositioned on the PET/CT table and a standard vertex to mid-thigh PET/CT scan (‘whole-body scan’) was obtained with the same imaging parameters as the standard of care imaging at our institution. PET images were reconstructed in all three planes at a slice thickness of 5 mm.

Image analysis

Two experienced PET/CT readers (OKO, DM) reviewed summed dynamic scans in consensus to identify focal areas of increased prostate activity and for suspicious metastatic lesions in the pelvis. The readers used the prostate mpMRI images and reports to correlate with the PET images. Each primary PI-RADS v2.1 ≥ 4 lesion identified in the prostate on mpMRI was correlated with findings on the 68 Ga Gozetotide PET/CT scans to generate TACs. If the patient had additional PI-RADS v2.1 ≥ 3 lesions with PSMA uptake, a TAC was also generated for these lesions. The mean ADC value from a region of interest (ROI) encompassing the majority of the lesion was included in the clinical mpMRI report per institutional standard-of-care and tabulated for each lesion included in this study.

TAC analysis

Quantitative analysis of the dynamic PET data was performed using PMOD software (PMOD Technologies LLC, Zürich, Switzerland). Iliac artery and prostate tumor ROIs were generated for each prostate lesion for which there was PET, MR, and biopsy confirmed data (N = 26). These lesions were defined on the PET/CT images by two experienced readers and correlated with prostate MRI. An experienced imaging scientist (QY) used the iscontour tool in PMOD to draw an iliac artery ROI on the early dynamic PET frame and a tumor ROI on the frame with the maximum tracer uptake. For the dynamic ROIs, lesion activity, expressed in kBq/cc, was chosen instead of SUV to reduce the number of dependent variables in the TAC analysis (SUV is lesion activity / injected activity (kBq) / body weight in kg) (Sarikaya et al. 2020). These ROIs were propagated to all dynamic frames and then used to generate blood pool and tumor TACs. Kinetic data including K constants and flux were generated from the TACs and exported into Microsoft Excel. In addition, logarithmic regression curves (base 10) were fitted to TACs for each lesion with endpoints of 5 min, 10 min, 15 min, and 30 min post-injection (Supplemental Fig. 1). The zero-time point for the prostate curve was defined as the time point when the arterial input reaches the maximum uptake (typically 15–30 s). For analysis of the whole-body static images, prostatic and metastatic lesions were identified, and SUV was calculated for each lesion as is standard at our institution. The mean logarithmic slope was calculated at each time point for each GG (5-, 10-, 15-, 30 min). For analysis, the GG 4 and GG 5 PCa’s were combined into a single group (GG ≥ 4). Since the ADC has been reported as a surrogate marker of tumor cellularity (Costa et al. 2019), we investigated the relationship between the lesion dynamic PSMA uptake and ADC. Logarithmic slopes at each time point were divided by ADC.

Radio-pathologic correlation

After completion of the PET/CT scans, subjects underwent standard of care targeted biopsies of MR suspicious lesions (PI-RADS v2.1 score 3 or higher) as is routinely done at the authors’ institution. Systematic biopsies of the rest of the prostate were performed at the discretion of the treating physician. Biopsies were performed either under US guidance (6 patients) using US-MRI fusion software for anatomic colocalization (DynaCad, Invivo, Gainsville, FL) or under direct MR guidance (13 patients) within the MRI scanner with a dedicated biopsy device (DynaTRIM, Invivo, Gainsville, FL). The biopsy method was left to the discretion of the referring physician.

Histologic lesion analysis was based upon biopsy results available for lesions with corresponding mpMRI and PET findings for each subject. Lesion categorization was based on the ISUP GG system. Lesions were grouped into clinically insignificant (benign results, GG 0 and GG 1 lesions), intermediate-favorable PCa (GG 2), intermediate-unfavorable PCa (GG 3), and aggressive PCa (GG 4 and 5). The highest GG was used if there was heterogeneity amongst multiple biopsy cores of the same lesion.

Statistical analysis

The mean of slopes for prostate lesions exhibiting benign histology or GG 1 PCa were compared to the mean of slopes for GG 2, GG 3, and GG ≥ 4 PCa using one-way ANOVA up to each time point. Separately, the mean slope divided by the ADC was compared by grade group using one-way ANOVA. Ad-hoc pairwise comparison was performed when the overall test of significance from ANOVA were significant. A linear mixed model was used to compare mean SUV of lesions on 60-min delayed static images between GG groups. Tukey Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) adjustment for multiple comparison was applied. R version 4.3.0 was used for analysis.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed on dynamic data, SUV, ADC, ratios of dynamic data over ADC and ratios of SUV over ADC. GG was dichotomized as GG ≤ 1 vs GG ≥ 2 and used as the dependent variable. Area under the curves (AUC) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were reported. P values were calculated with the DeLong method. R version 4.3.0 was used for analysis.

A total of 19 PI-RADS v2.1 ≥ 4 and 6 secondary PI-RADS v2.1 ≥ 3 lesions identified at mpMRI were also visible on dynamic PSMA PET/CT. Of these 25 lesions, 4 lesions (16.0%) were benign, 3 (12.0%) were GG 1, 6 (24.0%) were GG 2, 6 (24.0%) were GG 3, and 6 (24.0%) were GG ≥ 4 (Table 1). Discussion

This study aimed to assess the added value of dynamic 68 Ga Gozetotide imaging in predicting the aggressiveness of suspicious prostate lesions as identified on mpMRI. Our observations confirmed the ability to discriminate GG 0/1 PCa from GG 3, 4, and 5 PCa. Moreover, the combination of dynamic 68 Ga Gozetotide uptake data with the MRI-derived ADC offered synergistic information. Indeed, dividing the logarithmic slope by ADC improved discrimination between intermediate-favorable and aggressive disease by 5 min post-injection (p = 0.01). Although the mean slope/ADC ratio at 15 min demonstrated differences between intermediate favorable disease (GG 2) and intermediate unfavorable disease (GG 3), these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.686), possibly due to small number of observations in these groups. Further research with a larger patient cohort would be needed to validate these results and confirm the optimum timeframe of dynamic imaging as well as the appropriate numerical cut-off for the TAC slope value that can be used in clinical practice. However, by 30 min, there was no longer a significant difference between GG 0/1 and GG 3 lesions. Similar to the 30-min dynamic data, standard-of-care delayed images could not differentiate between GG 0/1 and GG 3 lesions. This suggests that the optimum cut-off for dynamic imaging is 15 min or less, possibly even as short as 5 min. ROC did not demonstrate a significant difference in sensitivity between time points or with the inclusion of ADC characteristics; this finding is attributable to the small sample size of the trial.

Previous studies assessing the value of dynamic PSMA PET acquisitions focused on the visualization of prostate cancer during the early phases post injection. Schmuck et al. (2017) reported visualization of primary prostate tumors on early dynamic and delayed imaging in 19/20 (95%) patients. Prostate cancer demonstrated higher tracer uptake than benign prostate tissue at any time point (P ≤ 0.001) and increased over time leading to improved tumor-to-nontumor ratio within the prostate on delayed images. In contrast to this study, the authors found no differences in TAC between neoplasms with Gleason score ≤ 7 (i.e., corresponding to ISUP GG 1–3) and Gleason score ≥ 8 (i.e., corresponding to ISUP GG 4–5). A possible explanation for these differences includes the dynamic acquisition scheme (10 min total duration and reconstructed to 18 frames [12 × 10 s, 4 × 30 s, 2 × 180 s] compared to the 30 min protocol in this study), and the differences in inclusion criteria (patients with biopsy-proven PCa scheduled for prostatectomy versus biopsy-naïve patients with MRI-visible lesions in this study). Perveen et al. assessed the value of dynamic 68 Ga Gozetotide PET/CT acquisition to reduce or eliminate the negative effect of bladder activity in the detection of primary prostate tumors in 15 biopsy-confirmed PCa patients (Perveen et al. 2018). The authors concluded that early dynamic imaging demarcated the primary tumors clearly and, compared to delayed imaging, had comparable efficacy in detecting metastatic pelvic lymph nodes. However, in that study, an analysis of radiotracer kinetics in tumors with different histologic GG was not reported. Lastly, Sachpekidis et al., evaluated the pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of 68 Ga Gozetotide in 24 biopsy-confirmed PCa patients and reported differences in TACs for primary PCa tumors versus metastasis to bone and lymph nodes (Sachpekidis et al. 2016) although TACs for primary tumors with different histologic GG were not reported.

Methodologically, our study is unique in how the dynamic curves were calculated. Prior studies analyzed radiotracer dynamics via defined pharmacokinetic parameters (i.e. k1, k2, etc.; representing various binding constants and internalization of receptors) (Sachpekidis et al. 2016; Perveen et al. 2018; Heuvel et al. 2021; Schmuck et al. 2017). While these fits were attempted, logarithmic functions fit to the lesion TAC proved superior in differentiating among various GG PCa. The biological significance of differences in TAC is unknown. Several factors can influence TACs including tumor vascularity (i.e., radiotracer delivery), receptor density, cell density, and renal/blood pool clearance. While vascularity may have a substantial effect on intratumoral radiotracer activity in the early phase of the TAC, the differences in SUV uptake in the 60-min delayed images among GGs suggest that the receptor density may increase also with GG. Our results are consistent with those of Kalapara et al. reporting a greater median SUVmax for GG 3–5 tumors (6.40, interquartile range [IQR] 4.47–11.0) than for benign and GG 1–2 tumors (3.14, IQR 2.55–3.91; p < 0.001) Kalapara et al. 2022). Nevertheless, the effect of each of different pathophysiologic variables on the tumor TAC and their correlation with tumor GG deserves further investigation.

Several studies have assessed the use of PSMA and mpMRI in the evaluation of PCa, including the PRIMARY trial in Australia, which employed both Gozetotide PET/CT and MRI for biopsy decisions (i.e., identification of suspicious lesions for targeting) but did not combine quantitative metrics (Fendler et al. 2019). For the detection of prostate cancer of any Gleason score, Wang et al. (2020) demonstrated a sensitivity and specificity of the SUVmax/ADC ratio of 81.2% and 88.4%, respectively, compared to 90.6% and 58.1% of ADC value and 67.2% and 97.7% of SUVmax alone in a retrospective study of 107 lesions in 63 patients. In our study, four patients had benign or indolent disease (GG 1; patients 2,3, 4, and 8 (Table 1)). These four patients had 6 lesions, of which 5 were visible on PET. Thus, although these patients would be potentially eligible for active surveillance, this determination would not be possible with visual inspection of PET data. Nevertheless, SUV alone was only able to differentiate between GG 0/1 and GG 4/5 lesions. Although combining SUV with ADC improved discrimination between GG 2 and GG 4/5, SUV and SUV/ADC did not discriminate GG 0/1 from GG 3. In contrast, dynamic images were able to discriminate GG 0/1 from GG 3. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating improved differentiation between GGs using a single combined metric (lesion TAC/ADC) compared to dynamic PET/CT data alone.

This study has several limitations. First, the cohort of patients was small. Second, the time between the PET/CT and mpMRI scans was variable. This limitation can be obviated by obtaining the two exams at a fixed, close interval or with near-simultaneous acquisitions on a hybrid PET/MR scanner. Furthermore, several visible lesions on mpMRI had no dynamic (or delayed) PSMA correlate. Even in the absence of a visual correlate to a suspicious mpMRI lesion, hybrid real-time PSMA/MRI would allow the generation of precisely correlated TACs; a benign uptake curve (Supp. Figure 1) could offer reassurance that a lesion is indolent.

Third, the anatomic correlation between mpMRI and PET/CT as well as between the dynamic images, summed dynamic images, and static images of the PET scan was performed with manual visual co-registration instead of overlaying images, potentially resulting in misregistration. Finally, the PET/CT interpreters were not blinded to the mpMRI reports; these reports were used to correlate precisely with the PET findings and may have introduced bias. Since standard of care targeted biopsies are performed based on the result of the mpMRI, this approach was necessary to ensure histologic confirmation.

Conclusions

In this prospective study, dynamic 68 Ga Gozetotide kinetics improved discrimination between clinically insignificant and intermediate-unfavorable/aggressive prostate cancer in patients with suspicious prostate lesions (PI-RADS ≥ 4) at multiparametric MRI. The combination of the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values with dynamic 68 Ga Gozetotide kinetics further improved discrimination between suspicious prostate lesions. Further validation with larger cohorts is necessary.
