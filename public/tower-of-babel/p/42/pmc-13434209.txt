Tumor genome and microenvironment alteration by trastuzumab deruxtecan as neoadjuvant therapy for HER2-mutant NSCLC
Authors: Jiangyang Li, Xianfeng Lu, Shuai Yue, Ruyi Hang, Xiaoyan Dai, Xunjie Kuang, Wei Guo, Zhou Huang, Yanli Xiong, Mengxia Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients carrying HER2 exon 20 insertion (HER2 20ins) are respond poorly to conventional therapies and pan HER inhibitors. Trastuzumab Deruxtecan (T-DXd) has been approved in second line treatment in advanced NSCLC, but its efficacy in operable patients remains unclear. Here, we report a case of a patient with operable, HER2 20ins NSCLC who received neoadjuvant T-DXd, along with a second HER2 20ins patient who received conventional chemotherapy as a clinical reference. We utilized whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and immune microenvironment analysis to investigate the treatment’s impact. WGS analysis revealed marked reduction in tumor clones with genome instability and extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) following T-DXd treatment. Meanwhile, immune profiling demonstrated substantially increased CD8 + T cell infiltration in tumor cores with elevated PD-1 expression. In contrast, such changes in CD8 + T cell infiltration and PD-1 expression were not evident in another HER2 20ins patient who received conventional chemotherapy. These findings suggest that neoadjuvant T-DXd may represent a promising therapeutic option for locally advanced HER2-mutant NSCLC, warranting further investigation in larger cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53779-8.

Introduction

HER2 exon 20 insertion (HER2 20ins), a prevalent mutation encoded by the ERBB2 gene, predominantly presents as the Y772_A775dupYVMA subtype. It represents the most common HER2 alteration in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with a prevalence of 2–4% among non-squamous NSCLC cases1,2. For patients with advanced HER2-mutant NSCLC, chemotherapy remains the standard first-line regimen, and such patients typically achieve superior treatment responses compared to those without other actionable driver mutations3. Meanwhile, conventional HER2-targeted and pan-HER-targeted therapies, including monoclonal antibodies and small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors, have yet to demonstrate satisfactory efficacy and a tolerable safety profile in this patient population4.

Trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) is the first antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) approved for the second-line therapy in patients with HER2-mutant NSCLC. The DESTINY-Lung02 trial demonstrated an objective response rate (ORR) of 54.9% and a median progression-free survival (mPFS) of 8.2 months5, and the DESTINY-Lung05 study subsequently confirmed comparable efficacy and safety in a Chinese population6. However, the efficacy and safety of T-DXd in locally advanced disease, particularly in patients who are intolerant to chemotherapy and requiring neoadjuvant therapy, remain to be established.

Here, we report a case of an elderly patient with stage IIIA HER2 20ins NSCLC who achieved marked tumor regression and underwent successful surgical resection after two cycles of neoadjuvant T-DXd. A second HER2 20ins patient who received conventional chemotherapy was included as a clinical reference. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) were performed on the paired pre- and post-treatment tumor specimens, and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was conducted on tissue samples from different regions of the resected tumor after treatment. This approach allowed an exploratory characterization of changes in the tumor genome and immune microenvironment. These findings provide preliminary insights into the genomic and immunological alterations induced by neoadjuvant T-DXd, and may inform future investigation of combination strategies with PD-1 inhibitors.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples collection

All human sample collection procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved under an ethical exemption by the Ethics Committee of Army Medical Center of the PLA. All specimens were obtained from the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Daping Hospital & Army Medical Center of PLA. For the main study subject, biopsy samples were collected before T-DXd treatment, while surgical specimens—including tumor tissue, proximal peritumor tissue (3 cm from the tumor margin), and distal peritumor tissue (15 cm from the tumor margin)—were obtained after T-DXd therapy from the same patient. In addition, paired pre- and post-treatment formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples were collected from a reference patient who received conventional chemotherapy.

DNA extraction and whole-genome sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from both FFPE biopsy samples and fresh frozen (FF) surgical specimens, including tumor tissues and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues (3 cm from tumor margin). Adjacent samples were used to filter out germline variants. High-quality genomic DNA was extracted using a commercial DNA extraction kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were constructed with the TruSeq Nano DNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina) and subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) on the Illumina HiSeq X platform. High-quality reads were aligned to the UCSC human reference genome (hg38) using Burrows−Wheeler Aligner (BWA)7. The resulting alignments were sorted, converted to BAM format, and indexed using Samtools8. All downstream preprocessing (duplicate marking and base quality recalibration) and somatic variant calling were executed using GATK49. Functional annotation of somatic variants was performed with ANNOVAR10. Copy number variations (CNVs) were analyzed using CNVkit to identify amplified and deleted genomic regions11. Structural variants (SVs) were detected with Delly12. Complex amplicon structures such as extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) and breakage-fusion-bridge (BFB) cycles were identified with AmpliconArchitect and JaBbA13,14. Clonal evolution analysis was performed using PhyloWGS15. The genomic landscape of somatic mutations was visualized using Circos16. Regions of kataegis were identified and analyzed using the Maftools package17. Chromothripsis events were identified using ShatterSeek with SV and CN data18. Mutational signatures were extracted and deconvoluted with MutationalPatterns R package19. All statistical analyses were conducted in R v4.3.1.

RNA isolation and rna-sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from post-treatment surgical specimens, including tumor tissue, proximal peritumor tissue (within 3 cm of the tumor margin), and distal peritumor tissue (beyond 15 cm from the tumor margin). High-quality total RNA was isolated using a commercial RNA extraction kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform to generate high-throughput paired-end sequencing data. Gene expression quantification with FeatureCounts20 was followed by differential expression analysis via DESeq221. Functional enrichment of differentially expressed genes was performed for Gene Ontology (GO)22 terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)23 pathways. Immune cell fractions were estimated from RNA-seq data using CIBERSORTx24.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed on FFPE tissue section (4 μm thick). The sections were incubated with the following primary antibodies: anti-PD-1 antibody (Zhongshan Goldenbridge Biotechnology; dilution 1:200) and anti-c-Kit antibody (Selleck Chemicals; dilution 1:400). Antigen retrieval was conducted using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions. After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% hydrogen peroxide, the sections were blocked with non-immune serum to reduce nonspecific binding. The antibody-antigen complexes were then visualized using a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled polymer detection system and diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen. Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. All stained slides were independently reviewed by two pathologists to ensure consistency. Image analysis was performed on randomly selected microscopic fields with identical dimensions using ImageJ software.

Multiplex immunofluorescence analysis

After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, sections were blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min. The following primary antibodies were sequentially applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C: anti-CD8 (abcam, ab178089), anti-pan Cytokeratin (panCK) (abcam, ab215838), anti-CD4 (abcam, ab133616), anti-CD20 (abcam, ab64088), anti-FoxP3 (abcam, ab20034), and anti-CD68 (abcam, ab192847). After each primary antibody incubation, slides were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 30 min, followed by a 5 min incubation with fluorophore-conjugated tyramide signal amplification reagent. Each staining cycle was followed by TBST washing and 100 °C heat-induced antibody stripping. Finally, slides were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired using the AKOYA Vectra Polaris system. Quantification of positively stained cells was performed using Q-path and Phenochart.

H&E staining

Conventional hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on FFPE tissue sections. Briefly, sections were first baked at 65 °C for 2 h, followed by deparaffinization and hydration. Subsequently, the nuclei were stained with hematoxylin and the cytoplasm was counterstained with eosin. After dehydration, the sections were mounted with neutral balsam and imaged under an inverted microscope.

Results

Clinical courses

Case 1

A 74-year-old male, a heavy smoker, was hospitalized due to an incidental finding of a nodule in the right lower lung during preoperative evaluation for a vocal cord cyst. Enhanced computed tomography revealed a 4.0 × 2.1 cm lesion in the dorsal segment of the right lower lobe, with mediastinal lymph node enlargement at stations 4 L and 7. Needle biopsy of the lung lesion confirmed lung adenocarcinoma, staged as cT2aN2M0 (stage IIIA). ARMS-qPCR-based driver gene testing detected a HER2 exon 20 insertion mutation (YVMA). Given a complex medical history including sick sinus syndrome, coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, grade 3 hypertension (very high risk), syphilis, and chronic hepatitis B, a multidisciplinary team (MDT) consultation suggested that the lesions were potentially resectable with effective preoperative systemic therapy. After discussion with the patient and his family members, the patient declined standard-of-care immunochemotherapy out of concerns regarding advanced age and multiple comorbidities. With informed consent, we administered trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) monotherapy at a dose of 5.4 mg/kg every 3 weeks for two cycles as neoadjuvant therapy. Only grade 1 constipation occurred during neoadjuvant treatment. The first tumor evaluation showed a partial response with 47% tumor shrinkage, but the tumor remained metabolically active on PET-CT (SUVmax 13.99). A second MDT discussion recommended definitive surgery. Right lower lobectomy and hilar/mediastinal lymph node dissection were successfully performed one week after the second dose of T-DXd. Moderate tissue adhesion was observed during surgery (Fig. 1). Pathological evaluation after T-DXd induction showed downstaging to ypT1N2M0, with 60% residual viable tumor cells, corresponding to a pathological regression grade 3 (CAP/NCCN). After nine cycles of adjuvant T-DXd, the patient discontinued treatment due to economic reasons and was followed up every 3 months. As of the latest follow-up in October 2025 (16 months of disease-free survival), no recurrence or metastasis has been observed.

Case 2

(clinical reference): A 46-year-old female presented with headache and was diagnosed with left upper lobe lung adenocarcinoma (cT3N2M1b, stage IVB) harboring a HER2 exon 20 insertion mutation, accompanied by left hilar/mediastinal lymph node metastases and right occipital lobe brain metastasis. Initial conversion therapy (bevacizumab + pemetrexed/cisplatin, 1 cycle) achieved stable disease. She subsequently underwent navigational microscope-assisted resection of the right occipital lobe brain metastasis under general anesthesia, followed one month later by video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) left upper lobectomy, systematic lymph node dissection, and pleural adhesion lysis. Postoperatively, seven cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy were completed, with stable disease control at follow-up. This case provided a clinical reference for contextualizing the immune findings observed in Case 1 without T-DXd exposure.

Cancer genome profiling before and after T-DXd treatment

WGS analysis of paired samples from the T-DXd treated patient revealed genomic instability in the pre-treatment specimen (Fig. 2A, Fig. S1A). Specifically, ERBB2 copy number gain (CN = 13.34) and multiple known driver gene alterations (e.g., EP300, KMT2B, KDM5C, ABL1, MED12) were detected. Following T-DXd treatment, the tumor exhibited substantial genomic remodeling, including a decrease in ploidy from 2.94 to 2.22, normalization of ERBB2 copy number (CN = 2.1), and the majority of pre-existing driver gene alterations became undetectable. These observations suggest that the tumor underwent rapid evolution under treatment pressure and are consistent with the elimination of clones characterized by genomic instability (Fig. 2G). WGS further characterized the full spectrum of genomic rearrangements, including structural variations (SVs), somatic copy number alterations (SCNAs), extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA), kataegis and chromothripsis. A high burden of SVs was identified in the pre-treatment tumor (Deletion: 8444; Duplication: 1800; Inversion: 92,232; Insertion: 1366; Breakend: 1971). In the post‑treatment sample, inversions were dramatically reduced to 1,051, whereas the counts of deletions (9099), duplications (1761), insertions (1582), and breakends (2252) remained broadly similar. Analysis of single-base substitution (SBS) mutational signatures showed that SBS1, SBS5, and SBS46, which were present before treatment, were markedly diminished after treatment, with only a small number of SBS5 mutations remaining (Fig. S1C). This suggests that tumor subclones bearing SBS1 and SBS46 were substantially reduced during therapy. Furthermore, copy number analysis demonstrated greater intratumoral heterogeneity in the pre-treatment sample than in the post-treatment sample, consistent with a cytotoxic response to T-DXd (Fig. 2F).

Extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) refers specifically to large circular DNA identified in tumors, and has attracted considerable attention owing to its reported roles in oncogene amplification and accelerated tumor evolution during tumorigenesis and drug resistance25–27. Due to the absence of centromeres, ecDNA is randomly distributed during cell division, resulting in easier copy number gain or loss of ecDNA under survival stress13,27. Analysis of ecDNA amplification dynamics revealed that, prior to treatment, the tumor harbored two ecDNA amplicons: one located on chr17 carrying ERBB2/CDK12, and the other on chr12 carrying MDM2/CDK4 (Fig. 2B,E). A complex structural variant on chr12, characterized by a breakage-fusion-bridge (BFB) cycle, was observed to overlap with this ecDNA region (Fig. 2D). After T-DXd treatment, only a non-circular amplification event retaining the MDM2/CDK4 genes was detected, representing a focal amplification that evolved from the pre-existing chr12 ecDNA (Fig. 2C). Notably, prominent chromothripsis was observed in the same genomic region both before and after treatment, and both kataegis and chromothripsis were predominantly localized to chr12 (Fig. S1B,D).

Tumor microenvironment analysis

Pre- and post-treatment tissue samples were collected to evaluate the therapeutic effects of T-DXd. Multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) showed markedly increased CD8 + T cell infiltration after treatment, while immunohistochemistry further showed upregulated PD-1 expression in the tumor core. In contrast, a reference patient with HER2 exon 20 insertion mutation who received conventional chemotherapy maintained low CD8 + T cell infiltration with no remarkable alteration in PD-1 expression after treatment (Fig. 3A,E). These distinct immune patterns suggest that T-DXd, unlike conventional chemotherapy, may remodel the tumor immune microenvironment toward a more inflamed phenotype. Furthermore, transcriptome-based cellular deconvolution of post-treatment tumor samples suggested the presence of multiple effector immune cell subsets, including NK cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, and NKT cells, within the tumor core, consistent with the CD8 + T cell infiltration observed by mIF (Fig. 3A,C). Collectively, these findings suggest a potential shift toward an immune-hot phenotype from an immune-cold to an immune-hot state following T-DXd treatment. In addition, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) have been documented to promote tumorigenesis and metastasis and dampen immunotherapy efficacy28. In the present case, transcriptome-based deconvolution of post-treatment tumor and peritumoral tissue samples suggested that neutrophil signatures were more abundant in peritumoral and distant nontumor regions than in the tumor core. Whether this distribution pattern correlates with synergistic immunotherapeutic effects warrants further investigation. Taken together, these exploratory findings suggest that T-DXd can induce substantial tumor immune remodeling, providing a rational basis for future clinical exploration of T-DXd combined with immune checkpoint blockade.

Mechanism of adverse effects by T-DXd treatment

Patients treated with T-DXd carry a risk of pulmonary toxicity, particularly interstitial lung disease (ILD), which most commonly manifests as interstitial pneumonitis.29 In the present case, CT imaging after two cycles of T-DXd revealed subclinical interstitial changes in the right lower lobe (Fig. 3B). To further characterize these alterations, distal and peritumoral normal lung tissues were analyzed by RNA-seq and pathological examination. Previous studies have shown that alveolar macrophages within the perivascular microenvironment play a pivotal role in T-DXd-related ILD, with SPP1 (osteopontin) recognized as a critical mediator of this process.30 In our transcriptomic analysis of post-treatment surgical specimens, SPP1 expression was markedly higher in tumor tissue than in adjacent normal lung tissues, including both proximal (< 3 cm from the tumor) and distal (> 15 cm from the tumor) regions. Pathological examination demonstrated extensive inflammatory cell infiltration across all three sampled regions (Fig. 3D). Immune microenvironment deconvolution of RNA-seq data further suggested that mast cell signatures were more prominent in distal peritumoral lung tissues than in proximal regions, a pattern that a pattern that may be associated with the development of ILD. Immunohistochemical staining corroborated a relative increase in mast cell abundance in distal peritumoral tissues (Fig. 3D). These preliminary findings suggest that mast cells may contribute to T-DXd-associated pulmonary changes; however, direct evidence linking mast cell infiltration to pulmonary fibrosis and clinical ILD remains to be established.

Discussion

This exploratory case study describes a patient with locally advanced (cT2aN2M0, stage IIIA) HER2 exon 20 insertion-mutant NSCLC who achieved a partial response and underwent successful surgical resection following neoadjuvant T-DXd therapy. Although T-DXd has well-established efficacy in the advanced setting, its role as a neoadjuvant therapy for locally advanced disease remains largely unexplored. This case suggests that T-DXd can achieve substantial antitumor activity with a manageable safety profile.

WGS of paired pre- and post-treatment specimens revealed marked genomic remodeling in post-treatment tumors, including reduced ploidy, normalization of ERBB2 copy number, and the loss of most pre-existing driver gene alterations. These alterations are consistent with the elimination of dominant tumor clones characterized by genomic instability and ecDNA enrichment. Importantly, the substantial genomic heterogeneity between pre- and post-treatment samples highlights tumor genome evolution under therapeutic pressure, although sampling variation and intrinsic tumor heterogeneity may also partly account for these results.

MIF showed markedly increased CD8 + T cell infiltration and upregulated PD-1 expression in the tumor core after T-DXd treatment, immune features not observed in the reference patient receiving conventional chemotherapy. T-DXd, as an antibody-drug conjugate carrying a topoisomerase I inhibitor payload, can induce immunogenic cell death (ICD), a process that promotes dendritic cell maturation and cross-presentation of tumor antigens through the release of DAMPs31,32. This cascade may contribute to the observed increase in CD8 + T cell infiltration and the upregulation of PD-1. While these divergent immune patterns suggest that T-DXd may remodel the tumor immune microenvironment toward an inflamed phenotype, definitive causal inference is limited by the small sample size and the lack of matched pre-treatment transcriptomic data for comparison. The observed enhancement of CD8 + T cell infiltration and PD-1 upregulation provides a rationale for future prospective trials investigating T-DXd combined with PD-1 immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Patients treated with T-DXd carry a risk of interstitial lung disease (ILD), with a notably higher incidence in lung cancer than in breast or gastric cancer4. Preclinical studies have identified alveolar macrophages as primary cellular targets of T-DXd within the lung microenvironment30. The development of T-DXd-related ILD/pneumonitis may be attributed to HER2 protein expression in the bronchial and bronchiolar epithelium of the lung or a direct cytotoxic effect of T-DXd on lung tissue33. In the present case, both transcriptomic deconvolution and immunohistochemistry suggested more prominent mast cell signatures in distal peritumoral lung tissues compared with proximal regions. The preferential enrichment of mast cells in distal lung regions may reflect a drug concentration gradient, with lower T-DXd exposure driving chronic inflammatory responses that facilitate mast cell recruitment and activation. Previous studies have demonstrated that mast cells can secrete profibrotic mediators such as TGF-β, and mast cell-derived tryptase can promote fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition34. Pathological examination of resected lung tissues revealed chronic inflammatory infiltration, multinucleated giant cell reactions, and fibroproliferative changes in the peritumoral lung parenchyma. These findings suggest a potential association between mast cell infiltration and T-DXd-related pulmonary injury. However, our current data cannot establish a direct causal relationship among mast cell infiltration, pulmonary fibrosis, and clinical ILD, which warrants further mechanistic and functional investigations.

This study has several limitations. First, it only included one T-DXd-treated patient and one chemotherapy-treated reference case, which precludes formal statistical comparison and limits generalizability. Furthermore, the two cases differ substantially in clinical stage and treatment context, further limiting direct comparison. Second, transcriptomic analyses were mainly performed on post-treatment specimens; without paired pre-treatment RNA-seq data, treatment-specific alterations in gene expression and immune infiltration cannot be fully defined. Third, immune cell compositions were inferred by computational deconvolution and should be interpreted as estimated abundances rather than direct histological quantification. Fourth, the observed fibrotic changes and mast cell accumulation were noted concurrently in this single case and do not establish a causal or correlative relationship. Mechanistic insights into ILD pathogenesis require further validation in larger patient cohorts and experimental models.

In conclusion, this case report illustrates the promising efficacy and manageable toxicity of neoadjuvant T-DXd in HER2-mutant NSCLC, accompanied by profound genomic remodeling and substantial tumor immune microenvironment remodeling. These preliminary findings support further prospective evaluation of T-DXd-based combination regimens, particularly in combination with PD-1 inhibitors, and highlight the need for translational studies to predict and prevent T-DXd-associated pulmonary toxicity.
