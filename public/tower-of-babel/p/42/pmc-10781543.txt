Disease Flares Following COVID-19 Vaccination in Patients with Inflammatory Bowel Disease
Authors: Yuichiro Yoshida, Shin Fujioka, Tomohiko Moriyama, Junji Umeno, Keisuke Kawasaki, Yuta Fuyuno, Yuichi Matsuno, Yutaro Ihara, Takehiro Torisu, Takanari Kitazono
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveFlares of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) can occur infrequently after vaccination for coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), although the details of this phenomenon are poorly understood. To clarify the possibility of an unfavorable response in patients with IBD, we investigated IBD-related symptoms during the COVID-19 vaccination.MethodsBetween October 2021 and February 2022, we obtained the COVID-19 vaccination status of 411 IBD patients who were being treated at our institution. The disease course of IBD after vaccination was investigated in 188 patients with ulcerative colitis (UC) and 119 patients with Crohn's disease (CD) who had received at least one dose of the vaccine during the clinical remission phase. The baseline characteristics before vaccination were compared between the patients with UC with or without disease flares.ResultsDuring the 30-day follow-up period, eight patients with UC (4.3%) and one patient with CD (0.8%) experienced disease flares following vaccination. Disease flares occurred after the first vaccination in six patients and after the second vaccination in three patients. As for the timing of onset of disease flares, eight events (88.9%) occurred within one week of vaccination. Two patients required hospitalization, and one patient with CD required surgery for an intra-abdominal abscess. The baseline characteristics did not significantly differ between patients with UC who experienced flares and those who did not.ConclusionIBD flares following COVID-19 vaccination are rare and vaccination should therefore be recommended for patients with IBD. However, the possibility of disease flares should be considered for approximately one week after each vaccination, especially in patients with UC.

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) is a systemic infection caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), and vaccination is recommended as an important strategy to control the pandemic (1). Vaccination is recommended for most people, with particular emphasis on immunocompromised patients (2). In immunosuppressive therapy, corticosteroid treatment is associated with COVID-19 severity (3,4). Patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) often require immunosuppressive therapy, including corticosteroids. Therefore, an expert statement recommends vaccination for patients with IBD as early as possible, even if they are receiving immunomodulatory therapy (5).

Previous studies have reported that the use of vaccines, except for live vaccines, in patients with IBD is safe regardless of the administration of biologics or immunosuppressive therapies (6). However, patients with IBD are concerned about the efficacy and safety of such vaccines (7). Acute reactions such as pain, swelling, redness, fatigue, fever, headache, arthralgia, and myalgia at the injection site have been reported as common adverse events (AEs) of the COVID-19 vaccination (8); these are mild and last only a few days. Recent studies have shown that the incidence of AEs associated with vaccination was similar in patients with IBD and the general population, and most AEs tend to be minor symptoms (9,10). Among these AEs, several studies have reported instances of IBD flares following vaccination (10,11). The details regarding the disease status (e.g., type of IBD, medication status, timing of flares, treatment for flares, and outcome after treatment) of the few IBD flare cases are limited. Therefore, questions remain regarding the management of unfavorable situations for patients with IBD and their physicians.

We conducted this study to clarify the possibility of an unfavorable response to IBD and demonstrate the detailed clinical characteristics of such disease flare cases.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Study Protocol

This study was a single-center survey of patients with IBD who had been regularly treated at our institution between October 2021 and February 2022. The patient's history of COVID-19 vaccination was obtained at the time of the hospital visit. The clinical characteristics related to IBD before vaccination, treatment history, disease status during the 30 days after vaccination, and treatment for disease flare were analyzed based on the medical records of patients who had received vaccination as a reference (12).

The disease activity of ulcerative colitis (UC) was assessed using the partial Mayo score (pMayo), and the baseline remission status was defined as pMayo ≤2. Disease flare was defined as a post-vaccination pMayo ≥3 and an increase in pMayo ≥2 from the baseline. In patients with Crohn's disease (CD), the disease activity was assessed using the Harvey-Bradshaw index (HBI), whereas the International Organization for the Study of Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IOIBD) score was adapted for patients with stoma because of the difficulty of HBI evaluation. Baseline remission status was defined as an HBI ≤3 or IOIBD ≤1. In patients with CD without a stoma, disease flare was defined as an HBI ≥4 after vaccination and an increase in HBI of ≥2 from baseline. In CD patients with a stoma, disease flare was defined as an increase in IOIBD of ≥2 from baseline.

After vaccination, some patients experience fever and general malaise as vaccine-induced AEs. To distinguish these symptoms from IBD flares, only those that lasted ≥4 days were reflected in each score.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Kyushu University Hospital (No. 21164-00).

Statistical Analysis

Vaccination coverage and the prevalence of UC and CD were compared using Fisher's exact test. The clinical characteristics of patients with an exacerbation of UC conditions after vaccination and those who remained in remission were compared using the Mann-Whitney U-test, Fisher's exact test, or chi-square test, where appropriate. Incidence was compared using the Cox proposal hazard method. p values＜0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro version 15 software program (SAS Institute, Tokyo, Japan).

Results

Study flow

The flowchart of the study is shown in Figure. A history of COVID-19 vaccination was investigated in 411 patients with IBD (232 UC and 179 CD) during the study period. Of these, 208 patients with UC (89.6%) and 148 patients with CD (82.7%) had received at least one vaccination, with significantly lower rates of vaccination in patients with CD (p=0.04). As the purpose of this study was to determine the risk of IBD flares caused by vaccination, 20 patients with UC and 29 patients with CD who were in the active phase at the time of vaccination were excluded from the study. Finally, 188 patients with UC and 119 with CD who were vaccinated while in remission were included in the analysis; 10 patients with CD had stomas.

Characteristics of patients with flares of IBD conditions after vaccination

The clinical characteristics of disease-flare cases after vaccination are shown in Table 1: eight cases (4.3%) of UC and one case (0.8%) of CD had disease flares with no significant difference in the flare rate between the two diseases (p=0.16). In UC patients, the incidence of disease flares following vaccination was 2.9 per 100 person-months compared with 1.3 in non-vaccinated patients. The Cox proportional hazards method showed no significant differences between the two groups. Three of the nine patients were male and six were female; the median age was 62 years (range, 37-83 years). All cases of disease flares were vaccinated with BNT162b2 (BioNTech-Pfizer), and flares occurred in five cases of UC and one case of CD after the first dose. In addition, flares occurred in three patients with UC after the second dose. Eight cases (88.9%) of disease flares occurred within one week of vaccination, and two patients (22.2%) required hospitalization. Among the eight patients with UC experiencing digestive disease, five had hematochezia. Three patients with UC underwent endoscopy, and these patients were confirmed to have active disease. Additionally, one case of CD was confirmed to have active disease on endoscopy and computed tomography (CT) examination. None of the patients discontinued IBD treatment during vaccination. Regarding treatment, systemic steroids were introduced or increased in three cases of UC, while biologic therapy was introduced in three cases of UC, and surgery was performed for an intra-abdominal abscess in one case of CD. Five of the nine cases (55.6%) had prolonged symptoms for ＞30 days after vaccination. Of the six patients with disease flares after the first vaccination, three cases received a second vaccination after remission and progressed without disease flares. Two patients had persistent symptoms but were unaware of their vaccination involvement and received a second dose four weeks after the first vaccination. One patient declined a second vaccination because of persistent symptoms.

Comparison of the clinical characteristics of patients with UC with flares and those who remained in remission after vaccination

For the eight cases of UC with disease flares, the clinical characteristics were compared with those of non-flared cases to investigate the risk factors of disease flares (Table 2). Flared and non-flared cases were compared for age, sex, disease type, duration of disease, and duration of the active phase in the six months before vaccination, but there were no significant differences between the two groups in any of these parameters. At the time of the survey, there were three cases of previous COVID-19 infection in non-flared patients. Regarding medication at the time of vaccination, none of the flared cases were receiving biological therapies, and the use of each therapeutic agent, including biological agents, did not significantly differ between the two groups. All flared cases received BNT162b2 (BioNTech-Pfizer), but at least 83.9% of non-flared cases also received the same vaccine, with no significant difference in vaccine type between the two groups (p=0.6). Seven of the flared cases and all non-flared cases received a second dose of the same type of vaccine as the first dose. In Japan, a third dose of priority vaccination has been available for healthcare workers since December 2021, but no patients had been vaccinated for the third time during the study period.

Discussion

Flares of the primary disease after COVID-19 vaccination have been reported in autoimmune diseases, including systemic rheumatic diseases (13), systemic lupus erythematosus (14,15), and hematologic diseases, including idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (16), paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (17), and IgA nephropathy (12,18). Similarly, disease flares after COVID-19 vaccination have been reported in IBD, although only infrequently (19-21). Several studies have reported the flare rate after COVID-19 vaccination has been shown. In the largest American study on AEs of the COVID-19 vaccine in patients with IBD, the flare rate requiring treatment modification was only 2% (11). In addition, a meta-analysis of four studies that mentioned IBD flares after COVID-19 vaccination reported an integrated flare rate of only 1% (10,22-24). In the vaccine database, diarrhea occurred in 2-3% cases (25), and it is difficult to distinguish between abdominal symptoms caused by vaccination and IBD flares. The greater proportion of abdominal symptoms in UC cases in this study and some cases of IBD flares confirmed by imaging suggest that there are few cases of disease flares following vaccination. Previous studies have not examined AEs and flares separately for different IBD types. There tended to be fewer disease flares with CD than with UC, but this may be because CD is known to be more difficult to diagnose than UC, especially in the small bowel type (26).

Previous studies on patients with IBD have provided limited information on the timing of flares after vaccination and the course of treatment for disease flares. In addition, the causal relationship between vaccination and disease flares was unclear because patients who were in an active phase at the time of vaccination were not excluded from these studies. This study was limited to patients in remission, and the clinical characteristics and course of treatment after COVID-19 vaccination were followed for an extended period, thus indicating that most disease flares occur within one week of vaccination. We also observed several cases of disease flares after the second vaccination, although these patients did not experience any flare-up after the first dose. These results suggest that regardless of the number of previous COVID-19 vaccinations or a history of IBD flares after vaccination, attention should be paid to the IBD status for approximately one week after vaccination. However, in this study, most of the flare cases after vaccination avoided serious events, such as hospitalization or surgery, with adequate treatment. Even in cases with disease flares after the first vaccination, patients who were adequately treated remained stable after the second vaccination. The flare rate of IBD following COVID-19 vaccination has been reported to be comparable to that of matched unvaccinated patients with IBD (27), and the risk of severe COVID-19 has been shown in patients with IBD receiving systemic steroids. Therefore, vaccination should not be avoided, especially in patients with IBD receiving immunomodulatory therapy, considering the benefits of preventing severe COVID-19.

We herein investigated the clinical predictors of disease flare following vaccination in patients with UC in remission but found no significant risk factors. In addition, several studies have shown that most AEs associated with vaccination for IBD are mild. These results suggest that at least in remission, patients with UC should be vaccinated regardless of their baseline clinical characteristics. Furthermore, none of the eight patients with UC in this study with flares following COVID-19 vaccination were receiving biologic treatment, although 20% of the patients with UC in remission were. This result may have a similar mechanism to the low incidence of AEs following COVID-19 vaccination in patients with IBD receiving biological treatments (9). A recent study showed that patients with IBD receiving anti-tumor necrosis factor α had lower antibody titers after two vaccinations than those who did not receive this treatment, although a minimal change in the IBD activity was observed due to vaccinations compared with that in patients who did not receive this treatment (28). Patients receiving biologics may require a more intensified vaccination schedule than those not receiving biologics to achieve adequate vaccine efficacy (29). However, the occurrence of AEs and their impact on the disease course of IBD are of less concern and appear to be encouraging for patients on biologics.

However, the immunological mechanisms underlying IBD flares remain unclear. Vaccines stimulate T-cell immunity (30) and induce elevated cytokine production in IBD patients (20). Considering that the multifactorial nature of IBD flares involves infection, intestinal bacteria, and various environmental factors (31), the potential impact of vaccines on IBD flares might be limited in this study.

One limitation associated with this study was the small number of cases included in the analysis because this was a single-center study. In addition, as a university hospital, we received many referrals of poorly controlled IBD cases, which may have caused some selection bias and a relatively high flare rate. Furthermore, in this study, CD flares were defined mainly by HBI, which focuses on the assessment of subjective symptoms but may not correctly assess the disease activity because it includes several subjective interpretations (32,33). To resolve these concerns, more studies with a larger number of cases, including biomarker, endoscopic, and cross-sectional imaging studies, are required. The indications for vaccination in active cases and the timing of vaccination should continue to be tailored for each patient.

In conclusion, the impact of vaccination on disease flares was limited in most patients with IBD, and vaccination should continue to be recommended while COVID-19 persists. However, clinicians should pay attention to the possibility of flare-ups for approximately one week after COVID-19 vaccination, especially for patients with UC, and take appropriate measures to prevent the occurrence of flares. We believe that this study provides important insights into the routine management of patients with IBD, as COVID-19 vaccination may continue to be required in the near future.
