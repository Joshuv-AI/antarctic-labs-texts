Ribosome Tunnel Environment Drives the Formation of α-Helix during Cotranslational Folding
Authors: Takunori Yasuda, Rikuri Morita, Yasuteru Shigeta, Ryuhei Harada
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Protein conformations in cells are not solely determined by amino acid sequences; they also depend on cellular environments. For instance, the ribosome tunnel induces its specific α-helix formation during cotranslational folding. Owing to the link between these temporally α-helix and biological functions, the mechanism of α-helix formation inside the ribosome tunnel has been previously explored. Consequently, the conformational restrictions of the tunnel were considered one of the driving forces of α-helix formation. Conversely, the ribosomal tunnel environment, including its chemical properties, appears to influence the α-helix formation. However, a comprehensive analysis of the ribosome tunnel environment’s impact on the α-helix formation has not been conducted yet due to challenges in experimentally controlling it. Therefore, as a new computational approach, we proposed a ribosome environment-mimicking model (REMM) based on the radius and components of the experimentally determined ribosome tunnel structures. Using REMM, we assessed the impact of the ribosome tunnel environment on α-helix formation. Herein, we employed carbon nanotubes (CNT) as a reference model alongside REMM because CNT reproduce conformational restrictions rather than the ribosome tunnel environment. Quantitatively, the ability to reproduce the α-helix of nascent peptides in the experimental structure was compared between the CNT and REMM using enhanced all-atom molecular dynamics simulations. Consequently, the REMM more accurately reproduced the α-helix of the nascent peptides than the CNT, highlighting the significance of the ribosome tunnel environment in α-helix formation. Additionally, we analyzed the properties of the peptide inside each model to reveal the mechanism of ribosome tunnel-specific α-helix formation. Consequently, we revealed that the chemical diversities of the tunnel are essential for the formation of backbone-to-backbone hydrogen bonds in the peptides. In conclusion, the ribosome tunnel environment, with the diverse chemical properties, drives its specific α-helix formation. By proposing REMM, we newly provide the technical basis for investigating the protein conformations in various cellular environments.

Introduction

Proteins must undergo proper folding and adopt optimal conformations to perform their desired functions. Hence, to elucidate the factors that determine protein conformations enormous efforts have been made.1−4 To date, a thermodynamic hypothesis has been established, in which the amino acid sequence determines the native protein conformations in a dilute environment.5,6 Contrastingly, previous studies revealed that protein conformations also depend on the several factors in cells.7−10 For example, external conditions such as temperature11−13 pH14−16 dielectric properties17−19 and the density of the protein20−23 affect protein conformations. Furthermore, inside each organelle24,25 or liquid droplet26,27 proteins have specific conformations, and the relationships between such environment-specific conformations and protein functions have been reported.28−30 Nonetheless, given the challenges in observing protein conformations inside a specific environment, in addition to its experimental control, the effect of each cellular environment on protein conformations has not yet been well-elucidated.31 Therefore, we propose a new computational approach to mimic the cellular environment and focus on the ribosome tunnel as the key cellular environment. Based on this new approach, we investigated how the ribosome tunnel environment affects protein conformations.

By referring to protein conformations inside the ribosome tunnel during cotranslational folding,32−34 its specific α-helix formation substantially differs from dilute solution.35−39 For instance, some strand proteins in dilute solutions form α-helix when present in the ribosome tunnel.40−42 Furthermore, such temporary α-helix formation is strongly related to biological functions, such as the translational regulation,43−45 and the assistance of protein folding in a postribosomal release.39,46−51 Since the α-helix formation inside the ribosome tunnel is not observed for all sequences, in addition to the intrinsic potential of sequence to form α-helix,52,53 the ribosome tunnel environment is considered to act as a driving force of the α-helix formation.45,54

Among the ribosome tunnel environment, the effect of conformational restrictions of the tunnel on α-helix formations was widely evaluated. For example, a theoretical study showed that only the confinement in a cylindrical tunnel can entropically stabilize α-helical conformations.55 Furthermore, carbon nanotube (CNT) was adopted as a well-established tunnel model.56−58 For instance, several MD simulation studies using CNT revealed that the radius and water-mediated interactions of CNT strongly affect α-helix formation.59,60 According to these studies, the conformational restrictions from the confinement of the tunnel was considered to be a driving force in α-helix formation. Contrastingly, the chemical interactions between the α-helix residues in the nascent peptides and the RNA or proteins inside the tunnel were frequently detected in the experimental structures.42,61,62 Furthermore, computational studies have revealed that the electrical potential and hydrophobic properties of the ribosome tunnel affect the conformations of the nascent peptide.63−65 These studies indicate that ribosome-specific chemical properties such as electricity, hydrophobicity, and direct-binding receptors influence α-helix formation in addition to the conformational restrictions of the tunnel. However, the influence of the ribosomal tunnel environment, including its chemical properties, on α-helix formation has not been completely elucidated. This is attributed to the challenges in exhaustively determining the conformations of nascent peptides and experimentally controlling the ribosome tunnel environment.66

Herein, ribosome environment-mimicking model (REMM) was proposed as a new computational approach to investigate how the ribosome tunnel environment influences α-helix formation during cotranslational folding. To construct the REMM, we extracted the ribosome structures in complex with the nascent peptides inside the tunnel through a comprehensive search of the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Subsequently, we constructed the REMM based on the radius and components of the extracted ribosome tunnel structures to mimic the ribosome tunnel environment, including the conformational restrictions and chemical properties of the tunnel. After constructing the REMM, we investigated the properties of the 17 extracted nascent peptides in complex with the ribosome inside the CNT, in addition to the REMM with an enhanced molecular dynamics simulation (MD). Herein, the CNT was adopted as a reference model because it only mimics conformational restrictions and cannot consider the chemical properties of the ribosome tunnel. Based on these all-atom enhanced MD simulations, we evaluated the ability of the CNT and REMM to reproduce the α-helix of the nascent peptides in the experimental structures and revealed whether the ribosome tunnel environment influences α-helix formation. Furthermore, we elucidated the ribosome tunnel-specific mechanisms of α-helix formation by investigating the conformations of those peptides inside each model. Finally, we discuss the prospects of REMM as a new computational protocol for investigating protein conformations in various cellular environments.

Measuring the Ribosome Tunnel Radius and Components

The properties of the tunnel were investigated to determine the optimal conditions for the CNT and REMM using the extracted 9 ribosomal structures complexed with the nascent peptides. Particularly, the radii (Sections 2.3, 2.4.) and components of the ribosome tunnel (Section 2.5) were measured.

Measuring the Tunnel Radius with a Probe

Initially, the 9 complexes were aligned, and the ribosome structures were edited so that the residues in the ribosome tunnel were located <12 Å from each nascent peptide (Figure S2). Moreover, for all the complexes, only the tunnel structures were selected by excluding the nascent peptide (Figure 2A). Subsequently, MOLE2.5,72 which is a tunnel search algorithm used in previous studies,73 was employed to measure the tunnel radius for each ribosome tunnel. The detailed parameters of MOLE2.5 are provided in the Supporting Information. Consequently, for all complexes, the radius with MOLE2.5 (rProbe: Figure 2B, upper) was calculated at successive points along the centerline of the tunnel from the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) to the exit (Figure S3), and their profiles were averaged (Figure 2C, upper). Herein, to normalize the coordinate space, the distance of each sphere center was defined as the distance from the common origin. The tunnel radius ranged from 1.2 to 8.3 Å, and the average value of rProbe was approximately 5.3 Å (Table 1). Additionally, two major troughs were present around 30 and 50 Å from the PTC. These profiles of the tunnels corresponded to those reported in previous studies,73 concluding that the parameters of MOLE2.5 software were suitable for tunnel investigation. The distance with the smallest radius (34 Å from the PTC) around the first major trough was defined as a constriction site, and each tunnel was divided into upper and lower parts before and after this site. Subsequently, the relationship between the rProbe and the α-helix formation was investigated to check whether it was suitable for describing the inner radius used in each model (the CNT or REMM). Quantitatively, the number of α-helix residues at an arbitrary rProbe was calculated based on the frequency of the α-helix residues along the distance from the PTC (Figure S4). Almost half of the α-helix was formed with rProbe < 5.0 Å (Figure S5, blue). However, the preceding study demonstrated that the α-helix of the poly-A sequence was not formed in the CNT with inner radius <5.6 Å owing to the physical restrictions of the cylinder.59 Furthermore, the radius of the ribosome-mimicking cylinder model was set at approximately 9.0 Å in a previous study.51 These contradictions with previous studies suggest that the rProbe may not provide sufficient space to form the α-helix inside the ribosome tunnel.

Measuring the Effective Tunnel Radius with Area Integration

We defined an effective radius (rEffective: Figure 2B, lower) that allows the calculation of multiple area integration at successive points along the centerline of the tunnel from the PTC to measure the inner radius of the ribosome tunnel more accurately. Detailed schemes of measuring rEffective are provided in the Supporting Information and Figure S6. We obtained the profiles of rEffective along the ribosome tunnel from the PTC to the exit for all complexes (Figure S7), and their profiles were averaged. The average profiles of rEffective were similar to those of rProbe, indicating that rEffective reproduced the structural features of the ribosome tunnel (Figure 2C). The rEffective value was distributed from 4.7 to 13.2 Å, and the average value of rEffective was approximately 10.56 Å (Table 1). As well as rProbe, the distance with the smallest radius (33 Å from the PTC) around the first major trough was defined as the constriction site and divided each tunnel into upper and lower parts before and after the construction site. Table 1 lists the average, minimum, and maximum values of rEffective for each part (whole, upper, and lower). In the same way as rProbe, the count of α-helix residues was traced along rEffective (Figure S5, red). Consequently, all α-helix-formed residues were observed over 7.0 Å, suggesting that rEffective provides sufficient space to form the α-helix. Thus, rEffective was used as the radius parameter for the CNT and REMM.

Measuring the Components on the Tunnel Surface

The protocol described in the Supporting Information detects atoms on the tunnel surface at successive points from the PTC to the exit. Based on the correspondence listed in Table S2, the detected atoms were divided into the following 12 classified residues:(1–2) proteic backbone: the carbonyl and amino group (3–6) proteic sidechain: the polar, nonpolar, positive, and negative groups, (7–8) nucleic backbone: the ribose and phosphate groups, (9–12) nucleic sidechain: the adenine, uracil, guanine, and cytosine groups. Consequently, the components of the classified residues on the tunnel surface were obtained at successive points along the centerline of the tunnel from the peptidyl the PTC to the exit for all 9 complexes (Figure S8), and their profiles were averaged (Figure 2D). The ribosome tunnel was composed of various residues, and the tendency of each component was clearly different between the upper and lower parts. Specifically, the proteic residues were scarcely present in the upper part, whereas they were present in the lower part. Constructing the CNT and REMM

The REMM and CNT were constructed based on the investigated radii and components. For the construction of REMM, we prepared the 12 types of functional residues (Figure 3A) to mimic the classified residues defined in Section 2.5. These functional residues were arranged in a circular pattern in a single layer, and the layers were vertically stacked (Figure 3B, left). Consequently, the REMM was modeled into a cylindrical form with a consistent radius maintained. The REMM was independently constructed for the upper and lower parts to reflect a clear difference in the component residues of the ribosome tunnel (Figure 2D). The REMM radius for the upper and lower parts was set to 19.4 and 22.68 Å by taking the average value of rEffective in each part. The number of functional residues included in a single layer was set to 10 and 11 in the upper and lower models, respectively. The number of functional residues was defined by considering the residue density of the ribosome tunnel and the REMM uniformity (Supporting Information). The population of each residue in an individual layer was defined to align with the population of the classified residues in the experimental structures based on the number of functional residues (Table 2). The order of the functional residues was randomized in each layer to maintain the REMM uniformity. Through these processes, the 3D conformations of the REMM for the upper and lower parts were constructed (Figure 3C; left, center). For the construction of the CNT, a cylindrical conformation was constructed using the cnt-gaff program74,75 (Figure 3B,C, right). The radii of CNT in the upper and lower parts were set to be the same as each radius for the REMM. Finally, the vertical lengths of the CNT and REMM (ZModel) were determined according to the length of each sequence (ZPeptide); that is ZModel = ZPeptide + 15 × 2 (Figure 3B). The box size was set to 50 × 50 × ZBox for all the systems, implying the vertical length of each box was specified as follows: ZBox = ZModel + 15 × 2.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations of Peptides inside the CNT and REMM

The sequence of each nascent peptide in the upper and lower parts was determined based on the constriction site defined in average rEffective. Consequently, 9 sequences in the upper part and 8 sequences in the lower part were prepared, and Figure S1 provides their amino acid sequences for each part. Finally, conformations of each sequence were investigated inside the CNT and REMM using all-atom MD simulations, that is, 34 systems were considered.

The replica exchange solute tempering 2 (REST2) method76 was employed as an enhanced sampling method to efficiently sample the secondary structure (SS) formation of each peptide inside each model. In the present REST2 simulations, the temperatures ranged from 300 to 550 K, and a 250 ns simulation was performed for each replica. The first 50 ns were discarded as an equilibration process, and the last 200 ns of the trajectories at 300 K were analyzed. In all replicas, the time interval for exchanging them was 2 ps. We established a consistent exchange rate between 20 and 30% by adjusting the number of replicas within the range of 6 to 14 (Figure S10). All simulations was conducted thrice for statistical analyses. The arrangement of functional residues in the REMM was randomized in each iteration. Totally, the computational cost of all systems was 228 μs, and Table S4 provides the computational details. The conditional control and relaxed processes in the REST2 simulation are provided in the Supporting Information. Notably, the initial conformation of each peptide was modeled as a fully α-helix using AmberTools23.77

Computationally, the REST2 simulations were performed using the GPU version of the GROMACS 2022.5 package78,79 with PLUMED 2.82.80,81 The Amber14SBonlysc force field82 was employed for all peptides. For the force field parameters, the cnt-gaff74,75 was employed for the CNT, and quantum mechanics (QM) simulations with Gaussian 1683 were conducted for the functional residue in the REMM. The computational details of the QM simulation are provided in the Supporting Information.

Analyzing the Peptide Conformations in the REST2 Simulations

Inside the CNT and REMM, the SS of each peptide was measured using the Define Secondary Structure of Proteins (DSSP) implemented in CPPTRAJ.84 All amino acid sequences were divided into three groups based on the measured SS: α-helix: α-helix, Sheet: parallel β-sheet and antiparallel β-sheet, and Coil: other SS types. Additionally, we analyzed the hydrogen bonds (H-bond) of each peptide inside the CNT and REMM (Section 3.2) with the H-bond analysis package in MDAnalysis.85,86 This program was also used to calculate the distance-based contact (Sections 3.2, 3.3) and the distribution of each peptide inside the CNT and REMM during the REST2 simulation (Section 3.3).

Results and Discussion

Ribosome Tunnel Environment is Indispensable for α-Helix Formation

We compared the abilities of the CNT and REMM in reproducing the α-helix of the 17 nascent peptides determined inside the ribosome tunnel extracted from the PDB (Sections 2.1 and 2.7) to evaluate whether the ribosome tunnel environment influences the α-helix formation. First, the SS population of the nascent peptides in the experimental structures was computed. The coils were predominantly observed (approximately 70–80%), α-helix comprised approximately 20–30% of all populations, and sheets were infrequently seen in the upper and lower parts (Figure 4A). Subsequently, the average SS population of the peptides inside the CNT or REMM during the REST2 simulations was computed for the upper and lower parts (Figure 4B). Consequently, as well as experimental structures, the sheets were rarely formed inside either model. Contrastingly, the α-helix population was clearly different between the CNT and REMM. Specifically, the α-helix were scarcely formed inside the CNT, whereas α-helix constituted 15–20% inside the REMM, indicating that the reproduction of the experimental SS population in the REMM was higher than that in the CNT. To evaluate the ability to reproduce the α-helix formation in experimental structures in detail, we measured the α-helix frequency of the residues that formed α-helix in the experimental structures. The target residues inside the CNT hardly formed α-helix in the upper and lower parts, whereas the target residues inside the REMM successfully formed α-helix approximately 20% (the upper part) and 30% (the lower part) of the total simulation time (Figure 4C). As a more detailed analysis, we assessed the α-helix tendency of each amino acid (aa) between the experimental structures and REST2 simulations for the CNT and REMM (Figure 4D). For the experimental structures, the α-helix tendency was defined as using the total number of the amino acids of all peptides, NaaTotal, and that of the amino acids observed as α-helix, NaaHelix. For the REST2 simulations, the average frequency at which each formed α-helix during each simulation was computed. Quantitatively, we calculated the correlation coefficient of the α-helix tendency, R, between the experimental structures and the peptide conformations sampled by the REST2 simulations. Consequently, the R values of the CNT were −0.201 and 0.187 for the upper and lower parts, respectively, whereas those of the REMM were 0.611 and 0.671. Regarding the p-values, that of the CNT were 0.410 and 0.445 for the upper and lower parts, respectively, whereas those of the REMM were 0.005 and 0.002. These results indicate that the REMM more accurately reproduced the α-helix formation tendency of 20 types of amino acids found in the experimental structures. Based on these analyses, we concluded that REMM has a higher ability to reproduce the α-helix of the nascent peptides in the experimental structures. Thus, the ribosome tunnel environment, including its specific chemical properties, is indispensable for the α-helix formation since CNT is a minimal model that only reflect conformational restrictions from the confining.

Differences in the Backbone and Other Atom Contact between the CNT and REMM

The result in the previous section indicates that the ribosome tunnel environment is one of the driving forces of its specific α-helix formation. Hence, we attempt to unravel the mechanism of the ribosome tunnel-specific α-helix formation by investigating why the REMM had a higher ability to reproduce the α-helix formation in experimental structures than the CNT. Particularly, we focused on the contact of the backbone atoms (Cα, C, O, and N) of the peptide inside each model because the backbone contact determines the α-helix formation. Initially, the average number of H-bounds in backbone-to-backbone, backbone-to-sidechain and backbone-to-water was counted for the upper and lower parts inside the CNT and REMM. The number of H-bonds was normalized to the total number of residues in each peptide and averaged over all simulations and systems. Consequently, in all systems, the most H-bonds were formed in the backbone-to-water molecules, followed by the H-bonds in the backbone-to-backbone, and backbone-to-sidechains (Figure 5A). Subsequently, the backbone-to-tunnel distance-based contact was counted owing to the lack of the hydrogen bond receptors or acceptors in the CNT. Herein, the contact distance between the heavy atoms of the backbone and that of the tunnel was set as 4.0 Å to identify potential contact that replaces hydrogen atoms with the backbone atoms. As shown in Figure 5B, the backbone-to-tunnel contact in the REMM was clearly lower than that in the CNT for the upper and lower parts. Finally, those 4 types of contact (Self H-bonds of the backbone, the backbone H-bonds with sidechains, water molecules, and distance-based contact between the backbone and the tunnel) in the REMM was divided by that in the CNT to evaluate the differences of each contact between the CNT and REMM more quantitatively. Consequently, contacts other than backbone-to-water were different between the CNT and REMM (Figure 5C). Specifically, the backbone-to-backbone contact (Figure 5C, red) in the REMM was approximately double (Lower) to triple (Upper) than that in the CNT. Contrastingly, the contact of the backbone-to-sidechain (Figure 5C, yellow) and backbone-to-tunnel (Figure 5C, blue) in the REMM was approximately one-half only in the lower part and one-half in both parts than those in the CNT, respectively. Considering the relationships between (1) backbone-to-backbone, (2) backbone-to-tunnel, and (3) backbone-to-sidechain contact, decreasing either the backbone-to-tunnel or backbone-to-sidechain contact can enhance the mobility of backbone atoms, leading to an increase in the backbone-to-backbone contact. Based on these relationships, the decrease in the backbone-to-tunnel/sidechain contact in the REMM appears to increase the backbone-to-backbone contact, which induces its high ability to reproduce the α-helix formation in experimental structures.

Reasons for Differences in the Contact between the CNT and REMM

Since the absolute amount of backbone-to-tunnel contact in the REMM were remarkable compared to that of the backbone-to-sidechain contact (Figure 5A,C), we first explored why the backbone-to-tunnel contact decreased in the REMM. Specifically, we hypothesize that the attractive forces on the REMM tunnel are weaker compared to those on the CNT tunnel. To evaluate this hypothesis, we investigated the distributions of backbone and sidechain atoms inside both models by measuring their distance from the tunnel center (Figure 5D). According to these distributions, the backbone and sidechain atoms were extremely restricted near the tunnel in the CNT, whereas they were not restricted near the tunnel and broadly distributed as a Gaussian with a peak around the midpoint between the center and the tunnel in the REMM. In other words, the mobility of backbone and sidechain atoms in the REMM was enhanced compared to that in the CNT. Hence, we conclude that the attractive forces on the REMM tunnel are weaker than that on the CNT, which might be one of the reasons for the decrease in backbone-to-tunnel contact.

Substantially, upon comparing the distributions of sidechain atoms with those of backbone atoms in the REMM, we found that the peak points of sidechain atoms tended to be closer to the tunnel than those of backbone atoms, a trend not evident in the CNT. To confirm this tendency more quantitatively, we measured the relative orientations of Cβ atoms for Cα atoms as Δd = dCβ – dCα, where dCα and dCβ are the distances between the tunnel center and each atom. Herein, positive Δd values resulted in the Cβ atoms localizing closer to the tunnel than the Cα atoms. According to the distributions of Δd values in CNT, besides a narrow distribution centered around Δd = 2, a broader distribution centered around Δd = 0 was also observed (Figure 5E, left). In contrast, in the REMM, a broader distribution centered around Δd = 0 was absent, and the frequency of Δd in the REMM monotonically increased (Figure 5E right). Quantitatively, when integrating the area where Δd > 0 in Figure 5E, we found that the areas in the upper and lower parts of the REMM increased by approximately 30 and 20%, respectively, compared to those in the CNT. These results suggest that the tendency for sidechain atoms to be closer to the tunnel than the backbone atoms is more evident in the REMM than in the CNT. Because sidechain atoms that are closer to the tunnel than backbone atoms can spatially inhibit or displace backbone-to-tunnel contact, having more sidechain atoms near the tunnel in the REMM compared to the CNT might explain the decrease in backbone-to-tunnel contact in the REMM.

Supplementally, we discussed the decrease in the backbone-to-sidechain contact in the lower part of the REMM compared to the CNT (Figure 5C, lower, yellow). Since an increase in the sidechain-to-sidechain contact can directly reduce the backbone-to-sidechain contact, we calculated the average number of sidechain-to-sidechain H-bonds for both the upper and lower parts of the CNT and REMM. Subsequently, the relative ratios were calculated by dividing the REMM contact by that in the CNT. Consequently, the sidechain-to-sidechain H-bonds in the REMM were about one-and-a-half times more numerous than those in the CNT (Figure S11). Therefore, the increase in sidechain-to-sidechain H-bonds might be one of the reasons for the decrease in the backbone-to-sidechain contact in the REMM. Herein, the increase in the sidechain-to-sidechain contact might result from an increase in the mobility of sidechain atoms, as illustrated in Figure 5D. Additionally, in the upper region, the absolute number of the backbone-sidechain contact was small (Figure 4A), which may explain why a decreasing trend in the sidechain-to-backbone contact was not clearly observed (Figure 5C, upper, yellow).

In summary, the weakened attractive forces on the REMM tunnel might be one of the reasons for the decreased backbone-to-tunnel and backbone-to-sidechain contact. Additionally, the stronger tendency for sidechain atoms to be closer to the tunnel than the backbone atoms also seems to contribute to the decrease in the backbone-to-tunnel contact.

Mechanism of α-Helix Formation inside the Ribosome Tunnel

In this section, we summarize the reason why the α-helix formation inside the CNT and REMM differed. Furthermore, based on the REMM analyses, the mechanism of ribosome tunnel-specific α-helix formation was discussed.

In the CNT, the strengthened attractive force on the tunnel seems to decrease the backbone-to-backbone contact, inducing the low reproductivity of α-helix formation in experimental structures (Figure 5F, left). Indeed, previous studies have shown that reducing the interaction between the peptide and CNT leads to an increase in α-helix formation.57,59 Conversely, in the REMM, the weakened attractive forces on the tunnel and the strengthened tendency for sidechain atoms to be closer to the tunnel than the backbone atoms seem to increase the backbone-to-backbone contact, thereby improving the reproducibility of the α-helix formation in experimental structures (Figure 5F, right).

Given that CNT is a uniform and excessively hydrophobic cylinder, the weakened attractive forces on REMM tunnel might be due to the diverse chemical properties inside REMM. Furthermore, considering that peptides have chemically diverse sidechains, the diverse properties of REMM tunnel seem to contribute to a stronger tendency for sidechain atoms to be closer to the tunnel than the backbone atoms. Indeed, when investigated the frequency of distance-based contact between the sidechain atoms of α-helix-formed residues and the functional residues of the tunnel (see the Supporting Information for the definition of the contact), various sidechain-to-tunnel interactions were observed, as shown in Figure 5G. In summary, the diverse chemical properties of REMM tunnel are important for the increase in the backbone-to-backbone contact and contribute to the higher ability to reproduce the α-helix formation.

The mechanism of α-helix formation in the ribosomal tunnel may be the same as that of REMM because our model reproduced the α-helix of the nascent peptides in the experimental structures to some extent (Figure 4). Specifically, the diversity of the ribosome tunnel residues might induce the backbone-to-backbone contact of the nascent peptides, promoting the α-helix formation. In other words, the ribosome tunnel environment, with its diverse chemical properties, is one of the driving forces of its specific α-helix formation.

Limitations and Prospects of REMM

The modeling ribosome tunnel by considering its component and radius may be effective for representing α-helix formation inside the computational tunnel models since REMM reproduced the α-helix of the nascent peptides in the experimental structures to some extent. Contrastingly, REMM has room for improvement in the reproduction of α-helix formation (Figure 4). To improve REMM, we focused on four gaps between the ribosome tunnel and REMM that might influence the α-helix formation. The first is the difference in the tunnel shape; the ribosome tunnel is a structurally complicated shape with the various radii (Figure 2C), whereas REMM is modeled as a cylinder with a uniform radius. The second is the difference in the residue arrangement as components; this arrangement depends on their location in the ribosome tunnel, whereas REMM randomly arranges them on the tunnel surface. The α-helix formations partially depend on the specific geometry and arrangement of the residues inside the ribosome tunnel since the contact between the peptide and the tunnel is essential for the α-helix formation as shown in Section 3.4. Hence, the gaps in the above points may effect on the reproductivity of α-helix formations in REMM. Third, the difference in the water molecules distribution in the tunnel. Previous study have reported that water molecules distribution is not uniform inside the ribosome tunnel, that is, there are dense/sparse regions exist inside the tunnel.87 Contrastingly, the water distribution in REMM was almost uniform because REMM was modeled as an open-ended CNT. The areas with few water molecules in the ribosome tunnel could promote the formation of α-helix since the H-bond between the backbone and water molecules displaces the backbone-to-backbone H-bond. Hence, the difference in the water molecules distribution also affects the reproductivity of α-helix formation in REMM. Indeed, the backbone frequently formed H-bonds with water molecules (Figure 5A), indicating that the amount of water molecules in REMM seems to be excessive. Additionally, previous studies reported that water molecules significantly impact ribosomal functions,88,89 and further support the possible influence of water molecules on α-helix formation. Fourthly, we consider the difference in dynamic properties of a nascent peptide inside the tunnel. In REMM, the growth of a nascent chain inside the tunnel was not accurately simulated because the peptide was split into two parts and not elongated to assess only the effect of the ribosome tunnel environment on α-helix formation. Conversely, in cells, the synthesis of amino acids happens continuously at a rate of several to dozens per second (0.01s ∼ 0.1s/aa),90 pushing out a nascent peptide toward the tunnel exit. Furthermore, previous studies have identified such force and speed of growth as factors that influence α-helix formation inside the tunnel.29,91 Consequently, the omission of these dynamic properties in the folding of a nascent peptide also affects the reproducibility of α-helix formation in REMM. In summary, REMM’s ability to reproduce the formation of α-helix in the ribosome tunnels can be enhanced considering these gaps.

As a prospect, REMM can approximately predict α-helix formation inside ribosome tunnels with low computational costs. We need to investigate whether target proteins form α-helix inside the ribosomal tunnel from the relationship between α-helix formation and translational regulation.43−45 However, the experimental costs of determining the conformations inside the ribosome tunnel are still high. Since ribosome profiling is regarded as an established method that can measure the translational rate of a target sequence,92,93 integrating it with REMM allows one to investigate the relationship between the formation of secondary structures inside the tunnel and the translation rate. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that combining force-profile analysis with photoinduced energy-transfer fluorescence correlation spectroscopy can identify the conformational fluctuations of a nascent peptide inside the tunnel.42,50,94 However, these methods provide only limited information, including whether there are any large-scale structural changes or intermediate states. Thus, integrating REMM enables a more detailed tracking of the cotranslational folding process. In summary, REMM bridges data gaps between experimental and computational approaches, which might enhance our understanding of how α-helix formation relates to biological functions.

As an extension, REMM can be applied to other cellular environments because the parameters of this model, such as the components and radius, can be easily altered depending on the target environment. For instance, by changing REMM component, we can model a nuclear pore complex95,96 and translocon97,98 because these environments are known as the tunnel-like cellular environment, as well as the ribosome tunnel. Furthermore, the environment inside a protein chaperone can be mimicked by changing REMM component and shape. Thus, the application of REMM enables the investigation of the properties of various cellular environments and their influence on protein conformation.

Limitations of CNT as a Model of the Ribosome Tunnel

As described in Sections 3–4, CNT was hardly able to reproduce the α-helix of nascent peptides owing to excessive attractive forces on the tunnel. In contrast, previous studies regarded CNT as a model of the ribosome tunnel.57,59,60 In this section, we will discuss this contradiction and the limitations of using CNT as a model of ribosome tunnel. Based on the previous research, the α-helix formation occurred under highly restricted conditions, such as specific diameters or interaction strengths. Specifically, computational studies with CNT using a polyalanine have demonstrated that α-helix formation was confined to an inner diameter of 6.8 Å, and excluding this diameter, the α-helix was only formed when the interactions between CNT and the peptide were substantially reduced by modifying λ values of the potential energy.57,59 Additionally, other studies have shown that sequences experimentally expected to form α-helix were hardly able to form it inside CNT at any given inner diameter.60 Considering these findings, the present result, i.e., CNT rarely reproduces α-helix formations, does not contradict but rather aligns with the previous research. In other words, the present result supports the argument from earlier studies that CNT may not serve effectively as a universal model of the ribosome tunnel, given that the parameters facilitating α-helix formation are dependent on the specific sequence.57

Conclusions

Herein, we proposed a new ribosome tunnel model called REMM to reveal how the ribosome tunnel environment induces its specific α-helix formation. We constructed the REMM to mimic the ribosome tunnel environment, including the conformational restrictions and chemical properties of the tunnel based on the radius and components of the experimentally determined ribosome tunnel structures.

We compared the abilities of CNT and REMM to reproduce the α-helix of the 17 nascent peptides experimentally determined inside the ribosome tunnel extracted from the PDB. CNT was adopted as a reference model since it mimics conformational restrictions and not the ribosome tunnel environment. Specifically, we computed the conformation of each peptide inside the CNT and REMM using the enhanced MD simulation (REST2). Consequently, the REMM more accurately reproduced the α-helix of the nascent peptides than the CNT, indicating the ribosome tunnel environment is indispensable for the α-helix formation in addition to the conformational restrictions of the tunnel. Furthermore, based on the analysis of the peptide inside each model, we found that the diverse chemical properties of the tunnel are essential for backbone-to-backbone contact. Finally, we conclude the ribosomal tunnel environment with diverse chemical properties drives its specific α-helix formation.

From a technical standpoint, REMM can be considered a standard model for investigating protein conformations in various cellular environments because REMM’s parameters can easily alter depending on each target environment.
