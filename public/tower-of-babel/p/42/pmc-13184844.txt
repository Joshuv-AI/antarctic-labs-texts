Complex Percutaneous Intervention and Intravascular Lithotripsy for Critical Cabrol Anastomotic Stenosis Jeopardizing Global Coronary Circulation
Authors: Thomas Meredith, Mayil Krishnam, Jennifer P Woo, William F Fearon, William Hiesinger, Alan Yeung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLate coronary anastomotic complications after Cabrol composite graft repair for aortic dissection are rare and pose unique revascularization challenges.Case SummaryA 72-year-old man with a prior Cabrol composite mechanical aortic graft presented with progressive exertional angina and dyspnea. Stress imaging showed extensive ischemia. Coronary angiography revealed occlusion of the right Cabrol limb and a severely calcified stenosis at the left coronary anastomosis, jeopardizing global coronary supply. After multidisciplinary planning, high-risk percutaneous coronary intervention was undertaken with standby extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support. Conventional balloon techniques failed to sufficiently modify the lesion. Intravascular ultrasound demonstrated dense circumferential calcification at the anastomotic site. Intravascular lithotripsy enabled partial lesion preparation, permitting successful drug-eluting stent deployment.DiscussionThis case highlights multiple unique interventional challenges in a single-remaining-vessel situation with limited hemodynamic support options.Take-Home MessageIntravascular imaging and lithotripsy enabled successful high-risk percutaneous coronary intervention of a heavily calcified Cabrol graft.Key words: cardiac surgery, complex PCI, intravascular lithotripsy

History of Presentation

A 72-year-old man presented to clinic with subacute right-sided chest pain and associated dyspnea, relieved by sublingual nitrate. The patient had experienced 2 episodes lasting <10 minutes at rest.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of acute Stanford type A aortic dissection in 2001, necessitating emergent surgery with a Cabrol composite mechanical-valve graft (27-mm St Jude mechanical aortic prosthesis). Other pertinent cardiac history included treated hypertension and hyperlipidemia.

Differential Diagnosis

In a patient with a history of repaired acute type A aortic dissection and multiple cardiovascular risk factors, important differential diagnoses for chest pain include recurrent aortic dissection and coronary ischemia. Pulmonary embolus is possible, although less likely given therapeutic anticoagulation, and other pleural and pericardial pathologies warrant consideration.

Investigations

Electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm, normal cardiac axis, and precordial R-wave progression with normal conduction intervals and repolarization. An echocardiogram demonstrated a nondilated and mildly hypertrophied left ventricle with normal systolic function (left ventricular ejection fraction: 53%) and no resting regional wall motion abnormalities. Right ventricular function was normal, and right ventricular systolic pressure was 26 mm Hg. The mechanical aortic valve prosthesis demonstrated a mild gradient (14 mm Hg), with no other significant chamber or valvular abnormalities.

The patient underwent stress echocardiography, which demonstrated severe global ischemic ST-segment changes at 4 minutes and 16 seconds of the Bruce protocol. He was symptomatic, with severe fatigue at peak exercise. At peak stress there was evidence of hypokinesis in the entire anteroseptal, inferoseptal, and apical inferior segments, consistent with global ischemia.

Arterial-phase thoracic computed tomography demonstrated severe stenosis at the anastomosis of the Cabrol graft to the left main coronary artery (LMCA), although poorly evaluated owing to artifact from dense calcifications and presumed suture material at the site (Figure 1). Additionally, residual dissection extending from the proximal ascending aorta to the distal arch/proximal descending aorta was noted, with maximal dilation of the proximal ascending aorta measuring 59 mm (Figures 1B and 1D). The dissection also involved the brachiocephalic trunk to the mid right and left common carotid arteries, as well as the proximal left subclavian artery.

Coronary angiography demonstrated absent native coronary ostia, with a posterior Cabrol graft orientation (Figure 2). The Dacron limb supplying the right coronary artery was occluded, and the limb to the LMCA demonstrated a calcified critical focal stenosis at the anastomosis, with TIMI flow grade 3. The native left coronary circulation was free of significant atheroma, and retrogradely collateralized the right coronary artery.

Management

Our multidisciplinary team consensus was that high-risk percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) with surgical bailout, despite the technical challenges, represented the safest initial strategy, as the reoperative risk of surgical revascularization was considered extreme given the prior sternotomy, mechanical aortic prosthesis, and residual aortic arch dissection involving the brachiocephalic trunk and bilateral common carotid arteries. Primary access was via a 7-F right femoral sheath. In preparation for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) escalation, 5-F and 6-F sheaths were placed in the left femoral vein and artery, respectively. A 7-F JR4 guide catheter was used to intubate the Cabrol coronary conduit. A Runthrough wire (Terumo) was advanced beyond the critical anastomotic lesion and placed in the distal circumflex artery. With Guideliner (Teleflex) support, a 3.0 × 15 mm semicompliant balloon was advanced to lesion and inflated with inadequate expansion. Intravascular ultrasound pullback recording was performed, demonstrating a widely patent LMCA with a severe focal anastomotic stenosis, with 360° dense calcification (Figure 3, Video 1). A 4.0-mm Wolverine cutting balloon (Boston Scientific) was inflated at high pressure, with inadequate lesion modification.

An inflated 4.0-mm Wolverine balloon demonstrates inadequate lesion modification.

We therefore escalated to intravascular lithotripsy (IVL) using a 4.0 × 15 mm Shockwave balloon (Shockwave Medical), which ruptured after 30 pulses, likely owing to hostile anastomotic calcification and nonuniform expansion despite 1:1 lesion sizing. Care was taken to withdraw the balloon between each set of 10 lithotripsy pulses, as the deflated balloon still occluded flow and resulted in hemodynamic compromise. Further dilation was performed with a 5.0-mm noncompliant balloon to rated burst pressure. We did not employ rotational or orbital atherectomy owing to the lumen-burr size discordance (precluding meaningful therapy), significant depth of calcification, and, most importantly, the risk of burr entrapment or injury at or beyond the calcified anastomotic site. As such, it was felt that no further lesion modification was possible, and we therefore proceeded to place a 4.5 × 18 mm Resolute Onyx drug-eluting stent (Medtronic) to scaffold the anastomosis, ensuring that at least 6 mm of stent straddled either side of the lesion to cater for postdilatation flaring. This was performed with a 5.0-mm noncompliant balloon to high pressure.

Given a residual waist, we proceeded with another round of lithotripsy therapy with a 4.0 × 15 mm Shockwave IVL balloon. The full 120 pulses were deployed, with intermittent balloon withdrawal to facilitate coronary perfusion. Final angiography (Figure 4) and intravascular ultrasound demonstrated adequate anastomotic stent apposition with a minimum stent area of 4 mm2 (Figure 5).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient tolerated the procedure well and was discharged home without residual symptoms. At the follow up clinic visit, the patient reported to be angina free.

Discussion

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of Cabrol composite grafts represents a unique subset of high-risk complex PCI, with few published reports available to guide procedural strategy. The present case is, to our knowledge, the first to report the combination of IVL for a calcified Cabrol anastomotic stenosis in the context of single-remaining-vessel physiology and a mechanical aortic valve precluding transaortic microaxial circulatory support. Described by Cabrol in 1981, this surgical approach connects the aortic graft to the coronary ostia with a Dacron conduit to prevent pseudoaneurysms due to excessive tension on the anastomosis, however the procedure is less commonly performed currently.1 Optimal angiography of Cabrol conduits is dependent on successful catheter engagement, which can be challenging given the lack of calcific ostial markers and variable graft orientation. In our case, the graft originated posteriorly and was relatively easily engaged with a JR4 guide, with added support from a guide extension. The literature frequently cites the Amplatz (AL) catheter as a favorable choice. Preprocedural computed tomography is particularly useful in this setting for appropriate guide selection and to mitigate excessive catheter manipulation in a hostile aortic root.

Both acute and late conduit patency complications have been reported, including early kinking and chronic anastomotic fibrotic remodeling. Management of graft stenosis may include medical therapy, redo surgery, or PCI. Coram et al2 described the first case of left main anastomotic PCI in 2005 in a patient at 12 years postsurgery, who was successfully treated with a 4.0 × 15 mm bare metal stent, yielding a good angiographic result. The anatomy in our case was complicated by dense, 360° calcification 24 years after surgery, which rendered standard noncompliant and cutting balloon angioplasty ineffective. After escalation to IVL, the lesion proved refractory to advanced lesion modification, which has not been previously reported. Given the inability to further modify the lesion, and the perceived futility and risk of atherectomy, we elected to deploy a stent to provide scaffold support to maintain anastomotic patency, albeit with a final minimum stent area of 4 mm2. Although this is below the contemporary guideline consensus threshold of 6 mm2 for LMCA significance,3 we felt that this represented the best area achievable given the unique anatomy.

Overall procedural risk was amplified by the absence of right coronary circulation given the chronic total occlusion of the Cabrol limb to the right coronary artery. Although there is a single report of Cabrol conduit chronic total occlusion PCI,4 this approach was felt to be of low yield in our case given the retrograde collateralization via the left circulation. Conventionally, last-remaining-vessel PCI is a relative indication (Class 2b) for the use of mechanical circulatory support.3 The presence of a mechanical aortic valve precluded use of a transaortic microaxial flow pump, and an intra-aortic balloon pump was undesirable given the residual dissection flap extending into the descending aorta. Given the peripheral vascular complexity, preserved biventricular function, and hemodynamic stability at baseline, we decided to pursue a standby strategy for urgent ECMO escalation, if necessary. Bilateral femoral pre-access and the physical availability of the cardiothoracic surgery and perfusion teams in the procedural suite permitted ECMO escalation within minutes of hemodynamic deterioration. Additionally, we considered left atrial venoarterial ECMO as a strategy if left ventricular venting became necessary, but mechanical support was ultimately not required.

Conclusions

We present a case of a “single-remaining-vessel” PCI where conventional lesion preparation failed, necessitating the novel use of intravascular lithotripsy to treat a critical, calcified anastomotic stenosis.
