Impacts of Accelerated Carbonation on the Leaching of Harmful Elements in MSWIFA
Authors: Qiang Zhou, Yuanyuan Zhai, Bo Xing, Yuling Ye, Xiaonan Liu, Shengyong Lu, Zhicheng Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Municipal solid waste incineration fly ash (MSWIFA) is a promising carbon sequestration material. However, the leaching behavior of harmful elements during the carbonation process is a problem that must be considered. This paper screened four different MSWIFAs to explore their physicochemical properties, the carbonation efficiency, and the leaching behavior of harmful elements. All of the MSWIFAs exhibited a relatively high carbonation efficiency (17.2–27%). The carbonation of MSWIFA effectively inhibited the leaching of Pb, Ba, and Se in the fly ashes. However, the leaching concentration of Cd in the carbonized fly ash increased instead. The leaching concentration of Cr in MSWIFA was not significantly affected by the carbonation reaction. With the addition of 5% CaO, not only was the carbonation efficiency of MSWIFA significantly improved (the efficiency increased by 16.6%), but also the leaching of harmful elements was greatly reduced, achieving the purpose of stabilizing MSWIFA.

Introduction

Global warming and extreme weather are just two of the major climate issues brought on by excessive CO2 emissions. In 2023, China is the world’s largest emitter of CO2, with an annual CO2 emission of 12.6 billion tons, accounting for more than 30% of the global CO2 emissions. Therefore, the reduction of the CO2 emission is now extremely urgent. CO2 capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) is regarded as the most effective and feasible carbon emission reduction technology at present. ,

The CO2 mineralization technology first proposed by Seifritz can achieve permanent CO2 storage at room temperature. Specifically, CO2 dissolves in chemical absorption solutions (such as MEA or aqueous ammonia) to produce carbon-containing ions, which then undergo neutralization reactions with alkaline minerals to obtain stable solid carbonates. These carbonates will not decompose over long geological periods, enabling the permanent storage of CO2. Natural alkaline minerals such as serpentine (Mg3Si2O5(OH)4), forsterite (Mg2SiO4), and wollastonite (CaSiO3) are usually used as mineralizers in the CO2 mineralization process due to their wide availability and low prices. However, the carbonation of natural minerals is limited by the slow reaction kinetics (such as low diffusion rate and surface reaction activity), and additional energy input (such as mechanical activation or heating) is required to achieve a significant conversion rate. − Therefore, it is significant for the CO2 mineralization to attempt to select alkaline industrial solid waste with higher reactivity to replace natural alkaline minerals as CO2 mineralizers.

As a substitute for natural alkaline minerals, the high content of basic oxides (CaO and MgO), fine particle size, and high specific surface area in municipal solid waste incineration fly ashes (MSWIFAs) indicate that they have great potential for CO2 mineralization. As there are many types and high contents of heavy metals in MSWIFAs, it is necessary to carry out stabilization and harmless treatment before landfill or reuse.

MSWIFAs are generally alkaline with a pH range of 11 to 12. Usually, they are rich in oxides of Ca, Al, Mg, etc., which can easily react with CO2 to form a stable and hard “eggshell” carbonate, thereby stabilizing the fly ash. Gunning et al. conducted CO2 mineralization experiments on various solid wastes such as bauxite, sludge, MSWI, steel slag, cement dust, and fly ash from paper incineration. The research showed that the reactivity between cement dust and paper incineration fly ash was the strongest, which proved that the efficiency of carbonation is directly related to the calcium content in the waste. Van Gerven et al. studied the leaching of heavy metals of the waste incineration bottom ash. The research results showed that the leaching concentrations of Cu and Ba decreased significantly after the CO2 mineralization reaction. And at 50 °C, with a liquid–solid ratio of 13–25%, the leaching concentration of Cu and Ba reached the lowest point, and the pH of the bottom ash decreased from 11.5 to 8–9. Chen et al. adjusted the performance of CO2 by adding ammonia–water, as the amount of ammonia–water increased from 0 to 20%, the carbon sequestration efficiency rose from 65.2 to 98.3%. However, excessive addition of ammonia–water led to the leaching concentration of heavy metals in the fly ash increasing instead. The optimal addition amount of ammonia–water was 8%. Although some researchers have conducted studies on the heavy metals leaching behavior of fly ash from waste incineration, − comprehensive research is still necessary on the performance of CO2 mineralization collaborative reduction of heavy metals leaching in MSWIFA, especially in terms of the impact of the active component content in fly ash on the CO2 mineralization performance and the ability to inhibit the leaching of harmful metals. In view of this, this paper took four types of MSWIFAs with similar components as mineralizers, in an attempt to minimize the confusion factors caused by combustion technology or waste feedstock. Exploring the performance of CO2 mineralization in synergistically reducing the leaching of heavy metals from these fly ashes. Furthermore, the influence of CaO on the CO2 carbonation efficiency and heavy metal leaching was also studied. We believe that this work will provide a deeper understanding of the mineralization and fixation of CO2 by MSWIFA and contribute to the large-scale promotion of this technology.

Raw Materials

Four different MSWIFAs were selected, which were generated at different waste incineration power plants in Zhuhai, Guangdong Province, China. Analytical pure MEA (≥99%) and CaO were procured from Chengdu Colon Chemical Co. Ltd. CO2 (>99.9%) was purchased from Zigong Dingtai Energy Co. Ltd. 2 mol/L MEA solution was obtained using deionized water and pure MEA.

Accelerated Carbonation Experiment

This experiment consists of two parts: the CO2 absorption section and the MSWIFA carbonation regeneration section.

CO2 Absorption Section

The part of the CO2 absorption includes a carbon dioxide gas cylinder, a three-necked flask, and a constant-temperature heated magnetic stirrer (DF-101S Yuhua). Two mol/L MEA solution is placed in a three-necked flask equipped with a collector and a constant-temperature heated magnetic stirrer, with the temperature set at 35 °C. Then, CO2 gas (300 mL/min) was injected into the MEA solution for 1 h to achieve saturation.

Actually, when the absorption reaction time was 45 min, the pH value of the MEA solution was maintained at about 8.5 ± 0.2, indicating that the absorption solution was saturated by CO2, and the corresponding absorption load was about 0.5 mol CO2/mol MEA. (The specific methods are provided in the Supporting Information.) To ensure that the MEA solution reached saturation, the absorption reaction time was set to 1 h to maintain the stability of the CO2 absorption amount and avoid any impact on the subsequent experimental results.

MSWIFA Carbonation Regeneration Section

The MSWIFA was immediately added to the MEA saturated solution for accelerating carbonation at 65 °C with a 50 g/L solid–liquid ratio for 30 min. After the carbonation reaction, solid–liquid separation was carried out using a suction filtration device (DF-101S Yuhua).

The filter residue was dried for 12 h at 105 °C using an electric hot-air circulating dryer (SHA-B Guohua). The carbonation efficiencies of four MSWIFAs were calculated based on a thermogravimetric analyzer (TG-DSC).

The carbonation efficiency of MSWIFA was calculated according to the following equations: , CO2(wt.%)=ΔmCO2m105°C×100% 1 carbonationefficiency(wt.%)=CO2carbonated(wt.%)−CO2initial(wt.%)100−CO2carbonated(wt.%)×100 2 where m 105°C is the dry mas of the fly ash sample at 105 °C and Δm CO2 is the mass loss of the CaCO3 decomposition sample at 600 ∼ 900 °C.

Leaching Toxicity and pH

The leaching behavior of toxic elements in MSWIFA was investigated by the “Leaching Toxicity Level Vibration Method for Solid Waste Extraction Procedure” (HJ/T557-2010). Specifically, 3 g of carbonated fly ash with a particle size less than 5 mm was placed in a 400 mL polyethylene bottle. 30 mL of deionized water was then added at a liquid–solid ratio of 10:1. At room temperature, the slurry was subjected to horizontal oscillation in a horizontal shaker at a frequency of 110 ± 10 times per minute with an amplitude of 40 mm for a duration of 8 h. Then, the slurry was left to stand for 16 h and subsequently filtered through a 0.45 μm filter membrane (in accordance with the national environmental protection standards of the People’s Republic of China (HJ/T557-2010)). Then, the pH and Ba, Pb, Cr, Cd, and Se concentrations in the filtrate were determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (GB/T 15555.12-1995).

Characterization Methods

X-ray fluorescence (XRF, Thermo Fisher Scientific, ARL Perform ’x) was used to determine the elemental composition and mass fraction in RFA.

The crystal phase compositions of RFA and CFA were determined by an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Bruker, D2 PHASER) with Cu as the target source and SSD160 as the detector.

The laser particle size analyzer (Winner2309, Jinan Micro-Nano) was used to analyze the distribution of MSWIFA within different particle size ranges.

The physical adsorption instrument (PhysiMaster71, Huapu Hengchuang) was used to determine the specific surface area and pore size of the sample before and after the reaction.

The carbonized fly ash (CFA) was heated at a heating rate of 10 K/min in a nitrogen atmosphere at 30–900 °C using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TG-DSC) produced by NETZSCH Company to explore the content of calcium carbonate.

The inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (PerkinElmer NEXION 1000G, USA) was used to determine the content of harmful elements in the leachate.

Results and Discussions

XRD Analysis before and after Carbonation

The XRD analyses of MSWIFAs before and after carbonation are shown in Figure . The result indicates that the main components of RFA are Ca­(OH)2 (Portlandite), CaCO3 (calcite), CaSO4 (anhydrite), CaClOH (calcium chloride hydroxide), NaCl (Halite), KCl (Sylvite), etc. CaClOH is an intermediate product generated by absorbing HCl during the deacidification process. On the one hand, the diffraction peaks of CaClOH and Ca­(OH)2 disappear, and on the other hand, the intensity of the diffraction peak of CaCO3 increases, after carbonation treatment. This means that soluble Ca2+ (such as CaClOH and Ca­(OH)2 reacts with carbon-containing ions to form insoluble CaCO3 (calcite). It is worth noting that after the carbonation reaction, the diffraction peaks of sodium and potassium salts in the MSWIFA also disappeared. This is because during the carbonation process, these sodium and potassium salts have already dissolved in water, so they were not detected in the filter cake. Furthermore, the calcium sulfate diffraction peaks vanished, as well. This is because the carbonate ions in the solution formed sodium and potassium carbonate solutions through a reaction with the sodium and potassium ions in the fly ash during the mineralization process. The calcium sulfate in the fly ash then underwent double decomposition with the intermediate products (potassium and sodium carbonate) produced by this reaction, leading to the formation of soluble sulfates and calcium carbonate precipitate, which ultimately caused the calcium sulfate diffraction peaks to vanish.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of MSWIFA before (a) and after (b) carbonation reaction.

Carbonation Efficiencies of Four MSWIFAs

Figure shows the carbon sequestration efficiency of the four MSWIFAs. The results show that the carbonation efficiencies of CFA1, CFA2, CFA3, and CFA4 are 17.2, 19, 27, and 25.2%, respectively. The maximum carbonation efficiency of CFA3 is higher than that in Chen et al.’s research (26.4%) under ultrasonic carbonation conditions.

Carbonation efficiencies of different MSWIFAs.

In Figure , the carbonation efficiencies of CFA3 and CFA4 are observed to be higher than those of CFA1 and CFA2. A smaller particle size of MSWIFA provides a larger contact area for the liquid phase, thereby enhancing the diffusion and migration rate of molecules into the interior of the fly ash. This phenomenon aligns with the specific distribution ranges and proportions of different particle size segments presented in Table . Specifically, the proportion of MSWIFA within the 10–50 μm particle size range in RFA3 and RFA4 is significantly greater than that in RFA1 and RFA2.

On the other hand, calcium sulfate, as an inert component in fly ash, plays an inhibitory role in the carbonation reaction. It can be known from the XRD pattern that the content of CaSO4 in RFA3 and RFA4 is relatively low; therefore, their carbonation efficiencies are relatively higher. Meanwhile, the larger specific surface area of MSWIFA also makes it easier for Ca2+ in the fly ash to leachate and diffuse into the liquid phase to react with CO2, thereby enhancing the carbonation efficiencies. The specific surface area of RFA2 (13.58 m2/g) is larger than that of RFA1 (6.67 m2/g), so its carbonation efficiency is slightly higher than that of RFA1.

Leaching Behavior of Harmful Elements before and after Carbonation

As shown in Figure , the leachate concentrations and pH values of RFA and CFA for different MSWIFAs are presented. Generally, the leaching behavior of harmful elements depends on the overall properties of the host particles, their chemical speciation, the pH of the leachate, and the fineness of the particles.

Leaching concentration and pH of fly ash before and after carbonation reaction (a: pH of fly ash, b: leaching concentration of Ba, c: leaching concentration of Se, d: leaching concentration of Cd, e: leaching concentration of Cr, and f: leaching concentration of Pb).

The pH values of the RFA and CFA leachates of four different MSWIFAs are presented in Figure a. Both RFA and CFA are alkaline. The pH of RFA is within the range of 11 ∼ 12. This is mainly because a large amount of calcium hydroxide needs to be added to remove SO2 and HCl from the flue gas to ensure that the exhaust gas emissions meet environmental requirements (GB, 18485-2014). The pH value of CFA decreases to 8 ∼ 9 after carbonation. Because most of the basic oxides in RFA (such as CaO) react with CO2 during the carbonation process to form stable CaCO3. The pH value plays a key role in the leaching behavior of Pb, Zn, Cr, Cd, Se, Cu, Fe, and Si in the carbonation of MSWIFA. ,

Figure b–f shows that there are some differences in the types and contents of harmful elements in different MSWIFA. However, the leaching concentration of Pb among these four types of MSWIFA is the highest, followed by Ba, Se, Cr, and Cd. Their leaching concentrations of the raw ashes are 2659 ∼ 11,729 μg/L­(Pb), 864 ∼ 2583 μg/L­(Ba), 131 ∼ 461 μg/L­(Se), 48.7 ∼ 278.48 μg/L­(Cr), and 0.15 ∼ 7.43 μg/L­(Cd), respectively. After the carbonation reaction, the leaching concentrations are 49.93 ∼ 172 μg/L­(Pb), 113 ∼ 299 μg/L­(Ba), 22 ∼ 61.4 μg/L­(Se), 65.92 ∼ 263.08 μg/L­(Cr), and 3.16 ∼ 22.31 μg/L­(Cd), respectively. Because Pb, Ba, and Se are controlled by the dissolution/precipitation of carbonates during the carbonation process, the leaching concentrations of Pb, Ba, and Se were significantly reduced after the carbonation reaction. −

Figure b compares the Ba concentration in the leachate of RFA and CFA. The Ba concentration in CFA leachate is significantly lower than that in RFA. In other words, the carbonation reaction has a significant inhibitory effect on the leaching of Ba in the four fly ashes. The carbonation process is a chemical reaction between MSWIFA and carbon-containing solutions. So, the Ba2+ in MSWIFA reacts with CO3 2– to form BaCO3 precipitate, which leads to a significant reduction in the Ba content in the CFA leachate.

The carbonation reaction can also significantly reduce the leaching of Se in fly ashes, as shown in Figure c. On the one hand, the soluble selenium in fly ash (such as SeO3 2–(selenite) and SeO4 2–(selenate)) reacts with metal ions (such as Ca2+) during the carbonation process to form compounds (Se­(IV) and Se­(VI)) with extremely low solubility under alkaline or neutral conditions. On the other hand, minerals (such as CaCO3 (calcite)) generated in the carbonation process can adsorb or encapsulate selenium compounds. So, the Se concentration in the CFA leachate is higher than that in the raw ash (RFA) in Figure c.

However, by comparing the Cd concentrations in the leachates of CFA and RFA, it is found that after the mineralization reaction, the leaching concentrations of Cd in all four fly ashes increase instead as shown in Figure d. RFA’s unusually high pH value (11–12) and the fact that Cd is prone to forming weakly soluble hydroxides in strongly alkaline environments could be one of the causes. On the other hand, the produced HCO3 –/CO3 2– forms a soluble complex (CdHCO3 +) with Cd2+ during the carbonation reaction, which prevents Cd from precipitating. However, the pH of CFA falls to 8–9 as a result of the carbonation reaction consuming OH–. As a result, Cd (OH)2 or CdCO3’s dissolving equilibrium moves in the direction of the ionic state (Cd2+). However, as Figure d illustrates, Cd2+ is more likely to react with Cl– and cannot be adsorbed on CaCO3, increasing the leaching Cd concentration of CFA. ,

Carbonation efficiency of MSWIFA with different amounts of CaO added.

There are mainly two oxidation states of Cr in MSWIFA: trivalent (Cr­(III)) and hexavalent chromium (Cr­(VI)). Cr­(VI) is highly water-soluble, carcinogenic, and toxic than its trivalent oxidation state and has been classified as a Group A halogen carcinogen by the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). However, the impact of carbonation on the Cr leaching concentration is relatively small. The possible reasons are as follows: (1) high solubility and chemical inertness of Cr­(VI) (not reacting with carbon-containing ions), (2) the decrease in pH leads to the partial dissolution of the hydroxide of Cr­(III) during the carbonation process, and (3) the lack of a stable Cr-carbonate phase.

In Figure f, the Pb leaching concentrations of the four MSWIFAs decrease significantly after the carbonation reaction. Pb is an amphoteric semivolatile metal and usually exists in the form of a chloride (PbCl2), which is easily leached under the alkaline conditions. Furthermore, under high pH conditions, Pb also exists in the forms of Pb­(OH)3 – and Pb­(OH)4 2–. This leads to a relatively high concentration of Pb in the RFA leachate with a high pH value. Pb2+ in fly ash may react with CO3 2– to form carbonates, or it may adsorb on the surface of CaCO3 to form coprecipitates after the carbonation reaction. On the other hand, the CaCO3 generated by carbonation covers the surface of fly ash, further preventing the leaching of Pb from the CFA. − So, the Pb concentration in CFA leachate is much lower than that in RFA.

However, to sum up, the limits of harmful elements in domestic waste landfills stipulated in the “Pollution Control Standards for Domestic Waste Landfills” (GB 16889-2008) are taken as the concentration indicators of leachate. Although the leaching concentrations of Pb, Ba, Cr, and Se decrease after the carbonation reaction, most of the elements still could not meet the requirements for direct discharge (except Ba). Pb in the leaching solutions of CFA1 and CFA3 (≤100 μg/L), Se in CFA1–4 (≤20 μg/L), Cr in CFA1 and CFA2 (≤100 μg/L), and Cd in CFA1, CFA3, and CFA4 (≤10 μg/L) are slightly higher than the limit values in GB 16889-2008. Therefore, in order to make carbonated fly ash directly meet the GB 16889–2008 standard, we consider adding CaO to further reduce the leaching risks of harmful elements.

MSWIFA Accelerated Carbonation Based on Calcium Composition Regulation

Influence of Adding CaO on the Carbonation Efficiency

This study added various mass fractions of CaO (1% CaO, 5% CaO, and 10% CaO) to investigate the effects of varying CaO addition quantities on increasing the carbonation efficiency. Figure displays the findings. The carbonation efficiency of MSWIFA steadily increased when more CaO was added. This was mostly due to the fact that the addition of CaO produced more active components, specifically Ca2+, which joined with CO3 2– to create CaCO3 precipitates, thus accelerating the reaction. However, when the addition amount of CaO reached 10%, the increase in the carbonation efficiency of MSWIFA was not significant. This was due to the formation of a protective layer, and excessive Ca­(OH)2, after partial carbonation, formed a dense and impermeable CaCO3 layer, covering the unreacted Ca­(OH)2 core inside, restricting mass transfer, and the high temperature intensified this passivation effect, promoting the growth of CaCO3 crystals. Therefore, from the perspective of energy conservation and consumption reduction, we believe that the optimal addition amount of CaO is 5% CaO.

The thermal properties of RFA3, CFA3, and CFA3 + 5% CaO before and after carbonation are shown in Figure . Four weight loss stages are detected in the TG curve of RFA3, and two weight loss stages are detected in the TG curves of CFA3 and CFA3 + 5% CaO. The most significant weight loss occurred between 600 ∼ 800 °C. At this stage, the weight losses of fly ash with RFA3, CFA3, and CFA3 + 5% CaO were 15.3, 38, and 40.8%, respectively. This is caused by the decomposition of CaCO3 at high temperatures. Furthermore, at temperatures ranging from 400 to 500 °C, the weight loss of RFA3 is 2.7%, which might be due to the pyrolysis of calcium hydroxide. Weight loss at 30 ∼ 150 °C is due to the dehydration. According to Figure , the carbonation efficiencies of MSWIFAs without and with 5% CaO are 27and 31.4%, respectively. This means that adding a small amount of CaO can significantly improve the mineralization performance of MSWIFA. Its carbonation efficiency increases by 16.6%. This is mainly because with the addition of CaO, more Ca2+ is ionized in the solution, consuming a large amount of carbon-containing ions, thereby improving the carbonation efficiency of fly ash.

Thermal decomposition curve of MSWIFA.

Influence of Adding CaO on the Leaching of Heavy Metals

Figure presents a comparison of the leaching behavior of harmful elements in carbonated fly ash with varying additions of CaO (calcium oxide). As the amount of CaO increases, the leaching toxicity of all of the harmful elements decreases. This can be attributed to the presence of a large amount of free Ca2+ on the surface of the fly ash following CaO addition. These Ca2+ ions rapidly react with carbonate ions in the liquid phase to form CaCO3 crystalline nuclei, which facilitate the crystallization and growth of CaCO3 on the surface of the fly ash, resulting in the formation of a hardened layer. This layer effectively inhibits the leaching of harmful metals from the fly ash. , However, when the addition of CaO increased from 1 to 10%, the leaching concentration of Ba2+ was observed to rise. According to the dissolution–precipitation equilibrium equation of BaCO3(BaCO3(s) ↔ Ba2+(aq) + CO3 2– (aq), K sp ≈ 2.58 × 10–9), more CO3 2– was consumed by the excessive Ca2+, causing the dissolution–precipitation equilibrium of BaCO3 to shift to the right, thereby increasing the solubility of Ba2+ . At a 1% CaO addition rate, the leaching concentration of Cd was 10.88 μg/L, which failed to meet the direct discharge requirements specified in GB 16889-2008. In contrast, at 5% CaO addition, the leaching concentrations of Ba (84 μg/L), Cd (1.1 μg/L), Pb (93.4 μg/L), Cr (15.1 μg/L), and Se (18.6 μg/L) all complied with the aforementioned standard. Therefore, it can be concluded that 5% CaO represents the optimal addition rate.

Leaching concentrations of different types of fly ash.

All things considered, the addition of 5% CaO to MSWIFA not only enhances the mineralization efficiency significantly (from 27 to 31.4%) but also effectively immobilizes these five targeted harmful metals, resulting in leaching concentrations that all comply with national emission standards. This is a quick and easy way to stabilize fly ash.

The SEM images make it evident that the surfaces of CFA3 and CFA3 + 5% CaO become denser following the carbonation treatment. This is due to the fact that upon carbonation, a thick layer of the carbonate product CaCO3 covers the fly ash particles’ surface, filling in the interparticle gaps and surface spaces while causing a 17–24% decrease in specific surface area (Table ). This physical encapsulation is the initial defense mechanism against the leaching of toxic materials, as it considerably reduces the area in contact with water and produces a physical barrier that hinders the diffusion of soluble compounds into the leachate. ,

By comparing the EDS of carbonated fly ash (CFA3, CFA3 + 5% CaO) and raw ash (RFA3), it can be found that the contents of C and O elements on the surface of fly ash increase significantly after the carbonation process. This once again proves that the carbon-containing ions in the liquid phase are mineralized and fixed on the surface of the fly ash. Furthermore, by comparing the SEM pictures of CFA3 and CFA3 + 5% CaO, it can be seen that CaCO3 is more likely to nucleate and develop on the fly ash surface because the addition of CaO produces the more active component Ca2+. As a result, CFA3 + 5% CaO has a denser surface structure than CFA3. This finding is in line with the particular surface area and ICP-MS determination results. The dangerous components in fly ash are effectively sealed off by this dense framework. This lessens the detrimental chemicals in fly ash from leaching even more.

Conclusions

The potential of accelerating carbonation of MSWIFA for CO2 sequestration and the stabilization of harmful elements was investigated. All four types of MSWIFA with high CaO content were able to effectively fix CO2, with a carbonation efficiency ranging from (17.2 to 27%). At the same time, they inhibited the leaching of Pb, Ba, and Se. However, this treatment exhibited significantly different effects on other harmful elements: Cd leaching increased significantly, while Cr was not affected. For the purpose of complying with China’s landfill standard (GB 16889-2008), the impact of the amount of CaO added on carbonation efficiency and heavy metal leaching was examined. According to the findings, 5% CaO is the ideal amount to add throughout the carbonation process, and this results in a 16.57% increase in carbon sequestration effectiveness. This double advantage demonstrates that optimal carbonation can support both the stabilization of hazardous waste and carbon neutrality at the same time. Future investigations should concentrate on mechanism studies, elucidating the reasons for the rise in Cd leaching concentration following carbonation and developing inhibitory techniques. Furthermore, it is necessary to assess the carbonized MSWIFA’s long-term stability under real disposal circumstances.
