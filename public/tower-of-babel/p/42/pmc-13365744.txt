Introduction

Procurement is a core operational function through which health systems acquire the goods, services and works needed to deliver care.1,4 Yet procurement has also become one of the largest contributors to carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions in healthcare, with the sourcing and supply of surgical instruments representing a significant share of this environmental burden.5 6 Sustainable procurement (SP) directly addresses this challenge by requiring that procurement decisions generate ‘benefits not only for the organisation, but also for the society and the economy, while minimising damage to the environment’.4 Recently, calls for the adoption of SP practices in public and private institutions have intensified, as it is considered a critical avenue for achieving sustainable development and promoting human and environmental health and well-being.7 Organisations adopting SP seek to achieve the appropriate balance between the three pillars of sustainable development—social, environmental and economic—when procuring goods, services or works at all stages of the production cycle.1 8 9

The United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development includes a call on member states to develop and scale up SP practices in the production and consumption of goods and services. SDG (Sustainable Development Goal) 12 sets out to “Ensure sustainable consumption and production patterns” through eleven targets.10 In particular, SDG 12.7 aims to “Promote public procurement practices that are sustainable, in accordance with national policies and priorities.”10 This underscores the global community’s acknowledgement of the importance of SP in sustainable development efforts. Interestingly, there is a growing concern that the procurement practices across health systems are currently unsustainable.2 9 11 12 Therefore, robust analyses of the evidence on SP in healthcare contexts are critical for understanding the nature and extent of its current practice, establishing policy development priorities and providing further research directions.

The health sector’s procurement chain produces a significant amount of toxic substances, consumes large amounts of energy and water and generates large volumes of waste. For instance, the health sector is estimated to contribute 4.4% of global net greenhouse gas emissions.5 11 This presents SP as a human and environmental health issue. On one hand, managing SP practices in health systems can contribute to the attainment of good health and well-being (SDG 3). On the other hand, the Paris Agreement of 2016, which aims to mobilise a global response towards combating climate change, emphasises the need for SP practices to keep global temperature at 2°C and reduce it further to 1.5°C. The agreement is linked to SDG 13 on combating climate change and its impacts. Therefore, SP in healthcare (SDG 12) underscores actions countries can take to align with the Paris Agreement’s ambition to combat climate change (SDG 13) while simultaneously achieving good health and well-being (SDG 3).

Given the intersection of severe resource constraints and heightened climate vulnerability characterising sub-Saharan African (SSA) health systems,9 13 SP practices represent a critical pathway toward resilient and environmentally responsible healthcare delivery. Despite growing recognition of SP’s potential to enhance health system sustainability,5 7 11 the evidence base remains fragmented, with existing reviews limited to sustainable food procurement in healthcare settings.3 14 To address this knowledge gap, we are conducting a scoping review to systematically map empirical evidence on SP practices within SSA health systems. This review will characterise existing research, identify implementation barriers specific to the SSA context and delineate priority knowledge gaps. The findings will provide a synthesis of evidence to inform policymakers and researchers in developing contextually appropriate SP strategies that advance health system resilience, environmental sustainability and health equity across SSA.

SSA represents a distinct health system context compared with high-income regions. The region bears 25% of the global disease burden15 yet imports more than 95% of its active pharmaceutical ingredients and over 70% of the medicines it consumes.16 17 This leaves the region critically vulnerable to supply chain disruptions and price shocks that fundamentally shape procurement decisions. Additionally, up to 50% of the SSA population does not have reliable access to essential medicines, exacerbated by fragmented procurement systems and inefficient supply chains.18 There is persistent fragmentation and complexity in procurement decision-making, with inadequate technical and contract management capacity undermining strategic procurement practices.7 19 Compounding these, SSA is disproportionately affected by climate change, making the environmental pillar of SP both more urgent and more contextually complex than in other regions.13 20 A dedicated scoping review focused on this region is, therefore, necessary to generate context-specific evidence to inform SP policy and practice.

The population, concept and context (PCC) framework21 guiding this review is that the efforts of SSA health systems (population) towards achieving sustainable health outcomes and addressing current and future impacts of climate change on human health require scholarly and policy-level attention to scientific evidence on SP (concept) to enable us to develop a comprehensive understanding of emerging lessons from practices, initiatives and strategies across different health systems (context). A detailed description of our PCC framework is presented in the ‘Inclusion and exclusion criteria’ section.

Methods and analysis

The review team found the scoping review methodology as most appropriate for systematically mapping an emerging and insufficiently characterised evidence base22 and generating the foundational knowledge necessary to inform evidence-based practice on SP in SSA health systems. Unlike systematic reviews that address specific intervention questions, scoping reviews map the breadth of evidence across diverse study designs and contexts,22 23 accommodating varied definitions and implementation approaches typical of emerging fields. The review will follow the JBI (Joanna Briggs Institute) scoping review methodology24 25 and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension for scoping reviews reporting guidelines.26 An initial search started in October 2023, culminating in the protocol development. We plan to begin the substantive search and scoping review by September 2026. Further details of the review are outlined in our registered protocol.27

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible studies need to be empirical studies published in a peer-reviewed journal or as book chapters, as such literature distinctively offers robust evidence.22,24 Our focus on only peer-reviewed empirical publications while excluding sources such as grey literature and books sits within recommendations by scoping review proponents that reviewers may focus on particular sources of knowledge to serve the purpose of the review team.22 23 26 28 As the demand for health systems to demonstrate sustainability consciousness and climate change resilience intensifies,5 11 29 focusing on empirical studies is essential for mapping evidence-based practices on SP in healthcare contexts. While acknowledging the potential of reporting biases due to our focus on peer-reviewed evidence published in academic journals and as book chapters, our application of such an eligibility criterion is also informed by practical challenges associated with synthesising heterogeneous sources of evidence, including time and resource constraints and the challenge in obtaining a representative sample of grey (often unpublished) literature.30 31 A detailed inclusion and exclusion criterion for this scoping review is defined in the PCC framework presented in table 1.

Search strategy

Following the JBI recommendations for a scoping review,24 25 our search for evidence will follow a three-step approach. Step 1 involves an initial exploratory search where all three reviewers independently conducted a preliminary search on Google Scholar in October 2023 using the broad search entry ‘sustainable procurement in sub-Saharan Africa health systems’. Following Haddaway et al’s32 recommendation, the three reviewers independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of the first 200 results, meeting subsequently to discuss and agree on potential articles (18 in total, see online supplemental appendix 1), which informed our search term development. One reviewer further searched PROSPERO and the Open Science Framework (OSF) Database in November 2023 but did not find an ongoing review on the topic.

Step 2 comprises a systematic search across multiple electronic databases, including MEDLINE (via PubMed interface, US National Library of Medicine), Business Source Complete (via EBSCOhost), ProQuest Central (via ProQuest interface), Web of Science Core Collection (via Clarivate) and Scopus (via Elsevier). The search will combine controlled vocabulary (Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, EMTREE) and free-text terms. The search strategies for these databases are included in online supplemental appendix 1. As highlighted in the ‘Inclusion and exclusion criteria’ section, our search will be limited to empirical peer-reviewed journal articles published in the English language (the reviewers are literate in English). We will not place limitations on publication dates and study design. Step 3 involves searching the reference list of articles that qualify for inclusion for potentially missed evidence.

Evidence screening and selection

All search results from electronic databases will be imported into the Covidence systematic review software for centralised data management. Covidence’s automated deduplication algorithms will be used to remove duplicate records, supplemented by manual verification to ensure accuracy. Two reviewers will independently screen titles/abstracts and full-texts using Covidence’s parallel screening functionality, which allows for independent review. Covidence will automatically flag disagreements between reviewers for discussion and resolution among the review team.

As recommended for scoping review,22 a formal quality appraisal of included studies will not be conducted. Data extraction

Data extraction will be conducted using an extraction form (see online supplemental appendix 2) developed following JBI guidelines and structured to systematically capture study characteristics and key findings relevant to the four review questions. The draft template will be piloted with a sample of 5–10 studies selected for inclusion to test the comprehensiveness and clarity of the extraction fields, ensure consistent interpretation across reviewers and refine the template where necessary before full implementation. Two reviewers will then independently extract data within Covidence, using its dual data extraction feature. The software will automatically flag any discrepancies for discussion and resolution among the review team. Extracted data will be exported from Covidence into Microsoft Excel for further analysis and thematic coding.

Data extraction and charting on SP practices (RQ 1) will be guided by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) guidelines for SP of healthcare commodities and services,33 which provides a healthcare-specific framework for evaluating procurement practices across three pillars. Environmental metrics include carbon footprint and CO₂ emissions, waste generation, energy consumption and the use of single-use vs reusable instruments and supplies. Social metrics encompass health workforce conditions, equity and access to medicines and supplies, community benefit and fair labour practices across supply chains. Economic metrics cover cost savings, value for money, total cost of ownership, local procurement spend and supplier diversity. As this is a scoping review, these metrics are framed as data items to be extracted and charted from included sources, rather than as predetermined outcome measures. The UNDP framework has direct contextual relevance to SSA, having been applied in African countries such as South Africa, Tanzania and Zambia through the UNDP Sustainable Health in Procurement Project, implemented in partnership with Health Care Without Harm.

Data on the scope of SP practices (RQ 2) will be extracted using the procurement cycle framework,34 which organises procurement activities across sequential stages: needs assessment and planning; tendering and bidding; supplier selection and evaluation; contract management; delivery and distribution; and postdelivery lifecycle management. This framework recognises that effective procurement practice should extend across the full procurement cycle.

For RQ 3, data on factors influencing implementation will be extracted using the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).35 The framework is widely used for analysing contextual determinants of implementation across five domains: innovation characteristics, outer setting, inner setting, characteristics of individuals and the implementation process. The operationalisation of these domains is captured in the draft data extraction template (online supplemental appendix 2).

For RQ 4, data extraction will follow the JBI scoping review guidance for gap identification,25 which recommends mapping gaps across three dimensions aligned with the PCC framework: population gaps; concept gaps; and context gaps. Gaps explicitly identified by authors will be extracted verbatim alongside reviewer-identified gaps observed during the extraction process. Data on future research directions recommended by the study authors will be extracted.

Analysis and presentation of results

Extracted data will be analysed and presented using descriptive and narrative synthesis approaches consistent with JBI scoping review methodology.25 Findings will be presented in tabular and narrative form. Quantitative descriptive analysis will summarise the characteristics of included sources, such as frequency distributions of study design, data collection approach, country and SSA subregion.

For RQ 1, findings will be presented descriptively across the three UNDP sustainability pillars (environmental, social and economic) in a structured charting table cross-tabulated by SSA subregion, healthcare setting and health system domain. The reported presence, absence or partial coverage of each metric will be summarised, with particular attention to which pillars are most prominently represented in the literature and how coverage varies across SSA. Findings on the scope of reported SP practices (RQ 2) will be presented using a dual-dimension mapping framework that cross-tabulates procurement cycle stages34 against the UNDP three sustainability pillars.33 This will generate a structured evidence map illustrating which stages of the procurement cycle have received the most and least attention in the literature, and which sustainability pillars are most prominently represented across different healthcare domains and subregions. Reported factors influencing implementation (RQ 3) will be synthesised and presented in a structured summary table organised by CFIR domain,35 with narrative synthesis describing patterns, variations, and interactions across subregions and healthcare settings. For RQ 4, evidence gaps will be classified by dimension, namely, PCC, in line with JBI guidance,25 and presented alongside a structured narrative discussion of recommended future research priorities derived from both author-stated and reviewer-identified gaps.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is not required as this review involves analysis of previously published data. Findings will be disseminated through a peer-reviewed publication, conference presentation and social media engagements. The datasets for the study protocol are already published in the OSF, an open-access repository, in compliance with the principles of findability, accessibility, interoperability and reuse.
