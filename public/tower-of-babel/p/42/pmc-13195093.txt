A High-Resolution Forest Soil Organic Carbon Dataset for China Derived from an Enhanced Quantile Regression Forest Model
Authors: Jizhen Chen, Yuxing Ou, Zihao Fan, Xin Zhang, Wenfa Xiao, Qiwu Sun, Xiangyang Sun, Zhilin Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate monitoring of forest soil organic carbon (FSOC) is essential for climate change mitigation and biodiversity conservation. Although digital soil mapping has proven effective in capturing the spatial variability of FSOC, its application remains challenging in ecologically heterogeneous regions such as China. This study developed a national 90 m gridded FSOC content product (four depth intervals) with quantified uncertainty at 90 m resolution, covering four depth intervals from 0 to 100 cm (0–20, 20–40, 40–60, and 60–100 cm). By integrating 8,709 soil profiles with 41 environmental covariates within a quantile regression forest (QRF) framework enhanced by forward recursive feature selection (FRFS), we simultaneously predicted FSOC content and quantified associated uncertainties. The model demonstrated robust and unbiased performance, with 10-fold cross-validation mean explained variance (MEC) ranging from 0.69 to 0.80 across soil depths. This high-resolution FSOC content dataset provides a critical spatial baseline for forest carbon management under China’s “Dual Carbon” strategy, with direct applications in carbon sink verification and soil carbon sequestration potential assessment.

Background & Summary

Soil organic carbon (SOC), the largest organic carbon reservoir in the terrestrial biosphere1,2, is highly sensitive to even minor changes, which can significantly affect atmospheric CO2 concentrations and contribute to global climate change3–5. Forest soils, which represent the largest terrestrial carbon pool, play a crucial role in maintaining the global carbon balance, regulating climate systems, and supporting the stability of terrestrial ecosystems. As the world’s fifth-largest holder of forest resources6,7, China is home to a diverse range of forest types typical of the Northern Hemisphere8 and serves as a vital carbon sink, making a significant contribution to biodiversity conservation9. However, due to the complex spatial variability of forest soil organic carbon (FSOC), accurately characterizing its spatial distribution remains a major challenge.

Traditional soil surveys, which are often polygon-based and dependent on expert knowledge, are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and provide coarse, outdated spatial information10–13. In response to the scarcity of soil data and the growing demand for high-quality data, digital soil mapping (DSM) has become an essential tool for quantifying the spatial heterogeneity of soil attributes14,15, particularly the distribution patterns of SOC at various scales11,16–18. Globally available soil maps, such as the 250 m resolution SOC map from SoilGrids250m and the SOC estimates from the Harmonized World Soil Database, are critical for filling data gaps in regions that are difficult to access for field sampling19. Continental and national-scale efforts have achieved higher spatial resolutions, as exemplified by a 35 year, 250 meter resolution model of topsoil organic carbon storage in Argentina20 and detailed maps of the spatial and vertical distribution of SOC content across Canada21.

At the regional scale, research on SOC in China has successfully produced high-resolution spatial distribution maps through various methods. For example, Song et al.22 applied the geographically weighted regression (GWR) model in a case study of the Heihe River Basin to accurately estimate the spatial distribution of SOC. Wang et al.23 used the random forest (RF) method to evaluate the SOC storage in the permafrost region of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau’s from 2006 to 2015, estimating it to be approximately 37 Tg. Su et al.24 also applied the RF method to generate a 1 kilometer resolution SOC map of Yunnan Province, highlighting the key impacts of terrain, climate, and soil variables. With the advancement of statistical modeling, computing power, and Earth-system descriptors, as well as the efforts of numerous research teams, an increasing number of DSM products with varying resolutions, broader coverage, and higher accuracy have been developed21,25. These studies have demonstrated the capability of DSM to generate high-precision SOC maps at various scales and provide reliable data support.

Despite growing interest, the application of DSM to SOC research in China has largely focused on agricultural ecosystems26,27 or specific provinces and zones28,29. A high-resolution FSOC content map that captures vertical stratification and associated uncertainties is still lacking. This gap is critical for several reasons. First, China’s diverse forest distribution pattern, driven by complex climate and topographic gradients30–32, poses a significant challenge for nationwide FSOC prediction. Second, as China accelerates its ecological civilization and climate mitigation initiatives, the demand for accurate and scalable FSOC data is rising. National-scale clarification of FSOC spatial patterns is therefore essential for optimizing forest soil management, achieving climate and carbon neutrality targets, and informing evidence-based environmental policymaking. Third, most existing models fail to effectively quantify uncertainty. While deep learning models provide high predictive accuracy, they lack inherent mechanisms for spatial uncertainty estimation33,34. Conversely, geostatistical models can output prediction variance but are constrained by assumptions such as data stationarity35 limiting their use in heterogeneous landscapes. Consequently, methodological innovations are urgently needed to enhance the accuracy and reliability of FSOC mapping across China.

Building upon these challenges, this study first integrated the available historical soil data and then conducted a dedicated national forest soil survey in 2023 to establish a unified, up-to-date FSOC database. Leveraging this consolidated database, we developed a high-resolution (90 m) FSOC content dataset for China’s forests using a quantile regression forest (QRF) framework enhanced with forward recursive feature selection (FRFS), which quantified the spatial distribution and uncertainties of FSOC across four depth intervals. This dataset provides a critical baseline for supporting forest carbon management and assessing soil carbon sequestration potential under China’s “Dual Carbon” strategy.

Methods

We developed high-resolution (90 m) FSOC content grids for China (0–100 cm depth) using an optimized QRF model, a machine learning algorithm adept at quantifying prediction uncertainty in spatial modeling36. This framework incorporated 8,709 georeferenced forest soil profiles compiled from two nationwide forest soil surveys conducted in 2018 and 2023, supplemented by independently conducted regional surveys during the intervening years to enhance spatial and ecological representativeness. These surveys employed a stratified sampling design to ensure broad coverage across China’s major climatic zones, forest types, and topographic gradients. Soil profiles were harmonized into standardized depth intervals (0–20, 20–40, 40–60, 60–100 cm) using an automatic-equal-area spline interpolation13,37. A systematically curated suite of 41 environmental covariates were processed via bilinear interpolation to a 90 m grid, aligned with the Soil-Landscape Paradigm38. The QRF model, enhanced by feature selection and hyperparameter tuning, achieved robust FSOC simulations, with predictive accuracy rigorously evaluated through 10-fold cross-validation (training set) and independent validation (validation set) to ensure generalizability. Soil data

To address uncertainties in FSOC content dynamics caused by climate variability and long-term anthropogenic influences, this study establishes a comprehensive national FSOC content database by harmonizing data from two major nationwide forest soil surveys conducted in 2018 and 2023, supplemented by regional surveys conducted independently during the intervening years. The integrated dataset includes 18,193 FSOC samples from 8,709 soil profiles, of which 907 newly collected profiles (totalling 2,368 samples) were obtained through targeted supplementary sampling, supported by the Special Survey Fund for Basic Resources of the Ministry of Science and Technology of China. Based on a stratified sampling design, dominant forest vegetation types were systematically selected that span the full climatic spectrum from tropical rainforests to alpine permafrost ecosystems, thereby ensuring comprehensive regional representativeness. Soil samples were collected from four standardized depth intervals: 0–20 cm, 20–40 cm, 40–60 cm, and 60–100 cm, corresponding to characteristic forest soil strata. All soil samples were air-dried at room temperature, homogenized through a 2 mm sieve to remove coarse debris and roots, and analyzed for FSOC quantification using potassium dichromate oxidation-titrimetric methods. Reference materials were used throughout the analytical process to ensure measurement accuracy and control data quality. Field protocols and laboratory workflows adhered to standardized procedures to ensure methodological consistency. Geospatial coordinates were recorded using handheld GPS devices (horizontal accuracy ± 3 m), with ancillary ecological parameters (stand structure) documented to support FSOC pattern interpretation.

Soil data preprocessing procedure

Data preprocessing procedure involved handling duplicate coordinate points, alignment of soil depth intervals to the four target layers using automatic equal-area spline interpolation, outlier removal, and data normalization. After rigorous quality control and data harmonization, the final integrated dataset comprises 8,709 soil profiles and 18,193 soil samples.

Handling duplicated locations

Due to some plots sharing the same central coordinate during data recording, duplicate location points appeared in the dataset. To address this, we calculated the average FSOC content for these duplicate points to represent the FSOC levels at those coordinates.

Standard soil depths

Soil profiles exhibited inconsistencies in horizon definitions due to differences in sampling standards, with some based on pedogenic horizons and others following their respective mechanistic stratification standards. We used an automatic equal-area spline interpolation13,37 to standardize the depth intervals (0–20 cm, 20–40 cm, 40–60 cm, 60–100 cm) and ensure comparability across all data sources.

Outlier handling

We employed the interquartile range method to conduct an initial detection of outliers in FSOC content across soil layers. For each identified outlier, we evaluated its plausibility considering established soil biogeochemical principles and regional ecological context. Only data plots rigorously verified as irreparable analytical errors were excluded; all subsequent statistical analyses incorporated robust estimators (e.g., median-based measures and trimmed means) to mitigate the influence of residual extreme values without compromising data integrity or ecological representativeness.

Data normalization

The FSOC data were highly skewed, so the sample data were log transformed prior to modeling. The skewness values ranged from −0.37 to 0.3 after logarithmic transformation. Figure 3 shows the histograms of soil properties before and after the transformations.

Climate data (2003–2023) were obtained from the National Tibetan Plateau Data Center (https://data.tpdc.ac.cn). To represent both long-term climatic conditions and seasonal dynamics influencing FSOC, annual and growing-season (May–September) averages for temperature (TEM_A, TEM_GS), precipitation (PRE_A, PRE_GS), and potential evapotranspiration (PET_A, PET_GS) were computed from monthly records. Solar radiation parameters (SR_A, SR_GS) were obtained from the TerraClimate dataset.

Parent material was characterized using remote sensing data. Sentinel-2 satellite imagery detected mineralogical features via the B12 band (Short-Wave Infrared-2, 2.19 µm), while the B8/B12 band ratio served as an indicator of clay mineral content. Regolith thickness data reflected weathering intensity and erosional status. Ancillary information was further supported by the Geological Map of China.

Topographic attributes were derived from the NASADEM digital elevation model (https://lpdaac.usgs.gov/products/nasadem_hgtv001/). Seven terrain variables—elevation (DEM), slope gradient, aspect, profile curvature (ProCur), plan curvature (PlanCur), topographic wetness index (TWI), and topographic position index (TPI)—were calculated using SAGA GIS software (https://www.saga-gis.org). The Geomorphological Map of China was used to enhance the representation of landform patterns.

Vegetation-related covariates were obtained from MODIS time-series data (2003–2023), including multi-year and growing-season (May–September) averages of the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI), Normalized Difference Water Index (NDWI), and Leaf Area Index (LAI). NDVI captured temporal variations in vegetation coverage, while NDWI (based on the B8/B12 band ratio) reflected changes in canopy water content. LAI values were extracted from the MCD15A3H product to characterize canopy structural heterogeneity. Annual Net Primary Productivity (NPP) was estimated using light use efficiency algorithms. Forest type classification (coniferous, broadleaf, mixed) was incorporated based on the Spatial Distribution of Forest Types in China dataset.

Continuous covariates were resampled to a consistent spatial resolution of 90 m using bilinear interpolation, while categorical covariates were resampled using the nearest-neighbors method.

Digital soil mapping

Our mapping framework was built upon a hybrid methodology that integrated feature engineering with the uncertainty-quantifying capability of the QRF. The core process involved: (1) preparing soil profiles and environmental covariates; (2) selecting the most relevant predictors for each soil depth using FRFS; and (3) training QRF models to generate spatial predictions of FSOC content along with their 90% prediction intervals. The QRF model was chosen for its capacity to capture complex, non-linear relationships and to provide quantile estimates for uncertainty quantification. To mitigate overfitting and enhance model interpretability, the FRFS algorithm was applied prior to QRF training. Using the curated environmental covariates (Table 1), FRFS dynamically selected optimal variable subsets while preserving their pedological interpretability. This combined approach allowed the QRF algorithm to simultaneously predict the spatial distribution of FSOC content and its associated uncertainty through quantile estimation. The final output comprised continuous FSOC content maps at a 90 m resolution, with prediction uncertainty expressed as inter-quantile ranges to support robust decision-making.

The selection process incorporated an early stopping mechanism that terminated iterations when no further RMSE improvements were observed (i.e., <1% change over three consecutive cycles). This reduced the feature space significantly, from 41 to horizon-specific optimal sets: 16 predictors for 0–20 cm, 8 for 20–40 cm, 9 for 40–60 cm, and 10 for 60–100 cm. This protocol eliminated 61.9–81.0% redundant covariates while retaining critical pedogenic drivers (Table 1), decreasing model training time by 63%, all while maintaining cross-validated accuracy. Quantile regression forest

QRF, a nonparametric ensemble method extending the theoretical framework of random forest, was implemented to characterize non-stationary relationships between environmental covariates and FSOC content, with explicit quantification of prediction uncertainty distributions41. As a state-of-the-art algorithm in DSM13,42,43, QRF’s dual randomness framework-bootstrap aggregation of regression trees combined with randomized feature subset selection at node splits-enables robust modeling41 of high-dimensional data. The superiority of QRF lies in its ability to deliver precise prediction intervals, providing not only point estimates but also credible uncertainty ranges for each FSOC prediction.

The methodological superiority of QRF originates from its ability to preserve the complete conditional probability distribution F(y∥X=x) across terminal nodes, enabling simultaneous generation of point estimates qˆ0.5 for and probabilistic intervals(qˆα for α∈(0,1)) through quantile regression. This formulation generates three interconnected outputs: the median predictor qˆ0.5(x) as robust central tendency estimate, prediction intervals [qˆα/2(x),qˆ1−α/2(x)] for heteroscedastic uncertainty quantification, and the complete conditional distribution through parametric evaluation of qˆα(x) across the α continuum.

To enable nationwide implementation of QRF across China’s forest ecosystems, we adopted a spatially explicit parallel computing framework. The study area was divided into 461 contiguous grid units (10 × 10 km) using the Albers equal-area projection, ensuring geometric consistency during parallel processing. Given the computational intensity of QRF’s bootstrap aggregation (1,000 replicates), we utilized the quantregForest implementation5 in R 4.2.1, distributing tasks across 36 logical cores of a high-performance computing node. This configuration achieved a 7.82-fold acceleration (95% CI: 7.45–8.19; n = 50 benchmark iterations) compared to single-threaded execution, reducing total computation time from 148.6 ± 3.2 hours to 19.0 ± 0.8 hours. Spatial continuity between adjacent grids was preserved through Gaussian kernel-based edge matching algorithms, enabling seamless 90 m resolution FSOC mapping without boundary artifacts.

Hyperparameter tuning

To ensure the reproducibility of all random processes, a fixed random seed (seed = 666) was set prior to model training and evaluation. Hyperparameter optimization was systematically conducted for both mtry (number of covariates per split) and ntree (number of regression trees) to ensure model robustness across different soil depth intervals. For each pedogenic stratum, we applied a grid search protocol with 10-fold leave-location-out cross-validation, minimizing RMSE. Prediction and uncertainty quantification

The spatial distribution of FSOC content across China’s four soil depth intervals was modelled and mapped using QRF algorithms, incorporating spatial environmental covariates. The mapping procedure exclusively incorporated covariates identified through FRFS selection for their respective soil layers. Following DSM conventions, mapping uncertainties were quantified using 90% prediction intervals (PIs)42,44, with lower bounds (0.05 quantile) and upper bounds (0.95 quantile) calculated from QRF-generated distribution functions. These probabilistic ranges were represented as complementary layers to each FSOC content map, with the 0–20 cm layer shown as an example in Fig. 6. The framework ensures that true FSOC content values are expected to fall within the 90% PI in 9 out of 10 predictions at any mapped location.

The forest mask includes coniferous forests, broadleaf forests, and mixed coniferous-broadleaf forests, as sourced from the NESSDC dataset (see Table 1).

where q0.95 and q0.05 represent the upper and lower bounds of the 90% PI, respectively, and q0.50 denotes the median prediction. The PIR is a dimensionless metric that quantifies the relative spread of prediction uncertainty around the central estimate. Higher PIR values indicate greater dispersion and, consequently, higher predictive uncertainty.

Additionally, the reliability of the uncertainty estimates was evaluated using the prediction interval coverage probability (PICP), calculated from the independent validation dataset. The PICP quantifies the proportion of observed values falling within their corresponding 90% PIs; a well-calibrated model should achieve a PICP close to the nominal 90% coverage level. Systematic deviations from this benchmark reflect potential miscalibration in the uncertainty characterization.

Technical Validation

Performance of modelling

The dataset was partitioned into training (80%) and test (20%) subsets. Prediction accuracy of FSOC content maps was assessed using 10-fold cross-validation repeated 5 times on the training set to ensure robustness. Final model evaluation was performed on the independent test set. Both cross-validated training results and test set predictions were quantified using the RMSE, model efficiency coefficient (MEC), and mean error (ME) for each FSOC content in both depths. The prediction accuracy of the compiled FSOC content maps is summarized in Table 4. The ME values indicate nearly unbiased predictions across all depth intervals. In the 10-fold cross-validation, the RMSE ranged from 6.54 to 14.42 g kg−¹, while in the independent validation, it varied from 8.74 and 21.76 g kg−¹. All ME values were positive (0.10–1.39), reflecting a consistent improvement over the baseline predictions. The MEC ranged from 0.69 to 0.80 in cross-validation and from 0.33 to 0.54 in independent validation. Notably, the lower MEC values in the independent validation for the 60–100 cm interval indicates a limited capacity of the model to generalize to unseen data at this depth. This is likely exacerbated by the sparse sample distribution for deeper soils, which constrained the model’s ability to learn transferable patterns during cross-validation training. Despite this, the overall performance is considered satisfactory. The strong agreement between predicted and observed FSOC values across all four depth intervals is visually confirmed in Fig. 7, where the data points closely follow the 1:1 line. Based on these results, the prediction accuracy of the FSOC maps is acceptable, confirming their suitability for practical applications.

Spatial pattern of FSOC

As illustrated in Fig. 8, the spatial distribution of forest soil organic carbon (FSOC) across China is presented for different soil depths. The results demonstrate a clear declining trend in FSOC content as the depth increases. Elevated FSOC levels are primarily observed in the northeastern and southwestern regions, where well-developed forest ecosystems prevail, and where environmental conditions such as lower temperatures, higher precipitation, and dense vegetation cover favor the accumulation of organic carbon. In contrast, lower FSOC values are found in the central and eastern regions, which can be attributed to regional differences in climate and forest composition.

To further assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a comparative analysis with several established digital soil datasets, including CSDLv240, ChinaSoilInfoGrids13, and SoilGrids 2.042. These datasets were processed using spline interpolation and data resampling, followed by masking with forest boundaries to generate FSOC content maps for the soil depths of 0–20 cm, 20–40 cm, 40–60 cm, and 60–100 cm (Fig. 9). In general, the spatial patterns observed in our dataset align with those in the other datasets, with higher FSOC values consistently located in northern, northeastern, and southwestern China, and lower values in the south-central regions. However, while the overall spatial distributions are similar, the extent of the high FSOC areas in our dataset differs somewhat. Specifically, for the surface layer (0–20 cm; Fig. 9a–d), the high FSOC area in our dataset is larger than that in ChinaSoilInfoGrids but smaller than those in CSDLv2 and SoilGrids2.0. These differences may stem from the inclusion of data from various ecosystem types in the other datasets, where SOC content distribution patterns can vary substantially across different land uses, such as cropland versus forest. Consequently, the FSOC maps based on forest-specific sampling in our study provide a more accurate representation of its spatial distribution pattern across China’s forest ecosystems.

Prediction uncertainty of FSOC

The reliability of the uncertainty quantifications was rigorously evaluated through 10-fold cross-validation on the training data. This critical validation step ensures that biased uncertainty estimates do not compromise practical applications of the model. The PICP plot was served as a primary diagnostic tool, comparing the expected coverage probability of symmetric PIs against with their empirically observed coverage rates. A 90% PI should theoretically encompass 90% of the data points, with optimal calibration indicated reflected by the alignment of plotted values along the y = x line across multiple confidence levels. Cross-validation results showed demonstrated strong agreement with this benchmark, with showing less than 2% deviation between observed and expected coverage probabilities (Fig. 10). This robust validation framework confirms that the model’s uncertainty quantifications generalize effectively generalize across heterogeneous soil profiles, ensuring it establishing their reliability for operational decision-making. To complement the quantitative evaluation and illustrate the geographical patterns of this uncertainty, we calculated the prediction interval ratio (PIR) and mapped its spatial distribution across the four depth intervals. Figure 11 reveals that areas with the highest relative uncertainty are primarily located in the topographically complex southwestern region and the climatically harsh northern regions of China pronounced relative uncertainty are predominantly located in the topographically complex southwestern region and the climatically harsh areas of northern China. This spatial pattern of predictive uncertainty offers crucial guidance for the operational application of the FSOC content maps.

Usage Notes

It is essential to emphasize that the uncertainty associated with the spatial predictions of FSOC content was not only quantified but also explicitly integrated into each generated map, providing crucial insights into their reliability. Users are strongly encouraged to interpret FSOC content maps alongside their corresponding uncertainty estimates to maintain scientific rigor in downstream analyses and to support evidence-based decision-making or policy development. Importantly, the inclusion of quantified uncertainties in the published maps should not be viewed as a limitation; rather, the standardized protocols for uncertainty quantification and reporting-widely adopted in DSM-enhance the practical utility of such outputs. Furthermore, interpreting any spatial map as a definitive representation of reality is both scientifically invalid and inherently risky, as it disregards the uncertainties inherent in predictive modelling.

The High-Resolution FSOC Grids for China Product is constrained by the distribution of soil sampling sites, which are limited to mainland China (excluding Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan due to data availability constraints). While this geographic scope reflects the current sampling framework, future updates will address this gap by incorporating expanded sampling efforts. Users should explicitly acknowledge this limitation when applying the dataset to large-scale biogeochemical modelling or regional policy assessments.

Within the DSM workflow, environmental covariates exhibited spatially heterogeneous data boundaries, with localized gaps along the margins of specific variables (e.g., elevation gradients or remote sensing-derived indices). To address incomplete environmental data coverage during large-scale spatial extrapolation, soil attribute predictions were restricted to the common spatial domain shared by all covariates (i.e., areas with no missing values in the input variables). This deliberate exclusion of regions outside the shared boundary reduces the mapped forest spatial extent, as areas with incomplete covariate data were omitted. Users should cross-validate the geographic boundaries of their study areas against the covariate intersection layer (provided as ancillary metadata) to ensure spatial applicability.

This dataset provides soil organic carbon content (FSOC, in g kg−¹). It should not be interpreted directly as carbon stock. To estimate carbon stock (e.g., kg C m−²), users must combine this concentration data with corresponding estimates of soil bulk density, coarse fragment content, and layer thickness.

At national and regional scales, the spatially explicit FSOC maps establish a critical baseline for evidence-based ecosystem management in China. As a national-scale FSOC content mapping effort focused on forest ecosystems, this work addresses a long-standing data gap that has hindered precision in carbon budget accounting under the China’s “Ecological Civilization” strategy and “Carbon Neutrality” targets. The 90 m spatial resolution facilitates direct application in guiding forest conservation policies, optimizing carbon sink zoning, and prioritizing restoration efforts. These outputs also serve as essential inputs for terrestrial biosphere models (e.g., soil carbon-climate feedback simulations) and forest management assessments at provincial to national scales. While the current resolution may limit site-specific precision for localized applications (e.g., individual forest stands), the dataset provides a foundational framework for calibrating regional monitoring networks and establishing county-level soil health benchmarks. Globally, this pioneering inventory enhances China’s capacity to engage with multilateral frameworks, including the United Nations Decade on Ecosystem Restoration, REDD + mechanisms, and Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 13 and 15, while offering spatially structured benchmarks for cross-country comparisons of FSOC sequestration potentials under diverse climatic and management regimes.
