Hepatic metabolites indicate differences during late mid-lactation in Holstein cows with different levels of pasture inclusion
Authors: Mercedes García-Roche, Ana Laura Astessiano, Daniel Talmón, Alejandro Mendoza, Adriana Cassina, Celia Quijano, Mariana Carriquiry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study was to explore hepatic metabolic adaptations in mid-lactation Holstein cows managed under feeding strategies with different levels of pasture inclusion. Sixteen multiparous North American Holstein cows were assigned from calving to 180 days in milk (DIM) to either a fixed pasture strategy (FixP; n = 8), in which grazed pasture represented approximately one-third of dry matter intake and the remainder was provided as total mixed ration (TMR), or a maximum pasture strategy (MaxP; n = 8), in which pasture intake was maximized and cows were supplemented with concentrate and conserved forage according to pasture availability. At 180 ± 20 DIM, plasma samples and liver biopsies were collected for biochemical analyses, quantitative PCR, and targeted liver metabolomics by gas chromatography time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Milk yield, milk components, and body condition score did not differ between feeding strategies. Plasma urea nitrogen was greater in MaxP than FixP cows (6.64 vs. 4.96 mmol/L; P = 0.01). In liver, FixP cows showed greater abundance of metabolites related to carbohydrate metabolism, including phosphoenolpyruvate, fructose-6-phosphate, glucose-6-phosphate, and sucrose, together with greater expression of genes related to the pentose phosphate pathway and fatty acid synthesis, including glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase, ribose-5-phosphate isomerase A, acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha and fatty acid synthase (p ≤ 0.10). In contrast, MaxP cows showed greater abundance of metabolites associated with nitrogen metabolism, including citrulline, ornithine, glutamine, and creatinine. These results indicate that, during mid-lactation, greater pasture inclusion is associated with enhanced hepatic nitrogen metabolism, whereas partial replacement of pasture with TMR is associated with greater hepatic carbohydrate-related metabolism and fatty acid synthesis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46842-x.

Introduction

Pasture-based systems have evolved substantially and now hold a position on the global stage1. Pasture-based dairy systems are generally perceived positively by consumers, particularly in terms of animal welfare and product quality2. Nonetheless, there are conflicting views due to growing awareness of their environmental impact3 highlighting the need for balanced and sustainable production strategies. However, further intensification of pasture-based systems is a challenge due to variable levels of nutrient intake4. Studies in confined systems with total mixed rations (TMR) have shown that consistent diet formulations optimize dairy herd performance and health5. Indeed, ensuring precise diet formulations in pasture-based systems is challenging, especially due to the variations in crude protein concentrations of the diet which in turn have an effect on nitrogen metabolism in dairy cows6. Hence, nutrient balance is one of the factors associated with feed efficiency7. In this context, the liver plays a central role in nutrient allocation and reallocation during lactation, as it is involved in a wide range of metabolic processes, including glucose, nitrogen, and cholesterol metabolism8. Hepatic efficiency —potentially impaired by transition disorders9— is critical for managing increased metabolic demands without a proportional increase in liver mass10, thereby establishing a close link between hepatic and feed efficiency. We have previously shown that hepatic mitochondrial function measured as oxygen consumption related to ATP synthesis was decreased in high-yielding dairy cows in a pasture-based system (pasture grazing and concentrate) compared to a TMR system at 35 days in milk (DIM) and mitochondrial function was restored at 250 DIM11. We also found poorer hepatic mitochondrial function at 21 vs. 180 DIM in the pasture-based system with higher levels of pasture inclusion when comparing two pasture-based systems: pasture grazing and a pelleted concentrate and pasture grazing and TMR12. In addition, when comparing New Zealand and North American Holstein strains, the North American strain under the system with higher levels of pasture inclusion showed the lowest mitochondrial function and the highest levels of lysine acetylation12,13. Lysine acetylation is the most abundant post-translation modification in mitochondrial proteins and has been to shown to negatively affect ATP synthesis14, fatty acid oxidation, ketone body synthesis, urea and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, among other metabolic pathways15 These findings raise the question of whether, in addition to the well-recognized metabolic adaptation to early lactation negative energy balance, feeding systems with higher levels of pasture inclusion are detrimental to cellular energy metabolism, potentially hindering recovery during mid-lactation and limiting the cow’s ability to prepare for the subsequent lactation in a productive and efficient manner. Indeed, dairy cows in a pasture-based systems with inclusion of TMR have improved performance and a lower metabolic overload -lower plasma urea nitrogen and non-esterified fatty acids- when compared to a pasture-based system with pelleted concentrate supplementation6. Therefore, the objective of this study was to explore hepatic metabolic adaptations in mid-lactation Holstein cows managed under feeding systems with different levels of pasture inclusion. We hypothesized that cows subjected to feeding systems with higher levels of grazed pasture would exhibit hepatic metabolic demands associated with adaptive and catabolic pathways rather than anabolic functions.

Methods

Experimental design, animals and feed

Animal use and procedures were approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee (CEUA) of the Instituto Nacional de Investigación Agropecuaria, Uruguay (file number: INIA2017.2). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

The experiment was part of a larger study designed to evaluate the effect of cow strain (New Zealand and North American Holstein) under different feeding strategies (FS) on individual animal and whole-farm biophysical performance with a 2 × 2 factorial arrangement of treatments conducted at the Experimental Station “La Estanzuela” of the Instituto Nacional de Investigación Agropecuaria (Colonia, Uruguay). A more detailed description of Holstein strains, animal and grazing management, as well as milk production, body weight (BW) and body condition score (BCS) from June 2017 through May 2019 was previously reported16. For the present analysis, we focused on a subset of 16 multiparous cows from the North American Holstein strain to specifically evaluate hepatic metabolic adaptations to FS while minimizing variability associated to genetic background. This strain was selected because it showed the lowest hepatic mitochondrial function and the highest levels of acetylated mitochondrial proteins in response to the FS x strain interaction in a previous reported12. Plasma samples, liver biopsies and plasma metabolites

Blood samples and liver biopsies were collected at 180 ± 20 DIM. Blood was collected during AM milking by venipuncture of the coccygeal vein using BD Vacutainer® tubes with heparin (Becton Dickinson) and plasma was separated by centrifugation at 2,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C within 1 h after collection. Plasma samples were stored at − 20 °C until analyses were performed. Liver biopsies were taken using a 14-gauge biopsy needle (Tru-Core-II Automatic Biopsy Instrument; Angiotech) after local intramuscular administration of 3 mL of 2% lidocaine hydrochloride as described previously18 and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. All samples were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Metabolomic analyses were performed within two years of sample collection19, while RNA was extracted and cDNA synthesized within 6 months after samples collection, all samples were analyzed within the same analytical batch.

Plasma glucose, urea nitrogen, creatinine, albumin and total protein were determined spectrophotometrically with commercial kits (Biosystems S.A.) in a microplate reader (Multiskan GO, Thermo Scientific) according to manufacturer’s protocols. All samples were analyzed in one assay for each metabolite and the intra-assay coefficient of variation was less than 6%.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA extraction from hepatic biopsies was performed in a subset of 8 FixP and 8 MaxP cows using the Trizol reagent followed by lithium chloride precipitation and DNase treatment using Ambion™ DNA-free™ DNA Removal Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) as previously detailed18. Concentration of RNA was determined by measuring absorbance at λ = 260 nm (NanoDrop ND-1000 Spectrophotometer; Nanodrop Technologies), and purity and integrity of RNA isolates were assessed from 260/280 and 260/230 absorbance ratios (greater than 1.9 and 1.8, respectively). Samples of RNA were stored at − 80 °C. The SuperScript™ III Reverse Transcriptase kit (Invitrogen™ from ThermoFischer Scientific) was used for retrotranscription together with random hexamers and 1 μg of total RNA as a template. The cDNA was stored at − 20 °C until real-time PCR assays. Primers (Supplementary information 1) to specifically amplify cDNA of target genes: ß-actin (ACTB), hypoxanthine phosphoribosyl transferase (HPRT1); argininosuccinate synthase 1 (ASS1), ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC), carbamoyl-phosphate synthase 1 (CPS1), N-acetylglutamate synthase (NAGS), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 1 (HMGCS1), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR), acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha (ACACA), fatty acid synthase (FASN), ribose 5-phosphate isomerase A (RPIA), glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta (GSK3B), phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (PGD), glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), succinate dehydrogenase complex, subunit A, flavoprotein (SDHA), methylmalonyl-CoA mutase (MMUT), glucose-6-phosphatase (G6PC), pyruvate dehydrogenase E1 subunit alpha 1 (PDHA1), cytosolic phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PCK1), pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase (PDK4), pyruvate carboxylase (PC), citrate synthase (CS) were obtained from literature or specifically designed using the Primer3 website (http://www.bioinformatics.nl/cgi-bin/primer3plus/primer3plus.cgi) and bovine nucleotide sequences available from NCBI (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Real time PCR reactions were carried out in a final total volume of 15 µl using Maxima SYBR Green/ROX qPCR Master Mix 2X (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with the following standard amplification conditions: 10 min at 95 °C and 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 60 °C, and 30 s at 72 °C in a 48-well StepOne™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems™ from Thermo Fischer Scientific). Melting curves were run on all samples to detect primer dimers, contamination, or presence of other amplicons. Each plate was designed including a pool of total cDNA from bovine liver samples analyzed in triplicate to be used as the basis for the comparative expression results (exogenous control) and duplicate wells of non-template control (water). Gene expression was determined by relative quantification with respect to the exogenous control20 and normalized to the geometric mean expression of the endogenous control genes (ACTB and HPRT). Expression stability of two selected housekeeping genes was evaluated using MS-Excel add-in Normfinder (MDL, Aarhus, Denmark), values obtained with Normfinder were for 0.004 for ACTB and 0.003 for HPRT. Amplification efficiencies or target and endogenous control genes were estimated by linear regression of a cDNA dilution curve (Supplementary information 1). Intra-assay CV values were less than 2%, (n = 5 dilutions, from 100 to 6.25 ng/well).

Targeted metabolomics

Metabolomic analysis was performed in a subset of randomly chosen FixP cows (N = 7) and MaxP cows (N = 5) at 180 DIM using gas chromatography time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GCToF/MS) as previously described by21 at the Core Facility West Metabolomics Center (UC Davis Genome Center, Davis, CA, USA). Sample preparation and metabolite extraction were performed at the analytical facility following their standard protocols. Unequal group sizes resulted from technical limitations that prevented the inclusion of all initially selected simples. The final metabolomic dataset consisted of 170 metabolites (Supplementary information 2). Selected cows had all previous variables analyzed and tissue was available for metabolomic analysis. Briefly, analyses were performed in a Leco®Pegasus IV mass spectrometer with a Rtx-5Sil MS, Restek®Corporation column (30 m length × 0.25 mm internal diameter with 0.25 µm film made of 95% dimethyl/5%diphenylpolysiloxane). Mass spectrometry parameters were unit mass resolution set at 17 spectra/s from 80 to 500 Da and ionization energy set in -70 eV and equipped with an 1800 V detector voltage, 230 °C transfer line, and a 250 °C ion source. The ChromaTOF 2.32 was used for data preprocessing without smoothing and apex masses were reported for use in the BinBase algorithm. Ion annotation was performed as described by21. Data is reported as peak height normalized by the sum of all peak heights of each sample.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the SAS software (SAS Academic Edition; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Production variables, plasma metabolites and hepatic gene expression were analyzed using generalized linear mixed models (PROC GLIMMIX). Feeding strategy (FixP vs. MaxP) was included as a fixed effect in the models. Dependent variables included milk yield and composition, plasma metabolites, BW, and hepatic gene expression. Initial BW and BCS measured at the beginning of the experimental period were included as covariates in the analyses of BW and BCS, respectively. The lognormal distribution was used for mRNA abundance of CPS1, NAGS, HMGCS1, ACACA, FASN, PGD, G6PD, PDHA1, PCK1, PDK4 and PC. Because BCS is an ordinal variable, differences between FS were evaluated using a nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Results are presented as median and interquartile range Data are presented as the least square means ± standard error. For transformed variables, the back-transformed means ± standard error are reported. Prior to model fitting, univariate analyses were performed to evaluate data distribution and identify potential outliers. However, no extreme outliers were identified. Least square means were obtained using the LSMEANS statement. Differences were considered significant at p ≤ 0.05 and a statistical tendency was declared when 0.05 < p ≤ 0.10.

Metabolomic data analysis were performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/, accessed on 4 January 2022). Data were normalized using log transformations and data quality was assessed comparing pooled samples to individual samples in multivariate analysis. For statistical analyses, p-values (false discovery rate (FDR), α level = 0.10) and fold changes were calculated for comparisons between groups for each molecular feature. A principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the metabolomic dataset to analyze the overall variance among cows. The partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed and variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were obtained. Metabolites were considered to differ when VIP > 1.0 and pathway enrichment analysis was performed with the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway database22. Significant enrichment of metabolic pathway was set at FDR < 0.10. Statistical evaluation of group differences by t-test was performed and visualized with volcano plots setting the FDR at < 0.10.

Discussion

Metabolomics and gene expression analyses showed an enrichment of anabolic pathways such as pentose phosphate and fatty acid synthesis but a downregulation of nitrogen catabolism in the liver of late mid-lactation Holstein cows that were offered one grazing session and supplemented with TMR (FixP) when compared with cows fed a diet with greater pasture inclusion (MaxP).

In this study, we found increased gene expression of rate-limiting enzymes in fatty acid synthesis in hepatic biopsies of FixP Holstein cows: acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACACA, Fig. 4) which catalyzes the carboxylation of acetyl-CoA to malonyl-CoA and fatty acid synthase (FASN) which catalyzes the synthesis of palmitate from acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA in the presence of NADPH (Fig. 4). Most of the requirement of reducing equivalents for de novo palmitate synthesis is covered by the oxidation of glucose 6-phosphate via the pentose phosphate pathway23. Indeed, in this work, liver metabolomics revealed that glucose 6-phosphate was increased, and hepatic gene expression showed an upregulation of pentose phosphate pathway reactions that yield NADPH: glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) and 6-phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (PGD) in FixP cows. In contrast to monogastric animals, the ruminant liver plays a minor role in fatty acid synthesis24, in fact, although we found an upregulation of this pathway for FixP vs. MaxP cows we did not find differences in milk fat output or BCS, indicating that the overall contribution of hepatic fatty acid synthesis is not significant. Hence, lipogenesis in the liver is probably in response to a decreased requirement of glucose for lactogenesis so glucose is spared for the pentose phosphate pathway as well as to an increased for lipids for use in the hepatocyte. The fact that we did not find differences in gene expression of rate-limiting enzymes of cholesterol synthesis (HMGCR and HMGCS1), could indicate that acetyl-CoA is being channeled toward fatty acid synthesis. In a previous study using cows from the same animal trial, we found that levels of liver triglyceride were lower during this stage of lactation, while no differences were found among FS12. This suggests that during this stage of lactation, lipids are not primarily esterified as triglycerides but rather used as constituents of phospholipids, an important component of the plasma membrane. Metabolomic analysis revealed that inositol, a constituent of phospholipids25 was upregulated in FixP compared with MaxP cows, together with citrate. In contrast, the hepatic metabolome of MaxP cows showed higher levels of 2-hydroxyglutarate, a substrate of the enzyme D-2-hydroxyglutarate dehydrogenase which in turn transfers its electrons to the mitochondrial electron transfer flavoprotein-ubiquinone oxidoreductase (ETF-QO). The ETF-QO is a mitochondrial enzyme that participates in fatty acid beta-oxidation and transfers the resulting electrons to ubiquinone in the respiratory chain27. This agrees with our previous work12 in which we found that MaxP cows had greater hepatic palmitate-driven cellular respiration than FixP cows during late mid-lactation. Fatty acid oxidation and synthesis are opposing pathways regulated by energy availability and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) which inhibits fatty acid synthesis28. We have previously reported higher levels of phosphorylated AMPK in the liver of dairy cows during early lactation compared to late lactation29. These findings suggest that during late mid-lactation hepatocyte energy availability in FixP cows may favor fatty acid synthesis for phospholipid production and cellular maintenance10.

Plasma urea nitrogen concentrations were higher in MaxP than FixP cows, which was supported by the hepatic metabolome showing increased citrulline, ornithine and glutamine were upregulated. These results paired with the higher crude protein intake in MaxP cows compared to FixP cows during Spring (5.29 ± 0.34 vs. 4.64 ± 0.04 kg/cow/d) indicate that increased nitrogen metabolism in MaxP cows. Previous authors have reported that elevated plasma urea nitrogen concentrations are associated with excess dietary protein in dairy cows30, and both milk urea nitrogen and plasma urea nitrogen are increased in cows with higher inclusion of pastures in the diet6. Consistent with Li et al.,31 who reported higher plasma glutamine glutamine and arginine in dairy cows with lower nitrogen utilization efficiency (milk N yield/N intake), we found glutamine was upregulated in liver of MaxP cows.

We studied hepatic gene expression of urea cycle enzymes to complement our findings related to plasma urea nitrogen and upregulated hepatic metabolites. However, gene expression of urea cycle enzymes was not upregulated in MaxP cows, in contrast, mRNA abundance of CPS1 the rate-limiting step tended to be lower in FixP vs. MaxP cows. Previous studies have found that treatments such as feed restriction in dairy cows did not affect the gene expression of urea cycle enzymes although differences in plasma urea nitrogen levels were observed32. This may be because urea cycle enzymes are strongly controlled post-transcriptionally by modifications such as acetylation33,34 and O-GlcNAcylation35. Indeed, substrate availability is an important short-term regulatory mechanism, whereas changes in amino acid nitrogen flux resulting from dietary protein intake or catabolism of endogenous protein may affect ureagenesis in the long term36. In this study, glutamine -which may be converted to glutamate, a substrate of CPS1 – was upregulated in MaxP cows suggesting increased urea cycle activity in response to the greater concentration of crude protein in the diet. Variations in plasma urea nitrogen levels have been previously associated with variations in pasture crude protein6. Plasma creatinine concentrations did not differ between FixP and MaxP cows, suggesting that plasma urea was not derived from muscle proteolysis37. However, hepatic metabolome data showed higher creatinine levels in MaxP in the liver. As creatinine formation in the liver is not enzymatically catalyzed, this increase likely reflects spontaneous cyclization of creatine a reaction that occurs in the liver, kidney and pancreas as a result of arginine and/or glycine transamination38. Together these findings support the upregulation of hepatic pathways involved in nitrogen homeostasis in cows exposed to higher pasture inclusion and greater variability in dietary crude protein.

We also studied the fate of carbon skeletons from amino acids and did not find significant differences in milk yield or components, nor in plasma glucose or gene expression of gluconeogenic enzymes MMUT, PC PCK1, G6PC between FixP and MaxP cows. Thus, it is possible that instead of providing carbon skeletons for gluconeogenesis, ketoacids that result from amino acid catabolism were oxidized and reducing equivalents were directed towards energy synthesis in MaxP cows.

Conclusion

Our results showed Holstein cows with different levels of pasture inclusion in their diet presented a differential enrichment of hepatic metabolic pathways. Cows fed a diet with greater pasture inclusion during mid-lactation had higher plasma urea nitrogen and hepatic urea cycle metabolites suggesting that greater pasture intake is associated with increased activity of hepatic nitrogen catabolism. In contrast, cows offered one grazing session and supplemented with TMR had higher hepatic gene expression of rate-limiting enzymes of the pentose phosphate pathway and fatty acid synthesis, as well as pentose phosphate pathway metabolites in liver indicating an upregulation of fatty acid synthesis contributing to cell maintenance. Information regarding hepatic metabolism presented in this study could be useful to further determine the metabolic burden of including greater levels of pasture in the diet.
