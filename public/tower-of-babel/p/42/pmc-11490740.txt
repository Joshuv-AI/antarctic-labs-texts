Introduction

Shoulder dislocations are common, accounting for 45 % of large joint dislocations in an emergency department. Anterior dislocation is the most frequently observed type and accounts for 95 % of shoulder dislocations [1,2].

Chronic or neglected shoulder dislocation refers to a shoulder joint that has remained dislocated for at least three weeks. This condition is extremely rare, with reported prevalence rates ranging from 0.12 % to 0.18 %. Diagnosis poses a challenge, particularly in the case of posterior dislocations, which can be easily overlooked on standard radiographs. Treatment difficulties often arise due to fibrous and capsular contractures that develop across the glenoid base [3]. Treatment options for neglected anterior shoulder dislocation include open reduction, often combined with stabilization procedures [4]. Indications for treatment typically arise when the dislocation has been unrecognized for at least 3–4 weeks. Treatment choice depends on factors such as bone deficiencies, soft tissue damage, and the presence of Hill-Sachs or Bankart lesions [4].

This case demonstrates the effectiveness of this surgical technique in managing a complex shoulder injury that had been untreated for five months. Additionally, the study provides valuable insights into the long-term outcomes of this procedure. The case report contributes to the limited literature on neglected anterior shoulder dislocations and offers a potential treatment protocol for similar cases. This case is written in line with the SCARE 2023 guideline [5].

Case presentation

A 56-year-old housewife presented with a neglected right shoulder dislocation that had occurred five months prior. The injury resulted from a cow kick while she was leaning forward behind the cow. Initially, the patient sought treatment from a traditional healer who did not attempt reduction but managed the injury with topical application of a thin wooden stick and a cloth wrap. When her symptoms did not improve, she sought medical attention from an unqualified health practitioner who attempted closed reduction but was unsuccessful. The practitioner prescribed pain medication. After four and a half months, she visited a local hospital, which referred her to a regional referral hospital for orthopaedic intervention. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [5].

Clinical findings

The patient's main complaint was mild pain and restricted range of motion, particularly in adduction and external rotation. Upon clinical examination, the limb was found to be in a position of abduction below 90 degrees and internal rotation. The normal round surface anatomy of the right shoulder was lost, and the head of the humerus was palpable in the axilla. The Dugas test was positive, indicating a dislocation. There was no evidence of neurovascular compromise in the distal limb.

Diagnostic assessment

An anteroposterior (AP) view of a plain X-ray was taken, which revealed an anterior subglenoid dislocation of the right shoulder. The X-ray showed good bone quality with no associated fractures.

Time line

Therapeutic intervention

Due to the neglected nature of the five-month-old dislocation, the patient was scheduled for closed reduction under sedation. If stability testing indicated the need, an open reduction and Laterjet procedure would be performed under general anaesthesia. As expected, the closed reduction attempt failed, and the plan for open reduction and Laterjet procedure was carried out. Coracoid fixation was performed using one cancellous screw, providing adequate stability.

Postoperatively, the patient's limb was supported in a sling for three weeks to reduce pain and promote soft tissue healing. Pendulum (Codman's) exercises were initiated in the first week, followed by passive range of motion exercises, and later advanced to active range of motion exercises.

Follow-up and outcome

After one year of surgery, the patient achieved a full range of joint movement. No episodes of dislocation were reported during the one-year follow-up, and the patient returned to normal work. However, the X-ray at the one-year follow-up revealed pseudoarthrosis of the glenohumeral joint with inferior subluxation. See Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5 for details.

Discussion

Shoulder dislocation is the most mobile joint in the body, which makes it relatively unstable and vulnerable to dislocations. Neglected anterior shoulder dislocations account for 65 % of chronic unreduced or neglected shoulder dislocations [6]. The subglenoid (humeral head beneath the inferior rim of the glenoid) subtype of anterior shoulder dislocations accounts for 30 %, while the subcoracoid (humeral head in front of the neck of the scapula) subtype constitutes 70 % [7]. Subclavicular and intrathoracic dislocations are often associated with fractures and result from high-impact forces.

The definition of chronic shoulder dislocation is quite controversial. However, the most widely used definition is a joint that has remained unreduced for a period of three or more weeks [1]. Chronic anterior shoulder dislocation primarily affects the elderly due to weakened and degenerated soft tissues surrounding the shoulder joint. However, it can also occur in younger individuals with predisposing factors such as alcoholism, epilepsy, and repetitive trauma [1]. Most acute anterior shoulder dislocations are caused by traumatic events, such as a fall onto an outstretched arm or with the arm in an elevated and externally rotated position. They are frequently observed in contact sports, bicycle accidents, falls, and high-impact trauma [8].

Recurrent dislocations may indicate concurrent instability of the shoulder stabilizers. In individuals over 40, rotator cuff tears are more commonly associated with recurrent instability [9].

A case series by Abhishek Rai, like our case, shows that the majority of cases were neglected by patients and managed conservatively by unqualified individuals through methods such as massage, bone setting, or improvised slings. All cases were of the subcoracoid subtype, unlike our case, which had a subglenoid subtype of anterior shoulder dislocation.

According to existing literature, treatment options include closed reduction, open reduction with Kirschner wires, Bankart repair, the Latarjet procedure, hemiarthroplasty, and reverse shoulder hemiarthroplasty. Most reports concerning operative management have indicated favorable outcomes ranging from good to excellent [3]. Reduction through the deltopectoral approach followed by coracoid transfer and stabilization is the most preferred procedure in the recent series [1]. Rai et al. reported that all patients with open reduction and laterjet expressed satisfaction with pain alleviation and improved shoulder joint function. The authors, in a case involving a 10-month-old anterior shoulder dislocation, which was successfully managed through open reduction using the Latarjet procedure, reported findings that suggest coracoid osteotomy contributes to preventing redislocation and enhancing the functional status of the shoulder joint [10]. Li and Jiang reported a successful laterjet procedure if subscapularis tenotomy is not done. Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty is helpful if reduction is not possible [11]. Less severe glenohumeral osteoarthritis was attributed to laterjet procedures, while improved shoulder functional status was paramount in shoulder arthroplasty [11]. In our case, we performed a laterjet procedure without subscapularis tenotomy to prevent redislocation of the shoulder. Followed by early passive range of motion exercises.

Compared with Bristow procedure, Latarjet is generally preferred if there is a large glenoid bone loss (>20–25 %) and is more complex in relation; thus, it requires more surgical experience [12,13] It confers more excellent stability, given the triple blocking mechanism: bone block, capsular repair, and dynamic sling effect, all in one piece ([14]) coupled with the application of adequate fixation. It may also better maintain external rotation compared with the Bristow procedure. In addition, Latarjet has lower recurrence rates than Bristow, especially in high-risk populations and cases with complex situations with associated lesions. In contrast, Bristow may be preferred in cases involving a small coracoid process or one considered coracoid size is inadequate for a Latarjet procedure [12,13].

In certain conditions, arthroplasty is indicated for neglected anterior shoulder dislocation. These include when there are severe humeral head defects or when the collapse is >40 % on the surface of the head [15], in the presence of large glenohumeral osteoarthritis, or osteonecrosis. It is also indicated in older patients with poor bone quality, in cases with a large amount of soft tissue damage, and in cases wherein reduction cannot be achieved without subscapularis tenotomy—a factor associated with poor results from other procedures [14]. Arthroplasty may also be necessary for failed previous surgical interventions, more so if they are accompanied by progressive pain and limitation in range of motion or in chronic dislocations with massive, irreparable rotator cuff tears [16].

However, it is important to note that arthroplasty outcomes can be unpredictable in these complex cases 10. Reverse shoulder arthroplasty may be preferred over hemiarthroplasty, especially in older patients or those with poor rotator cuff function [14].

Despite the bone graft resorption associated with the Laterjet procedure, it is a validated treatment for neglected anterior shoulder dislocations with the presence or absence of significant glenoid bone defects [17]. Operative intervention followed by early and meticulous physiotherapy contributes to better Rowe scores and favorable outcomes [3].

Conclusion

Shoulder dislocations, particularly anterior dislocations, are common and represent a significant portion of major joint dislocations. Various associated injuries, including fractures and soft tissue damage, can occur with these dislocations. The preferred mode of treatment for chronic neglected shoulder dislocations is operative intervention. The Laterjet procedure helps prevent the recurrence of shoulder dislocations. Early rehabilitation and physiotherapy proved to be crucial in outcome and improved shoulder function.
