Unmasking Type 1 Brugada Pattern During Ajmaline Challenge Despite Left Bundle Branch Pacing
Authors: Paolo Pastori, Cristina Balla, Francesco Vitali, Giovanni Tortorella, Matteo Bertini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 67-year-old man with recurrent syncope and chronic right bundle branch block underwent left bundle branch pacing for infra-Hisian atrioventricular block. Because a prior electrocardiogram showed a type 2 Brugada pattern, an ajmaline challenge was performed after device implantation using high-precordial lead positioning. The test unmasked a diagnostic type 1 Brugada pattern during both intrinsic and left bundle branch block–paced beats. This case illustrates that diagnostic Brugada repolarization abnormalities may remain recognizable despite altered ventricular activation associated with conduction system pacing.

Case Summary

A 67-year-old man was admitted for recurrent nonprodromal syncope over 2 months. Admission electrocardiogram (ECG) showed complete right bundle branch block (Figure 1A), previously documented in 2021. In 2019, an ECG demonstrated a type 2 Brugada pattern with saddleback morphology in leads V1-V2 (Figure 1A), but sodium-channel blocker testing was not performed. His brother carried an implantable loop recorder for unexplained syncope. Continuous telemetry revealed paroxysmal 2:1 atrioventricular block. Electrophysiological study confirmed infra-Hisian conduction disease, with negative programmed ventricular stimulation. Given the prior type 2 Brugada pattern and suspicion of SCN5A-related progressive conduction disease within the Brugada syndrome spectrum,1,2 ajmaline testing (1 mg/kg over 5 minutes) was performed before discharge to clarify the significance of the earlier ECG finding.3 Structural heart disease, metabolic disturbances, and Brugada phenocopies were excluded. High-precordial leads (hV1-hV6) positioned in the second to fourth intercostal spaces (Figure 1B) were used to enhance diagnostic sensitivity. Three minutes after completion of the infusion, a diagnostic type 1 Brugada pattern emerged in the high-precordial leads (Figure 1B, blue inset). This was accompanied by transient 2:1 atrioventricular block. Premature ventricular complexes with left bundle branch block morphology were observed. The type 1 morphology (coved ST-segment elevation ≥2 mm followed by T-wave inversion in V1-V2) remained evident during LBBP-paced beats (Figure 1B, red inset), most prominently in the high hV1-hV3 leads, and later regressed to a type 2 pattern visible mostly during pacing. Capture thresholds and paced QRS morphology remained stable throughout infusion, excluding loss of left bundle branch capture as an explanation for the observed changes. The diagnostic ST-segment elevation was identifiable independently of QRS configuration. The presence of a qR pattern in V1 with T-wave inversion during pacing was consistent with the expected physiological left-to-right septal activation.

Despite this altered depolarization, the coved ST-segment elevation observed during ajmaline challenge markedly exceeded the expected secondary repolarization changes associated with pacing. These findings may not be explained by pacing-induced activation alone and may instead reflect a Brugada-specific electrophysiological substrate. After documentation of a diagnostic type 1 Brugada pattern, genetic testing was performed; no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified, consistent with the known limited diagnostic yield of genetic testing in Brugada syndrome.

The identification of a drug-induced type 1 Brugada pattern in a patient with syncope raises important considerations for risk stratification and ICD therapy; while LBBP successfully addressed the conduction disease, it does not mitigate the arrhythmic risk inherent to the underlying Brugada substrate. Clinical decision-making remains individualized in this context. Notably, the diagnostic Brugada pattern remained clearly identifiable during both intrinsic and paced rhythms despite LBBP-induced modification of the depolarization sequence. These findings suggest that diagnostic Brugada repolarization abnormalities may remain electrocardiographically recognizable during sodium-channel blocker challenge even in the presence of altered ventricular activation produced by conduction system pacing. Careful ECG interpretation, together with high-precordial lead positioning, is essential when sodium-channel blocker testing is performed in paced patients.
