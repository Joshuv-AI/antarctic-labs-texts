Harnessing essential oils for sustainable food preservatives: Chemical composition, mechanisms, applications, and safety insights
Authors: Rim Rais, Nadia Ziyate, Zineb Soubai, Rania Chaqchaq, Mohammed.S. Al-Eissa, Mohammed Al-zharani, Tarik Aanniz, Abdelhakim Bouyahya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Essential oils (EOs) are secondary metabolites of plants, made up of a wide range of aromatic volatile compounds found in different concentrations. These essential oil components (EOCs) are widely recognized for their diverse biological activities, including antibacterial, antifungal, and antioxidant properties, among others. Consequently, EOs have garnered significant interest across various industries, such as pharmaceutical, agri-food, and perfumery. In the food sector, their bioactive properties are mainly utilized to combat foodborne microorganisms and retarding oxidative deterioration of products, which helps extend food shelf life, improve sensory qualities, and boost consumer safety. However, these aromatic molecules are known for their instability under external stress as well as their toxicity, which can range from safe at low doses to very toxic at higher concentrations. To address this limitation, encapsulation technology has emerged as an effective strategy for incorporating EOs into wall materials, thereby improving their molecular stability and effectiveness. This method not only enhances sensory acceptance but also, in some cases, reduces toxicity. In this context, this review critically highlights and examines the potential and limitations of EOs and EOCs for food industry applications. It synthesizes current research on the chemical compositions of EOs and assesses their antibacterial, antifungal, and antioxidant effectiveness when used in both free and encapsulated forms. It presents a summary of the current state of knowledge on the interactions between EOCs and the food matrix, elucidating how these interactions affect the biological efficacy of these components. Furthermore, the analysis incorporates the effects of free versus encapsulated forms on sensory attributes and safety, thereby offering a comprehensive and contemporary perspective on the practical implementation of EOs in food systems.

Introduction

Nowadays, consumers are more mindful of their health and dietary habits, which leads them to choose more natural and additive-free products. Therefore, exploring natural substitutes for chemical additives is crucial. Such alternatives could be a game-changer in the food industry. In recent years, essential oils (EOs) have become popular as a natural alternative. They are aromatic, lipophilic, and highly volatile compounds known to be the secondary metabolites of plants, serving as protection against microbial and herbivore attacks (El Omari et al., 2024; Sobieszczańska et al., 2020). These EOs are with great promise to substitute the chemical food preservatives, due to their inhibitory activities, which enable them to control food spoiling and ensure a longer shelf life effectively (Feng et al., 2025; Kačániová et al., 2025; Orffer et al., 2025). Each plant species has the ability to synthesize a distinctive array of volatile essential oil components (EOCs): terpenes, terpenoids, their oxygenated derivatives, aromatic compounds, and phenylpropanoids (B. D. da Silva et al., 2021; Saoulajan et al., 2022), with the major components typically playing a crucial role in determining the biological properties of EOs (Aanniz et al., 2025; El Omari et al., 2023; Elhrech, Aguerd, El Omari, et al., 2025; Paventi et al., 2020).

Many EOs and EOCs, such as thymol, eucalyptol, carvacrol, and cinnamaldehyde, have demonstrated significant antibacterial and antifungal activity against a wide range of microorganisms, especially foodborne bacteria and fungi, including Listeria monocytogenes, Campylobacter jejuni, Salmonella spp., Shigella spp., Aspergillus flavus, Penicillium spp. and other species that have a significant role in food putrefaction (Barbieri et al., 2022; Goli et al., 2024; Kačániová et al., 2025; Touhtouh et al., 2023; Touhtouh et al., 2024). Additionally, plant EOs and their compounds have been widely proven in the literature for their antioxidant properties (Konfo et al., 2023). Nevertheless, due to their strong aroma, EOs can alter the desired organoleptic characteristics when incorporated into the food, such as color, flavor, and texture, making it very challenging to satisfy consumers' organoleptic acceptance (Aanniz et al., 2024; Ahmed et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2021; Zomorodian et al., 2023). However, usage of encapsulation technology to shield bioactive EOCs against external factors using various techniques, e.g., nanoliposomes, nano-emulsion, and gelled emulsion, as demonstrated by Aanniz et al. (2024) and Ben-Fadhel et al. (2024), has shown a great potential to blend the EO's strong aroma while retaining its biological functionalities, and to increase its stability against external stressors in the food matrix for an extended storage duration (Anvar et al., 2023; Bilen et al., 2024; Emadzadeh et al., 2021; Taherzadeh et al., 2025).

Although many studies have investigated the usage of EOs as a food preservative, only a few have examined their possible toxicological profile. This is due to the great variation of the chemical composition of each EO, which strongly depends on the phenological changes, extraction methods, geographical plant location, and the part used, making it difficult to determine a characteristic toxicological profile of each EO (Aeineh et al., 2023; Narayanankutty et al., 2021; Vilkickyte & Raudone, 2021).

This review provides a comprehensive analysis of EOs and their main components as natural preservatives in the food industry. We assess current research on their chemical compositions and evaluate their antibacterial, antifungal, and antioxidant effectiveness when used in both free and encapsulated forms. Unlike earlier studies, this work emphasizes the important interactions between EOCs and food components such as lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates, which can significantly affect their preservative effects. Additionally, the review consolidates findings on consumer sensory acceptance and toxicological safety, addressing key requirements for commercial use. By examining these interconnected factors, this report offers a comprehensive and timely perspective on the practical challenges and opportunities associated with EO-based food preservation.

Chemical composition of essential oils predominantly used in the food industry

D. da Silva et al., 2021; Saoulajan et al., 2022). Chemical characterization through gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS), and attenuated total reflectance Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) indicated that the majority of the investigated EOs were predominantly composed of terpenes and terpenoids. However, certain EOs were characterized by aromatic compounds as their primary constituents, along with the presence of aliphatic complexes.

First, terpenes and terpenoids such as erpinen-4-ol (20.23 %), sabinene (20.18 %), and 1,8-cineole (16.69 %) are the major components of A. speciosa EO (Brandão et al., 2022); as well as camphor (45.01 %) and (16.29 %) of the Moroccan A. odorata Subsp. Pectinata and S. officinalis, respectively (Assaggaf et al., 2022; Benali et al., 2020); among others, as represented in Table 1.

Moreover, many EOs were dominated by the presence of compounds derived from the shikimate pathway. Namely, the EOs of O. minutiflorum, O. onites, and O. vulgare, originated from Turkey, with carvacrol (67.86 %), (52.01 %), and (81.35 %), respectively (Tekin et al., 2025), along with F. vulgare Mill. EO containing trans-anethole (70.43 %), and estragole (14.83 %) (Song et al., 2024).

According to Narayanankutty et al. (2021) and his team, different extraction methods on the same plant material can lead to significant variations in the quantities of major EOCs. The same study reported that the major EOC obtained by steam distillation, HDE, ultrasound-assisted extraction, and microwave-assisted extraction of Indian C. amada rhizomes was β-myrcene at 43.20 %, 37.40 %, 30.80 %, and 25.50 %, respectively. Moreover, the variation in extraction mode affects the EO composition not only quantitatively but also qualitatively, as it changes the disappearance/appearance of certain compounds. Notably, steam distillation is known to cause chemical changes in monoterpenes (Tahir Saleh et al., 2023).

Furthermore, the plant's geographical location can have a major impact on the EO's chemical composition. For example, the EO extracted from leaves of M. alternifolia originating from Brazil was primarily composed of α-terpinene (20.4 %), and γ-terpinene (17.4 %) (J. C. da Silva et al., 2025). Whereas the EO extracted from Indian M. alternifolia was predominantly composed of α-pinene (21.64 %) (Sevik et al., 2021). Additionally, the plant organ used in EO extraction is another factor influencing the chemical composition (X. Liu et al., 2023). Knowing that EOs are synthesized and stored in a variety of plant parts, including seeds, stems, leaves, flowers, bark, or roots. Each part often has unique secretory glandules, leading to distinct secondary metabolism compositions (Butnariu, 2021). In addition, each geographical region possesses unique climatic and soil characteristics, including variations in nutrient availability, pH, temperature, humidity, light exposure, and altitude. Consequently, plants of the same species growing in different regions may adapt uniquely to their local environments. These adaptations, along with environmental stressors, can influence the plant's metabolic pathways, resulting in variations in secondary metabolism composition, including EOs (Asres et al., 2024; X. Liu et al., 2023; Tahir Saleh et al., 2023). For instance, the release of terpenes from aromatic plants, including isoprene and monoterpenes, is significantly influenced by temperature. The emission of EOs tends to be more pronounced in warm conditions, as elevated temperatures enhance the activity of enzymes responsible for synthesizing volatile organic compounds (Aćimović, 2025). As for the variation of the extraction technique employed, Gumustepe et al. (2023) and Tahir Saleh et al. (2023) have demonstrated that steam distillation may cause the hydrolysis of certain compounds, whereas HDE can partially degrade thermolabile components. During HDE, the immersion of plant material in boiling water leads to the water solubilization of some aromatic constituents and lowers the pH; as a result, the plant's original constituents are subjected to combined heat and acidic conditions (Katekar et al., 2022).

Additionally, solvent extraction captures a broader range of EOCs, including some non-volatile substances that may be absent in steam-distilled EOs. The supercritical CO₂ extraction, on the other hand, preserves a composition similar to the plant's natural profile due to its lower operating temperatures and unique solvent properties (Hegazy et al., 2025). More factors can lead to a chemical variation such as the harvesting time and the stage of plant growth can influence the concentration and composition of EOs, for instance, M. piperita EO reaches its peak of EO content shortly before flowering, with the ideal harvest period being when the calyces are just beginning to appear at the tips of the stems (Łyczko et al., 2020). Furthermore, environmental stress conditions can stimulate the production of specific EOCs as an element of the plant's protection system, changing the EO composition (Laftouhi et al., 2023). Each plant species has the ability to synthesize a distinctive array of volatile compounds, with the major components typically playing a crucial role in determining the biological properties of EOs (Masyita et al., 2022).

Antibacterial activity of EOs against foodborne pathogens

EOs are widely acknowledged for their antibacterial abilities, which enable them to effectively control food spoiling and fight harmful foodborne bacteria due to their diverse array of active volatile compounds (Angane et al., 2022; Sobhy et al., 2025). Fig. 2 illustrates the biological mechanisms by which EOs act against microorganisms. EOs demonstrate broad-spectrum antimicrobial effects against both bacteria and fungi by employing various complementary mechanisms (Tang et al., 2020). Their antibacterial activity mainly results from disrupting membrane integrity, which affects permeability, changes membrane potential, and causes leakage of vital ions and metabolites. This disruption impairs respiration and enzymatic functions, ultimately killing the cell. Phenolic compounds like carvacrol and thymol target the cytoplasmic membrane and perturb cellular homeostasis (Khwaza & Aderibigbe, 2025; Wijesundara et al., 2022). EOs can inhibit fungal growth by interfering with sporulation, damaging cell walls, and permeabilizing mitochondrial and plasma membranes (Abdi-Moghadam et al., 2023). These changes influence membrane fluidity, lead to electrolyte and macromolecule leakage, disrupt protein and nucleic acid structures, and impair calcium regulation. Moreover, EO-induced mitochondrial depolarization and the inhibition of ATP production interfere with energy metabolism and damage cytochrome C pathways, resulting in apoptosis or necrosis (Duarte et al., 2022; Mani-López et al., 2020). Due to their powerful antimicrobial ability to act on multiple cellular targets, EOs represent an effective antimicrobial strategy with potential uses across various fields, especially in food safety.Fig. 2Inhibitory mechanisms of EOs against bacteria and fungi.Fig. 2

Different EOs were tested against some Gram-negative foodborne pathogens, i.e., E. coli, S. typhimurium, S. dysenteriae, C. sakazakii, C. jejuni, V. parahaemolyticus, and others. To begin with, Microdilution assays revealed that the MIC of L. angustifolia EO against C. jejuni NCTC 11168 was 0.25 mg/mL (Rami et al., 2021). For V. parahaemolyticus, thymol and eugenol were tested using the agar-well diffusion method, resulting in an inhibition zone diameter (IZD) of 8–13 mm (Touhtouh et al., 2024; Toushik et al., 2022). Accordingly, the antimicrobial activity of C. martini EO against E. coli and S. typhimurium, showed IZD values of 12.91 mm and 17.34 mm, respectively (Sobhy et al., 2023). These values indicate the varying effectiveness of various EOs and EOCs against Gram-negative pathogens. In some cases, certain EOs demonstrated limited activity against Gram-negative bacteria with IZD values less than 11 mm (Amassmoud et al., 2023). These results suggest that some EOs are relatively less effective against specific Gram-negative strains, possibly due to variations in the EOs' chemical composition. The MIC of C. aurantium EO was determined to be 1 mg/mL against E. coli and 2 mg/mL against S. typhimurium (Değirmenci & Erkurt, 2020). This action may be due to linalool-L (14.12 %), the major component of C. aurantium EO. The differences observed in MIC values may result from differences in experimental conditions, such as variations in temperature, pH, or incubation time, which can significantly affect antimicrobial effectiveness (Laure et al., 2021). This variability may also depend on the specific bacterial strains tested, since genetic diversity among strains can lead to variations in susceptibility to antimicrobial agents. (Katale et al., 2020; Merker et al., 2020). Additionally, it could be due to variations in the present EOCs, which are known to vary depending on the plant part used for EO extraction.

Building on that, the different methodologies employed for assessing the antimicrobial efficacy of EOs complement each other. Typically, studies begin with an agar diffusion assay to assess bacterial susceptibility to the EO, followed by the determination of the MIC/MBC. Additionally, using disk diffusion, vapor phase method, and microdilution technique, the EO of T. zygis has demonstrated significant inhibitory actions against three strains of L. monocytogenes with IZD exceeding 30 mm of both contact-based and volatile compound activity, and an MIC value of 0.05 % (v/v) (Coimbra et al., 2022). The application of A. tsao-ko EO against four foodborne bacteria has shown IZDs values exceeding 20 mm, and significant MIC values of 0.20 mg/mL, 0.39 mg/mL, 1.56 mg/mL, and 3.13 mg/mL toward S. aureus, S. flexneri, L. monocytogenes, and E. coli strains, respectively. This makes A. tsao-ko EO an effective candidate for food preservation application (Guo et al., 2024). In another study, EOs of L. stoechas, originating from three sites, have exhibited a significant yet varying antibacterial action against B. subtilis, P. mirabilis, and P. aeruginosa with IZD exceeding 13 mm (El Omari et al., 2023). Similarly, the application of EOs from J. thurifera (JTEO) originating from two geographical locations, against E. coli, P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and L. monocytogenes has resulted in MIC values of 0.25, 0.25, 0.125, and 0.125 mg/mL, respectively, for JTEO-TH, besides 0.125, 0.125, 0.125, and 0.25 mg/mL, respectively, for JTEO-A (Elhrech, Aguerd, El Omari, et al., 2025). This distinction in antibacterial effectiveness may be due to variations in the quality and quantity of the EOCs in each plant sample, as the environmental factor plays a crucial role in the EO's chemical profile (Benali et al., 2020).

Since each of these studies uses different methods, the obtained results are expressed in varying units. It would be advantageous to standardize testing protocols and the units of the obtained values to enhance comparability and reproducibility across future studies. Such standardization would support more accurate assessments of the antimicrobial potential of EOs.

In the study of Tran et al. (2020), the MIC and minimum fungicidal concentration (MFC) of C. zeylanicum EOs extracted from bark and leaf against three C. albicans reference strains were very low, which is due to the strong antifungal action of both tested EOs. This enhanced antifungal activity may be attributed to the major presence of Eugenol (62.57 %), and trans-Cinnamaldehyde (66.43 %) in C. zeylanicum EOs extracted from leaf and bark, respectively, which are known to cause secondary membrane damage in fungi (Shahina & Dahms, 2024; L. R. Zhou et al., 2024). Furthermore, the evaluation of contact base and vapor phase antifungal activity of C. zeylanicum (from bark) EO has demonstrated that the volatile action was slightly more effective than the contact base one, when comparing the obtained IZDs (Tran et al., 2020). This discrepancy is due to the nonpolar nature of C. zeylanicum (from bark) EO's active compounds, notably trans-Cinnamaldehyde (66.43 %), which prevents them from diffusing through the agar (Ibi & Kyuka, 2022). However, a divergence was noticed between the MIC and MFC values in the obtained results. This phenomenon can be attributed to the ability of microorganisms to enter a dormant state in response to various stresses, such as nutrient deprivation, antibiotic treatment, acidic conditions, or oxidative stress. These dormant cells, often referred to as “persisters,” exhibit metabolic inactivity toward stress (Kaushik et al., 2022). In another study, the antifungal activity of R. officinalis, M. piperita, and Lavandula spp. EOs was determined to be very weak against C. glabrata, C. albicans, C. krusei, and C. tropicalis strains, given that all obtained IZDs were lower than 10 mm (Valková et al., 2021).

Nevertheless, if the MICs and MFCs for the tested plant EO differ, this could limit their utility in therapeutic scenarios where a definitive fungicidal effect is necessary, regarding the efficacy of these antifungal agents, e.g., of S. officinalis EO, expressing an MIC of 28.40 μL/mL and an MFC of 56.81 μL/mL against the C. albicans 8937 strain (Aćimović et al., 2022), as well as M. graeca EO that has shown MIC and MFC values of 3.12 mg/mL and 12.5 mg/mL, respectively, against C. albicans ATCC 10231 (Benali et al., 2021).

Antioxidant potential and mechanisms of EOs

Oxidative reactions are a chemical form of food spoilage that damages lipids, proteins, vitamins, and pigments (Bensid et al., 2022). This oxidation is promoted by the presence of highly active free radicals and non-radical substances, collectively known as reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) (Elhrech et al., 2024). In food matrices, the commonly found RONS are the hydroxyl radical (OH), peroxyl radicals (ROO), hydroperoxyl (HO₂), alkoxyl radicals (RO), nitrite (NO₂), peroxynitrite (ONOO), and nitric oxide (NO) (Parcheta et al., 2021). The process of oxidation shortens food shelf life by inducing detrimental physicochemical changes in food. These alterations result in the degradation of organoleptic attributes and nutritive content, rendering the food product unacceptable for consumption or in violation of regulatory standards (Soladoye et al., 2024).

To mitigate this problem, the use of various antioxidants is necessary to slow down, prevent, or even repair damage caused by oxidation, either by inhibiting free radical formation or by interrupting their propagation (Goyal et al., 2025). Especially in the food industry, this can be achieved through direct application or by incorporating it into packaging systems (Basavegowda & Baek, 2021; Parveen et al., 2025). Among these anti-free radicals, synthetic antioxidants commonly used as food additives include butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA), butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), and tert-butylhydroquinone (TBHQ), as well as tocopherols and phenolic compounds of plant origin, such as flavonoids, phenolic acids, and diterpenoids (Konfo et al., 2023). However, recent studies suggest that synthetic compounds may pose potential health risks to humans (Hoang & Park, 2024). Thus, growing concerns about the long-term health risks of synthetic antioxidants are prompting researchers and industries to shift their focus toward natural antioxidants, such as plant-based compounds like polyphenols, tocopherols, and EOs, which are well-known for their antioxidant properties (Ciobanu et al., 2024; Parveen et al., 2025).

Plant EOs and their compounds have been widely proven in the literature for their antioxidant properties (Konfo et al., 2023). Table 4 assembles some investigations on the antioxidant potential of various EOs used in the food industry, tested by different methods. The antioxidant activity of the EOCs stems from multiple molecular mechanisms. Phenols, such as thymol and eugenol, act as radical scavengers by donating hydrogen atoms to lipid peroxyl radicals (ROO), which stabilize them and terminate the lipid peroxidation chain reaction. Role of EOs' encapsulation strategy in the food industry

Despite the diverse biological properties, including antibacterial, antifungal, and antioxidant activities of EOs and their compounds in food applications as food stabilizers (Al-wendaw et al., 2021; Benali et al., 2025; Konfo et al., 2023). EOs' application in vivo is often limited due to their vulnerable physicochemical properties and instability. These include notable high volatility, photothermal sensitivity, poor water solubility, and strong flavor (Fakhariha et al., 2025). Consequently, many delivery methods have been developed over the past decade to maintain and enhance their efficacy in the food systems (Y. Zhang et al., 2025). Encapsulation technology is among the top delivery methods. It functions by enclosing EOCs (known as the core material) within biocompatible carriers, referred to as wall material. This process enhances their long-term stability, facilitates targeted controlled release, and improves EOs' dispersion in food without significantly affecting its organoleptic properties (Aanniz et al., 2024; Reis et al., 2022). The efficacy of the encapsulation technology strongly depends on many factors, including the incorporated agent, the wall material, the preparation process, and the technological goal of the application (Ben-Fadhel et al., 2024; Saoulajan et al., 2022). Therefore, choosing proper encapsulation methods helps maximize effectiveness while reducing potential side effects. For instance, nanoencapsulation techniques, particularly nanoemulsions, nanoliposomes, and polymer-based nanocarriers, are gaining increasing recognition in the food industry. These methods effectively improve dispersibility, controlled release, and stability of EOCs by protecting them from environmental factors such as heat, light, and oxygen (Aanniz et al., 2024; Maurya et al., 2024). Furthermore, delivery systems built with wall materials, including lecithin, cyclodextrin, gelatin, chitosan, starch, and alginates, have proven their effectiveness in increasing encapsulation efficiency and preserving bioactivity (Ben-Fadhel et al., 2024). However, emulsion-based techniques are the most commonly utilized methods to transform encapsulated EO into a stable powder, often dedicated to food applications, including spray-drying and freeze-drying of nanoemulsions (Banožić et al., 2025; Maurya et al., 2024). Moreover, membrane emulsification is gaining attention as a promising alternative, distinguished by its capacity for precise control over droplet size and distribution, and its potential to function as a single-step encapsulation method (Reis et al., 2022). The efficacy of these encapsulation technologies is supported by their widespread use in various real food systems (Biswas et al., 2022). Extensive research has confirmed that nanoemulsions, nanoliposomes, and biopolymer-based carriers improve the physicochemical stability, bioactivity, and sensory acceptance of EOs and their compounds in different food matrices. These technologies help preserve the nutritional, physicochemical, and microbiological stability of food, thereby extending its shelf life (Gadallah et al., 2024; Roselló et al., 2024; Shafiei et al., 2024). A detailed discussion of these applications, with specific examples from different food categories, is provided in Section 11.

Interaction of EOCs with the food components

The choice of a food preservative depends on the nature and characteristics of the food, which are determined based on intrinsic factors such as nutrient content, pH levels, redox potential, salt, water activity, and other utilized additives (Cox et al., 2021). These criteria are critical when using EOs or their bioactive compounds as a conservative agent.

In their study, Gómez-Llorente et al. (2024) have demonstrated that the antibacterial effect of vanillin, when applied in a liquid food matrix, depends on the type and concentration of the food compounds. Carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins have a hindering effect on the antimicrobial activity of vanillin, while organic acids and alcohols improve it. Fig. 3 depicts the impact of the main components of food on the EOs' antimicrobial activity. The presence of proteins alters the antimicrobial effectiveness of EOs. This is due to the ability of proteins to bind with the EOs' phenolic compounds, stopping the antimicrobial activities of the hydroxyl groups (Ameur et al., 2021; Ju et al., 2022). The presence of lipids creates a shielding layer around the bacterial membrane, preventing contact between the microorganisms and the functional groups of the EO (Babic et al., 2021). Different carbohydrates also have varying effects on the EOCs (Maurya et al., 2021).Fig. 3Impact of the main component in food on EO's antibacterial activities.Fig. 3

Conversely, organic acids (e.g., citric acid) can penetrate and alter bacterial membrane permeability, enhancing the bactericidal effect. Alcohols also maintain or improve antimicrobial activity (Gómez-Llorente et al., 2025). The antimicrobial efficacy of EOs depends on the pH levels of the food matrix. This is due to the increase in EOs' hydrophobicity at low pH, which makes the dissolution in the target bacteria's phospholipid membrane easier (Nourbakhsh et al., 2022; Wijesundara et al., 2022). Moreover, the antimicrobial activity of carvacrol and thymol when applied as a food preservative against the growth of E. coli was incompletely or entirely stopped in the presence of lipids, as sunflower oil, and protein, when using bovine serum albumin (Gómez-Llorente et al., 2025).

The anti-staphylococcal effect of cinnamaldehyde and vanillin in milk is primarily affected by milk components, including proteins and fats (Boukhatem et al., 2020; Hou et al., 2022). Hence, higher concentrations of the bioactive compounds are necessary to achieve the same antibacterial activity in food as in vitro studies. This also accounts for why cinnamaldehyde demonstrates a more significant bactericidal effect in low-fat milk compared to high-fat milk (Babic et al., 2021). This is due to the lipophilic character of EO, which allows it to be easily trapped in the lipidic phase, thereby making it difficult for the EOCs to reach the microorganism of interest in the water-based medium (Hou et al., 2022; Y. Liu et al., 2021).

As for water activity (aw), it does not directly impact the microbiological efficacy of EOs. Yet simply reducing the aw in food systems can significantly decrease bacterial growth (Odeyemi et al., 2020). The efficacy of EOs in conserving foods and prolonging their shelf life can be limited by the interaction with the food compounds, particularly concerning organoleptic perception (Ellouze et al., 2024). Therefore, high concentrations of EOs may lead to undesirable changes in taste and aroma, which can negatively affect consumer acceptance (Angane et al., 2023; Ruiz-Hernández et al., 2023).

Effect of encapsulated and non-encapsulated EOs on the food's sensory attributes

EOs have successfully demonstrated great antimicrobial, antifungal, and antioxidant activities when applied in food to conserve its quality and extend its storage life (Hou et al., 2022; Prakash et al., 2024). Nevertheless, EOs incorporated into the food can alter the desired organoleptic characteristics such as color, flavor, and texture, causing consumers to reject them (Christaki et al., 2021; Ruiz-Hernández et al., 2023). To manage this problem, encapsulation technology has been applied in the food sector to overcome the sensory problem of using EOs as a conservative agent in food (Aanniz et al., 2024; Emadzadeh et al., 2021; Reis et al., 2022). The effect of encapsulating EOs on the organoleptic characteristics of the food system is often evaluated via a hedonic scale test (Bento et al., 2020). The encapsulation of EOs using various techniques has shown better organoleptic results in comparison to EOs used in their free form. As shown in a sensory investigation made by Anvar et al. (2023), the encapsulated A. graveolens L. EO treatment had better sensory acceptance than the free-form group in minced meat stored at 4 °C. The results of EOs' sensory investigation vary depending on the food matrix, the encapsulation technology used, and the bioactive EOCs. The conservation of papaya using edible coatings technology incorporated with C. citratus, Mentha x villosa, and M. piperita EOs has successfully protected the organoleptic characteristics of the studied fruit. Results have shown a conservation of the papaya's weight, texture, and enzymatic activity while also preventing color changes (dos Passos Braga et al., 2020). This was mainly due to the chitosan layer that formed an effective barrier, reducing or completely stopping gas and moisture interaction between the fruit and the external environment (Dharini & Sadiku, 2023).

The conservation of shrimp using nano-form emulsion coating treatment containing 1 % Hyssopus officinalis EO has gotten very favorable sensory ratings of the odor, shade, texture, and total score during 12 days of storage (Mehraie et al., 2023). However, another investigation has shown that the organoleptic acceptance of shrimp samples when packed in edible film infused with C. cassia was significantly affected when highly concentrated C. cassia EO was combined with film-forming solution, resulting in an unpleasant odor (H. Yang et al., 2025). Recently, Oyom et al. (2024), proved that applying a 2 % concentration of T. vulgaris EO to chicken nuggets preserved their physicochemical properties while also improving the color and texture of deep-fat fried chicken nuggets. The food industry has now embraced encapsulation technology extensively as one of the optimal solutions to preserve sensory quality while retaining EO functionalities, and to increase the thermal stability of its bioactive compounds when added to various food products (Amiri et al., 2020).

Toxicity potential of EOs on consumers' safety

The “natural” origin of EOs does not automatically guarantee their safety. Therefore, a thorough evaluation of their potential side effects is crucial before their inclusion in consumer products, whether in their free or encapsulated form, as represented in Table 6. Using a single oral dose test on mice, the median lethal dose (LD50) (the dose needed to kill 50 % of the tested animals) of B. cinerea EO was found to be 507.5 mg/kg body weight (b.w), which might be due to the major presence of beta-thujone in the studied EO at 46.80 %, whose LD50 value is determined to be 442 mg/kg b.w (Ben Moussa et al., 2025). These results indicate that the cytotoxicity of the EO correlates with its chemical composition. Moreover, a recent Phase I clinical study indicated that doses of 1-2 mg/kg of carvacrol, administered daily for thirty days, were well-tolerated in healthy individuals (Ghorani et al., 2021). However, these findings contrast with evidence of carvacrol's cytotoxic and genotoxic effects at higher concentrations. Notably, concentrations exceeding 0.15 mg/mL have demonstrated cytotoxicity in human blood cells (Konig et al., 2024). Furthermore, the administration of carvacrol at concentrations lower than 0.00015 mg/mL may induce endocrine disruptions, posing risks to embryonic development (X. Zhang et al., 2021). Investigations of the toxicity potential of selected free and encapsulated EOs.

In another study, free C. pulegiodorus EO's acute oral toxicity to mice was determined to be an LD50 of 460.42 mg/kg b.w (Monteiro dos Santos et al., 2024). However, it has caused alterations in biochemical and hematological parameters even at non-lethal concentrations (50 and 250 mg/kg), as well as in the mice's liver and kidneys, and no genotoxic effects. Likewise, Oliveira et al. (2024) have tested another species of the same plant material. According to their study, free C. argyrophyllus EO has demonstrated a cytotoxic effect exceeding 90 % toward all examined cell lines (HeLa, HT-29, MCF-7 and Vero cell lines). However, it did not cause any toxic effects or DNA damage when fed to mice at a 2000 mg/kg single dose. In another study, the results of a cytotoxicity study of C. Limon, E. globulus, and R. officinalis EOs indicated a reduction of cell viability, with mean half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values varying between 0.08 and 0.17 %. R. officinalis EO exhibits the most significant toxic potential, with an IC50 being 0.08 ± 0.06 %. Moreover, all tested EOs showed a high mucous membrane irritation potential at 0.5 % (Lanzerstorfer et al., 2021). According to Righi et al. (2023) T. algeriensis EO is safe to utilize in the pharmaceutical sector, given that oral evaluation at a limit dose of 2000 mg/kg bw of free T. algeriensis may show obvious toxicity symptoms, which disappear after four hours, causing no noticeable harm to the liver or the kidney tissues.

Based on a study conducted by Yoncheva et al. (2021), the cytotoxicity testing results demonstrated that O. vulgar EO (with 29.80 % of carvacrol) did not cause any significant cytotoxic effect on HaCaT (Human epidermal keratinocyte) dermal cells, with an IC50 = 0.2264 % of encapsulated EO into chitosan-alginate nanoparticles, and an IC50 = 0.00631 % for free O. vulgar EO. Both obtained IC50 values are greater than 1000 μg/mL, which is advised as an indicator of non-toxicity (Tahira et al., 2022). A comparative study of free and encapsulated R. officinalis EO revealed that the toxic potential of free EO (IC50 = 4394 ± 147 μg/mL) is higher than its encapsulated form (IC50 = 599 ± 38 μg/mL) (Amani et al., 2022). Moreover, Peng et al. (2024) confirm that nano-encapsulated Litsea cubeba EO at 0.0078 to 0.5 mg/mL concentrations is non-toxic to normal human hepatocytes. To sum up. These findings support the application of encapsulation as an effective and safe packaging approach to boost the stability and bioactivity of EOs in various industries.

Mode of application of EOs in the food industry

During storage, food is exposed to many deterioration factors, including chemical reactions, environmental conditions, and microbial activity. Therefore, the role of food preservatives is to prevent food from putrefaction and preserve its nutritional value for an extended shelf life. Nowadays, consumers opt for healthier food choices, such as unprocessed or minimally processed foods and those without chemical additives. Thus, several studies have inspected the possibility of substituting chemical preservatives with natural ones, including EOs, which are known for their strong antimicrobial and antioxidant properties (Brandt et al., 2023; Goli et al., 2024; Sindhu et al., 2023). Both in vitro and in situ (Ed-Dra et al., 2025; Kačániová et al., 2024; Vuković et al., 2024) EOs can effectively preserve nutritional, physicochemical, and microbiological stability and ensure an extended stability period of the food (Benali et al., 2020; Feng et al., 2025; Kačániová et al., 2025; Orffer et al., 2025).

Baked goods

Investigations have been conducted on EOs as conservative agents. Indeed, the application of T. vulgaris EO encapsulated in Zein nanocapsules has successfully prolonged the bread shelf life to 90 days (Gonçalves da Rosa et al., 2020). This can be explained by the T. vulgaris EOs' antioxidant and antimicrobial properties, on the one hand, and the protective character of microparticles constituting the wall material of the encapsulation, on the other hand, which allows for better stability of the EOs against environmental factors and controlled release during storage time.

A study carried out by Kringel et al. (2021), demonstrated a high antifungal activity of C. sinensis EO against A. flavus when used free, unlike its encapsulated form. From a technical point of view, the incorporation of C. sinensis EO, in both free and encapsulated forms, diminishes the cake's adhesiveness. In addition, when EO is applied freely, it has shown a high intensity of orange aroma, whereas the cake with β-cyclodextrin/EO microcapsules features a barely noticeable orange aroma. In fact, even if encapsulation may be a solution to shield bioactive EOCs against external factors and reduce the intensity of EOs' aroma, its application is not always efficient. However, it strongly depends on many factors, including the incorporated agent, the wall material, the preparation method, and the technological goal of the application, among others (Banožić et al., 2025; González-Reza et al., 2021; K. Wu et al., 2023).

Fruit juices and beverages

The application of M. officinalis (42.7 % geranial and 28.4 % neral) to newly made watermelon juice successfully maintains its microbiological stability for 7 days at 4 °C, exhibiting significant antimicrobial efficacy against L. monocytogenes strains, and inhibiting virulence-related traits, including quorum sensing and biofilm formation (Carvalho et al., 2023). Additionally, application of C. sinensis EO (95.1 % limonene) free or encapsulated in chitosan nanoemulsion, at a 0.2 μL/mL concentration, in synergy with mild heat (52 °C) to freshly made orange and apple juices has exhibited more valuable antibacterial effects against E. coli O157:H7 Sakai., in comparison to the use of EO treatment alone (Bento et al., 2020).

In another study, Pineapple juice was conserved using A. officinarum EO emulsion (0.2 %) for 4 days at 25 °C. This non-chemical conservative has successfully decreased microbiota when used alone and has an even better effect when used in combination with a thermal treatment. However, no noteworthy changes were detected in the physicochemical, antioxidant, and vitamin C content attributes in the studied juice (W. Zhou et al., 2021).

Dairy products

M. communis EO has exhibited effective antioxidant and antimicrobial activities in lactic butter conservation (0.02 % direct application) combined with heat treatment (70 and 80 °C for 15 s) and sheltered from the light at 4 °C for 50 days. Thus, M. communis EO can function as a stabilizer to extend the expiration period of lactic butter (Keceli & Mertoglu, 2024). In the yogurt product, Abed et al. (2022) demonstrated an antifungal activity of C. citratus (DC.) EO when directly applied against the mold and yeast strains responsible for yogurt spoilage, achieving 80-100 % inhibition at 5 °C for up to 90 days of conservation.

Similarly, O. compactum EO is widely applied in the cheese-making industry due to its known antimicrobial and antifungal efficiency. Its direct application in cheese at a concentration of 0.5 % (v/v) has caused bacterial, fungal, and yeast inhibition in 16 days of fresh cheese conservation (Benassila et al., 2025). A study carried out by Gadallah et al. (2024) and their team demonstrated an inhibitory antibacterial activity of R. officinalis EO (9.72 % 1, 8-cineole) nano-emulsion in vitro against L. monocytogenes, A. flavus, P. aeruginosa, and B. cereus in Karish and cream cheese. Moreover, the use of bacterial nanocellulose packets loaded with 20 % Zataria multiflora EO to store white cheese has successfully reduced the bacterial count of E. coli O157:H7 by 1.6 log CFU/g compared to the control test over a storage period of 12 days; in this case, the packaging material enabled a prolonged release of Z. multiflora EO into the food system (Shafiei et al., 2024).

The antimicrobial effect of EOs in vitro is greater than in food products (Boukhatem et al., 2020). This phenomenon may be due to the availability of excess nutrients in food matrices compared to laboratory culture media. This helps bacterial repair and increases turnover of cellular components, which may increase bacterial resistance toward many different stresses (Abebe, 2020; Uruén et al., 2020).

Fruits and vegetables

The application of E. caucasicum EO (43.03 % carvone) nano-emulsion particles as a coating material to extend the strawberries' shelf life and availability showed a good maintenance of the coated fruit's sensorial, physicochemical, and microbiological characteristics after 14 days of storage at refrigerated temperature (Gharibzahedi & Altintas, 2025). The same results were obtained when applying an edible coating enriched with C. bergamia EO (0.1 % v/v) for strawberry conservation (Bruno et al., 2023). To preserve pomegranate arils, encapsulation technology was applied separately in two different studies, using chitosan nanoparticles as wall material for E, caryophyllata EO (Hasheminejad & Khodaiyan, 2020) and S. hortensis L. EO (Amiri et al., 2021). Results have shown that both encapsulated EOs successfully maintained the studied fruit's good biochemical and sensorial properties and successfully extended the fruit's stability for up to 54 days for the clove EO, and 18 days at 5 °C for S. hortensis EO.

To combat potato sprouting during storage, a treatment with encapsulated P. cablin (Blanco) Benth. EO into chitosan nanoemulsion was applied. In their study Das and Chaudhari (2025) have observed a 100 % anti-potato sprouting action at 25 ± 2 °C for up to 60 days. At day 70, germination in the coated potato tubers begins to show, and by day 90, sprouting has attained 23.68 %. Thus, nanoemulsion coating allows for the prolonged P. cablin EO supply in the aliment over 70 days of storage, consequently maintaining potato storage quality. In their investigation, Z. Wang et al. (2025) have successfully achieved the quality maintenance and extended shelf life of cherry tomatoes. Hence, incorporating O. vulgare EO into gelatin/carrageenan has demonstrated excellent conservation ability for 6 days against B. cinerea with a half-maximal effective concentration of 0.18 mg/mL. Moreover, incorporating Thymus serpyllum L. EO (at 300 μg/mL) into water/Tween 20/agar exhibited a total antifungal protection with 100 % healthy fruits after 7 days against A. alternata, B. spicifera, B. fuckeliana, C. hawaiiensis, and P. italicum (Roselló et al., 2024). The same capsule wall was utilized by Santamarina et al. (2025) to encapsulate T. zygis L. EO in a coating biofilm for Tiger Nut Tubers. The author explained that the adopted technique is considered safe for human health, as the agar–agar base matrix functions as a gelling agent in food, to limit EOCs' volatility.

Hence, the application of EO in food matrices can be combined with other technologies to ensure the best conservation conditions and maximize product shelf life. In a study made by Luo et al. (2024), the O. vulgar EO nano-emulsion has been applied alone and in combination with ultrasound treatment to preserve blueberries' shelf life. Results have demonstrated that EO nano-emulsion alone, at 0.04 and 0.06 μL/mL, decreased the bacterial count of L. monocytogenes by 0.04 and 0.15 log CFU/mL. However, better antibacterial results were obtained when combined with ultrasound treatment (255 W/cm2 + 0.06 μL/mL EO), which achieved an 8.67 ± 0.17 log CFU/mL reduction in the same bacterial strain.

Meat

To prevent the spoilage of chilled pork, Song et al. (2024), have used F. vulgare EO encapsulated in hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin inclusion complex at a ratio of 14:1 g/mL, which have successfully prolonged the storage duration by 6 days at a temperature of 4 °C, this was justified by the gradual release of the antibacterial components of F. vulgare EO (trans-anethole 70.43 %, and estragole 14.83 %) along the storage time. Two application methods have successfully prolonged the stability of chicken meat by 5 days at refrigeration temperatures and have prevented microbiological spoilage. The first method involves using 0.5 % bacterial cellulose produced by A. xylinum ATCC 53524/T. mongolicus (Ronniger.) EO emulsion coating (Xu et al., 2024). The second method requires incorporating E. caryophyllus (Spreng.) EO in chitosan/pullulan-based films that exhibited significant antibacterial action in opposition to the growth of P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and E. coli (Gasti et al., 2022). Additionally, C. dinisii Schwanke EO (1,8-cineole 35.32 %) was nano-encapsulated in zein and then in a chitosan matrix to be included in packaging films, thereby preserving quality and prolonging the storage duration of ground beef. The presence of carotenoids and amino acids in the composition of the obtained chitosan films demonstrated an antioxidant activity, along with a microbial inhibition against

S. aureus, S. typhimurium, E. coli, and S. flexneri. Consequently, this preparation has succeeded in prolonging beef's shelf life up to 12 days under refrigerated conditions (6 ± 2 °C) (Xavier et al., 2021).

Today, long-term frozen storage is a crucial method for ensuring meat imports and exports. However, the shelf life of thawed meat is much shorter compared to fresh meat. Thus, alginate-based edible coatings incorporated with T. vulgaris EO at 0.05 % have shown a good, retained coloration during meat's exposure and greatly reduced lipid oxidation (Guerrero et al., 2020). The application of coating material fused with EO can be combined with other conservation technologies. In their study, Chen et al. (2021) have successfully used O. vulgare EO (0.15 %) or C. zeylanicum EO (0.60 %) in a chitosan edible coating coupled with modified atmosphere packaging (30 % CO2 and 70 % N2) to elongate the stability period of roast duck meat when stored at 2 ± 2 °C for more than 21 days. This is due to the bactericidal action of EOs against spoilage microorganisms and the capacity of modified atmosphere packaging not only to block lipid oxidation but also to maintain a desirable vibrant beef color (Kandeepan & Tahseen, 2022).

Seafood

Seafood is known for its very short shelf life, which leads to financial losses and quality issues for both consumers and the industry (El-Hay, 2022). In this light, Shen et al. (2024) used Z. officinale EO in a complex coacervation (mung bean protein/ isolate-chitosan) at a 1:1 ratio, which successfully extended the freshness of crab meatballs by 3 days under 4 °C storage conditions. Moreover, packaging films containing O. vulgare EO /β-cyclodextrin microcapsules demonstrated microbial inhibition of S. putrefaciens at 66.67 %, Aeromonas spp. at 62.87 %, and Pseudomonas spp. at 61.52 %. Hence, the adopted technique prolongs the shelf life of Grass carp fillets. Further, the addition of 2 % O. vulgare EO /β-CDs helps strengthen the film's tensile (Ying et al., 2024). A recent study, carried out by Shi et al. (2025), demonstrated that 0.8 % O. basilicum EO microemulsions markedly enhanced not only the resistance to microbial contamination but also the cooking quality, flavor, and overall acceptability of snakehead fillets. The use of O. vulgare EO /litsea cubeba EO in combination at a 6:4 ratio demonstrated remarkable microbial activity against foodborne microorganisms, namely S. putrefaciens, A. hydrophila, and Pseudomonas spp. Moreover, the nanoemulsion coating technique significantly extends the grass carp fillets' shelf stability by inhibiting fat oxidation, maintaining a reduced value of total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) and thiobarbituric Acid (TBA) levels, enhancing physical texture, and promoting a tight muscular structure, as well as a tolerable smell (M. Yang et al., 2024).

Concluding remarks and perspectives

Currently, consumers are more mindful of their health and dietary habits, leading them to choose more natural and additive-free products. In recent years, EOs have gained popularity as a natural alternative to chemical preservatives in the food industry, due to their significant biological activities, particularly antibacterial, antifungal, and antioxidant effects, which enable the effective control of food spoilage and extend the food's shelf life. This review consolidates current knowledge on the application of EOs in the food industry. It evaluates the chemical compositions, the antibacterial, antifungal, and antioxidant effectiveness of EOs in both free and encapsulated forms, as well as the interactions between EOCs and the food matrix, an area that remains underexplored in previous reviews, highlighting how these interactions influence the biological efficacy of these components. Additionally, the analysis of consumer acceptance and safety provides a comprehensive and up-to-date perspective on the practical use of EOs to improve food stability. Although encapsulation technology has come to address the physicochemical vulnerabilities of EOs in food applications, this topic still faces economic, regulatory, and consumer acceptance challenges. Future research should focus on clarifying the molecular mechanisms of interactions between EOs and food components in their free and encapsulated form and conducting more detailed safety and sensory evaluations in real food matrices using multidisciplinary methods to convert laboratory findings into accepted products.
