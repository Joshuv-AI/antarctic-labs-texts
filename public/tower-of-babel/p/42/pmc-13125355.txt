Outcomes from blind versus ultrasound‐guided uterine evacuation: A retrospective cohort analysis
Authors: Sarah Dowthwaite, Ann Quinton, Samantha Thomas, Timothy E Schlub, Jillian Clarke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWomen may need a surgical uterine evacuation for miscarriage, surgical termination, or retained products of conception. Previous research has indicated possible complications of uterine dilatation and curettage and potential benefits of an ultrasound‐guided procedure. This study aimed to compare the rate of complications for uterine evacuation when performed blind or with ultrasound guidance, including whether the presence of patient risk factors determined the use of ultrasound guidance.Material and MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 733 cases in an Australian hospital between January 2019 and December 2023. Medical records, imaging, and surgical reports were searched for patient demographics, risk factors, and the presence of surgical complications. Primary outcomes were complications of uterine perforation, false passage, incomplete curettage, heavy blood loss, or laparoscopy. Secondary outcomes were patient demographics and potential presurgical complication factors. The study created two cohorts: patients receiving evacuation performed blind or with ultrasound guidance.ResultsThe uterine evacuation was performed blind for 421/733 (57%) women and ultrasound‐guided for 312/733 (43%) women. The ultrasound‐guided group included higher gestational ages 8 [7–11] weeks vs blind 8 [7–9] weeks, more patients with risk factors (OR 1.7, CI: 1.3 to 2.3), more patients for surgical termination (OR 15.3, CI: 5.4 to 43.3), or postpartum RPOC removal (OR 2.3, CI: 1.3 to 3.9). The ultrasound group had lower overall blood loss (20 mLs vs 30 mL) and clinically significantly lower volumes of continuing retained products of conception, 0.66 cm3 [0.53–1.23] vs 8.35 cm3 [1.49–18.94]. There was no difference in complications between groups after adjusting for gestational age (OR 1.0, CI: 0.5 to 2.0).ConclusionsThere were low complication rates for blind and ultrasound‐guided uterine evacuations. However, ultrasound guidance was utilized for higher‐risk cases and significantly reduced the volume of retained tissue in complicated cases. The use of ultrasound guidance by doctors and sonographers for uterine evacuation is a compelling choice where available.Keywords: dilatation and curettage, intraoperative complications, pregnancy complications, ultrasonography

Key message

Complication rates for blind and ultrasound‐guided uterine evacuations were similarly low. Ultrasound tended to be used in higher‐risk cases, with evidence of reduced blood loss and lower volumes of RPOC despite these cases being higher risk.

INTRODUCTION

An average of 49 686 Australian women per year between 2019 and 2023 required surgical evacuation of gravid uterine contents, 1 playing an integral role in management of elective pregnancy termination—abortion, postintrauterine fetal demise, retained products of conception (RPOC)—incomplete abortion, gestational trophoblastic neoplasia, 2 , 3 failed expectant management, 4 or medical management of miscarriage. 5 , 6 Surgical evacuation offers the greatest complete evacuation rate, 7 reduces unplanned admissions postfailed management, 6 reduces bleeding and infection from RPOC 4 , 8 and decreases the role RPOC plays in the pathogenesis of infertility. 9 , 10 Surgical evacuation, however, has risks of hemorrhage, 5 uterine perforation, 7 , 11 or uterine trauma with late sequelae of intrauterine adhesions or infertility. 12 , 13

To reduce the risk of surgical complications continuous ultrasound guidance (USG) during uterine evacuation (D&C) is frequently recommended 14 to monitor procedure progress and confirm complete evacuation, 15 , 16 lowering rates of excessive curettage, which may cause Asherman's syndrome 17 , 18 and improving accuracy of tissue resection. 19 , 20 Ultrasound guidance can reduce operative time, 21 rates of RPOC, infection, perforation, cervical laceration and hemorrhage. 2 , 15 , 16 Traditionally, uterine evacuations are performed ‘blind’ with the experienced feel of the surgeon using vacuum suction or sharp curetting instruments inserted into the uterus without direct visualization of cervical canal or uterine cavity. 5 , 21

While complication rates for uterine evacuations are low and reported between 0.9% and 8.4%, 5 preconditions potentially increase the complication risk. These include scarred cervix or uterus, 22 , 23 , 24 Mullerian anomaly, 2 , 23 , 25 fibroid uterus, 23 , 26 previous evacuation, 25 previous unsuccessful evacuation, 26 previous surgical termination of pregnancy (STOP) or caesarean section (CS), 27 increasing GA 28 and surgical inexperience. 24 , 25 Complication rates may improve with ultrasound guidance from experienced sonographers or obstetricians. Sonographers are highly skilled allied health professionals, qualified and experienced in real‐time image optimization, orientation, obstetric and gynecological anatomy and pathophysiology. Sonographers can provide a contemporaneous narrative of instrument position during operative procedures, 29 dependent on visibility of the uterus seen posteriorly to an adequately filled bladder. Obstetricians can be trained to assess endometrial appearance, provide contemporaneous guidance and confirm complete evaluation using ultrasound. 5

The study objective was to compare surgical complication rates between blind uterine evacuation (BLIND) and ultrasound‐guided uterine evacuation (USG) and assess differences in patient risk factors between groups. The study aim was to compare outcomes of ultrasound‐guided versus blind uterine evacuations in routine clinical practice.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

This was a retrospective cohort study set in a single 750 bed private hospital in Sydney Australia with purposeful sampling of hospital records for women presenting for uterine evacuation during the period Jan 2019‐Dec 2023. Women were included if they had a clinical history of RPOC, incomplete miscarriage, STOP or adherent placenta, nonobstetric conditions were excluded. The study created two cohorts—patients receiving a uterine evacuation performed blind (BLIND) or with ultrasound guidance (USG). The USG cohort contained two subgroups with the ultrasound guidance being performed either intermittently as a Point of Care Ultrasound (POCUS) by an obstetrician or continuously by a sonographer (SONO). Data was extracted from surgical and pathology reports, patient files and admission records in the hospital database, and the ultrasound department database for referral information and imaging reports. Primary outcome data included the presence of uterine perforation, incomplete evacuation with continued RPOC, use of laparoscopy, creation of a false passage, estimated blood loss and heavy blood losses occurring during the procedure. Heavy blood loss was defined as 500 mLs or more based on the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (RCOG) postpartum hemorrhage Greentop guideline No. 52. 30 Secondary outcome data included patient demographics and potential complication factors including patient age, BMI, gravidity, parity, gestational age, previous uterine surgery including CS, hysteroscopy, uterine evacuation or failed evacuation, previous uterine perforation, presence of fibroids or Mullerian anomaly. Surgical procedural characteristics of cervical priming, the use of either vacuum suction, forceps, sharp curettage or manual removal of RPOC and the indication for evacuation were collected from patient and obstetrician files, surgical and imaging reports.

Women within the POCUS USG cohort were scanned by a C‐2‐5 MHz abdominal transducer (SonoSite X‐Porte TTC, FUJIFILM Corporation). The standard technique of one OBGYN who performed 101/132 (79%) of POCUS USG evacuations assessed uterine position before the sterile field was set up, with the transducer then left on the patient's abdomen beneath the sterile field setup. The transducer was held with sterile drapes for intermittent assessment of dilators, forceps, or suction position within the uterine cavity and at the completion of uterine evacuation. The remaining eight OBGYNs performed POCUS USG evacuations with varying techniques of scanning before, during, or postprocedure.

Women within the SONO USG cohort receiving continuous SONO guidance were scanned by a C‐1‐5 MHz abdominal transducer (GE Voluson E10 OLED, GE Healthcare Austria GmbH & Co OG). The uniform technique of SONO continuous guidance undertaken by all sonographers included sagittal and transverse planes for assessment of uterine position and uterine cavity contents prior to sterile setup and instrumentation. Continuous scanning was performed of the cervical canal and uterine cavity for the introduction of instruments and contemporaneous verbal assistance for direction of instruments toward RPOC and to assess completion of uterine evacuation as required. A transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS) was performed at the conclusion of surgery in the operating room if requested to assess if the evacuation of the cavity was successful. Women were only included if their obstetrician performed surgeries both blind and ultrasound‐guided in the overall study period to limit selection bias. The study included 5 years of data to allow for adequate comparative analysis.

Statistics

Data analysis was performed using the Jamovi software (version 2.6; The jamovi project, 2025) and included descriptive analyses of variables reported as mean (standard deviation), median [interquartile range] or number (percentage). Statistical methods used significance at p < 0.05 using t‐test or regression for continuous parametric data, Chi‐square or logistic regression for categorical data, and Mann–Whitney U for nonparametric data. Relative Risk (RR) and Odds Ratio (OR) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Blood loss and gestational age were log‐transformed to address skew before linear regression analysis. Binomial logistic regression was performed for associations of surgical complication. Since all procedures were performed by 17 obstetrician gynecologists, potential clustering and nonindependence of observations were assessed using mixed logistic regression with a random intercept clustered by doctor. Mixed‐effects results are reported only when they differ qualitatively from results not accounting for clustering. This study was retrospective and exploratory in nature and was not designed around a single prespecified primary hypothesis or comparison. A range of complication outcomes with differing baseline prevalences were examined (e.g., uterine perforation, heavy blood loss, incomplete evacuation), for which different effect sizes would be considered clinically meaningful. In this context, a posthoc power calculation would necessarily require the arbitrary selection of one outcome and effect size and would not add information beyond that already provided by the effect estimates and their confidence intervals, directly conveying the precision of the estimates and the range of effect sizes compatible with the observed data, so the degree of statistical uncertainty for each outcome can be assessed. Follow up was not performed due to the retrospective cohort design.

RESULTS

Between January 2019 and December 2023, 733 women met the inclusion criteria, with 421/733 (57.4%) BLIND and 312/733 (42.6%) USG. There were 1776 women excluded for nonobstetric conditions. Overall, the indications for uterine evacuation included: STOP 44/733 (6.0%), evacuation of miscarriage 573/733 (78.2%) and removal of RPOC 116/733 (15.8%) (Table 1). Miscarriage was the main indicator within the BLIND group for 373/421 (89%) of women and within POCUS USG for 119/132 (90%) of women. For SONO USG, there were more varied indicators of miscarriage 81/180 (45%), RPOC 60/180 (33%), and STOP 39/180 (22%).

Overall, gestational age at the time of procedure was first trimester 606/733 (83%), second trimester 48/733 (6.5%), and postpartum 57/733 (7.8%). Gestational ages were missing for 15/421 (2%) BLIND and 7/312 (1%) USG (Table 1). The median gestational age based on indication for surgery was miscarriage 8 [7–9] weeks, STOP 12 [11–13] weeks, and RPOC 12 [8–16] weeks.

All operations were performed under general anesthesia by a total of 17 consultant level Obstetrician Gynecologists (OBGYN) with experience ranging from under 2 years, 1 (5.9%); 2–5 years, 2 (11.8%); 6–9 years, 1 (5.9%); 10–14 years, 6 (35.3%); 15–20 years, 3 (17.7%); and more than 20 years, 4 (23.5%). The type of surgical equipment used during BLIND included 391/421 (93%) vacuum suction, 134/421 (32%) forceps, and 214/421 (51%) sharp curettage. Surgical equipment during USG included 290/321 (93%) vacuum suction, 212/321 (68%) forceps, and 117/321 (38%) sharp curettage. The manual removal of placenta without instruments was undertaken in 24/421 (6%) BLIND and 3/312 (1%) USG. Presurgical cervical ripening using misoprostol or cervagem was undertaken in 140/421 (33%) BLIND cases and 66/312 (21%) USG cases.

The USG group included a subgroup of 132/312 (42.3%) women in whom ultrasound was only used intermittently during the procedure (POCUS) (Table 1). One OBGYN performed 101/132 (79%) of the POCUS USGs. Eight OBGYNs performed the other 31/132 (21%) POCUS USGs. The second subgroup of 180/312 (57.7%) women had continuous ultrasound guidance performed by 14 sonographers (SONO) with a median of 15 [12–19] years postqualification. Within the SONO USG, 15 women had a TVUS in theater to assess completion of evacuation.

There was no significant difference in patient age in years or BMI (Table 2); however, there were differences in the gestational age at the time of procedure and indications for surgery between groups. There was strong evidence that there was a difference in range of gestational ages between groups (U = 42 720, p = 0.0001); median gestational ages were BLIND 8 weeks [7–9], USG 8 weeks [7–11]. The 57 women with postpartum RPOC were more likely to have evacuation under USG (x 2 (1) = 8.664, p = 0.003), OR: 2.3, CI: 1.3 to 3.9 (when accounting for clustering by obstetrician p = 0.05, OR = 2.0, CI: 1.0 to 2.8) (Table 1). The proportion of postpartum women was 35/57 (61.4%) in the USG group compared with 22/57 (38.5%) in the BLIND group. The odds of USG were higher for STOP and removal of RPOC, and lower for miscarriage compared to the odds of BLIND. (Table 1).

Patient histories were combined as risk factors for uterine evacuation to allow for statistical analysis, including CS, pelvic surgery, previous uterine evacuation or failed evacuation, previous perforation, false passage, fibroids and Mullerian anomaly (Table 2). The odds of women with risk factors for surgery being in the USG group was 1.7 times the odds of BLIND (x 2 (1) = 12.057, p = 0.0005, OR: 1.7, CI:1.3 to 2.3). The proportion of patients with risk factors in the BLIND group was lower 136/421 (32.3%) compared to the USG group 140/312 (44.9%). All 5 patients with previous uterine perforation had continuous SONO USG. All 19 patients with previous failed BLIND were performed using POCUS USG 2/19 (10.5%) or continuous SONO USG 17/19 (89.5%). SONO USG was also used for completion of evacuation after one BLIND perforation, guidance for one insertion of foley catheter and Bakri balloon for two patients with heavy blood losses.

Complication rate

Of the 733 women, 46/733 (6.3%) women experienced a surgical complication. In the BLIND group there were 23/421 (5.5%) women and in the USG group there were 23/312 (7.3%) women with complications, with no association between group and surgical complication (x 2 (1) = 1.110, p = 0.292) (when accounting for clustering by Obstetrician p = 0.065, OR = 0.53, CI: 0.27 to 1.04) (Table 3). There was evidence of an association between complicated surgery and gestational age (U = 5801, p = 0.0001). The median gestational age for noncomplicated surgeries was 8 weeks [7–9] and the median gestational age for complicated surgeries was 10 weeks [9–13]. The 15 patients with missing gestational ages were all uncomplicated. While holding gestational age constant, the odds of a complication in USG were similar to the odds of BLIND, OR: 1.0, CI: 0.5 to 2.0.

There was strong evidence of an association between complicated surgery and patients who were postpartum (x 2 (1) 6.726, p = 0.010). Although numbers were small, the proportions of postpartum complications in each group were BLIND 4/22 (18%) and USG 5/35 (14%). The four postpartum complications in the BLIND group all had heavy blood loss immediately postnormal vaginal delivery (NVD) with manual removal of placenta. The five postpartum USG complications included four with blood loss: one woman immediately post‐NVD, two women 2–6 weeks post‐NVD, one woman 6 weeks post‐CS, and one uterine perforation under suction and sharp curettage. However, there was no evidence of an association between complicated surgery and previous CS (x 2 (1) 0.8449, p = 0.358), previous uterine surgery (x 2 (1) 0.056, p = 0.813), previous evacuation (x 2 (1) 0.746, p = 0.388), Mullerian anomaly (x 2 (1) 0.134, p = 0.715), or fibroids (x 2 (1) 0.055, p = 0.815).

The uterine perforation rate was 3/733 (0.4%). There were two BLIND perforations, both recognized at the time of surgery; one proceeded to be completed under laparoscopic guidance, showing no bowel perforation, and one proceeded to USG SONO. There was one perforation under POCUS USG, discovered postsurgically by pathological fimbrial tissue. The patient was later readmitted for laparoscopy, showing sigmoid epiploica and left tube within the left uterine cornua without bowel perforation. There were no perforations in the USG SONO continuous guidance subgroup. There were no instrumental false passages recorded in either group.

The incomplete evacuation rate with persistent RPOC was 23/733 (3.1%). Women had similar prevalence of incomplete evacuation in BLIND 14/421 (3.33%) and USG 9/312 (2.88%), RR = 1.0, CI: 0.97 to 1.02, p = 0.74. However, there was evidence (U = 23, p = 0.019) that the volume of persistent RPOC measured in 3 planes (cm3), was larger in BLIND 8.35 cm3 [1.49–18.94] compared to USG 0.66 cm3 [0.53–1.23]. (Supplementary 1) The following clinical symptoms elicited an ultrasound to diagnose RPOC after evacuation; prolonged or excessive bleeding 31 (n = 9), pain without bleeding (n = 5), no return of menstrual cycle (n = 3), scan by obstetrician (n = 3), negative pathology (n = 1), known RPOC at the end of evacuation (n = 1) and unknown (n = 1). Ultrasound criteria used to define RPOC included a focally thickened heterogenous endometrium 5 or endometrial mass exhibiting vascularity when scrutinized with Doppler which dramatically increases the diagnostic confidence for identifying RPOC. 32

There was evidence of an association between gestational age and continued RPOC (x 2 (1) = 5.274, p = 0.022). Within USG the following technical factors that obscured visualization of the uterus were observed to potentially contribute to continued RPOC; an empty bladder (n = 1), bowel obscuring uterine fundus (n = 1), blood filled cavity (n = 1) and small RPOC in the cornua (n = 2).

There was strong evidence of association between group and use of forceps during surgery (x 2 (1) = 93.809, p = <0.0001), BLIND 134/421 (32%) and USG 212/312 (68%). The odds of forceps use in USG was 4.5 times more likely than the odds in BLIND (CI: 3.3 to 6.2). There was no evidence of a difference in the proportion of repeat surgery rate between groups (x 2 (1) = 0.857, p = 0.355). The prevalence of repeat surgery was 1.6 times higher in BLIND, OR 1.6, CI: 0.5–4.8. Within BLIND, 11/421 (2.6%) women had a successful repeat evacuation, 6 performed by hysteroscope, 3 by ultrasound‐guided hysteroscope and 2 by USG SONO. Within USG, 5/312 (1.6%) women had successful repeat surgery, one performed by hysteroscope and four by USG SONO.

The rate of heavy blood loss over 500 mLs was 20/733 (2.7%), which included 7 BLIND and 13 USG women. There was evidence of an association between increasing gestational age and mean blood loss (t (607) = 10.95, p < 0.001). On average, blood loss increased by a factor of 18% for every 10% increase in gestational age (CI: 15% to 21% increase). There was evidence (U = 53 190, p = 0.047) that overall estimated blood loss was less in USG 20 mL [10–59] compared to BLIND 30 mL [10–53]. There was no evidence of association between the amount of heavy blood loss (mL) and group (U = 34, p = 0.376). Median heavy blood loss was 1000 mL [550–1600] in the BLIND group and 800 mL [500–1000] in the USG group. There was no evidence of association between group and blood loss complication (x 2 (1) = 2.625, p = 0.105). The median gestational age for 9 women with heavy blood loss in USG was 13 weeks [11–19] and for 3 women in BLIND was 10 weeks [9–14]. There were 4 postpartum women with blood loss complications in each group, one patient in BLIND proceeded to uterine embolization and subtotal abdominal hysterectomy.

DISCUSSION

This study highlighted low complication rates for uterine evacuation when performed blind or with ultrasound guidance. Ultrasound guidance was selected for patients with previous complications and preferred for STOP and RPOC cases, which included higher gestational ages and postpartum women. There was a lower volume of retained tissue postprocedure in the US group.

The overall study complication rate was low at 6.3% (Table 3), similar to the 6% complication rate suggested when obtaining consent from women undergoing surgical management of miscarriage and RPOC. 33 The uterine perforation rate of 0.4% was within the range 0.1%–1.5% reported by RCOG. 33 Reporting of perforation can be dependent on diagnosis being made during surgery, the patient having post‐surgical symptoms 24 or indicated by pathology results, as was the case with one POCUS USG perforation. This highlights that intermittent assessment of instruments within the cavity (POCUS) is not equivalent to the continuous vision and guidance offered by the SONO group.

Binomial logistic regression analysis found the only variables associated with a complication were gestational age and the postpartum state. Our results differ from the findings of Levin, 34 who found intraoperative ultrasound guidance during the second trimester, the only independent factor positively associated with the occurrence of an adverse outcome. Similar to Shakir's study, our results demonstrated blood loss increased with increasing gestational age. 23 As our study included 606/733 (83%) first‐trimester gestational ages, our results were similar to Acharya 15 and Elsayed, 16 who found that ultrasound guidance at first‐trimester STOP reduced overall blood loss, with average blood loss significantly less in our USG group. In addition, our study rate of significant hemorrhage (blood loss more than 500 mL) was equal for ultrasound‐guided and blind evacuations, consistent with Acharya. 15

Ultrasound guidance was preferred for STOP and RPOC. Guidance can be performed according to obstetrician custom and practice rather than case selection, 34 however, our study found USG was more likely chosen for patients with risk factors for evacuation. Previous studies reported only the presence of fibroids, 15 , 16 previous STOP, 15 previous CS, 34 and one study excluded women with previous uterine scars or surgery. 9 In our study, ultrasound guidance was used for all patients with previous perforation or failed blind evacuation, to support after blind complication or assist the insertion of a foley catheter or Bakri balloon for blood loss management. Ultrasound guidance was successful for all our repeat surgical evacuation of the uterus for women with incomplete evacuation. 35

Australian guidelines state vacuum suction is conditionally recommended as the preferred surgical procedure for miscarriage under 14 weeks and that dilatation and sharp curettage is preferentially avoided. 36 A low grade of evidence suggests sharp curettage is associated with an increased risk of intrauterine adhesions. 36 Based on our findings, the use of vacuum suction alone, sharp curettage, or a combination of both followed the surgeon's preference (Table 4) The ongoing selective use of sharp curettage in our department highlights the need for larger prospective studies to evaluate infrequent or long‐term outcomes. The ability of ultrasound guidance to direct forceps to the exact position of RPOC could explain the higher forceps use in the USG cohort (Table 4).

Ultrasound guidance does significantly increase the amount of RPOC removed; however, it does have limitations. Our study concurs with Maslovitz, 8 that it is not uncommon to find residual tissue after surgical evacuation. There were 14 women with continuing RPOC within BLIND and 9 women within USG. Larish 5 suggested an implementation of transvaginal ultrasound as an effective safety check to confirm complete uterine evacuation following surgery. One patient with a history of 4 previous D&Cs underwent SONO USG, including transvaginal ultrasound in theater at the completion of surgery, and was found to have a small necrotic RPOC within the right cornua on sonohysterogram 9 weeks later. This suggests that, although transvaginal ultrasound is a very useful tool to check completion, small amounts of RPOC can be obscured by blood within the cavity or be difficult to detect within the uterine cornua. Further research is needed to assess the effectiveness of transvaginal ultrasound during uterine evacuations.

The availability of USG in this study was utilized to support higher risk surgeries, with obstetricians preferring to perform STOP and removal of RPOC under USG. Therefore, training of obstetricians and gynecologists, sonographers, and health care assistants should be considered, given the evidence of improved patient outcomes for both routine and higher risk surgeries. The success of surgical evacuation may be influenced by GA, 7 and as our study demonstrated, increasing GA increases blood loss and incidence of RPOC, indicating ultrasound guidance would be valuable for surgeries in later gestations. A higher prevalence of repeat surgery within the BLIND group could potentially be explained by larger residual RPOC volumes after BLIND was performed. Trauma to the uterine lining during D&C is implicated in the formation of intrauterine adhesions which are associated with pain, placental implantation disorders and decreased fertility. 37 An USG evacuation can improve reproductive outcomes, 9 reduce excessive curettage 15 and minimize unnecessary trauma to the endometrium. 38 Ben Ami 39 found the presence of residual trophoblastic tissue rather than surgical intervention in the gravid uterus is primarily related to RPOC‐associated infertility. The reduction in residual RPOC could therefore be crucial for a woman's fertility. Hysteroscopic assessment for adhesions 4–8 weeks after D&C has been suggested to further understand patient outcomes after ultrasound‐guided curettage. 37 However, appearances of the uterine cavity and endometrial disruptions on transvaginal ultrasound can be preferably used to assess intrauterine adhesions without the need for hysteroscopy. 40

The strengths of this study included access to extensive imaging data from a dedicated women's imaging department run by obstetrician gynecologists specializing in ultrasound and detailed surgical and patient history data from obstetricians within a maternity hospital. This study was set in a large private hospital, highlighting the benefit of access to ultrasound machines, trained sonographers, and experienced obstetricians. Review of outcomes at differently sized facilities and training hospitals may highlight differing complication rates of less experienced POCUS USG and availability of SONO USG. The retrospective, nonrandomized design means that ultrasound guidance was preferentially used in selected cases, introducing selection bias, and limiting full comparability between the BLIND and USG groups. Future studies should be adequately powered to definitively comment on statistically significant complication rates. For some particularly rarer complications such as uterine perforation, laparoscopy and heavy blood loss which is affected by gestational age, the confidence intervals are relatively wide, indicating limited power to detect small group relative differences. A potential confounder of surgical inexperience for uterine perforation was not a factor in our study; 22 , 28 however, variability in ultrasound operator experience could impact comparability between groups. Outcomes may differ in other hospital settings due to surgical training levels, access to ultrasound equipment and trained sonographers available to assist. This study did not evaluate postprocedure infection or surgical time. Lastly, a key limitation of the study is that blind procedures have a higher risk of unreported 16 or unrecognized false passages and perforations. 41

CONCLUSION

Despite being more commonly used in patients with more advanced gestational age and higher surgical risk, ultrasound guidance was associated with reduced estimated blood loss and a lower volume of RPOC. Overall, there were low complication rates for evacuation performed BLIND or USG; however, blind evacuations have the potential for unrecognized and unreported false passages or perforation. Ultrasound guidance is a compelling choice for patients with previous complications, higher gestational ages, postpartum RPOC, or repeat surgeries. Future studies on the availability of sonographer or obstetrician guidance are warranted to address gaps in service provision and improve patient outcomes.
