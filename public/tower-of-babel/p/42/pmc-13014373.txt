Reexamining Transposed-Word Effects in the Grammatical Decision Task
Authors: Yun Wen, Jonathan Grainger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract: Transposed-word (TW) effects have been repeatedly observed when comparing responses to two types of ungrammatical sequence of words in a grammatical decision task: 1) transposed-word sequences formed by changing the order of two words in a grammatically correct sentence (e.g., The white was cat big); and 2) ungrammatical control sequences that cannot be transformed into a correct sentence by transposing any two words (e.g., The white was cat slowly). When the task is speeded grammatical decision, and when all words are presented simultaneously in a format that is typical of regular reading, TW effects are observed in both response times (RTs) and error rates. Specifically, responses to the TW sequences are slower and less accurate than to the matched control sequences. However, when written words are presented sequentially, one after the other, then TW effects typically only emerge in error rates. Finally, when the word sequences are presented auditorily, TW effects are significant in both RTs and accuracy. Building on the meta-analysis of TW effects described in Wen and Grainger (2025a), here we present a more detailed analysis of this general pattern and provide a tentative explanation for the observed differences across presentation procedure and modality.

Transposed-word (TW) effects have been observed in many different studies using different methodologies (see Mollica et al., 2020, for a review of early studies) and therefore now constitute a key phenomenon that models of written and spoken sentence comprehension must account for. Recently, the principal methodology for revealing TW effects has become the grammatical decision task (Mirault et al., 2018), a task where participants are requested to judge as rapidly and as accurately as possible if a sequence of words is grammatically correct or not. To observe TW effects, responses to two types of ungrammatical sequences of words are compared in this task: 1) transposed-word sequences formed by changing the order of two words in a grammatically correct sentence (e.g., “The white was cat big”); and 2) ungrammatical control sequences that cannot be transformed into a correct sentence by transposing any two words (e.g., “The white was cat slowly”). TW effects generally manifest through longer RTs and more errors to TW sequences relative to control sequences (see Mirault et al., 2018 for a design that enabled the use of the exact same words in the TW and control conditions). Of course, for the purpose of the grammatical decision task, grammatically correct phrases or sentences must be included and these are randomly intermixed with the ungrammatical sequences. The grammatically correct sequences are not related to the ungrammatical sequences.

The focus of the present study is on TW effects in the speeded grammatical decision task. This task can be thought of as the sentence-level equivalent of the lexical decision task (Grainger, 2024) – a popular task in studies of visual and auditory word recognition. Here, we examine possible reasons for why transposed-word effects only emerge in error rates and not in RTs under certain conditions, while in other conditions the effects are robust in both RTs and error rates (Wen & Grainger, 2025a). For completeness, we first review studies on the transposed-word effect by highlighting its theoretical implications for understanding skilled reading before we summarize key elements of the results of such studies. We then comment on what these results might reflect. Finally, we offer an overarching interpretation of TW effects obtained in the grammatical decision task. The interpretation is based on an account of processing in the speeded grammatical decision task that is inspired by computational models of the lexical decision task, and in particular the leaky competing accumulator (LCA) model proposed by Dufau et al. (2012) based on Usher and McClelland’s (2001) LCA model of speeded binary decision making.

Transposed-Word Effects as a Hallmark of Noisy Word Position Coding

The question of how readers encode the order of words in which they occur within a sentence is at the crux of reading research. Before the discovery of TW effects, it was generally accepted that processing one word at a time is the sine-qua-non of precise word position coding (e.g., Reichle et al., 2009).1 This serial processing account of word-order encoding is appealing because it accords with our intuition that we seldom confuse the order of words in written texts despite the fact that our eyes do fixate words out of order (e.g., Kennedy & Pynte, 2008). At odds with the eminent serial processing view, TW effects obtained with a grammatical decision task (see Pegado & Grainger, 2020, for a TW effect using a same-different matching task; and Wen et al., 2022, for a TW effect using a postcued word identification task) reveal the noisy nature of word-order encoding that is akin to the flexible letter position coding operating in visual word recognition (Gómez et al., 2008; Grainger & Ziegler, 2011).

As explained in Mirault et al. (2018) and later studies (e.g., Liu et al., 2020; Snell & Grainger, 2019b; Wen et al., 2021b), TW effects not only concur with the idea of good-enough parsing during sentence comprehension (e.g., Ferreira & Patson, 2007; Ferreira et al., 2002), but also constitute evidence for a parallel processing system that inevitably applies noisy word position coding (for a review, see Snell & Grainger, 2019a). The latter conclusion was reinforced by the OB-1 reader model (Snell et al., 2018) since it successfully implemented noisy word position coding within a computational model of parallel word processing during reading. In the hindsight of TW effects, Huang and Staub (2021a, 2021b) proposed a modified serial processing account in which serial mechanisms are relaxed during postlexical integration but maintained during lexical processing. That is, although words are recognized in a strictly serial fashion, recognized words are not integrated immediately, thus allowing words to be reordered via syntactic and semantic constraints. In a nutshell, while both theoretical camps agree that TW effects demonstrate noisy word position coding, whether TW effects originate during lexical or postlexical processing (or both) remains a contentious topic.

Another area of contention concerns TW effects obtained with the Rapid Serial Visual Presentation (RSVP) technique in the grammatical decision task (for an early combination of RSVP and grammatical decisions, see McElree, 1993; McElree & Griffith, 1995). As a serial presentation technique, RSVP should reduce the possibility of processing words in parallel. Given the TW effects consistently obtained under parallel visual presentation (e.g., Mirault et al., 2018), the absence of TW effects under RSVP would indicate that the serial processing account of TW effects is not tenable, whereas a reliable TW effect under RSVP would imply that parallel processing is not a prerequisite for TW effects. Although the logic of testing TW effects under RSVP is straightforward, the results obtained at present preclude straightforward interpretations. This is because TW effects under serial presentation do not always emerge in RTs (e.g., significant RT differences were found in Hossain & White, 2023; Milledge et al., 2023; Spinelli et al., 2024, but not in Liu et al., 2022; Mirault et al., 2022; Snell & Melo, 2024), whereas the effects systematically emerge in RTs under parallel presentation (see Wen & Grainger, 2025a, for a review). Why such different patterns occur as a function of presentation mode is the subject of the present study. We acknowledge, nevertheless, that presentation mode (serial vs. parallel) does not map directly onto the processing mechanisms involved in making speeded grammatical decisions, and that additional inferences have to be made to unambiguously establish this mapping (cf. the hotly debated issue of very fast serial processing vs. parallel processing of letters during single word recognition: see e.g., Staub et al., 2025).

Given that TW effects under RSVP contribute to adjudicating the serial and parallel processing accounts of word-order encoding, it is vital to understand why TW effects do not always emerge in RTs when words are presented serially (RSVP). To this end, Wen and Grainger (2025a) used a meta-analysis approach and demonstrated that the effect size of TW effects in RTs is small under serial presentation (i.e., Cohen’s d = 0.107). Building on this recent meta-analysis, the present study aims to provide a theoretical framework to begin to elucidate the underlying factors that might modulate the magnitude of TW effects. This tentative theoretical framework is based on a re-examination of the patterns emerging from RT and error data in visual and auditory studies, which is the focus of the next section.

The Key Data Patterns to Be Explained

Effects in RTs Occur When the Grammatical Condition Generates Faster RTs Than the TW (Ungrammatical) Condition

In light of the typical patterns seen in the lexical decision task, studies using a grammatical decision task should also report faster RTs to YES responses than to NO responses. Note for this to be valid, the task has to be speeded grammatical decision and not untimed grammaticality judgments. Furthermore, there may well be exceptions to this general rule of thumb for this particular task. For example, this is not true for the unspaced (e.g., Theylikedthescenery) and backward (e.g., scenery the liked they)2 reading conditions tested by Mirault et al. (2023) where there was no TW effect in RTs, yet the grammatical condition yielded faster RTs than the TW condition. Here, we focus on the pattern of TW effects seen under 1) parallel visual presentation; 2) serial visual presentation; and 3) auditory presentation. In the Visual Modality, TW Effects in RTs Are Larger and More Robust Under Parallel Presentation Than Serial Presentation

We propose that under parallel presentation, information about several word identities and their positions can be gathered more rapidly than under serial presentation. This enables faster correct RTs for grammatical sequences under parallel presentation (see Table 1), and particularly when the ungrammaticality is determined by the final word in the sequence (which is typically the case). However, detecting a syntactic/semantic anomaly under parallel presentation is harder for TW sequences than control sequences, hence slowing responses to the TW sequences (i.e., a TW effect in RTs). This occurs mainly under parallel presentation because of the increased uncertainty about word order in this condition relative to serial visual presentation. That is, serial presentation provides unequivocal information about word order, whereas parallel presentation, under the assumption of a certain amount of parallel word processing in these presentation conditions, generates greater uncertainty with respect to word order.

Under serial presentation, participants can only be sure that the sequence is grammatical or not when the final word in the sequence is presented. Hence, the longer RTs under serial presentation. However, participants can still fail to notice the TW error, and hence, the TW effect is maintained in error rates. Moreover, it is important to note that error rates are much lower in the TW condition under serial presentation compared with parallel presentation (see Table 1). This result suggests that under parallel presentation, participants are willing to make an early decision based on partial information gleaned from several words in parallel, hence increasing error rates. This fast global decision making would not be possible with serial presentation which would depend more on the local detection of ungrammaticality. We return to discuss the global-local distinction in more detail below.

What About the Auditory Modality?

One might expect to observe the same pattern in the auditory modality as with serial visual presentation – but that is not the case (see Table 1). TW effects arise in both RTs and errors under auditory presentation (Dufour et al., 2022). We first suspected that the typical versus atypical presentation procedures might play a role given the results reported in Mirault et al. (2023). That is, serial visual presentation (RSVP) is not how we typically read text, whereas seriality is imposed by auditory presentation. Crucially, however, although the means in Table 1 reveal a difference in TW effects obtained with serial visual and auditory presentation, more detailed analyses to be presented below revealed more similarities between these two serial procedures compared with parallel presentation.

Our preferred explanation for this pattern of TW effects in the auditory modality is that a grammatical decision in the auditory modality cannot be accurately made until participants have heard the entire sequence. Participants will therefore tend to wait to the end of the sequence before making their response to be sure that the sequence is indeed grammatically correct. Hence, grammatical RTs will be slower than ungrammatical RTs except when participants make ungrammatical decisions after hearing the complete sequence. As shown in Table 2, a post hoc analysis of the results of Dufour et al. (2022) revealed that this is indeed the case. When participants responded after the last word in the sequence, then there was a robust 32 ms TW effect in RTs (1,852 ms in the TW condition vs. 1,820 ms in the control condition). On the other hand, when participants responded before the end of the sequence, the TW effect in RTs was not significant (1,402 ms in the TW condition vs. 1,416 ms in the control condition).

Moreover, as we will show below, with more detailed analyses of performance under visual parallel presentation, visual serial presentation, and auditory presentation, it is clear that it is the serial (visual, auditory) versus parallel (visual) presentation that is the principal element in determining the pattern of TW effects. But first, we present our explanation of the differing patterns of TW effects in more detail.

A Tentative Account of Decision Making in the Grammatical Decision Task

Here we present a tentative model of decision making in the speeded grammatical decision task based principally on random-walk theory applied to speeded binary decision making. A highly influential binary decision version of random walk theory, the Drift Diffusion Model has been successfully applied to account for results obtained with the lexical decision task (Ratcliff et al., 2004). However, we decided to frame our account of the grammatical decision task using the Leaky Competing Accumulator model (LCA; Usher & McClelland, 2001) which has been successfully applied to stimulate data of YES and NO decisions for the lexical decision task (Dufau et al., 2012). It is noteworthy that both models (Drift Diffusion and LCA) assume noisy accumulation of information over time to two response criteria, and the first criterion to be reached determines the speed and accuracy of participants’ responses in speeded binary decision making. Furthermore, these two approaches to describing the mechanisms involved in speed binary decision making have been shown to be mathematically equivalent (Bogacz et al., 2006). We opted for the LCA approach because it is conceptually simpler to think of evidence for ungrammaticality as absence of evidence for grammaticality. This is particularly important in the context of the present account of grammatical decision making, since we invoke the possibility of an additional mechanism for making ungrammatical decisions that involves the explicit detection of ungrammaticality. That is, an ungrammatical decision could be made on the basis of: 1) the absence of evidence for grammaticality or 2) explicit evidence for ungrammaticality.

The proposed model of speeded binary decision making in the grammatical decision task builds on the model of lexical decision making proposed by Grainger (2018), which itself was derived from Grainger and Jacobs’ (1996) model. Grainger’s model of lexical decision combines a global evaluation of lexicality (using the LCA model of binary decision making as described in Dufau et al., 2012) combined with a local decision of lexicality based on word identification (i.e., I have recognized a word, therefore the response must be “yes – this is a word”). This model therefore proposed that lexical decisions could be based on either a global evaluation of lexicality or the local identification of a lexical item. The difference with respect to the model proposed by Grainger and Jacobs (1996) concerns the evaluation of global lexical activity and how that is used to make a lexical decision. This change was motivated by criticisms of the Grainger and Jacobs model described in Ratcliff et al. (2004) and Wagenmakers et al. (2008).

Building on this prior work on decision making mechanisms in the lexical decision task, here we analyze what might be the decision making mechanisms that are specific to the grammatical decision task, and why might they differ as a function of stimulus presentation. In the speeded grammatical decision task (i.e., with instructions to respond as rapidly and accurately as possible), participants set criteria for deciding whether a sequence of words is grammatically correct or not. Evidence that a sequence of words is grammatically correct or not evolves over time following the general principles of noisy accumulation of evidence over time (i.e., a random walk). Response criteria (for grammatical and ungrammatical responses) are set as a function of on-going experience with stimuli tested in the experiment with an aim to optimize speed and accuracy of responding (i.e., following the instructions given to participants). When the criterion for a grammatical response is reached first, then a grammatical decision is made and the time to make that decision is determined by the time taken to reach the grammatical response criterion. An ungrammatical response is made when the criterion for this response is reached before the grammatical response criterion. Errors occur when either of these response criteria are reached before the correct response criterion. So far, this is a straightforward extension of models of lexical decision (Dufau et al., 2012; Ratcliff et al., 2004) to the case of speeded grammatical decisions.

Concerning TW effects, we suggest that under parallel visual presentation, TW sequences (e.g., the white was cat big) would provide stronger evidence in favor of a grammatical response compared with the control sequences (e.g., the white was cat slowly), hence the slower RTs observed in the TW condition (more evidence for a grammatical response implies less evidence for an ungrammatical response). However, the strongest evidence for a grammatical response is generated by the grammatically correct sequences, hence the faster RTs with these sequences. That is, under typical presentation conditions in the visual modality, participants would apply a global evaluation of the grammaticality of the word sequence and base their response on this information. This global evaluation would be facilitated by the partly parallel word processing that is enabled with parallel visual presentation.

The key question then is, what is the nature of this global evidence for grammaticality that is hypothesized to be used to determine responding in the grammatical decision task under typical presentation conditions? We propose that, with short sequences of words presented in a typical format for reading, then evidence for grammaticality can be rapidly evaluated on the basis of the sequence of parts-of-speech that the word sequences generate (e.g., Declerck et al., 2020). In other words, the global evaluation of grammaticality is enabled by the rapid extraction of a primitive sentence-level representation via partly parallel word processing. Accessing information about the syntactic categories of words would enable the rapid evaluation of whether or not the sequence of words is grammatically correct or not.3 This explains why TW effects are observed in RTs when the grammatical condition generates faster RTs than the TW (ungrammatical) condition.

We further suggest that the rapidity of this calculation of grammaticality would be greatly hindered by RSVP, a procedure that would impede the rapid calculation of a primitive syntactic structure. This might induce participants to indulge in alternative strategies for generating their grammatical decisions. One obvious alternative strategy would be to simply search for local evidence for ungrammaticality as opposed to the global evidence for grammaticality, that was evoked above. Local evidence for ungrammaticality would be driven by detection of a word-order violation in the transposed-word sequences or by any form of syntactic violation in the control sequences. This strategy would work well under RSVP (e.g., when serially reading the sequence “the white was cat big” local detection of the word order violation “was cat” would immediately signal an ungrammaticality). This local response strategy is hypothesized to reduce TW effects in RTs given that local ungrammaticalities should be detected at the same time for the TW and the control sequences (e.g., detecting the ungrammaticality of “was cat” in the control sequence “The white was cat slowly”). The fact that the ungrammaticality of TW sequences goes unnoticed more often than the ungrammaticality of the control sequences can be attributed to the greater global evidence for grammaticality in the TW sequences once the entire sequence has been read. This results in more incorrect grammatical responses to TW sequences than control sequences, hence the TW effect in error rates under serial presentation conditions.

The results of Spinelli et al. (2024) provide support for the use of local ungrammaticality detection under conditions of RSVP. In their Experiment 2, RSVP was used to present grammatical sequences and TW and control ungrammatical sequences, as well as an additional type of ungrammatical sequence that became ungrammatical only at the end at the end of the sequence (termed the SW condition: e.g., The white cat was slowly). The SW condition generated the slowest RTs under RSVP, but this was not the case under parallel presentation (their Experiment 1) where the TW condition was the slowest and the control condition was the fastest. Another relevant study with respect to the hypothesized global versus local detection of ungrammaticality is that of Dunagan et al. (2025). This study used the rapid parallel visual presentation (RPVP) procedure but with a same-different matching task and EEG recordings (for a study using the RPVP procedure and a grammatical decision task, see Wen et al., 2021a). The averaged EEG data (ERPs) did not reveal a difference between grammatical sentences (e.g., the dogs chase a ball) and ungrammatical sequences that contained agreement errors (e.g., the dogs chases a ball). These findings are at odds with the classic RSVP studies that revealed a P600 effect for agreement errors (e.g., Osterhout & Mobley, 1995). Dunagan et al. suggested that their participants may have simply ignored morphosyntactic information that is not useful for the rapid identification of the semantic ‘gist’ of a sentence as postulated in ‘good enough’ parsing models of sentence comprehension (e.g., Ferreira & Patson, 2007; Ferreira et al., 2002).

As concerns the auditory modality, we propose that both local and global measures of grammaticality are at play. When the sequence of words is grammatical, participants can only be sure of the grammaticality of the sequence after hearing the last word in the sequence. Hence, RTs are determined by the rapidity with which a grammaticality evaluation can be performed after hearing the complete sequence of words. On the other hand, ungrammaticality can be detected locally before the end of the sequence. This leads to a mix of strategies for making a grammatical decision: either locally (I detect an ungrammaticality and base my decision on this) or globally (I wait to the end of the auditory sequence and then decide if it is grammatical or not). We hypothesize that it is the partial use of a global strategy in the auditory modality that generates relatively small but significant TW effects in RTs in this modality. That is, waiting until the end of the auditory sequence to make a grammatical decision would implicate the global decision mechanism discussed above, hence generating a TW effect in RTs and faster RTs to grammatically correct sequences. The global strategy could be partially applied in auditory grammatical decision, since the presentation format of the word sequences is typical of that modality. This is not the case in RSVP and other types of atypical visual presentation formats. In other words, as the auditory word sequence unfolds, evidence would gradually accumulate in favor of a grammatical or ungrammatical response following the general principles of speeded binary decision making described above. However, local detections of ungrammaticality would change the overall pattern of effects seen in the auditory modality compared with parallel visual presentation. This could well be exaggerated by the fact that everyday speech often includes ungrammatical errors, and at least more often than the text that is typically read in books, newspapers, and on-line media.

What is immediately clear in Figures 1–3 is that performance under parallel and serial visual presentation is very different. The other interesting point that can be made on a purely visual analysis of these Figures is that performance under auditory presentation is quite similar to serial visual presentation and definitely more similar to serial visual presentation than parallel visual presentation. However, there are two subtle, but nevertheless important differences between the visual serial and auditory presentation results. One is the early bump in the delta plots (around 1,700 ms) that is clearly visible in the auditory data but not in the serial visual data. This difference is also visible in the RT distributions, with a TW effect emerging around the median of the distribution in the auditory condition but not in the serial visual condition. We take this as evidence that there is indeed a difference in how participants respond in an auditory grammatical decision task compared with serial visual presentation, and it is this difference that drives the small but significant TW effect in RTs under auditory presentation.

Summary of How the Model Accounts for the Main Findings

As noted above, it is clear from Figures 1–3 that the pattern of TW effects in the auditory modality is more in line with serial visual presentation than parallel visual presentation. However, compared with serial visual presentation, the auditory presentation data show a greater effect in RTs that is visible around the median RT. In the delta plots, there is an initial increase in the size of TW effects around 1,700 ms that is not visible in the serial visual delta plots. This pattern can also be seen in the density plots. We take this as evidence in support of our hypothesis that auditory grammatical decisions involve both local and global detection of ungrammaticality. It is the faster detection of local ungrammaticality that would be responsible for the early effect in RTs in the auditory modality compared with serial visual presentation. This would be the factor driving the small but significant TW effects in RTs seen with auditory presentation (Dufour et al., 2022) and typically not seen with serial visual presentation.

Finally, the conditional accuracy functions shown in Figure 2 clearly distinguish between the pattern of TW effects in errors rates under serial and parallel presentation in the visual modality. TW effects in errors are very large in the initial phase of parallel presentation and diminish thereafter. On the contrary, TW effects under serial presentation are minimal at the shortest RTs and gradually get larger as RTs increase. Once again this is evidence for our proposed distinction between global and local grammaticality evaluation, with the global strategy (under parallel presentation) generating the widespread TW effect in error rates that starts at the shortest RTs.

Future Work

We have argued that the discrepant findings concerning TW effects obtained under serial and parallel presentations are due to participants’ application of global and/or local strategies when a speeded grammatical decision is made. In this section, we advocate for the continued use of the speeded grammatical decision task in future research by outlining experimental factors that might modify decision making strategies in the grammatical decision task.

It should be noted that Mirault et al. (2018), as well as follow-up studies, provided visual feedback (e.g., a green tick for a correct response or a red cross for an incorrect response) for all experimental trials. Although employing feedback in terms of accuracy is a useful way to engage participants in a difficult experimental task (in contrast to the fact that in a relatively easy task, such as lexical decision, typically no feedback is provided except possibly during practice trials), it is unclear whether participants would adopt the same strategy in absence of such feedback. Therefore, it seems necessary for future studies to compare TW effects with and without feedback across different presentation domains.

An alternative means of modulating decision making strategies would be to manipulate the proportion of grammatical and ungrammatical trials. When ungrammatical sequences are more often encountered in an experiment setting, participants may tend to adopt a more prudent strategy when trying to detect an ungrammaticality, leading to slower responses to grammatical sequences relative to ungrammatical sequences. The use of unequal numbers of grammatical versus ungrammatical trials could be further combined with a systematic manipulation of response deadlines (for an example of such a combination using a flanker task, see Heitz & Engle, 2007). Since imposing a time-limit for responding is effective in changing the trade-off between accuracy and speed (for a review, see Heitz, 2014), including a range of response deadlines for grammatical decisions may provide a more detailed picture of how participants employ global or local strategies depending on the demands of the task.

Finally, varying the nature of the ungrammaticality and the moment where this arises in the ungrammatical sequences could provide further leverage in helping understand exactly how participants perform the grammatical decision task, and how this task generates TW effects.
