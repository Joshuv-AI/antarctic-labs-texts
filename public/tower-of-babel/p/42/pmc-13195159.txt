A STDFT-CEEMD approach with wavelet packet thresholding for exon prediction in eukaryotic cells
Authors: Shaik Benarjee, Naveen Kumar Vaegae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
One of the most important tasks in genomic analysis is the precise identification of protein-coding regions in Deoxyribo nucleic acid (DNA) sequences. The robust exon prediction methodology presented in this paper is based on Hadamard numerical mapping, Short-Time Discrete Fourier Transform (STDFT), Complete Ensemble Empirical Mode Decomposition (CEEMD) and Wavelet Packet Thresholding (WPT). In order to provide orthogonal and noise-resistant representation, the DNA sequence is first numerically encoded using Hadamard mapping. The frequency components, specifically the period-3 signal indicative of coding regions are subsequently extracted using STDFT. After the signal is broken down into Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMFs) using CEEMD, noise-dominant IMFs are removed using WPT and a self-correlation function. The resulting signal has higher exon peaks and less background noise. MATLAB simulations were conducted using two benchmark data sequences: F56F11.4 (C. elegans), PSMB5 (Mus musculus) and HMR195 dataset. Using the HMR195 dataset and gene sequences (C. elegans F56F11.4 and M. musculus PSMB5), the proposed approach demonstrated outstanding metrics like accuracy, sensitivity and specificity. The MATLAB simulated results show that the proposed method works well and is reliable for predicting exons.

Introduction

The human genome contains biological information in the form of DNA sequences which act as a blueprint for cell activity and protein creation. DNA consists of four nitrogenous bases-adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C) and thymine (T)1. These bases pair are complementary paired i.e. A with T, C with G and vice versa to produce the well-known double helix shape. The genetic code encoded in DNA sequences directs the development of proteins that are required for almost all biological functions2. The coding instructions are stored in genes, which are unique sections of DNA. Each gene is further divided into exons which contain significant coding information and introns which are non-coding portions.

DNA is first translated into RNA during protein synthesis and RNA is subsequently processed to remove introns, leaving only exons. The messenger RNA (mRNA) which is created by splicing these exons together contains codons which are triplets of nucleotide bases that determine the final protein’s amino acid sequence. The transformation of DNA information into useful proteins is a very regular and well-organised process. But since this activity is limited to specific regions of the genome called exons3, accurate identification is essential for comprehending gene function and biological development.

Gene-finding methods can be classified into two main groups: model-dependent (non-DSP) and model-independent (DSP-based) frameworks4. Model-dependent approaches predominantly depend on biological, statistical, or machine learning concepts. Early gene finders such as GENSCAN5, GeneMark6,7, and AUGUSTUS8,9 employed Hidden Markov Models (HMMs)10 and probabilistic models11,12 to learn the statistical characteristics of coding regions from annotated datasets. These methods work well for making predictions, but they need large, high-quality labelled datasets to work well, and they may not work well for species that haven’t been studied yet or for noisy genomic sequences.

Model-independent (DSP-based) approaches, on the other hand, DNA sequences are converted as discrete numerical signals and analyze their frequency-domain or spectrum properties. In these techniques, the first step is numerical mapping method, where symbolic nucleotides (A, T, G, C) are converted into numerical representations such as Voss13, Electron–Ion Interaction Potential (EIIP)14, Integer15, Two-bit Binary Encoding (TBNE)16, Four-bit Binary Encoding (FBNE)17, Walsh based Numerical Encoding (WBNE)18, Gray-code19, and Hadamard based Numerical Encoding (HBNE)20. After mapping, various digital signal processing (DSP) tools are used to find biologically important periodic components directly from raw sequences. They take advantage of the well-known three-base periodicity (TBP) feature of protein-coding regions, which originates from the triplet codon structure used during translation. This characteristic creates a unique spectral peak at frequency 1/3 (or k = N/3), which is a strong indicator of exonic regions.

Nucleotides in protein-coding areas display the period-3 feature due to codon bias linked to the translation process. However, genomic sequences also exhibit long-range correlations (1/f spectra), which act as background noise and make exon identification more difficult. Nevertheless, the period-3 feature remains one of the most effective indicators for detecting coding regions. Numerous studies have utilized Fourier spectral analysis21,22, correlation-based methodologies13,23, and parametric spectral estimation based on autoregressive (AR) modeling24,25 and digital filters26–28 for exon prediction. These approaches are mostly unsupervised and do not require any biological annotation beforehand, making them faster and more robust to dataset biases.

Non-DSP (model-dependent) methods depend on learned biological patterns, while DSP-based (model-independent) methods exploit the intrinsic biophysical periodicities inherently present in DNA sequences. As a result, signal processing-based frameworks have gained considerable attention in recent years for their ability to uncover hidden periodicities and structural characteristics. Nonetheless, it is observed that short exons may not exhibit a distinct period-3 signature, rendering these approaches more suitable for longer coding regions.

A few DSP tools are discussed here. Among the earliest and most influential methods is the Short-Time Discrete Fourier Transform (ST-DFT) introduced by Tiwari et al.21 which analyzes DNA sequences using a sliding window approach to detect the three-base periodicity indicative of exons. To improve identification accuracy, adaptive window method has been developed because ST-DFT’s fixed window length frequently restricts its adaptability. It was suggested by Shakya et al.29 to dynamically change the window size, however this approach still uses a fixed reference length to identify the best threshold differences.

The Modified Gabor Wavelet Transform (MGWT)30,31 offers multiscale analysis without depending on a fixed window length. However, its performance is sensitive to the selection of wavelet scale and bandwidth. Similar properties are found in the S-transform32, which offers better adaptability through frequency-dependent resolution and phase preservation. However, its accuracy depends on careful tuning of the Gaussian window scaling parameter and the design of the time–frequency mask to balance resolution and extract the period-3 component effectively. Furthermore, Kotlar and Lavner’s investigation of spectrum rotation improves the phase angle distribution in Fourier analysis, enhancing the 1/3 frequency component that is essential for detecting short exons33. By representing the DNA sequence as a stochastic process, autoregressive models and parametric spectral estimation techniques presented by Chakravarthy and Akhtar24,25 provide an alternate statistical strategy for enhancing spectral resolution and boundary identification.

To separate the distinctive three-base periodicity signal from genomic noise, a few filtering strategies have also been developed concurrently. In order to extract the 1/3 frequency component linked to coding regions, Vaidyanathan and Yoon34 presented early filter-based techniques such as anti-notch filters (ANF), real narrow-band bandpass filters (RNBPF) and complicated narrow-band filters. These filters sensitivity to background noise hampered their performance, despite their early promise. In order to solve this, Hota and Srivastava proposed improvements such as the Conjugate Suppression ANF (CSANF) and the Complex Narrow-Band Bandpass Filter (CNBPF)35, which increased noise suppression and detection accuracy. Subsequently, the Savitzky–Golay (SG) filter36 and other signal smoothing and augmentation techniques were employed to improve exon peak detectability and localisation accuracy.

Numerous decomposition strategies have been investigated in the field of DNA exon prediction in order to extract significant periodic signals from complex genomic data. Zhang and Yan first presented Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD), one of the oldest and most popular methods37. During the process, a DNA sequence is divided into several IMFs. Local spectral characteristics that signify exons are first identified using IMF. Nevertheless, EMD have a drawback, such as mode mixing, residual noise, and subpar performance in noisy environments.

EMD as a filter bank38 and EMD with MGWT39 were later introduced and they both increase the robustness of decomposition. Ensemble Empirical Mode Decomposition (EEMD)40 was created to address these issues. It involves adding white noise to the signal and averaging several decompositions to lessen mode mixing and boundary artefacts. This was expanded upon by the introduction of a more sophisticated method called Complementary Ensemble Empirical Mode Decomposition (CEEMD). By averaging complementary noise-assisted ensembles, CEEMD further improves the quality of decomposition, substantially decreasing mode mixing and facilitating the extraction of biologically significant IMFs.

The novelty of our paper lies in the development of a Hadamard–STDFT–CEEMD–WPT pipeline for exon prediction as it mainly addresses the limitations of conventional EMD/EEMD-based techniques. In this pipeline, HBNE provides an efficient and noise-tolerant mapping of DNA sequences. The integration of STDFT with CEEMD enables localized frequency analysis combined with adaptive decomposition to capture both spectral and temporal exon features. Additionally, a self-correlation-driven approach is used to eliminate noisy or duplicated components by choosing only biologically relevant IMFs. Ultimately, WPT improves the denoising procedure by successfully decreasing intronic noise while maintaining exon-specific periodicity. When combined, these advancements produce a strong and innovative framework that outperforms conventional DSP and decomposition techniques, providing more accurate exon identification and enhanced classification capabilities.

In this work, exon prediction performance is assessed and validated using a standard benchmark dataset and two well-characterized as well as established gene sequences. Multiple coding exons are present in the Caenorhabditis elegans F56F11.4 gene (GenBank: FO081497), which is a standard model for C. elegans gene structure study. The Mus musculus PSMB5 gene serves as a model for mammalian coding areas and encodes a proteasome subunit necessary for protein breakdown. Additionally, the accuracy and generalizability of the proposed method across different eukaryotic genomes are assessed using the HMR195 dataset, which comprises 195 genomic sequences from humans, mice and rats.

The resources, the process and the suggested method for exon identification are all covered in Sect. “Materials and methods”. The simulated results are shown in Sect. “Results and discussion” together with a thorough analysis. Section 4 concludes the work with potential avenues for further scope of research.

The HMR195 dataset43 comprises 195 genomic sequences from human, mouse, and rat species, with a sequence distribution of 103 human, 82 mouse, and 10 rat sequences. The mean length of these sequences is approximately 7,096 base pairs (bp). The dataset includes both single-exon and multi-exon genes, with 43 single-exon genes and 152 multi-exon genes. Numerical mapping method (HBNE)

DNA sequences can be transformed into numerical form by using Hadamard mapping20. The Hadamard mapping is preferred as it enables further use of sophisticated signal processing algorithms. This mapping employs a Hadamard matrix and it is a unique type of square matrix with only + 1 and − 1 values. The orthogonal feature of this matrix performs unique encoding of DNA patterns making them completely different from each other.

This work uses a Hadamard matrix of 4 × 4 dimension. Each of the four DNA bases—adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), and thymine (T) is associated with a particular row in this matrix. Where, A is represented as [1, 1, 1, 1], G as [1, − 1, 1, − 1], T as [1, 1, − 1, − 1], and C as [1, − 1, − 1, 1]. As a result, the DNA sequence is transformed into a string of four vectors representing different nucleotides. All of the DNA sequence information is still included in these vectors, which are now in a format more suited for signal analysis.

Short time discrete fourier transform (STDFT)

STDFT is a time–frequency analysis method21 used to investigate the evolution of frequency content of a signal across time. The general Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) analyse tiny portions (or windows) of the signal separately, whereas STDFT provides frequency information for the full signal at a stretch.

Typically DNA sequences are non-stationary signals and contain localized features like exons. The STDFT approach is highly helpful in these scenarios. In this process, DFT is applied to each frame after dividing the signal into overlapping frames using a window function. Decomposition techniques

Decomposition techniques divide complex signals into simpler components for further analysis. In genomic digital signal processing, these techniques help in the extraction of hidden patterns in DNA sequences mainly the period-3 property present in coding regions (exons). After the conversion of DNA sequences into numerical signals using Hadamard mapping, STDFT21, decomposition techniques such as EMD37, EEMD40 and CEEMD can be applied. Decomposition techniques are very much useful for identifying components in different frequency ranges and separating noise from useful biological signals. These techniques enhance the accuracy of feature extraction process in exon prediction.

Empirical mode decomposition (EMD)

Empirical mode decomposition (EMD) is a fully data driven decomposition. EMD breaks down a signal into simpler intrinsic mode functions (IMFs)37. Each IMF represents a basic oscillation with varying frequency and amplitude. The EMD algorithm works by identifying local maxima and minima of the signal, creating upper and lower envelopes using interpolation, finding their mean and subtracting it from the original signal. This process is repeated (shifting) until the result meets IMF conditions.

The method then proceeds with the residual after the initial IMF is eliminated from the signal. CEEMD further enhances EEMD by eliminating the residual noise left after ensemble averaging. In CEEMD, both positive and negative white noises are added in pairs to the signal45, so their effects cancel each other. EMD is applied to each of these noise-added signals, and the IMFs are averaged pairwise. This ensures better consistency and cleaner decomposition. Wavelet Packet Thresholding (WPT)

The wavelet packet thresholding (WPT) plays an important role in denoising performance. It also influences the threshold selection46. Soft- and hard-thresholding methods possess effective noise suppression capability. Even though these methods are typically used in practical scenarios, the fixed threshold leads to several limitations.

Hard thresholding is most commonly employed as it preserves the signal amplitude efficiently. Nevertheless, the reconstructed signal is discontinuous at the threshold point, which may lead to oscillations and the appearance of a pseudo-Gibbs phenomenon. Soft thresholding alleviates the discontinuity issue but does not retain the amplitude preservation property of hard thresholding. As a result, a constant deviation exists between the estimated wavelet coefficients and the original coefficients. Consequently, both hard and soft thresholding methods may introduce discontinuities and signal distortion.

Discussion

Through the combination of many signal processing techniques, the proposed method improves the identification of exon regions in DNA sequences. The first step is to translate the DNA sequence into numerical form using the Hadamard mapping technique. Each of the DNA bases (A, C, G, and T) is represented by a different orthogonal vector in this technique, which uses a 4 × 4 Hadamard matrix. While preserving all the DNA information, this modification ensures that the resulting signal is appropriate for spectrum analysis. The Hadamard method captures more structural information and minimises noise interference. Following Hadamard mapping, the signal’s local frequency information is extracted using the Short-Time Discrete Fourier Transform (STDFT). The triplet codon structure in protein-coding areas is known to give exons a period-3 feature, and STDFT successfully captures this periodicity. Low-frequency 1/f noise, which can obscure the exon peaks and lower prediction accuracy, is frequently present in the raw STDFT data.

Three decomposition techniques EMD, EEMD, and CEEMD were investigated in order to overcome this. By extracting IMFs, EMD eliminates noise; nonetheless, mode mixing, in which several frequency components overlap, might be a problem. By averaging the decomposition outcomes of several noisy signals, although EEMD improves performance, considerable noise still exists. The suggested approach offers a notable improvement by combining STDFT with CEEMD and WPT. By including pairings of positive and negative white noise, which cancel each other out and lessen the remaining noise in the signal, CEEMD provides more accurate decomposition. A self-correlation coefficient is used to identify which IMFs are noise dominant. These noisy IMFs are denoised using WPT, a powerful method that removes both low- and high-frequency noise components. The cleaned IMFs are then reconstructed to obtain a final signal with stronger exon peaks and suppressed intronic noise.

The results from both datasets F56F11.4 and PSMB5, the CEEMD based output spectrum showed much precise localization of exon regions compared to other methods. The performance metrics shown in Tables 3 and 5, and the comparison plots in Figs. 6 and 8 confirm that the proposed method achieves higher sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F1-score, GM, MCC, and AUC than the other techniques. This proves that Hadamard mapping when combined with STDFT, CEEMD, and WPT gives a highly effective approach for exon prediction in genomic sequences.

Conclusion

In this paper, by combining Hadamard based numerical mapping, STDFT, CEEMD and WPT a robust exon prediction approach was presented. An orthogonal and noise-resistant numerical representation of DNA sequences was made possible by Hadamard mapping, which improved STDFT’s capacity to identify the period-3 signal linked to protein-coding regions. By applying CEEMD, the signal was adaptively decomposed into intrinsic mode functions (IMFs), and a self-correlation-based criterion was used to identify noisy IMFs, which were subsequently denoised using WPT. The output spectrum exhibited clearer exon peaks and reduced background noise leading to improved exon–intron discrimination. In comparison to existing methods such as STDFT alone, STDFT + EMD, and STDFT + EEMD, performance evaluations on the F56F11.4 and PSMB5 datasets shown continuous gains in sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, AUC, and other metrics, confirming the superiority of the suggested strategy. However, the proposed method has certain limitations. The MATLAB-based implementation requires relatively high computational time for long genomic sequences due to multiple decomposition stages. Moreover, the performance is sensitive to parameter choices (e.g., window length in STDFT) may need dataset-specific fine-tuning. In the future, this approach can be enhanced by incorporating deep learning models, such as CNNs, to further improve classification accuracy and robustness and the current work has limitation that it only evaluates benchmark genomic datasets of mostly vertebrate origin. While these datasets are widely used in the literature, future research will focus on validating the proposed approach on whole genomes from various organisms. Furthermore, future implementations will attempt to provide a standalone command-line interface capable of processing conventional FASTA files, improving usability and reproducibility.
