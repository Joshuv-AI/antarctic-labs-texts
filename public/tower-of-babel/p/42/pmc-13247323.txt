Success Rate of Partial Two-Stage Exchange Arthroplasty for Late Chronic Periprosthetic Joint Infections in Total Hip Arthroplasty: A Meta-Analysis
Authors: Byung-Ho Yoon, Suyeon Park, Eunseo Joung, Naye Kang, Nah Yon Kim, Ki Tae Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePeriprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) resulting from total hip arthroplasty (THA) pose significant challenges. Partial two-stage exchange arthroplasty (TSEA) retains either the femoral stem or acetabular cup by preserving bone stock and soft tissue and offers a potential alternative to total TSEA. Our meta-analysis evaluated the pooled success rate of partial TSEA in treating late chronic PJIs in THA. Our study also focused on the outcomes associated with retained prosthetic implant (stem or cup) and the type of prior surgery (primary vs. revision arthroplasty) performed.Materials and MethodsWe analyzed data from 355 hips of patients with PJI following THA across 11 studies using a random-effects model to estimate the success rate of partial TSEA and spacer dislocation that occurs during the interval between the first and second stages of partial TSEA. The heterogeneity between the subgroups was also calculated.ResultsThe overall pooled success rate of partial TSEA in the study cohort was 87% (95% confidence interval [CI] 83-91). Lacking statistical significance (P=0.904), the success rates were 88% for stem-retained and 90% for cup-retained implant cases. The partial TSEA success rates were 88% for primary arthroplasty and 80% for revision arthroplasty, although this was not statistically significant (P=0.193). Spacer dislocation occurred in only 5% of the cases (95% CI 1-9).ConclusionPartial TSEA achieves high success rates comparable to those of total TSEA. Although revision arthroplasty cases carry a high risk of recurrence, partial TSEA remains a viable, tailored treatment option with a low complication rate.Keywords: Periprosthetic joint infection, Partial two-stage exchange arthroplasty, Revision arthroplasty, Meta-analysis

INTRODUCTION

Infection that develops following total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a disastrous complication that poses significant challenges to both patients and clinicians1,2). This is particularly true in cases of treatment of late chronic periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) following the initial THA3,4). Although total two-stage exchange arthroplasty (TSEA) is widely regarded as the gold standard to manage late PJIs, variations, such as the partial TSEA have emerged as viable alternatives5-7). In partial TSEA, select prosthetic components, rather than the entire prosthesis, are removed and subsequently re-implanted with an aim to balance both eradicating infection as well as preserving bone stock and soft tissue8,9).

TSEA selectively retains either the femoral stem or the acetabular cup, depending on the specific characteristics of the infection and the implant10,11). Despite its growing application in clinical practice, there is limited information, in particular, on its overall success rate to eradicate infection and other complications12). It is pivotal to address these limitations to optimize treatment strategies, yet existing studies often fail to differentiate outcomes based on the retained component or the type of index arthroplasty (primary vs. revision)13,14). It is also essential to understand these subcategories separately to tailor interventions to individual patient profiles, including differences in the quality of soft tissues, bone stock, and maximizing treatment efficacy15,16).

Therefore, our meta-analysis aims to evaluate the pooled success rate of partial TSEA to treat late chronic PJI-infected THA. We specifically sought to determine the overall success rate and to compare the outcomes based on the retained prosthetic site (cup or stem) and the type of prior arthroplasty. By exploring these subcategories, our study aims to refine clinical decision-making and promote tailored treatment strategies to manage late chronic PJIs in THA patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Search Strategies to Select Studies

Spanning the period from inception to November 2024, we conducted a comprehensive search of electronic databases to identify studies to compare the outcomes of stem- or cup-retaining implants as well as primary and revision arthroplasty on the success rates of partial TSEA in treating infected hips. The search was conducted following the updated guidelines of the PRISMA-P (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses-Protocols) 2020 statement (Supplementary Table 1). To ensure a broad and inclusive review of relevant literature, the search encompassed multiple databases, including MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, Cochrane Library. A detailed overview of the search strategy on each website has been provided in Supplementary Material 1.

2. Criteria for Selection of Studies

All the publications were classified using Endnote X20 for Windows (Clarivate). Two reviewers (B.H.Y. and K.T.P.) autonomously reviewed all the relevant titles and abstracts of studies and subsequently selected studies based on a full-text review. A third reviewer (N.Y.K.) resolved discrepancies in the publications reviewed by the other two reviewers. Inclusion criteria for the studies were as follows: (1) an original article published in English, (2) either a comparative analysis or a single-cohort investigation, which evaluated the outcomes of partial TSEA in treating late chronic PJI following THA, (3) reports of the primary clinical outcome (infection-free survival), and (4) a minimum follow-up of 1 year.

Exclusion criteria included the following: (1) PJI following knee arthroplasty, (2) inclusion of single-stage exchange arthroplasty, (3) non-partial retaining stem or cup [total removal], (4) very small sample size (<5 patients), and (5) use of national registry data, a review, expert opinion, case report, animal study, or basic science study.

3. Outcome Measures and Data Extraction

The main focus of our meta-analysis was infection-free survival (success rate of eradicating infection). We identified infection recurrence when there were additional surgical interventions (total TSEA, girdlestone surgery), or other revision surgery-related infection following second-stage revision THA reimplantation. We also investigated spacer dislocation that occurred during the interval between the first and second stages of partial TSEA.

For each eligible study, the two reviewers extracted and recorded the following data in a spreadsheet: the last name of the first author, publication year, enrollment period, the number of patients with infected hips, mean age at surgery, type of index surgery (primary THA, bipolar hemiarthroplasty, revision hip arthroplasty), retained implant during TSEA (stem or cup), the type of antibiotic-impregnated cement spacer (AICS), dislocation of spacer, and mean years following the index surgery.

4. Quality Assessment and Publication Bias

Two authors (B.H.Y. and K.T.P.) separately evaluated the study quality using the Newcastle–Ottawa scales designed for observational studies. This tool includes three key parameters: selection, comparability, and outcome, each with subcategories. The methodological quality of the included observational studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale, which assesses three domains: selection (maximum of 4 stars), comparability (maximum of 2 stars), and outcome (maximum of 3 stars). Consistent with previous systematic reviews, studies that scored fewer than 5 stars were regarded as poor quality, those scoring 6-7 stars were considered moderate to good quality, and studies with 8 or more stars were considered high quality. In our analysis, all included studies achieved either 6 or 7 stars, thereby indicating that the overall methodological quality was moderate to good17-19). We examined publication bias using Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test.

5. Statistical Analysis (Subgroup Analysis and Sensitivity Analysis)

The main analysis consisted of a proportion meta-analysis that utilized data from all pertinent studies that reported the occurrence of re-infection. Meta-analysis of proportions serves as a method to compute a comprehensive proportion from studies that report a single proportion20,21).

In our investigation, the proportion (p) was determined by the ratio of the number of patients with treated hips that do not develop recurrent infection to the total number of patients with treated hips that present with chronic PJI (n). Standard errors and confidence intervals for a single proportion were computed, and adjusted proportions were subsequently calculated through a logit transformation, as outlined below22,23).

6. Subgroup Analysis

All patients with partial TSEA-treated hips presenting with chronic PJI were then subclassified into two groups based on implant retention (stem retaining and cup retaining). The heterogeneity between these groups was then computed. All patients with partial TSEA-treated hips presenting with chronic PJI were also subclassified based on the index surgery: primary arthroplasty and revision arthroplasty.

7. Sensitivity Analysis

The AICS can be classified as either non-articulating or articulating2). Articulating AICSs may improve hip function during the AICS stage, make the revision THA reimplantation technically easy, and allow for rapid rehabilitation that could be an important covariant in clinical outcomes2,24).

So, we tried to perform a sensitivity analysis by analyzing studies according to the type of cement-impregnated spacer used. However, it was not possible to perform subgroup analysis because articulating AICSs were used in most studies.

Stata software was used for the statistical analyses (ver. 14.0; Stata Corp.). Since our study used published data, it did not require ethical approval.

RESULTS

1. Description of the Included Studies

Using our inclusion and exclusion criteria, the primary database search yielded 4,729 records. After duplicates were removed, 2,030 articles were screened by title and abstract. As a result, we selected and reviewed 114 full-text articles for eligibility. A total of 11 studies were finally included in the systematic review (Fig. 1)2,11,25-33). Among 11 studies, 5 cohort articles included those that retained implants during partial TSEA (stem-retaining and cup-retaining)11,25-28) and 8 cohort studies were classified on the basis of the index surgery that was either primary or revision surgery (Table 1)11,26,28-33).

2. Overall Success Rate and Spacer Dislocation Rate

In the proportion meta-analysis of the 11 studies, the pooled success rate was 87% (95% CI 83-91) from all studies (Fig. 2A). Spacer dislocation was evaluated in all the 5 studies included in this review.

Proportion meta-analysis used to calculate the overall pooled rates. (A) Overall success rates. (B) Spacer dislocation. ES: effect size, CI: confidence interval.

In the proportion meta-analysis, the pooled rate of spacer dislocation was 5% (95% CI 1-9). (Fig. 2B).

3. Overall Success Rate Based on the Retained Implant

The cohorts in five of the studies included in this review were categorized into subgroups based on the types of retained implants. In the proportion meta-analysis, the success rate of overall infection-free survival due to partial TSEA was 88% (95% CI 84-93) in patients with stem-retained and 90% (95% CI 46-99) in patients with cup-retained implants (Fig. 3). There was no statistically significant difference in the success rate between these two groups (P=0.904).

Proportion meta-analysis of the success rate in the subgroup analysis based on the retained implant. ES: effect size, CI: confidence interval.

4. Overall Success Rate Based on Primary or Revision Surgery

The cohorts in eight of the studies included in this review were subclassified based on the type of prior surgeries (primary or revision surgery). In the proportion meta-analysis, the success rate of survival of partial TSEA was 88% (95% CI 81-95) in treating PJI in patients following primary surgery and 80% (95% CI 69-90) in treating PJI in patients following revision surgery (Fig. 4). There was no statistically significant difference in the success rate between these two groups (P=0.193).

Proportion meta-analysis of the success rate in the subgroup analysis based on the type of primary or revision arthroplasty. ES: effect size, CI: confidence interval.

5. Quality Assessment and Publication Bias

After evaluating the methodological quality, the mean value of awarded stars was 6.5 (6 stars in 5 studies, and 7 stars in 6 studies) (Supplementary Table 2). The Begg’s funnel plot was symmetrical, and the P-values for bias were not significant for all the outcomes (Fig. 5).

DISCUSSION

This is the first study to estimate the pooled success rate of partial TSEA to treat late chronic of PJIs following THA and to analyze outcomes based on the retained prosthetic component (cup or stem), providing essential insights for future clinical practice. The high success rate indicates that partial TSEA is effective to eradicate infections while preserving critical bone stock and maintaining soft tissue integrity

The pooled success rate of 87% for partial TSEA compares favorably to the reported success rates for traditional total TSEA, which typically range from 85% to 95% in published studies34-37). Partial TSEA involves retaining one prosthetic component (either the stem or cup) during the treatment of late chronic PJIs27,32). However, this approach may not be applicable to all patients, particularly those with extensive infection or compromised implants that require complete removal29,30,38).

Therefore, it is essential for clinicians to ensure that the retained implant demonstrates sufficient mechanical stability, shows no evidence of loosening, and is not directly involved in infection or associated with significant biofilm formation29,30).

Our analysis revealed there was no significant difference in the success rates of partial TSEA on infection-free survival between stem-retained (88%) and cup-retained (90%) cohorts (P=0.904). Our finding suggests that both approaches can be similarly effective and can allow surgical decisions to be guided by patient-specific factors, such as bone quality, implant condition, and the localization of infection28,29,31,39,40). The absence of a statistically significant difference also underscores the importance of meticulous surgical techniques and infection management protocols, irrespective of the retained component38,40,41).

The success rates of partial TSEA in treating late chronic PJIs following THA were slightly higher in patients with infections following primary arthroplasty (88%) than in those following revision arthroplasty (80%). Although the difference was not statistically significant (P=0.193), this trend underscores the inherent challenges of managing PJIs after revision arthroplasty and highlights scenarios where total TSEA can be a broad and safe option for PJI following revision hip surgery25,26,42). Patients with PJIs following revision arthroplasty should be informed of the high risks of recurrence of infection and the potential need for TSEA43-45).

Articulating spacers have gained attention for their potential to provide superior functional outcomes when compared to those provided by static spacers during the interim phase of TSEA2). However, this benefit comes with a trade-off—high rates of spacer dislocation26,46). Based on our analysis, the dislocation rate of articulating spacers was relatively low at only 5% (95% CI 1-9) when partial TSEA was performed. Our finding suggests that articulating spacers may be a reliable and effective choice during the interim phase of TSEA to treat PJI. The inability to perform a subgroup analysis based on articulating versus non-articulating AICS represents a limitation of our current study. Given the potential benefits of articulating spacers to facilitate rehabilitation and to simplify reimplantation, further investigations are warranted to clarify their impact on clinical outcomes27,47).

Our study has limitations. First, we need to consider other potential factors that could not be included in our analysis, such as the type of infecting organism, the comorbidities of patients, the use of cement, and the type of inserted implants. Second, there are inherent limitations by relying on observational studies, including potential confounding variables as well as heterogeneity in surgical protocols and patient populations.

CONCLUSION

Partial TSEA offers a promising approach with high success and low complication rates to treat late chronic PJIs following THA. By understanding the nuances of implant retention and prior surgical history, clinicians can make informed decisions to optimize patient outcomes.
