ViMST: vision transformer-based dual modality multi-task graph contrastive network for spatial transcriptomics microenvironments investigation
Authors: Cheng Ding, Qiaoming Liu, Yuming Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInvestigating spatial transcriptomics microenvironments is crucial for unraveling cellular heterogeneity. Existing methods struggle to extract non-redundant information from histopathological images, as well as to simultaneously and spatially resolve gene expression profiles. We propose a vision transformer-based dual-modality multi-task graph contrastive network for exploring the spatial transcriptomics domain (ViMST), which integrates gene expression, image features, and spatial coordinates to investigate tissue microenvironments. It employs Vision Transformer (ViT) for feature extraction and dual masked Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) to model modalities separately. A novel joint topology decoder learns the spatial covariation between morphology and expression, thereby enhancing relationship modeling across multiple tasks.ResultsThe evaluation results across nine spatial transcriptomics datasets reveal that ViMST consistently outperforms eight state-of-the-art methods in spatial domain identification and data denoising. Its robust performance across multiple datasets and tasks highlights its broad applicability and practical value in deciphering tissue spatial organization. Background

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) is an emerging genomics technology that enables the spatially resolved analysis of gene expression patterns within tissues, providing deeper insights into the structure and function of biological tissues [1]. Researchers can achieve more refined spatially hierarchical analyses by integrating spatial location information with gene expression profiles [2]. A variety of spatial transcriptomics technologies have been developed, including RNA sequencing-based methods such as 10 × Visium [3], Slide-seq [4], and Stereo-seq [5], which support genome-wide analyses across tissue locations. Meanwhile, single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH)-based techniques, such as MERFISH [6, 7], seqFISH [8, 9], and seqFISH + [10], provide single-cell resolution data, effectively bridging gene expression and spatial location information. The development of these technologies has enabled researchers to explore spatial gene expression patterns within tissues [11, 12], uncover intercellular interactions [13], and drive significant advances across multiple biological fields [14, 15].

Spatial heterogeneity in complex tissue structures is often characterized by distinct gene expression patterns and tissue morphologies in different regions, referred to as spatial domains. Spatial domains typically exhibit unique cellular compositions, transcriptomic features, and intercellular communication patterns, serving as a critical foundation for tissue functionality and regulatory mechanisms [16–18]. Therefore, identifying spatial domains within tissues is essential for understanding their physiological and pathological states.

Existing methods for spatial domain identification can be broadly categorized into two groups: traditional clustering methods and deep learning-based approaches. The former includes classical clustering algorithms such as K-means and Leiden [19], which typically perform clustering after dimensionality reduction using methods like principal component analysis (PCA), t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) [20], or Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) [21]. However, these methods rely solely on gene expression data and fail to integrate tissue imaging and spatial location information, making it challenging to ensure spatial continuity in the identification results. To address this, some scholars have focused on modeling valuable spatial coordinate information. For example, BayesSpace [22] enhances spatial consistency in clustering by incorporating spatial priors; Giotto [23] models spatial dependencies using Hidden Markov Random Fields (HMRF); stLearn [24] improves clustering accuracy by combining spatial location and smooth embedding strategies; and BASS [25] employs a Bayesian hierarchical model to achieve multi-scale and multi-sample clustering.

In recent years, Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs) have shown great potential for processing unstructured data and modeling complex relationships, leading to the development of advanced methods for spatial domain identification. CCST [26] utilizes unsupervised GCN to extract information from graph structures to learn about cell embeddings; SpaGCN [27] combines histological images to build three-dimensional spatial graphs enhancing model perception; STAGATE [28] introduces a graph attention mechanism to merge expression and spatial relationships; SEDR [29] uses a masked variational graph autoencoder for modeling spatial neighborhood structures; DeepST [30] employs tissue imagery to enhance spatial domain recognition; GraphST [31] applies graph contrastive learning to improve structural modeling capabilities; STGMVA [32] employs a Gaussian mixture variational autoencoder for clustering spatial structures; STMGCN [33] constructs multi-type adjacency matrices with attention mechanisms for spatial graph learning; conST [34] uses multi-level contrastive learning for integrating gene expression, spatial information, and images; Stardust [35] is a space-aware modularity optimization-based clustering method that balances spatial proximity and transcriptomic similarity through a tunable weight; STMIGCL [36] introduces a graph contrastive learning framework for jointly modeling spatial structures and multimodal information, enhancing clustering performance through cross-view interactions; ConGI [37] aligns gene expression with histopathological images via intra- and inter-modality contrastive losses for spatial domain identification. Masked graph autoencoders have recently emerged as a promising paradigm for spatial transcriptomics analysis. SpaMask [38] employs dual‑masking of node features and edges with contrastive learning to improve clustering robustness, while STMGAC [39] uses a masked graph autoencoder with contrastive augmentation and self‑distillation. These designs have inspired our work, and our model partly draws on their underlying concepts.

Despite the progress these methods have made in spatial domain identification, they still face significant challenges. Firstly, most of these methods do not fully exploit the rich spatial structural information in histological images, limiting spatial structure recognition accuracy [24]. Second, although some multimodal approaches improve the alignment of cross-modal representations, they typically lack explicit modeling of spatial topology under cross-modal fusion, restricting their capacity to dynamically refine spatial relationships. Third, masking-based methods enhance robustness but are generally confined to unimodal settings and do not account for multimodal interactions.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose ViMST, a dual-modality graph neural network designed to jointly model tissue microenvironments and learn deep representations by integrating gene expression data, spatial coordinate information, and image-derived features. The novelties of our methods are summarized as follows:

(1) ViMST utilizes a Vision Transformer (ViT) to extract morphological features from images, constructing a cross-modality mapping between images and expression profiles at the cellular level. This approach mitigates information loss due to sequencing sparsity in ST data and improves the accuracy of expression profile reconstruction.

Results

Overview of ViMST

This study proposes ViMST, a denoising and clustering model for spatial transcriptomics (ST) data. ViMST integrates gene expression data and image-derived features to jointly model and learn deep representations of spatial microenvironments as a multimodal graph neural network tailored for spatial transcriptomics. The model innovatively establishes a gene-image dual-modality masked graph autoencoder architecture, coupled with a dynamic topology preservation mechanism, to accomplish four major tasks within a unified framework: denoising gene expression, reconstructing image features, restoring spatial relationships, and identifying the spatial domain.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the overall workflow of ViMST consists of two main stages: Data preprocessing and augmentation, and Dual Modality Graph Autoencoder Learning, which are depicted as follows: (i) In the data preprocessing and augmentation stage (Fig. 1A), ViMST divides the histological image into multiple local patches based on the coordinates of ST spots, ensuring that each image patch accurately corresponds to a specific ST spot. These image patches are then processed with a Vision Transformer (ViT) to extract localized morphological features. (ii) During training, we minimize the prediction error between the reconstructed and the original gene expression profiles, guiding the ViT to effectively capture the underlying associations between image morphology and gene expression. In this way, we derive image-derived features that share the same dimensionality as the reduced gene expression features (Fig. 1A(a)). (iii) For gene expression profiles, we perform dimensionality reduction on gene expression features by selecting highly variable genes and constructing the spatial graph structure using the K-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm (Fig. 1A(b)). Ultimately, we obtain three types of features for each spot: gene expression features, image features, and spatial location information. To enhance model robustness, we further introduce data augmentation by randomly masking elements in both the image feature and gene expression matrices, and perturbing the spatial adjacency structure to create complete and incomplete input sets for training (Fig. 1A(c)).

(b) ViMST performs feature dimensionality reduction and constructs the spatial graph structure by selecting highly variable genes and applying the KNN algorithm. (c) ViMST applies random masking to both the image-derived feature matrix and the gene expression matrix and perturbs the spatial topological structure. Node masking is applied to infer missing image-derived features from spatial neighbors. (b) Edge masking is used to construct a contrastive loss framework that captures spatial covariation patterns between gene and image latent features through their fusion. (c) Node masking is similarly applied to infer missing gene expression features from spatial neighbors. C The learned latent representations are then used for downstream tasks, including spatial clustering, gene expression denoising, multiple ST data integration, EMT score estimation, and other analyses

The core component of ViMST is a dual-modality parallel graph autoencoder architecture, corresponding to the dual modality graph autoencoder learning stage depicted in Fig. 1B. The proposed framework comprises three main modules: a graph autoencoder-decoder channel for histological feature enhancement, a graph autoencoder-decoder channel for gene feature enhancement, and a joint topological graph autoencoder-decoder channel for spatial structure enhancement. In the histological feature enhancement channel, the model receives as input Gmask-y, which consists of masked image-derived features combined with the complete spatial graph structure. Through model training, this channel outputs the enhanced image-derived representation for gene expression prediction, denoted as Zy. By reconstructing image features via the encoder–decoder process, this pathway reinforces the ability of image features to complement missing gene expression information (Fig. 1B(a)). In the gene feature enhancement channel, the input is Gmask-x, comprising masked gene expression features and the complete spatial graph. After encoding and decoding, this channel generates enhanced gene representations Zx (Fig. 1B(c)). In the joint topological channel, the model processes two types of perturbed input graphs: Glose-y, which combines raw image-derived features with a corrupted spatial graph structure, and Glose-x, which combines the whole gene expression matrix with a corrupted spatial graph structure. The model encodes these two inputs separately and then fuses the resulting embeddings using a weighted sum. The fused embeddings are regarded as edge features for contrastive learning between positive and negative edges. The model captures the authentic topological relationships among spatial spots more accurately by maximizing the difference between positive and negative edge representations. This channel jointly models gene and image latent features to learn their spatial covariation patterns, thereby improving the model’s understanding of the tissue’s spatial organization and increasing its robustness to spatial perturbations (Fig. 1B(b)). Finally, we integrate the image reconstruction loss, gene reconstruction loss, and spatial topology contrastive loss into a single weighted objective function. By minimizing the overall loss, the model is guided to jointly optimize morphological, molecular, and spatial information, ultimately improving multimodal spatial transcriptomics feature representation and structural inference capabilities.

ViMST can accurately identify spatial heterogeneity in the DLPFC dataset

To evaluate the spatial clustering performance of ViMST, we conducted a systematic validation using the 10 × Visium dataset of the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) [40]. This dataset consists of 12 brain slice samples that have been meticulously annotated, with each sample divided into L1–L6 cortical layers and white matter regions based on morphological features and genetic markers. To objectively assess clustering performance, we conducted a comparative analysis against twelve state-of-the-art algorithms (Leiden [19], SpaGCN [27], DeepST [30], CCST [26], STAGATE [28], SEDR [29], GraphST [31], STGMVA [32], ConGI [37], STMIGCL [36], STMGAC [39], and SpaMask [38]) using five evaluation metrics: Adjusted Rand Index (ARI), Normalized Mutual Information (NMI), Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI), Homogeneity Score (HS), and Purity. Detailed parameter configurations and evaluation metric descriptions for all methods are presented in Additional file 1 and Additional file 2, respectively. Boxplots and scatter plots were used to illustrate the overall ARI scores for different methods across the 12 tissue slices, with the central line and white diamonds representing the median and mean ARI values, respectively. The detailed performance of all methods on the 12 slices is shown in Additional file 3: Fig. S1-S6. As shown in Fig. 2A, ViMST ranked first with an average ARI of 0.643 and a median ARI of 0.615. ViMST also achieved the highest ARI scores in 6 out of the 12 slices. SpaMask ranked second only to ViMST, with both a mean ARI and a median ARI of 0.58. GraphST and STGMVA also demonstrated good spatial clustering performance, with average ARI values of 0.541 and 0.588, respectively. In contrast, the average and median scores of other methods did not exceed 0.55, and some samples exhibited severe inter-layer confusion in their clustering results. For a more detailed evaluation, we further assessed ViMST’s clustering performance using NMI, AMI, HS, and Purity metrics. Experimental results showed that ViSMT consistently maintained the best performance on the other four metrics (Additional file 4: Fig. S7). Next, we used tissue slice 151,507, which contains 4226 spots. As shown in Fig. 2D, ViMST (ARI = 0.628), SpaMask (ARI = 0.605), DeepST (ARI = 0.571), and SEDR (ARI = 0.564) all achieved relatively good clustering performance and successfully identified spatial domains that closely matched the manually annotated tissue layers (Fig. 2C). However, DeepST exhibited unclear boundaries, and SEDR misclassified certain spots in the third layer. In comparison, CCST and STAGATE showed systematic biases in identifying the L5 layer. ConGI, STMGAC, and STMIGCL exhibited inconsistent performance on layers L3–L6. GraphST incorrectly merged adjacent cortical layers, such as L1–L2 and L3–L4. Although STGMVA established relatively clear hierarchical structures, its point distribution was disordered in multi-layer boundary regions. Leiden clustering performed the worst, distinguishing only two layers, with the remaining clusters blending into other layers. Overall, ViMST effectively identified cortical layer structures in the DLPFC dataset, demonstrating stability and robustness across several slices.

In addition, we performed Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) [21] visualization and Partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) [41] trajectory analysis on embeddings generated by ViMST. Taking slice 151,507 as an example (Fig. 2E), the algorithm shows a continuous spatial trajectory from L1 to WM in the UMAP visualization, with natural transitions between adjacent cortical layers. The PAGA trajectory graph of ViMST embeddings shows an approximately linear developmental trajectory from layer 1 to layer 6, with similarities between adjacent layers. Furthermore, we performed the same analysis on five methods (SpaMask, DeepST, STMGAC, SEDR, and ConGI) with higher ARI scores. On slice 151,507, SpaMask also performed well, with smooth and clear transitions between layers. In contrast, the UMAP visualization shows that the methods STMGAC and ConGI exhibit mixed layers between L1–WM and L4–L6, respectively. SEDR and DeepST exhibit better UMAP visualizations, but the trajectory networks in the PAGA trajectory graphs exhibit abnormal connections, indicating significant defects in spatial information modeling.

To further explore downstream tasks, we identified Spatially Variable Genes (SVGs) using the same procedure as SpaGCN (Fig. 2F). Fifty six SVGs were detected in slice 151,673, scattered across different domains. Different colors were used to represent the relative expression levels of relevant genes, and Fig. 2F shows that the SVGs in different structural domains identified by ViMST match the cortical structure of manual annotations and clustering results. Since detecting neuronal layers in some slices with a single gene is difficult, we constructed meta-genes to label specific domains. Taking slice 151,671 as an example, we used the same program as SpaGCN to accurately mark these structural domains by constructing metagenes, as shown in Fig. 2G. The layers marked here, including layer 3, layer 5, layer 6, and WM, match the cortical structure of manual annotations and clustering results well. Metagenes refer to aggregated gene expression profiles derived from multiple genes that exhibit spatial coherence, serving to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio and highlight spatially informative patterns that may be difficult to discern using single genes alone [22, 27].

ViMST can denoise and impute gene expression to better characterize spatial expression patterns

Spatial transcriptomics data analysis is often affected by high noise and missing gene expression, which can significantly reduce the resolution accuracy of biological features. The ViMST method proposed in this study uses gene expression matrices, without PCA dimensionality reduction, as input to reconstruct gene expression profiles via a decoder module, thereby enhancing the ability to identify spatial-resolution features that are lost in the raw data.

To validate ViMST’s denoising performance, we first analyzed seven cortical layer specific marker genes on slice 151,673 of the DLPFC dataset. Figure 3A shows that these genes exhibit significant spatial mixing in the raw data, with high-expression sites distributed diffusely. After denoising by ViMST, these genes can accurately match their spatial patterns. For instance, the distribution patterns of marker genes such as FABP7 and ADCYAP1 change significantly after denoising: the former is specifically enriched in the first cortical layer, while the latter is accurately localized to the third layer, consistent with previous literature reports [42]. Additionally, by comparing the violin plots of raw and denoised data (Fig. 3B), we found that the expression patterns after ViMST processing highly match the spatial structure annotated histologically, indicating that ViMST can better represent the spatial patterns of stratified marker genes through effective denoising.

To further investigate ViMST’s denoising and imputation capabilities, we next applied it to a healthy human lymph node dataset [43] (Fig. 3C). Lymph nodes contain substructures known as germinal centers (GCs), and we first identified three GCs and plotted the denoised values of three gene markers within their substructures. As shown in Fig. 3D, compared to the raw data, the denoised BCL6 (marking mature B cells) shows significant enrichment in the core area of the GC, FCER2 (marking naive B cells in the follicular mantle) forms clear boundaries around the GC, and EGR1 (marking activated B cells) is expressed explicitly outside the follicle [44]. We further assessed the correlation of genes before and after denoising. IGHD is known to be positively correlated with MS4A1 and CD1C and negatively correlated with CD3D [29]; the more significant expected correlations were displayed after ViMST processing (Fig. 3E). As shown in Fig. 3F, the IGHD-CD1C correlation score was slightly lower than that of SEDR, but ViMST outperformed existing methods, such as Sprod and SEDR, in terms of correlation strength after processing, further demonstrating that ViMST performs better in gene denoising and imputation.

ViMST provides improved clustering performance in complex spatial domains and tissue structures.

In the next benchmark experiment, we applied ViMST to two 10 × Visium datasets with more complex tissue structures (the human breast cancer (BRCA) dataset [45] and the mouse brainanterior and posterior dataset [27]) and compared it with all 12 benchmark methods.

The BRCA dataset was meticulously annotated by Xu et al. (Fig. 4A) [29]. The data primarily includes four types of spots: ductal carcinoma in situ/lobular carcinoma in situ (DCIS/LCIS), healthy tissue (Healthy), invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), and tumor-adjacent regions with lower malignancy (Tumor Edge). ViMST maintained the best clustering performance on this dataset compared to the other 12 methods, achieving the highest ARI value of 0.657 (Fig. 4B). SpaMask also performed well, achieving an ARI of 0.631, whereas none of the other methods achieved an ARI above 0.6 (Fig. 4C). ViMST also outperformed other methods across evaluation metrics such as AMI, NMI, HS, and Purity, maintaining the best performance. The complete spatial domain identification results and the five metric values for all 13 methods are provided in Additional file 5: Fig. S8 and S9. In terms of the identified spatial domains, Leiden, SpaGCN, and STMIGCL performed the worst in spatial domain identification tasks, showing chaotic spot assignments across multiple spatial domains. STAGATE, SEDR, ConGI, and CCST exhibited disordered spot assignments and uneven boundaries, while STGMVA, GraphST, DeepST, STMGAC, and SpaMask displayed some outliers in different spatial domains. In contrast, the spatial domains identified by ViMST were the closest to the manual annotations, particularly demonstrating superior identification for IDC_2, IDC_4, and DCIS/LCIS_4 (Fig. 4A and B).

Since tissue samples can be significantly larger than the capture slides used in spatial transcriptomics, horizontal integration enables data from multiple capture slides to be stitched together. Here, we used two slices of mouse brain 10 × Visium data (divided into forebrain and hindbrain slices) to test the horizontal integration capability of ViMST for complex data (Fig. 4D), and compared it with five other competitive methods that support horizontal integration (CCST, SpaGCN, DeepST, STGMVA, and SEDR). For comparison, we set the number of clusters for all algorithms to 26, consistent with the demonstration of SpaGCN [27]. Using the mouse brain atlas annotations [46] (Fig. 4E), we compared the results of the two slices. As shown in Fig. 4H, Most clusters identified by the six methods agreed well with known anatomical structures. However, many clusters from CCST and DeepST were fragmented and failed to align along the shared edges of the two tissue slices. Although SpaGCN achieved anterior–posterior alignment, it divided the upper cortical layers into only 2 layers, whereas the correct number of layers in the upper cortex of the mouse brain, according to annotations (Fig. 4F), is between 4 and 6. The clustering results from STGMVA, SEDR, and ViMST accurately captured the cerebral cortex, cerebellum, and corpus callosum. However, some key regions within the tissue were not captured. For example, STGMVA and SEDR failed to identify the dorsal (upper) and ventral (lower) hippocampal regions, as indicated by the white boxes on the H&E images (Fig. 4D). In contrast, ViMST accurately revealed these regions. Additionally, to quantitatively evaluate ViMST’s clustering performance, we used the Calinski-Harabasz Score (CH index) to assess clustering quality. ViMST achieved the highest CH index among the six methods, further demonstrating its superiority in horizontally integrating complex data (Fig. 4G).

ViMST demonstrates strong performance in identifying subtle and rare spatial domains

To systematically evaluate ViMST’s capability to identify minute or rare spatial domains, we analyzed a human liver cancer dataset [47] generated with 10 × Visium technology and compared it with the GraphST [31] method, which demonstrated excellent performance in previous experiments. Initially, we set the number of clusters to 6 for both methods’ preliminary clustering analysis (Fig. 5A). The clustering results from the whole tissue sections showed high consistency between ViMST and GraphST.

In the tumor microenvironment (TME), stromal cells (MSCs), such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and dendritic cells (DCs), play a crucial role in regulating tumor development, invasion, and metastasis [48, 49]. Based on this theory, we selected several stromal cell-specific marker genes for spatial visualization analysis (Fig. 5B). At the raw gene expression level, these marker genes were predominantly enriched in spatial domain 3 of both ViMST and GraphST, although the distribution pattern was uneven. Notably, after noise reduction, the gene expression signals of ViMST were clearer and more clearly showed the enrichment of stromal cell marker genes in spatial domain 3.

To explore the functional characteristics of this area, we performed differential gene analysis and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on spatial domain 3 obtained from ViMST clustering (Additional file 6: Fig. S10). The results indicated that the GO terms of this region were significantly enriched in functions such as chemotaxis, cell activation, leukocyte-mediated immunity, and dendritic cell chemotaxis, all of which are typical features of stromal cell functions. For instance, MSCs often mediate the recruitment of immune cells through chemotactic factors, playing a central role in inflammation and tissue repair [50]. Studies have shown that MSCs can induce immune tolerance by activating the endogenous immune regulatory system of the receptor, thereby providing long-term therapeutic effects [51]. Moreover, cells in this region exhibited significant functional heterogeneity in the metabolism of reactive oxygen species, the respiratory burst, cell killing, and the positive regulation of tumor necrosis factor production. Enrichment of reactive oxygen species metabolism-related genes suggests that the region may experience oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, which are closely associated with the activation of immune cells and tumor progression [52]. These findings not only validate that Spatial Domain 3 exhibits typical stromal cell characteristics (matrix remodeling mediated by CAFs, immune regulation mediated by TAMs, and antigen presentation involving DCs) but also reveal the potential for significant functional heterogeneity in the region, corroborated by the uneven distribution of marker gene expression. These characteristics suggest that Spatial Domain 3 may play a central role in the dynamic regulation of the tumor microenvironment, and further research into this region could facilitate the discovery of interactions between tumor immunity and metabolism.

To further validate these observations, we identified SVGs within Spatial Domain 3, yielding a total of 96 SVGs. Among them, several known stromal cell-specific marker genes were detected, including ACTA2, COL1A1, and SPP1, thereby confirming the correspondence between Spatial Domain 3 and stromal cell functionality from an SVG perspective. To evaluate the clinical relevance of these newly identified SVGs, we obtained gene expression and survival data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) for liver cancer patients and performed survival analysis of the 96 SVGs (Fig. 5D). The results showed that the expression levels of the SVGs containing these marker genes were significantly associated with overall patient survival: the high-expression group exhibited significantly better overall survival than the low-expression group (p = 0.00053), highlighting the potential clinical value of these genes for prognostic assessment and therapeutic decision-making.

Based on these findings, we conducted a secondary clustering analysis of Spatial Domain 3 using ViMST to further resolve cellular heterogeneity. We subdivided the region into three distinct cell subtypes (Fig. 5C). We scored these three cell subtypes using the EMT gene set and the “AddModuleScore” function in Seurat [53]. The results indicated that Subtype 2 had the highest EMT score (0.192), suggesting stronger mesenchymal characteristics; Subtype 1 had a moderate EMT score (0.093), potentially representing a transitional state in the EMT process; while Subtype 0 had the lowest EMT score (0.004). The three subtypes identified by ViMST showed substantial differences in EMT scores, with the gap between the highest and lowest scores reaching 0.188 (Fig. 5E).

In contrast, following a similar secondary clustering analysis of Spatial Domain 3 by GraphST, the EMT scores for its three subtypes were 0.083 (Subtype 0), 0.075 (Subtype 1), and − 0.018 (Subtype 2), with the difference between the highest and lowest scores being only 0.101, and the EMT scores of Subtype 0 and Subtype 1 differed by a mere 0.008. This indicates that the subtypes identified by ViMST exhibit greater heterogeneity in the EMT state and superior secondary clustering performance compared to those identified by GraphST, further validating ViMST’s advantage in identifying subtle/rare spatial domains (Fig. 5E).

Furthermore, we performed GO enrichment analysis on the three cell subtypes identified by ViMST (Fig. 5F). The results showed that Subtype 0 was primarily enriched in functions such as ribosomal small subunit biogenesis, RNA splicing, and mitochondrial respiratory chain complex assembly, with no significant enrichment of EMT-related biological processes. Additionally, GO terms related to cellular components were mainly concentrated in chromosomal regions, mitochondrial intermembrane space, and nuclear periphery, suggesting that this cell population is more inclined toward an epithelial-like state and remains in the early stages of the EMT process, consistent with its low EMT score.

On the other hand, subtype 1 was mainly enriched in GO terms related to extracellular matrix (ECM) structure and tissue development, such as extracellular matrix, extracellular matrix binding, and vasculature development. These genes play crucial roles in ECM remodeling and tissue development, although their direct association with the EMT process is relatively weak. Therefore, the EMT score of Subtype 1 is at a moderate level.

In contrast, Subtype 2 showed GO term enrichment for processes highly related to core EMT, such as chemotaxis and focal adhesion, which are closely associated with cell migration and adhesion remodeling [54]. Additionally, endocytosis is associated with remodeling of signaling pathways, in which the endocytic degradation of cell junction proteins, such as E-cadherin, during the EMT process promotes loss of cell polarity, thereby enhancing EMT [55]. Furthermore, Subtype 2 was enriched in the enzyme-linked receptor protein signaling pathway, which may involve the TGF-β signaling pathway [56]. The TGF-β signal is one of the most important pathways known to induce EMT [57]. Subtype 2 is more directly involved in cell migration, signal transduction regulation, and focal adhesion, all of which are core mechanisms of EMT. Thus, Subtype 2 has the highest EMT score, indicating stronger migratory and invasive capabilities, whereas Subtype 1 is more inclined toward regulating ECM structure.

In summary, the three cell subtypes identified by ViMST exhibit significant dynamic changes in EMT states, reflecting the complexity of cellular plasticity and functional differentiation within the tumor microenvironment. This further validates ViMST’s effectiveness in identifying subtle/rare spatial domains and cellular-state heterogeneity, offering new perspectives for future research into the EMT process and its role in tumor progression.

Evaluation of hyperparameter selection and ablation analysis

We assessed the impact of key parameters such as the number of selected genes, neighborhood size, loss weights, metric distance, graph convolution layer type, mask ratio, and image feature extraction model on the clustering performance of ViMST across 12 slices of the DLPFC slice dataset. Additionally, we conducted ablation experiments to verify the importance of each module in ViMST. Finally, we evaluated the computational efficiency of the model.

The results indicated that ViMST achieved optimal clustering performance with 2000 genes as input and a neighborhood graph of 12 neighbors. ViMST is trained through three loss functions: gene feature loss, image feature loss, and spatial topology loss. To ensure the balance of the overall loss function, we explored three weight parameters (∂,β,γ). We further investigated the impact of various graph convolutional layers on the performance of ViMST, including GCNConv, TransformerConv, SAGEConv, GATConv, and GATV2Conv. As depicted in Fig. 7D, the model performed best when GCNConv was used as the backbone of the graph autoencoder across 12 slice datasets. To evaluate the impact of feature mask rates on model performance, we systematically assessed 25 combinations of the gene feature mask rate (gene_mask_rate) and the image feature mask rate (img_mask_rate), each ranging from 0.1 to 0.5, on the 10 × Visium DLPFC dataset. Notably, the model maintained reasonably good performance under other mask rate combinations, demonstrating its robustness to the choice of mask rates.

In our model design, we chose ViTL/16 as the histological feature extractor. Unlike Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) that rely on local receptive fields, ViT’s self-attention directly models long-range spatial interactions between image patches, capturing the multiscale tissue architecture essential for spatial transcriptomics [60]. To test this, we substituted ResNet-50 for ViT while keeping all other components identical. On the 12 DLPFC slices, the CNN variant achieved a mean ARI of 0.527 (vs. ViMST’s 0.643), along with consistently poorer clustering metrics and more fragmented spatial domains (see Supplementary Material 5 for specific numerical values). A detailed empirical comparison and corresponding advantage analysis are provided in Additional file 15 [61–63]. These results confirm that ViT’s global context modeling and flexible representation learning offer a distinct advantage in linking morphological features to gene expression. While ViT incurs a higher computational cost, its performance gain outweighs the overhead, and ViMST already runs more efficiently than comparable multimodal methods.

To evaluate the effectiveness of ViMST’s dual-modality parallel encoder-decoder architecture and joint topological contrastive learning, we conducted ablation experiments and used ARI to measure clustering performance. We evaluated the following three ablation scenarios: removal of the image feature enhancement module (“Without Image”), where clustering is performed using only gene features without referencing image features; removal of the topology decoder module (“Without Topo”), where spatial information enhancement is omitted, and raw spatial information is used; and removal of the gene feature enhancement module (“Without Gene”), where clustering is performed using only image features without gene features. As shown in Fig. 7E and Additional file 16: Fig. S18, the clustering performance of all three ablation models was inferior to that of the complete ViMST model. Among them, the “Without Gene” model performed worst, with significantly lower clustering performance when using only image features, indicating that gene information is critical for spatial transcriptomics data. The “Without Topo” model also performed poorly, yielding a mean ARI of 0.37 and a mean NMI of 0.49. ViMST achieved approximately 70% improvement in ARI over this variant. This suggests that, compared with merely enhancing modality features, joint topological contrastive learning plays a critical role in recovering spatial positional relationships. Additionally, the “Without Image” model performed reasonably well overall but still showed a performance gap compared to the complete ViMST model. By incorporating enhanced histological features, ViMST improved ARI across all 12 slices, with an 18% increase in overall model performance. The improvement was particularly pronounced in slices 151,669–151,672, underscoring the model’s critical need to incorporate histological features.

To further understand the running efficiency of ViMST, we conducted comparative experiments on ViMST and several other mainstream deep learning methods (including SpaMask, GraphST, SEDR, STAGATE, and ConGI) on spatial transcriptome datasets (DLPFC dataset and mouse olfactory bulb dataset) generated by two different platforms (10X Visium and Stereo-seq). On the DLPFC dataset, GraphST has the shortest running time, followed by SpaMask and SEDR. In terms of GPU memory usage, SpaMask and GraphST use the least, followed by SEDR and STAGATE. Both ViMST and ConGI exhibited relatively high runtime and memory usage because, as multimodal methods, they require additional high-resolution histological image segmentation and feature extraction. Nevertheless, ViMST consumed less time and memory than ConGI on both metrics. In the experiment on the mouse olfactory bulb dataset, ConGI could not be executed because the Stereo-seq technology did not provide matched high-resolution histological images, and it was therefore excluded from this comparison. SpaMask has the shortest running time, followed by GraphST and ViMST, while SEDR has the longest running time. In terms of GPU memory usage, SEDR occupy the least resources, followed by SpaMask and ViMST, while GraphST occupy the most resources. Notably, in this experiment, ViMST did not perform additional image segmentation or feature extraction, and its runtime and memory usage were substantially reduced to levels comparable with those of other advanced methods. Overall, although ViMST offers certain efficiency advantages over other multimodal methods, its time and GPU resource costs when processing high-resolution histological images still require further optimization. In summary, we systematically evaluated the key parameters of ViMST and validated the importance of each module through ablation experiments. The experimental results indicate that ViMST exhibits robust performance across various configurations and effectively enhances clustering performance for spatial transcriptomics data by integrating gene, histological, and topological features.

Discussion

This study proposes an innovative multimodal graph neural network, ViMST, that integrates histological images and transcriptomic expression data to enable precise analysis of spatial transcriptomic data. Through systematic experiments on multiple datasets, ViMST demonstrates superior performance in spatial clustering, gene expression denoising, and various downstream analysis tasks.

On the DLPFC dataset, ViMST accurately identifies the layered structure of the cerebral cortex, outperforming current mainstream methods, highlighting its significant advantage in analyzing spatial heterogeneity within tissues. ViMST effectively identifies subtle and boundary-blurred spatial structures such as IDC, DCIS, and LCIS on breast cancer data. On mouse combined anterior and posterior brain data, ViMST robustly aligns multi-slice information and accurately reconstructs key regions such as the cortex and hippocampus. Its clustering results also achieve significantly higher CH indices than other methods, demonstrating robust performance in integrating complex tissue structures. Furthermore, extensive experiments validate ViMST’s superior generalization across different tissue types (healthy and cancerous) and various spatial transcriptomic platforms (e.g., 10 × Visium and Stereo-seq). Whether applied to organs with complex structural organization, such as the mouse kidney, brain, and olfactory bulb, or cancerous tissues like pancreatic cancer, ViMST consistently outperforms existing methods on various unsupervised clustering metrics. It exhibits stable, robust spatial domain identification capabilities, making it suitable for spatial transcriptomic data across different scales and resolutions.

In identifying spatial heterogeneity within complex tumor microenvironments, ViMST demonstrates a heightened sensitivity in capturing subtle and rare spatial structures. For instance, in liver cancer data, ViMST not only accurately locates spatial regions enriched with stromal cells but also significantly enhances the visibility of key marker gene expression through denoising and feature enhancement mechanisms, revealing the functional characteristics of spatial domain 3 in immune response, cell activation, and inflammation signal regulation. Further subtype analysis shows that ViMST can distinguish cell-state differences within a functionally enriched region. The three identified cell subtypes exhibit significant differences in EMT levels, providing a more precise trajectory of cell-state transitions and revealing the complexity of cellular plasticity and dynamic regulatory mechanisms in the tumor microenvironment. In contrast, existing methods, such as GraphST, suffer from insufficient precision in spatial domain analysis and in characterizing cellular heterogeneity, with insignificant variations in EMT scores across subtypes, making it challenging to uncover functional heterogeneity among cells. ViMST’s systematic advantages in spatial denoising, functional enrichment, and subtype differentiation enable the identification of high-resolution spatial structures and open new avenues for understanding cell-fate transitions and tumor-immune interactions. It provides crucial spatial transcriptomic support and theoretical insights for future studies on tumor heterogeneity, the discovery of personalized therapeutic targets, and the analysis of disease mechanisms.

In the tasks of gene expression denoising and imputation, ViMST also demonstrates exceptional performance. On the DLPFC dataset, ViMST successfully reconstructs the spatial expression patterns of several cortical layer marker genes, thereby concentrating and precisely aligning previously diffuse expression signals with tissue structures, thereby significantly improving the interpretability of biological features. Additionally, in the germinal center regions of lymph node data, ViMST accurately restores the specific expression patterns of key marker genes across substructures and significantly outperforms mainstream methods such as Sprod and SEDR in the correlation analysis of multiple gene pairs. These results indicate that ViMST effectively denoises and enhances the structural consistency and biological interpretability of expression data, providing a more reliable foundation for subsequent spatial biomarker identification.

Most existing multimodal spatial transcriptomics methods rely on fixed spatial graphs for contrastive learning or other feature enhancement strategies. In contrast, ViMST employs a joint topological decoder to explicitly reconstruct spatial connections from multimodal latent features, strengthening the spatial structure through cross-modal fusion while enhancing feature representations (see Additional file 19: Table S4 for a detailed method comparison). Ablation experiments show that ViMST achieves an approximately 70% improvement in ARI over a variant that only enhances modality features without enhancing joint topological spatial relationships, further confirming the unique contribution of the joint topological decoder.

Although ViMST has achieved strong performance, several promising directions remain for future improvement. First, since the model relies on histological images, its accuracy can be affected by variations in image quality, staining conditions, and noise. Introducing noise‑resistant feature extractors, applying multi‑scale image augmentation during training, or building adaptive fusion strategies that dynamically balance gene expression and image‑derived features could all enhance model robustness under diverse imaging conditions and reduce sensitivity to low‑quality or artifact‑containing slides. Second, ViMST can be extended to multi‑slice integrative analysis. Recently, a series of methods such as SpaMask [38], SpaCross [64], and SpaBatch [65] have made important contributions to multi-slice spatial transcriptomics integration analysis. These methods leverage cross-slice graph construction, cross-masked contrastive learning, and two-stage training with triplet contrastive learning, respectively, to correct batch effects. Inspired by these excellent multi-slice methods, ViMST can construct a unified graph that combines intra-slice and inter-slice connections, with its ViT-extracted morphological features providing complementary spatial references for more accurate cross-slice alignment. Third, cell‑type deconvolution represents another highly promising extension. Building on the notable contributions of SpaDAMA [66] and MACD [67]—which have successfully demonstrated that combining masked autoencoders with adversarial domain alignment can effectively transfer scRNA-seq-derived cell-type signatures to ST data—ViMST, sharing a similar dual‑modality masked autoencoder backbone, can project such cell‑type signatures into its pre‑trained latent space and incorporate a dedicated deconvolution head to jointly infer cell‑type proportions from gene expression and morphological features; meanwhile, the joint topological decoder can provide spatial regularization for this process. Together, these extensions will advance ViMST from a single‑slice spatial domain identification tool toward a versatile, robust, and morphology‑aware multimodal framework for integrative spatial transcriptomics analysis.

Conclusions

In summary, ViMST provides a powerful, flexible tool for multimodal spatial transcriptomic analysis. It helps uncover spatial associations between tissue structure and gene expression, offering new perspectives for downstream tasks such as disease microenvironment analysis and spatial biomarker discovery.

Methods

Data preprocessing and spatial graph construction

In ViMST’s data preprocessing, genes are initially filtered to retain only those expressed in at least 50 spots, with counts exceeding 10 in each spot. The data for these genes are then normalized, and the top Nh highly variable genes are selected using the Seurat [2] method for input into the model. Ultimately, a gene feature matrix Xgene∈RNspot×Nh is constructed, where Nspot denotes the number of spots and Nh represents the number of selected genes (Fig. 1A(b)).

The strength of spatial transcriptomics lies in its spatial information, which enables the identification of spatially co-localized similar cellular states and the delineation of tissue substructures. To fully leverage spatial information, we determine the k-nearest neighbors for each spot using Euclidean distance and construct an undirected neighborhood graph G=V,E. In graph G, V represents the set of spots (each corresponding to a node), and E denotes the edges connecting the nodes. The adjacency relationships are defined by an adjacency matrix A∈RNspot×Nspot, where aij = 1 if spot j is one of the k-nearest neighbors of spot i, and aij = 0 otherwise. The complete spatial relationship is denoted as ℇspa (Fig. 1A(b)).

Generating image-derived featuresvia vision transformers

This study proposes a multimodal deep learning framework based on Vision Transformers (ViT) to establish a mapping relationship between histopathological image features and spatial gene expression profiles. By integrating local morphological features with positional information from spatial transcriptomics, this method enables cross-modal prediction of cellular structural features from molecular expression patterns, critical for understanding cellular architecture and alterations in tissue samples.

Histological images are first divided into small patches aligned with spot positions in ST data. This step ensures the precise localization of features from each image region and their association with corresponding gene expression profiles. Subsequently, a ViT model is employed to process these patches and extract local morphological features. ViT divides images into non-overlapping patches as a Transformer-based neural network and linearly embeds them into the model. During training, a loss function quantifies the discrepancy between predicted and actual gene expression, ensuring the model accurately learns the relationship between image features and gene expression and enabling deeper exploration of molecular characteristics in tissue space.

To analyze histological images, we segment them into patches corresponding to individual spots and convert them into a matrix of shape Nspot × (3 × W × H) as the primary input for ViT. Here, 3 represents the RGB color channels, Nspot represents the number of spots, and W and H are the width and height of the patch, respectively. The input matrix is then processed through a ViT backbone to extract deep morphological representations. Specifically, each patch of size W × H with 3 color channels is first divided into 16 × 16 sub-patches and linearly projected into a 1024-dimensional embedding space, following the standard ViT-Large (ViT-L/16) architecture [68]. The ViT-L/16 model employs a hidden dimension of 1024, which has been demonstrated to offer a favorable balance between representational capacity and computational efficiency compared to the smaller ViT-Base (768-d) and larger ViT-Huge (1280-d) variants. This 1024-dimensional specification has been widely adopted in computational pathology foundation models, including recent whole-slide image analysis frameworks such as PathVQ [69], as well as in large-scale vision-language models such as CLIP ViT-L/14 [70]. The resulting feature matrix F of size Nspot × 1024 is then passed through a trainable transformation layer W for task-specific adaptation, enabling subsequent deep learning analysis (Fig. 1A(a)).

Additionally, we incorporated spatial positional information from ST data to achieve more precise integration of image features with ST data. The spatial coordinates of each spot are represented as an Nspot × 2 matrix containing (x, y) coordinates of all spots. The x-coordinates are further transformed into an Nspot × m one-hot encoded matrix, where m corresponds to the maximum x-coordinate value across the entire tissue section. After integrating image features and spatial coordinates, we constructed an initial image feature vector that served as input to the ViT model (Fig. 1A(a)).

To mitigate potential overfitting caused by limited ST data, several strategies are adopted. First, a relatively lightweight ViT architecture (with a small number of transformer layers) is employed to reduce model complexity. Second, dropout regularization is applied within the embedding and transformer layers. Third, during the reconstruction phase, only the parameters of the gene prediction head are optimized, while the ViT backbone remains stable, effectively reducing the number of trainable parameters. In addition, incorporating spatial positional embeddings provides a strong inductive bias, further constraining the learning process.

Data masking augmentation

This study addresses the technical challenge of traditional autoencoders being prone to the “identity mapping dilemma” in feature dimension matching scenarios [3] where models may degenerate into simple input replicators and lose feature learning capability when input feature dimensions are less than or equal to encoded dimensions. To overcome this limitation, we draw on the core concept of denoising autoencoders [4] and construct a feature extraction framework with robust representation learning capability by introducing a dual mechanism of controlled noise interference and graph structural constraints.

First, we perform PCA on the image-derived matrix Yvit∈RNspot×Nh, retaining Np principal components to obtain a reduced matrix Yvit∈RNspot×Np, where Nspot is the number of spatial spots. Similarly, we apply an edge masking strategy to the spatial graph. After removing Elose, we obtain the remaining edge set Erem, and the perturbed spatial structure is denoted as ℇlose.

The input to this channel is Gmask-x, which consists of the randomly masked gene expression matrix Xm combined with the complete spatial graph ℇspa. Joint topological decoder model for spatial relationship enhancement

In this study, we propose a graph contrastive learning framework based on heterogeneous information fusion, which enables cross-modal alignment and reconstruction of topological structure in spatial transcriptomics data through dual-modality feature interaction and dynamic edge relation reasoning. The input to this channel consists of two types: the first type, denoted as Glose-y, is formed by combining the complete image-derived matrix Yvit with a spatial graph ℇl where some edges have been removed. The second type, denoted as Glose-x, comprises the complete gene expression matrix Xgene and the same perturbed spatial graph ℇl. When Glose-y is input into the model, the encoder, under a parameterized mapping function GCNeny, projects it into latent representations Hs∈RNspot×Nf. Notably, the parameters of this encoder are shared with the encoder that processes Gmask-y in the histological feature enhancement channel, and both encoders learn a mapping function GCNeny parameterized by φ. Similarly, when Glose-x is passed through its encoder, it is mapped into latent representations Hg∈RNspot×Nf under a separate parameterized mapping function GCNenx. The parameters of this encoder are shared with the encoder from the gene feature enhancement channel that processes Gmask-x, with both encoders learning the mapping function GCNenx parameterized by φ1.
