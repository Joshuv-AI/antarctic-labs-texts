Current and Desired Behavioral Health Resources for Children: A Survey of Illinois Emergency Departments
Authors: Jennifer A. Hoffmann, Kendall Arslanian, Cindy Durr, Jennifer Monti, Suhanee Mitragotri, Stephanie Atella, Ashley A. Foster
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesIn the context of rising emergency department (ED) visits by children for behavioral health (BH) concerns, our objective was to understand current and desired resources for the care of children presenting to Illinois EDs with BH concerns.MethodsWe surveyed nurse pediatric emergency care coordinators (PECCs) and nurse managers working in Illinois EDs participating in the Pediatric Facility Recognition Program from March 15 to June 30, 2025. We assessed ED characteristics, pediatric BH care practices, and current and desired BH resources.ResultsAmong 101 eligible EDs, 65 (64%) responded. Respondents included nurse PECCs (77%), nurse managers (17%), and nurses serving in both roles (6%). Most EDs (60%) were in hospitals without a pediatric inpatient unit, 22% were in critical access hospitals, and 67% had <4999 annual pediatric ED visits. On-site mental health professionals and telemental health services were available in 56% and 44% of EDs, respectively. Universal suicide risk screening was reported in 88% of EDs, most frequently using the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale Screen Version (87%) and starting at age 8 (36%). Among EDs, 56% reported always or often providing all elements of safety planning. Frequently desired resources included activity kits (82%) and a portal to view pediatric psychiatric inpatient bed availability (73%). High priority resources included a mechanism to escalate difficult cases (23%) and increased access to mental health professionals in the ED (23%).ConclusionSurvey findings inform opportunities to standardize and improve pediatric BH emergency care through the development and dissemination of desired resources.

The Bottom LineEmergency departments (EDs) by children with behavioral health concerns are rising, but an understanding of current and desired resources for this population remains limited. In this survey of EDs participating in the Illinois Pediatric Facility Recognition Program, just over half reported on-site support from mental health professionals and most reported universal suicide risk screening, but only about half consistently implemented all recommended safety planning components. Respondents identified limited disposition options and prolonged boarding as major barriers. Commonly desired resources included activity kits and real-time psychiatric bed tracking. These findings highlight actionable opportunities to improve pediatric emergency behavioral health care.

Introduction

Background

Pediatric emergency department (ED) visits for behavioral health (BH) concerns have increased substantially over the past decade, particularly visits for suicide-related concerns.1 Concurrently, pediatric BH boarding has become more frequent and prolonged.2 Despite these trends, little information exists regarding the resources available to support pediatric BH care across US EDs. In the 2021 National Pediatric Readiness Project (NPRP) assessment, only three items focused on BH care.3 Of responding EDs, 73% reported having pediatric BH-related policies,3 although the content and nature of these policies were not characterized.4 EDs with lower annual pediatric ED volume were less likely to report having pediatric BH-related policies,4 suggesting variability in preparedness to serve children with BH needs.

Importance

ED clinicians face multiple barriers when caring for children with BH concerns, including limited access to mental health professionals, inadequate infrastructure to support children during boarding, and scarce outpatient BH resources after discharge. Together, these constraints disrupt patient flow and may compromise the quality and consistency of care.5,6 Provision of specific resources, including activity kits for boarding youth and telepsychiatry consultation, has been found to be highly acceptable, although evidence regarding effectiveness in reducing length of stay remains mixed.7, 8, 9 To date, there are limited data to guide prioritization of resources to improve pediatric BH emergency care.

Goals of This Investigation

The Illinois Pediatric Facility Recognition Program, established in 1998, supports pediatric emergency readiness by designating Illinois EDs as pediatric critical care centers, approved for pediatrics, or standby for pediatrics, on the basis of meeting core requirements.10 One of these requirements is the designation of a nurse pediatric emergency care coordinator (PECC).11 PECCs play a key role in pediatric quality improvement, policy development, and staff education, particularly in EDs with lower annual pediatric volume.12,13 Thus, their perspectives are critical to identifying gaps and prioritizing resources to support pediatric BH emergency care.

In 2022, the US Health Resources and Services Administration expanded Pediatric Mental Health Care Access Program (PMHCA) funding beyond its original focus, supporting BH consultation in primary care settings, to also support EDs and schools.14, 15, 16 In Illinois, PMHCA funding allocated to the Illinois Department of Public Health supports efforts to assess existing ED resources and identify priorities for development of new resources. In this context, we aimed to understand current and desired resources to care for children presenting to Illinois EDs with BH concerns. Given rising suicide-related ED visits among Illinois youth,17 a secondary objective was to compare reported implementation of evidence-based suicide risk identification and management practices by annual pediatric ED volume.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a cross-sectional survey of nurse PECCs and nurse managers from EDs participating in the Illinois Pediatric Facility Recognition Program. Of Illinois EDs, 81% have low annual pediatric volume (<4999 visits), 65% are located in facilities without a pediatric inpatient unit,3 and 56% participate in the Illinois Pediatric Facility Recognition Program. Of participating EDs, 45% are located in facilities without a pediatric inpatient unit. Reporting followed the consensus-based checklist for reporting of survey studies (CROSS) guidelines. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) institutional review board reviewed and approved the study.

Selection of Participants

Eligible participants included nurse PECCs and nurse managers from EDs open 24/7 that participate in the Illinois Pediatric Facility Recognition Program.

Survey Development

To develop the survey, we adapted relevant items from the NPRP assessment, Ontario, Canada’s ED Paediatric Readiness survey, the PMHCA needs assessment, and the AAP Improving Mental Health Processes, Workflows, and Responses collaborative environmental scan.3,15,18 New items were also developed, addressing pediatric BH emergency care resources and practices. Feedback on the survey draft was solicited from the Illinois Academy of Family Physicians, the Illinois Emergency Nurses Association, the Illinois College of Emergency Physicians, and the Illinois Critical Access Hospital Network. The survey was pilot tested with 34 nurse PECCs and nurse managers working outside Illinois, including cognitive interviews with 6 nurse PECCs.19 Revisions were made following pilot testing.

Survey Distribution

The survey was distributed electronically from March 15 to June 30, 2025, through a listserv of nurse PECCs maintained by the Illinois Emergency Medical Services for Children program. One response per ED was requested. Nonresponders received up to 3 reminder emails and 1 telephone call. Respondents received a gift card and a curated list of Supplementary-based pediatric emergency BH resources (Supplementary Appendix 1).

Measurements

Survey content areas included ED characteristics; BH policies and procedures; triage, screening, assessment, disposition, and boarding of pediatric patients with BH concerns; availability of telemental health services; and desired resources to improve pediatric BH emergency care, categorized into processes, personnel, supplies, and knowledge (Supplementary Appendix 2).

Analysis

Duplicate responses (ie, surveys received from the same ED) were removed through the following stepwise process: (1) surveys with < 40% completion were excluded, (2) if both a nurse PECC and nurse manager responded, the survey completed by the nurse PECC was retained, (3) the most recently submitted survey was retained, presuming it reflected the most up-to-date information.

Descriptive statistics were used to report counts and percentages. Risk differences with 95% CIs were calculated to compare reported suicide prevention practices in EDs with low annual pediatric volume (<4999 visits) and medium/high volume.3 Analyses used complete cases, with denominators defined as EDs providing responses to both outcome and volume classification; denominators varied due to item nonresponse. Open-ended responses were analyzed using inductive qualitative content analysis20 conducted by two coders, a pediatric emergency medicine physician and an expert in qualitative analysis (JH and KA), with discrepancies resolved by consensus. Analyses were conducted in R version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025) and Excel version 16.108 (Microsoft, 2026).

Respondents included 50 (77%) nurse PECCs, 11 (17%) nurse managers, and 4 (6%) nurses who held both roles. Emergency Care Practices for Children with Suicidal Thoughts or Behaviors

Of EDs, 89% had a guideline, pathway, or policy on suicide risk that included pediatric considerations. Universal suicide risk screening (regardless of the presenting complaint) was reported in 88% of EDs, while selective screening of patients with BH complaints was reported in 6% of EDs (Table 3). The most frequently used screening tools were the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale Screen Version (87%) and Ask Suicide-Screening Questions (20%). Among EDs reporting universal screening, the lower age limit for screening was most commonly 8 years (36%), followed by 12 years (30%), while 9% of EDs did not specify a lower age threshold. Pediatric Behavioral Health Boarding

Most EDs reported that 0 to 1 children were BH boarding on a typical day, regardless of whether school was in session (60%) or out of session (56%), while 26% of EDs reported that 2 to 5 children were boarding on a typical day when school was in session (Supplementary Appendix 4). The most common service provided during boarding was orders for behavior escalation medications (71%). During boarding, 53% of EDs initiated or changed psychiatric medications, and few EDs provided psychotherapy by mental health professionals in-person (15%) or via telehealth (15%).

Among 55 unique responses to the open-ended question, “If you could change 1 thing to improve the care of children with mental and BH symptoms in your ED, what would it be?” Six themes emerged: creating a safer and more therapeutic ED environment; increasing access to BH professionals; improving assessment and care process efficiency; reducing boarding; strengthening community-based BH services; and enhancing ED staff education (Supplementary Appendix 6). Respondents from rural and critical access EDs particularly emphasized the need to improve access to BH professionals with pediatric-specific BH expertise and reduce transportation barriers associated with interfacility transfers.

Limitations

Survey data were self-reported and therefore subject to response and social desirability bias. Practices were not independently verified through electronic health record review or direct observation. Responses reflect the perspectives of nurse PECCs and nurse managers—selected for their familiarity with pediatric ED policies and processes—although perspectives of physicians, mental health professionals, youth, and caregivers may differ. Although the response rate was relatively high for a statewide survey, approximately one-third of eligible EDs did not participate, introducing the potential for selection bias. Finally, findings from EDs in Illinois may not generalize to other states.

Discussion

In this statewide survey of nurse PECCs and nurse managers working in EDs participating in the Illinois Pediatric Facility Recognition program, pediatric BH care practices and available resources varied across EDs, particularly with respect to access to on-site mental health professionals and suicide care practices. Although most EDs reported universal suicide risk screening and routine assessment of suicidal intent, only about half consistently implemented all elements of safety planning, and few EDs distributed firearm storage devices. Respondents also identified major operational challenges related to prolonged boarding, limited disposition options, and insufficient access to pediatric mental health professionals. Actionable solutions identified by respondents included activity/sensory kits, real-time bed visibility, and mechanisms for escalation of cases with challenging placements. Findings highlight the need for policies to promote system-wide investment across the pediatric BH care continuum.

Most EDs reported having pediatric-specific suicide risk guidelines, consistent with a growing emphasis on suicide prevention in emergency care settings.21 While the Joint Commission requires screening patients with BH complaints, the AAP Blueprint for Suicide Prevention recommends universal screening.22 In this survey, most EDs reported conducting universal suicide risk screening, and screening practices did not differ significantly by annual pediatric volume, suggesting broad uptake across ED types. However, only about one-third of EDs tracked compliance with suicide risk screening, although the NPRP Quality Initiative recommends tracking this quality measure.23 We found that the most frequent lower age limit for initiation of universal screening was 8 years old, in contrast to a prior systematic review that identified ages 10 and 12 years as the most common ages for initiating universal screening in EDs.21

Although assessment practices were generally strong, implementation gaps remained in safety planning, with only about half of EDs consistently providing all recommended components. Safety planning is an evidence-based intervention associated with reductions in suicidal behaviors after ED discharge and reduced return visits among adults, with emerging evidence of effectiveness among youth.24, 25, 26 Safety plans are designed to help patients recognize warning signs of a suicidal crisis, use coping skills, identify contacts to ask for help, and create a safer home environment.26 In a large national survey conducted from 2017 to 2018 that was not pediatric-specific, only 15% of EDs reported routinely implementing all components of safety planning.27,28 The higher rate of implementation identified in our study may reflect increased adoption of safety planning over time or increased use of safety planning among youth relative to adults, but nevertheless demonstrates a persistent implementation gap. Research is needed to design novel strategies to overcome barriers to safety planning in EDs, such as task shifting or technology assistance.29,30 Although provision of all safety planning elements did not differ significantly by annual pediatric volume category, provision of information about firearm storage devices occurred more frequently in EDs with low annual pediatric volume, perhaps reflecting increased household firearm ownership rates in rural areas where lower volume EDs are frequently located.3,31 We found that few EDs distributed firearm storage devices, despite evidence that combining counseling with provision of firearm storage devices improves safe storage practices.32

Approximately 2 in 5 EDs reported using telemental health services, most commonly for treatment recommendations and level-of-care determinations. In a nationally representative survey, use of telemental health care was reported in 68% of US EDs associated with general medical hospitals, with more frequent use in rural, smaller volume, and critical access EDs.33 Telehealth may offer an important strategy to expand pediatric mental health expertise, with some studies finding associations with reduced waiting time for evaluation and lower admission rates compared to in-person evaluation.34 In our study, fewer than 1 in 5 EDs reported using telehealth for post-discharge follow-up, representing a potential opportunity to bridge gaps in care during the high-risk postdischarge period.35,36

Respondents consistently identified prolonged boarding and limited disposition options as major barriers to care, consistent with nationally described trends.37,38 Desired solutions included enhancing the ED environment with therapeutic spaces, activities, and support services; expanding capacity via more pediatric-trained BH professionals and inpatient pediatric psychiatric beds; and streamlining care processes. Emerging work has begun to establish the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of several of these interventions. For instance, protocols to support reduction of BH symptoms during boarding are feasible to implement,39,40 the distribution of activity kits to EDs is acceptable to ED staff,7 and around-the-clock ED BH staffing has been associated with increased provision of recommended safety planning and discharge practices.41 Future work is needed to understand the impact of specific interventions on ED operations as well as patient- and family-reported outcomes.

Our findings underscore the importance of system-level solutions extending beyond individual EDs. Several states have implemented policies intended to bolster BH services for children across the care continuum and reduce pressures on EDs,42 although evaluation of such programs remains limited. A quasi-experimental study of Connecticut’s mobile crisis response program found that receipt of mobile crisis services was associated with lower odds of a subsequent BH ED visit among youth.43 Additionally, the Virginia Psychiatric Bed Registry was created by the state government to maintain a centralized, publicly accessible statewide dashboard of available inpatient psychiatric beds, enabling improved coordination and transparency.44 In 2022, the children’s BH transformation initiative in Illinois convened partners across six Illinois child-serving agencies to adjust BH provider capacity and streamline ways families can access care, enabling earlier intervention.45 These policy approaches directly address several solutions proposed by survey respondents, including real-time visibility of inpatient bed availability and expansion of BH services outside the ED.

In Illinois, these survey findings will be used to inform development and dissemination of resources to improve emergency BH care for children. Already, EDs can access free firearm storage devices for distribution to families from the Illinois Department of Public Health and local ChildSafe law enforcement partners.46 Additionally, EDs can access family handouts, handoff tools, and activity purchasing guides through the New England Regional BH Toolkit.47 The emergency medical services for children innovation and improvement center website provides sample care pathways and educational materials related to pediatric suicide prevention and agitation management in the ED.48

In conclusion, in this survey of nurse PECCs and nurse managers in EDs in Illinois, we identified substantial variability in pediatric BH care practices across EDs, alongside shared challenges that reflect both local and system-level constraints. Addressing these gaps will require coordinated improvements to ED-based care, including implementation of evidence-based practices, along with system-level investments to reduce ED boarding, expand the pediatric BH workforce, and support connection to community services.
