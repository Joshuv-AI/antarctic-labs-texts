Sequence-dependent generation of pancake π-dimer anions with peptide-flanked perylene diimides
Authors: Yuqi Song, Chana R. Honick, Grant E. K. Hall, Xubo Wang, Sayak S. Panda, Luke R. O'Connor, Rachel A. Dziatko, Lan Cheng, John D. Tovar, Arthur E. Bragg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In contrast to weak noncovalent interactions between π-conjugated molecules, the strong multi-centered/two-electron intermolecular π–π interaction referred to as a “pancake” bond results from the constructive intermolecular π-orbital overlap of two singly-occupied molecular orbitals on adjacent chromophores. Pancake bonding is not only interesting as a novel chemical bonding behavior, it is a potential carrier trapping mechanism within organic semiconductor materials. We report on the formation kinetics of pancake π-dimer dianions following photoinduced electron transfer from anthracene carboxylate donors to aggregates of peptide-functionalized perylene diimide (PDI) in buffered aqueous solution. Pancake dimer ion formation is sensitive to the sequence of the solubilizing oligopeptide tethered to the PDI imide positions: positioning an alanine or glycine residue adjacent to PDI stifles or enables, respectively, the frontier-orbital overlaps that stabilize pancake π–π bonding in these species, as corroborated with DFT-computed relative bonding stabilities for PDI dimer dianions. We track photoreduction mechanisms using time-resolved absorption spectroscopy, observing that dimer anions are formed through the interaction of singly-charged monomers with temperature-dependent studies revealing a low energy barrier to π-dimer anion formation. We observe a slower build-up of PDI-peptide anions and π-dimer dianions using 9-anthracene carboxylic acid as the donor in aerated solutions and under “dark” conditions (after irradiation) compared to what is observed with argon-sparged solutions. We attribute this difference to the photooxidation of this donor, which generates long-lived redox-active species that are photoreduced during irradiation and subsequently shuttle electrons to PDI-peptides through a redox cascade that exceeds the duration of light exposure. Our findings illustrate how the structure-specificity of peptide scaffolds can be used to support novel chemical interactions in molecular aggregates.

Noncovalent interactions between planar π-conjugated organic chromophores drive the formation of π stacks (or aggregates). Assembly-controllable energy and charge-transport behaviors1 in π stacks are appealing for manipulating material properties and function.2 In contrast to weak noncovalent packing interactions (i.e. van der Waals forces), the strong multi-centered/two-electron (mc/2e) intermolecular π–π interaction referred to as a “pancake” bond3 results from the constructive intermolecular π-orbital overlap of two singly-occupied molecular orbitals (SOMO), as illustrated in Fig. 1.4 In addition to exhibiting novel chemical bonding behavior, pancake dimers can facilitate intermolecular and interlayer electron transport,5 and they are frequently observed in organic functional materials with wide bandwidths and high conductivities.5 Various efforts have succeeded in synthesizing pancake-bond π-dimers using either neutral radicals6,7 or polar/charged radicals;8 in contrast, pancake bonding between neutral closed-shell species is generally not possible. Most of these dimers only exist in crystalline environments, as pancake bonding requires π-stacking geometries that maximize multicenter overlap between adjacent molecules,5 or in solutions but at low temperatures and/or with specific polar solvents to facilitate dimerization and to outcompete σ-dimer formation.9 Herein we report on the photochemical generation of pancake-bond pi-dimer anions of peptide-functionalized perylene diimides (PDI-peptides) in buffered basic aqueous solutions, whereby the strength of pancake bonding is controlled by the structure of the appended peptides.

The extended planar π-conjugation within PDI is well-known to facilitate molecular self-assembly into one-dimensional stacks. Such assembly supports frontier orbital overlaps between adjacent stacked chromophores that have well-documented impacts on charge-transfer interactions that underlie excitonic coupling, symmetry-breaking charge separation, and singlet fission.10–15 The energetically low-lying LUMO level of PDIs also results in a low reduction potential and enables the generation of stable radical anions in polar solvents, including water, with anionic PDI stable in both singly and doubly reduced states.16 Additionally, modifying the imide site allows for the facile synthesis of the PDI pi-dimer by incorporating pendant electron-deficient groups or sterically-hindering substituents. Pi-dimers can then be reduced via charge transfer.17,18 Several studies have already reported that PDI supports pi-dimer anions following chemical and electrochemical reduction, and the structural sensitivity of PDI dimer anion generation based on the identity of pendant groups at the imide position has already been observed.9,19–23 To our knowledge, photo-induced π-dimer dianion formation has not been observed.24 In contrast to chemical and electrochemical reductions, a photochemical reduction should provide a window into mechanisms and kinetics of dimer dianion formation, which remains elusive.

Here we report specifically on the photoinduced generation of dimer dianions between PDI-peptides in buffered aqueous solutions. PDIs are solubilized in water through symmetric modification with short peptide chains at the imide positions, as shown in Chart 1. These species exhibit signatures of weak assemblies in buffered basic solutions.25 We demonstrate that these are reduced in solution following the photoexcitation of anthracene carboxylate cosolutes, D1–D3. We find that π-dimer dianions are and are not formed with reduction of A2 and A1, respectively, due to differences in the extent of pi-overlap allowed by peptide substituents that control interaction of the PDI core: the VEVAA sequence of A1 was shown previously to foster more of a rotated offset intermolecular pi-stacking among PDI chromophores whereas the VEVAG sequence of A2 led to more of a cofacial stacking geometry.25 This structure sensitivity was further verified from transient observation of anionic species following chemical reduction of PDI-peptide solutions and supported with DFT computations of the relative bond-stability of PDI dimers with variation in stacking twist. We show that pancake bonding occurs through the direct reaction of PDI-peptide monomer anions and that peptide interactions can be used to control π–π bonding interactions.

Results and discussion

Details of experimental methods are provided in the SI. 9-Anthracene carboxylic acid (D1, Chart 1) (>99%) and 9,10-anthracene dicarboxylic acid (>95%) (D3) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used as received. 9-Anthracene acetic acid (D2) was prepared following literature precedent.26 The synthesis of PDI-peptides A1 and A2 (Chart 1) with peptide sequences VEVAA and VEVAG, respectively, has been described previously.25 We also investigated the generation of pi-dimer anions in solutions of PDI-peptides with peptide sequences DAIA (A3) and DAVG (A4). The structures of these acceptors are shown in Chart S1; data obtained with these structures are included in the SI and are only referenced in the main text. All aqueous solutions of PDI-peptides and donors were prepared with a Na2CO3/NaHCO3 buffer (pH = 10.8) to ensure that carboxylate groups on both species were fully deprotonated and that PDI-peptides did not form larger assemblies, as occurs for pi-peptides in solutions with neutral to acidic pH.27

Fig. 2 presents the steady-state UV-vis absorption spectra of D1 and A1 in a pH = 10.8 buffer solution. Solutions were prepared with PDI-peptides included at concentrations that correspond with optical densities (ODs) of ∼0.3–0.4 at 506 nm (roughly a few µM). Anthracene donors were present at ∼0.11–0.12 mM, corresponding to ODs near ∼1.1–1.2 at 365 nm,28 in order to enhance the relative yield of reduced acceptors upon donor irradiation at 385 nm. The UV-vis absorption spectrum of the S0 → S1 transition for each donor spans 300–400 nm and exhibits a vibronic progression associated with symmetric ring-stretching;29 donor spectra are similar to what have been reported in the literature previously for anthracene but exhibiting a 7–9 nm redshift of vibronic features arising from electronic interactions with the solubilizing groups.30 The absorption spectra of PDI-peptides A1 and A2 in buffer solution differ from the absorption spectrum of PDI dissolved in less polar solvents but are characteristic of PDI in water.31 Notably, the absorption spectrum of A1 in methanol (Fig. S1) exhibits peaks at ∼524, 488, and 458 nm that correspond with the 0–0, 0–1, and 0–2 vibronic transitions arising from a conjugated C–C stretch;32 in contrast, only two resolved transitions at ∼545 and 503 nm are observed in aqueous solutions and correspond with the 0–0 and 0–1 transitions, respectively (Fig. 2 and S1). This difference, and particularly the predominance of the 0–1 transition for PDI-peptides in water, signifies the presence of H-aggregates of PDI-peptides in water prior to chemical or photochemical reduction of the PDI core.14,31 We note that this solvent-dependent behavior is observed regardless of the peptide sequence appended to the PDI core, with A1–A4 all exhibiting similar absorption spectra at this pH.20,33

Photoreduction of PDI-peptides in buffer solution (pH = 10.8)

Time-resolved UV-vis absorption spectroscopy was used to track the photoreduction of PDI-peptides on timescales of seconds to minutes during and after irradiation with an LED at 385 nm. Time-resolved UV-vis spectra collected with argon-sparged and aerated solutions of D1 with A1 and A2 are presented in Fig. 3; solutions were irradiated for ∼8–15 seconds, until the initial stages of electron transfer from the donor to the acceptor could be observed, after which the LED was turned off to observe further charge transfer and the generation of π-dimer dianions. Under all conditions, a negative feature appears at ∼400–450 nm during the period of irradiation and corresponds with D1 emission. In a sparged solution of D1 + A1 (Fig. 3a), the absorption peak of A1 is slightly depleted (bleached) following the irradiation of D1 at 385 nm. At the same time, a new absorption feature appears at 730 nm and is assigned readily to the radical anion of A1, A1−, given its similarity with the spectrum of the radical anion of a modified PDI reported previously.24,34–36 We note that there is precedent for the spectrum of the radical anion shifting and broadening in aqueous (relative to nonaqueous) solution environments due to aggregation (vide infra).20 Thus, although we ascribe this feature to a radical “monomer monoanion,” the H-aggregate character apparent from the steady-state absorption spectrum of A1 (Fig. 2 and S1) suggests that the excess electron is most likely associated with a single A1 unit within a weakly assembled aggregate – i.e., the electron is not or is at best only weakly shared between A1 monomers; this is clarified through chemical reduction experiments described further below. Spectral signatures of A1− persist for several minutes following irradiation of sparged D1 + A1 solutions.

Time-resolved UV-vis absorption spectra collected with irradiation of D1 with a 385 nm LED: (a) D1 + A1 and (b) D1 + A2 in argon-sparged buffer solutions. (c) D1 + A1 and (d) D1 + A2 in aerated buffer solutions.

Fig. 3b illustrates that Ar-sparged solutions of D1 + A2 in aqueous buffer also exhibit signatures of D1 emission (400–450 nm) and A2 depletion upon irradiation at 385 nm. However, only a weak signature of A2− appears at 730 nm. Instead, a broad and intense band peaked at 600 nm appears and persists for a few minutes following irradiation. This feature resembles the absorption spectrum of PDI π-dimer dianions that have been observed spectroelectrochemically with a “hand-cuffed” PDI dimer species.19 We therefore attribute this feature to (A2)22−, the “dimer dianion” of weakly-interacting PDI-peptides; that is, two excess electrons are shared as a pancake pi-dimer of two A2 monomers within an aggregate.

Photoinduced reduction of A1 and A2 is also observed following irradiation of aerated solutions, but with somewhat different outcomes and time-dependence: Irradiation of aerated D1 + A1 solutions predominantly generates A12−, the closed-shell “monomer dianion,” which has an intense, characteristic absorption spectrum peaking at 550 nm (with a weaker shoulder at 500 nm and a smaller sharp feature near 600 nm), with weaker A1− absorption apparent at 730 nm (Fig. 3c). Similar behavior is observed for solutions of D1 with A3 (Fig. S2a). Irradiation of aerated D1 + A2 solutions similarly generates A22− shortly after irradiation, with signatures of (A2)22− appearing as the concentration of A22− decays (Fig. 3d). Similar spectral features are observed with D1 + A4 (Fig. S2b). It is noteworthy that the donor concentration used was considerably lower in aerated vs. sparged solutions (Fig. 3), yet the most highly reduced PDI-peptide species dominates for the former. As discussed in more detail further below, the differences in photoredox behavior in aerated vs. sparged solutions is speculated to originate from donor photooxidation that generates a long-lived electron-transfer intermediary that increases the effective concentration of photogenerated electron donors.

We corroborated these spectral assignments to various reduced states of the PDI-peptides with spectral measurements made following chemical reduction (Fig. S3–S15). Full details of chemical reductions are described in the SI. In summary, PDI-peptides were reduced in both 50 : 50 water : ethanol and basic aqueous solutions with the addition of excess sodium dithionite. For both solution types, addition of excess reductant resulted in the appearance of features characteristic of monomer dianion PDIs for both A1 and A2 (i.e.A12− and A22−). In both water–ethanol solutions as well as basic aqueous solutions of A1, the gradual oxidation of the dianions by dissolved oxygen produced spectral signatures of PDI-peptide monoanions (i.e.A1− and A2−) and ultimately the neutral PDI-peptides. In contrast, the gradual oxidation of A22− generated by reduction of basic aqueous solutions yielded the same broad band peaking at 600 nm observed in photochemical reduction experiments; this subsequently decayed with the appearance of A2− and then neutral A2 features. The clear appearance/predominence of the broad absorption feature at 600 nm as one of the intermediate (−1) redox states of A2 in basic aqueous solution (Fig. S3d and S7) and not water–ethanol solution (Fig. S3b and S5) highlights that PDI-peptide aggregation is critical to support the corresponding species and supports its assignment to the dimer dianion. Similar behavior is observed in the reduction of A4vs.A3 (Fig. S9–S15). Our chemical reduction experiments highlight that the monomer dianion species (i.e.A12− and A22−) correspond with the most highly reduced state; this observation reveals that aerated (compared to sparged) solutions must facilitate greater photochemical reducing efficiency (Fig. 3dvs.3c). We discuss possible mechanisms for this behavior further below.

A low electron transfer efficiency in sparged solution environments (characterized in more detail below) required very high donor concentrations to observe reduction of PDI-peptides; this resulted in spectral saturation below 400 nm in Fig. 3a and b and no discernible evolution in donor spectral features. In contrast, a number of the spectral features observed in Fig. 3c and d are attributed to the photochemistry of D1 and are more clearly observed with irradiation of aqueous solutions of D1 in absence of the PDI-peptide acceptors (Fig. S16a and b). Irradiation of D1 at 385 nm induces a persistent depletion of the sharp vibronic features (350–400 nm) associated with D1 and the appearance of a broad absorption below 350 nm that is attributable to the generation of (D1)2 – i.e. a covalent anthracene butterfly dimer, which is a common photoproduct of excited anthracenes.37 We have verified generation of this dimer by NMR (Fig. S17). We also observe an absorption band between 400–450 nm attributable to the generation of anthranoxide,38,39 a known photoproduct of D1; this feature is most clearly observed when sparged solutions of D1 are irradiated in absence of A1 (Fig. S16b). D1 is bleached to even greater extent in aerated samples (Fig. S16a vs. b), with concomitant increase in a broad background associated with soluble and insoluble photooxidation products of D1 that absorb and scatter light, respectively. The irradiation times required to efficiently generate PDI-peptide anions are shorter under aerated vs. sparged conditions. Further, the PDI π-dimer dianion concentration both builds up and decays more slowly in aerated vs. sparged solutions, with anion concentrations even continuing to increase in aerated solutions after the irradiation source is extinguished. We posit that these differences in photoreduction behavior arise from the generation of photooxidation products from D1 that can act as intermediary electron donors for the photoreduction of PDI. This is discussed in more detail below.

Finally, we note that we observe A1− (and A3−) or A2− (and A4−) regardless of the donor used for photoinduced electron transfer (D1–D3). However, monomer and dimer dianions of A2 and A4 only formed when D1 and not D2 or D3 served as the photoreductant (Fig. S18), signaling that the efficiency for photoreduction is greatest for D1.

Computation of relative pancake pi-bond stability for PDI dimers

Previous work has revealed that the PDI core of PDI-peptides exhibit greater site-to-site alignment in supramolecular assemblies of A2 and A4 compared to assemblies of A1 and A3;25 this behavior is apparent from the cumulative probability distribution functions for the neighbor-to-neighbor twist angle as determined via molecular dynamics simulation. These differences in mean stacking twist angle (and therefore relative molecular alignment) is attributable to differences in the peptide–peptide interactions: specifically, for A1 and A3, the alanine residues nearest the imide site impose steric packing constraints that limit alignment of the chromophore (introducing a ∼39° displacement for organized 1D stacks of A1 prepared under acidic solution conditions), whereas a glycine residue adjacent to the pi-core in A2 and A4 allows greater alignment (for A2, a 9° twist). Thus, while the PDI chromophores in all of these PDI-peptides should be able to stack cofacially in small aggregates and larger organized assemblies, the greater PDI-core alignment between neighboring A2 (or A4) molecules enables the possibility for a greater degree of frontier orbital overlap that is critical to stabilizing a pancake bond in a pi-dimer dianion. We previously demonstrated that this difference in peptide-controlled electronic interaction impacts exciton transport mediated by charge-transfer coupling within larger assemblies of PDI-peptides.40

With this understanding, quantum chemistry calculations were performed using density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the ORCA program41 to better understand the relative structure-dependent stability of PDI dimer dianions; computational details are provided in the SI (under methods). Specifically, we evaluated the energies of the PDI dianion as a function of twist angle (Fig. 4). The energy minimum is located at a twist angle of 0°, where enhanced SOMO overlap between monomers stabilizes the dianion by accommodating excess electrons within the “pancake” bond (cf.Fig. 1). Structures with twist angles between 5° and 15°, close to the mean stacking twist angle of 9° observed in A2 from molecular dynamics simulations, lie within approximately 1 kcal mol−1 of this energy minimum. In contrast, structures with twist angles in the range of 35° to 45°, which corresponds closely to the mean stacking twist angle (39°) observed in A1 from molecular dynamics simulations, are between 4–6 kcal mol−1 above the energy minimum, and therefore relatively less stable than pancake bonds in A2 dimer dianions.

Energies in kcal mol−1 of PDI dimer dianions with twist angles spanning from 0–90° relative to 0° computed at ωB97X-V/def2-SVPD level of theory.

We also calculated the energies associated with a monomer dianion + neutral pair and, separately, two singly-charged monomer ions, each pair at infinite separation. The former is less stable (+52 kcal mol−1) and the latter more stable (−27.5 kcal mol−1) relative to the most stable pancake-bound dimer dianions. The first result is consistent with the prevalence of pancake-bound dimer anions as monomer dianions are oxidized after chemical reduction. The latter is consistent with the inability to form dimer dianions through chemical reduction of A2 or A4 in 50 : 50 water : methanol solutions, in which the pi-peptides are fully separated, and highlights that a local energetic minimum associated with pancake bonding is facilitated by the preexisting aggregation in basic buffer solutions.

Photoreduction mechanism in sparged solutions

We conducted a series of ultrafast, nanosecond, and microsecond time-resolved transient absorption measurements in order to elucidate the charge-transfer mechanism underlying photoreduction of PDI-peptides in sparged buffer solutions following the photoexcitation of anthracene carboxylates at 385 nm, which promotes the donor to its S1 excited state. Transient absorption spectra of the donor D1 in buffered solutions in absence of PDI-peptides exhibited signatures of an anthracene S1 absorption with a 1.21 ns lifetime (Fig. S19a and S20); the disappearance of the singlet absorption is concomitant with the appearance of donor triplet state absorption at 426 nm. In the presence of the PDI-peptide A2, no signatures of photoreduction were observed on a ∼1 ns timescale (Fig. S21), revealing that the donor in its singlet excited state does not contribute significantly to electron transfer. Comparisons of the TAS spectroscopy of D1 and D3 on timescales less than 10 ns are provided in Fig. S22. These comparisons show that D3, like many 9,10-substituted anthracenes, has a much longer singlet lifetime than the asymmetrically, 9-substituted anthracenes (like D1); 9,10-disubstituted anthracenes generally have very high fluorescence QYs and very low ISC quantum yields compared with asymmetric structures.42 This is consistent with the less efficient charge transfer with D3 observed if its triplet rather than singlet state serves as the electron donor.

Microsecond TAS was used to investigate the role of the donor triplet state in the photoreduction of PDI-peptides; data obtained following the excitation of D3 and D1 in the presence of A1 are presented in Fig. 5a and S23, respectively. The triplet states of both donors live for several microseconds in sparged solutions following photoexcitation in the presence of a PDI-peptide electron acceptor. As highlighted in Fig. 5a, with buffered solutions of D3 + A1, we observe a broad depletion of the A1 ground-state absorption feature near 500–550 nm and photoinduced absorption at 730 nm corresponding with A1− that grow in concert with the decay of the D3 triplet (426 nm). A similar spectral evolution is observed for D1 in presence of A1 (Fig. S23). The temporal evolution of these features for D3 + A1 and D1 + A1 are plotted in Fig. 5b and S23b, respectively, and were fit using an effective first-order kinetic model. The triplet decay and concomitant appearance of A1− were observed to occur with 3.2 µs and 27.8 µs lifetimes for D1 and D3, respectively, when roughly the same concentration of A1 was in solution.

(a) Time-resolved microsecond transient absorption spectra for D3 + A1. (b) Constrained kinetic fit of triplet decay, and acceptor bleach and anion appearance for D3 + A1. (c) Linear dependence of τT−1 for D3 + A1 with increasing A1 concentrations.

In order to confirm the role of the donor triplet as the photoreductant, we examined the dependence of donor triplet lifetime with acceptor concentration. We examined D3 + A1 specifically because the intrinsic D3 triplet decay rate is relatively slow (compared to D1), such that the impact of charge-transfer quenching is easier to quantify using microsecond TA. Because the PDI-peptides are weakly assembled under basic conditions (as revealed by the characteristic exciton coupling in their UV-vis spectra, Fig. 2), we used the absorption (optical density) of the PDI-peptide component as a stand-in for relative acceptor concentration. Fig. 5c highlights the linear increase in the effective triplet decay rate constant with increase in A1 concentration for D3 + A1 in sparged buffer solutions, as anticipated with quenching.

Collectively, our TAS data (Fig. 5 and S19–S24) reveal convincingly that the donor triplet state serves as the electron donor for the photoreduction of PDI-peptides under sparged solution conditions. This mechanism was further verified using a triplet sensitization experiment described in the SI (Fig. S25 and S26). This mechanism is expected to underlie the “prompt” reduction of PDI-peptides in sparged aqueous solutions (Fig. 3a and b) immediately upon and during sample irradiation.

Photoreduction mechanism in aerated buffer solutions

Fig. 3 reveals significant differences in the charge-transfer kinetics for D1 + A1 in aerated vs. sparged buffer solutions. These differences can be attributed to photooxidation of D1, which leads to the generation of new optically and electro-active species. We presume the following impact on the electron-transfer mechanism: prolonged (several seconds) irradiation of D1 results in the build-up of photooxidation products that become cosolutes with additional triplet D1 generated at later times during irradiation. The D1 concentration (relative to PDI-peptides) is relatively high, such that significant photoproduct concentrations are expected. Thus, electron transfer from triplet D1 to a photoproduct may occur during the irradiation period to produce other long-lived redox active species, enabling the build-up of reducing species that may transfer electrons to PDI-peptides after the light source is extinguished.

Scheme 1 summarizes the established photochemistry of D1.43,44 As noted above, direct photoexcitation of D1 not only generates a short-lived singlet excited state (D1*, lifetime ∼1.21 ns) and a triplet state that lives several microseconds, D1* can also react with ground-state D1 to form a covalent “butterfly” dimer via a [4 + 4] cycloaddition (P1).43 Photoexcited D1 also produces anthranoxide (P3)43via the extrusion of CO. All of these processes occur with or without oxygen present, and, as describe above, spectral signatures of P1 and P3 are apparent for direct excitation of D1 in absence of PDI-peptides under either condition (Fig. S3), and thus could not be responsible for the differences in redox behavior observed (as explored in more detail in the SI). In the presence of oxygen, D1* can also form an endoperoxide species (P2).44 Furthermore, anthranoxide reacts with oxygen (a dark reaction) to produce anthraquinone (P4),44 which can react further to make bianthronyl (P5).44 We have considered the potential role of each photooxidation product in the photoreduction of PDI-peptides in aerated solutions, as described in more detail in the SI.

Based on those considerations, we speculate that P2 may serve as the electron-transfer intermediate in aerated solutions. Anthracene endoperoxide decomposition has been investigated under various conditions.45 Our speculative assignment is consistent with the literature precedent that endoperoxide O–O bond cleavage occurs by electron transfer to form radical anions,46 including carbanions that would be highly reducing.47 As our focus here is on the kinetics of pancake dimer formation, a detailed investigation of the redox pathways operative under aerated conditions is beyond the scope of this study and will be presented separately.

Mechanisms of dimer dianion generation

Whereas the mechanisms for generating singly-charged, radical “monomer monoanions” (e.g.A1− and A2−) is easily rationalized from the microsecond time-resolved data presented above, the mechanism for generating dimer dianions such as (A2)22− is not directly obvious and a few may be possible; these include:

1. Singly-reduced, unassembled PDI-peptide acceptors A2− are generated independently in solution by electron transfer from triplet D1 (or other photogenerated reductants in aerated solutions) and subsequently meet diffusively to form dimer dianions, as summarized through eqn (1).1a2A2 + 2e− → 2A2−1bA2− + A2− → (A2⋯A2)2−

2. A single aggregate of PDI-peptides is doubly reduced through interactions with more than one photogenerated reductant; a pancake dimer dianion is generated through subsequent electron transfer within an assembly to a common site (dimer) where pi-core dimerization stabilizes the electron pair, as expressed through eqn (2).2aA2⋯A2 + e− → A2−⋯A22bA2−⋯A2 + e− → A2−⋯A2−2cA2−⋯A2− → (A2⋯A2)2−

3. A single PDI-peptide within an aggregate is first doubly reduced to generate the monomer dianion (A22−); this species interacts with a neutral neighbor to form a dimer dianion, as expressed by eqn (3). The efficiency of this mechanism might benefit from spin conservation.3aA2⋯A2 + e− → A2−⋯A2A2−⋯A2 + e− → A22−⋯A2or3bA2−⋯A2 + e− → A2−⋯A2− → A22−⋯A23cA22−⋯A2 → (A2⋯A2)2−

Results from our chemical reduction experiments provide clues about the likelihood of each of these mechanisms: For example, spectral features associated with dimer dianion species are most significant under solution conditions that support aggregation of PDI-peptides; in water–ethanol solutions, diffusive interaction of A2− to produce (A2)22− does not appear to occur (therefore disfavoring the likelihood of the mechanism captured by eqn (1)), with the caveat that the stability of the pancake dimers could simply be limited by the surrounding solvent environment. Similarly, results of chemical reduction experiments disfavor the mechanism summarized by eqn (3): although the positions and breadths of spectral signatures of A2− are impacted by solution conditions (i.e. water–ethanol vs. basic aqueous solutions, Fig. S3), those of A22− are not, suggesting that the monomer dianion disaggregates in aqueous solution. This is further supported by circular dichroism UV-vis spectra presented in Fig. S29 and S30: comparison of the shapes and positions of absorption (Fig. S3) and CD (Fig. S29 and S30 for A3 and A4 redox species) features observed with chemical reduction in different solvent environments reveals that A22− has no chiral signature, which is consistent with a disaggregated state (in contrast, (A2)22−, a dimer/aggregate species by definition, has a weak chiral signature, as does A2−, which is embedded within a dimer/aggregate in solution). This can be rationalized by the fact that a near-complete conversion of A2 to A22− through reduction of aqueous assemblies would result in stacks of negatively charge chromophores with no net stabilization (c.f.Fig. 1, with a filled dimer HOMO and LUMO), such that electrostatic repulsion dominates to favor disaggregation. Further, under (photo)redox conditions that produce significant concentrations of A22−, it is unlikely that much if any neutral A2 would remain in solution to sustain the mechanism for dimer dianion generation expressed through eqn (3).

Time dependence in the photoinduced build-up of dimer dianions presented in Fig. 3 provide further clues about which mechanism(s) is most plausible. We first consider the mechanism for dimer dianion generation in aerated solutions. Under aerated conditions, A22− is formed very efficiently and relatively rapidly. We attribute this to the photooxidation of D1 that generates a longer-lived concentration of reducing species in solution than is possible under sparged conditions; for the latter, reduction kinetics are limited by the D1 triplet lifetime and acceptor-to-donor charge recombination. Spectral snapshots at selected time delays following irradiation of aerated solutions of A2 are presented in Fig. 6a alongside time-dependent cuts of selected features associated with each of the anion species in Fig. 6b; the shaded region of Fig. 6b highlights the period of LED irradiation, such that the first time point plotted in Fig. 6a represents the steady-state absorption of D1 + A2. After irradiation (18 s, at which time the LED is off), the spectrum of D1 has been depleted, the signatures of P1 (<350 nm) and P3 (450 nm) appear, and a distinct signature of A2− appears at 725 nm. By 22 s, the spectral signature of A22− first appears (peaked at 550 nm), increases in magnitude for the next 10–15 seconds, and then continues to grow modestly in size until 136 s. Notably, a weak feature of (A2)22− appears as early as 18 s, but is overtaken by the sharp vibronic feature associated with A22− that appears near 610 nm. After 136 s, a decay of A22− is accompanied by a simultaneous increase of A2− and (A2)22−, indicating that the monomer dianion undergoes oxidation under aerated conditions, consistent with our chemical reduction-oxidation experiments. Moreover, the absorption spectrum of (A2)22− is most significant by 262 s, coinciding with an increase of the A2− concentration.

The sequence in which these anion features appear and disappear suggests a reduction mechanism similar to eqn (3), yet this mechanism is inconsistent with steady-state absorption and circular dichroism UV-vis spectra presented in Fig. S29 and S30 and collected with chemical reduction that reveal that PDI-peptides disaggregate once reduced to monomer dianions. The spectral sequence observed from photoinduced reduction rather is more consistent with a mechanism expressed by eqn (4), in which the final step is the same as eqn (1b).4aA2⋯A2 + e− → A2−⋯A24bA2−⋯A2 + e− →→→ A22−4cA22− + O2 → A2−4dA2− + A2− → (A2⋯A2)2−

In this mechanism, PDI-peptides are first singly reduced to generate weakly aggregated monoanions, which are further reduced (nearly to completion, as signified with multiple arrows in eqn (4b)) to yield a solution of disassembled monomer dianion (A22−). Any A2− generated prior to complete reduction to A22− could combine to make dimer dianions per mechanism 1 or 2 (eqn (1) and (2)), which would explain the slight rise in dimer dianion absorption at 612 nm that lags the induction in A2− absorption at 725 nm and precedes the overwhelming appearance of spectral features for A22− (e.g. as observed shortly after 18 s). Interestingly, spectral signatures of both A2− and (A2)22− drop sharply after this initial rise and as the signature of A22− increases (542 nm). It is interesting that dimer dianion concentration does not grow more significantly with initial reduction of A2; we attribute this to the significantly long-lived concentration of photogenerated donors in aerated solutions, meaning that any A2− generated is more likely to encounter an electron donor to make A22− than another photogenerated A2− to form a dimer dianion. (In short, the pi-peptides are the limiting reagent for the photoinduced electron transfer, resulting in the overwhelming production of A22−.) With subsequent oxidation of A22−, the A2− and (A2)22− signatures both increase. The growing baseline from D1 photooxidation products makes it difficult to distinguish kinetics of interconversion between A2− and (A2)22− on the 100 s of seconds timescale. Nonetheless, this observation and our steady-state data support that the oxidation of disaggregated A22− generates A2− that subsequently reaggregate to form dimer dianions (eqn (4d)). Thus, the feature kinetics observed under aerated conditions suggest that the basic aqueous solution environment may provide a driving force for pi-peptide pancake dimer formation vis-à-vis aggregation.

The mechanism for dimer dianion formation under Ar-sparged conditions can be gleaned from the time sequence of spectral features observed in time-resolved UV-vis data (Fig. 3b). Spectral snapshots at selected times following irradiation of Ar-sparged solutions of A2 are presented in Fig. 7a alongside time-dependent cuts of selected features in Fig. 7b; the shaded region of Fig. 7b highlights the period of LED irradiation. Initially, absorbance growth at 725 nm indicates an initial generation of A2−, while the subsequent induction in absorption attributable to (A2)22− (612 nm) and then A22− (542 nm) mirror behavior of aerated solutions at the earliest times after illumination (Fig. 6b). From time-dependent cuts obtained with sparged solution conditions, it is clear that both the A2− and (A2)22− concentrations grow significantly during illumination, although the former rises faster than the latter, revealing that most of the dimer dianion concentration likely develops via mechanism 2 (i.e. viaeqn (2)) given that pi-peptides are already aggregated. Only a very weak spectral signature of the monomer dianion (A22−) is observed at best in sparged solutions. The delayed absorbance growth at 612 nm after the irradiation period coincides with a slight drop in the absorbance at 542 nm, corresponding with this small concentration of A22−, and would result from a mechanism captured by eqn (4) associated with photoreduction under aerated conditions. Nonetheless, the generation of (A2)22− in sparged solutions proceeds predominantly through direct interactions of A2− pairs in assemblies generated from electron transfer from triplet D1.

Temperature-dependence and activation energy of dianion generation and oxidation kinetics

We conducted time-resolved UV-vis experiments such as those highlighted in Fig. 3 under both aerated and sparged solution conditions at a series of temperatures (15–30 °C with 5 °C interval for sparged condition, 25–40 °C with 5 °C interval for aerated condition) in order to assess activation energies associated with the kinetics of reduced species under each solution condition.

Per the discussion above, the rate limiting step for observing a significant concentration of dimer dianions under aerated conditions is attributed to oxidation of A22−. We therefore examined the temperature-dependent oxidation rate for A22− in aerated and sparged solutions. It is noteworthy that we observe a temperature threshold at which A22− production (and, hence, dimer dianion signals) turns on significantly in aerated solutions: no significant build-up of the absorption feature at 542 nm was observed below room temperature. We believe this is sensible, given that kinetics of A22− generation is impacted by the presence of long-lived photogenerated reductant(s) formed through photooxidation of D1 or its photoproducts that persists after illumination ceases and whose generation may also be temperature sensitive. Thus, for aerated solutions, we only examined the temperature-dependence above room temperature. However, for sparged solutions, no turn-on threshold for dimer dianion generation was observed, with the formation of dimer dianion observed at 15 °C. As illustrated above, for sparged solutions, charge transfer starts from the D1 triplet excited state and no charge-transfer intermediate is involved.

Cuts associated with the decay of A22− absorption at various temperatures are plotted in Fig. S31. By fitting the rate constant for decay of A22− absorption via an Arrhenius analysis, as presented in Fig. 8a and b, the activation energy of oxidation are determined as 9 ± 3 kcal mol−1 and 4.9 ± 1.5 kcal mol−1, respectively. We note that these values agree within the large associated error bars, wherein the uncertains have the limitations noted above for each solution condition. It is notable that the prefactor for this process (A) is smaller under sparged vs. aerated conditions, which is consistent with the significantly reduced presence of oxidizing collision partners in sparged solutions.

Temperature dependence of the oxidation rate of A22− under (a) aerated condition and (b) sparged condition. (c) Temperature dependence of formation rate for dimer dianions from A2−.

Based on our analysis of spectral evolution in Fig. 6 and 7, the formation of dimer dianions from pairwise combination of monomer ions must be relatively prompt, given that the concentration of A2− does not build up during the interconversion of A22− to dimer dianions. We estimated the appearance rate of dimer dianions formed under sparged conditions based on the slope of the absorption at 612 nm as a function of time, which rises in slight lag to that of A2−. Fig. 8c presents an Arrhenius analysis of these rate constants with temperature. The activation energy for dimer dianion formation that is obtained from this analysis is ∼0.8 ± 1.4 kcal mole−1. Despite the sizable uncertainty in this value, resulting from the appreciable scatter in measured rates with temperature, this analysis highlights that the activation energy for pancake bond formation is relatively small within the preassembled pi-peptide structures (i.e. via eqn (2)).

Conclusions

In this work we have demonstrated the generation of pi-dimer dianions of peptide-functionalized perylene diimide through facile interaction or combination of singly reduced PDI-peptide (monomer anions). We find that peptide sequences that allow for greater molecular alignment between stacked PDI moieties supports the SOMO–SOMO orbital interaction needed to stabilize a pi-dimer pancake bond from two singly reduced PDI-peptide acceptors. Historically we have employed pH control over PDI peptides, observing in acid-triggered assemblies aligned or rotationally shifted co-facial aggregates depending on the amino acid sequence appended to the chromophore, with spectral signatures that correspond with charge-transfer J-aggregates or H-aggregates, respectively.25 That work has shown that simple sequence changes can allow for stimuli-responsive J-like and H-like aggregation, as well as monomers, dimers, and mixed aggregates, resulting in highly tunable absorption and emission responses over a broad range of wavelengths. Here, we expanded the modes of aggregation vis-à-vis the same peptide scaffold by changing the electron distribution on the chromophore through reduction. This demonstrates the extreme tunability of the PDI-peptide systems to support novel intermolecular electronic interactions between the pi-conjugated moieties.

Although other assembly techniques have been explored to couple or dimerize chromophores, including encapsulation or covalent scaffolding,48 the pi-peptide platform has the important benefit of enabling the decoupling and recoupling of PDI motifs to fully probe charge interactions and combination associated with pancake dimerization via a kinetic approach enabled by photoreduction experiments presented here. Further, the symmetric substitution with peptides favors a more eclipsed and less slipped stacking between PDIs, improving the pi-dimer bonding interactions. In contrast, previous work with covalently bound dimers fused the components into a static structure that could be fully reduced electrochemically to the dimer of PDI dianions,19 limiting the potential to observe and separate contributions to pancake dimer generation from preaggregated species vs. diffusively combining species, each of which are observed here under different redox conditions. A more recent report demonstrated that imide substitution can be used to stabilize PDI radical anions produced through reduction in dimethyl formamide solution that subsequently form pi-dimers upon heating.49 That work demonstrated a means to control and determine the strength of pi-bonds, with less insight on the mechanism of formation and how this is dictated by pi-core stacking geometry, which we control here with the symmetric peptide substitution.

Interestingly, our work reveals significant difference in photoinduced charge-transfer behavior with the D1 donor under Ar-sparged (oxygen free) and aerated solution conditions. Under sparged conditions, electron transfer is observed to occur from the donor triplet state, which limits reduction to the irradiation period given the short lifetime of the donor triplet state. In the presence of dissolved oxygen, we observe a much slower but more efficient build-up of pi-peptide anions and π-dimer dianions, extending past the irradiation period, indicating that the reduction occurs via an electron-transfer cascade in which an intermediate photogenerated acceptor species accepts electrons from the excited donor. We deduce that product of donor photooxidation serves as the electron “shuttle” that allows for the build up of a large photoreductant concentration that in turn improves the yield of dianionic pancake dimers. Similar multielectron cascades have recently reported using excess sacrificial donors in nonaqueous solution environments.50 Our findings suggest a similarly devised photoreduction cascade could be used to amplify charge transfer from donor to acceptor in support of multielectron reduction of an acceptor in aqueous solutions.
