Incidence and characteristics of poisonings in older adults with and without dementia: a matched cohort study using Swedish registry data
Authors: Ilsa R Wojt, Minh Tuan Hoang, Jonas W Wastesson, Kristina Johnell, Rose Cairns, Sara Garcia-Ptacek, Maria Eriksdotter, Edwin C K Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPoisonings are a global public health challenge, especially for older adults with dementia. This study examined the incidence, types and medications involved at the time of poisoning among older people with and without dementia in Sweden.MethodsUsing linked Swedish national registries, we conducted a matched cohort study of individuals ≥65 years with dementia diagnosed between 2014 and 2018, matched 1:3 to controls by age and sex, and followed until December 2019. Poisonings were identified via International Classification of Disease-10 codes. Incidence rates (IR) and hazard ratios (HR) were estimated using survival models.ResultsAmong 415 016 participants, dementia was associated with higher poisoning incidence (IR 100.3 vs 76.4 per 100 000 person-months; HR at 6 months 1.48). Risks were greatest for unintentional poisonings, toxic pharmaceutical effects and adverse drug reactions within 6 months postdiagnosis. Psychotropics, antithrombotics and cardiovascular medications were the most commonly used.DiscussionDementia is associated with poisoning risk, warranting cautious prescribing and prevention strategies.

Background

Poisonings are a public health challenge and a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. People with dementia are susceptible to poisonings, involving commonly prescribed or easily accessible substances, often leading to hospitalisation or death [2]. Older adults, particularly those with dementia, are disproportionately affected due to cognitive decline and a high prevalence of polypharmacy, increasing the risk of medication errors [3]. As dementia progresses, individuals can become more vulnerable through self-neglect and an impaired ability to recognise hazards [4]. In Sweden, where a quarter of the population are ≥65 years old, [5] approximately 150 000 individuals were living with dementia by 2023, with projections nearly doubling by 2050 [6, 7]. Sweden has a decentralised tax-funded universal healthcare system with high accessibility and low out-of-pocket costs. Prescription medications are subsidised, with an annual cost cap for patients [8]. During the study period, the cap was approximately 2900 SEK, after which medications became free of charge. Globally, dementia prevalence is expected to reach 153 million by 2050 [7], highlighting the urgency of addressing poisoning risks in this growing population.

As people with dementia are exposed to complex medication regimens, the safe and effective use of medicines is critical. Reducing medication-related harm is essential to preserve quality of life and delay or avoid admission to residential aged care. Minimising harm alleviates caregiver burden and supports an individual’s sense of agency, self-trust and self-esteem [9]. Few studies have described the nature and patterns of poisonings in older people with dementia.

As dementia rates rise, an investigation of the epidemiology and characteristics of poisonings in this population can inform targeted prevention strategies and public policy. Thus, this study aims to estimate and describe the: (i) incidence rate (IR), (ii) types and (iii) drug utilisation patterns associated with poisonings in older people with dementia compared to those without dementia in Sweden.

Methods

Study design, setting and data sources

This was a matched cohort study using linked Swedish national registries: the Total Population Register, the National Patient Register, the National Cause of Death Register, the National Prescribed Drug Register (NPDR) and the Swedish Longitudinal Integrated Database for Health Insurance and Labor Market Studies, as depicted in Appendix 1 in the Supplementary data. Register linkage was performed deterministically using Personal Identification Numbers, but it was pseudonymised for data protection. Individuals across Sweden aged ≥65 years old with and without an initial dementia diagnosis from 1 January 2014 to 31 December 2018 were included (Figure 1).

Study inclusion flowchart for selection of study participants (n = 415 016).

Participants

People with dementia

Individuals with dementia were identified using International Classification of Disease-10 (ICD-10) codes specific to dementia (Supplementary Appendix 2), or a recorded dispensing of an antidementia medication (ATC code N06D: memantine, donepezil, galantamine or rivastigmine). The date of the dementia diagnosis (index date) was the registered date, whichever came first.

Matching and eligibility

There were 105 032 older people diagnosed with dementia from 2014 to 2018; individuals with dementia diagnosed prior to January 2014 were not eligible for inclusion. (Figure 1). Individuals with dementia were matched to persons without dementia by birth year and sex at the index date without replacement in a 1:3 ratio. Therefore, one person with dementia could have multiple controls, and each control was only included once.

People poisoned within 3 months prior to the index date were excluded to address potential reverse causation bias. People diagnosed with dementia on their date of death were excluded to ensure incident diagnoses. The start date was chosen due to changes in residential care data documentation.

Variables

Poisoning cases

Poisoning cases were defined as: ‘an individual’s medical or socially unacceptable condition as a consequence of being under the influence of an exogenous substance in a dose too high for the person concerned’ [10]. As such, poisonings were identified and classified as individuals with any of the following ICD-10 codes for poisonings: by pharmaceuticals (T36–T50), substances other than pharmaceuticals (T51–T65) and external codes for unintentional poisonings (X40–X49), intentional poisonings (X60–X69) and adverse drug reactions (ADRs; Y40–Y59) (Supplementary Appendix 2). ADRs (Y40–Y59) were included to capture the full spectrum of medication-related harm and to align with our operational definition of poisoning. Given ICD-10 codes lack granularity in distinguishing individual medications, it was not possible to accurately determine which specific drugs were implicated in poisonings. Consequently, we described the medications used at the time of poisoning.

A single participant could have multiple poisoning events, such as repeat poisonings, and multiple classifications associated with one admission, e.g. an unintentional poisoning and an ADR. Individuals were followed from the index date until their first poisoning outcome, emigration, new dementia diagnosis (for the non-dementia group only), death or study end, whichever came first.

Covariates

Sociodemographic factors included age at the index date, sex, cohabitation status (living alone vs cohabiting), living arrangement (nursing home vs community dwelling) and education. Highest attained education level was categorised into compulsory education (years 1−9), upper secondary (years 10−12) and tertiary (college, university or higher vocational education). Chronic comorbidities were summarised using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), [11] applying the algorithm developed by Quan et al. [12], which calculates a weighted sum of diagnosed chronic conditions prior to the dementia diagnosis, excluding dementia itself. Medication use within the 3 months preceding the index date was classified according to the second level of the ATC code. The selection of comorbidities and drug classes included in the analysis was guided by clinical relevance, prevalence in older adults, and the potential to influence poisoning risk.

Statistical methods

Baseline characteristics were reported using standard descriptive statistics, including n (%), means (SD) and medians (interquartile range, IQR). IR was calculated for poisonings occurring after the index date for each participant. For each outcome, we reported number of events, total time at risk (100 000 person-months), IR per 100 000 person-months (95% confidence interval, CI) and hazard ratios (HR). IRs were calculated as the number of events divided by total person-time at risk, expressed per 100 000 person-months with 95% confidence intervals. In addition, Kaplan–Meier methods were used to estimate the cumulative incidence of poisoning over time among people with and without dementia. To estimate HR (95% CI) of each outcome between the dementia and non-dementia group, we employed flexible parametric survival regressions due to violation of the proportional hazard assumption. Two models were constructed: Model 1 was unadjusted, and Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, cohabitation status, education, living arrangement, number of drugs used during 3 months before index date and comorbidities. A directed acyclic graph was used to identify confounders for estimating the total effect of dementia on poisoning risk (Supplementary Appendix 4). All covariates were time-fixed and proportional hazards were assumed for these confounders. For Supplementary Appendix 3, we report the model-estimated average HR for dementia across the full follow-up period. Because flexible parametric survival models allow for time-varying effects, this estimate represents the overall effect rather than the HR at any specific time point. Time-specific HRs are presented separately in Supplementary Appendix 6. Given that HRs varied over time, we estimated time-specific HRs at multiple follow-up intervals postindex date.

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on living arrangement as residing in residential care may confer a higher poisoning risk [13]. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the impact of different wash-out periods, excluding people with poisoning events within six and 12 months prior to the index date. All statistical analyses were performed with STATA version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and R version 4.4.1. This study followed the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely collected health Data (RECORD) statement (Supplementary Appendix 7).

Ethics

The Swedish Ethical Review Authority approved the research ethics for this study (decision number 2016/1001-31/4, 2020-03525 and 2021-02004).

Results

Study population characteristics

Among 415 016 people, (103 943 people with dementia and 311 073 people without), those with dementia were more likely to live alone or in residential care, have lower education and have more comorbidities (Table 1). The most common comorbidities in people with dementia were hypertension (n = 58 099, 55.9%), ischaemic heart disease (n = 29 018, 27.9%) and atrial fibrillation (n = 22 269, 21.4%). People with dementia were more likely to take more medications than those without dementia (≥5 medications: 44.9% vs 36.4%; ≥10 medications: 11.2% vs 8.0%). The most used medication classes at index date in people with dementia were cardiovascular system (ATC code C), blood and blood-forming organ (ATC code B) and nervous system (ATC code N).

Incidence of poisonings

During the follow-up period, 2889 people with dementia and 8428 people without dementia experienced poisoning. The IR of any kind of poisoning was higher in people with dementia than those without [100.3 per 100 000 person-months (95% CI, 96.7–104.0) vs 76.4 per 100 000 person-months (95% CI, 74.8–78.1)] (Table 2, Figure 2).

Poisoning type

People with dementia had a consistently higher IR of poisoning across most subtypes of poisonings, compared to those without dementia (Table 2). This was most pronounced in unintentional (pharmaceutical) poisonings [9.4 per 100 000 person-months (95% CI, 8.4–10.6) vs 3.8 per 100 000 person-months (95% CI, 3.5–4.2)], toxic effects of pharmaceuticals [22.6 per 100 000 person months (95% CI, 20.9–24.4) vs 14.4 per 100 000 person-months (95%CI 13.7–15.1)] and ADRs [ADRs; 82.0 per 100 000 person-months (95% CI, 78.8–85.4) vs 65.2 per 100 000 person-months (95% CI, 63.8–66.8)]. For non-pharmaceutical poisonings and intentional self-harm, the IRs were similar.

Risk of poisoning and repeat poisonings

Individuals with dementia had a higher HR of any poisoning compared to controls in the first 6 months (HR = 1.48, 95% CI, 1.39–1.58) (Figure 3, Supplementary Appendix 6). This HR decreased over time and reversed after 24 months with a time-varying HR below 1. Similar trends were observed across poisoning types, with HRs of ADRs (HR = 1.46, 95% CI 1.37–1.57) and intentional poisonings (HR = 1.12, 95% CI 0.81–1.54) being higher in those with dementia compared to controls in the first 6 months and decreasing over time (Figure 3, Supplementary Appendix 6). Compared to controls, people with dementia had higher HR of unintentional poisoning for the first 3 years (HR at 6 months: 2.48, 95% CI 1.96–3.13).

HRs of different types of poisoning between older people with and without dementia.

Individuals were unlikely to have a repeat poisoning after their first. In people without dementia, 0.4% of participants experienced a second poisoning, compared to 0.3% of people with dementia. ADRs were the most common first repeat poisoning (Supplementary Appendix 8).

Medication use

Drug utilisation patterns on day of poisoning were substantially different between people with and without dementia (Supplementary Appendix 9). Among those with dementia, psychotropic drug use was higher across all categories. On the day of any poisoning event, 60.3% of individuals with dementia had been exposed to psychotropics, compared with 43.8% of controls. This difference was highest in cases of intentional self-harm (88.5% vs 81.1%) and pharmaceutical toxic effects (69.7% vs 57.8%). Antidepressant (39.7% vs 20.4%), antipsychotic (14.9% vs 2.6%) and anxiolytic (18.8% vs 11.9%) use was considerably more common in people with dementia on the day of poisoning. Hypnotic use was comparable but highest in intentional poisonings irrespective of dementia status (57.7% vs 64.0%). Use of cardiovascular medications, including ACE-inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (42.2% vs 47.8%), beta-blockers (47.2% vs 54.0%), calcium channel blockers (19.9% vs 24.5%) and diuretics (38.7% vs 49.6%), was lower in dementia across all categories. Supplementary Appendix 10 lists the top 20 medications used on the day of any poisoning among individuals with dementia, led by paracetamol and furosemide.

Sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses found that in residential care individuals without dementia, the rates of ADRs initially decreased, yet resurged in frequency in later years of life (Supplementary Appendix 5). In comparison, ADRs in people with dementia steadily decreased with time. Sensitivity analyses found alternative wash out periods impacted minimally on poisoning incidence (Supplementary Appendix 11).

Discussion

By analysing Swedish registry data, our study contributes country-specific estimates of poisoning incidence in people with dementia, helping address critical gaps needed to inform prevention strategies. Our study found a higher incidence of poisoning in people with dementia compared to controls. This may reflect functional and cognitive impairments of dementia increasing vulnerability to medication errors and unsafe substance exposure. This aligns with prior research showing elevated poisoning risks during hospitalisations for medication-related harms in this population [14]. We observed a similar HR to prior research of 1.4 for unintentional poisonings, [15] although previous studies classified poisonings as a subset of all injuries, which limits direct comparability. Conversely, other studies describe lower rates of poisoning-related hospitalisations associated with dementia [16]. While international comparisons are limited by differences in registries, trends suggest psychotropic medications are particularly concerning in Nordic countries, and that globally, current medication safety strategies inadequately address unintentional poisonings in older adults [17–19].

In our study, poisonings were more often unintentional among older adults, with people with dementia at a particularly high risk of unintentional poisonings, toxic effects of pharmaceuticals and ADRs. This contrasts with prior Swedish research describing a higher prevalence of intentional poisonings in this population [20]. We found that intentional poisonings contributed minimally to overall incidence and declined over time following a dementia diagnosis, with repeated events uncommon and predominantly ADRs. Methodological differences likely explain the discrepancy, including outcome definitions (Möller et al.[20] examined poisonings as a subset within injuries), substance characterisation and changes in prescribing practices. Our findings align with international research showing that older adults, especially those with dementia, are more likely to experience unintentional poisonings [2]. Nonetheless, intentional poisonings may be underreported due to coding, stigma and challenges in determining intent. Prior studies suggest intentional poisoning is more common in dementia than in controls, underscoring the importance of mental health assessment, especially in countries like Sweden where poisoning remains a common suicidal means [1].

People with dementia had an increased poisoning risk within 6 months of diagnosis, with the risk declining over time. This may reflect the early prescribing of psychoactive medications in response to behavioural and psychological symptoms, alongside continued cardiometabolic and anticholinergic prescriptions [21, 22]. Over time, deprescribing is encouraged, reducing risk [23]. Factors including care setting, care trajectories and survival time may also contribute, as we explored. Increasing use of pharmacy-prepared dose-dispensing systems (e.g. ApoDoS) may further mitigate risk by simplifying regimens and limiting access to bulk medications [24]. Despite many commonly used agents being non-acutely toxic, polypharmacy remains challenging. We found that high hazards of polypharmacy in dementia conveying higher associated poisoning risk. Medication reviews, simplified dosing regimens and pharmacy-prepared dose-dispensing systems may improve safety.

This study’s strengths include describing medications used on the day of poisoning. Psychotropic medications were commonly taken at the time of poisoning, particularly in people with dementia. Older adults are susceptible to psychotropic adverse effects including sedation, confusion, falls and memory impairment, due to age-related changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, as well as the prevalence of polypharmacy [25]. Antidepressants were the most commonly used subclass at the time of poisoning, consistent with previous findings [26]. Of particular concern are anticholinergic variants, which can exacerbate cognitive impairment and drowsiness, also posing toxicity risks in overdose.

Strengths and limitations

This study benefits from a large, nationwide cohort of older adults and comprehensive data on medical diagnoses and medications from national registers, thereby minimising attrition and recall bias. However, dementia is underreported in these national registers, with only 40.2% sensitivity, [27] though specificity remains high. Our use of incident dementia cases to define ‘dementia-free’ controls may have introduced bias, as these controls are likely healthier than the general population, potentially underestimating poisoning incidence in people with dementia. We attempted to mitigate this with a lag-time period. The registers we used capture only hospital-coded events and did not include GP primary data. Therefore, under-ascertainment of community-managed poisonings and dementia diagnoses is possible and may be attributed to differences if people with dementia are more likely to be admitted when a poisoning is suspected due to diagnostic uncertainty, communication difficulties and safety concerns in this population. Individuals with dementia may have more frequent hospital contact, which could increase the likelihood of a poisoning diagnosis being recorded; we acknowledge this as a potential source of detection bias, although it does not affect our time-to-event analysis. The NPDR does not capture over-the-counter medications, medications used in hospitals and medications provided from nursing home supply rooms. Further, combination products including more than one active ingredient are only counted as one medication in the NPDR. Thus, medication involvement in poisonings may be underestimated. Moreover, while prescription fills are recorded, adherence remains unknown. Lastly, ICD-10 lacks specificity in poisoning codes, limiting substance-specific information. We described medications used on the day of poisoning yet lack any way of ascertaining their direct involvement in a poisoning event.

Conclusion

This study found that older adults with dementia have a significantly higher incidence of poisoning compared to those without dementia, particularly in the first 6 months following diagnosis. The greatest risk difference was observed for unintentional poisonings involving medications. High risk medications, including psychotropics, antithrombotics and cardiovascular medications, were most used on the day of poisoning. Given the limited data on poisoning rates in people with dementia, these findings contribute important evidence to a growing body of research highlighting their vulnerability. Future studies should evaluate the impact of interventions like deprescribing and medication reviews to reduce poisoning risk and promote safer medication use in this population.
