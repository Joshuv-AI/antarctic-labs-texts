Negative and Nonlinear Association Between Particulate Matter and Cardiorespiratory Fitness Among Children and Adolescents: The Mediating Effect of Adiposity
Authors: Feng Zhang, Cun‐jian Bi, Ya‐ru Guo, Yuan Liu, Peng‐wei Sun, Jun Hong, Yan‐yan Hu, He Liu, Li‐xin Hu, Yu‐shan Zhang, Ya‐jun Chen, Xiao‐jian Yin, Xin‐ming Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) has been investigated as a broad, holistic health indicator for children and adolescents. The adverse effects of particulate matter on lung function and cardiovascular health have been widely reported, whereas the association between particulate matter and CRF and its potential pathways remains unclear. The study aims to estimate the association between particulate matter and CRF in children and adolescents and the mediating effect of adiposity on this association.MethodsA multistage random cluster sampling method was used to obtain a nationwide sample of 99 995 children and adolescents in China. Daily exposure to particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter ≤2.5 and <10 μm surrounding schools was extracted from the China High Air Pollutants data sets. As surrogates of long‐term exposures, we used 3‐year average (2013–2015) concentrations before the study for analyses. CRF was estimated by a 20‐m shuttle run test.ResultsPer 10‐μg/m3 increase in particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter ≤2.5 and <10 μm was associated with −0.12 (95% CI, −0.13 to −0.10) and −0.09 (95% CI, −0.10 to −0.08) mL/kg per min lower CRF levels. Exposure‐response curves suggested a nonlinear association of particulate matter with CRF (all P nonlinear <0.001). Waist/height ratio mediated 15.97% (95% CI, 13.99%–18.27%) and 16.15% (95% CI, 13.92%–18.75%) of the association between particulate matter with aerodynamic diameter ≤2.5 and <10 μm, respectively, and CRF among children and adolescents (all P<0.001).ConclusionsThere is a negative and nonlinear association between long‐term exposure to particulate matter and CRF among children and adolescents, and this association may be partially explained by adiposity. Cohort studies are needed to further validate the findings.

Although the concentration of particulate matter (PM) has experienced 10 years of continuous decline in China, it still exceeded the World Health Organization's annual air quality guideline 1 of 5 μg/m3. As 1 of the most influential environmental risk factors among air pollutants, PM has been reported to be related to various adverse health outcomes, such as obesity, 2 lung cancer, 3 hypertension, 4 and metabolic syndrome. 5 Furthermore, PM can enter the respiratory system, and trigger inflammatory responses in the lungs, leading to the release of proinflammatory cytokines and the generation of reactive oxygen species. This sustained oxidative stress damages the alveolar‐capillary barrier and impairs gas exchange efficiency. 6 , 7

Children and adolescents are in a critical period of cardiopulmonary function development, which may be more affected by PM. 8 As a core element of physical fitness, cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) refers to the ability of the cardiorespiratory system to sustain muscle activity by supplying oxygen to muscles during exercise. 9 The effects of CRF in children and adolescents on various types of health will continue into adulthood, leading to an increased risk of various types of chronic cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and cancer in adulthood. 10 , 11 Recently, CRF has been investigated as a broad, holistic health indicator for children and adolescents. 9 , 12 , 13

Among the limited research focusing on the association between PM and CRF, the conclusions are not always consistent. Most of them showed that children in high‐pollution areas had significantly lower CRF compared with those in low‐ and moderate‐pollution areas. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Nevertheless, a cross‐sectional study conducted in Bogotá, Colombia, suggested that CRF did not differ among children attending schools exposed to high compared with low levels of PM. 18 Besides, these studies were conducted in restricted areas, such as 1 to 2 cities with limited participants or simple comparative analyses. Finally, the association between PM and CRF and the potential pathways remains unclear.

On the basis of the above, the present study aimed to: (1) estimate the relationship between PM and CRF in Chinese children and adolescents; and (2) explore the mediating effect of adiposity indexes on the association between PM and CRF.

METHODS

The data used in this study were available on request from the authors. All study procedures had been approved by the Human Experiment Ethics Committee of East China Normal University (approval No. HR2016/12055). Informed consent was obtained from both participants and their parents. All information was sufficiently anonymized.

Study Design and Participants

The present study was a cross‐sectional study conducted in 2015 to 2016 among 26 of 34 provinces of China. A multistage random cluster sampling method was used to obtain a nationwide sample of 127 schools from 43 cities (Figure 1). The specific procedure for selecting participants is shown in Figure S1 and has been described elsewhere. 19 In short, after selecting cities from the 7 administrative regions of China, considering urban and rural, middle and high school distribution, 3 to 4 schools were selected from each city, and classes were randomly selected from each grade in each school. All the students in the class without physical disability and who could complete the tests were invited as participants. A total of 107 413 children and adolescents completed the survey; after excluding 7418 extreme data (6.91%), 99 995 participants (93.32%) were obtained for analyses.

Sampling map of 99 995 children and adolescents in China.

Estimation of PM

The China High Air Pollutants data set 20 , 21 , 22 was used to extract daily exposure to PM ≤2.5 μm in aerodynamic diameter (PM2.5) and PM ≤10 μm in aerodynamic diameter (PM10) around schools using a tree‐based machine learning model with a spatial resolution at 0.01° (≈1 km). This model predicted PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations at each geocoded address based on information from meteorology, satellite remote sensing, ground‐based observations, and multiresolution emission inventory with high predictive performance (coefficients of determination, 0.80–0.92; root‐mean‐square errors, 7.99–290 μg/m3 daily). 23 The detailed method information can be found on the China High Air Pollutants data set websites (https://weijing‐rs.github.io/product.html). As surrogates of long‐term exposures, we used 3‐year average (2013–2015) concentrations before the study for analyses.

Estimation of CRF

The 20‐m shuttle run test was conducted following the FitnessGram protocol (Cooper Institute for Aerobics Research, 1992), which has been used worldwide to estimate CRF. The detailed measurement procedure has been described elsewhere. 19 , 24 , 25 In short, the participants run back and forth between the 2 poles at a distance of 20 m, starting at a speed of 8.0 km/h, increasing to 9.0 km/h after 1 stage (≈ 1 min) thereafter, and then increasing by 0.5 km/h every stage. There were a series of audible signals during the running process, and participants needed to complete a 20‐round run for each signal, which was marked as 1 lap. The test ends when the participants cannot keep up with audible signals or are exhausted. The last lap completed (not necessarily the level stopped at) was recorded as the result. Speed at the last complete stage (km/h) was obtained according to the recorded laps. On the basis of the speed at the last complete stage, peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak, mL/kg per min) was estimated using an equation developed by Leger et al 26 (formula), which is an internationally recognized method for estimating VO2peak (CRF). S: speed at the last completed stage (km/h); Age: age at the last birthday.

Covariates

A self‐administered questionnaire was used to collect related covariates. After the trained staff explained the research purpose and the filling requirements for the questionnaire, the participants completed the questionnaire by themselves. The staff was present throughout the whole process, and the questionnaires were collected on the spot after completion.

Age (years), sex (boys versus girls), nationality (Han versus other), region (south of China versus north of China), and residential district (rural versus urban) were collected by the self‐administered questionnaire. Socioeconomic status (SES), which reflects family economic conditions, was calculated from annual per capita household income (≤2000, 2001–5000, 5001–8000, and >8000 Chinese Yuan), family educational level (primary school, junior middle school, senior high school/technical secondary school, or college or above), and family occupation status (civil servant/physician/teacher), and the detailed calculation procedure has been described in our previous studies. 27 Gross domestic product (GDP) per capita was obtained from the China Statistical Yearbook 28 for every city. Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) is the most used indication of vegetation density and health in greenspace studies. 29 , 30 Derived from Copernicus Sentinel‐2 satellite images with a high resolution (10×10 m), NDVI was calculated by 2 wavelengths of near‐infrared light and red light with the formula as: (near‐infrared light–red light)/(near‐infrared light+red light). NDVI values range from −1 to 1, with close to 1 indicating lush vegetation, close to 0 indicating bare soil or water, and close to −1 indicating nonvegetation cover.

Height (to the nearest 0.1 m), weight (to the nearest 0.1 kg), and waist circumference (WC) were measured using standardized protocols 31 , 32 by trained staff 3 times with participants wearing light clothing and taking off shoes. The average of 3 measures was considered as the results. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by weight (kg)/height (m)2. Waist/height ratio was calculated by WC (m)/height (m).

Using DAGitty V3.1 software (www.dagitty.net), a directed acyclic graph was conducted to obtain potential confounders (Figure S2). As a result, the following variables were retained: age, sex, residential district, nationality, GDP per capita, SES, NDVI, and elevation.

Statistical Analysis

Absolute frequencies and relative frequencies in percentages are described for categorical variables. The minimum, the maximum, the median, and the interquartile range or mean and SD were used to describe numerical variables. Missing values were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations with predictive mean matching. Associations between PM exposure and CRF were examined using multiple linear regression models. CRF was treated as a continuous dependent variable, and PM was included as the main independent variable. The models were adjusted for age, sex, residence, nationality, GDP, SES, NDVI, elevation, and school. All models were fitted using the ordinary least squares method, assuming a Gaussian error distribution with an identity link function. The underlying assumptions of the linear regression models included linearity of associations between predictors and the outcome, independence of observations, homoscedasticity of residuals, approximate normality of residuals, and absence of perfect multicollinearity among covariates.

To examine potential effect modification by age, an interaction term between PM and age group was included in the multiple linear regression model. The significance of the PM×age group interaction was used to evaluate whether the association between PM exposure and CRF differed across age groups. Stratified analyses were also performed to investigate whether participants' age (7–12 and 13–18 years), sex (boys or girls), residential district (urban or rural), or elevation (>1000 or <1000 m) modified the results.

Mediation analysis was conducted to examine whether adiposity indexes (BMI/WC/waist/height ratio) mediated the association between PM exposure and CRF. Two multiple linear regression models were fitted: a mediator model with BMI as the dependent variable and PM as the main independent variable, and an outcome model with CRF as the dependent variable, including both PM and BMI as predictors. Both models were adjusted for age, sex, residence, nationality, GDP, SES, NDVI, elevation, and school. The indirect (mediated) effect, direct effect, and total effect were estimated using the causal mediation analysis framework implemented in the mediate function, with nonparametric bootstrapping (1000 simulations) to obtain CIs.

The “exposure‐response” relationship between PM2.5, PM10, and CRF was conducted by a restricted cubic spline regression model. Graphs show β and 95% CIs according to PM2.5, PM10 adjusted for age, sex, residential district, nationality, GDP per capita, SES, NDVI, elevation, and school. Data were fitted with 4 knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of PM2.5 and PM10 (reference is the median). Solid lines indicate β, and shadow shapes indicate 95% CIs.

All statistical analyses were performed in R software (version 4.4.04, R Development Core Team). P<0.05 for a 2‐tailed test denoted statistical significance.

Mediating Effect of Adiposity Indexes on the Association Between PM and CRF for N=99 995 Participants

Estimates from mediation analyses in Table 4 suggest that waist/height ratio mediated 15.97% (95% CI, 13.99%–18.27%) and 16.15% (95% CI, 13.92%–18.75%) on the associations between PM2.5 and PM10 and CRF (all P<0.0001). We also observed that 4.42% (95% CI, 3.31%–5.59%) and 5.23% (95% CI, 4.04%–6.53%) of the effects were mediated by levels of BMI on the associations between PM2.5 and PM10 and CRF, respectively (all P<0.0001). A similar mediating effect was also found in WC.

Sensitivity Analyses

Several sensitivity analyses were also performed to testify to the robustness of the results. First, we extracted participants with age‐ and sex‐specific CRF z‐scores >−2 and <2 to conduct the generalized linear regression models (Table S3). Second, we also conducted the association between PM and CRF after deleting participants with missing values (Table S4 and Figure S4). Third, we checked whether the association between PM2.5, PM10, and CRF was modified by participants' age, sex, residential district, and elevation by performing subgroup‐level analyses (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

The present study estimated the association between PM2.5, PM10, and CRF using a nationwide sample of 99 995 children and adolescents from China. We found a negative association of PM with CRF, and this association was mediated by adiposity indexes, with waist/height ratio mediating approximately a quarter of this association.

Our results showed that long‐term exposure to PM was associated with lower CRF. Similar to our results, the existing limited studies focusing on this association also reported a negative relationship. For example, Gao et al reported that with per 10‐μg/m3 increase in PM10 annual mean, the CRF was estimated to reduce 1.53 mL/kg per min, which was larger than the results of our study (−0.42 mL/kg per min; 95% CI, −1.79 to −1.54 mL/kg per min). 14 Nevertheless, the range of PM10 concentrations involved in their study was 45 to ≈65 μg/m3, in which range the CRF in our study also experienced a larger reduction (the PM10 ranges from 44.85 to ≈178.39 μg/m3 in our study, and the “exposure‐response” curves showed that CRF reduced sharply at ≈60 to ≈100 μg/m3). Therefore, there is an urgent need for policy intervention to reduce air pollution levels in China.

The “exposure‐response” curves of the association between PM and CRF appear to be nonlinear, with a relative plateau at high PM concentrations. To our knowledge, “exposure‐response” curves of the association of PM with CRF were rarely reported. Although with different outcomes, Guo et al 33 found out that lung function decreased slightly more sharply up to a PM2.5 concentration range of 20 to ≈25 μg/m3, and decreased slightly more slowly thereafter, with a similar overall trend to our findings (the PM2.5 ranges from 22.09 to ≈133.79 μg/m3 in our study, and the “exposure‐response” curves showed that CRF reduced sharply at ≈25 to ≈75 μg/m3). They speculate this may be because the participants were saturated at high PM2.5 concentrations, causing biological adaptation and making lung function growth less sensitive. 34 Besides, this also might be attributed to the reduced outdoor activity because in times of heavy pollution, the Chinese government, the China Meteorological Administration, and the media urge people to stay indoors if necessary to reduce the damage caused by air pollution. The results once again illustrate the importance and urgency of reducing air pollution levels.

Our results suggested that the CRF of girls (versus boys) experienced a larger decrease with long‐term exposure to PM, which was similar to the result of Gao et al. 14 Previous studies found that PM2.5 exposure had a more serious impact on the lung function of boys because they tend to spend more time outdoors and are more likely to be exposed to air pollutants 34 or no significant difference between boys and girls, 35 whereas others have reported evidence of greater vulnerability among girls. 36 , 37 Besides, children and adolescents residing in high‐elevation areas were found to be more vulnerable to the detrimental effects of PM2.5 exposure on CRF. Indeed, existing literature indicates that long‐term PM2.5 exposure is associated with increased cardiorespiratory morbidity. 38 Meanwhile, residents at high altitudes may exhibit altered baseline cardiorespiratory function—such as erythrocytosis—because of chronic hypoxic conditions. 39 This physiological adaptation could potentially predispose them to heightened susceptibility to chronic inflammation or vascular dysfunction triggered by PM2.5 exposure.

Evidence has indicated that exposure to PM is associated with childhood risk for overweight and obesity. 40 Besides, overweight and obese children (both defined by BMI and WC) exhibit significantly lower CRF compared with their normal‐weight peers. 25 , 41 Our results confirmed the mediating role of obesity on the PM‐CRF relationship. Existing evidence suggests that obesity is significantly associated with declined lung function and elevated inflammatory mediators. 42 Moreover, when exposed to air pollutants, such as PM, individuals with obesity exhibit more pronounced lung function impairment, 43 and this effect may be linked to a chronic low‐grade inflammatory state associated with obesity, which increases pulmonary sensitivity to pollutants. 44 Consequently, air pollution contributes to obesity through mechanisms, such as oxidative stress and adipose tissue inflammation, whereas obesity further exacerbates the burden on the cardiopulmonary system. 45

There are many limitations in this article. The first limitation of the study lies in the cross‐sectional design, and cohort studies are needed to further confirm the association of PM with CRF. The second limitation is that residual confounding may persist because of unmeasured lifestyle and environmental factors, such as diet, physical activity, and indoor air quality. Future research should collect more detailed data on these variables and consider designs, like sibling or twin studies, to better account for unobserved backgrounds.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study revealed the negative and nonlinear association between long‐term exposure to PM and CRF among children and adolescents in China. Adolescents, girls, and participants living in rural and high‐altitude areas experienced a larger decline in CRF than their counterparts. Results of mediation analyses suggest that the association may be partially explained by adiposity indexes. These results underscore the need for targeted air quality interventions, and studies are recommended to clarify causality and explore additional mediators, enabling more precise interventions for vulnerable populations.
