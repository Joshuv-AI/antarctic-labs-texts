Piloting an intraoral pH device to track gastroesophageal reflux in adults
Authors: Shijia Hu, Clement Wei Ming Lai, Yu Fan Sim, Alex Yu Sen Soh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated in vivo, an intraoral device against the gold standard esophageal device for gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) monitoring. Subjects scheduled for a catheter-based esophageal pH/impedance testing at a gastroenterology clinic were recruited. They were screened using the GerdQ questionnaire, demographics and dental conditions recorded. A prototype intraoral device, consisting of a Bravo™ capsule embedded in an Essix-style retainer fabricated for each subject, monitored intraoral pH. Concurrently, subjects underwent 24-h esophageal pH-impedance monitoring. A self-administered survey elicited the comfort and acceptance of both devices. The study recruited ten adult subjects (23 to 60-years-old) with a median GerdQ score of 9.5 corresponding to a 79% likelihood of GERD. Subjects with severe dental erosion had significantly (p < 0.05) higher acid exposure time and more non-meal reflux events. No adverse events were associated with the intraoral device while one was recorded for the esophageal device. The intraoral device was significantly more comfortable to place, more comfortable to wear, and interfered less with daily routine compared to the esophageal device. Accuracy of the intraoral device ranged between 86.15% and 37.82%. Being more tolerable than traditional esophageal pH monitoring, intraoral pH monitoring may be a useful adjunct for the diagnosis and management of GERD.

Introduction

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is one of the most common gastrointestinal disease with a pooled global prevalence of around 14%, or more than a billion sufferers worldwide1. Among this population, it was suggested that only 10% of people with GERD were managed appropriately2.

Acid reflux can injure the lining of the esophagus and cause considerable damage. Resulting in esophageal manifestations such as reflux esophagitis, peptic stricture and even metaplastic changes such as Barrett’s esophagus and adenocarcinoma3. In a subgroup of patients with laryngopharyngeal reflux, stomach content can reach the pharynx, larynx and even the oral cavity4. This can result in extraesophageal manifestations such as cough, laryngitis, pharyngitis, sinusitis5 and tooth erosion6. GERD was found to severely affected quality of life in patients, resulting in lower scores across all dimensions such as physical, emotional, social, and school7.

Unfortunately, the diagnosis of GERD remains a complex conundrum as there is currently no single test that can either rule out or confirm the diagnosis8. The clinician has to employ all available tools to determine if reflux was pathologic. Of the currently available studies, esophageal pH combined with multichannel intraluminal impedance monitoring is the established gold standard for diagnosis9. However, these studies require the placement of a nasopharyngeal catheter for 24- to 48-h of continuous monitoring. Moreover, the procedure to place the monitor is invasive and uncomfortable to undergo. For the some patients, this may necessitate some form of sedation10 which carries with it increased risks11 and may affect the diagnostic accuracy of the tests12.

Previous studies have found clinical signs of dental erosion in 24% to 98% of subjects with GERD6,13,14. As such, the oral cavity appears to be a viable area for the monitoring of acid reflux. The development of an intraoral pH monitor can provide a less invasive study to aid in the diagnosis and management of GERD.

This study aimed to pilot in vivo, the use of wireless intraoral pH monitors for the diagnosis and management of subjects with GERD. It evaluated the limitations and advantages of the intraoral device and accuracy of the intraoral device against the esophageal device in adults.

Materials and methods

This single centre cohort study enrolled subjects from the Division of Gastroenterology, National University Hospital, Singapore between September 2021 and January 2023. Eligible subjects were above 21-years-old with suspected GERD and scheduled for a catheter-based esophageal pH/impedance testing by their primary physicians. Subjects with significant co-morbidities or do not have enough sound teeth for the retention of the oral device were excluded from the study. They were screened prior to enrolment using the GerdQ questionnaire 15 and demographics such as age, sex, race, BMI, and current GERD medication were recorded. Additionally, they underwent an oral examination by a single dentist (SH) conducted under artificial light. Dental conditions such as dental erosion (Basic erosive wear examination (BEWE) score16), caries status (DMFT), periodontal status, and salivary flow rate were recorded. All experiments were performed in accordance with all relevant regulations and guidelines. The study received ethics approval from the institutional review boards of the National Healthcare Group, Singapore (DSRB reference D/2018/00658).

Intraoral device prototype

The Bravo™ capsule (Given Imaging, Yokne'am Illit, Israel) is a wireless self-contained pH monitor currently available for continuous monitoring of esophageal pH17. It is typically placed in the esophagus with a catheter and transmits pH measurement to an external recording device for up to 72 h. The Bravo™ capsule fulfils most of the criteria required in an intraoral pH monitor including: (1) Wireless transmission, (2) Ability to withstand temperature changes and physical forces and (3) Self-contained for continuous real-time monitoring. However, it can benefit from miniaturization for better comfort, and increased battery life for longer monitoring. As it is self-contained, it can be attached to an Essix-style retainer18 and serve as the intraoral pH monitor prototype in this study.

The intraoral device was constructed for individual subjects by a dentist (SH), taking approximately 20 min of clinical time and one hour of dental laboratory time for each device. Enrolled subjects had their maxillary impression taken with alginate and casts poured in type 3 dental stone. Wax was used to embed a modified model of a Bravo™ capsule on the palatal surface, taking care to allow the sensor to be exposed to the oral environment and that the capsule was not resting directly on the palate (Fig. 1A). An Essix-style retainer (Essix ACE Plastic, Dentsply Sirona, Charlotte NC, USA) was thermoformed over the cast and model capsule, then trimmed to fit. During the visit to issue the intraoral monitor, a new Bravo™ capsule was activated according to manufacturer instructions, modified to reduce its size and affixed to the retainer using cyanoacrylate. An adhesive (VPS Tray Adhesive, 3 M, Two Harbors MN, USA) was applied to the inner surface of the retainer and capsule to improve the adhesion of silicone impression material. A vinyl polysiloxane (VPS) silicone material (Imprint™ 4 Super Quick Heavy VPS Impression Material, 3 M, Two Harbors MN, USA) was applied to the fitting surface of the retainer, moulded to fit the palate using the cast, and excess trimmed (Fig. 1B). Esophageal pH and impedance testing

Subjects enrolled were previously indicated for an esophageal pH and impedance testing by their primary physicians because of GERD symptoms. They were scheduled to undergo a 24-h pH-impedance monitoring using the pH-impedance catheter probe (MMS, Enschede, Netherlands) or the Digitrapper™ pH-Z Testing System (Medtronic, Dublin, Ireland), consisting a single pH-sensor and six impedance channels, inserted nasally with the pH sensor placed 5 cm above the upper border of the lower esophageal sphincter. The test results were read by a single board-certified gastrointestinal physician (AYSS) and GERD parameters such as acid exposure time (%) and non-meal reflux events were recorded. A threshold pH of < 4 for the esophageal device was selected for the recording of reflux events in accordance to established diagnostic criteria19.

Study procedures

Prior to the study, subjects were instructed to stop taking proton pump inhibitors for seven days.

The study was conducted over three visits.

Initial visit

Consisted of initial screening and informed consent taking, gathering of demographics, oral examination and impression taking. The retainer was fabricated prior to the second visit.

Second visit

This was the scheduled esophageal pH and impedance testing consisting of high-resolution manometry followed by the placement of the catheter for the esophageal device. Subsequently, the intraoral device was activated and issued to the subject. A food intake/sleep diary was issued and the subjects were instructed to wear the intraoral device full time except during oral hygiene practices.

Final visit

The esophageal and intraoral devices were removed. The data from both devices were downloaded. A self-administered survey was completed by the subjects regarding the comfort and acceptance of the esophageal and intraoral devices. The responses for the survey was based on a 5-point Likert scale (Strongly Disagree = 1, Disagree = 2, Neutral = 3, Agree = 4, Strongly Agree = 5).

As this is a pilot of a prototype device, the sample size suggested was between 10–30 subjects20.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to report demographic data. Normality was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparison of signs of dental erosion with GERD parameters was done using the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparison of comfort between the esophageal and intraoral devices was done using the Wilcoxon sign-rank test. Statistical significance was set at p value < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 28.0 statistical software (IBM Corporation, New York, NY, USA).

The intraclass correlation coefficient were calculated to evaluate the correlation between the esophagael pH testing and intraoral devices. The accuracy, sensitivity and specificity were calculated to evaluate if the intraoral device could classify pH < 4 events from the esophageal device under different threshold cut-off settings.

Ethics approval

Ethics approval was granted from the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board (Singapore) (NHG DSRB study reference 2018/00658).

Consent to participate

Informed consent or assent was obtained from all subjects prior to enrolment.

Results

The study recruited ten subjects, with the mean age of 42 (Range 23 to 60) years old. The majority of the subjects were male (7/10) and Chinese (8/10), with an average BMI of 24.5 (Range 19.2 to 32.32). Most (7/10) had no other co-morbidities, except three subjects with asthma, diabetes and hepatitis B carrier status. The median GerdQ score was 9.5 (Range 4 to 15) corresponding to a 79% likelihood of GERD. Most (8/10) were on medications (Proton pump inhibitor, Antacid) for GERD symptoms and around half (4/10) presented with esophagitis. With respect to their dental condition, most (8/10) showed signs of dental erosion with half (5/10) having BEWE score of 2 (hard tissue loss involving < 50% of the surface area), and half had periodontitis (5/10). The subjects had an average DMFT of 5.4 (Range 0 to 10) and average saliva flow rate of 0.7 (Range 0.3 to 2.1) ml/min. (Table 1). Relationship between dental erosion and esophageal reflux events

The severity of dental erosion was compared with the pH results from the esophageal device. The parameters included acid exposure time (%), non-meal reflux events, and number of pH < 4 events (units of time pH < 4). Subjects with severe erosion (BEWE score 2,3) had significantly (p < 0.05) higher acid exposure time (3.50 ± 3.96% vs. 0.30 ± 0.16%), more non-meal reflux events (49.00 ± 24.01 vs 14.80 ± 9.23), and higher number of pH < 4 events per hour (128.94 ± 136.70 vs. 13.34 ± 8.45) (Table 2). Viability of the prototype intraoral device

Regarding the intraoral device, possible adverse events included: dislodgement of device becoming a choking risk, intraoral trauma from wearing the device, and inability to tolerate device for routine activities (eating/sleeping). During the course of study, there were no recorded adverse events associated with the intraoral device. There was one recorded adverse event for the esophageal device whereby the subject vomited and dislodged (which was replaced) the catheter.

Based on the self-administered survey at the final visit, the intraoral device was significantly (p < 0.05) more comfortable to place (median 4.5 [IQR:1.0] vs. 2.0 [IQR:1.3]), more comfortable to wear (median 4.0 [IQR:0.5] vs. 2.0 [IQR:1.3]), and interfered less with daily routine (median 3.0 [IQR:2.0] vs. 2.0 [IQR:0.3]) when compared to the esophageal device. This was collaborated by a majority when the two devices were compared directly in terms of comfortable to place (10/10), comfortable to wear (9/10), and interfered less with daily routine (9/10) (Table 3). Correlation, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of the intraoral device

The pH readings of the intraoral device were correlated with the esophageal device using Intra Class Correlation (ICC). The overall ICC was − 0.415 [95 CI − 0.431 to − 0.399], but there was wide variability ranging from 0.655 [95 CI 0.643 to 0.666] to − 0.919 [95 CI − 0.984 to − 0.855]. The average number of esophageal pH < 4 events were 273.3 ± 359.4 (Range 27 to 1272).

When setting a ≤ 10th percentile threshold cut-off of intraoral reading as positive events, the intraoral device achieved mean accuracy of 86.15% (Range 80.87 to 88.92), mean sensitivity of 18.71% (Range 0.00 to 58.62), and mean specificity of 88.73% (Range 87.40 to 91.62). When setting a ≤ 60th percentile threshold cut-off of intraoral reading as positive events, the intraoral device achieved mean accuracy of 37.82% (Range 34.52 to 40.01), mean sensitivity of 64.32% (Range 44.44 to 100.00), and mean specificity of 37.81% (Range 34.87 to 41.42) (Table 4). Discussion

The current study showed that an intraoral device was potentially more acceptable than an esophageal device, in terms of (1) having an easier procedure to place, (2) better comfort to the patient during wear, and (3) less disruptive to daily routine. Additionally, subjects with more severe dental erosion presented with more severe GERD parameters, suggesting that acid reflux does reach the oral cavity in more severe cases and the oral cavity is a viable monitoring site for GERD.

According to the Lyon consensus, acid exposure time is an important criteria for GERD diagnosis and for esophageal pH, the threshold cut-off value had been set at ph < 4 from large scale studies8. However, for intraoral pH, there are currently no optimal threshold cut-off values with regards to diagnostic values. As such, it is not possible to compare acid exposure time for the esophageal and intraoral devices without first establishing a clinically relevant diagnostic criteria for GERD when intraoral pH is measured using the intraoral device. In the present study, cut-off thresholds were examined from the lowest 10th to 60th percentile oral pH values. Accuracy and specificity were reasonably high (> 80%) at 10th percentile, but the sensitivity was low. Conversely, raising the cut-off to 60th percentile increased the sensitivity but lowered the accuracy and specificity. Although, the overall correlation was poor between oral and esophageal pH, there was great variability among the subjects. This finding could have been affected by the small sample size and the lack of a healthy control group. Moreover, the study group had only one subject (008) with a GERD diagnosis according to the reference values of acid exposure time (%) (> 3.7 to > 5.8) and number of non-meal reflux events (> 46 to > 76.5)8. In a larger sample of subjects with a greater portion of GERD, it may be possible to determine a threshold cut-off with better accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Additionally, the ingestion of acidic foods may lower the intraoral pH. There were no dietary restrictions placed on the subjects during the study, but they were required to keep detailed diet dairies with time of consumption and description of food and drink consumed. A qualitative examination of the diaries did not reveal the consumption of overtly acidic foods by the subjects, though future studies should take into account this possible confounding factor. One limiting factor was that there have been suggestion that measuring the proximal extend of reflux using multiple pH sensors suffers from poor sensitivity and reproducibility21; however, there was evidence of proximal progressing changes in esophageal pH in GERD patients that may be measurable in the oral cavity22. Future studies should consider examining the association of intraoral pH with laryngopharyngeal reflux as described by the Dubai consensus23, with the addition of proximal esophageal or hypopharyngeal pH sensors for a more accurate measure of pH fluctuations24.

The current prototype was found to be safe, with no adverse events recorded during the study, compared to one adverse event for the esophageal device. However, some subjects still reported the oral device to somewhat interfere with daily routine, albeit less than the esophageal device. This could be due to the device being constructed with a modified Bravo™ capsule, thus having a larger profile than ideal. The original Bravo™ capsule was partly conceived on the principal that having an uncomfortable nasopharyngeal catheter place often resulted in patients limiting their diet and physical activity resulting in underestimating the actual reflux events25. The improved tolerance of the Bravo™ capsule also allows for longer study time compared to the traditional 24-h catheter-based studies, allowing for longer monitoring and better diagnostic yield26. Subsequent purpose-built prototypes should take this into account, have a reduced profile and longer battery life. This will increase the tolerance by patients and allow for longer term monitoring. Similar dental appliances were routinely constructed for patients with good compliance and acceptability, suggesting that the intraoral device would be similarly tolerated27.

The current front-line treatment of GERD is the use of acid suppressants such as proton pump inhibitors to raise gastric pH, reduce clinical symptoms and promote healing28. Sometimes, trial courses of PPIs were prescribed as a diagnostic tool. This resulted in PPIs being one of the most frequently prescribed drug, with some studies suggesting widespread over-prescription29. However, PPIs have wide ranges of effective doses especially when used in children, 0.7 to 3.3 mg/kg for omeprazole30 and 0.7 to 3.3 mg/kg for lansoprazole31. Moreover, the use of proton pump inhibitors had been associated with various adverse effects such as headaches, diarrhea, nausea, and constipation, with 34% of patients affected32. This makes the ability to continuously monitor treatment effect extremely valuable to determine effective dosage and reducing adverse effects, allowing for the delivery of precision medicine.

In conclusion, intraoral pH monitoring may be a useful adjunct for the diagnosis of GERD. Being more tolerable than the traditional nasopharyngeal catheter used in esophageal pH monitoring, it has the potential to be used in the long-term monitoring of treatment effect.
