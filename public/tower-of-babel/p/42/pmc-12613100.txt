CBPR is indeed political
Authors: Rebekah Israel Cross, James Huynh, Taylor B. Rogers, Natalie J. Bradford, Mienah Z. Sharif
Published: 2025

A Lancet Regional Health commentary asserted that community-based participatory research (CBPR) is not political.1 We argue that this position is dangerous—claiming CBPR is “apolitical” and encouraging “policymakers across the political spectrum… to lean into CBPR,” conflates “partisan” (commitment to a political party) with “political” (relating to the distribution of power and resources). CBPR is fundamentally focused on addressing systems of power.2, 3, 4 An apolitical approach jeopardizes CBPR initiatives and erases the movement's history. Opara writes that CBPR's “ethical imperative is inclusive by nature, accounting for all perspectives and addressing doubts from community partners and members.”1 However, CBPR prioritizes centering those at the margins, rather than “all perspectives.”5 Consider the possible harm of law enforcement included in community violence interventions. CBPR would confront power like police violence rather than reinforce it. After denouncing the political nature of CBPR, Opara describes it having “the power to coproduce knowledge and raise the voices of those who may have felt ignored.”1 This clearly contradicts the apolitical assertion given that the factors leaving some voices ignored are political: political will or lack thereof. We challenge that CBPR “helps insulate public health from the political currents.” Instead, we reaffirm that CBPR underscores an approach to address socio-political contexts driving health inequities and to disrupt research practices rooted in colonial, white supremacist traditions. Overall, we agree that the “field of public health is under attack and facing a profound crisis of trust.”1 These threats will worsen should our field fail to acknowledge the political roots of our work.
