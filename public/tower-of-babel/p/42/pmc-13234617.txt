Effects of exposure to ethinyl estradiol during pregnancy and lactation on the postinvolution mammary gland
Authors: Joshua P Mogus, Priyanka Maharaj-Chama, Laura N Vandenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextThe mammary gland undergoes extensive reorganization during pregnancy, lactation, and involution. During involution, mass apoptosis of epithelial cells and infiltration of immune cells return the gland to a state similar to the nulliparous female. However, the postinvolution gland has structural differences to the nulliparous gland, which may provide protection against breast cancer. Changes associated with parity are hormone dependent and could be vulnerable to endocrine-disrupting chemicals, particularly those that are estrogen receptor (ER) agonists.ObjectiveWe evaluated the effects of ethinyl estradiol (EE2), a pharmaceutical ER agonist, administered during pregnancy and lactation on the structure and steroid function of the postinvolution mammary gland.MethodsCD-1 mice were exposed to vehicle or EE2 (20 μg/kg/day) from pregnancy day 9 until weaning. An unexposed nulliparous group was included for comparison. Five weeks after exposure, we evaluated the mammary glands for treatment effects on tissue architecture, proliferation, immune cell populations, and steroid function.ResultsEE2 exposure reduced the parity-driven effects on mammary gland epithelial volume, with more modest (nonsignificant) effects on mammary gland functional components including a reduction in resident macrophages, an increase in ER- and a decrease in progesterone receptor-expressing cells. EE2 exposure also restricted maternal and pup growth during the exposure period and delayed pup eye opening.ConclusionThese results suggest that EE2 exposure during pregnancy and lactation disrupts some of the typical changes of parity seen in the mammary gland. This pharmaceutical may provide insights into effects of other estrogenic endocrine-disrupting chemicals on the maternal mammary gland.

The mammary gland goes through extensive reorganization during pregnancy, lactation, and regression [1]. Transforming from a relatively simple structure of ductal branches in early pregnancy to a complex differentiated epithelium in lactation [2], the gland then returns to a simpler ductal structure again after the mass death of epithelial structures and regression during involution [3, 4]. Though the mammary gland returns to a state similar to that of the nonpregnant female following involution, the ductal structure is changed and distinguishable from the nulliparous animal with increased differentiated epithelium and secondary ductal branching, decreased proliferation, decreased steroid receptor expression, and increased macrophage presence. These alterations are induced by hormonal changes throughout pregnancy and lactation. Furthermore, these features of parity and involution may play an important role in the protective nature of pregnancy on lifetime breast cancer risk [5-7].

As a hormone-responsive organ [8, 9], the mammary gland is sensitive to endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) [10-12], particularly those that interact with estrogen receptor (ER) [13, 14]. Estrogenic EDC exposures in utero and during the perinatal period alter the development of the mammary gland [15-17], yet relatively few studies evaluate the effects of EDCs on the long-term health of the mammary gland when exposures occur during pregnancy and lactation.

Exposure to estrogenic EDCs during pregnancy can have long-term consequences for maternal health [9]. For example, Bisphenol A exposure leads to during pregnancy-exacerbated, pregnancy-related insulin resistance and to obesity, disturbed metabolic hormones, and insulin resistance in female mice 4 months after exposure had ceased [18]. Previous studies from our laboratory have shown that estrogenic EDC exposures during pregnancy and lactation can alter the long-term development of the mammary gland [14, 19, 20], often reducing some of the structural and functional features of the postinvolution gland. Some effects of these estrogenic chemicals are consistent across chemicals we have studied. For example, exposure to oxybenzone [14], propylparaben [19], or butyl benzyl phthalate [20] reduces the number of macrophages in the mammary stroma, reversing the effect of parity. However, other effects of estrogenic EDCs were more variable; exposure to oxybenzone or propylparaben resulted in reduced postinvolution ductal epithelial volume, whereas butyl benzyl phthalate exposure during the same period increased ductal epithelial volume. Based on these results, it is not yet possible to predict the effect of EDC exposures on specific mammary gland endpoints based on a chemical's ER agonist properties alone. Because these EDCs also have other known mechanisms of action, studying pharmaceuticals with strong affinity for just 1 hormone receptor can reveal the impact of receptor activation on mammary gland development.

To understand the effect of ER agonist exposures during pregnancy and lactation and their long-term effects on mammary gland health, we evaluated a highly specific ER agonist, 17α-ethinyl estradiol (EE2), a pharmaceutical commonly used in contraceptives [21] that is also often included as a positive control in studies of other estrogenic EDCs. EE2 has a long history as a therapeutic treatment for breast engorgement in nonnursing (human) mothers [22], and its affinity for and specificity to ER is well established [23]. The usability of EE2 as a therapeutic estrogen is in large part due to its oral bioavailability, unlike 17β-estradiol or other estrogenic drugs like estradiol benzoate; this makes EE2 a good choice to balance between ER specificity and relevance to human exposures to estrogenic pollutants (ie, often experienced through the ingestion route) [24]. Furthermore, recent studies from our laboratory provide evidence that mice exposed to EE2 during lactation alone have reduced ductal epithelial architecture at the time of weaning, consistent with reduced lactogenesis [25].

Here, we exposed mice to EE2 during pregnancy and lactation and evaluated several outcomes during the period of exposure, including growth and development in both the mothers and the pups, as well as features of the postinvolution mammary gland, several weeks after exposures had ceased. We also included age-matched unexposed nulliparous females as a comparator group. The results of this study indicate that EE2 exposures affect the typical body weight changes that mice experience during pregnancy and lactation and limit pup growth and development. Additionally, EE2 reduces parity-associated increases in postinvolution mammary ductal epithelial volume but has only modest effects on epithelial steroid signaling and duct-associated innate immune cell populations. Taken together, the results of this study indicate that exposures to ER agonists like EE2 during pregnancy and lactation disrupt the morphology and cellular composition of the postinvolution mammary gland, with some similarities to estrogenic EDCs.

Methods

Animals

CD-1 mice were housed in polysulfone cages at the University of Massachusetts Animal Facility in a light- and temperature-controlled environment. Food (LabDiet Chow 5058; LabDiet, St. Louis, MO) and tap water was provided ad libitum. All design, animal care, and experimental processes were approved by the University of Massachusetts Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

All animals were randomly assigned to treatment groups, coded as nulliparous vehicle (NULL-VEH), parous vehicle (PAR-VEH), or parous EE2 (PAR-EE2). Statistical analysis confirmed no significant differences in weight between groups at onset of exposure. Following pairing of males and females, females were checked twice per day for vaginal plugs to determine date and timing (Am, pm) of conception (pregnancy day 0 [P0]).

Exposure to EE2 (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO; >98%) before P9 can cause complications in implantation resulting in critical losses in litter size or resorption of entire litters [26]. To avoid this risk, pregnant females were exposed starting on P9 to either tap water with 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (PAR-VEH) or 0.12 mg/L EE2 dissolved in tap water (with 0.1% DMSO) (PAR-EE2) Administration of drinking water containing DMSO and/or EE2 continued until weaning of litters at lactation day 21 (LD21). The concentration of EE2 was calculated to provide approximately 20 μg/kg body weight/day in a 30-g mouse that drinks an average of 5 mL of water per day (Charles River), matching exposure levels within the daily range of common EE2-based contraception use. Water consumption was measured daily using graduated volumetric bottles with regular replacement of fresh water.

Females were checked twice daily (Am, pm) for the presence of pups and the number of days between the observed sperm plug to parturition was calculated as a measure of gestation length. The number of pups per litter varies, which can affect the allocation of nutrients and level of maternal care that dams give between litters [27]. To ensure a more even distribution of resources, litters were weighed and then culled to 10 pups on LD2; every attempt was made to maintain litters with balanced sex ratios. Litters were reweighed following culling to establish a new baseline for pup weights. When litter size was less than 10, no pups were culled.

Litters were assessed for weight gain on postnatal day 1 (PND1), PND2, PND5, PND7, PND14, and PND21 with mean pup weights calculated by dividing litter weight by the number of pups per litter (aka litter size). Pups were also checked for the number of ears open per litter on PND5, and the number of eyes open per litter on PND15. Dams were weighed every other day from P1 until parturition, and on lactation days LD1, LD2, LD5, LD7, LD14, and LD21.

On LD21, pups were separated from their mothers and housed in new cages. Effects of EE2 on these pups at puberty have been published elsewhere [28]. Dams were given fresh, untreated tap water for 5 weeks (until postinvolution week 5 [PIW5]) to allow for complete involution of the mammary gland without EE2 exposure. Dams were weighed once each week during the postinvolution period. The mothers were then humanely euthanized via CO2 inhalation followed by decapitation. Following euthanasia, vaginal smears were performed to determine estrous stage, but necropsies were not timed to match any specific estrous stage. A group of age-matched nulliparous vehicle-exposed female mice was included and euthanized at the same age as the PAR-VEH and PAR-EE2 groups.

Tissue collection, processing, storage

Following euthanasia, the third (pectoral) mammary gland was removed from the skin and snap frozen in dry ice and stored at −80 °C. The fourth (inguinal) mammary glands were collected for histological and immunohistochemical analysis. The right fourth mammary gland was removed, laid flat on a charged glass slide (Epredia) as a whole mount, and then fixed overnight in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) (Thermo Scientific). The left fourth mammary gland was removed and fixed overnight in NBF. All tissues fixed in NBF were washed the following morning in 2 fresh baths of PBS (Thermo Scientific) for 10 minutes each and then stored in 70% ethanol at 4 °C for further processing.

Processing and imaging of whole-mounted mammary glands

Whole mount mammary glands were processed for carmine staining of the ductal epithelium using standard protocols [29]. Briefly, whole mounts were processed in a series of dehydrating alcohols, cleared of fat in 2 washes of toluene, rehydrated in a series of alcohols, and then stained overnight in carmine alum solution at 4 °C. The next morning, the whole mounts were dehydrated in a series of alcohols, cleared in xylene, and then stored in methyl salicylate (Arcos Organics, Morris Plains, NJ) inside sealed kPax (Thermo Fisher Scientific) bags.

Whole mounts were imaged using an AxioImager dissection microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Jena, Germany) with a Zeiss high-resolution color camera. All images were taken at 30× magnification and evaluated with Zen Blue 3.7 software (Carl Zeiss Microscopy). A single image at 30× magnification was taken at the farthest anterior point from the lymph node with complete coverage of ductal structures. A 180-point (12 × 15) stereological grid was placed on the entire field of view, and at each crosshair, the presence of epithelium was determined. The number of each type of structure was counted across the 5 images of each sample and then divided by the total grid points (180 points) to give a single value, representing sample proportion of epithelium (shown as percents in the figures for simplicity), for each animal.

Histology processing and imaging

Fixed left fourth mammary glands were processed for histopathological evaluation through a series of alcohols and xylenes and then embedded in paraffin wax under vacuum. The sample blocks were then sectioned at a 5-μm thickness using a rotary microtome and mounted onto positively charged slides (Thermo Scientific). All images were collected on an Axio Observer.Z1 inverted microscope and high-resolution camera (Carl Zeiss Microscopy) and evaluated with Zen Blue v3.7 software (Carl Zeiss Microscopy).

To assess changes in mammary gland histomorphology, sections were stained with Gimori's Trichrome (Epredia Richard Allan Scientific, 87020) following the manufacturer's protocol. Five nonoverlapping images were taken at different locations across the mammary gland at 100× magnification. On each image a 180-point (12 × 15) stereological grid was placed on the entire field of view. At each crosshair, the type of cell (epithelial or adipose) or structure (duct, periductal stroma, vasculature) was identified. The number of each type (cell, structure) was counted across the 5 images of each sample and then divided by the total grid points (180 points per image × 5 images = 900 points) to give a sample proportion (shown as percents in the figures for simplicity).

To evaluate mast cell migration into the mammary gland, we stained slides with toluidine blue. Sections were imaged at 200×, with 5 nonoverlapping images taken of individual ducts of similar size and shape. Each duct then was traced using Zen software polygon contour tool to outline the duct and immediate surrounding stroma to determine the area of the ductal unit. All mast cells within the ductal space were counted. The total number of mast cells across 5 images of each sample were added together and then divided by the total ductal area to calculate proportion of mast cells per ductal area for each sample.

Immunohistochemistry

To assess changes in protein expression and quantify the number of macrophages, mammary gland sections were processed for immunohistochemistry (IHC) using an established procedure for paraffin-embedded samples [19]. In brief, sections were deparaffinized in a series of xylenes, hydrated in a series of ethanols, equilibrated in PBS, and subjected to antigen retrieval using heated 10 mM of citrate buffer. This was followed by quenching of endogenous peroxidases using 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol. Sections were then blocked in 1% milk protein in 5% goat serum (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) before overnight (14-16 hours) incubation with primary antibodies (diluted in blocking solution) in humidified chambers at 4 °C. Sections were washed, then incubated with biotinylated secondary antibody, followed by conjugated streptavidin peroxidase complex (Abcam, Cat# ab24269). Sections were treated with diaminobenzidine chromogen (Abcam, Cat#64238), counterstained with Harris modified hematoxylin (Thermo Scientific), dehydrated in a series of ethanols, cleared in several washes of xylenes, and cover slipped with permanent mounting medium (Fisher Scientific). Images were collected on an Axio Observer.Z1 inverted microscope and high-resolution camera (Carl Zeiss Microscopy) and evaluated with Zen Blue v3.7 software (Carl Zeiss Microscopy). For assessments of cells expressing pSTAT3, pSTAT5, ERα, and PR, 3 to 6 ducts (minimum of 500 epithelial cells total) of similar shape and cross section were imaged at 200× magnification. For each image, cells that were positive and negative for the marker of interest were counted to calculate mean positive percentages. Assessment of cell proliferation using Ki67 expression followed the same process, but with a minimum of 1000 total cells counted across images.

F4/80-positive cells were counted to evaluate macrophage populations in our samples. Images were taken of 3 ducts per mammary gland, each of similar size and cross-section. Because of the amorphous nature of macrophages, F4/80 expression was analyzed using the color analysis software TissueQuant. Briefly, diaminobenzidine chromogen-positive color from F4/80 IHC-tagged images was isolated and transformed to a black and white 8-bit image using Fiji (ImageJ) software with the IHC Cell Profiler plugin. Eight-bit images were analyzed against a pretrained color/intensity baseline in TissueQuant to calculate a relative coverage of F4/80-positive signal in each image. These coverage values were averaged to generate a mean value for each sample. F4/80+ area was normalized to the total number of epithelial cells counted in each sample.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using R statistical software (v4.3.2). To contend with repeated measures of individuals, maternal body weights during pregnancy, lactation, and postinvolution, as well as pup weights, were analyzed using general linear mixed models using the core R glm() function. For each measuring period, the normal (gaussian) family distribution was used based on assessment of QQplot residuals and confirmation of homoscedasticity. Treatment group-day interaction was a fixed effect in each model with Dam ID included as a random effect. In the pregnancy period, we also included litter size as an additional random effect to contend with the influence of pup number on maternal weight. Differences between groups were assessed via Tukey post hoc test with P < .05 considered statistically significant. Statistical outliers were calculated, a priori, as any values outside 1.5× the interquartile range for all data points in a given outcome [30]. Removed outliers include the following: 2D Epithelial Fraction (1× NULL-VEH); 2D Periductal Stroma (3× NULL-VEH, 1× PAR-EE2), 2D Adipose Fraction (2× NULL-VEH); 2D Vasculature Fraction (1× NULL-VEH, 1× PAR-VEH, 1× PAR-EE2); F480 Area per Epithelial Cell (1× NULL-VEH); and Mast Cell per ductal area (1× NULL-VEH, 1× PAR-VEH). All outliers were removed from subsequent analysis and visualization.

All other outcomes were first assessed for normality using QQplots and the Shapiro-Wilks test. All comparisons with P values ≥ .05 were considered normally distributed and were further analyzed using parametric significance tests, whereas any Shapiro-Wilks P values < .05 were considered not normally distributed and nonparametric significance tests were used.

All parametric significance tests of more than 2 groups were first analyzed for significance (α = 0.05) using ANOVA. When P < .05, group pairwise comparisons were made via Tukey post hoc test (α = 0.05). All nonparametric significance tests of more than 2 groups were first analyzed for significance (α = 0.05) using Kruskal Wallis. When P < .05, groupwise comparisons were tested via Mann-Whitney U (α = 0.05). In any case where P values are reported for a groupwise test, the appropriate analysis of variance was conducted and a P value of <.05 was observed.

All statistically significant findings are depicted in the figures with an asterisk. For any pairwise comparison or post hoc significance test, a P value, .05 ≤ P < .10, was reported as a difference between means nearing statistical difference and noted in figures with the symbol #. Means and effect sizes (d) are reported in the text for all significant or near significant findings as well for any notable comparisons. Depending on the endpoint evaluated, sample sizes for treatment groups were: NULL-VEH, n = 10-13; PAR-VEH, n = 11-13; and PAR-EE2, n = 11-13 with the exception of progesterone receptor IHC evaluation which had NULL-VEH, n = 7; PAR-VEH, n = 5; and PAR-EE2, n = 8, respectively.

Results

EE2 alters gestation and litter size

To assess whether EE2 exposure during pregnancy alters reproductive outcomes, we measured both gestation length and litter size. We observed that mothers exposed to EE2 had a slightly longer, though statistically significant, gestation period (19.0 days) compared to mice in the PAR-VEH control group (18.4 days) (Fig. 2A, d = 3.2%, Mann-Whitney U, P = <.001). Despite longer gestational time, the size of the litters born to PAR-EE2 mothers was smaller (Fig. 2B, d = -11.8%, Mann-Whitney U, P = .064) though this difference did not reach statistical significance.

EE2 exposures during pregnancy and lactation reduce dam body weight, but the effect is attenuated in the postinvolution period when exposures cease

In mice, weight gain is a feature of both pregnancy and early lactation. To determine if EE2 alters the body weight trajectories of exposed females during pregnancy, lactation, and/or the postinvolution period, mice were routinely weighed. Treatment groups had similar weights at the time of sperm plug detection on P1, before the administration of EE2. Weight in both groups increased at a similar rate until P11 (2 days after EE2 treatments started) (Fig. 3A, d = −9.1%, Tukey; P = .048) when PAR-EE2 females began to significantly lag in body weight compared to PAR-VEH controls. These differences in body weight continued to exaggerate until P17 (Fig. 3A, d = −12.7%, Tukey; P = .006).

Starting at P11, body weights for PAR-EE2 females (dotted red line) were significantly lower than PAR-VEH females (solid gray line), and this pattern continued until P17. Note that litter size was included as a random effect in the GLM for this assessment. By the time of parturition and though the start of lactation (LD1 and LD2) (Fig. 3B), differences in weight between the groups were diminished. However, by LD5, PAR-EE2 dams again were significantly lighter than PAR-VEH dams (Fig. 3B, d = −12.1%, Tukey; P = .002). This trend continued through weaning at LD21 (Fig. 3B, d = −12.7%, Tukey; P = .003).

By 1 week postinvolution onward (when exposures to EE2 also ceased), there were no significant differences in body weight due to treatment for the PAR-VEH or PAR-EE2 groups. Body weights of aged-matched NULL-VEH dams were not statistically significant from either parous group (Fig. 3C).

To determine whether EE2 exposures during gestation and lactation affect pup growth, pups were weighed at several timepoints from birth to weaning at PND21. Although there were no differences in body weight because of treatment at PND2, a delay in growth was evident by PND5 (Fig. 4A, d = −10.9%, Tukey; P = .083) with a growing and statistically significant lag in the body weight of PAR-EE2 pups compared to PAR-VEH pups observed by PND7. This pattern continued until PND21 (Fig. 4A, d = −33.4%, Tukey; P < .001).

Pups from the PAR-EE2 litters were also observed to lag behind their PAR-VEH counterparts in reaching some developmental milestones. EE2-exposed litters had the same number of ears open per litter as PAR-VEH controls at PND5 (Fig. 4B, d = +3.0%, Mann-Whitney U, P = .903), but a significant reduction in the percent of eyes open per litter on PND15 (Fig. 4C, d = −57.9%, Mann-Whitney U, P < .001).

EE2 exposure during gestation and lactation has modest long-term effects on post-involution mammary gland structure

Previous studies show that exposures to estrogenic chemicals during pregnancy and lactation reduce some of the typical changes seen in mammary glands from parous compared to nulliparous mice [14, 19, 20]. To evaluate any structural changes in the postinvolution mammary gland, we first measured epithelial volume fraction in whole mounted glands. Here we observed that PAR-VEH controls had significantly more epithelial volume than NULL-VEH controls (Fig. 5A, d = +47.8%, Dunn with Holm correction, P < .001), as expected based on outcomes in previous studies. We observed no statistically significant difference in epithelial volume between PAR-EE2 and PAR-VEH groups (d = −7.5%, Dunn with Holm correction, P = .21), whereas epithelium volume in PAR-EE2 was significantly higher than in NULL-VEH mammary glands (d = +43.6%, Dunn with Holm correction, P < .01). The mammary gland comprises several cell types and structures including ductal/alveolar epithelial cells, adipose, periductal stroma, and vasculature (Fig. 6A). Previous studies have shown that parity increases the fraction of epithelial cells in the mammary gland and that estrogenic chemical exposure during pregnancy and lactation can restrict this parity-induced increase. To evaluate if EE2 has similar effects, we assessed trichrome stained sections of mammary glands for differences in cell histomorphology. First, we observed that the percentage of epithelial cells was increased in PAR-VEH compared to NULL-VEH as expected (Fig. 6B, d = +98.0%, Dunn; P < .05). However, a significant increase in epithelial cells was not observed in the PAR-EE2-exposed group; instead, this group had an intermediate phenotype compared to the PAR-VEH (d = −7.5%) and NULL-VEH groups (d = +77.3%). Interestingly, we found no differences due to parity or treatment for the volume fraction of adipose (Fig. 6C), periductal stroma (Fig. 6D), or vasculature (Fig. 6E).

EE2 exposure during pregnancy and lactation has no long-term effect on ERα expression in mammary gland epithelia

ER agonist exposures are known to alter ERα and PR expression, and we used IHC to evaluate expression of these receptors in the mammary epithelium. No difference was found due to parity, but there was a modest increase in ERα expression in the PAR-EE2 group compared to PAR-VEH, although this difference was not significant (Fig. 7A and B). We also found a modest decrease in the percentage of epithelial cells expressing PR in the PAR-EE2 group compared to both controls; however, these were also not significantly different (Fig. 7C and D).

EE2 exposure during pregnancy and lactation does not alter proliferation or pSTAT expression in the mammary gland epithelial cells

To determine if proliferation or cell survival contributes to the increase in epithelial cell volume fraction we observed in PAR-VEH females, we conducted IHC using 3 relevant markers: Ki67 (proliferation), pSTAT3 (early apoptosis initiation), and pSTAT5 (differentiation and survival) (Table 2). Although we observed a greater than 2-fold increase in the mean percent of Ki67+ cells in the PAR-VEH animals compared to NULL-VEH (d = +118.2%), there were no statistically significant differences found between groups. Similarly, neither pSTAT3 nor pSTAT5 were different due to parity or EE2 treatment.

Discussion

We evaluated the effects of the highly specific ER agonist, EE2, on growth and development of dams and pups as well as the long-term effects on mammary gland structure postinvolution. EE2 has been extensively studied in both humans and animals, making it an excellent candidate to evaluate the effects of ER agonist exposures during pregnancy and lactation on health outcomes. Furthermore, the effects of EE2 administration on women exposed during lactation is well-documented in studies where the pharmaceutical was administered as a therapeutic treatment to reduce breast engorgement [22]; however, the long-term effects of such exposures on women administered the drug postparturition have not been evaluated.

We observed that EE2 exposure disrupted reproductive outcomes by increasing gestation length and decreasing litter size in mice. More specifically, we found that EE2 increased gestation length, resulting in a small but developmentally important increase of about a half day. This was similar to a previous finding from a study using Wistar rats exposed to EE2 during gestation [31]. Additionally, we found that EE2 exposure reduced the number of pups per litter by more than a pup. However, the majority of litters in this experiment had more than 10 pups, leaving comparable litter sizes between treatment groups after culling. These results confirm that EE2 is a reproductive and developmental toxicant and suggest that the administered dose was sufficient to alter physiological parameters.

To monitor potential health concerns for dams during pregnancy and to track if dams were maintaining their pregnancies, mothers were weighed routinely throughout their pregnancy. Despite no differences in weight at P9 (when EE2 exposures started in the PAR-EE2 group), we observed that EE2 exposure hampered weight gain in the PAR-EE2 females during gestation, even when accounting for differences in litter size. This difference became statistically significant by P11 and continued to increase in severity through P17. Immediately following parturition, however, the difference in weight between the treatment groups was lost.

Maternal weight gain during the lactational period is typical in mice as the mother requires additional nutrients to produce milk to nourish pups. In mice, maternal body weight typically peaks around 2 weeks postpartum [32], after which maternal weight wanes as pups transition to solid food. We observed a similar trajectory for the early lactation period in PAR-VEH mothers; however, starting at LD5, PAR-EE2 dams weighed significantly less than their control counterparts, remaining so throughout the remainder of the lactation period and ultimately recovering by 1 week after weaning, after EE2 exposure ceased. Based on this finding, it is likely that EE2 either is acutely toxic or that it alters weight-based aspects of lactation (eg, water and nutrient accumulation for milk production).

Reductions in maternal weight gain could affect the nourishment of pups. We routinely weighed pups throughout the nursing period and observed that pups exposed to EE2 began to lag in weight gain compared to VEH controls as early as PND5, a difference that became exacerbated for the remainder of the nursing period, similar to what has been reported in previous studies in rats [33]. Given the depressed weights from the EE2 exposed dams, it is likely that pups were lacking adequate nutrition to maintain proper growth. Still, it cannot be ruled out that EE2 may have had a direct effect on pup organ development and metabolism, independent of the mother, as EE2 has been shown to be transferred via milk [34, 35]. Importantly, lack of early growth in offspring can have immediate effects on the development of body systems, as evidenced by the lack of eye opening at LD15 in the mice of this study. Poor nutrition and growth during early development can have consequences for both short- and long-term health. In humans, low body weight between fetal development up to 3 years is associated with poor cognitive and psychosocial development [36, 37], and reduced weight gain in early life is associated with later metabolic and cardiovascular disease [38, 39]. Lack of sufficient nutrients from breast feeding is estimated to contribute to 10% of disability-adjusted life years [40].

The main purpose of this study was to evaluate the long-term effects of EE2 exposure on the maternal mammary gland. Previous studies from our laboratory indicate that pregnancy and lactational exposure to estrogenic EDCs alter mammary gland structure long after exposures cease; in some of these cases, these EDC exposures also reduced other cellular or molecular changes that typically occur due to parity [14, 19, 20]. The most common of these effects are reductions in the volume of epithelium in the gland, which we assessed here. Exposure to EE2 during pregnancy and lactation reduced the expansion of ductal epithelium typical of postinvolution, resulting in an intermediate phenotype compared to nulliparous and parous controls. The mitigating effect of EE2 was particularly pronounced with histological epithelial volume fraction, where the EE2 exposed group was not different to either the PAR-VEH or NULL-VEH control, though they were different from each other. This is both quantitatively and qualitatively similar to effects to seen in mice following administration of oxybenzone [14] and propylparaben [19], where exposure at human relevant levels resulted in intermediate levels of epithelial volume and loss of parity-typical differences. This outcome may be a signature of exposure to ER agonists during pregnancy and lactation on the postinvolution mammary gland; however, it was not observed in mice exposed to the ER agonist butyl benzyl phthalate, so the signature is not universal for estrogenic chemical exposures [20].

There are other changes in the mammary gland that are consistently observed following lactation, after involution. For example, the postinvolution epithelium has a greater percentage of differentiated epithelial cells compared to the nulliparous female [41], and these cells proliferate at a lower rate than less-differentiated ductal epithelium [14], a feature believed to contribute to reduced risk for long-term tumorigenesis. Though a marker of proliferation (Ki67) was not affected by EE2 treatment in this study, the estrogenic chemicals oxybenzone [14] and propylparaben [19] have been shown to mitigate the parity-induced reduction in Ki67 expression in the mouse mammary gland (ie, Ki67 expression was increased following both of these treatments, when compared to nulliparous controls). Conversely, butyl benzyl phthalate exposure led to a modest decrease in Ki67 expression in the postinvolution mammary gland, potentially because of its potential (and context-dependent) antiestrogenic effects, a feature not previously associated with EE2, or the other 2 EDCs we have tested, but one that is linked with decreased proliferation in the breast [42].

Though critical for driving proliferation needed for epithelial expansion in pregnancy [43], ER activation in breast tissue has long been associated with tumor development and growth and is a common target of many therapies for ER+ breast cancers [44]. Previous findings suggest that parity results in reduced ER expression in the postinvolution gland [45], potentially reducing the downstream proliferative effect of ER activation. Here, we found no effect of parity or EE2 exposure on ER expression. We also did not find evidence of increased proliferation due to EE2 exposure in this study, but future studies should evaluate this outcome for longer periods postinvolution, into older ages where cancers are more likely to develop. Future studies should also determine if females administered EE2 during pregnancy and lactation have reduced protection against carcinogenic agents.

We also evaluated epithelial PR expression and found that it was modestly decreased in the EE2-exposed females compared to both controls. Though this difference was not statistically significant, the pattern of expression was similar to effects seen in females exposed to butyl benzyl phthalate, which reduced the percentage of epithelial cells expressing PR [20]. PR is an important and essential driver of lactogenesis [46, 47], guiding the 3-dimensional structuring and differentiation of lobuloalveolar units in late pregnancy and early lactation [2, 48, 49], and in the mammary gland, PR expression is increased by ER activation [50].

Innate immune cells are known to infiltrate the mammary gland during involution [51] with an increased resident population remaining in the gland long term [52]. Macrophages have key roles in the involution process, contributing to the degradation of alveolar epithelial cells and removal of their debris [53, 54]. Previously, we found that estrogenic EDCs (eg, oxybenzone [14], propylparaben [19], butyl benzyl phthalate [20]) can reduce the number of some immune cells, particularly F4/80+ macrophages, resulting in an intermediate number of these cells compared to either parous or nulliparous controls. In the present study, macrophages were only modestly (and nonsignificantly) decreased after EE2 exposure. Furthermore, we did not find a statistically significant effect of parity, a finding that has been relatively consistent in postinvolution BALB/C mice [19, 20], although the overall decrease in macrophages following EE2 exposure compared to PAR-VEH was similar in scale to those observed after other estrogenic EDC exposures. Interestingly, macrophages have been implicated in the increase of short-term pregnancy-related breast cancer [55] while they have also been described as having antitumorigenic capabilities depending on their polarization subtype and location [56]. Alterations to duct-associated macrophages by EE2 or other ER agonists could alter the risk of breast cancer through manipulation of either pro- or anti-tumorigenic macrophage populations. One key to untangling this relationship in the future may be determining the role EE2 plays in polarizing macrophages, a process that specifies physiological roles for macrophages and alters the association of the macrophage with tumor development [57].

Mast cells serve a similar immune function during tissue remodeling, including involution, where their influx increases removal of materials from regressing alveolar structures [58]. Additionally, mast cells express ER [59] and have been associated with tumor development [58, 60], principally through their angiogenic actions on fibroblast growth factor [61]. However, the number of mast cells in the postinvolution mammary gland was unaffected by either parity or EE2 exposure. The effects of EE2 and other EDCs on mast cells remains understudied; future work should identify potential changes in mast cell number and function from EE2 exposure during the early involution period.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that EE2 alters weight gain in dams and may decrease the expansion of the maternal mammary gland epithelium. These changes create acute challenges for nursing, restricting the growth of their pups. Examination of the postinvolution mammary gland revealed fewer and more subtle effects of EE2 than the direct effects observed on the mother during the period of exposure. Together, these findings suggest that pregnancy and lactation are vulnerable periods for ER agonist exposure, but the effects on long-term mammary gland morphology are modest.

Women are exposed to hundreds of estrogenic EDCs, and the results of this study indicate that activation of ER by some or all of these chemicals during pregnancy and lactation could have effects on both mother and child. Furthermore, the effects of EE2 exposure during pregnancy and lactation on the postinvolution mammary gland have important similarities to other common estrogenic EDC exposures in women, such as a reduction in the volume of mammary epithelium. However, differences between EE2 and EDCs with less specific ER agonists properties suggest that a more nuanced approach may be needed to understand the effects of common EDCs on long-term mammary gland health in women and model organisms like mice. Many of the estrogenic EDCs that we and others have studied have other endocrine modes of action (eg, antiandrogenic, antiestrogenic, obesogenic properties). For this reason, future studies should include more integrated, multihormone system assessments of EDCs that could identify enhanced, dampened, or modified effects on ER activity. Future work and consideration should be given to the estrogenic exposures that pregnant and lactation women face and precautions that can be taken to lessen the burden of these exposures.
