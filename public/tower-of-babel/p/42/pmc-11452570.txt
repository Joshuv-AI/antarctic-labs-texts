Photodynamic therapy with intratumoral injection of photosensitizers combined with immunotherapy brings hope to patients with refractory cutaneous malignant ulcers
Authors: Dan Tian, Chong Teng, Yanju Lv, Xiaozhuo Duan, Yi Wang, Xiaowei Song, Qiuying Jiang, Dayong Huang, Tao Xin, Yu Yang, Li Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) is common among the elderly, typically treated with surgery. However, for surgery-ineligible patients or those with non-healing wounds progressing to malignant ulcers, non-surgical local treatments are viable. This case details an 80-year-old with recurrent back CSCC and intractable malignant ulcers post-radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Treatment involved Hematoporphyrin Derivative (HpD) Photodynamic Therapy (PDT) with low-dose cindilimab immunotherapy (intravenous and intralesional). Two cycles achieved lesion remission, altering peripheral immune cell counts. HpD-PDT combined with immunotherapy is promising for treating CSCC, particularly with malignant ulcers.

Introduction

Cutaneous Squamous Cell Carcinoma (CSCC), the second most common skin cancer after basal cell carcinoma, constitutes 20% of cases globally and is increasing in incidence [1]. It primarily affects the elderly and often leads to intractable ulcers due to deep invasion, challenging conventional treatments [2]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICB), effective in melanoma, show limited response in CSCC, with many cases recurring due to immune evasion [3]. Combining therapies to enhance ICB efficacy is crucial. Photodynamic therapy (PDT), known for safety, specificity, and minimal invasiveness, is promising. PDT employs photosensitizers to induce reactive oxygen species, killing tumor cells and boosting immune response through immunogenic cell death and T-cell infiltration [4].

This report examines a CSCC patient treated with local porphyrin derivative injections and low-dose ICB, assessing feasibility, safety, efficacy, and impacts on post-treatment immune cell levels.

Case report

One year ago, an 80-year-old male developed a painless 5 × 5 cm skin lump on his back and underwent excision at a local hospital, revealing moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma invading superficial muscle. No further treatment was given. Five months ago, the wound ulcerated, resisted healing, and despite palliative radiation and topical chemotherapy with fluorouracil, the ulcer grew. The patient sought treatment at our hospital, where a 15 × 15 cm lesion on his back with irregular ulcerated surfaces, surrounded by mild serous exudation and 1 cm peritumoral nodules. After evaluation, the patient's clinical stage is T3N0M0 (stage III). In formulating treatment plans for patients, we conducted detailed physical assessments. The results of cardiac ultrasound examination showed that the patient's left ventricular ejection fraction was 51%, and there was a history of coronary stent implantation. The serum creatinine value was 169 mol/L, indicating poor cardiac and renal reserve function. In addition, the patient had undergone tumor resection surgery in the early stage, but the lesion did not receive effective relief. CT scan showed that the depth of tumor invasion was large, and the patient had a long history of diabetes. Given these factors, performing a second surgery may not have a satisfactory relief effect on the lesion and may increase the risk of complications for the patient. And the patient is of advanced age, and their family members do not agree to undergo systemic chemotherapy and electrotherapy. Therefore, after fully informing the patient of their condition and communicating, the patient signed an informed consent form and adopted PDT combined with immunotherapy, without choosing to undergo surgery or electrotherapy treatment.

Photofrin (produced by Chongqing Huading Modern Biopharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) is a photosensitizer of HpD. A skin scratch test was conducted on the forearm 20 min before treatment with the original solution. If no adverse reactions appeared within 15 min, 25 mg of hematoporphyrin was diluted in 20 ml of normal saline (NS) and injected into the lesion within 10 min. Lidocaine was administered for local infiltration anesthesia to manage pain during treatment. The laser used had a wavelength of 630 nm, power density of 800 mW/cm2, and an irradiation time of 20 min per point, delivering an output energy of 470 J over an irradiation area of approximately 5 cm in diameter above the lesion. The day after PDT, 50 mg of Sintilimab was injected into the tumor lesion, with an additional 50 mg administered intravenously in 100 ml of NS. Postoperative skin swelling was managed with daily iodine disinfection to aid wound healing. By the third day, the wound scabbed (Fig. 1b), and follow-ups occurred on days 1, 5, 7, and 30 post-PDT (Fig. 1). By day 7, significant necrosis and scabbing were observed, with surrounding exudation and swelling (Fig. 1c). By day 30, the lesion had noticeably reduced in size, with fewer peritumoral nodules (Fig. 1d). One month later, a second PDT session further reduced the lesion, achieving clinical remission (Fig. 1a–e). Despite initial success, the patient declined further PDT due to intolerable pain. Discussion

CSCC, a common non-melanoma skin cancer, manifests as firm, or hyperkeratotic nodules, potentially ulcerating centrally [2]. However, the local recurrence rate of CSCC patients with poor pathological grade may be as high as 41% [5]. Late-stage CSCC poses significant treatment challenges.

PDT, a non-invasive cancer treatment, activates photosensitizers under light to convert triplet oxygen into highly cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS), killing tumor cells and adjacent vessels [4]. Importantly, PDT induces immunogenic cell death (ICD) in tumor cells. ICD involves apoptosis accompanied by the release of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). These released antigens or DAMPs stimulate antigen-presenting cell maturation, enhancing antigen presentation efficiency and promoting effector T cell proliferation and infiltration to boost anti-tumor immune responses in solid tumors [6].

This study presents a case of using intratumoral injection of hemoglobin to treat CSCC recurrence, challenging the traditional intravenous injection method. Hematoporphyrin has traditionally been used for PDT in malignant tumors, but intravenous administration may not achieve effective concentrations within cells, leading to insufficient ROS generation and unsatisfactory therapeutic effects. Wang et al. [7] treated CSCC patients with intratumoral injection of photosensitizer HpD and achieved good therapeutic effects, which supports the effectiveness and safety of local injection of photosensitizer as PDT therapy.

Notably, we analyzed the blood test indicators of the patient before and after PDT. We found that on the 3rd day after PDT, the number of neutrophils in the patient's peripheral blood significantly increased. Interestingly, the numbers of T cells and NK cells showed a downward trend. Combined with the previous studies of Cecic and Theodoraki [8, 9], we speculate that this change is because innate immunity is first activated after PDT treatment, and a large number of neutrophils are activated. The early peripheral blood lymphocyte reduction may be due to the delayed maturation and activation of T cells, and the previous lymphocytes accumulated in the tumor. One month after treatment, the patient's T cells and NK cells increased significantly, indicating that the patient's adaptive immune response had been activated. In a study on PDT treatment for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma patients (n = 9), it was found that after 24 h of treatment, the number of T lymphocytes in the peripheral circulation of patients decreased, while it gradually increased from 1 to 4 weeks after PDT [9]. This is consistent with our observation results. Yuan et al.'s study [10] showed that the combination therapy of PDT and PD-L1 inhibitors can effectively inhibit the growth of local and distant tumors in colorectal cancer mice, and provide better protection by enhancing CD8 + T cell response. In addition, in the CSCC mouse model, the combination of PDT and PD-L1 inhibitors significantly increased the generation of tumor specific effector T cells, demonstrating the potential improvement effect of combination therapy in CSCC [11].

However, there are currently no relevant clinical studies on the combination of intratumoral injection of HpD and immunotherapy. This further demonstrates the innovation of our research and provides valuable insights into the combined application of immunotherapy and PDT.

In this case, we did not strictly adhere to photoprotection measures except during the procedure. The patient had no complications related to HpD, Sintilimab, or light exposure during PDT, with significant pain as the only reported issue. PDT combined with immunotherapy proved viable for the patient's limited options. After two treatments, the lesion size significantly reduced, perilesional nodules disappeared, and immune cell counts increased. However, this report has certain limitations. Firstly, due to the patient's inability to continue treatment due to pain, we were unable to obtain CT scan results after treatment. Therefore, we cannot determine changes in the depth of lesion infiltration, and can only calculate changes in the area of the lesion. Moreover, the depth of PDT radiation can only reach 7 mm. According to the patient's pre-treatment CT scan, the depth of tumor infiltration into subcutaneous connective tissue is thicker, making it difficult to cover deeper tumor tissue and cells. Therefore, the original intention of combining ICB is to expand the range of PDT light sensitive killing of tumor tissue. However, due to the abundant nerves on the surface of the skin, during photodynamic therapy, although powerful painkillers such as Bucinnazine Hydrochloride Injection, Morphine Hydrachloride Injection, and Fentanyl Transdermal Patches are used, the patient's subjective perception of pain still remains during the light exposure process, which affects the continuity of light exposure and may lead to a decrease in efficacy. Secondly, the patient's tumor has a large area and deep infiltration, requiring a more aggressive treatment plan for comprehensive management. However, considering the objective factors of the patient's body, it is difficult to tolerate combination therapy with more drugs. Therefore, it has not been able to activate the tumor's immune microenvironment more strongly and enhance the efficacy of PD-L1 inhibitors more effectively. I believe that local injection or external application of chemotherapy drugs is feasible. Thirdly, as a single case, this is only a preliminary exploration to prove that local injection of photosensitizers is feasible, safe, and inexpensive. In the future, more comprehensive use of PDT combination therapy will be applied, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and so on. We believe HpD-PDT combined with immunotherapy is a viable treatment for intractable cutaneous malignant ulcers.
