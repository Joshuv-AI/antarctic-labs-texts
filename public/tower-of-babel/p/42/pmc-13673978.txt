Inflatable mediastinoscopic esophagectomy for refractory benign esophageal fistulas: An effective therapeutical methods
Authors: Changchun Wang, Liwei Xu, Weitian Wei, Qiong He, Kelong Shao, Tianxing Ying, Weimin Mao, Qixun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveFor refractory benign esophageal fistulas unresponsive to endoscopic therapy, inflatable mediastinoscopic esophagectomy with primary reconstruction effectively addressed thoracic adhesions in 6 cases.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 6 patients with refractory benign esophageal fistulas treated, documenting etiology, preoperative treatment, surgical techniques, and postoperative results.ResultsThe causes were spontaneous esophageal rupture in 2 cases and iatrogenic damage in 4 cases. Disease duration ranged from 1 month to 13 years. One patient had an esophagopulmonary fistula, and 5 patients had empyema. The upper and intermediate esophagus were dissected using inflatable mediastinoscopy. The lower esophagus was dissected using laparoscopy (4 cases) or laparotomy (2 cases). All patients underwent gastric tube reconstruction with a cervical hand-sewn anastomosis. One patient experienced a cervical anastomotic leak, which was resolved with drainage within 2 weeks. The esophagopulmonary fistula resolved on the first postoperative day, and the empyema was cured in 3 to 4 weeks.ConclusionsInflatable mediastinoscopic esophagectomy with primary gastric tube reconstruction is a safe and effective treatment for refractory benign esophageal fistulas that have failed conservative therapies.

Central Message

Inflatable mediastinoscopic esophagectomy with primary gastric tube reconstruction is a safe and effective treatment for refractory benign esophageal fistulas that have failed conservative therapies.

Perspective

We developed a safe and effective treatment for refractory benign esophageal fistulas.

Benign esophageal fistulas in adults are usually caused by spontaneous rupture or iatrogenic injury. Initial therapies focus on fistula closure to save the esophagus. Some patients acquire chronic esophageal, pleural, or pulmonary fistulas, which can lead to recurrent empyema, pneumonia, and sepsis. Surgical esophagectomy is ultimately essential but difficult because of severe thoracic adhesions.

With developments in minimally invasive techniques, inflatable mediastinoscopic esophagectomy is gaining popularity. Originally applied in elderly patients with esophageal cancer with comorbidities,1 this method can reduce morbidity, mortality, and recovery time.2,3 A 2025 meta-analysis verified its safety and viability as an alternative to thoracoscopic esophagectomy.4

Inflatable mediastinoscopic esophagectomy is gradually maturing. Inflatable mediastinoscopy avoids thoracic adhesions, simplifies resection, and reduces morbidity in refractory benign fistulas. We present 6 cases of refractory fistulas cured using this approach.

Materials and Methods

The ethics committee of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital approved this retrospective study (IRB-2025-791) on June 23, 2025 and waived the need for individual consent. We reviewed 6 patients with refractory benign esophageal fistulas treated at our institution. Condition of the patient at admission

The surgery followed Hu Yang's approach,5 as a transcervical, transhiatal esophagectomy performed under inflatable mediastinoscopy (Figure 1). All patients took 30 to 50 mL of olive oil orally 4 hours before the operation. During the operation, the thoracic duct was milky white, which not only helps to avoid injury but also enables immediate detection of any injury. To avoid harm during after surgeries, cervical dissection began with the exposing of the left recurrent laryngeal nerve. With a 5-mm diameter Ligasure (produced by Medtronic), the esophagus was freed from the neck to the level of inferior pulmonary vein, along with the esophageal wall. Lymph node dissection was unnecessary because it was not esophageal cancer. In locations with dense adhesions or scarring, the esophageal wall was cut or transected. If the esophagus was cut through the mediastinal path, resulting in mediastinal contamination, the mediastinum should be rinsed clean with normal saline after the esophagus was removed from the neck. During the abdominal dissection, the surgeon first released the stomach before dissecting the lower esophagus through the diaphragmatic hiatus. The crucial element was to stick closely to the esophagus in order to avoid damaging the surrounding tissues. Case 1's esophageal fistula was close to the right inferior pulmonary vein and communicated with the right lower lobe. Active open abdomen surgery was selected. Suture was used to close the pulmonary fistula after it was cut (Video 1). The lesion of case 2 was located in the cardia, forming a dense scar. As a result, the abdomen was opened, and the diaphragmatic hiatus was cut to complete the dissociation of the lower esophagus. The fistula was severed above the diaphragmatic hiatus, and the left pleural fistula was sutured. The remaining 4 cases were completed the dissociation of the lower esophagus under laparoscopy. However, the fistulas were far from the esophageal hiatus and had not been sutured. Only mediastinal drainage tubes were inserted. The gastric tube and esophagus were anastomosed at the neck. For empyema, just the thoracic drainage tube was retained after admission, and no intervention was performed during the procedure. The absence of thoracotomy is one of the advantages of this technique. With the resolution of the esophageal fistula, the chest infection will gradually improve with good drainage. Discussion

Six patients had long-standing fistulas (1 month to 13 years) that had not responded to previous treatments such as stenting, surgical repair, or drainage. We removed the esophagus with an inflatable mediastinoscope and rebuilt it with a gastric tube in 1 step. All 6 patients were cured. Inflatable mediastinoscopy circumvented thoracic adhesions, enabling safe esophageal resection. The surrounding tissue space in the esophagus without a lesion was highly flexible and easy to operate. In the area of esophageal fistula, we managed by mucosal resection. Thus, we avoided dissecting the area around the esophagus where adhesion was dense and injuring critical structures (such as the inferior pulmonary vein, bronchi, and so on). Gastric tube reconstruction via the esophageal bed promoted mediastinal healing.

The fistula itself could be sutured (in the first 2 cases of this study) or not (in the last 4 cases). According to the findings of this study, when the esophageal fistula was treated, the empyema or airway fistula was healed in a month merely through thoracic drainage. For patients with fistulas located above the cardia, the diaphragmatic hiatus cannot be pulled open due to scar fibrosis, and it was difficult to complete esophageal dissociation in the lower mediastinum under laparoscopy. To avoid accidental injury, it was reasonable to incise the diaphragmatic hiatus by open surgery (case 2). For patients with diaphragmatic hiatus without scars, laparoscopic dissection of the lower esophagus could achieve better exposure. The disease histories of the 4 patients in our investigation lasted more than 6 months, and the inflammatory response around the lesion was reasonably confined. The surgeons did not have any difficulty dissecting the esophagus. Case3's disease course lasted 1 month, and dissecting the esophagus was not difficult.

We searched PubMed for the terms “esophageal fistula AND mediastinoscopy,” “esophageal rupture AND mediastinoscopy,” and “esophageal perforation AND mediastinoscopy” in the complete text and found no relevant studies.

The most typical way for treating esophageal perforation caused by nonmalignant diseases, such as esophageal rupture, sharp injury, iatrogenic injury, is to combine primary repair and thoracic drainage. Endoscopic treatment, such as endoscopic stenting,6,7 clips,8 filling esophageal fistulas with polyglycine tablets and fibrin glue,9 endovascular vacuum-assisted closure and repair,10 is commonly used for those whose primary repair fails and an esophageal fistula forms. According to the literature,8 the overall success rate of endoscopic treatment is 50%. If the fistula does not heal within 6 months, the chances of clinical success are significantly diminished.

If the patient's condition persists and does not heal after using the aforementioned endoscopic therapy procedures, surgical intervention is indicated. Takise reported that the esophageal fistula with omentum was repaired using thoracoscopy and laparoscopy.11 The benefit of this approach was that the esophagus was preserved. The thoracic cavity adhesion was insufficient to prevent the surgeon from exposing the esophagus and locating the fistula, which was required for the procedure to be performed successfully. It was unquestionably the greatest therapy option for those who satisfied the criteria for this procedure. The 6 patients in this study did not appear to fit these criteria. For patients with extensive pleural adhesions caused by previous surgery or inflammation, which make thoracotomy difficult, or severe pulmonary parenchymal diseases that make them unable to tolerate 1-lung ventilation, inflatable mediastinoscopic esophagectomy is a valuable approach.

The greatest challenge of esophagectomy via the mediastinal approach is intraoperative bleeding. In more than 200 cases of esophageal cancer resection with inflatable mediastoscopy in the past, we have encountered 1 case of abnormal venous bleeding during the operation. However, none of the surgeries in this study required lymph node dissection. Only the esophagus needed to be removed. In areas with severe adhesions, the esophagus could be damaged or a small amount of esophageal tissue could remain, thus avoiding damage to blood vessels and trachea. Only the second patient had thoracic duct injury. We clamped the thoracic duct at both ends of the rupture using Hem-o-lok and avoided postoperative chylothorax.

Limitations

Despite these advantages, certain limitations of this technique must be acknowledged. Surgeons should have mature experience in cervical mediastinoscopy and esophageal surgery. The technique requires the patient to tolerate a supine position with neck hyperextension. It is not suitable for patients with cervical spine disease, severe kyphosis, or other conditions that prevent adequate neck extension. For patients with a history of gastric surgery (eg, gastrectomy), the stomach may not be available for esophageal reconstruction. In such cases, colonic can be considered when necessary, but this alternative carries additional risk and should be weighed carefully against the benefits of fistula eradication. Esophageal resection may reduce quality of life in comparison to organ preservation. This is a cancer center, and we primarily accept referrals from patients who have failed other treatments. As a result, we only treated a few patients with benign esophageal fistula and were unable to conduct a prospective investigation. Inflatable mediastinoscopic esophagectomy is a potential treatment option for endoscopically refractory cases.

Conclusions

This study reports 6 cases of refractory benign esophageal fistulas treated with inflatable mediastinoscopic esophagectomy and primary gastric tube reconstruction. The technique avoided thoracotomy, and enabled single‑stage esophagectomy with cervical anastomosis. All fistulas resolved; 1 cervical anastomotic leak healed with conservative management. No major complications occurred. This approach is safe and effective for patients who have failed endoscopic therapy, although it requires experienced surgeons and careful patient selection.
