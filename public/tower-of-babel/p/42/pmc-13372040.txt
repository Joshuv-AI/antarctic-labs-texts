Genome-Wide Analysis of an Endangered Axolotl Endemic to Mexico Reveals Genomic Variation Associated with Body Condition, Environment, and Infection by a Pathogenic Fungus
Authors: Enrique Soto-Cortés, M Delia Basanta, Víctor Ávila-Akerberg, Gabriela Castellanos-Morales, Luis E Eguiarte, Jaime Gasca-Pineda, Tanya M González Martínez, Eria A Rebollar, Matteo Fumagalli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmphibians are the most threatened group of vertebrates on the planet due to habitat loss, climate change, and the emerging disease chytridiomycosis, caused by the pathogenic fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis. Axolotls, species from the genus Ambystoma endemic to Mexico, are a highly vulnerable group of salamanders with restricted distributions that are severely affected by habitat fragmentation, invasive species, and pollution. In this context, population genomic studies are urgently needed to implement adequate conservation and management decisions. In this study, we identified abiotic and biotic factors associated with the genomic diversity and structure of Ambystoma altamirani through genome-wide single-nucleotide polymorphism analyses. Based on 110,761 neutral single-nucleotide polymorphisms from 99 individuals across five populations of A. altamirani, we found that genomic diversity of individuals was positively correlated with their body condition. Moreover, patterns of genetic structure identified three main genetic clusters, which were correlated with habitat environmental differences. Specifically, Elevation and temperature-associated variables significantly contributed to the genomic structuring of A. altamirani populations. Latent factor mixed models identified 1,670 single-nucleotide polymorphisms associated with environmental variables as well as 80 and 47 single-nucleotide polymorphisms associated with the biotic variables B. dendrobatidis infection intensity and body condition, respectively. We identified single-nucleotide polymorphisms significantly associated with differences in B. dendrobatidis infection and body condition among homozygous or heterozygous genotypes. Overall, our study teases apart some of the factors likely influencing axolotl genetic diversity and population structuring, which should be considered for conservation strategies for this and other endangered amphibian species.Keywords: genomic diversity, salamander, axolotl, SNPs, Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis, body condition

In light of the threats that amphibian populations are facing worldwide due to habitat loss, climate change, and emergent diseases, it is fundamental to explore the potential drivers shaping neutral and adaptive genomic variation. Here, using genome-wide analyses of wild populations of the endangered axolotl Ambystoma altamirani, we identified that body condition, environmental conditions, and pathogen infection are factors associated to genomic variation and population structure. Our findings contribute to fundamental knowledge needed to implement genomic-based management and conservation strategies of A. altamirani populations and establish the first basis for genome-wide single-nucleotide polymorphism analyses for Ambystoma species.

Introduction

Amphibians are the most threatened group of vertebrates in the world, with around 40% of all amphibian species under some threat category according to the IUCN (Luedtke et al. 2023). Habitat degradation due to human activities, including land use change and pollution, is the main factor impacting amphibian populations worldwide (Wake and Vredenburg 2008; Luedtke et al. 2023). Climate change, specifically changes in temperature and precipitation, are also causing population declines in amphibians (Winter et al. 2016). In addition, chytridiomycosis, an emergent disease caused mostly by the fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd), has caused worldwide population declines and extinctions of amphibian species (Lips et al. 2006; Berger et al. 2016). In sum, all these factors can have detrimental effects on populations’ genomic diversity and structure. In this context, the maintenance of genomic diversity within and among populations is critically important for the conservation of threatened species (Willoughby et al. 2015).

It is known that several abiotic and biotic factors can influence genomic diversity and population structure in natural settings (Beebee 2005; Thompson et al. 2021). Abiotic factors, including geographical and environmental conditions, may promote genomic differentiation among populations by reducing population sizes and by disrupting or reducing gene flow, causing isolation by distance (IBD) or isolation by environment (IBE) (Sexton et al. 2014; Jiang et al. 2019; Nava Martinez et al. 2025). Habitat fragmentation (Stevens et al. 2018) and climate change (Pauls et al. 2013) can also impact genetic diversity levels by reducing population sizes and increasing the effects of genetic drift through the loss of connectivity among localities (Frankham 1996). In addition, biotic factors such as infectious diseases can additionally impose strong selective pressures, as a consequence of host–pathogen evolutionary interactions (Van Valen 1974). For example, in boreal toads (Anaxyrus boreas), it was documented that the selective pressure from the presence of the pathogen Bd is stronger than the effect of the environment (Trumbo et al. 2023).

Genetic diversity in natural populations is linked with fitness, where higher levels of heterozygosity are associated with higher fitness (Hansson and Westerberg 2002). In this respect, body condition is a metric of animal health and fitness commonly used to evaluate heterozygosity–fitness correlations (HFCs) (Peig and Green 2010). In amphibians, body condition and body mass have shown positive correlations with genetic diversity in the anurans Rana temporaria and Hyla arborea (Pröhl and Rodríguez 2023). Additional studies along these lines in other amphibian groups, such as salamanders, are needed to understand the relationship between body condition and genomic diversity.

After the United States, Mexico is the second most diverse country for salamander species in the world (Suazo-Ortuño et al. 2023). Some of the most emblematic salamanders from Mexico are axolotls, which belong to the genus Ambystoma (SEMARNAT 2018) and are part of the North American Ambystoma tigrinum complex (Shaffer and Mcknight 1996). Ambystoma altamirani is a facultatively paedomorphic salamander, transforming from aquatic larvae to terrestrial juveniles under certain genetic and/or environmental conditions (Shaffer 1984). The distribution of A. altamirani is restricted to the Sierra de las Cruces and the Chichinautzin Biological Corridor within the Trans Mexican Volcanic Belt (TMVB), inhabiting streams in the highlands surrounded by fir and oak–pine forests (Sunny et al. 2024). This axolotl species is categorized as endangered by the IUCN Red List (IUCN, 2024), and its main threats include changes in habitat conditions due to agricultural activities, urbanization, and deforestation (Lemos-Espinal et al. 2016; Sunny et al. 2024). Overall, these factors might cause reductions of suitable habitats for the species, promoting reductions in connectivity and increases in fragmentation and population isolation (Ruiz-Reyes et al. 2024; Sunny et al. 2024). In addition, biotic factors such as the presence of the pathogen Bd might impact A. altamirani populations, where the average prevalence across populations is high (70.3%) (Basanta et al. 2023). Previous studies based on microsatellites in A. altamirani found significant genetic structuring across populations, suggesting low gene flow and small effective population sizes (Heredia-Bobadilla et al. 2017; Monroy-Vilchis et al. 2019).

In light of previous genetic data and the high prevalence of the pathogen Bd in A. altamirani (Basanta et al. 2023), we hypothesize that abiotic and biotic factors will be associated with varying levels of genetic diversity and genomic structuring across A. altamirani populations. In this study, through the use of double digest restriction-site-associated DNA sequencing (ddRAD-seq) data, we conducted genome-wide analyses of five A. altamirani populations to identify some of the factors that may be influencing population genetic diversity and structure. In addition, we explored the association of specific single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNPs) and genotypes to the evaluated factors.

Results

Heterozygosity in A. altamirani Is Associated with Body Condition

Considering all individuals (N = 99), the mean multilocus heterozygosity (MLH) was 0.40 (SD ± 0.013) and the mean inbreeding coefficient (FIS) was 0.015 (SD ± 0.09). We found significant differences in MLH among localities (Kruskal–Wallis test, χ2 = 46.2, degree of freedom [df] = 4, P-value < 0.001), with locality E having a significantly higher MLH value than the rest (Dunn test, P-value < 0.01; Fig. 1a; Table S1a). Regarding FIS, we did not identify significant differences of values among localities (Kruskal–Wallis test, χ2 = 5.19, df = 4, P-value = 0.26; Fig. 1b). Furthermore, we found differences in MLH between metamorphic stages (Wilcoxon test, W = 571.5, df = 1, P-value < 0.01; Fig. S1a), with nonmetamorphic individuals showing significantly higher MLH than metamorphic individuals. We did not find significant differences in FIS between metamorphic stages (Wilcoxon test, W = 1129, df = 1, P-value > 0.05; Fig. S1b).

a) Multilocus heterozygosity (MLH) and b) inbreeding coefficient (Fis) of the axolotl a. altamirani comparison among sampling localities. Letters in boxplot indicate significant differences among localities based on Dunn tests (Table S1a).

With respect to the presence of the pathogen Bd, we found a total prevalence of 63%. Pairwise comparisons among localities showed that locality A had a significantly lower Bd prevalence than C, D, and E (χ2, df = 4, P-value < 0.05; Fig. S2a, Table S1b). We did not find significant differences in infection intensity (log10(Bd load)) among sampling localities (Kruskal–Wallis test, W = 2.5, df = 4, P-value = 0.64; Fig. S2b). Moreover, we did not identify differences in Bd prevalence (χ2 = 2.94, df = 1, P-value = 0.09; Fig. S2c) or infection intensity (Wilcoxon test, W = 423, df = 1, P-value = 0.054; Fig. S2d) between metamorphic and nonmetamorphic infected individuals.

We built a linear mixed model (LMM) to identify the factors associated with MLH and FIS values, considering body condition, metamorphic stage, and Bd infection intensity as fixed variables (Fig. 2a). Locality was considered a random-effect variable to take into account the potential genetic differences among sampling sites. The LMM showed that body condition was the only variable with a significant positive association with MLH (t = 2.1, df = 70, P-value < 0.05). The significant association was confirmed with a Spearman correlation test in which the body condition was positively correlated with MLH (rho = 0.37, df = 93, P-value < 0.001; Fig. 2b). In contrast, the LMM showed no significant associations of FIS with any of the predictor variables (Table S2).

a) Linear Mixed Model (LMM) result of the effects of body condition, metamorphic stage, and Bd infection intensity on MLH. b) Spearman correlation between A. altamirani body condition (scale mass index) and MLH.

Ambystoma altamirani Populations Are Genetically Structured and Potentially Influenced by Environmental Conditions

We conducted a principal component analysis (PCA) using the neutral SNPs dataset and found that individuals from five localities were grouped into three main genetic clusters: Cluster 1 (locality E), Cluster 2 (locality B), and Cluster 3 composed of localities A, C, and D (Fig. 3a). We calculated pairwise FST to explore genetic differences among the five sampling locations and found that the highest genetic differentiation was found between locality E and all the other localities, with E and B having the highest genetic differences (FST = 0.16), while the lowest pairwise differentiation was found between A, C, and D (FST ≈ 0.06) (Fig. 3b). The ADMIXTURE analysis mirrored PCA and FST results, where the cross-validation error estimates showed that the model fit was optimized at K = 3 (Fig. 3c; Fig. S3). At K = 5, the genetic clusters detected corresponded to the sampling localities (Fig. 3d).

Genetic structure of A. altamirani. a) PCA of all individuals using neutral SNPs showing three genetic clusters. b) Pairwise FST genetic differences among the five sampled locations. ADMIXTURE analysis plot showing individual genetic cluster assignations at c) K = 3 and d) K = 5.

Mantel tests showed that Nei's genetic distance in A. altamirani was positively correlated with environmental distance across localities (R = 0.85, P-value < 0.05), while controlling for geographical distance. In contrast, geographical distances were not significantly associated with Nei's population genetic distances (R = 0.24, P-value = 0.36), while controlling for environmental distance. When exploring environmental differences across localities through a PCA obtained from environmental variables (Fig. S4), we found three main clusters that mirrored A. altamirani genetic clusters (Fig. 3a).

To identify the abiotic factors that could influence A. altamirani genetic structure, we performed a redundancy analysis (RDA) (Fig. 4). This analysis showed that the constrained variables explained in sum 20.4% of the genetic structure variation. Permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) results showed that elevation (Elevation) had the strongest effect on genetic structure variation (pseudo-F = 7.6, df = 1, P-value < 0.001), followed by dissolved oxygen (DO, pseudo-F = 5.01, df = 1, P-value < 0.001), water temperature (WaterTemp, pseudo-F = 3.41, df = 1, P-value < 0.001), water pH (WaterpH, pseudo-F = 3.05, df = 1, P-value < 0.001), mean maximum temperature (MeanMaxTemp, pseudo-F = 1.92, df = 1, P-value < 0.001), and mean minimum temperature (MeanMinTemp, pseudo-F = 1.22, df = 1, P-value < 0.05) (Table S3).

SNPs Putatively Associated with Environmental Factors, Bd Infection Intensity, and Body Condition

We conducted a latent factor mixed model (LFMM) to identify specific SNPs that were significantly associated with abiotic (Elevation, DO, WaterTemp) and biotic (Bd infection intensity, body condition) factors. The model identified a total of 1,797 SNPs significantly associated with one or more factors (Table S5). We compared LFMM SNP dataset with RDA outlier SNPs datasets, finding that 788 SNPs (29.9%) were identified by the two approaches, while 842 (31.9%) were only identified by pcadapt/RDA and 1,009 (38.2%) only by LFMM (Fig. S5).

Based on the LFMM SNP dataset, we identified 290 uniquely associated with Elevation, 507 with WaterTemp, 387 with DO, 78 with Bd infection intensity, and 45 with body condition (Fig. 5a). No SNPs were shared across all factors, and very few of them were shared between pairs of factors, with the exception of 449 SNPs that were shared between Elevation and WaterTemp. Annotation of SNPs (Table S5) showed that 57% of them were located in intergenic regions, the rest were located in coding regions, where 43% were annotated as intron variants, 0.57% as exon variants, and 0.47% as untranslated region of messenger RNA (UTR) variants (Fig. 5b).

WaterTemp, water temperature; DO, dissolved oxygen.

When we evaluated differences in Bd infection intensity and body condition among individuals’ genotypes (homozygous reference: 0/0; heterozygous: 0/1; homozygous alternative: 1/1), we identified 13 and 19 SNPs in coding regions whose genotypes presented significant differences in Bd infection intensity (Table S6) and body condition (Table S7), respectively. Specifically, we identified five SNPs in which heterozygous individuals presented lower Bd infection intensity than homozygous individuals and four SNPs in which heterozygous individuals presented higher Bd infection intensity than homozygous individuals (Fig. 6). Regarding body condition, we found 14 SNPs in which heterozygous individuals presented higher body condition than homozygous individuals, and one SNP in which heterozygous individuals presented lower body condition than homozygous individuals (Fig. S6).

Discussion

In this study, we described the genome-wide genetic diversity of the axolotl A. altamirani using ddRAD-seq data to elucidate the potential factors that shape their genomic diversity and population structure. Within a ≈300 km2 region, we identified significant genomic diversity variation, strong population genetic differentiation, and outlier SNPs putatively associated with individuals’ phenotypic variation and habitat environmental conditions.

Genomic Diversity Is Associated with A. altamirani Body Condition

We found that genomic diversity measured by MLH was significantly higher in one site (locality E) compared to the others, in agreement with a previous study in A. altamirani using microsatellites, which showed heterozygosity differences between two localities (Monroy-Vilchis et al. 2019). Our results target locality E as a population with higher genomic diversity than the rest and, thus, should be considered a key population for conservation management. Moreover, attention should be paid to other localities like locality B, which has been severely affected by illegal logging, causing streams to almost disappear in recent years (López-García and Navarro-Cerrillo 2021). In addition, more localities should be studied to obtain a wider perspective on the genetic diversity and inbreeding status of the species along the southern extreme of its distribution range.

We identified that body condition had a positive correlation with MLH. Body condition is associated with animal health and is considered an important determinant of fitness (Peig and Green 2010). Thus, body condition and related metrics such as body size, body mass, and weight have commonly been used as metrics for HFCs in vertebrates, including birds (Monceau et al. 2013), mammals (Brommer et al. 2015), reptiles (Moss et al. 2019), and amphibians (Allentoft and O’Brien 2010; Pröhl and Rodríguez 2023). In agreement with previous studies, we show that in A. altamirani individuals, higher genomic diversity correlates with higher body condition. While HFCs in natural populations are widespread (Chapman et al. 2009), it is worth noticing that threatened species can also be affected by population bottlenecks modulating heterozygosity–fitness relationships (Grueber et al. 2008). Our results suggest that decreases in genomic diversity in A. altamirani populations could potentially have a negative impact on individuals’ health and fitness, highlighting the importance of maintaining genomic diversity in these populations.

Populations of A. altamirani Show Genetic Structuring Associated with Differences in Habitat Conditions

The identification of abiotic and biotic factors influencing genetic diversity patterns across space is one of the central goals of population genetics and molecular ecology (Foll and Gaggiotti 2006), especially in the current context of climate change and habitat loss (Pauls et al. 2013; Schlaepfer et al. 2018). In A. altamirani, we found three main genetic clusters with relatively high genetic differentiation. We identified that genetic differentiation is partially associated with environmental differences, suggesting that processes such as IBE might be influencing populations genetic populations (Wang and Bradburd 2014).

In A. altamirani, we identified that elevation and temperature-related variables are partially associated with population genetic structuring. Elevation as a factor influencing genetic structure has been reported in Ambystoma macrodactylum populations from Montana (Giordano et al. 2007) and in Rana luteiventris from Montana and Idaho, USA (Funk et al. 2005). Regarding temperature, in other amphibians, environmental temperature affected life history traits that might subsequently have an impact on population structure, on metamorphosis rate (Bellakhal et al. 2014), as well as individual's behavior and reproduction (Dervo et al. 2016; Dalpasso et al. 2023). In addition, IBD has been reported in Ambystoma annulatum, A. maculatum, and A. opacum populations in Massachusetts and Missouri, USA (Whiteley et al. 2014; Peterman et al. 2015). In agreement with a previous work evaluating IBD in an Ambystoma species from Central Mexico (Sunny et al. 2014), we did not identify such process in A. altamirani. However, a larger sampling across the species distribution range will be necessary to further explore the effect of geographical distance in A. altamirani genetic structure.

It is relevant to mention that many other environmental variables and landscape features were not measured in our study and could also contribute to A. altamirani genetic structuring. For example, A. altamirani populations from Sierra de las Cruces (our study region) are highly affected by habitat fragmentation, agricultural activities, and urbanization (López-García and Navarro-Cerrillo 2021; Sunny et al. 2024). Environmental niche modeling and functional connectivity analyses showed that rivers, altitude, and combination of Pinus and Abies coverage were positively associated with the potential distribution of A. altamirani, while agriculture and urbanization had a negative association (Ruiz-Reyes et al. 2024). Differences in connectivity levels in A. altamirani populations could potentially impact individuals’ dispersion among populations to subsequently promote genetic isolation. Future studies should incorporate these and other parameters to have a broader picture of the environmental and landscape effects on population genomic diversity and structure.

SNPs Putatively Linked to Environmental Conditions, Bd Infection, and Body Condition

By using reduced representation genomic sequencing methods, such as ddRAD sequencing (Peterson et al. 2012), it is possible to identify SNPs that could be subject to natural selection as part of an adaptive process to local conditions. These candidate SNPs appear as outliers from the patterns observed at the neutral genomic background (Allendorf et al. 2010, 2022).

In amphibians, studies trying to identify SNPs associated with health and disease states are scarce. In boreal toads (Bufo boreas) from Colorado, a study found a high number of SNPs associated with Bd infection and a lesser amount linked to environmental conditions (Trumbo et al. 2023). In contrast, we identified a higher number of SNPs associated with environmental variables (1,670 SNPs) than with Bd infection (80 SNPs) or body condition (47 SNPs). Until this work, no studies had evaluated the relationship between Bd infection and host genomic variation in an Ambystoma species. A genome-wide study in the anuran Eleutherodactylus coqui (Torres-Sánchez and Longo 2022), showed that individual heterozygosity was associated with a reduced probability of Bd infection. Even though we did not find an association between individual host genomic diversity (MLH) and Bd infection intensity, we did identify SNPs whose genotypes were associated with changes in Bd infection intensity. These findings prompt further investigation into the role of these abiotic and biotic factors as drivers of potentially adaptive genomic variation in A. altamirani, as well as the potential impact of the SNPs on Bd host susceptibility or tolerance.

In addition, we identified a few SNP loci whose genotypes were associated with individuals’ body condition variation. Interestingly, heterozygous individuals for most of these SNPs presented higher body condition values. Further studies are needed to evaluate if the identified loci or others are under a heterozygous advantage mechanism that could be maintaining genomic diversity. In other vertebrate species, through genome-wide association studies, associations between proxies of body condition such as body size and body weight and SNP loci have previously been detected (Cai et al. 2018; Jiang et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2024). In amphibians, a study exploring the genetic architecture of body size in Greek smooth newt (Lissotriton graecus) found SNPs putatively associated with this trait (Toli et al. 2024). Our findings raise questions regarding the putative role of these SNPs in individuals’ body condition and overall fitness.

Our work contributes to the understanding of biotic and abiotic factors that may shape genomic diversity and structuring in an Ambystoma species that is highly threatened by anthropogenic activities. In light of climate change, diseases, and habitat deterioration, our findings provide baseline information that could inform management and conservation strategies for this species. Moreover, our work provides a framework to explore the factors shaping genomic diversity and structuring in other amphibian species.

Materials and Methods

Study Site and Axolotl Sampling

We sampled 99 A. altamirani individuals at five localities from the Sierra de las Cruces in the State of Mexico (Fig. 7). This is the largest sampling effort of A. altamirani for genomic diversity and genetic structure studies conducted so far. Sierra de las Cruces is a small mountain range (total length of ∼110 km) shared among the States of Mexico, Morelos, and Mexico City (García-Palomo et al. 2008). The landscapes are predominantly fir and pine–oak forests with clear evidence of habitat deterioration due to the impact of human activities (Sunny et al 2024). Most of the sampled localities are within the A. altamirani distribution range reported by the IUCN. We could not obtain samples from localities from the southern distribution due to local insecurity problems caused by illegal logging and timber trafficking.

Five localities in the highlands of central Mexico where A. altamirani individuals were sampled. Map on the left shows A. altamirani localities sampled and species potential distribution area in gray (IUCN, 2024). A zoomed map on the right shows the sampled localities indicated by A, B, C, D, and E. Ramified lines represent the streams. Values between parentheses indicate the elevation of each locality in meters above sea level.

From April 28 to May 1 of 2021, we collected tissue samples from 75 A. altamirani individuals from localities A, B, D, and E. In addition, we obtained 24 tissue samples from locality C in October 2019 as part of a previous survey (Martínez-Ugalde et al. 2022), but samples had not been processed until this study. The exact location and original names of localities are not provided to protect the species from illegal extraction. Recognizing that gilled individuals of A. altamirani could be either juvenile or paedomorphic adults, we classified gilled axolotl as nonmetamorphic and nongilled axolotl as metamorphic (Table 1).

We captured individuals within the streams using clean hand nets. We put each animal in individual plastic containers containing stream water and manipulated them with sterile nitrile gloves to obtain tissue samples. For each axolotl, we clipped 2 mm of tip tail tissue, and preserved each sample in a 1.5 mL tube with 200 μL of RNA Later (Invitrogen, USA). We stored samples at 4 °C until arrival at the laboratory where we stored them at −80 °C until processing.

Collection of Biotic Variables

Before collecting the tissue samples, we swabbed the skin of A. altamirani individuals to detect the pathogen Bd. To reduce the proportion of transitory microorganisms, we rinsed each animal with 25 mL of sterile water before swabbing (Lauer et al. 2007). Following rinsing, we swabbed each axolotl 30 times (5 times in ventral and dorsal surface and 5 times on each limb joint) with a sterile swab (MW-113, Medical Wire). All swabs were stored in 1.5 mL sterile microcentrifuge tubes with 170 μL of DNA shield (Zymo Research, United States) and kept at 4 °C until arrival to the laboratory where they were stored at a −80 °C freezer until processing.

After collecting tissue and swab samples, we weighed (body mass) and measured (snout–vent length, SVL) each individual. We calculated body condition (Peig and Green 2010) using body mass and SVL as a fitness metrics approximation using the “scaled mass index”. This method predicts a standardized estimation of mass based on a specific size (L0), and uses as a scaling exponent (bSMA) the slope from a standardized major axis (SMA) regression between log10(mass) and log10(SVL). The SMA regression was conducted using the function lmodel2 from the lmodel2 package (Legendre 2024). After data visualization using a boxplot, we removed one sample that presented an outlier value.

Collection of Abiotic Variables

We characterized the physicochemical conditions of the aquatic environment using a calibrated electrical probe (Hanna Instruments HI98194) at three different points along each stream (locality). We measured water temperature (°C), DO (%), and water pH. We also recorded coordinates and elevation for each locality using a GPS. In addition, based on the collection month at each sampling locality, we obtained monthly climatic data (precipitation as well as minimum and maximum temperature) available in WorldClim 2.1 with a spatial resolution of 30 s and the latest data available from 2021. We constructed a matrix that included the following variables: Elevation, WaterTemp, WaterpH, DO, MeanMaxTemp, MeanMinTemp, and precipitation. Henceforth, when we refer to the use of the environmental matrix, we mean the use of these seven variables. The complete metadata associated with each individual (biotic and abiotic variables) is included in Table S4. Environmental differences among sampled localities were explored with a PCA, using the environmental matrix.

Molecular Analyses for the Detection of Bd

We isolated microbial DNA from all swabs using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit following the manufacturer's protocol including a pretreatment with lysozyme (Rebollar et al. 2016). DNA extracts were used as templates in TaqMan real-time PCR assays (Boyle et al. 2004). We performed qPCRs with a final reaction volume of 15 μL, and each sample was run in triplicate. Negative controls (sterile water) were also included in triplicate on each 96-well plate. To quantify the pathogen load (intensity), we used six standards of DNA synthetic fragments to estimate the number of Bd internal transcribed spacer (ITS) copies on each sample (1, 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000, and 100,000 ITS copies) (Basanta et al. 2023). We considered the samples positive for Bd if at least two of three qPCRs had a signal above 1 ITS copy. Samples that showed a positive signal in only one replicate of the two were run for a third time: If the sample had a positive signal in the third replicate, we classified it as positive. Bd infection intensity per sample was obtained by averaging the Bd ITS copies estimated by qPCR from replicates.

Prevalence and Infection Intensity of Bd

We made all statistical analyses and plots in R v. 4.1.1 (R Core Team 2021). The number of ITS copies (values of Bd infection intensity) per individual were log-transformed (log10(Bd load)) to approximate a normal distribution. To test for differences in Bd infection intensity among localities and between metamorphic stages, we used the Kruskal–Wallis test. To identify differences in Bd prevalence among localities and between metamorphic stages, we applied Pearson's χ2 test using the functions pairwise_prop_test and prop_test from the rstatix package, respectively (R Core Team 2021).

Molecular Processing of Samples for Genome-Wide Population Genetics

We isolated genomic DNA from A. altamirani tissue using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer's protocol, modified by adding 4 μL of RNase (100 mg/mL) for 2 min at room temperature at 25 °C. DNA yields were quantified using a Qubit Fluorometric Quantitation assay (Invitrogen, USA). Only samples containing >20 ng/μL of total DNA were considered acceptable for sequencing.

We used a ddRAD method (Peterson et al. 2012) to genotype SNPs. Libraries were prepared and sequenced in Illumina HiSeq (2 X 300 PE) in one single lane at the University of Minnesota Genomics Center using the restriction enzymes SbfI-HF + TaqI. Taql is sensitive to overlapping methylation. Enzymes were selected based on in silico analysis using the Ambystoma mexicanum genome (AmbMex60DD), which is the only available genome within the Ambystoma genus (Nowoshilow et al. 2018). The sequencing yielded a dataset of a mean 21 M raw reads per sample.

Bioinformatic Processing of ddRAD-Seq Data

We visualized raw sequences obtained with ddRAD-seq using FastQC (v0.11.9) (Andrews 2010). We removed adapters and trimmed low-quality data using TrimGalore (v0.6.6; https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore). We mapped clean sequences to A. mexicanum reference genome (AmbMex60DD) (Nowoshilow et al. 2018) with bwa (v0.7.17-r1188) using the -mem algorithm (Li and Durbin 2009). SortSam and MarkDuplicates from Picard (2.27.1; https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) were used to sort BAM files and mark duplicates, respectively. We performed SNPs calling in freebayes (Garrison and Marth 2012), using the parameters –use-best-n-alleles 4 –min-coverage 12. We used VCFtools (Danecek et al. 2011) to remove low-quality SNPs or potential paralogues, and we applied the following filtering criteria: we retained SNPs (1) with two alleles, (2) with a minimum minor allele frequency of ≥0.05, (3) with no missing data, (4) with a P-value threshold > 0.0001 for the Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium test. Finally, to reduce the influence of linkage disequilibrium (LD) on the assessment of population stratification, we considered an LD (r2) threshold to 0.7 for the SNP pairs in a sliding window of 50 SNPs and step size of 5 using PLINK v1.90b7 (Purcell et al. 2007). We removed Indels with VCFtools. The final SNPs number of the filtered dataset was 112,394.

We identified outlier SNPs using two methods to subsequently remove them from the dataset, only keeping potentially neutral SNPs. First, we ran PCAdapt with a burn-in of 200 steps. We chose K = 4, since most of the variation was explained when four principal components were retained, and a false discovery rate (FDR) lower than 0.01. This analysis performs PCA and calculates P-values to identify outliers based on the correlation values between genetic variation and the first principal components. Second, we used a multivariate approach in a RDA (Forester et al. 2018) to estimate the extent to which the explained variance in SNP genotypes was explained by the environmental variables (Table S4). RDA is a constrained linear ordination method that combines multiple linear regression and PCA. Using both approaches, we identified 1,633 outlier SNPs that were removed, leaving a total of 110,761 SNPs. This dataset was called neutral SNPs and was used for genomic diversity and population structure analyses.

Genomic Diversity Analyses

We estimated MLH for all individuals with the package “inbreedR” (Stoffel et al. 2016). MLH is defined as the total number of heterozygous loci in an individual divided by the number of loci typed in an individual. Thus, an MLH of 0.5 means that 50% of an individual's loci are heterozygous. We also calculated the FIS for each individual with plink v1.90b7 (Purcell et al. 2007), using the command –het. The FIS is calculated based on the observed versus expected number of homozygous genotypes and the values range from −1 to 1, where values approaching 1 indicate a complete deficit of heterozygotes and values approaching −1 indicate a complete excess of heterozygotes. To take into account the population effect on FIS estimation, we calculated FIS from individuals within each locality.

After visual examination of the data per locality using boxplots, we removed four samples that presented MLH outlier values and one sample with an outlier value for body condition. We evaluated differences in MLH and FIS mean values among localities and between metamorphic stages (metamorphic vs. nonmetamorphic individuals) using Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon tests using the stats package (R Core Team 2021), respectively. To compare pairwise differences between localities, we applied the Dunn test with Bonferroni correction using the package FSA (Ogle et al. 2025).

To identify the factors associated with A. altamirani MLH and FIS, we built two LMMs using the package nlme (Purcell et al. 2007). The first model included MLH as the response variable and the second model included FIS. The predictor variables for both models were body condition, Bd infection intensity, and metamorphic stage. We considered sampling locality as a random-effect variable in both models to consider potential differences in population structure across localities. We performed the Shapiro–Wilk test (using stats package) and the Levene test (using the car package) (Fox and Weisberg 2019) to the model residuals to evaluate normality and homoscedasticity, respectively. We found that MLH model residuals presented a normal distribution (W = 0.98, P-value = 0.2091) and homoscedasticity (F-value=0.75, df = 4, P-value = 0.56). The P-values of fixed effect predictors were calculated by the lme function from the nlme package. Furthermore, to evaluate the correlation between body condition and MLH or FIS, we performed Spearman tests using the stats package.

Population Genetic Structure Analyses

We analyzed A. altamirani population structure using PCAs using the gl.pca function with adegenet (Jombart and Bateman 2008) in R and we performed ADMIXTURE analysis using maximum likelihood with ADMIXTURE v.1.3.0 (Alexander et al. 2009) using a range of K values (1 to 9), with five iterations per K value. We estimated the best K value with a cross-validation error analysis. To evaluate genomic differentiation among localities, we calculated pairwise FST with hierfstat (Goudet and Jombart 2022). We plotted the ancestry values for each individual and the cross-validation error values using the ggplot2 package (Wickham 2016).

To evaluate the relationship between genetic distance with geographical and environmental distances, we performed two partial Mantel tests using the vegan package (Oksanen et al. 2020). We calculated Nei's genetic distance among localities with the function dist.genpop from the package adegenet (Jombart and Bateman 2008). The geographical and environmental distances across localities were obtained using the function dist from the R stats package (R Core Team 2021). Geographical distance was calculated based on geographical coordinates of localities. Environmental distance was calculated based on the dissimilarity among localities using the environmental matrix (Table S4). The first partial Mantel test evaluated the relationship between genetic and environmental distances while controlling for geographical distances. The second partial Mantel test evaluated the relationship between Nei's genetic distances and geographical distances while controlling for environmental distances.

To identify the specific factors associated with the A. altamirani genomic structure, we performed an RDA using the genetic distance matrix from the filtered SNPs dataset, and the variables from the environmental matrix as predictors (Table S4). We incorporated the measured variables into the model (i.e. Elevation, DO, WaterTemp, WaterpH, MeanMaxTemp, MeanMinTemp, and Precipitation) and allowed the model to discard highly correlated variables. We performed a PERMANOVA to test for the effect of individual predictor variables on genetic variation (anova.cca, by = terms) in vegan (Oksanen et al. 2020).

Identification of SNPs Associated with Abiotic and Biotic Factors

To find associations between the genotype and biotic/abiotic factors, we implemented LFMMs using the R package lfmm (Caye and François 2017) using the filtered SNP dataset of 112,394 SNPs. LFMM tests for correlations between allelic frequencies and the variables after controlling for genetic structure using latent factors. We built a model including Elevation, DO, WaterTemp, Bd infection intensity, and body condition as variables. We included these abiotic variables (Elevation, DO, WaterTemp) because they had the highest pseudo-F values from the PERMANOVA model and the rest were highly correlated or nonsignificant (see Fig. 4 and Table S3). To control for genetic structure, we selected as optimal number of latent factors K = 3 as the number of genetic clusters previously identified by the ADMIXTURE analyses (see Fig. 3a). Adjusted P-values (q) were calculated using the genomic inflation factor (λ) procedure (Devlin and Roeder 1999). Finally, to correct for multiple testing, the resulting P-values were adjusted using the FDR method with a significance threshold of Q < 0.05.

We annotated all SNPs identified by the LFMM model using SnpEff 4.3 (Cingolani et al. 2012). Since our reference genome was not included in the SnpEff databases, we built the database using the A. mexicanum reference genome AmbMex60DD (Nowoshilow et al. 2018) and the GTF file (https://www.axolotl-omics.org/dl/AmexT_v47-AmexG_v6.0-DD.gtf.gz). SnpEff performed the SNP annotation based on sequence ontology terms (Eilbeck et al. 2005). Annotation categories in the SNP dataset were intergenic_region, intron_variant, upstream_gene_variant, downstream_gene_variant, 5_prime_UTR_variant, 3_prime_UTR_variant, synonymous_variant, and missense_variant. We reclassified SNPs categorized as “intergenic_region”, “upstream_gene_variant”, and “downstream_gene_variant” as all part of “Intergenic region” variants. We also reclassified “5_prime_UTR_variant” and “3_prime_UTR_variant” SNPs as “UTR variant” as well as “synonymous_variant” and “missense_variant” SNPs as “Exon variant”. We visualized the percentage of annotated SNPs associated to each abiotic and biotic factor through bar plots using ggplot2 (Wickham 2016).

We further evaluated the association between SNP genotypes and two biotic variables: Bd infection intensity and body condition. We chose these variables because we had individual values, allowing us to apply statistical tests to evaluate differences between SNP genotypes. SNP genotyping was done by the SNP calling program freebayes (Garrison and Marth 2012), where “reference allele” (REF = 0) was assigned to reference genome variant (from A. mexicanum genome) and “alternative allele” (ALT = 1) was assigned to the variant identified in our ddRAD-seq data. Thus, for each SNP we had three possible genotypes: 0/0 = homozygous for the reference alleles, 0/1 = heterozygous, and 1/1 = homozygous for the alternative alleles. To identify the SNP genotype–phenotype associations, we retrieved SNPs genotypes from coding regions associated with Bd infection intensity or body condition obtained in the previous LFMM. Then, we built a matrix including the SNP genotypes for A. altamirani individuals and their corresponding Bd infection intensity (BdLog) and body condition values, and applied Kruskal–Wallis and Dunn tests to evaluate differences among SNP genotypes with both biotic factors. We visualized the SNPs with significant pairwise differences between genotypes through boxplots using ggplot2 (Wickham 2016).
