Patterns and associated factors of cigarette smoking among medical students in 2020: a cross-sectional study from a North African country
Authors: Imen Zemni, Maha Abbes, Syrine Doghri, Meriem Kacem, Amani Maatouk, Cyrine Bennasrallah, Wafa Dhouib, Behaeddin Garrach, Houcine azaza, Hela Abroug, Manel Ben Fredj, Inès Bouanene, Fouzia Trimeche, Asma Sriha Belguith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to describe patterns of cigarette smoking and to determine its associated factors among medical students in the university of Monastir, Tunisia.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey among medical students of the faculty of medicine of Monastir in October 2020. The data were collected through a self-administered anonymous questionnaire. We collected data on the students’ smoking status, age of first cigarette use, intention to quit smoking and the level of nicotine dependence among smokers. Chicha, e-cigarette, alcohol, cannabis use and other addictive behavior were also investigated through the questionnaire. A multivariate logistic regression model was employed to identify associated factors with cigarette smoking. The threshold of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsA total of 748 students participated in this study. Of all respondents, 12.6% (95% CI [10.22–14.98]) consumed at least one form of tobacco. The prevalence of cigarette smoking was 10.7% (95%CI [8.5–12.9]). Male sex (aOR = 5.12; 95%CI [2.39–10.99]), living alone or with colleagues (aOR = 3.90; 95%CI [1.49–10.23]), alcohol consumption (aOR = 20.03; 95%CI [8.66–46.32]) and a high stress level (aOR = 3.22; 95%CI [1.08–9.61]) were found to be independently associated with cigarette smoking. Conversely, smartphone addiction (aOR = 0.34; 95% CI [0.14–0.82]) was identified as being inversely associated with cigarette smoking.ConclusionThis study highlights a considerable prevalence of tobacco use among students. While several well-known factors were associated with cigarette smoking, an inverse association with smartphone addiction emerged as a novel finding. These results may help inform targeted interventions to address this public health concern among medical students.

Introduction

Tobacco use remains a major global public health challenge, contributing significantly to morbidity and mortality worldwide [1].

In Tunisia, according to the Tunisian Health Examinaton Survey THES-2016, the prevalence of smoking exceeded 25% in both sexes and 48% in males [2]. These data pertain to the general population. However, to our knowledge, in Tunisia, only a few older studies have specifically examined this issue in targeted populations, such as students [3–5].

This group is particularly relevant as they face a range of stressors, social dynamics, and newfound autonomy, all of which can influence smoking behavior [6–10].

Among university students, medical students represent a particularly important subgroup for investigation. As future healthcare providers, their personal attitudes and behaviors regarding smoking carry significant implications for public health. Medical students not only serve as role models within society but also play a pivotal role in advocating for and implementing tobacco control measures [11–13]. Their smoking habits can affect their credibility and willingness to counsel patients on smoking cessation. Investigating smoking habits among this group is essential to understanding their attitudes toward tobacco use and their readiness to serve as effective health promoters. By examining smoking patterns within this population, we can identify opportunities to strengthen tobacco control education and empower future healthcare professionals to lead by example in combating the global tobacco epidemic.

This study aimed to estimate the prevelance of cigarette smoking among medical students at the university of Monastir and to identify its associated factors.

Methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional study that was conducted as part of a health survey among students of the Faculty of Medicine of Monastir during the month of October 2020.

Study setting

The Faculty of Medicine of Monastir is one of the four medical schools in Tunisia. Its program consists of six years of undergraduate education, divided into two cycles: the first lasting two years and the second four years. The first five years are designed to prepare students for medical practice by combining theoretical knowledge with clinical experience. In the sixth year, which is an internship year, medical students are primarily engaged in practical activities within healthcare facilities. During the 2020–2021 academic year, approximately 1,400 students were enrolled at this institution.

Study population

The target population of our study was students enrolled in the Faculty of Medicine of Monastir during the academic year 2020–2021 ranging from the first year to the fifth year of medical studies. Medical interns and residents were not included in this study. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling.

The required sample size was calculated based on an estimated smoking prevalence of 15% [3] using the following formula: n = (zα/2)2 × p(1-p) /i2 with [zα/2 = 1.96; i = 0.05; p = estimated proportion]. The minimum number of subjects required was then 196.

Data collection and measuring instruments

For data collection, we used a structured, self-administered and anonymous questionnaire distributed to students during the days of university registration at the faculty in October 2020.

The questionnaire consisted of several sections: general information (demographic, academic and socio-economic characteristics), food habits, physical activity, smoking and substance use, Internet and video game use, mental health.

In the smoking and substance use section, we collected data on students’ cigarette smoking status (current smokers, former smokers, and never smokers), age of first cigarette, intention to quit smoking, chicha and e-cigarette use, and alcohol and cannabis use.

For statistical purposes, smoking status was dichotomized as “smoker” (current smokers) and “non-smoker” (former and never smokers) [4, 5, 14].

To assess the level of nicotine dependence in smokers, we used the Fagerstrom test [15, 16].

To characterize alcohol consumption, we used the ADOPSA (Adolescents and Psychoactive Substances) score [17, 18] to detect harmful use. A score ≥ 2 indicates presence of harmful use of alcohol.

To assess the risk of cannabis dependence, we used the Cannabis Abuse Screening Test (CAST) [19, 20]. A CAST score ≥ 7 indicates a high risk of cannabis dependence.

To assess Internet addiction, we used Dr. Young’s questionnaire [21, 22]. Five or more positive responses indicate the presence of Internet addiction. We used the SAS-SV scale (Smartphone Addiction Scale Short Version) [23] to screen for smartphone addiction. A score ≥ 31 in men and ≥ 33 in women indicates the presence of smartphone addiction.

To screen for Facebook addiction, we used the Bergen Facebook Addiction Scale (BFAS) [24, 25]. A response of “Often” or “Very Often” to four or more items indicates problematic Facebook use.

Video game addiction was screened using the Video Game Addiction Test [26, 27]. Four or more positive responses indicate possible video game addiction.

In the mental health section, we assessed levels of stress, anxiety, eating disorders, sleep disorders, depression, and social support. We therefore used several psychometric scales: Stress was assessed using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) [28, 29]. A score between 27 and 40 indicates a high level of stress.

Anxiety was assessed using the French version of the General Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale [30, 31]. A score between 15 and 21 corresponds to severe anxiety. Depression was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) scale [32, 33]. A score of 20 to 27 corresponds to severe depression. Sleep disorders were assessed using the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) scale [34]. A score between 22 and 28 corresponds to severe insomnia. Social support was assessed using the ten-item SPS (Social Provisions Scale) [34, 35]. A score ≥ 30 indicates high social support and a score < 30 indicates low social support. The reliability of all scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, with all values exceeding 0.7, indicating a sufficient level of reliability.

Data analysis

The collected data were entered and analyzed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) software version 20.

Qualitative variables were represented using numbers and percentages and quantitative variables were described using means +/- standard deviation (SD) or medians and interquartile ranges, after testing the normality of the distribution.

For the analytical part, we first carried out a univariate analysis using Chi 2 test and the Fischer test. To determine the factors independently associated with conventional cigarette consumption, we performed a binary logistic regression by introducing variables with a significance level ≤ 0.2 in univariate analysis. The level of statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This survey was conducted after receiving the approval from the ethics committee of the Faculty of Medicine of Monastir under the number: IORG 0009738 N°137/ OMB 0990 − 0279. Participation in this study was voluntary. Participants’ anonymity was guaranteed and their informed consent was obtained after explaining the scientific purpose of the study. The researchers ensured that the study is conducted in compliance with principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Our study was conducted among 748 students from the Faculty of Medicine of Monastir. Approximately 2/3 of the participants (64.2%) were female.

The majority of students (62%) were in the 2nd cycle of medical studies. Almost 60% of the participants had a normal BMI. Prevalence of conventional cigarette consumption and consumer profile

Of the 748 participants in this study, 80 students (10.7% (95% CI [8.5–12.9])) were cigarette smokers. The frequency of conventional cigarette consumption was higher among men (22.4% (95% CI [16.6–28.2])) than among women (5.8% (95% CI [3.7–7.8])) (Table 3). The majority of cigarette smokers (41.3%) started smoking between 12 and 19 years old. The number of cigarettes smoked per day was between 10 and 20 cigarettes for the largest number of smokers (32.5%). The initiation of smoking after joining the faculty concerned 28.4% of smokers.

Other forms of tobacco use

Among the participants, 61 students or 8.1% (95% CI [6.1–10]) were chicha consumers, the majority of whom consumed it either rarely (4.1%) or occasionally (3.1%). The frequency of chicha consumption was higher among men (19.4% (95% CI [13.9–25])) than among women (3.5% (95% CI [1.85–5.14])). E-cigarette use was recorded among 2.3% (95% CI [1.22–3.37]) of participants. The frequency of e-cigarette use was higher among men (5.6% (95% CI [2.38–8.81])) than among women (0.8% (95% CI [3.10-5–1.5])).

Of all respondents, 12.6% (95% CI [10.22–14.98]) of students consumed at least one form of tobacco. Within this group, the most frequent forms of consumption were, respectively, the concomitant consumption of classic cigarette and chicha (47.9%), the consumption of classic cigarette alone (28.7%) and the associated consumption of classic cigarette, chicha and E-cigarette (8.5%) (Fig. 1).

Other addictions

Of the 748 participants in our study, 69 students or 9.3% (95% CI [7.21–11.38]) were alcohol consumers with a higher frequency of consumption among men (16.8% (95% CI [11.6–22])) than among women (6.1% (95% CI [4-8.2])) (Table 5). Among alcohol consumers, the frequency of harmful use was 44.8%. This frequency was higher among men (64.3%) than among women (22.2%).

Among the respondents, 29 students (3.9% (95% CI [2.51–5.28])) were cannabis users. This frequency was higher among men (7.2% (95% CI [3.58–10.81])) than among women (1.9% (95% CI [0.6–3.1])) (Table 5). The risk of cannabis dependence was high among 17.2% of cannabis users.

In our study population, 28.2% (95% CI [24.97–31.42]) of students had Internet addiction and 6.7% (95% CI [4.9–8.49]) had Facebook addiction. The frequency of smartphone addiction was 25.3% (95% CI [22.18–28.41]) and that of video game addiction was 10.2% (95% CI [8.03–12.36]) (Table 5).

Factors associated with the consumption of conventional cigarettes

In univariate analysis, male gender, marital status (widowed or divorced), living alone or with colleagues, obesity, stress, anxiety, depression, insomnia, chicha use, e-cigarette use, alcohol use and cannabis use were significantly associated with conventional cigarette use (Table 6).

Discussion

Our study revealed that 10.7% of participants smoked conventional cigarettes. Although this prevalence is not excessively high, it remains alarming, especially considering the potential long-term health implications for medical students who are expected to serve as health advocates. Our results were very close to those found in China according to a systematic review and meta-analysis published by Niu L et al. where the prevalence of conventional cigarette consumption among medical students was 10.93% [36]. This result was also very close to that found in a cross-sectional study conducted at the Faculty of Medicine in 2013 where this prevalence among medical students was 10.2% [5].

In neighboring countries like Morocco, for example, a cross-sectional study published by Zaghba N et al., including 736 medical students, showed that 7.9% of the population smoked conventional cigarettes [37]. In neighboring Egypt, the prevalence of smoking was even lower at 5.6% [38], suggesting that cultural or public health initiatives may be more effective in reducing tobacco use. However, other studies found higher prevalence rates, with a 2009 cross-sectional study by Al Kaabba A et al., in Saudi Arabia reporting a smoking prevalence of 17.6% among 153 medical students [39], while a study conducted by Miri-Moghaddam M et al. in Iran among 500 medical students indicated a prevalence of 15.2% [40]. In South India, Ganesh K et al., found that 22.4% of medical students smoked [41], and in Italy, a multicenter cross-sectional study by Saulle R et al., revealed a prevalence of 20.4% [42]. Additionally, a cross-sectional study in Argentina showed that 27.3% of 1659 medical students smoked conventional cigarettes [43]. These higher rates may be attributed to various factors, including social norms that accept or promote smoking, increased accessibility of tobacco products, and less stringent regulations regarding tobacco use.

It is relevant to mention that the consumption of classic cigarettes concerned approximately four times more male students (22.4%) than female students (5.8%). This disparity in smoking rates among genders, which was also found in several Arab or Muslim countries such as Iran [39] and Saudi Arabia [38], could be due to the negative perception of women smokers in these regions.

Regarding the profile of classic cigarette smoking, our study showed that the majority of smokers had smoked for 1 to 5 years and consumed between 10 and 20 cigarettes per day.

Notably, this frequency of consumption was higher than that reported in Brazil [44] or Pakistan [45], where the majority of medical students smoked fewer than 10 cigarettes per day, and it was also greater than in China, where consumption was less than 5 cigarettes per day [46].

Our findings revealed that 48.8% of students who smoked conventional cigarettes had attempted to quit, highlighting a significant awareness of the health risks associated with smoking. This prevalence was higher than that found in Albania (43%) [47], similar to that of Pakistan (46%) [48] and lower than that of Morocco (58%) [34], or Brazil (67.3%) [44].

Our study revealed also that male gender (aOR = 5.13; 95% CI [2.39–10.99]) and alcohol consumption (aOR = 20.03; 95% CI [8.66–46.32]) were factors independently associated with conventional tobacco use. These results are consistent with the literature and have been found in many studies [5, 42, 49–54].

We also found that high stress levels and living alone or with co-workers were predictive factors of cigarettte smoking. These results could be explained by the use of cigarettes in situations of extreme stress, in search of the pleasurable sensation that follows smoking in order to reduce stress.

Regarding living alone or with colleagues, the initiation of tobacco consumption could be explained by the reduction of parental control and by the impression of acquiring more freedoms in addition to peer pressure which has been found in many studies [40].

Interestingly, our study found that smartphone addiction was inversely associated with conventional tobacco consumption, suggesting that those who are heavily engaged with their devices may be less inclined to initiate other addictive behaviors.

Strengths and limitations of the study

To our knowledge, few studies have focused on smoking among Tunisian medical students. This study, including a large sample size, made it possible to explore the profile of smoker among medical students and to determine the factors associated with this behavior.

However, our work was not without some methodological limitations: First, since this is a cross-sectional study, it does not allow us to establish a cause-and-effect relationship between the factors studied and cigarette smoking. Second, our study could have been subject to selection bias due to a non-random sampling technique or the existence of non-responding subjects. To mitigate this bias, we aimed to involve a diverse range of participants by inviting medical students from all academic years and categories. Conducting data collection during registration days allowed us to reach all student profiles, regardless of their schedules or levels of academic engagement. Additionally, the large sample size further enhances the generalizability of our findings to the broader population of medical students. Third, our study is subject to information bias. Since the information is self-reported, recall bias and social desirability bias could have been present. To limit social desirability bias, we ensured the anonymity of the participants.

In conclusion, our study highlights a relatively high prevalence of tobacco use among medical students, with a predominance of conventional cigarette smoking and an early age of initiation. Although attempts to quit are frequent, they are rarely supported by specialized services. Chicha and electronic cigarettes, while less commonly used, appear as popular alternatives, often motivated by curiosity or the perception of lesser harm. The concurrent use of multiple tobacco products and other addictive behaviors, such as alcohol consumption, is concerning.

Considering these results among medical students, implementing effective intervention strategies is crucial. Universities should integrate smoking cessation programs into medical curricula, ensuring that students are not only aware of the dangers of smoking but also equipped with the necessary skills to counsel their future patients. Additionally, policies such as smoke-free campus initiatives and improved access to smoking cessation services could significantly influence smoking behaviors. Tailored awareness campaigns for young adults, emphasizing both the personal and professional consequences of smoking, should also be reinforced.

In this context, strengthening medical education on tobacco control is essential. Medical students play a key role in promoting public health, and their attitudes toward smoking are shaped by their education. Enhancing tobacco-related education within medical training could encourage a more proactive approach to smoking cessation counseling. Ensuring that students recognize their responsibility as future healthcare providers in tobacco control may contribute to reducing smoking prevalence in both their personal and professional environments.

Additionally, replicating this survey over time would allow for continuous monitoring of smoking behaviors and assessment of prevention campaigns’ effectiveness. Conducting periodic surveys across different university institutions within the University of Monastir could further enhance epidemiological surveillance of this public health concern. Finally, implementing longitudinal studies would provide valuable insights into the long-term trends of smoking behaviors among medical students, helping to identify risk factors and inform targeted interventions for smoking cessation and prevention.
