Synergistic pollution-climate-host interactions drive nonlinear flea dynamics on Mongolian gerbils
Authors: Rui Geng, Yakun Liu, Haizhou Yang, Shuai Yuan, Heping Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs climate change and human activities intensify, the ecological responses of rodent ectoparasitic flea (Siphonaptera) communities and their population dynamics exhibit nonlinear characteristics. However, the synergistic effects of air pollution and climatic drivers, and the critical role of host traits in modulating these interactions, remain poorly understood.MethodsWe conducted a nine-month field survey of Mongolian gerbils and their fleas on the Ordos Plateau, China, integrating our collections with high-resolution meteorological and Air Quality Index (AQI) data. We developed a “climate-pollution-host” framework, employing Redundancy Analysis (RDA) and Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMMs) to systematically analyze the interactive effects of these multiple stressors on flea communities and populations.ResultsAir Quality Index (AQI), a previously overlooked factor, emerged as a key driver of flea dynamics. Complex, nonlinear interactions between AQI and climatic variables defined two critical high-risk transmission zones: (1) areas with moderate temperatures (15–20 °C) and high pollution (AQI > 60), particularly under southwesterly winds, and (2) areas with low temperatures (10–15 °C) and high pollution (AQI > 42) under low-wind conditions. The impact of these environmental stressors was significantly modulated by host biology; the positive effect of pollution on flea loads was most pronounced on subadult and reproductive individuals when AQI exceeded 50, a pattern consistent with the framework of an immunocompetence-energy trade-off. Ultimately, these complex drivers resulted in distinct ecological niche differentiation between the two dominant flea species: Xenopsylla conformis conformis preferred temperate, high-AQI conditions, whereas Nosopsyllus laeviceps kuzenkovi was more tolerant of low-temperature and high-wind stress.ConclusionsOur study identifies the Air Quality Index (AQI) as a critical driver of flea community dynamics. We demonstrate that complex, multi-way interactions between AQI, climatic variables, and host traits nonlinearly regulate flea abundance and define high-risk transmission zones. This climate–pollution–host interaction framework provides a novel and powerful approach for understanding and predicting vector ecology under multifaceted environmental change.Keywords: Nonlinear threshold effects, Flea dynamics, Air pollution, Climatic drivers, Multilevel interactions, Vector surveillance and control

Background

Ectoparasitic fleas (Siphonaptera) serve as vectors for a range of pathogens and play a pivotal role in shaping the interspecific dynamics of wildlife [1, 2]. Among rodent hosts, the Mongolian gerbil (Meriones unguiculatus) is the dominant species in northern China’s desert grasslands and agro-pastoral ecotones and is a principal reservoir of natural plague foci. Its population density and activity patterns greatly influence plague epidemiology [3]. M. unguiculatus exhibits distinct diurnal activity rhythms, seasonal breeding strategies, and long-distance dispersal capabilities, which are ecological traits that render it central to flea-borne pathogen transmission and Yersinia pestis circulation [3, 4].

Recent evidence has indicated that flea populations exhibit notable nonlinear dynamics under climate change and anthropogenic disturbance [5]. First, extreme climatic events-such as heatwaves and drought, directly restrict flea survival and dispersal capacity. Second, altered environmental conditions can modify host habitat suitability and surface activity rhythms, indirectly influencing flea persistence [6]. Despite these insights, most studies have focused on temperature and humidity, neglecting the influential roles of wind and air pollution. Wind speed and direction shape parasite dispersal by altering airflow patterns and generating wind barrier effects [7] while also governing the diffusion and deposition of airborne pollutants [8]. Air pollutants such as fine particulate matter (PM2.5), NO₂, and SO₂ serve as ecological stressors by inducing oxidative stress and suppressing host immune function [9, 10]. Such host immunosuppression may indirectly enhance the reproductive output and parasitic success of fleas, as the host’s ability to combat infestation is compromised. Furthermore, the early life stages of fleas are highly dependent on microhabitats, particularly soil substrates and host burrow nests [11]. Deposition of airborne pollutants potentially alter nest microclimates and substrate quality, which could in turn affect flea egg hatching and larval survival rates, given the strong dependence of pre-imaginal flea stages on local microenvironmental conditions [12, 13]. In addition to environmental factors, intraspecific variation in host traits is a critical determinant of flea population dynamics [14, 15]. However, much of the existing evidence has tended to evaluate habitat (abiotic) and host (biotic) drivers in a largely factor-isolated way. For example, habitat conditions and microclimatic constraints have been shown to structure flea assemblages across landscapes and to regulate the survival of pre-imaginal stages (e.g., temperature-humidity effects on eggs/larvae) [16, 17]. Empirical studies have also demonstrated that biotic processes such as host density can strongly shape flea burden and infestation patterns [18]. Moreover, owing to their larger home ranges and higher contact rates, male hosts are often infested with a broader assemblage of flea species [19, 20], consistent with broad evidence for male-biased parasitism across mammals [21]. Similarly, larger-bodied individuals generally support greater parasite loads by offering more stable microhabitats [22]. Host physiological conditions, including reproductive status, pregnancy, and age-also significantly influence the flea community composition and abundance [23]. Nevertheless, the current understanding of the relationships among fleas, hosts, and environmental drivers remains limited in several important ways. First, most studies have aimed to isolate single variables but have neglected the nonlinear interactions among climate, air pollution, and host attributes. Second, the ecological effects of the air quality index (AQI) on flea abundance remain poorly quantified. Third, the mechanisms by which wind speed and direction influence flea coinfestation patterns and transmission dynamics remain uncertain. To address these gaps, we incorporated climatic conditions, air pollution indices, and host biological traits into a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) framework. This study aimed to examine how multilevel interactions among climate, pollution, and host traits collectively influence the population abundance and community structure of ectoparasitic fleas on Meriones unguiculatus. By identifying key drivers under complex environmental regimes and determining high-risk transmission conditions, this study contributes to a mechanistic understanding of flea dynamics and offers theoretical support for predictive vector control strategies within the context of ongoing climate change and environmental degradation.

Methods

Study area

This study was conducted in the western desert grassland of the Ordos Plateau, Inner Mongolia, China (39.07297°N, 108.01227°E). This region experiences a temperate continental monsoon climate characterized by aridity, low precipitation, and strong winds, often resulting in sandstorms. The mean annual temperature is 7.1 °C, and the mean annual precipitation is approximately 260 mm, with more than 70% of the total rainfall occurring between July and September. The frost-free period spans approximately 122 days. The main soil types are Calcic Kastanozem, Calcaric Arenosol, and chestnut soil.

Rodent diversity in the study area is high. Our study focused on the wild populations of the Mongolian gerbil (Meriones unguiculatus), which exhibits strong ecological adaptability and dominates the small mammal community in this region. Other sympatric species include the Chinese striped hamster (Cricetulus barabensis), the Daurian ground squirrel (Spermophilus dauricus), and the midday gerbil (Meriones meridianus).

This area was selected because it lies within the core distribution range of Meriones unguiculatus and represents a historical natural plague focus in northern China, making it particularly suitable for investigating flea–host dynamics of epidemiological importance. Although the sites were not selected solely on the basis of pollution level, the region is subject to a certain degree of anthropogenic influence, and air quality was incorporated as an environmental variable to evaluate its ecological relevance in this host–flea system.

Host trapping and flea collection

From March to November 2024, seven fixed sampling sites were randomly selected within the core distribution range of the Mongolian gerbil. Each 1 ha site was overlaid with a 10 m × 10 m grid of 100 Sherman live traps (a 10-m spacing between traps in both rows and columns) baited with fresh peanuts. As M. unguiculatus is diurnal, the traps were open from 06:00 to 19:00 and inspected every 2–3 h. Captured gerbils were placed individually in cloth bags and transported to the field station laboratory. The animals were anaesthetized with isoflurane prior to ectoparasitic flea collection (induction: 3–5%; maintenance: 1–3%). The depth of anesthesia was assessed by loss of the righting reflex and absence of response to toe pinch. To allow for individual identification, each newly captured gerbil was marked using a uniquely numbered ear tag. For each gerbil, we recorded its sex, age, body weight, reproductive status, and capture frequency (i.e., initial capture or recapture). After flea collection and data recording, the animals were allowed to fully recover from anesthesia before being released at their original capture site. Trapping was performed for 15–20 consecutive days each month at each site. Over the course of the study, we recorded 929 capture events from 544 unique individual gerbils, from which we collected 5,293 fleas. All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Inner Mongolia Agricultural University (Protocol No. NND2023081).

Acquisition of environmental variables

We compiled daily records of key climatic variables-maximum temperature (MaxTemp), minimum temperature (MinTemp), precipitation (Precipitation), mean wind speed (WindSpeed), and wind direction (WindDirection) — along with the daily air quality index (AQI) to evaluate their effects on flea ecological processes [24]. Meteorological variables were obtained from the China Meteorological Administration (CMA) national station at Etuoke Banner (Station ID: 53529) via an online station-based query platform; official local meteorological records are also accessible through our collaboration with the Etuoke Banner Forestry and Grassland Bureau (Ordos). Air pollution was characterized using AQI as an integrative indicator of ambient pollution stress; pollutant-specific components (e.g., PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, SO₂, NO₂, CO, and O₃) were not analyzed separately in the present study. All environmental variables used in this study were matched to each capture event at a daily resolution based on the capture date. Vegetation indices and soil moisture were not included as covariates in the current analyses.

Data processing

Our analytical approach followed a hierarchical framework to dissect the complex drivers of flea dynamics. We first used Redundancy Analysis (RDA) to explore broad-scale patterns and identify key environmental correlates of flea community structure. RDA was conducted in R 4.4.2 using the vegan package, and the flea community matrix Hellinger-transformed using decostand (method = “hellinger”) to improve interpretability for sparse community abundance data. Multicollinearity among environmental predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). Predictors with VIF > 10 were considered to indicate problematic collinearity and were excluded or not entered simultaneously. Next, to assess the direct influence of host-intrinsic factors on flea burden at the individual level, we fitted Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) in SPSS 29 using a negative binomial distribution with a log link. Host sex, body weight, age class, capture frequency, and reproductive status were included as explanatory variables, and factors with significant effects (P < 0.05) were retained for interpretation. Finally, to test nonlinear and synergistic effects on the two dominant flea species, we implemented a two-step workflow in R 4.4.2: LASSO regression was performed using the glmnet package (cv.glmnet function) with 10-fold cross-validation, and terms were retained using the lambda.1se criterion [25]. Because glmnet does not implement a negative binomial family, the penalized screening step used a Poisson likelihood; final inference was conducted using negative binomial GLMMs fitted with the lme4 package (glmer.nb function), including CapturePoint as a random intercept. To evaluate the contribution of higher-order interactions, we compared models with interaction terms against main-effects-only models using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

Results

Seasonal dynamics of the flea community composition

“Our study was based on 929 capture events of 544 individual Mongolian gerbils (M. unguiculatus), from which we collected 5,293 ectoparasitic fleas representing six families, 11 genera, and 21 species.” The flea community compositions in spring (March–May), summer (June–August), and autumn (September–November) are shown in Fig. 1. The summer community comprised 12 species and exhibited a monodominant structure, with Xenopsylla conformis conformis accounting for 83.5% and Nosopsyllus laeviceps kuzenkovi accounting for 9.5% of the total community. In contrast, the spring and autumn communities were more diverse. In spring, 16 species were recorded, dominated by N. l. kuzenkovi (50.3%), with X. c. conformis as the subdominant species (19.9%). The autumn community included 11 species, with X. c. conformis and N. l. kuzenkovi codominant, at 45% and 43%, respectively (Fig. 1a). This seasonal shift in dominance and coexistence underscores dynamic changes in community structure and species composition, reflecting notable seasonal succession in response to environmental variation (Fig. 1b).

Seasonal patterns in flea community composition and infestation levels on Meriones unguiculatus. a Flea species compositions in spring, summer, and autumn. b Seasonal variation in community structure among dominant and subdominant species. c Flea index (average number of fleas per host) across the three seasons

With respect to overall parasitism levels, the total flea index varied significantly among seasons (Fig. 1c; p < 0.001). The index peaked in summer (9.54 ± 0.43), significantly exceeding the spring (1.90 ± 0.09) and autumn (3.04 ± 0.25) values, with spring having the lowest level of infestation. The summer peak highlights an elevated risk of pathogen transmission, whereas lower temperatures and reduced host activity likely contribute to the notable decrease in flea abundance during spring and autumn.

Response of the flea community composition to environmental factors

To evaluate how environmental variables shape the species composition of the flea community, we conducted seasonal redundancy analyses (RDA). The first two canonical axes captured the dominant seasonal patterns in species–environment associations, explaining 88.18%, 99.70%, and 95.31% of the variance constrained by the selected predictors for spring, summer, and autumn, respectively (Fig. 2a–c). The separation of species scores relative to environmental vectors along these axes indicates that the selected predictors are closely associated with seasonal community structure.

The line thickness indicates the strength of the correlation; the colour indicates direction (positive or negative) (spec1: Xenopsylla conformis conformis; spec2: Nosopsyllus laeviceps kuzenkovi; spec3: Neopsylla bidentiformis; spec5: Ophthalmopsylla praefecta praefecta; spec6: Citellophilus tesquorum mongolicus; spec7: Xenopsylla cheopis; spec8: Frontopsylla luculenta; spec11: Nosopsyllus laeviceps ellobii; spec12: Nosopsyllus laeviceps laeviceps; spec13: Ophthalmopsylla jettmari; spec14: Neopsylla galea)

In spring (Fig. 2a), AQI, wind speed, and precipitation exhibited the strongest contributions to the ordination, suggesting that air quality and wind–moisture conditions were key correlates of community shifts. Species scores for the dominant taxa (e.g., N. l. kuzenkovi and N. galea) were positioned in the direction of higher AQI and wind speed and opposite to precipitation, indicating positive associations with AQI and wind speed and negative associations with precipitation. Overall, springtime community patterns aligned with an environmental gradient characterized by poorer air quality, stronger winds, and drier conditions, with minimum temperature and wind direction showing additional secondary structure.

In summer (Fig. 2b), wind speed, wind direction, and AQI were the major ordination variables. X. cheopis and X. c. conformis showed positive associations with wind conditions and a negative association with AQI. These patterns are consistent with the possibility that wind conditions covary with local pollutant dispersion and thus with AQI levels, although causality cannot be inferred from ordination alone.

In autumn (Fig. 2c), temperature and precipitation dominated the constrained gradients. Several species clustered under cooler and wetter conditions, suggesting that N. l. laeviceps and X. cheopis are associated with relatively cool, moist environments.

The correlation network (Fig. 2d) was broadly concordant with the ordination patterns: edge colour denotes association direction (positive/negative), and edge thickness reflects effect strength. AQI, MinTemp, and WindDir emerged as central correlates of community structure. For example, spec12 (N. l. laeviceps), spec13 (O. jettmari), and spec14 (N. galea) showed positive correlations with AQI (p < 0.001), whereas spec3 (N. b. formis) was primarily associated with MinTemp and WindDir, highlighting species-specific climatic sensitivities. Notably, several taxa exhibited joint responses to multiple predictors, supporting our inclusion of multilevel interaction terms (e.g., AQI × MinTemp × Precipitation) in subsequent modelling.

Host Sex and Body Weight: Overall, male Mongolian gerbils (n = 436) hosted a significantly higher total flea load (mean = 6.41 fleas) than females did (n = 493; mean = 5.26; Fig. 3a). Our GLM analyses confirmed this male-biased parasitism at the species level. Specifically, abundances were significantly lower on females for X. c. conformis (B = − 0.219, p = 0.004) and N. l. ellobii (B = − 0.853, p = 0.003), indicating greater parasitic pressure on males for these key species. Furthermore, the effect of host body weight was species-specific. Body weight was negatively associated with the abundance of X. c. conformis (B = − 0.016, p = 0.01), indicating that heavier gerbils tended to host fewer fleas of this species. Capture Frequency and Host Age: We observed a strong effect of capture history on flea abundance. Individuals trapped for the first time consistently hosted more fleas than recaptured hosts did (Fig. 3b). This pattern was statistically significant for several species, with abundances being elevated in first-captured individuals for X.conformis (B = 0.358; p < 0.001), Citellophilus tesquorum mongolicus (B = 0.451; p = 0.032), and X.cheopis (B = 1.605; p < 0.001). Host age also played a critical role. For the most dominant flea, X. c. conformis, both adult (B = − 1.328, p < 0.001) and juvenile (B = − 0.457, p < 0.001) gerbils carried significantly fewer fleas compared to sub-adults, indicating that flea abundance was highest in sub-adults.

Reproductive Status: Host reproductive status was another important determinant, particularly for X. cheopis. Gerbils in a non-reproductive state (anoestrous) hosted significantly fewer X. cheopis than their reproductively active counterparts (B = − 1.068, p = 0.009).

Multifactor interaction effects on the abundance of X. c. conformis

Temperature × AQI: Significant nonlinear coupling between the temperature and AQI drove the year-round abundance of X. c. conformis (p < 0.001; Fig. 5a). Within a 10–20 °C window, each unit increase in the AQI corresponded to an approximately 40% increase in the flea load (p < 0.01), whereas extreme temperatures (< 5 °C or > 20 °C) caused sharp decreases (p < 0.001).

Interaction effects of environmental factors and host characteristics on X. c. conformis abundance. Panels a–d show key second- and third-order interactions among AQI, temperature, precipitation, wind speed, and wind direction. Panels e–h illustrates host-related interaction patterns involving body weight, age, and capture status

Age × AQI: Host age and AQI interacted significantly (p < 0.001; Fig. 5b). Juveniles exhibited minimal abundance shifts (< 10%) across AQI gradients, whereas subadults and adults demonstrated > 50% increases when the AQI exceeded 50 (p < 0.01), indicating heightened pollution susceptibility at the postjuvenile stage.

A strong interaction was observed between precipitation and AQI (p < 0.001; Fig. 5c). This effect was most dramatic under dry conditions (< 0.25 mm rainfall). Compared to the baseline of low pollution (AQI < 42), flea abundance peaked dramatically in the intermediate-to-high pollution range (AQI 42–54), increasing by approximately 350% (p < 0.001). This nonlinear response was absent under higher rainfall conditions, where abundance remained low across all AQI levels.

MinTemp × WindSpeed × WindDir: A three-way interaction among the minimum temperature, wind speed (Beaufort scale 5; fresh breeze, 8.0–10.7 m/s), and wind direction significantly influenced abundance (p < 0.001; Fig. 5d). Peak loads occurred with southwesterly winds at 10–15 °C and Beaufort scale 5 wind conditions, whereas northerly, easterly, and westerly wind regimes yielded lower loads. Southerly wind systems (S, SW, and SE) supported higher flea densities under similar low temperatures.

Weight × Age × Capture Status: A three-way host-level interaction revealed that the abundance of fleas found on juveniles fluctuated notably with body weight, while subadults and adults presented more stable loads (p < 0.001; Fig. 5f-h). Collectively, these interaction patterns (Fig. 5a-h) show how pollution, climate, and host traits jointly shape the population dynamics of X. c. conformis.

Multifactor interaction effects on N. l. kuzenkovi abundance

Temperature × Wind Speed: A significant interaction between temperature and wind speed shaped N. l. kuzenkovi abundance (p < 0.05; Fig. 6a, b). Higher wind speeds (Beaufort scale 3–4, gentle to moderate breeze, 3.4–7.9 m/s) supported higher flea loads than low wind speeds (scale 2), particularly under relatively cool conditions (≤ 15 °C), where abundance increased by more than 40%. Under low wind speeds, flea abundance followed a unimodal curve peaking at 20–25 °C, whereas at higher wind speeds the response curve flattened and broadened across 20–30 °C. At extremely high temperatures (> 35 °C), abundance decreased sharply (p < 0.05) regardless of wind speed.

Interaction effects of environmental and host-related variables on the abundance of N. l. kuzenkovi. Panels a–b show interactions involving temperature and wind speed. Panel c shows the interaction between AQI and host reproductive status. Panel d illustrates the interaction among minimum temperature, sex, and capture status. Panel e shows the three-way interaction among maximum temperature, AQI, and precipitation

AQI × Reproductive Status: Host reproductive condition and AQI strongly interacted (p < 0.001; Fig. 6c). In nonreproductive gerbils, the abundance was highest at low AQI (0–25) and decreased with increasing pollution (p < 0.01). Conversely, reproductive hosts exhibited a positive AQI–abundance relationship (p < 0.001), indicating divergent responses by reproductive state.

MinTemp × Sex × Capture Status: A three-way interaction among the minimum temperature, sex, and capture status revealed that first-captured females experienced a significant decrease in flea abundance with increasing MinTemp (p < 0.05), with greater variability at lower temperatures and stabilization above 15 °C. Recaptured females and males, regardless of capture status, showed no significant temperature-related changes (p > 0.05; Fig. 6d).

MaxTemp × AQI × Precipitation: A three-way coupling of MaxTemp, AQI, and precipitation significantly affected flea abundance (p < 0.001; Fig. 6e). Under high pollution levels (AQI > 48), abundance peaked at 10–15 °C with moderate to heavy rainfall (> 15 mm) before decreasing sharply as temperatures increased to 30–35 °C or as precipitation waned. At a lower AQI, the abundance remained uniformly low.

Discussion

We systematically evaluated how climatic variables, air pollution (AQI), and host traits jointly shape the flea community structure and the abundances of dominant species, revealing critical higher-order interactions. By incorporating the AQI into our analyses, we established a three-factor interaction framework linking climate, pollution, and host characteristics, which revealed nonlinear responses to multiple environmental pressures. Unlike conventional parasite ecology studies that focus on single environmental factors, such as temperature, humidity, precipitation, or host–parasite models that consider only host trait main effects without environmental modulation of infection risk [6, 21, 24], our study highlights the value of integrated interaction models for understanding flea dynamics in arid landscapes. More importantly, the novelty of this study lies not simply in recognizing AQI as an environmental indicator, but in quantifying its ecological influence on vector abundance and community structure within a natural host–parasite system. Future work should test these interactions experimentally and examine how such nonlinear couplings influence pathogen transmission dynamics.

AQI as a persistent ecological correlate of flea community dynamics

The multiseason RDA and correlation networks consistently identified AQI as an important environmental correlate of flea communities across spring, summer, and autumn, with the strongest signal observed in spring (Fig. 4a–d). During this season, flea community structure showed clear differentiation along the AQI axis, suggesting that AQI represents a major ecological gradient in this system. Notably, the abundances of vector species such as X. c. conformis and N. laeviceps were positively associated with the AQI in spring, underscoring that variation in air quality can have measurable ecological consequences for flea populations.

However, AQI did not act in isolation. Rather, its ecological effects should be interpreted within a broader interaction framework involving climatic and host-related variables. AQI may affect flea communities through multiple non-exclusive pathways, including altered host activity, host physiological stress, and changes in burrow microhabitats [25–31]. Although its influence was somewhat weaker than that of wind speed or temperature in summer and autumn, its repeated appearance across seasons suggests that AQI functions as a persistent ecological correlate rather than a transient seasonal factor. These results indicate that AQI is not only a broad indicator of atmospheric conditions, but also a quantifiable ecological predictor of flea population dynamics, with potential relevance for flea-borne disease risk in pollution-affected ecosystems [29].

Host traits: main effects and modulatory roles

Host characteristics notably shape flea burdens on Mongolian gerbils. We found that compared with females, male gerbils carried significantly more fleas, potentially reflecting their larger home ranges and more aggressive territorial behaviours [19, 30]. Body weight showed species-specific associations with flea abundance, being positively related to some flea taxa but negatively related to X. c. conformis, indicating that host size effects were not uniform across the flea community. This pattern is broadly consistent with previous work suggesting that host body size can influence parasite establishment by affecting resource availability and microhabitat conditions [16, 30].

Importantly, our results suggest that capture frequency may independently modulate flea load. Recaptured gerbils harboured fewer fleas than first-capture gerbils did (p < 0.01), possibly because of stress-induced grooming or enhanced self-cleaning behaviour following repeated handling [32].

These host-level effects underscore the need to consider individual variation when modelling parasite dynamics and planning targeted control measures.

Synergistic environmental regulation: interactions between climate and pollution

Our models revealed that environmental drivers do not act in isolation but interact synergistically. Both X. c. conformis and N. l. kuzenkovi exhibited clear temperature thresholds in their nonlinear responses, with abundance decreasing sharply outside their optimal thermal windows, consistent with the heat-stress hypothesis [12, 33]. Within these windows, flea abundance tended to increase with wind speed, suggesting that wind conditions may be associated with changes in flea dynamics, potentially through altered host surface activity, microhabitat use, or short-distance passive transport [34, 35]. Moreover, for X. c. conformis, a directional wind effect was evident, as the species was less abundant under northwesterly winds than under southerly winds. Nonlinear interactions involving AQI and precipitation further suggested a dual-modulation pattern, in which rainfall may buffer the positive association between AQI and flea abundance by reducing airborne pollutants and increasing humidity; under heavy rainfall, X. c. conformis showed reduced sensitivity to AQI [36].

Host-mediated modulation: phenotype-specific responses to environmental stress

A critical finding is that host life history and physiological state regulate the effects of environmental drivers. Host developmental stage regulated the effects of the AQI, as adults carried 40% more X. c. conformis at higher AQI compared with juveniles (p < 0.01). Even more striking was the role of reproductive status. We found that reproductively active hosts supported significantly greater N. l. kuzenkovi loads under pollution stress (p < 0.001), particularly females in oestrus. This ecological pattern, where hosts in energetically demanding states exhibit higher parasite loads under environmental stress, aligns with the theoretical framework of the immunocompetence-energy trade-off hypothesis [37–39]. This was particularly evident as females presented heightened N. l. kuzenkovi sensitivity to AQI during oestrus [40].

However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Our study demonstrates a strong ecological correlation but does not provide direct physiological evidence. Because we did not measure immunological markers, this trade-off is presented here as a plausible, well-supported mechanistic hypothesis rather than a tested conclusion. Future research integrating field ecology with eco-immunological techniques would be valuable for explicitly testing this mechanism and quantifying the physiological cost of pollution for host immunity. Nevertheless, our results suggest that specific host cohorts, defined by their phenotype, may become disproportionately important parasite reservoirs under increasing environmental stress.

Species-specific strategies: niche differentiation and temporal synchrony

While sharing some common responses, the two flea species exhibited species-specific adaptations, suggesting mechanisms that facilitate their coexistence.

First, they showed differential environmental adaptation. X. c. conformis achieved peak abundance under moderate temperatures (10–15 °C) and southwesterly winds, indicating a preference for temperate, stable microclimates. In contrast, N. l. kuzenkovi abundance increased greatly under low-temperature, high-wind-speed conditions. This response heterogeneity suggests mechanisms of competition avoidance or ecological niche complementarity driven by the microclimate [41].

Second, the two taxa demonstrated temporal differentiation. The abundance of X. c. conformis was highly sensitive to AQI variation within a broader environmental context that also included temperature, wind, and other interacting factors. Conversely, the abundance of N. l. kuzenkovi was more closely aligned with host reproductive peaks, indicating a dual coordination of environmental tracking and host utilization. Such divergence may reflect differences in life-history strategy and highlights the roles of niche differentiation and temporal matching in vector ecology [41, 42].

Conclusion

This study reveals a novel three-factor regulatory framework where climate, air pollution, and host traits interact to drive the dynamics of ectoparasite communities. By integrating air quality (AQI) as a key environmental predictor, our work moves beyond traditional single-factor analyses to elucidate the complex, nonlinear nature of this host-parasite system.

Our community-level analysis identified AQI, wind dynamics, and temperature as the primary correlates of seasonal shifts in flea species composition. At the population level, these factors did not act independently but instead defined context-dependent high-risk conditions through their interactions. In the present study area, two empirical windows of elevated flea abundance were identified: (i) moderate temperatures (15–20 °C) combined with relatively high pollution (AQI > 60), particularly under southwesterly winds, and (ii) lower temperatures (10–15 °C) combined with elevated pollution (AQI > 42) under low-wind conditions. However, these values should be interpreted as study-area-specific patterns rather than universal thresholds, because local environmental conditions, pollutant composition, and host–flea dynamics may differ across regions. These environmental pressures are filtered through the lens of host biology. We confirmed that host sex, body size, and reproductive status are important but species-specific determinants of flea burden, reflecting divergent ecological strategies that may facilitate coexistence. X. c. conformis demonstrated a preference for temperate, stable microclimates and high sensitivity to AQI, whereas N. l. kuzenkovi showed greater tolerance for wind-chill stress and a close synchronicity with host reproductive cycles, particularly under pollution pressure.

In synthesizing these multi-scale findings, our research provides a robust theoretical basis for the precise prediction and targeted control of flea-borne disease. This integrated climate-pollution-host framework offers a more mechanistic understanding of vector ecology and is a critical step toward forecasting disease risk in a world of concurrent climate change and environmental degradation.
