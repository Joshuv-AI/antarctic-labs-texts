Evaluating the role of monovalent and divalent salts on interfacial tension reduction using nitrogen doped carbon dots for enhanced oil recovery
Authors: Younes Gholamzadeh, Mohammad Sharifi, Abdolhossein Hemmati-Sarapardeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanofluids based on carbon dots (CDs) effectively reduce interfacial tension (IFT) in enhanced oil recovery (EOR), but their stability against salt ions can be compromised by structural defects. Divalent ions like Ca2+ and Mg2+ can destabilize CDs, causing precipitation, while salts can also enhance IFT reduction. This study evaluates the impact of salt concentration and CD type on IFT between crude oil and water, examining various synthesis parameters and nitrogen-doping reactants, specifically ethylenediamine, urea, and thiourea with citric acid to synthesize E_CDs, U_CDs, and T_CDs, respectively. Characterization and IFT tests reveal that T_CDs have the highest polarity, with Ca2+ ions most destabilizing CDs and Mg2+ ions most effectively reducing IFT when CDs are stable. NaCl significantly reduces IFT in low-polar E_CDs (18 mN/m IFT reduction), while Ca2+ and Mg2+ increase IFT due to instability. In high-polar T_CDs, NaCl’s IFT reduction ability decreases (0.67 mN/m IFT reduction), but Ca2+ and Mg2+ more effectively reduce IFT by occupying interface sites (2.53 and 3.37 mN/m IFT reduction, respectively). Moderate-polar U_CDs (urea-derived CDs) show varied IFT reduction based on salt type and concentration. Increased citric acid as a reactant enhances the polarity of T_CDs and improves their IFT reduction capability for MgCl2 (from 3.37 IFT reduction to 8.88 mN/m IFT reduction) and CaCl2 (from 2.53 to 5.3 mN/m), while reducing the impact of NaCl on E_CDs. These findings highlight the complex interactions between nitrogen-doped CDs and salts, providing valuable insights for optimizing EOR operations in dense rock reservoirs. Nitrogen-doping and specific salt interactions can significantly influence the development of effective EOR strategies, maximizing recovery while minimizing operational costs and issues such as nanoparticle instability and reservoir pore plugging. Introduction

As numerous oil reservoirs have reached the midpoint of their operational lives, a significant decline in oil production levels has been recorded1,2. Consequently, due to reduced large-scale field explorations, researchers now prioritize developing new techniques to extract additional hydrocarbons using enhanced oil recovery (EOR) methods3,4. Nanotechnology is a significant method for enhancing oil production by altering fluid–fluid and rock-fluid interactions. Unlike chemical EOR methods, nanofluid systems have a minimal negative environmental impact, making them widely applicable5.

Investigating the IFT behavior of a fluid–fluid system by nanoparticles (NPs) at different operational conditions is crucial for the EOR mechanism. In water flooding scenarios, NPs play a key role in reducing the IFT between oil and water or altering the wettability of reservoir rocks6. While NPs can be found near micro-nano-scale pore throats in unconventional reservoirs, their large size poses challenges for injection into dense rock reservoirs, emphasizing the need for innovative solutions7. Carbon dots (CDs), smaller and more stable carbon NPs, offer a promising solution to the challenges posed by large NPs. CDs are carbon NPs with lateral dimensions below 20 nm8, which provide abundant functionalization sites and easy doping possibilities9,10, making them suitable for EOR applications11–14. These emerging zero-dimensional materials15 have garnered attention due to their excellent photoluminescence, low toxicity, and biocompatibility16–18. Despite the extensive literature on IFT, operational challenges persist in using nanofluids (particularly CDs) under actual reservoir flooding conditions. The salinity of injection brine can affect the CDs’ performance as a synergy effect or antagonistic effect. The different salt types added to the water on the surface or mixed in nanofluid by reservoir brines can lead to CD instability or better IFT reduction19,20. In hydrocarbon reservoirs, water formation contains significant divalent ions (e.g., calcium and magnesium), which can further contribute to nanofluid instability21. Therefore, evaluating all types of NPs (especially CDs) in the presence of monovalent and divalent salts is crucial for IFT testing. Two key mechanisms—salting-in and salting-out effects—explain IFT behavior in hydrocarbon-brine systems based on salt ion concentration. The salting-in effect occurs when salinity increases at low salt concentrations, resulting in higher amounts of polar compounds at the oil-brine interface, thereby reducing IFT. Conversely, the salting-out effect occurs at high salt concentrations, lowering polar compound amounts at the interface and increasing IFT22,23. The IFT of asphaltenic crude oil and nanofluids at varying salt concentrations depends on NP stability, and asphaltene types may have more complicated mechanisms. Another explanation for IFT behavior was proposed by Chávez-Miyauchi et al.to the electrical double layer (EDL) and Debye length. The Debye length inversely relates to the ion concentration in brine. At low salt concentrations, it broadens, enhancing electrostatic screening and adsorption of asphaltenes and resins. At high salinity, the Debye length shortens, reducing electrostatic screening. In another theory, Farhadi et al. developed a model that integrates drift and diffusion phenomena to study IFT behavior in oil-brine systems. According to this model, diffusion desorbs asphaltenes and resins, which increases IFT, while drift forces from the EDL adsorb them, decreasing IFT25.

For the past few years, researchers have investigated the impact of IFT on the effectiveness of EOR methods, particularly examining how different salt ions influence the IFT between the aqueous and oil phases26,27. For instance, Kakati et al.28 evaluate the effect of salt concentration and various types of ions present in an aqueous phase on the IFT between pure hydrocarbon and water. They reported that the hydrocarbon/brine IFT shows a minimum value in a low salinity concentration, and after that, the trend is up. Lashkarbolooki et al.29 evaluate the synergy effects of salt ions, resin, and asphaltene on IFT with low/high salinity brines containing NaCl, MgCl2, and CaCl2. Their results indicate that MgCl2 and resin complex ions occupy interfacial sites. However, CaCl2 disrupts the MgCl2 and asphaltene arrangement due to Ca2+’s higher affinity for asphaltene compared to Mg2+. Zhang et al.30 investigate the dynamic IFT of oil and brine and evaluate the salt ions and asphaltene adsorption process based on IFT changes over time. Li, Dai et al.31 reported that low concentrations of surfactant-free active silica nanofluids could decrease IFT, similar to highly concentrated silica nanofluids. However, they did not assess the stability of the nanofluids in the presence of salt ions. Soleimani et al.32 used carbon nano-tubes to maximize surface tension in different brine salinity for EOR applications. Rostami et al.33 employed different salt concentrations by diluting seawater to reduce IFT between synthetic/crude oil and brine during low-salinity water flooding. They reported that the IFT value reached a minimum value in two times diluted seawater and then increased. Jafarbeigi et al.34 used graphene nanosheets without salinity to reduce IFT and increase the oil recovery factor for carbonate rock. They successfully decreased the IFT between oil and their 500 ppm nanofluid from the reference value of 19.34 to 10.8 mN/m. However, they did not examine the effect of ions on NP instability or IFT changes, which is crucial for practical operations. Zhou et al.35 reported a nanofluid prepared by silicon quantum dots, which showed good stability in synthetic high-salinity water and improved the surfactant’s impact on IFT reduction. They reduced the IFT of Bakken oil and the synthetic surfactant/brine from 0.83 mN/m to 0.085 mN/m when the concentration of silicon quantum dots was 1200 ppm. Many articles have explored using various NPs for IFT reduction and wettability alteration, ultimately applying these nanofluids in oil recovery through core flood or glass micromodel tests. However, they often overlook the impact of NPs’ functional groups on ions and the optimization of synthesis procedures. Baragau, Lu et al.36 employed sulfur-doped CDs to EOR. Although they evaluated the CD’s stability with salinity, their results showed no considerable difference in IFT. Shayan Nasr et al.37 evaluated the effect of nitrogen-doped CDs on EOR performance. Their study revealed that using 5000 ppm nitrogen-doped CDs significantly reduced the oil/water IFT from 16.88 mN/m to 0.975 mN/m and the n-heptane/water IFT from 50.24 mN/m to 27.87 mN/m, even without surfactant assistance. Despite these promising results, they did not evaluate the effect of salt ions on nanofluid stability and IFT variations, which is essential for practical EOR applications given the high salinity of reservoir brine. Our group38 assessed the impact of salt ions and stable/unstable SiO2 NPs/asphaltenes on the IFT between synthetic oil and nanofluids. We found that as NPs become unstable due to high salinity, the IFT increases significantly, and after NP deposition, the IFT returns to its base value. It was shown that the IFT of synthetic oil in water from 38.60 to 34.21 mN/m by using 500 ppm SiO2 NPs. However, it is essential to evaluate the effects of various ions with multiple NPs synthesized through different procedures by conducting characterization tests. This approach is necessary to understand the impact of their functional groups on IFT and to uncover the underlying mechanisms. Cao et al.39 investigated CD stability in different salinity levels using dynamic light scattering (DLS) tests. They used CD nanofluids to reduce injection pressure and reported a 1000 ppm CD solution without salinity leading to a 43% reduction in IFT. Mirzavandi et al.40 used 1000 ppm silica-CDs in deionized water (DW) to reduce IFT from 28.3 to 9.5 mN/m.

While there is abundant literature on IFT changes caused by various types of nitrogen-doped CDs (N-CDs), comprehensive studies connecting these IFT modifications to salt ions are still lacking. The salts can be mixed with nanofluids before the operation or combined with brine during injection. Existing studies mainly report modest IFT reductions for CDs, often using surfactants alongside them. However, the synergistic effects of combining CDs with salt ions or the impact of CD instability due to salts on IFT have not been thoroughly explored. Additionally, no prior research has examined the influence of CD reaction time or reactant ratios on IFT variations with salts. Our systematic investigation explores the stability and instability of N-CDs under varying salinity conditions, intending to reduce IFT. Additionally, we examine mechanisms through CD characterization tests. Understanding the effects of NPs’ functional groups on ions helps optimize reactant choices and synthesis procedures, enhancing nanofluid stability and performance. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the impact of salinity on the IFT of N-CDs synthesized using different reactants, reaction times, and reactant ratios. The findings from this study provide valuable insights for EOR operation in dense rock reservoirs, leveraging the synergy effect of salt ions without relying on surfactants. This research underscores the potential of tailored NPs in improving oil recovery efficiency and suggests future research and practical applications in the oil and gas industry.

Materials and methods

Materials

In this study, sodium chloride (NaCl), calcium chloride (CaCl2), and magnesium chloride (MgCl2) were reagent-grade and purchased from Merck Chemical Co. (Germany) with a purity of 99%. IFT studies were followed using crude oil with an API gravity of 18.61. Thiourea (CH4N2S), citric acid (C6H8O7), ethylenediamine (EDA, C2H4(NH2)2), and urea (CH₄N2O) were procured from Merck Co. Furthermore, CDs were purified using a dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cutoff of 1000 Da, which we acquired from Spectra/Por to ensure that all unreacted materials were removed, confirming that the IFT reduction is attributable to the CDs. The experimental crude oil was sourced from an Iranian oil field. The study included constant compositional expansion (CCE) analysis and determination of the SARA (saturates, aromatics, resins, and asphaltenes) fraction, which is shown in supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Density measurements were conducted using an Anton-Paar instrument under ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure conditions.

Synthesis of N-CDs

The reaction products in the synthesis process are N-CDs with various surface functional groups. The CDs nanofluid was synthesized using EDA (E), urea (U), and thiourea (T) as N-reactants, along with varying citric acid (CA) content: high (H), medium (M), and low (L). The synthesis involved mixing a specific amount of urea and citric acid monohydrate with 100 mL of ultrapure water. The resulting mixture was transferred into a Teflon-lined vessel and subjected to hydrothermal reaction in an electronic oven under specific conditions and 180 °C. After filtration through a 0.22 μm micropore filter membrane, the product was dialyzed using a 1000 Dalton dialysis membrane and freeze-dried to obtain a faint blue powder. Similar CDs were prepared using EDA or thiourea as nitrogen (N) sources and citric acid monohydrate as the carbon source41. Table 1 provides details of the prepared N-CDs.

Due to the complexity of the synthesis mechanisms, stoichiometric coefficients do not yield the highest quantum yield. The selected coefficients provided the highest quantum yield and reduced costs for field operation. For E_CDs, the ratio of 3 achieved the highest quantum yield (94%), and the ratio of 1 had 87%42,43. For U_CDs, the ratio of 3 also had a high quantum yield44. Additionally, T_CDs were synthesized with the ratios of 3, 1, and 0.545,46. Moreover, the IFT did not show significant changes for high or low ratios. Therefore, these ratios were selected to cover all cases and consider higher quantum yields.

Nanofluid characterization

The UV–Vis absorption spectrum was acquired using a DR6000 UV–Vis spectrometer, covering a wavelength range of 190–1100 nm. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra for N-CD samples were recorded with an Agilent-G980A/USA instrument. Surface composition analysis of N-CDs within a depth of approximately 10 nm was performed using an X-ray photoelectron spectroscope (XPS) (BESTEC EA10, X-ray photoelectron spectrometer, Germany). The morphology and microstructure of N-CDs were examined using Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) and High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HR-TEM) with a TEM Zeiss EM10 and a TECNAI F20, respectively. Additionally, X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) patterns of N-CDs were carried out using an EQUIN3000X X-ray diffraction machine. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy measurements were conducted on a Nicolet iS10 FTIR spectrometer.

Nanofluid preparation

A specific quantity of N-CD powder was added to deionized water for nanofluid preparation. Subsequently, salts were added to the nanofluid at predefined concentrations. Throughout this study, all nanofluids were prepared with 1000 ppm concentrations of each N-CD and salt concentrations ranging from 0 to 200,000 ppm for each salt. This wide range of salinity was chosen for IFT tests to fully explore the various effects of salt ions. Table 2 illustrates the symbols and colors used in IFT curves.

Interfacial tension measurement

The IFT between the nanofluid and crude oil was assessed using the pendant drop method with an HT-42-20P/Germany apparatus. This setup included a light source, an oil injection system, and a camera for image capture (see Fig. 1). The IFT measurements were conducted by placing oil drops in the nanofluid at ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure, followed by ImageJ software analysis. Results and discussion

Characterization of N-CDs

In unconventional reservoirs, the abundance of micro-nano-scale pore throats necessitates using small NPs to ensure injectability. Furthermore, the type and quantity of functional groups on these particles significantly influence their interaction with salt ions and their dispersion stability, directly impacting their application performance. The analysis of CDs involved several techniques: high-resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HR-TEM) is used to determine particle dimensions, crystallinity, and lattice spacing; X-ray Diffraction (XRD) provides insights into CD lattice spacing, crystallinity, and preferred orientations; UV–Vis and Photoluminescence (PL) characterize hydrothermal carbonization, surface states, optical properties, and functional groups; FTIR Spectroscopy reveals CD functionalities; and X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) determines surface chemistry and elemental composition. The particle size of E5_L CDs, determined from TEM images analyzed using ImageJ software (see supplementary Figure S1), ranges from 2 to 10 nm. HR-TEM images confirm a lattice fringe distance of 0.24 nm for E5_L CDs42,48,49. This finding is consistent with other studies, which report an in-plane lattice spacing of 0.24 nm50–52. Additionally, the U5_L CDs are approximately 2–17 nm in size, while the T5_L CDs measure around 4–35 nm, as indicated in supplementary Figure S2. The XRD patterns reveal diffraction peaks at 21.6°, 24.6°, and 20.6° for E5_L, U5_L, and T5_L CDs, respectively (supplementary Figure S3). These peaks relate to lattice spacings of 0.41 nm, 0.36 nm, and 0.43 nm. Notably, these values exceed those obtained from HR-TEM due to the presence of functional groups on the CD surfaces43,53. UV–Vis and PL spectra of E5_L, U5_L, and T5_L CDs reveal absorption bands at approximately 340 nm and 240 nm, as shown in supplementary Figure S4. These bands correspond to n → π* and π → π* transitions of C = O and C = C bonds within the CDs. Also, they exhibit strong, excitation-independent blue emissions around 450 nm. Furthermore, the blue color observed in the CDs solution under a 365 nm UV lamp confirms the N-doping of CDs. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were performed to elucidate the functional groups of synthesized N-CDs’ chemical structures. These analyses confirmed the presence of functional groups like carboxyl, amine, and thiol, depending on the nitrogen source used. CDs, especially those synthesized with multiple precursors, often display a range of sizes and compositions due to their complex formation process. Unlike surfactants, which have a well-defined and consistent molecular structure, CDs can exhibit a variety of functional groups and structural variations. This inherent heterogeneity arises from synthesis conditions, functional groups, and particle size distribution. Synthesis conditions, such as variations in temperature, reaction time, and precursor ratios, can result in differences in the resulting CDs. Different nitrogen sources (EDA, urea, thiourea) introduce unique functional groups that contribute to the diversity in the CDs. Even within a single batch, CDs can range from a few nanometers to several tens of nanometers, leading to a mix of physical and chemical properties. The composition and structure of N-CDs synthesized hydrothermally can indeed be quite varied and are not as uniform as smaller molecules like surfactants. Using a dialysis membrane helps to remove unreacted materials or high-size nanomaterials. This variability can influence their surface activity and interfacial properties.

The FTIR spectra for pure CA, the O–H stretching occurs around 3500–3200 cm−1, the C = O stretching around 1700–1750 cm−1, the C–O stretching around 1300–1100 cm−1, and the CH2 rocking at 782 cm−154–57. For pure EDA, the N–H stretching occurs at 3353 and 3281 cm−1, the C–H stretching vibrations are found at 2920 and 2849 cm−1, and the N–H bending at 1592 cm−154,58–62. For pure Urea, the N–H stretching appears at 3200–3500 cm−1, the C = O stretching appears at 1685 cm−1, the N–H bending at 1615 cm−1, the C–N stretching appears at 1464 cm−1, and the NH2 rocking vibration at 1154 cm−144,63–65. For pure Thiourea, the N–H stretching occurs around 3400–3100 cm−1, the N–H bending at 1617 cm−1, the C–N stretching at 1471 cm−1, the C = S asymmetric stretching around 1411 cm−1, the NH2 rocking around 1082 cm−1, and the C = S symmetric stretching at 731 cm−165–67. The FTIR spectra of E5_L, U5_L, and T5_L CDs (Supplementary Figure S5) identify the functional groups present on their surfaces. The findings reveal that CDs exhibit a diverse range of oxygen and nitrogen functionalities, contributing to their excellent solubility in water. For E5_L, U5_L, T5_L CDs, and pure CA, C = O and O–H stretching vibrations indicate the existence of –COOH. However, the FTIR spectrum of CDs exhibits absorbance bands of C = O and C–N stretching vibrations, indicating the formation of amide carbonyl bonds. In addition, the N–H rocking vibration and N–H stretching vibrations confirm the existence of –NH2 (amine) groups on the surface of CDs54,59. The XPS spectra (supplementary Figures S6 and S7) support the FTIR spectroscopy analysis and confirm the presence of carbon (C), oxygen (O), nitrogen (N), and sulfur (S) atoms on the surfaces of E5_L, U5_L, and T5_L CDs. The full-scan XPS spectrum exhibits distinct peaks at approximately 284 eV (C1s), 401 eV (N1s), 532 eV (O1s), and 164 eV (S2p). In the figure, high-resolution C1s spectra reveal three peaks at binding energies of approximately 284.5 eV (assigned to C = C/C–C), 286 eV (assigned to C-O/C-N/C-S), and 288.4 eV (assigned to C = O). The N1s spectra exhibit peaks at 399.8 eV (pyrrolic N) and 401.5 eV (graphite N). Additionally, O1s spectra show peaks at 531.4 eV (C = O) and 533.1 eV (C-O).

The formation of N-CDs occurs in two distinct steps. Initially, the amine serves as a base, facilitating the de-hydrolysis reaction. Citric acid molecules self-assemble into a sheet-like structure and undergo de-hydrolysis, forming a graphene framework approximately 2 nm in diameter70,71. During this process, amide molecules form between the citric acid and amine. These amides then react with neighboring carboxylic groups, producing pyrrolic nitrogen through intramolecular de-hydrolysis. As a result, nitrogen atoms are incorporated into the graphene framework, with the CDs continuing to grow as more nitrogen is doped into the structure42. More information about the chemical structure elucidation of CDs exists in other research papers42,44,72–76. XPS analysis confirms N and O doping in E5_L and U5_L CDs, as well as N, O, and S doping in T5_L CDs. Notably, E5_L CDs exhibit a higher N doping degree (9%) compared to U5_L (about 2.9%) and T5_L (about 3.2%). This suggests that E5_L CDs, rich in pyrrolic N as indicated in supplementary Table S3, form long straight-chain hydrocarbon branches, which enhance polar-nonpolar phase bonding42 similar to alkylamine or polyamine functional groups. Additionally, T5_L CDs have a higher O content (33%) than E5_L and U5_L CDs (both around 17.3%), potentially contributing to greater polarity in water solutions. The structures of the N-CDs can be visualized as core CDs with attached functional groups such as -COOH (carboxyl), -NH2 (amine), -SH (thiol), and others, varying with the nitrogen source. These structures were derived based on FTIR and XPS data. Figure 3 presents column charts derived from XPS spectra that can be utilized to analyze the IFT results.

The peak intensity ratio N1s/C1s for E-CDs is ∼0.12, indicating robust nitrogen doping relative to U-CDs (0.36) and T-CDs (0.51). High relative intensity ratios of sp3/sp2 C and pyrrolic N/graphitic N in E-CDs suggest substantial surface amine functionalities. Conversely, the O1s/C1s ratio for T-CDs is 0.54—the highest among all samples—correlating with greater polarity in aqueous environments42,77,78. In this paper, for IFT reduction in EOR applications in the petroleum industry, only the surface properties and functional groups of CDs were important for evaluating their effect on the oil–water interface for IFT reduction tests.

NMR spectral analysis of N-CDs

The structural features of the synthesized CDs were further investigated using both proton (1H) and carbon (13C) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. Spectral profiles are presented in Fig. 4.

1H-NMR and 13C-NMR spectra of E-, U-, and T-CDs.

E-CDs displayed characteristic signals at δ = 8.08 ppm, attributed to amide-type N–H protons, and a cluster of peaks between δ = 3.06–3.48 ppm, corresponding to CH2 or CH groups adjacent to nitrogen or oxygen atoms. Peaks at 2.47–2.58 ppm were associated with aliphatic CH2 units near carboxyl or thiol groups, and a minor signal at 1.95 ppm was assigned to terminal aliphatic protons. U-CDs exhibited a similar high-field amide signal at δ = 8.40 ppm, reflecting urea-derived N–H protons. Peaks at δ = 3.00–3.16 ppm correspond to CH2 or CH groups near carbonyl functionalities, while the resonance at 2.45 ppm indicates aliphatic protons adjacent to polar groups. T-CDs presented distinct signals in the aliphatic region, with multiple peaks between δ = 2.49–2.79 ppm, likely arising from CH2–S and CH2–N groups introduced via thiourea. Additional peaks between δ = 0.82–1.42 ppm suggest saturated terminal CH3 groups44,74,79–83. Notably, 13C-NMR experiments were performed twice for U-CDs at different concentrations, yet no discernible signals were observed—only baseline noise. This absence is likely due to a combination of factors: the inherently low natural abundance of 13C nuclei (∼1.1%), poor relaxation behavior of carbon atoms in aggregated nanostructures, and low signal-to-noise ratio typical of dilute or poorly dispersed carbon nanomaterials84. Such limitations are well-documented in the analysis of graphitic and nanoparticle systems, where 13C-NMR sensitivity is significantly reduced unless samples are highly concentrated or isotopically enriched85.

E-CDs exhibited the highest hydrogen content (46.6 mol %), consistent with the presence of extensive aliphatic chains and hydroxyl/amine functionalities introduced via ethylenediamine. The nitrogen content (7.5 mol %) corroborates N-doping and amine incorporation, while oxygen accounted for 23.8 mol %, indicative of carboxyl and hydroxyl groups. U-CDs displayed a slightly higher nitrogen fraction (7.7 mol %) and elevated carbon content (28.0 mol %) compared to E-CDs, suggesting a greater retention of graphitic domains. The presence of sulfur (0.3 mol %) further confirms successful sulfur doping, while a lower nitrogen value (4.9 mol %) implies reduced amine incorporation relative to E- and U-CDs. All tests were conducted until the oil drop reached static equilibrium. Figure 6 illustrates the tree diagram summarizing all IFT tests with different salt ions.

The IFT changes for NaCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2 salts with the crude oil are shown in Fig. 7.

The IFT of NaCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2 solution with the crude oil.

The line graph reveals that as NaCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2 concentrations increase, the IFT generally decreases at low salt concentrations, followed by an increase at high salt concentrations. An alternative theory for evaluating IFT variation is Gibbs’s adsorption isotherm equation. Gibbs related IFT changes to Eq. (1)86,87.

where dγ is the alteration in IFT, Γi represents surface excess concentration, αi shows the chemical activity of ith component in the solution, R is universal gas constant, and T denotes the absolute temperature. This equation reveals that the salt ions adsorb to the interface in low salinity, resulting in a positive surface excess (Γ) and decreased IFT. Conversely, the interface becomes saturated with ions in high salinity, leading to a negative surface excess and increased IFT. Another theory for evaluating IFT is the salting-in and salting-out mechanism. The salting-in effect, observed at low salt concentrations, increases ion adsorption at the phase interface, thereby reducing the IFT. In contrast, the salting-out effect at higher salt concentrations decreases ion availability at the interface, increasing IFT. However, this mechanism primarily applies to simple systems, such as pure hydrocarbon systems with salt water. Thus, it lacks clarity when more complex components like asphaltenes, resins, or NPs are present25. The primary reason for changes in IFT can be attributed to molecular interaction mechanisms at the oil-brine interface, such as the adsorption and desorption of asphaltenes and resins influenced by salt ions.

Another explanation for IFT behavior was proposed by Chávez-Miyauchi et al.24, based on measurements of the interfacial viscoelasticity of crude oil-brine systems at different salinity levels of NaCl solutions. In the absence of salts, polar components in the oil, such as asphaltenes and resins, tend to adsorb onto the interface due to their inherent polarity. The reduction in IFT at low salinity levels was linked to the electrical double layer (EDL) and Debye length88. The electric field generated by the EDL pulls polar molecules from the oil phase toward the interface, thereby reducing the IFT. The thickness of the EDL is governed by the Debye length. The Debye length, κ−1, represents the distance over which a charge carrier’s electrostatic effect is screened by surrounding ions in a solution and is expressed by Eq. (2).

where ϵ0 is the permittivity of free space, ϵr the dielectric constant of brine, NA the Avogadro number, T is the temperature, kB the Boltzmann constant, e the electron charge, and I the ionic strength, defined as Eq. (3).

where zi is the valence of species i, ci is the molar concentration of species i, and n is the number of different species. According to this equation, the Debye length is inversely related to the ion concentration in brine. The diffuse layer becomes broader at low salt concentrations, resulting in stronger electrostatic screening. This enhanced screening facilitates the adsorption and organization of polar oil components (asphaltenes and resins) at the interface through electrostatic attractions (asphaltenes/resins adsorption effect). Conversely, at high salinity levels, the Debye length becomes shorter, and diminishes the electrostatic screening. This reduction in screening weakens the attraction of polar components to the interface (asphaltenes/resins depletion effect)24. Figure 8 illustrates this mechanism.

Variation in Debye length (κ−1, dashed line) and electrostatic potential (ψ) in relation to asphaltene adsorption and repulsion at the water-oil interface: (a) Without ions, (b) Low salt concentration with a large Debye length, (c) High salt concentration with a short Debye length.

The IFT values of CaCl2 and MgCl2 salts sharply decrease with increasing salt concentration. This reduction is attributed to the high ionic strength and the ability of Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions to absorb more asphaltenes and resins at the water–oil interface. Figure 7 indicates that the IFT decreased from 18.71 to 4.9 mN/m by increasing CaCl2 concentration from 0 to 50,000 ppm, and IFT decreased from 18.71 to 8.66 mN/m by increasing MgCl2 concentration from 0 to 25,000 ppm. Based on Eqs. (2) and (3), divalent ions (e.g., Ca2+, Mg2+) strongly influence ionic strength, Debye length, and IFT at the oil–water interface. Their squared charge number (zi2​) significantly increases ionic strength, even at low concentrations, resulting in a shorter Debye length and more effective electrostatic screening. With higher charge density, divalent ions neutralize interfacial charges more effectively, reduce IFT, and enhance the adsorption of asphaltenes and resins, stabilizing the interface. Previous studies have reported varying IFT reduction values for salts in different crude oils27,29. For this specific crude oil, MgCl2 exhibits a lower IFT value than CaCl2 up to a concentration of 25,000 ppm. This difference may be attributed to the stronger electronegativity of Mg2+, which facilitates the absorption of asphaltenes and resins at the fluid interface, leading to decreased IFT. However, at 50,000 ppm and higher concentrations, CaCl2 demonstrates a lower IFT than MgCl2. This behavior could be due to the higher affinity of Ca2+ ions for asphaltene molecules compared to Mg2+ ions29. Asphaltenes and resins exhibit distinct behaviors with increasing salinity, and their interaction within oil further affects IFT. Consequently, Ca2+ ions form stronger bonds with asphaltene molecules, leading to a reduction in IFT29.

Farhadi et al. proposed a model combining drift and diffusion phenomena to analyze IFT behavior in oil-brine systems25. In the brine phase, an oil drop is surrounded by an EDL due to salt ions. The EDL generates an electric force (drift current) pulling surface-active molecules toward the interface, while diffusion caused by the concentration gradient (diffusion current) pulls them back. Without dissolved ions (deionized water), IFT reduction is minimal, suggesting the concentration gradient alone is insufficient, requiring a balance with drift forces. Diffusion can desorb asphaltenes and resins, increasing IFT, while drift forces from the EDL adsorb them, decreasing IFT. Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions, with higher charge density and ionic strength, create stronger drift currents and suppress diffusion currents compared to Na+ ions. In summary, the effect of each ion depends on its concentration, ionic strength, hydration radius, and the properties of asphaltenes and resins, altering the balance between currents and resulting in IFT changes.

Combined impact of salt types and N-CDs on IFT

To assess the stability of CDs in the presence of monovalent and divalent salts, 1000 ppm of E5_L CDs was utilized at varying NaCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2 concentrations (Fig. 9). The investigation aimed to evaluate these salts’ potential antagonistic or synergistic effects on the IFT behavior of CDs.

IFT value between crude oil and 1000 ppm E5_L CDs solution with different NaCl, MgCl2, and CaCl2 concentrations.

In the NaCl concentration graph, the addition of 1000 ppm E5_L CDs leads to a sharp reduction in IFT. Remarkably, this IFT reduction of approximately 18 mN/m (compared to the base case without CDs and salt) occurs even without the presence of any surfactant, highlighting the synergistic effect between NaCl ions and E5_L CDs. E_CDs exhibit higher N doping levels compared to other N-doped CDs and are rich in pyrrolic N, as indicated in supplementary Table S3, which facilitates the formation of long straight-chain alkylamine or polyamine functional groups. Additionally, their surface features polar atoms like O, capable of absorbing polar water molecules. Consequently, these CDs function effectively as connectors between two phases, similar to surfactants. XPS and FTIR tests confirm the elevated N content (9%) on the surface of E5_L CDs. Furthermore, the freeze-dried E_CDs exhibit low polarity due to their sticky NP behavior, similar to resin, and prolonged dissolution time in water. When E5_L CDs interact with NaCl, they attract asphaltene and resin from the oil bulk to the phase interface, enhancing bonding with the nonpolar phase and significantly reducing IFT. The CaCl2 curve reveals an initial increase in IFT with rising salt concentration, followed by a subsequent decrease at high salt levels. The elevated IFT in E5_L solution with increasing CaCl2 concentration can be attributed to CD instability upon encountering Ca2+. This instability is influenced by several factors, including Electrostatic Interactions, Van der Waals Forces, and Hydration Shells89,90. Electrostatic interactions arise from the charges on the NP surfaces. When NPs have like charges, they repel each other, which helps stabilize the suspension and prevent aggregation. However, in the presence of high ionic strength, these repulsive forces can be screened, leading to reduced stability and increased aggregation91. Van der Waals forces are attractive forces arising from temporary dipoles in atoms or molecules. These forces are always present and can lead to the aggregation of NPs if not counteracted by repulsive forces92. Hydration shells refer to the layers of water molecules that surround a NP. These shells can stabilize by creating a physical barrier that prevents NPs from coming into close contact. However, if the hydration shells are disrupted, for example, by changes in ionic strength or pH, the NPs can aggregate93.

The high ionic strength of Ca2+ and Mg2+ can neutralize repulsive forces and disrupt the hydration shells of NPs. This disruption allows the NPs to come into close proximity, which counteracts Van der Waals forces and results in NPs aggregation. XPS and FTIR tests confirm that the CDs contain O, N, and S atoms, along with carboxyl, amine, and thiol functional groups. These functional groups significantly affect NP stability. Carboxyl groups provide negative charges, causing electrostatic repulsion and stabilization of CDs. However, Ca2+ and Mg2+ with high positive ionic strengths can screen this repulsion, leading to aggregation. Additionally, amine and thiol groups can destabilize NPs through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, depending on the surrounding environment. Previous studies indicate that NP instability disrupts both NP and salt performance, leading to increased IFT38. Consequently, E5_L CDs exhibit an antagonistic effect with Ca2+. Furthermore, visual observations confirm that adding CaCl2 salt to E_CDs induces instability, supporting this hypothesis. This instability is evident through the precipitation of CDs at a concentration of 5000 ppm, which significantly increases to 25,000 ppm. Beyond 100,000 ppm, substantial CD flocculations are deposited. Removing CDs from the system restores the IFT to its base value. In the case of MgCl2, E5_L CDs exhibit minimal instability, resulting in reduced IFT with increasing salinity. However, a slight increase occurs at concentrations exceeding 50,000 ppm. The greater stability of CDs in the presence of Mg2+ compared to Ca2+ is attributed to the larger hydration radius of Mg2+. The hydration radius, which is the effective radius of an ion surrounded by water molecules, is crucial in determining ion behavior in aqueous solutions, particularly in nano aggregation, deposition, and instability. Although Mg2+ has a smaller ionic radius, it has a higher charge density than Ca2+, resulting in a larger hydration radius. This higher charge density causes Mg2+ to attract more water molecules, forming a more substantial hydration shell94,95. Consequently, the larger hydration radius of Mg2+ reduces the tendency for CDs to aggregate compared to Ca2+, leading to the observed lower instability in E5_L CDs. Figure 10 illustrates the mechanism of IFT change due to the interaction of monovalent and divalent salts with CDs.

IFT change due to the interaction of monovalent and divalent salts with CDs and the related crude oil droplet.

The increase in IFT during CD precipitation (case E in Fig. 10) can be explained by the adsorption of NPs aggregations to the interface. As mentioned, this aggregation occurs through interactions between the negatively charged carboxyl groups on CDs and divalent ions like Ca2+. Additionally, these aggregates may contain a mixture of positive and negative ions. When these aggregates adsorb at the oil–water interface, both attractive and repulsive forces act on the oil phase, eliminating the powerful drift current caused by CDs or ions alone. Therefore, the diffusion current, driven by the concentration gradient, depletes asphaltenes and resins to the oil phase, resulting in increased IFT. Furthermore, when CDs agglomerate, they lose their ability to orient their functional groups effectively, reducing their attractive force towards the oil phase and preventing the formation of a strong EDL. Consequently, asphaltenes and resins are depleted, leading to higher IFT. As CD aggregates grow and increase in size (case F in Fig. 10), the NP solution reaches a deposition point, clearing the interface of both positively and negatively charged aggregates. This leaves only salts at the interface, which create a strong drift current, drawing asphaltenes and resins back to the interface and reducing the IFT.

Subsequently, the impact of salt ions on IFT for U5_L and T5_L CDs, which differ in precursor atoms (O, S) and molecular structure, was evaluated. Figure 11 displays the IFT measurements using U_CDs across various NaCl, CaCl2, and MgCl2 concentrations.

IFT value between crude oil and 1000 ppm U5_L CDs solution with different NaCl, MgCl2, and CaCl2 concentrations.

In the NaCl graph for U5_L CDs, the IFT decreases by adding 1000 ppm of these CDs, from 19.03 to 12.33 mN/m. However, U_CDs do not exhibit as significant an IFT reduction as E_CDs. Thus, it seems U_CDs don’t create EDL like E_CDs, which generate a strong drift current that pulls asphaltene and resin molecules toward the interface. The underlying cause lies in the synthesis steps of CDs, including dehydrolysis, aromatization, and critical supersaturation, affecting the structure of U_CDs. XPS tests reveal that U5_L CDs have a low N content (2.9%). Unlike EDA, urea molecules have a single C atom and three binding sites, facilitating three-dimensional aggregation during synthesis. These characteristics enhance the polarity of U5_L CDs, restricting their presence at the fluid interface and preventing substantial IFT reduction. Additionally, U_CDs exhibit superior solubility in water compared to E_CDs, emphasizing their polarity. On the other hand, the IFT of U5_L and CaCl2 decreases smoothly up to 10,000 ppm salt concentration, followed by an increasing trend up to 200,000 ppm. This rise in IFT results from U5_L CDs instability at high salt concentrations (similar to E5_L at low salt concentrations). The high ionic strength of Ca2+ neutralizes the repulsive forces between U5_L CDs and disrupts their hydration shells, allowing the NPs to come closer and counteract Van der Waals forces, resulting in U5_L CD aggregation. At 25,000 ppm CaCl2, U5_L CDs precipitate visibly (usually occurring at lower salt concentrations but remaining invisible). These precipitates, containing a mixture of positive and negative charges, adsorb at the oil–water interface. The simultaneous action of attractive and repulsive forces neutralizes each other, preventing the formation of a strong electric double layer. Consequently, the power of the drift current is reduced. As a result, the diffusion current driven by the concentration gradient depletes asphaltenes and resins to the oil phase, leading to an increase in IFT. Unlike E5_L CDs at high CaCl2 concentrations, U5_L CD aggregates exhibit minimal growth and do not reach the deposition point due to greater stability. Consequently, the IFT does not decrease at high CaCl2 concentrations. Specifically, at 100,000 ppm CaCl2 concentration, the IFT value for E5_L CDs reaches 5.26 mN/m (see Fig. 9), which is close to the IFT value of the base CaCl2 solution (5.28 mN/m, see Fig. 7). However, at the same CaCl2 concentration, the IFT value for U5_L CDs is 17.20 mN/m, significantly different from the CaCl2 base solution’s IFT. This considerable difference indicates that the U5_L CDs’ aggregation prevents the formation of a strong EDL by Ca2+ ions, similar to case E in Fig. 10.

The U5_L CDs exhibit a similar decreasing slope with MgCl2 as NaCl, indicating their stability. Visual observations also confirm that U5_L CDs remain stable with MgCl2, unlike CaCl2. This stability is attributed to Mg2+ having a larger hydration radius than Ca2+. Consequently, the larger hydration radius of Mg2+ reduces its tendency to attract U5_L CDs compared to Ca2+, leading to the observed stability of U5_L CDs with MgCl2. As observed by XPS tests, T5_L CDs exhibit a higher O content (33%) and a lower N content (3.2%) on their surface. Additionally, the presence of extra sulfur atoms enhances the CDs’ polarity compared to others. Notably, the molecular structure of thiourea is more similar to urea. Both groups’ S and O atoms have similar hybrid orbitals, specifically an sp2 structure. Consequently, T5_L CDs tend to create bonds with oil components, preventing ions from affecting the interface. This effect is due to the higher charge density and ionic strength of Mg2+ and Ca2+ ions, which create stronger drift currents and suppress diffusion currents compared to Na+ ions. Mg2+ ions, with their smaller ionic radius, have a higher charge density than Ca2+ ions, giving them a stronger ability to form an electric double layer that pulls surface-active molecules from the oil phase toward the interface. Consequently, the IFT of T_CDs solutions is more effectively reduced by Mg2+ ions than by Ca2+ ions.

Consequently, E10_L/E15_L CDs lose their ability to effectively interact with the oil phase, leading to an increase in IFT. The data reveal that T5_M CDs exhibit a more pronounced reduction in IFT with increasing concentrations of CaCl2 and MgCl2 compared to T5_L CDs. The IFT reductions are 3.86 mN/m for CaCl2 and 7.44 mN/m for MgCl2 by increasing salinity from 0 to 200,000 ppm (5.3 mN/m and 8.88 mN/m IFT reduction compared to the initial case without salt and CDs, respectively). This suggests that CaCl2 and MgCl2 exhibit a better synergistic effect with CDs that are produced with a higher proportion of citric acid or contain more carboxyl functional groups.

Overall, CDs synthesized from EDA demonstrate a significant synergistic effect with NaCl salt, suggesting that their use in EOR operations could be advantageous. The straightforward synthesis methods for these CDs make them a potentially cost-effective option for EOR applications when used with the appropriate salt. Additionally, the primary materials required for CD preparation, such as EDA at low concentrations (e.g., 1000 ppm), are relatively inexpensive. Nonetheless, further research and field trials are necessary to confirm the performance and economic viability of CDs in large-scale oil recovery under reservoir conditions. Applicability and limitations

Generalization to other NPs

The interaction of functional groups with divalent ions, leading to NP aggregation and changes in IFT, can apply to other NP systems with similar surface chemistries. NaCl works better with CDs having more alkylamine or polyamine functional groups, while CaCl2 and MgCl2 are more effective with CDs containing higher citric acid or carboxyl groups. NP precipitation typically increases IFT. Furthermore, the deposition of NPs after precipitation reverts it to baseline levels in the absence of NPs38. Longer reaction times initially degrade functional groups, increasing IFT. On the other hand, it can reduce oxygen content96,97, making CDs more hydrophobic and reducing IFT. However, further tests are needed to examine IFT changes across different oil types.

Unique behavior of N-CDs

Nitrogen doping introduces specific functional groups that influence interactions with ions and significantly reduce IFT. CDs (2–10 nm) are smaller than NPs (10–100 nm), allowing deeper reservoir penetration. CDs demonstrate superior stability under varying temperatures and salt conditions compared to NPs. CDs are more economical to produce and require lower concentrations for effectiveness. They also offer a wider variety of surface functional groups. These characteristics make CDs a promising candidate for EOR applications compared to traditional NPs35,36,98,99.

Applicability of results

Understanding CD behavior aids in developing EOR strategies leveraging NP-salt interactions. The findings have practical implications for field operations and economic optimization. This study provides guidelines for using ions in nanofluids, either mixed at the surface or combined with brine in the reservoir. The goal is to enhance performance and avoid NP deposition. The study also highlights the effects of synthesis duration and reactant ratios on NP properties and effectiveness. By applying the findings of this study, operation designers can optimize the use of ions for enhanced performance and avoid NP deposition in reservoirs.

Limitations and broader impact

The study provides insights into N-CDs’ IFT behavior, though specific interactions and stability profiles may differ from other NPs. Functional groups significantly impact stability and IFT reduction in different oil types. Future research should explore a broader range of NP types and surface modifications to better understand factors influencing NP stability and IFT behavior in EOR.

Conclusions

This study investigates the impact of single and divalent salt ions on the IFT behavior of various N-CD solutions to determine their synergistic or antagonistic effects. The CDs were synthesized using EDA, urea, and thiourea as N dopants, with citric acid serving as the C source, under different reaction times and reactant ratios. The IFT between these CDs and crude oil was measured in the presence of NaCl, MgCl2, and CaCl2 salts. The findings highlight that mixing different salt types either before the operation or with brine during injection can cause CD instability or synergistic IFT reduction, optimizing EOR operations. Understanding N-CDs’ functional group effects on ions helps in choosing reactants and synthesis methods, enhancing nanofluid stability and performance. This also points to potential future research and applications in the oil and gas industry. Key findings include:

Characterization tests indicate that T_CDs have the highest polarity, followed by U_CDs, then E_CDs. IFT tests reveal that Ca2+ ions with high ionic strength and low hydration radius most significantly destabilize CDs. Also, Mg2+ ions, with higher charge density, are most effective in reducing IFT for T_CDs.

For optimal results with the specific type of oil used in this study, when dealing with NaCl salts, it is recommended to use EDA as a reactant to create polyamine functional groups on CDs, shorter synthesis times, and lower citric acid levels. In contrast, when dealing with CaCl2 and MgCl2 salts, it is preferable to use reactants such as urea and thiourea, with longer synthesis times and higher amounts of citric acid and carboxylic acid groups. However, for field applications, the water salinity composition should be considered, and synthesis can be refined based on the specific oil and water composition of each field.
