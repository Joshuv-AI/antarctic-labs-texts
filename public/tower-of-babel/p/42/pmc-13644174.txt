L-NAME promotes foam cell formation and synthetic phenotype marker changes in vascular smooth muscle cells independent of hemodynamic effects
Authors: Huanhua Du, Jia Ling, Zheng Lu, Yutong Liu, Jinsong Yan, Junli Ma, Li Li, Gang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study investigated whether L-NAME, a nitric oxide synthase (NOS) inhibitor commonly used to model hypertension-associated endothelial dysfunction, can exert direct pro-atherogenic effects on vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) independent of systemic hemodynamic influences.MethodsMouse aortic VSMCs (MOVAS) were treated with non-cytotoxic concentrations of L-NAME (50 µM) or oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL, 100 µg/mL), which served as a positive atherogenic control. Lipid accumulation was assessed by Oil Red O staining. The expression of contractile phenotype markers (ACTA2, MYH11, and α-SMA), the synthetic phenotype marker SPP1, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2 and MMP-9) was quantified by qRT-PCR and immunofluorescence.ResultsBoth L-NAME and ox-LDL treatments caused marked intracellular lipid accumulation in MOVAS cells, consistent with VSMC-derived foam cell formation. L-NAME and ox-LDL reduced the mRNA expression of contractile phenotype markers (ACTA2 and MYH11, p < 0.05) and decreased α-SMA protein expression (p < 0.001). Conversely, both treatments increased the mRNA expression of the synthetic phenotype marker SPP1 (p < 0.05) and matrix-remodeling genes MMP-2 and MMP-9 (p < 0.05). These effects were directionally similar to those induced by ox-LDL.ConclusionL-NAME directly promotes lipid accumulation and synthetic phenotype-associated marker changes in MOVAS cells under the present in vitro conditions. Because eNOS/NOS activity, intracellular NO levels, rescue pathways, and primary VSMC validation were not assessed, these data should be interpreted as phenotypic evidence that NOS/NO pathway disruption may contribute to VSMC dysfunction rather than definitive proof of a single eNOS-dependent mechanism.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is one of the most common causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide and is characterized by the slow, progressive accumulation of atherosclerotic plaque within the coronary arterial wall [1]. Atherosclerotic disease is an intricate and multifactorial disorder marked by lipid accumulation, inflammatory-cell infiltration, vascular endothelial injury, and profound changes in the cellular composition of the vascular wall [2]. Although hypertension is one of several CAD risk factors, it is an independent risk factor with high prevalence among CAD patients. Epidemiological and clinical studies indicate that hypertension approximately triples the risk of developing atherosclerotic disease in the aorta and increases the likelihood of developing CAD by about 70% [3]. Additionally, CAD patients with comorbid hypertension tend to have more advanced coronary lesions and a poorer long-term prognosis than CAD patients without hypertension [4]. Increased mechanical stress on the vascular wall caused by high blood pressure likely contributes to vascular injury; however, the cellular events activated by hypertensive conditions and their relationship with atherosclerotic plaque formation remain incompletely understood.

A large body of evidence supports the idea that hyperactivation of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) is at the core of hypertension-associated blood vessel damage [5]. Elevated levels of Angiotensin II (Ang-II) in hypertension are recognized as both a strong vasoconstrictor and an extremely strong pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidant signalling molecule [6]. Ang-II stimulates the production of large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within the vascular wall, resulting in oxidative stress, while also increasing the production of many pro-inflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules, resulting in chronic low-level inflammation [7]. Furthermore, it is widely accepted that oxidative stress and inflammation caused by Ang-II represent major mechanisms connecting hypertension to vascular dysfunction and continuing vascular injury.

Vascular Smooth Muscle Cells (VSMCs) are the primary cell type found in the middle (media) layer of arterial blood vessels. VSMCs play a vital role in the development of atherosclerosis. In healthy arteries, VSMCs have a contractile or quiescent phenotype, which is determined by VSMC-specific protein expression; these proteins include α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, a protein encoded by the ACTA2 gene), smooth muscle myosin heavy chain 11 (MYH11), and calponin [8]. VSMCs help to control the amount of blood to the tissue they supply and form the structural framework for the wall of the artery. VSMCs also can change from their quiescent contractile state to a synthetic phenotype when exposed to certain atherogenic stimuli, e.g., oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL), inflammatory cytokines, and mechanical stress [9, 10]. The synthetic phenotype is characterized by the down-regulation of proteins associated with the contractile phenotype and the up-regulation of proteins associated with the synthetic phenotype, e.g. secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1). Cells with a synthetic phenotype gain the capacity to undergo proliferation, migration, and produce large amounts of extracellular matrix components, as well as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [10]. The phenotypic change of VSMCs represents an important first step in the development of the fibrous cap and progression of the atherosclerotic plaque, and subsequently will contribute to narrowing of the artery’s lumen and instability of the plaque.

L-NAME (N-nitro-L-arginine methyl ester) is a competitive inhibitor of NOS isoforms and is widely used to reduce nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability in experimental models of hypertension [11]. Although L-NAME is often discussed in relation to endothelial NOS (eNOS), its interpretation in isolated VSMC cultures requires caution because MOVAS cells lack endothelial cells and may express low or variable levels of NOS isoforms. NO contributes to vascular homeostasis by promoting vasodilation, inhibiting platelet aggregation, limiting leukocyte adhesion, and restraining vascular smooth muscle cell growth. Reduced NO bioavailability can therefore contribute to vasoconstriction, increased peripheral resistance, and hypertension [12]. In vivo, chronic L-NAME administration also activates the renin-angiotensin system (RAS), increases Ang-II signaling, promotes oxidative stress, and enhances vascular inflammation [13–15]. Whether L-NAME also acts directly on VSMCs in the absence of endothelial and hemodynamic influences remains an important question.

Although independent evidence supports VSMC phenotype transitions and the use of L-NAME as a hypertension-associated vascular injury model, direct in vitro evidence for the effect of L-NAME on VSMC lipid accumulation, phenotype marker expression, and matrix-remodeling genes remains limited. It is also unclear whether these effects are mediated by NOS/NO pathway disruption, other NOS isoforms, oxidative stress, Ang-II signaling, or other downstream pathways.

We hypothesized that L-NAME can directly induce pro-atherogenic phenotypic changes in MOVAS cells, including contractile-to-synthetic marker shifts, intracellular lipid accumulation, and enhanced matrix-remodeling gene expression, independent of systemic hemodynamic factors.

To test this hypothesis, we performed an in vitro study using MOVAS cells, a mouse aortic VSMC line. MOVAS cells were treated with a non-cytotoxic concentration of L-NAME and compared with cells treated with the classic atherogenic stimulus ox-LDL. The specific aims were to: (1) determine non-cytotoxic working concentrations of L-NAME and ox-LDL; (2) evaluate L-NAME-associated lipid accumulation by Oil Red O staining; (3) quantify contractile phenotype markers (ACTA2, MYH11, and α-SMA) and the synthetic phenotype marker SPP1; and (4) measure matrix-remodeling markers MMP-2 and MMP-9.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and maintenance

MOVAS (mouse aortic vascular smooth muscle) cells were obtained from Qisai Biotechnology, Wuhan, China (Cat# QS-M039A). The cells were cultured in complete growth medium containing MOVAS CSF basal medium (QS-M039A) supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) premium fetal bovine serum (QS-S423) and 1% (vol/vol) penicillin-streptomycin solution (QS-S402). Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Complete growth medium was replaced every 48–72 h. When cells reached approximately 80% confluence, they were passaged using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (QS-S401) at a 1:10 split ratio. Cells between passages 3 and 10 were used for all experiments to maintain consistency in growth characteristics and phenotype [16].

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

The aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of L-NAME and oxidized low-density lipoproteins (ox-LDL) on cell viability at nontoxic working concentrations for use in future studies. The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, QS-S321, Qisai Biotechnology) was used as a method of determining cell viability. MOVAS cells were harvested in their logarithmic growth phase, counted, and then plated into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 10^3 cells per well with 100 µL of complete media. The plates were incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 overnight to allow for attachment of the cells.

Following incubation overnight, the media was changed to fresh media containing different concentrations of L-NAME (MedChemExpress, HY-18729 A) or ox-LDL (YuanYe Biotechnology, S24879). L-NAME was tested at concentrations of 6.25, 12.5, 25.0, 50.0, 100.0, 200.0, 400.0, and 800.0 µmol/L; ox-LDL was tested at concentrations of 6.25, 12.5, 25.0, 50.0, 100.0, 200.0, 400.0, and 800.0 µg/mL. Control wells contained only media with no drug treatment, while blank wells contained only media. Each concentration of drug treatment was replicated three times.

The supernatant was carefully aspirated off after 24 h of treatment. Each well was treated with a 10% CCK-8 solution (prepared by diluting the CCK-8 reagent in MOVAS cell-specific media) at 100 µL/well, followed by an additional one hour of incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The absorbance (optical density; OD) at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader (HTS-XT) from Bruker Optics (Germany) [17]. The formula used to calculate cell viability (relative proliferation) is as follows:

Cell Viability (%) = [(OD experimental well – OD blank well) / (OD control well – OD blank well)] × 100.

To avoid confounding results due to cytotoxicity, we chose the highest drug concentrations providing at least 90% cell viability vs. the untreated control group for subsequent mechanistic experiments. These concentrations were 50 µmol/L for L-NAME and 100 µg/mL for ox-LDL, as indicated in Fig. 2.

Oil Red O staining for lipid accumulation

Oil Red O staining was used to visualize and evaluate levels of intracellular neutral lipid accumulation. MOVAS cells were cultured onto sterile glass coverslips within 6-well plates, and once 70–80% confluence was achieved, they were treated with either 50 µmol/L L-NAME, 100 µg/mL ox-LDL, or control media for 24 h. After treatment, culture media was carefully removed, and cells were rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 1×, QS-S001; Qisai Biotech) for 1 min each rinse. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (BT-P202, Qisai Biotech) at room temperature for 15 min. After fixation, the fixative was removed and rinsed three times with PBS (3 min each rinse).

For staining, coverslips were first immersed twice in 60% isopropanol for 2 s each time. Cells were then incubated with freshly filtered Oil Red O working solution from the Oil Red O staining kit (BT-P104, Qisai Biotechnology) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Excess dye was removed by washing with 60% isopropanol until the background became colorless. After washing with distilled water for 2 min, nuclei were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin solution (BT-P107, Qisai Biotechnology) for 10 min and washed with PBS for 5 min. Coverslips were mounted with a water-soluble mounting medium (0100-01, SouthernBiotech) onto glass slides. Stained cells were viewed and photographed using a light microscope (LV150, Nikon, Japan) at 200x magnification. Lipid droplets appeared as bright red round spots [18].

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total mRNA quantification of either VSMC marker (miVSMC) or matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) was determined through qRT-PCR analyses from MOVAS cell cultures grown in 6-well plates and treated for 24 h with either 50µM L-NAME, or 100 µg/mL of ox-LDL or control media. After treating cells, they were collected, and total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (T9424; Sigma) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A260 and A280 nm absorbance values were used to determine the concentration and purity of each RNA sample (acceptable A260/A280 ratios range from 1.8 to 2.0). Approximately 1 µg of each RNA sample was used to generate cDNA via reverse transcription using the high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (N8080234; Thermo Fisher Scientific) following manufacturer’s instructions, which was done on a thermal cycler, and the resultant cDNAs were stored at -20 °C until needed.

The qRT-PCR assay was conducted using the AceQ qPCR SYBR Green master mix (Q111 from Vazyme Biotech in Nanjing China) and a real time PCR system. A total of 10 µl reactions were prepared for each sample containing: 5 µl of 2× SYBR Green Master Mix; 0.2 µl of 10µM forward primer, 0.2 µl of 10µM reverse primer (final concentration of each was 0.2µM); 2 µl of cDNA (1:5 dilution); and 2.6 µl nuclease-free water. Cycling conditions included an initial denaturation cycle at 95 °C for 10 min followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 60 s. A melt-curve analysis was performed for verification of products being amplified as specific completed amplification cycles. Melt curve protocol included 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 60 s followed by a ramp to 95 °C for 15 s. Immunofluorescence staining for α-SMA protein

To analyze the protein expression of the contractile marker α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) by means of immunofluorescence, MOVAS cells were grown on sterile glass coverslips in 6-well plates and subjected to treatment with 50 µmol/L L-NAME or 100 µg/mL ox-LDL or were incubated in a control medium. After treatment: (i) wash the 96 well plate with 1× PBST (PBS + 0.05%Tween-20, BT-P305, Qisai Biotechnology). Each wash was 3 min long; (ii) fix the cells in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and again wash with sufficient PBS-T. The cells were then permeabilized with(iii) 0.5% Trition X-100(ST795, Beyotime) diluted with 1× PBS at 5 min at room temperature. Each of these washes was also 3 min long. The cells needed to have their non-specific binding sites blocked; thus, the coverslips were incubated for 30 min at room temperature with normal goat serum (AR1009, Boster Bio, Wuhan, China).

The coverslips were incubated in a blocking solution at 4 °C under a humid chamber overnight followed by the addition of a rabbit anti-α-SMA monoclonal antibody (ab124964, Abcam), diluted 1:100 in blocking solution. After the overnight incubation period, the coverslips were washed 3 times with PBST (3 min each), and then they were incubated with a goat anti-rabbit FITC conjugated secondary antibody (BA1105, Boster Bio), diluted 1:100 in blocking solution, for 1 h at 37 °C in a dark, humid environment. All subsequent incubations were performed in the dark to protect the fluorophore.

Following the incubation with the secondary antibody, the coverslips were washed 4 times with PBST (3 min each) and subsequently, the nuclei of the cells on the coverslips were counterstained with DAPI (C1002, Beyotime) at room temperature, dark for 5 min, and then were washed 4 more times with PBST (5 min each) prior to mounting on a glass slide with an anti-fade mounting medium (0100-01, SouthernBiotech). Fluorescence images were taken with a light microscope (LV150, Nikon, Japan) fitted with appropriate fluorescence filters at 200× magnification. The mean fluorescence intensity of α-SMA (green) was quantified by Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software from at least 5 randomly chosen fields of view per coverslip. The mean optical density (IOD/area) was calculated as the relative α-SMA protein expression level.

Statistical analysis

For all in vitro experiments, data were obtained from three independent biological replicates per condition and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Between-group differences among the control, L-NAME, and ox-LDL groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant and is indicated in the figures as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results

Determination of non-cytotoxic working concentrations of L-NAME and ox-LDL

To determine whether the results from upcoming studies were a consequence of the specific biological effect of L-NAME and ox-LDL or an unrelated cytotoxic effect, a CCK-8 assay was conducted to assess MOVAS cell viability and relative toxicity of L-NAME (6.25 to 800 µmol/L) or ox-LDL (6.25 to 800 µg/mL) for 24 h.

As shown in Fig. 1A compared to MOVAS treated with L-NAME at 100 µmol/L or lower, there were no noticeable differences in cell viability between them and untreated controls (p > 0.05). At the first dose of L-NAME (200 µmol/L), the cytotoxic potential began to exhibit significant reduction in cell viability (p < 0.05), continuing at higher doses until cell viability reached approximately 50% at 800 µmol/L.

Figure 1B demonstrates that MOVAS cells exposed to ox-LDL at 100 µg/mL or less showed no difference in cell viability compared to untreated controls (p > 0.05). However, as with L-NAME, when MOVAS cells were treated with 200 µg/ml ox-LDL cells exhibited a statistically significant decrease in cell viability (p < 0.05) and continued to show increasing levels of cytotoxicity at greater concentrations.

Based on these data and the requirement for greater than 90% viability to minimize cytotoxic confounding, 50 µmol/L L-NAME and 100 µg/mL ox-LDL were selected as non-cytotoxic working concentrations for subsequent experiments. At these concentrations, changes in lipid accumulation, gene expression, or protein expression were interpreted as specific cellular responses rather than nonspecific consequences of overt cell death.

L-NAME induces intracellular lipid accumulation and foam cell formation in VSMCs

Foam cell formation was previously only ascribed to macrophages but has now been identified in Vascular Smooth Muscle Cells (VSMCs) as well. Foam cell formation in VSMCs is an early key event in atherogenesis. In order to demonstrate whether L-NAME causes foam cell formation in VSMCs, we performed Oil Red O staining in MOVAS cells after incubating with: (1) control medium, (2) 50 µmol/L L-NAME, or (3) 100 µg/mL oxidized-LDL (ox-LDL) as a positive control for 24 h.

Figure 2 shows representative images of the Oil Red O stained cells. The MOVAS cells that received the control medium had minimal to no Oil Red O staining, with only a few small, mild lipid droplets occasionally being visible in the cytoplasm and with no evidence of excessive lipid fill. The MOVAS cells appeared normal in regard to the cytoplasmic clarity, and they had a classic spindle-shape characteristic of VSMCs.

Conversely, treatment of MOVAS cells with 50 µmol/L L-NAME resulted in significant amounts of bright red, spherical lipid droplets in the cytoplasm of a large percentage of the cells. The morphological transformation that occurred confirmed that VSMCs have successfully transformed into foam cells. The extent of L-NAME-induced lipid accumulation within the cytoplasms of the MOVAS cells was higher than anticipated, with each cell containing many varying-sized droplets of lipid filling perinuclear and peripheral cytoplasm.

The effects observed with L-NAME were qualitatively similar to those observed with the traditional atherogenic agent ox-LDL at 100 µg/mL. The presence of numerous lipid droplets in ox-LDL-treated cells confirmed the responsiveness of the model to a positive foam cell-inducing stimulus. Overall, these results suggest that L-NAME can promote lipid accumulation in VSMCs under in vitro conditions independent of systemic hemodynamic factors; however, the present study does not establish whether this response is exclusively mediated by eNOS inhibition.

L-NAME induces contractile-to-synthetic phenotype marker changes in VSMCs

After determining that L-NAME promoted lipid accumulation, we investigated whether L-NAME altered VSMC phenotype-associated markers, a key molecular event in atherosclerosis. qRT-PCR was used to quantify contractile and synthetic phenotype marker mRNA levels, and immunofluorescence was used to assess α-SMA protein expression.

Figure 3 shows the qRT-PCR data. Compared with the untreated control group, L-NAME treatment (50 µmol/L) significantly down-regulated the mRNA expression of contractile phenotype markers. ACTA2 mRNA expression decreased to approximately 40% of control levels (p < 0.05). MYH11, another essential component of the contractile apparatus, was also significantly reduced after L-NAME treatment (p < 0.05). These data indicate that L-NAME treatment is associated with loss of contractile marker expression in MOVAS cells.

Along with reduced contractile marker expression, the synthetic phenotype marker SPP1 (secreted phosphoprotein 1) was significantly increased. SPP1 mRNA expression increased approximately 3.5-fold compared with the control group (p < 0.05), suggesting a shift toward a synthetic phenotype marker profile after L-NAME treatment. The positive control ox-LDL induced similar directional changes, including down-regulation of ACTA2 and MYH11 and up-regulation of SPP1 (all p < 0.05).

Immunofluorescence analysis further supported the reduction in α-SMA expression at the protein level. As shown in Fig. 4A, α-SMA-expressing control cells exhibited organized, filamentous, and strong α-SMA staining (green), consistent with a contractile phenotype. In contrast, cells treated with L-NAME exhibited markedly decreased α-SMA fluorescence intensity, with a fragmented and disorganized residual signal. Quantitative analysis of mean optical density (IOD/area; Fig. 4B) showed significantly lower α-SMA protein expression in the L-NAME group than in controls (p < 0.001). ox-LDL treatment also produced a similar reduction in α-SMA expression (p < 0.001). These findings provide mRNA- and protein-level evidence that L-NAME shifts MOVAS cells away from a contractile marker profile toward a synthetic phenotype-associated marker profile.

L-NAME up-regulates matrix metalloproteinase expression in VSMCs

Synthetic VSMCs are typically associated with enhanced extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling capacity. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are important mediators of ECM remodeling because they degrade basement membrane and interstitial matrix components, thereby contributing to plaque progression and destabilization. To evaluate whether L-NAME was associated with a matrix-remodeling gene profile, we quantified MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression using qRT-PCR. As shown in Fig. 3, treatment with 50 µmol/L L-NAME for 24 h significantly increased MMP-2 and MMP-9 mRNA expression compared with controls (p < 0.05). MMP-9 expression increased approximately four-fold from baseline levels. These results suggest that L-NAME not only alters VSMC phenotype markers but also promotes a matrix-remodeling gene-expression profile.

The positive control ox-LDL also significantly up-regulated MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression (p < 0.05), consistent with previous findings that ox-LDL promotes ECM degradation during atherosclerosis progression. Therefore, these results suggest that L-NAME increases the matrix-remodeling potential of VSMCs at the transcript level. Functional confirmation of ECM degradation, migration, or proliferation was not performed in the present study and should be addressed in future work.

Discussion

The transition of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) from a quiescent contractile state to a synthetic, migratory, proliferative, and matrix-remodeling state is a well-recognized event in atherosclerosis progression [8, 9]. Hypertension is a major clinical risk factor for CAD, but the specific signals in the hypertensive vascular environment that directly affect VSMCs remain incompletely defined. In the present in vitro study, we show that L-NAME, a NOS inhibitor commonly used in hypertension models, can directly induce pro-atherogenic phenotypic changes in MOVAS cells at non-cytotoxic concentrations. These changes include intracellular lipid accumulation, reduced expression of contractile markers (ACTA2, MYH11, and α-SMA), increased expression of the synthetic phenotype marker SPP1, and up-regulation of matrix-remodeling genes MMP-2 and MMP-9. Because this study did not measure NOS isoform expression, eNOS activity, or intracellular NO production, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of L-NAME-associated VSMC dysfunction rather than definitive proof that eNOS inhibition alone mediates these effects.

Nitric oxide (NO) is a gaseous signaling molecule that contributes to vascular homeostasis. In endothelial cells, NO promotes vasodilation and has anti-atherosclerotic effects by inhibiting platelet aggregation, leukocyte adhesion, and VSMC growth and migration. Some VSMCs may also express NOS isoforms under specific conditions, although NOS expression and activity can vary by cell type, species, passage, and culture conditions. Because MOVAS cells are derived from VSMCs and do not contain endothelial cells, the direct cellular response observed here cannot be attributed to endothelial dysfunction or hypertension-mediated hemodynamic stress. Instead, our results suggest that L-NAME can act directly on VSMCs, potentially through disruption of endogenous NOS/NO signaling or through effects on other NOS isoforms or downstream oxidative/inflammatory pathways. Direct measurement of eNOS/NOS expression and intracellular NO levels will be necessary to clarify this mechanism.

The molecular characterization of L-NAME-associated phenotype marker changes is informative but should be interpreted cautiously. Down-regulation of contractile apparatus genes, including ACTA2 and MYH11, together with reduced α-SMA protein expression by immunofluorescence, indicates loss of contractile marker expression. Conversely, up-regulation of SPP1, a matricellular protein associated with migration, proliferation, and inflammatory signaling, supports acquisition of a synthetic phenotype-associated molecular profile [10]. However, because scratch migration, transwell migration, proliferation assays, and rescue experiments were not performed, the present data establish marker-based phenotypic modulation rather than full functional confirmation of a synthetic phenotype. The pathways underlying these changes may involve reduced NO bioavailability, oxidative stress, MAPK signaling, RhoA/ROCK signaling, NF-κB activation, Ang-II/AT1R signaling, or other downstream mechanisms, but these pathways require direct experimental validation.

The functional implication of this marker shift is supported by up-regulation of MMP-2 and MMP-9, two zinc-dependent endopeptidases involved in ECM remodeling. These enzymes can degrade type IV collagen and gelatin, thereby facilitating VSMC migration and contributing to fibrous cap remodeling in atherosclerotic lesions [19, 20]. In the present study, L-NAME increased MMP-2 and MMP-9 mRNA expression; however, MMP enzymatic activity and ECM degradation were not directly measured. Therefore, these findings indicate acquisition of a matrix-remodeling transcriptomic profile, while functional ECM degradation remains to be confirmed by future gelatin zymography, migration, or matrix-degradation assays.

Using ox-LDL as a positive control was important because ox-LDL is a well-established inducer of VSMC dysfunction through mechanisms that include intracellular ROS generation, NF-κB activation, inflammatory cytokine expression, lipid accumulation, and phenotype modulation [21]. The similar direction of responses induced by L-NAME and ox-LDL across lipid accumulation, phenotype markers, and MMP expression suggests that distinct vascular risk stimuli may converge on overlapping pro-atherogenic VSMC programs. Nevertheless, the present study did not directly test ROS, Ang-II, AT1R, NF-κB, MAPK, or RhoA/ROCK signaling. Future studies should therefore include intracellular NO measurements, NOS/eNOS expression and activity assays, ROS quantification, rescue experiments using NO donors or ROS scavengers, and pharmacological blockade with AT1R inhibitors such as losartan to define the causal pathway.

Limitations of the study

While this study produced novel phenotypic findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study used an in vitro clonal murine VSMC line (MOVAS), which is a useful vascular biology model but does not fully recapitulate the heterogeneity of primary mouse or human VSMCs. Validation in primary mouse aortic VSMCs and human VSMCs is therefore required to strengthen translational relevance. Second, although L-NAME is commonly used to inhibit NOS and model NO deficiency, we did not directly measure eNOS expression, total NOS expression, NOS enzymatic activity, or intracellular NO production in MOVAS cells. Therefore, the data cannot establish that the observed effects were exclusively mediated through eNOS inhibition. Third, the study did not assess ROS generation, Ang-II production, AT1R activation, NF-κB, MAPK, or RhoA/ROCK signaling, nor did it include rescue experiments with NO donors, ROS scavengers, or AT1R inhibitors such as losartan. Fourth, only one time point (24 h) was used for the mechanistic assays; time-course studies are needed to determine whether lipid accumulation precedes or follows phenotype marker changes. Fifth, although MMP-2 and MMP-9 expression was measured as a marker of ECM remodeling potential, we did not perform functional proliferation, migration, transwell, scratch-wound, gelatin zymography, or ECM-degradation assays. These assays will be essential to confirm functional synthetic phenotype behavior. Finally, additional qPCR analysis of proliferation-related genes may further strengthen the interpretation in future studies. Conclusion

The present in vitro investigation shows that L-NAME directly promotes lipid accumulation, contractile marker loss, synthetic phenotype marker induction, and increased matrix-remodeling gene expression in MOVAS cells under non-cytotoxic conditions. These data support the concept that NOS/NO pathway disruption may contribute to VSMC dysfunction independently of systemic hemodynamic effects. However, because eNOS/NOS expression, NO production, downstream signaling pathways, rescue experiments, primary VSMC validation, and functional migration/proliferation assays were not performed, the conclusions have been revised to avoid overinterpretation. Future studies should determine whether these responses are mediated by eNOS-dependent NO depletion, other NOS isoforms, oxidative stress, Ang-II/AT1R signaling, NF-κB/MAPK/RhoA-ROCK pathways, or convergent mechanisms. These findings provide a basis for mechanistic and translational studies aimed at defining how hypertension-associated NO pathway disruption may accelerate VSMC involvement in atherosclerosis.
