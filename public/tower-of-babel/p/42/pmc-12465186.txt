Distal tubercle osteotomy in medial opening-wedge high tibial osteotomy is superior in rotational alignment changes compared to proximal tubercle osteotomy: a propensity score-matched analysis
Authors: Hong Yeol Yang, Youzhen Zheng, Sung Ju Kang, Gyung Hwan Bae, Jong Keun Seon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRotational alignment changes following high tibial osteotomy (HTO) can alter patellofemoral joint biomechanics, potentially leading to adverse clinical outcomes. This study aimed to compare rotational alignment changes and clinical outcomes between two different types of biplanar medial opening-wedge HTO: proximal tubercle osteotomy (PT-HTO) and distal tubercle osteotomy (DT-HTO).MethodsA total of 178 patients who underwent medial opening-wedge HTO for medial compartment osteoarthritis between January 2020 and March 2023, with a minimum follow-up of two years, were retrospectively identified. Patients were stratified into two groups based on the direction of the second-plane osteotomy: PT-HTO (n = 136) and DT-HTO (n = 42). Propensity score matching was performed based on age, sex, body mass index, preoperative Kellgren–Lawrence grade, preoperative mechanical hip-knee-ankle angle, and correction angle, yielding 42 matched patients in each group. Radiological assessments were conducted using preoperative and postoperative lower extremity computed tomography scans. The primary outcome measure was the change in rotational alignment (tibial torsion angle, knee rotation angle, and knee-ankle rotation angle). Secondary outcomes included the tibial tuberosity–trochlear groove (TT–TG) distance and clinical outcomes assessed using the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) and Kujala score.ResultsThe mean tibial torsion angle significantly decreased in both groups compared to preoperative values, with reductions of − 3.8° in the PT-HTO group and − 1.4° in the DT-HTO group, with a marginally significant difference between the groups (p = 0.062). The knee rotation angle exhibited a greater reduction following PT-HTO than DT-HTO (− 2.4° vs. −1.4°; p = 0.046), indicating increased external rotation of the proximal fragment after PT-HTO. The TT–TG distance increased in the PT-HTO group but was preserved in the DT-HTO group, with a significant difference in mean change between the groups (2.1 mm vs. −0.2 mm; p < 0.001). Patellar height significantly decreased in the PT-HTO group, as reflected by reductions in the Blackburne–Peel ratio (− 0.14; p = 0.001) and Caton-Deschamps index (− 0.16; p < 0.001), whereas the DT-HTO group maintained preoperative patellar height. At a mean follow-up of 31.2 months, the DT-HTO group showed significantly better postoperative clinical outcomes, with higher KOOS pain scores (82.6 vs. 74.0; p = 0.030) and Kujala scores (72.3 vs. 65.7; p = 0.028) compared to the PT-HTO group.ConclusionsDT-HTO resulted in reduced rotational alignment changes and superior patellofemoral joint alignment compared to PT-HTO, leading to improved clinical outcomes.Level of evidenceRetrospective comparative study; Level III.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06256-3.

Introduction

High tibial osteotomy (HTO) is an established treatment modality for medial compartment osteoarthritis of the knee associated with varus deformity [1]. The primary objective of HTO is to correct coronal malalignment, thereby redistributing weight-bearing forces from the degenerated medial compartment to the preserved lateral compartment [2–4].

Accurate correction of coronal alignment is essential for optimizing clinical outcomes and enhancing long-term survival rates [5, 6]. However, given the three-dimensional (3D) triangular configuration of the tibial anatomy, realignment occurs across multiple planes, potentially leading to unintended alterations in the sagittal and axial planes. Notably, several studies have documented unintended sagittal plane changes, particularly an increase in the posterior tibial slope (PTS), following conventional medial opening-wedge HTO [7–9]. Furthermore, conventional opening-wedge HTO has been associated with adverse effects on the patellofemoral joint, including increased patellofemoral contact pressure, patellar maltracking, and potential progression of patellofemoral osteoarthritis [10–13].

To mitigate these concerns, distal tubercle osteotomy in medial opening-wedge HTO (DT-HTO) has emerged as a promising alternative technique designed to prevent distalization of the tibial tuberosity [14–21]. DT-HTO, characterized by a descending frontal osteotomy posterior to the tibial tubercle, better preserves the native patellofemoral alignment compared to conventional approaches. Specifically, DT-HTO minimizes alterations in the Q-angle, patellar axial alignment, and patellar height relative to proximal tubercle osteotomy in medial opening-wedge HTO (PT-HTO), which employs an ascending osteotomy posterior to the tibial tubercle. Additionally, biomechanical investigations have shown that DT-HTO does not elevate patellofemoral joint contact pressure, in contrast to PT-HTO [11, 22]. Horikawa et al. [14] reported that DT-HTO preserves patellar height and prevents progression of patellofemoral cartilage degeneration, in contrast to PT-HTO. Similarly, a recent meta-analysis by Song et al. [18] showed that PT-HTO significantly reduced patella height indices, whereas DT-HTO preserved patellar height, despite a slightly higher rate of minor complications. Rotational tibial alignment plays a critical role in patellofemoral joint kinematics, while rotational changes during HTO may inadvertently increase medial compartment joint contact pressure, compromising the intended offloading effect [23]. Additionally, tibial tubercle displacement due to tibial rotation may affect patellar tracking and patellofemoral joint contact pressures, potentially exacerbating patellofemoral joint pathology and anterior knee pain [10, 12, 24]. Therefore, understanding and minimizing unintended rotational changes during HTO is critical to preserving patellofemoral joint kinematics. Kim et al. [24] reported that opening-wedge HTO increased tibial tuberosity–trochlear groove (TT–TG) distance through external rotation of the proximal tibia, whereas closing-wedge HTO decreased TT–TG distance, with corresponding differences in anterior knee pain. To date, few studies have specifically compared rotational tibial alignment changes following PT-HTO versus DT-HTO.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to (1) evaluate changes in rotational alignment of the tibia (tibial torsion angle, knee rotation angle, and knee-ankle rotation angle) and TT-TG distance following PT-HTO versus DT-HTO and (2) compare the clinical outcomes between these two techniques. We hypothesized that DT-HTO would demonstrate superior radiological outcomes in rotational alignment, ultimately leading to improved clinical outcomes compared to PT-HTO.

Methods

Patients

This study was approved by the institutional review board of our institution. A total of 205 patients who underwent HTO, either PT-HTO or DT-HTO, for medial compartment osteoarthritis of the knee between January 2020 and March 2023 at our single institution were initially considered. The general indications for HTO included patients aged ≤ 65 years, isolated medial compartment osteoarthritis (Kellgren–Lawrence grade ≥ II) with varus malalignment, and the absence of ligament instability. The choice between PT-HTO and DT-HTO was determined by the evolution of the senior surgeon’s surgical technique preference. Initially, PT-HTO was predominantly performed until late 2021, after which the senior surgeon transitioned to primarily performing DT-HTO from early 2022 to early 2023, with a period of procedural overlap.

Patients included in the study underwent preoperative and postoperative lower extremity computed tomography (CT) scans and had a minimum follow-up duration of two years following HTO. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) knee range of motion < 120° or flexion contracture > 15°, (2) prior knee surgery, (3) presence of patellofemoral or lateral compartment osteoarthritis, (4) secondary osteoarthritis, (5) ligamentous insufficiency requiring reconstruction, (6) absence of preoperative or postoperative CT scans, and (7) follow-up duration of < 2 years.

After applying these criteria, 178 patients were deemed eligible for final inclusion, comprising 136 patients who underwent PT-HTO and 42 who underwent DT-HTO. To mitigate potential selection bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was employed, matching each patient in the DT-HTO group with a corresponding PT-HTO patient. Propensity scores were calculated using logistic regression analysis, incorporating baseline variables such as age, sex, body mass index (BMI), preoperative Kellgren–Lawrence grade, preoperative mechanical hip-knee-ankle (HKA) angle, and correction angle. One-to-one matching was conducted using a variable-ratio, parallel, balanced nearest-neighbor method with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the propensity score to constrain the allowable distance between matched pairs. Covariate balance between groups was assessed by calculating standardized mean differences (SMDs).

Ultimately, the final study cohort comprised 84 patients, with 42 in the PT-HTO group and 42 in the DT-HTO group. The mean follow-up duration was 31.2 months (range, 24.1–50.0 months) (Fig. 1).

Surgical techniques

The correction angle was determined preoperatively following the method described by Miniaci et al. [25]. A single experienced orthopedic surgeon performed all HTO procedures, with routine arthroscopic evaluation conducted at the time of surgery.

Conventional PT-HTO

An anteromedial longitudinal incision was made from the medial joint line level to 3 cm below the tibial tubercle and overlying the pes anserinus tendons. Two guide wires were inserted 35 mm distal to the joint line, originating at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction and directed toward the fibular tip under fluoroscopic guidance. The initial horizontal osteotomy was performed along the guide wires while preserving the lateral cortex, followed by a second ascending osteotomy of the tibial tubercle, performed nearly parallel to the anterior tibial cortex. These two osteotomy planes formed an angle of approximately 100° to 110°. The osteotomy was extended superior to the tibial tubercle, which remained attached to the distal osteotomy fragment. The osteotomy gap was gradually opened to the predetermined wedge length, with fluoroscopic confirmation of the target mechanical axis. Fixation was achieved using a locking plate and screws.

DT-HTO

The horizontal osteotomy in DT-HTO was performed identically to the PT-HTO procedure. A separate descending osteotomy of the tibial tubercle was created distally in the frontal plane, with a thickness of 10 mm at an angle of approximately 100° to 110° relative to the horizontal osteotomy plane, following a modified technique described by Gaasbeek et al. [26]. The distal osteotomized tibial tubercle measured approximately 4 cm in length, with its proximal portion remaining attached to the proximal osteotomy fragment. The distal segment of the tibial tubercle was secured to the distal tibial fragment using a 4.5-mm partially threaded cannulated screw penetrating the posterior tibial cortex. Finally, fixation was completed with a locking plate and screws while maintaining the osteotomy gap (Fig. 2).

CT assessment for rotational alignment changes

All patients in both study groups underwent lower extremity CT scans preoperatively and at the one-year follow-up using a helical CT scanner (GE Healthcare). CT imaging was performed from above the knee joint to below the ankle joint, with a slice thickness of 1 mm to assess changes in rotational alignment. Patients were positioned supine with their lower limbs extended and maintained in a neutral rotation position, immobilized using appropriate strapping to prevent movement during image acquisition. Measurements were performed using a picture archiving and communication system (PACS) with an image analysis program (INFINITT).

Rotational alignment was evaluated using CT images at three anatomical levels. The first reference slice was obtained at the proximal tibia, superior to the osteotomy site, passing through the center of the proximal tibial epiphysis above the fibular head. The second slice was taken at the distal tibia, tangential to the superior aspect of the talar dome. The third slice was positioned at the distal femur, above the knee joint line, traversing the most prominent aspects of the lateral and medial epicondyles. Based on these landmarks, rotational alignment changes at the osteotomy site and tibiofemoral joint were measured. The tibial torsion angle was defined as the angle between a line tangent to the posterior margin of the proximal tibial plateau and the transmalleolar axis, determined using the centers of the anteroposterior diameters of the lateral and medial malleoli. The knee rotation angle was measured as the angle between the transepicondylar axis of the distal femur and the posterior margin of the posterior tibial plateau. Finally, the knee–ankle rotation angle was defined as the angle between the transepicondylar and transmalleolar axes (Fig. 3).

The TT–TG distance was measured using two superimposed CT slices: one at the deepest point of the femoral trochlear groove and the other at the most anterior prominence of the tibial tubercle [27, 28]. A perpendicular reference line was drawn from the deepest point of the trochlear groove relative to the posterior condylar tangent, and a second parallel line was drawn through the most anterior portion of the tibial tubercle. The distance between the two lines was defined as the TT–TG distance (Fig. 4).

Radiographic evaluation and arthroscopic assessment

Standardized anteroposterior, lateral, and Merchant views at 45° of knee flexion, as well as standing whole lower extremity weight-bearing radiographs, were obtained preoperatively and at each postoperative follow-up. Radiographic evaluation included measurements of the mechanical HKA angle, PTS, medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA), Blackburne–Peel (BP) ratio [29], Caton-Deschamps (CD) index [30], and lateral patellar tilt angle.

The status of the articular cartilage in the femoral trochlear groove was assessed via index arthroscopy using the International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) grading system [31]. In this system, superficial lesions, fissures, and cracks are classified as grade 1 defects, while lesions extending to less than 50% of the cartilage depth are categorized as grade 2. Grade 3 defects are characterized by lesions extending beyond 50% of the cartilage depth without subchondral bone involvement, whereas defects affecting the subchondral bone are classified as grade 4. All radiographic, CT, and arthroscopic evaluations were independently performed by two observers blinded to both the surgical procedures and the study objectives.

Clinical evaluation

Clinical assessments were conducted preoperatively and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, with annual follow-ups thereafter. Outcome measures included the KOOS [32], assessed both preoperatively and postoperatively, and the postoperative Kujala patellofemoral score [33]. All clinical evaluations were independently performed by two observers who were not involved in the surgical procedures and were blinded to the study’s purpose.

Statistical analysis

For normally distributed variables, paired and independent t-tests were used for statistical analysis. Non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. All measurements obtained by two authors (J.S.K and G.H.B) were analyzed using intraclass correlation coefficients to assess intra- and inter-observer reliability. The intraclass and interclass correlation coefficients ranged from 0.85 to 0.93 and 0.87–0.94, respectively, indicating excellent agreement. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Among the matching variables, no statistically significant differences were observed between groups except for sex and BMI (p = 0.025 and 0.038, respectively; Table 1). All selected variables demonstrated no significant differences post-matching, including age and sex (p = 0.655 and 0.100, respectively; Table 1). The mean follow-up duration was 32.2 ± 5.2 months in the PT-HTO group and 30.3 ± 3.9 months in the DT-HTO group, with no statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.100).

Rotational alignment changes between PT-HTO and DT-HTO groups

A comparison of rotational alignment changes, as measured on CT, is presented in Table 2. The tibial torsion angle significantly decreased from preoperative values in both the PT-HTO (− 3.8°) and DT-HTO (− 1.4°) groups, with a marginally significant difference between the groups (p = 0.062). The knee rotation angle demonstrated a significantly greater decrease following PT-HTO (− 2.4°) compared to DT-HTO (− 1.4°) relative to preoperative values, with a significant difference between the groups (p = 0.046). The TT-TG distance increased in the PT-HTO group (2.1 mm) but was preserved in the DT-HTO group (− 0.2 mm), resulting in a significant difference between the groups in mean change values (p < 0.001).

Radiographic outcomes and arthroscopic findings

No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of mechanical HKA angle, MPTA, PTS, or lateral patellar tilt angle (all p > 0.05) (Table 3). However, patellar height significantly decreased following PT-HTO compared to preoperative levels, as indicated by the mean changes in both the BP ratio (− 0.14; p = 0.001) and CD index (− 0.16; p < 0.001). In contrast, patellar height was preserved in the DT-HTO group. Furthermore, no significant between-group differences were identified in the arthroscopic evaluation of cartilage status in the femoral trochlear groove (p = 0.598).

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes for both groups are summarized in Table 4. Both PT-HTO and DT-HTO groups exhibited significant improvements from preoperative evaluation to the final follow-up across all clinical assessment parameters (p < 0.05). However, at the final follow-up, the DT-HTO group demonstrated significantly superior postoperative outcomes in KOOS pain (p = 0.030) and the Kujala scores (p = 0.028) compared to the PT-HTO group. Discussion

The primary finding of this study was that DT-HTO resulted in smaller rotational alignment changes in the tibia compared to PT-HTO and yielded more favorable outcomes in patellofemoral joint alignment on the axial plane, including knee rotation angle and TT-TG distance, relative to PT-HTO. Furthermore, our results indicate that PT-HTO preserved patellar height, as assessed by the BP ratio and CD index, whereas PT-HTO was associated with a significant reduction in patellar height. These radiologic improvements translated into superior clinical outcomes in the DT-HTO group, particularly in terms of KOOS pain and the Kujala scores.

The clinical benefits of HTO in significantly improving patient-reported outcomes, with satisfactory survival rates, have been well established [34–37]. Despite its favorable outcomes, several studies have reported a deterioration in results over time, raising concerns regarding the potential association between rotational alignment changes and worse postoperative outcomes following HTO [5, 6, 24, 34, 38, 39]. Given the 3D triangular structure of the tibia, HTO may induce changes not only in the coronal and sagittal planes but also in the axial plane. However, previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding rotational changes following HTO. Baumgarten et al. [40] compared tibial rotation changes based on valgus correction angles between the coronal plane and opening-wedge HTO techniques, demonstrating that greater valgus correction induced minor internal rotational changes (1.2°–4.5°) with the coronal plane technique but substantial external tibial rotation (16.0°–23.0°) with opening-wedge HTO. Similarly, Kendoff et al. [41] reported external rotation (2.7° ± 6.3°) following opening-wedge HTO, whereas Jacobi et al. [42] observed slight internal rotation (1.5° ± 2.9°) of the distal tibia after opening-wedge HTO. These discrepancies may be attributable to methodological limitations, particularly the reliance on tibial torsion angle measurements, which assess the relative angle between proximal and distal tibial fragments without evaluating overall tibial rotational alignment. Accordingly, additional assessment of knee rotation angle and TT-TG distance is essential, as these parameters provide critical insights into rotational alignment changes following HTO.

In the present study, the knee rotation angle decreased significantly more following PT-HTO than DT-HTO, indicating greater external rotation of the proximal fragment in PT-HTO. Previous studies have shown that excessive external tibial rotation negatively impacts gait kinematics and increases patellofemoral joint loading [43, 44]. This external rotation displaces the tibial tubercle laterally, thereby increasing TT–TG distance. Accordingly, the TT–TG distance increased significantly in the PT-HTO group but was preserved in the DT-HTO group. During PT-HTO, the tibial tubercle in the distal osteotomized fragment shifts laterally and distally, resulting in patellar descent and valgization of the tibial tubercle axis. Conversely, in DT-HTO, the tibial tubercle in the proximal osteotomized fragment maintains its position, resulting in relative varization of the tibial tubercle axis when accounting for the valgization of the mechanical axis following medial opening-wedge HTO. Lateralization of the tibial tubercle can lead to increased contact pressure between the lateral patellar facet and the lateral trochlear wall, whereas medialization may reduce this pressure [45, 46]. Some studies have reported that patients with patellofemoral pain syndrome exhibit a TT-TG distance that is 2–3 mm greater than that of asymptomatic controls, underscoring the clinical importance of minimizing TT-TG alterations [46, 47]. Based on these findings, DT-HTO may serve as an alternative technique aimed at minimizing patellofemoral joint alignment disruptions.

In this study, PT-HTO resulted in a significant reduction in patellar height, whereas patellar height was preserved following DT-HTO, consistent with prior research [16, 17, 26, 48].

The DT-HTO technique maintains the continuity of the anterior tibial cortex and the attachment of the tibial tuberosity to the proximal tibia, preventing distal migration of the tubercle and theoretically preserving patellar height postoperatively. Our findings confirm that patellar height remained unchanged after DT-HTO, consistent with previous studies [16, 26, 49–52]. Furthermore, biomechanical studies have demonstrated that, unlike PT-HTO, DT-HTO does not significantly alter patellofemoral cartilage pressure [11, 22]. A decrease in patellar height after HTO has important clinical implications, as it may contribute to patellofemoral symptoms, inferior postoperative outcomes, and technical challenges during subsequent total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [15, 53–56]. These findings suggest that DT-HTO may reduce the risk of patellofemoral joint cartilage degeneration and potentially improve clinical outcomes compared to PT-HTO. Clinical manifestations of patella infera and increased patellofemoral contact pressure include anterior knee pain and limited range of motion [14]. The preservation of patellar height following DT-HTO aligns with Horikawa et al. [14], who reported a protective effect of DT-HTO against the progression of patellofemoral osteoarthritis compared to PT-HTO. Although our study demonstrates these advantages at mid-term follow-up, further long-term studies are necessary to determine whether these benefits persist and translate into reduced patellofemoral osteoarthritis risk.

Clinically, patients in the DT-HTO group demonstrated significantly superior outcomes in KOOS pain and the Kujala scores compared to those in the PT-HTO group at a mean follow-up of 31.2 months. These findings suggest that the reduced alterations in rotational alignment and the preserved tibial tuberosity position following DT-HTO may confer clinical advantages. While anterior knee pain after HTO is multifactorial, changes in tibial rotation and tibial tubercle position are likely important contributing factors. Horikawa et al. reported that decreased patellar height after conventional OW-HTO significantly increases patellofemoral cartilage contact pressure, whereas DT-HTO preserves normal biomechanics by maintaining patellar position within the femoral trochlear groove [14]. In their biomechanical cadaveric study, Kloos et al. also demonstrated that DT-HTO markedly reduces patellofemoral pressure increases, depending on the correction angle [22]. This preservation of patellofemoral alignment likely underlies the greater improvements in pain and patellofemoral-specific scores.

This study has several limitations. First, as with any retrospective study, patient selection was not randomized. Second, the follow-up period was relatively short, meaning that a prospective study with long-term follow-up is needed to validate these findings. Third, measurements were performed using two-dimensional radiographic and CT images, which may be affected by limb positioning and scan direction. Fourth, preoperative Kujala scores were not assessed, limiting evaluation of changes in patellofemoral-specific symptoms over time. Fifth, although the influence of the surgical learning curve was minimized by excluding the first 15 DT-HTO cases, residual effects from surgical evolution cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, although we performed subgroup analysis based on excessive TT–TG distance and patella alta to assess clinical relevance, the small number of patients meeting these criteria was insufficient to demonstrate meaningful between-group differences. Despite these limitations, the present study provides a meaningful evaluation of the impact of rotational alignment changes on clinical outcomes following HTO.

Conclusions

DT-HTO resulted in reduced rotational alignment changes and superior patellofemoral joint alignment compared to PT-HTO, potentially contributing to improved mid-term clinical outcomes. Given its preservation of patellar height and TT–TG distance, DT-HTO may be particularly beneficial for patients at risk of patellofemoral joint problems. A comprehensive assessment of rotational alignment changes is recommended following HTO, and orthopedic surgeons should carefully evaluate these parameters to optimize postoperative outcomes for patients with medial compartment knee osteoarthritis. DT-HTO appears to be the preferred technique for minimizing unintended rotational changes and enhancing postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing HTO for medial compartment knee osteoarthritis. However, further long-term follow-up is needed to determine whether these benefits are sustained over time.
