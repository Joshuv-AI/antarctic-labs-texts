MRI-based 3D cartilage modelling in the elbow joint
Authors: Tasuku Miyake, Satoshi Miyamura, Ryoya Shiode, Toru Iwahashi, Arisa Kazui, Natsuki Yamamoto, Hiroki Kondo, Hiroyuki Tanaka, Seiji Okada, Tsuyoshi Murase, Kunihiro Oka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsTo construct and validate a 3D MRI-based cartilage model of the elbow joint by comparing it with laser-scanned models and to assess how cartilage morphology influences intra-articular stress distribution through finite element analysis (FEA).MethodsSeven formalin-fixed cadaveric elbows were scanned using high-resolution MRI (voxel size: 0.375 × 0.375 × 0.400 mm), with sequences selected based on the contrast-to-noise ratio. Cartilage models of the distal humerus, radial head, and proximal ulna were reconstructed and validated against laser-scanned reference models. Validation metrics included cartilage thickness differences, surface-to-surface distances, and Dice similarity coefficients. Subsequently, FEA was performed using models with and without cartilage, under physiological loading, to evaluate the intra-articular stress distribution.ResultsMRI-based and laser-scanned models showed similar cartilage thickness distributions, with differences within or close to the imaging resolution. The median cartilage thicknesses measured using the MRI-based and laser-scanned models were 1.15 mm (IQR 0.97 to 1.54) and 1.25 mm (IQR 1.14 to 1.29) in the distal humerus, 1.15 mm (IQR 0.97 to 1.18) and 1.03 mm (IQR 1.01 to 1.14) in the radial head, and 1.06 mm (IQR 0.93 to 1.21) and 1.05 mm (IQR 1.00 to 1.10) in the proximal ulna, respectively. The surface-to-surface root mean square distances were < 0.40 mm, and the Dice coefficients exceeded 0.96. FEA revealed physiologically plausible stress concentration patterns at anatomical contact areas only in models incorporating the cartilage.ConclusionThe 3D MRI-based cartilage models have high geometrical accuracy and biomechanical validity. These models offer a noninvasive tool for assessing cartilage morphology and joint mechanics, and may provide a foundation for future studies investigating the pathophysiology of elbow osteoarthritis.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(7):811–821.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a common degenerative joint disease affecting approximately 25% of the Japanese population,1 with the elbow joint being an important site in the upper limb. Elbow OA causes pain, stiffness, functional limitation, and reduced quality of life, impairing daily activities and upper limb independence.2 However, its pathophysiology remains inadequately understood.

A key limitation is the anatomical and biomechanical complexity of the elbow joint. The articular surfaces provide smooth, low-friction gliding interfaces and play a critical role in shock absorption3 and load distribution across the joint.4 Moreover, multiple articulations and thin, highly congruent cartilage surfaces complicate evaluation. Conventional imaging methods, including 2D radiography and CT, provide limited information about the cartilage. Although MRI is widely recognized as a noninvasive tool for cartilage assessment,5 its utility remains constrained. Conventional MRI relies on slice-based interpretation, which is insufficient to capture 3D cartilage geometry.

Recent studies have shown that elbow cartilage thickness is not uniform and varies regionally.5-8 This spatial variability highlights the limitations of single-plane 2D imaging, which lacks the dimensionality for comprehensive morphological assessment, underscoring the clinical potential of 3D cartilage modelling for anatomically accurate and quantitative assessment. Nevertheless, no study has validated MRI-based 3D cartilage models of the elbow joint.

This study aimed to validate the anatomical accuracy of MRI cartilage models, focusing on key morphological parameters such as cartilage thickness and surface representation. We constructed 3D cartilage models of the elbow joint from clinically feasible MRI scans and validated their accuracy against high-precision 3D models generated from laser scanning using cadaveric specimens. Furthermore, we investigated the applicability of MRI-based cartilage models in biomechanical analysis by incorporating them into finite element analysis (FEA) to evaluate intra-articular stress distribution in vivo using material properties of healthy cartilage.

Methods

Study design

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The University of Osaka and adhered to the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki9 and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from the volunteer, and cadaveric specimens were obtained through an accredited body donation programme with prior donor consent.

Figure 1 illustrates the study workflow.

Graphical representation of the study workflow. This schematic illustrates the overall research process. The workflow began with the determination of an optimal MRI sequence for visualizing the elbow cartilage based on contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) analysis across six candidate sequences. Once the most suitable sequence was selected, MRI data were acquired, and 3D MRI-based cartilage models were constructed. These models were then validated against high-resolution laser-scanned models created from cadaveric specimens. Quantitative validation included comparisons of the median cartilage thickness, surface-to-surface distance, and Dice similarity coefficient. Finally, finite element analysis (FEA) using the validated MRI-based cartilage model was performed to evaluate the joint stress distribution and examine the mechanical implications of cartilage morphology.

MRI acquisition and 3D MRI-based modelling

MRI was performed for ten formalin-embalmed cadaveric elbows using an identified sequence in an oblique orientation. All specimens were obtained from consenting donors following dissection of the upper limb at the shoulder joint. Formalin fixation alters cartilage MRI signals by shortening relaxation times and reducing intensity compared with in vivo tissue.6,19 Although not quantitatively corrected, all specimens were embalmed using the same protocol to minimize variability. After MRI, two board-certified orthopaedic surgeons (TM, KO) independently dissected the elbows and performed direct macroscopic inspection of the articular cartilage surfaces. Specimens were excluded when macroscopically osteoarthritic changes, including osteophyte formation or cartilage surface damage, were identified. Exclusion was based solely on macroscopic findings and conducted blinded to the MRI results. Three elbows meeting these criteria were excluded, and the remaining seven elbows (three females, four males; mean age 84.1 years (76 to 99) at the time of death), which were confirmed to be macroscopically normal by both surgeons, were included for further analysis.

The MRI datasets were imported into a workstation using Mimics software, v. 25.0 (Materialise, Belgium), to obtain multiplanar reconstructed images, facilitating cartilage visualization. The humeral, ulnar, and radial bones and cartilages were segmented. First, an MRI signal intensity threshold was defined for each target tissue, including the cartilage and bone, and pixels corresponding to the designated tissue were selected accordingly. The initial threshold was determined through automated processing and subsequently refined manually on a slice-by-slice basis, with real-time visual verification.20 Cartilage boundaries were delineated following established segmentation methods,21 with detailed procedures for handling segmentation ambiguities and quality control described in Supplementary Material 3. This standardized approach enabled reliable and reproducible segmentation of the articular cartilage and adjacent bony structures across all cases, ensuring model generalizability. The extracted tissue regions were reconstructed into 3D models following the segmentation process. A windowed-sinc filter (five iterations) was applied to the reconstructed surfaces to enhance model fidelity, reducing the stair-step appearance resulting from the slice thickness (Figure 2).22 To assess in vivo feasibility, 3D FSPGR MRI was performed in healthy volunteers using the same sequence parameters (Supplementary Material 4).

3D laser-scanned modelling

Dissected cadaveric specimens were scanned using a 3D optical laser scanner (Rexcan CS + 5.0 mega pixels; Medit, South Korea) with an accuracy of ± 20 µm to obtain joint dimensions. The scans were processed using ezScan7 software (Medit). Each specimen was scanned once for model generation, and repeatability was assessed using a phantom-based test (Supplementary Material 5).

The individual elbow bones with articular cartilage were scanned, and 3D cartilage-bone models were created (Figure 3a). Subsequently, the cartilage dissolution technique was used to create bone models without the cartilage for baseline cartilage measurements.6,22,23 The joint surfaces were soaked in 6.0% hypochlorite for six to 24 hours to dissolve the articular cartilage.24 The cartilage was visually inspected, and its complete dissolution was confirmed by touching the surface with a surgical probe.23 Subsequently, the cartilage-free bones were rescanned using the same procedure, and 3D bone models were created without the cartilage (Figure 3b). Surface model datasets that were obtained with a 3D laser scanner were analyzed in Geomagic Control (3D Systems, USA) in the STL file format. Bone models were superimposed onto the cartilage-bone models using the iterative closest point (ICP) algorithm to evaluate cartilage thickness as a projected thickness map. Cartilage thickness was evaluated by measuring minimum distance between the cartilage-bone and bone models on the cartilage surface (Figure 3c).

Diagrams of 3D models. a) Cartilage-bone model. b) Bone model. c) Cartilage model with the cartilage thickness information coded as a thickness map.

Validation of the 3D MRI-based model accuracy

The accuracy of the 3D MRI-based models was validated against the 3D laser-scanned models, which served as the ground truth. Model similarity was evaluated using three approaches. First, cartilage thickness in the MRI-based model was compared with that in the laser-scanned model. The bone models without cartilage were superimposed using the ICP algorithm to ensure precise comparison of identical articular regions. The median thickness of the cartilage was measured within the joint and compared. Second, the surface-to-surface distance was measured by superimposing the MRI-based model onto the laser-scanned model. The mean root mean square distances and mean absolute distances were calculated for both cartilage and bone regions. For surface-to-surface distance measurements, MRI-based and laser-scanned models were registered using the ICP algorithm, and meshes were matched to allow point-to-point distance comparisons on corresponding articular surfaces. Third, Dice similarity coefficients were calculated for both the cartilage and bone regions to quantitatively assess spatial overlap between the MRI-based and laser-scanned models following superimposition using 3D Slicer (v. 5.0.2), an open-source software for medical image processing.25 The sametwo orthopaedic surgeons (TM and KO) assessed intra- and inter-rater reliability of segmentation. Intrarater reliability was evaluated using repeated measurements after two weeks or more with a two-way mixed-effects model (intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)(3,1)) with absolute agreement, and inter-rater reliability using a two-way random-effects model (ICC(2,1)) with absolute agreement.

Mechanical evaluation via FEA of the in vivo 3D MRI-based model

CT-based bone models were constructed to create a model without the native cartilage and a model with the cartilage incorporating the MRI-based cartilage model to assess the validity of FEA for in vivo mechanical evaluation of the elbow with MRI-based cartilage. Since FEA is conventionally conducted using CT-based bone models,26 this approach allowed us to establish a comparative framework for assessing the biomechanical relevance of MRI-based cartilage.

Geometrical nonlinear FEA was performed using Mechanical Finder software v. 12.0 (Research Centre for Computational Mechanics, Inc., Japan). Two types of finite element models were compared: a model without the native cartilage, in which simplified virtual soft-tissue layers created from CT data were incorporated as cartilage substitutes, and a model with the cartilage, in which the MRI-based cartilage model was integrated into the same CT-based bone models. A static load of 80 N was applied to the radius and ulna,26,27 with the proximal humerus fully restrained (Figure 4). The minimum principal stress was measured, with lower values indicating stronger compressive stress. The percentage of high-stress region was defined as the proportion of the articular surface with stress exceeding 90% of the peak stress. The stress dispersion index was calculated as the percentage of the articular surface experiencing more than 20% of the peak stress, representing stress dispersion. Detailed CT acquisition parameters, material properties, meshing strategies, contact definitions, and boundary conditions are provided in Supplementary Material 6.26-35

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA), with the significance set at p < 0.05. The normality of all the variables was tested using Shapiro–Wilk parametric tests. Cartilage thickness between MRI-based and laser-scanned models was compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Effect sizes were calculated as Cohen’s d. Agreement was assessed with Bland–Altman analysis, calculating mean bias, SD, and 95% limits of agreement, which were interpreted relative to the MRI voxel size (0.375 × 0.375 × 0.400 mm) to evaluate clinical acceptability. Detailed statistical procedures, including definitions of the minimally important difference, post-hoc power analysis, and ICC calculations, are provided in Supplementary Material 7.

Results

The MRI-based and laser-scanned cartilage models demonstrated high geometrical agreement and measurement reliability across all evaluated metrics.

Comparison of the median cartilage thickness between the MRI-based and laser-scanned models

The MRI-based and laser-scanned models demonstrated similar thickness distribution (Figure 5), with error histograms centred near zero (Supplementary Material 8). The median cartilage thicknesses measured using the MRI-based and laser-scanned models were 1.15 mm (0.97 to 1.54) and 1.25 mm (1.14 to 1.29) for the distal humerus, 1.15 mm (0.97 to 1.18) and 1.03 mm (1.01 to 1.14) for the radial head, and 1.06 mm (0.93 to 1.21) and 1.05 mm (1.00 to 1.10) for the proximal ulna, respectively. In the direct comparisons, the p-values for the distal humerus, radial head, and proximal ulna were 0.938, 0.688, and 0.938, respectively, indicating no difference between the MRI-based and laser-scanned measurements (Table III). Bland–Altman analysis showed minimal bias with limits of agreement within approximately one voxel across all bones (Figure 6; Supplementary Material 9). Sensitivity analysis excluding one outlier specimen in the distal humerus confirmed comparable agreement.

Surface-to-surface distance

The representative heatmaps, generated by superimposing the MRI-based models onto the laser-scanned model, demonstrated overall geometrical similarity between the two models (Figure 5). The surface-to-surface distance maps showed that positive and negative deviations were spatially dispersed across articular surfaces without consistent regional clustering. Quantitative surface-to-surface distance metrics supported this finding across all bones (Table III).

Dice coefficient

The median Dice coefficient for the cartilage and bone regions was 0.96 (0.96 to 0.97) for the distal humerus, 0.97 (0.96 to 0.97) for the radial head, and 0.96 (0.96 to 0.97) for the proximal ulna (Table IV).

Difference in stress distribution between the finite element model with and without the cartilage

Stress contours demonstrated that the model without native cartilage exhibited broader stress dispersion with smaller high-stress regions, while the cartilage surface in the model with cartilage showed a more localized stress concentration, corresponding to a more physiological loading pattern (Figure 7).

Quantitatively, the cartilage-containing model consistently exhibited a higher percentage of high-stress region and lower stress dispersion index across all articular surfaces, indicating more focal load transmission compared with the model without cartilage. Sensitivity analyses using alternative threshold values confirmed that these relative trends were preserved (Supplementary Material 10).

In vivo feasibility

In vivo MRI in healthy volunteers demonstrated clear visualization of elbow cartilage with distinct tissue-specific contrast, confirming the clinical feasibility of the optimized MRI protocol (Supplementary Materials 4 and 11).

Discussion

In this study, we constructed and validated a 3D MRI-based cartilage model of the elbow joint, demonstrating high geometrical accuracy and biomechanical relevance through excellent agreement across thickness, surface distance, and similarity metrics with minimal bias against laser-scanned reference. Sensitivity analysis excluding one outlier specimen in the distal humerus yielded similar mean bias and limits of agreement, indicating no material effect on overall agreement. FEA incorporating the MRI-based model showed that cartilage geometry governs stress concentration and dispersion and may help identify regions susceptible to degeneration.

An important aspect of this study lies in the use of clinically feasible MRI protocols to reconstruct anatomically accurate elbow cartilage despite its thin, highly congruent surfaces. To our knowledge, this is the first validation of MRI-based 3D elbow cartilage models against laser-scanned ground truth with subsequent biomechanical application.

Bland–Altman analysis indicated 95% limits of agreement within approximately one voxel (0.35 mm to 0.55 mm), indicating that differences exceeding this range would likely represent true morphological changes rather than measurement variability. The spatial distribution patterns of cartilage thickness were anatomically consistent with elbow-specific normative data. Thicker cartilage was observed in the intermediate region of the distal humerus, the articular rim region of the radial head, and the anterolateral-to-anterior regions of the proximal ulna, in line with findings from prior cadaveric and imaging-based studies of normal elbows.6 Although validation used elderly cadaveric specimens, supplementary in vivo MRI analyses in younger healthy volunteers confirmed clear visualization of elbow articular cartilage with adequate tissue-specific contrast under physiological conditions. Cartilage thickness showed modest age-related variation while preserving distributions consistent with normative elbow data across adulthood.

Clinically, the high-precision MRI-based 3D cartilage model of the elbow joint developed in this study enables quantitative 3D cartilage assessment, previously limited to radiography or slice-based MRI. In routine clinical practice, standard MRI relies on measurements from selected image slices and does not provide joint-wide cartilage thickness mapping; in contrast, the proposed approach enables joint-level 3D visualization of cartilage thickness distribution. This method is based on a single high-resolution 3D MRI sequence with retrospective multiplanar reconstruction, obviating the need for multiple 2D acquisitions or additional cartilage-specific sequences. Conventional elbow MRI protocols typically require multiplanar 2D acquisitions with a scan time of approximately 30 minutes, whereas the proposed approach enables a shorter and more efficient examination while maintaining high spatial resolution. The MRI cost in the Japanese healthcare system is approximately USD 170, which is comparable to that of routine clinical elbow MRI. Taken together, this method offers a favourable balance between cost, acquisition time, and image quality, supporting its feasibility for clinical and research applications. 3D visualization of cartilage morphology enables clinically meaningful spatial assessment for future applications. This approach may be useful for preoperative evaluation of osteochondritis dissecans of the elbow, where articular cartilage status has traditionally been assessed most definitively by arthroscopy, by enabling preoperative spatial characterization of cartilage damage and lesion extent and providing clinically relevant information beyond conventional MRI.

The physiological plausibility of the FEA results was supported by prior cadaveric contact mechanics studies reporting peak ulnohumeral pressures of 0.6 MPa to 1.4 MPa under 80 N to 140 N36 and radiocapitellar pressures of 2.3 MPa (SD 0.16) under 100 N.37 Although joint configurations and boundary conditions differ from those in our model, the peak pressures predicted under an 80 N load were numerically comparable to these experimental values, providing indirect validation of the physiological relevance of our loading and material assumptions.

In the model with cartilage, stress was localized in the contact areas between the capitellum and radial head and between the trochlea and proximal ulna – regions corresponding to the anatomical joint surface. This pattern was consistent with physiological load transmission and prior cadaveric contact mechanics findings.27 In contrast, the model without native cartilage exhibited a broader, non-physiological stress dispersion, underscoring the importance of anatomically accurate cartilage geometry for realistic joint mechanics. Notably, stress concentrations were observed on the posteromedial aspect of the radial head,38,39 the apex and medial groove of the capitellum,38,39 and the coronoid process of the ulna38,40 – areas frequently associated with degenerative changes in previous studies. However, an alternative interpretation should be considered. Regions showing higher stress may reflect physiologically load-bearing areas with relatively thicker cartilage rather than sites predisposed to degeneration, resulting in locally elevated stress. Because this study did not include longitudinal evaluation or osteoarthritic specimens, no causal relationship with OA progression can be inferred.

Some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, formalin fixation may cause subtle geometrical changes due to dehydration. Thus, the fixed cartilage morphology may not fully reflect its in vivo shape, and shrinkage cannot be separated from measurement error in our validation. Second, cartilage segmentation remains time-consuming due to the complex elbow anatomy and manual refinement, requiring approximately two hours per bone, limiting clinical practicality. It also requires detailed knowledge of elbow anatomy and orthopaedic imaging. Future automated segmentation approaches will be essential to improve efficiency and reproducibility. Third, cartilage was modelled as a nearly incompressible linear elastic material because the finite element software does not support viscoelastic formulations. Although this simplification is common in previous upper-limb FEA studies, the absence of time-dependent properties may influence absolute stress values. Fourth, although in vivo MRI in healthy volunteers demonstrated clear visualization of elbow cartilage under physiological conditions, these data were not validated against a ground-truth reference; therefore, they confirm imaging feasibility but do not establish measurement accuracy in living subjects.

In conclusion, these models offer a validated noninvasive method for assessing cartilage morphology in cadaveric specimens. With further in vivo validation, this approach may contribute to understanding elbow joint mechanics and warrant future investigation in OA.
