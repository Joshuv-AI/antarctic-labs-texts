The predictive value of tidal volume challenge-induced hemodynamic changes for fluid responsiveness in patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery with one-lung ventilation: a prospective observational study
Authors: Yong Zhan, Qi-quan Zhang, Xiao-qiang Wang, Hao Yuan, Jun-qing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVideo-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) relies on one-lung ventilation (OLV) to achieve optimal surgical conditions. However, the lung protective ventilation strategies commonly employed during OLV diminish the accuracy of traditional dynamic parameters in predicting fluid responsiveness. The tidal volume challenge (TVC) has been proposed to overcome this limitation, yet its effectiveness in OLV patients remains to be validated. In this study, we used LiDCO to evaluate TVC-induced changes in SVV(ΔSVV_TVC) and PPV(ΔPPV_TVC), and assessed their ability to predict fluid responsiveness in patients receiving lung-protective OLV during VATS.MethodsAll patients received OLV in lateral position after general anesthesia induction. Upon achieving hemodynamic stability, the study protocol was initiated. The VLT consisted of infusing 7 mL/kg hydroxyethyl starch over 20–30 min. ROC analysis showed that the area under the curve (AUC) of ΔSVV_TVC for predicting fluid responsiveness was 0.83 (95% CI, 0.71–0.91), with an optimal cutoff value of > 2%, sensitivity of 77%, specificity of 76%, and a gray zone range of 1%–3% encompassing 23 patients (38%). Background

Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has emerged as a fundamental technique in the diagnosis and treatment of early-stage pulmonary diseases, attributed to its minimally invasive nature and accelerated postoperative recovery profile. A critical anesthetic requirement for VATS is lung isolation to achieve one-lung ventilation (OLV). While OLV provides an ideal surgical field, it inevitably leads to physiological disturbances such as ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch and inhibition of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV), which may precipitate hypoxemia and hemodynamic instability [1–3]. Appropriate fluid management is essential for maintaining circulatory stability and balancing oxygen supply and demand in these patients. However, accurately assessing fluid responsiveness during OLV remains a significant challenge in clinical practice.

In thoracic surgery, liberal fluid strategy (> 10 mL·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹) may predispose patients to pulmonary edema and cardiac dysfunction [4]. conversely, restrictive strategy (< 3 mL·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹) carries the risk of organ dysfunction due to inadequate perfusion [5]. Although a moderate approach (3–5 mL·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹) can serve as a balanced strategy [6, 7], its empirical nature still poses potential risks for fluid imbalance in clinical practice. In recent years, dynamic parameters derived from cardiopulmonary interactions, including SVV and PPV, have garnered attention for their superior predictive value for fluid responsiveness compared to static preload indices [8, 9]. Nevertheless, the lung-protective ventilation strategy commonly used during OLV with tidal volumes as low as 6 mL/kg reduces tidal fluctuations in intrathoracic pressure, considerably diminishing the predictive accuracy of SVV and PPV [10]. Recent studies [11–15] have proposed the tidal volume challenge (TVC) as a functional test to evaluate fluid responsiveness in mechanically ventilated patients. This maneuver involves a transient increase in tidal volume to amplify hemodynamic changes indicative of fluid need. However, existing evidence on TVC primarily derives from patients undergoing bilateral lung ventilation [16]. During VATS, unique conditions such as an open thoracic cavity, mediastinal shift, and unilateral lung collapse may alter normal cardiopulmonary interactions. Whether TVC-induced changes in SVV and PPV can reliably predict fluid responsiveness in this specific context remains inadequately investigated.

Therefore, this study aims to utilize the LiDCO hemodynamic monitoring system which provides continuous and accurate measurements of stroke volume and cardiac output to evaluate the predictive value of TVC-induced changes in SVV and PPV for fluid responsiveness during OLV in patients undergoing VATS. The findings may contribute to more precise and individualized fluid management in thoracic surgery.

Methods

Study design and patient population

The prospective study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fuyang Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (approval number: KY2023058). From September 2023 and May 2025, written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The trial was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2300075285) before patient recruitment began. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) body mass index (BMI) between 18 and 30 kg/m²; (3) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification II or III; (4) elective thoracoscopic lobectomy under general anesthesia is scheduled; (5) requirement for one-lung ventilation in the lateral decubitus position(positioning was determined by the surgical site and was not randomized). Exclusion criteria: (1)Significant cardiopulmonary comorbidities: cardiogenic pulmonary edema, moderate-to-severe valvular heart disease, heart failure (NYHA class > II), arrhythmia, or severe COPD.(2)Vasoactive agent use: Administration of any continuous or bolus vasoactive drug (e.g., norepinephrine, ephedrine) for hemodynamic support within 20 min before the study.These criteria were pre-defined to preclude significant confounding from intrinsic cardiac disease or extrinsic pharmacological effects, ensuring that SVV and PPV measurements primarily reflected volume status.

Anesthetic technique

Patients were fasted for at least 8 h and abstained from clear fluids for at least 4 h preoperatively. Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was established, including electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, and oxygen saturation. Under local anesthesia, radial artery catheterization was performed. The arterial catheter was connected to a multi-parameter patient monitor (Philips, Germany) for continuous invasive arterial pressure monitoring. Simultaneously, it was linked to the LiDCO hemodynamic monitoring system (Masimo Corporation, USA), which was equipped with an uncalibrated cardiac output monitoring module for continuous measurement of cardiac index (CI), systemic vascular resistance index (SVRI), stroke volume index (SVI), stroke volume variation (SVV) and pulse pressure variation (PPV). General anesthesia was induced using propofol, midazolam, sufentanil, and rocuronium following preoxygenation, and maintained with sevoflurane, remifentanil, and propofol. The depth of anesthesia was regulated to maintain a bispectral index between 45 and 60 throughout the surgical procedure. Following induction, a double-lumen endotracheal tube was placed under fiberoptic bronchoscopy guidance, and two-lung ventilation was initiated after confirming correct tube position. Once hemodynamic stability was achieved, the patient was repositioned into the lateral decubitus position. The arterial pressure transducer was re-zeroed to the level of the heart. Tube position was re-confirmed via fiberoptic bronchoscopy before commencing one-lung ventilation. A lung-protective ventilation strategy was implemented during OLV. Tidal volume was set at 6 mL/kg of predicted body weight PBW (determined as x + 0.91[height (in cm) − 152.4], where x = 50 for males and x = 45.5 for females). Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) was maintained at 5 cmH₂O, and the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) was set ≥ 60% to maintain SpO₂ above 90%. The inspiratory to expiratory ratio (I: E) was set at 1:2. Respiratory rate was adjusted to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO₂) between 35 and 45 mmHg. The peak inspiratory airway pressure (PIP) and dynamic lung compliance (Cdyn) were measured and recorded using the anesthesia machine (Drager primus, Germany).

During the video-assisted thoracic procedure, the non-ventilated lung was allowed to collapse naturally by its elastic recoil without CO₂ insufflation. All data collection was performed under closed-chest conditions while the patients were undergoing video-assisted thoracic surgery with one-lung ventilation. Intravenous lactated Ringer solution was administered continuously at 4 mL·kg⁻¹·h⁻¹.After achieving 20 min of one-lung ventilation and hemodynamic stability(defined as fluctuations in mean arterial pressure and heart rate within 10% of baseline), the following procedures were sequentially conducted: (1)Tidal volume challenge (TVC): Tidal volume was increased from 6 mL/kg to 8 mL/kg predicted body weight (PBW).(2)Volume Load Test (VLT): A hydroxyethyl starch solution (7 mL/kg) was infused intravenously over 20–30 min. According to the ΔSVI_VLT, the patients were categorized into two groups [10]: responders (ΔSVI_VLT ≥ 10%) and non-responders ΔSVI_VLT < 10%).

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

Sample size was calculated using PASS ver. 21.0 (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA). The primary objective was to evaluate the predictive performance of ΔSVV_TVC and ΔPPV_TVC for fluid responsiveness. We hypothesized that the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) for this indicator would be at least 0.75. Using a null hypothesis AUC of 0.50 (implying no discriminative ability), a type I error (α) of 0.05, power (1-β) of 0.90, and an anticipated responder to non-responder ratio of 1:1, the calculation indicated a minimum requirement of 25 patients per group. Accounting for an estimated dropout rate of 15%, the final target sample size was set at 60 patients.

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data are presented as median (interquartile range), mean ± standard deviation, or number of patients (%), as appropriate. Characteristics were compared between responders and non-responders using the independent t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test. Hemodynamic variables were compared between T1 and T2, as well as at T3 and T4, using the paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test, depending on the distribution of the data. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted for SVV, PPV, and their TVC-induced changes to predict fluid responsiveness. The area under the ROC curve and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were calculated. The DeLong method was used to compare the AUC values of different predictors. To control for inflation of type I error due to multiple comparisons, the P-values for the comparisons of multiple indices were adjusted using the Holm-Bonferroni method. Differences in AUC between patients in the left and right lateral positions were compared using the Hanley & McNeil method. The optimal cutoff value for each dynamic parameter was determined by maximizing the Youden’s index. A gray zone analysis was performed using bootstrapping with 1000 resamples to identify the range of values with inconclusive predictive ability.

Statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc ver.23.0 (MedCalc, Software, Ostend, Belgium), SPSS software ver. 22.0(IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism ver. 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). In all analyses. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 71 patients were initially screened for eligibility. Among them, 11 were excluded for the following reasons: 2 presented with arrhythmia, 4 underwent conversion to thoracotomy, and 5 required vasoactive drug support. Ultimately, 60 patients successfully completed the entire study protocol (Fig. 2). Based on the fluid responsiveness criterion (ΔSVI_VLT ≥ 10%), 26 patients (43.3%) were classified as responders (R group) and 34 (56.7%) as non-responders (NR group). The two groups were comparable in all baseline characteristics (Table 1). No statistically significant differences were observed in terms of age, sex, BMI, ASA physical status, comorbidities, respiratory parameters, or surgical position.

Discussion

This study is the first to demonstrate that tidal volume challenge induced changes in SVV and PPV exhibit good predictive value for fluid responsiveness in patients undergoing one-lung ventilation during video-assisted thoracic surgery, with optimal cutoff values of > 2% and > 3% respectively. Importantly, the predictive performance of these parameters was not influenced by the patient’s surgical position (left or right lateral position). These findings provide a novel and practical approach for precise fluid assessment during OLV.

Perioperative fluid management represents a critical component of anesthetic care during thoracic surgery. Dynamic indices such as SVV and PPV have demonstrated considerable predictive value for assessing fluid responsiveness within the perioperative period [17–20]. Their physiological basis lies in the cyclical changes in venous return and transpulmonary pressure induced by mechanical ventilation [21]. In VATS, although the operated hemithorax is opened, the chest wall and the pleural cavity of the ventilated lung remain intact. This anatomical configuration preserves the cyclical variation in venous return [22], thereby providing a theoretical foundation for the application of SVV and PPV. However, the collapse of the non-ventilated lung eliminates its contribution to cyclical intrathoracic pressure changes. Concurrently, the open hemithorax allows dissipation of part of the pressure fluctuations generated by mechanical ventilation into the atmosphere. These factors attenuate the regularity of intrathoracic pressure transmission and reduce the amplitude of dynamic indices. As Suehiro et al. highlighted, a tidal volume of at least 8 mL/kg is required for SVV and PPV to accurately predict fluid responsiveness during OLV [23, 24]. Conversely, the latest expert consensus in thoracic anesthesia strongly recommends a lung-protective ventilation strategy with tidal volumes set at 4–6 mL/kg [25]. The implementation of this protective strategy inherently limits the reliability of SVV and PPV, making it difficult to distinguish between fluid responders and non-responders during OLV [10].

In response to this clinical dilemma, anesthesiologists have explored various methods to transiently increase intrathoracic pressure, aiming to enhance the utility of functional hemodynamic indices. Myatra et al. first introduced the tidal volume challenge, which involves temporarily increasing tidal volume to amplify cardiopulmonary interactions and overcome the limitations imposed by low tidal volumes on SVV and PPV, without adding invasive procedures or altering the ventilation strategy [26]. Given that prolonged high tidal volume ventilation (≥ 8 mL/kg) during surgery increases the risk of barotrauma and may interfere with the surgical field [3, 27, 28], the TVC was strictly limited to one minute in our protocol. Our study further confirms the limited predictive value of SVV and PPV for fluid responsiveness during OLV with a lung-protective tidal volume of 6 mL/kg, consistent with the findings reported by Myatra et al. [10]. After implementing the TVC (increasing tidal volume to 8 mL/kg), both SVV and PPV increased, with more pronounced changes observed in hypovolemic patients, thereby improving the discriminative ability for fluid responsiveness. This aligns with the physiological principle that fluid responsiveness is influenced by tidal volume magnitude [23]. Moreover, the TVC-induced changes in SVV and PPV (ΔSVV_TVC and ΔPPV_TVC) effectively identified fluid responders, with AUCs of 0.825 (95% CI: 0.71–0.91) and 0.857 (95% CI: 0.74–0.93), respectively. These results are concordant with studies utilizing different interventions. For instance, Zhang et al. [13] reported that TVC-induced changes in stroke volume (ΔSV) and velocity–time integral (ΔVTI) predicted fluid responsiveness during OLV with AUCs of 0.81 (95% CI 0.68–0.90) and 0.79 (95% CI 0.66–0.89) respectively. Similarly, Kimura et al. [29] used recruitment maneuver to elevate intrathoracic pressure and found that the resulting ΔSV and ΔMAP provided AUCs of 0.84 (95% CI 0.67–0.95) and 0.80 (95% CI 0.61–0.92) for predicting fluid responsiveness. In contrast, a study by Liu et al. [30]involving pediatric patients undergoing OLV indicated that ΔMAP following a recruitment maneuver had limited predictive value, whereas ΔSV remained a reliable predictor (AUC = 0.828). Collectively, these studies support the reliability of transient intrathoracic pressure elevation as a method for assessing fluid responsiveness in patients under OLV, thereby providing a solid theoretical foundation for clinical application.

However, the cutoff values for ΔSVV_TVC (> 2%) and ΔPPV_TVC (> 3%) identified in the present study exhibit certain discrepancies compared to those reported in previous investigations, yet al.ign closely with the initial findings by Myatra et al. in supine patients with acute circulatory failure (ΔSVV_TVC > 2.5%, ΔPPV_TVC > 3.5%) [31]. In contrast, differences are observed when compared with the optimal cutoffs reported by Jun and Tang et al. (ΔSVV_TVC > 2%, ΔPPV_TVC > 1%) in patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery in the Trendelenburg position [12, 32], as well as with those described by Taccheri et al. (both > 1%) in critically ill patients in the semi-recumbent position [33]. Mechanically speaking, SVV and PPV originate from cyclic intrathoracic pressure variations induced by mechanical ventilation. Their magnitude depends on the transmission of airway pressure to the pleural space, which is inversely related to chest wall compliance and positively related to lung compliance [34, 35]. In the context of VATS performed in the lateral decubitus position, the dependent hemithorax exhibits restricted chest wall expansion and reduced compliance. Concurrently, diaphragmatic elevation and gravitational mediastinal shift contribute to decreased lung compliance. These factors collectively support the observation of higher optimal cutoff values in our cohort. Although the patient population in the study by Myatra et al. did not include factors typically altering chest wall compliance, their reported lung compliance in patients with circulatory failure was notably low (25.7 ± 3.8 mL/cmH₂O), a value comparable to that observed during OLV. This similarity in lung compliance may explain the proximity of the optimal cutoff values between the two studies. Furthermore, although lateral positioning could theoretically influence functional hemodynamic parameters by altering transthoracic pressure gradients, cardiac anatomy, and venous return, our subgroup analysis revealed no significant difference in the optimal cutoff values or diagnostic accuracy of ΔSVV_TVC and ΔPPV_TVC between the left and right lateral positions. This finding is consistent with the conclusion of Xiang et al. that patient position does not affect the optimal SVV cutoff [36]. Similarly, studies by Messina et al. in neurosurgical patients comparing supine and prone positions further support this observation. In these studies, ΔPPV_TVC% predicted fluid responsiveness with AUCs of 0.94 [95% CI 0.82–0.99] and 0.96 [95% CI 0.87–1.0] respectively, and with similar cutoff values (12.2% vs. 13.3%), indicating stable discriminative performance of TVC-derived parameters across different body positions [37, 38].

This study revealed that under lung-protective ventilation during OLV, the baseline values SVV_T1 and PPV_T1 demonstrated limited sensitivity and specificity for predicting fluid responsiveness. Following the tidal volume challenge, both SVV_T2 and PPV_T2 exhibited markedly improved sensitivity (89% and 88%, respectively), yet their specificity remained relatively low (59% and 65%, respectively). This profile suggests that while post-TVC values may be suitable for initial screening of hypovolemic patients, their high false-positive rate could increase the risk of fluid overload. In contrast, the TVC-induced changes in these parameters showed more balanced performance. ΔSVV_TVC demonstrated moderate discriminative ability (sensitivity 77%, specificity 76%), while ΔPPV_TVC achieved both high sensitivity (85%) and specificity (80%), substantially reducing the risks of both misdiagnosis and missed diagnosis. To address the limitations of binary classification based on a single cutoff value [39], we further performed gray zone analysis. The results indicated that 38% of patients fell within the gray zone for ΔSVV_TVC (1%–3%), suggesting limited utility of this parameter alone for predicting fluid responsiveness. Conversely, only 22% of patients had ΔPPV_TVC values within its gray zone (2%–4%), indicating that the optimal cutoff value of this parameter demonstrates superior discriminative ability and enables more reliable differentiation between fluid responders and non-responders. Based on our results, the following protocol is recommended for fluid responsiveness assessment during OLV: a ΔPPV_TVC> 3% indicates a high probability of fluid responsiveness, suggesting that a fluid challenge may be beneficial. However, for values falling within the diagnostic gray zone of 3%–4%, we recommend a conservative approach entailing either a mini-fluid challenge or a multimodal assessment incorporating additional dynamic indices to guide fluid administration. Conversely, a ΔPPV_TVC< 3% suggests a low likelihood of fluid responsiveness. In such cases, fluid administration should be avoided to prevent iatrogenic volume overload.

Previous studies have established that an elevation in intrathoracic pressure can reduce venous return and right ventricular (RV) preload, while an increase in transpulmonary pressure may raise RV afterload and impair ejection, collectively leading to a decrease in cardiac output [40]. This effect is particularly pronounced in hypovolemic patients [41]. Mechanistically, research by Douglas et al. [42] indicates that even a tidal volume of 6 mL/kg can generate sufficient alveolar pressure to cause collapse of perialveolar pulmonary microvessels (vascular waterfall) in over 50% of patients, thereby increasing RV afterload. An important limitation is that their study population consisted of post-cardiac surgery patients, whose RV function may have been more vulnerable. In contrast, our study enrolled patients without severe cardiopulmonary disease, who likely possess greater cardiovascular compensatory reserve. Furthermore, several studies suggest that TVC have minimal hemodynamic impact in various surgical populations [12, 43, 44], implying that the intervention intensity may be insufficient to trigger widespread vascular collapse and subsequent decompensation. During OLV, the entire tidal volume is delivered to a single lung, while the non-ventilated lung is collapsed, resulting in an elevated pulmonary vascular resistance. Theoretically, this unique physiology could render RV afterload more sensitive to changes in tidal volume in the ventilated lung. This potential risk is illustrated by a study from Aya et al. [29], in which a vigorous lung recruitment maneuver during OLV precipitated an approximate 24% decrease in blood pressure in hypovolemic individuals. Compared to such high-intensity interventions, the afterload increase associated with the TVC in our protocol is modest. In the present study, although the SVI and MAP decreased following the TVC, the magnitude of change was less than 5% in both, and values promptly returned to baseline upon restoration of the original tidal volume, supporting its procedural safety. Most importantly, the core metric validated in this study is the TVC-induced change, not the absolute value at any single time point. Even if a theoretically minor, transient afterload effect exists, it is inherently integrated into the cut-off value(ΔPPV_TVC/ΔSVV_TVC) we observed. The optimal cut-off values, determined using the actual change in cardiac output after a volume load test as the reference standard, essentially encapsulates the net hemodynamic effect of the maneuver, inclusive of both preload and afterload influences, within this specific patient population.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the volume loading protocol (7 mL/kg hydroxyethyl starch) and the threshold for defining fluid responsiveness (∆SVI ≥ 10%) were adopted from previous studies [10]; different protocols and criteria may influence the performance of functional hemodynamic indices. Second, potential interference in arterial waveform quality due to surgical manipulation and other procedural factors may have influenced the accuracy of SVV and PPV measurements. Third, our findings are specific to patients undergoing video-assisted lobectomy and may not be generalizable to procedures with differing degrees of thoracic exposure, such as open thoracotomy, esophageal surgery, or pneumonectomy. Fourth, the subgroup analysis examining the effect of patient positioning may have been underpowered due to the limited sample size in each group. Furthermore, as patient positions were not randomly assigned, potential bias could not be ruled out due to known physiological differences between left and right lateral decubitus positions. Future research may consider employing a paired design or recruit larger samples to better validate the effect of patient positioning. Finally, while thermodilution remains the gold standard for stroke volume measurement, the LiDCO system used in this study has inherent measurement error. Nevertheless, its core advantages include the absence of requiring external calibration and compatibility with standard arterial catheters, making it suitable for efficient, minimally invasive hemodynamic monitoring in clinical settings, with previous studies demonstrating good trending agreement with thermodilution [45–47].

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that TVC is a straightforward and robust functional hemodynamic test. The increase of ΔSVV_TVC > 2% and ΔPPV_TVC > 3% effectively discriminate fluid responders and can guide fluid management strategies. In cases where values fall within the gray zone, it is recommended to integrate additional hemodynamic indicators for comprehensive evaluation. These findings provide new evidence-based support for achieving individualized and precise fluid management in patients undergoing one-lung ventilation.
