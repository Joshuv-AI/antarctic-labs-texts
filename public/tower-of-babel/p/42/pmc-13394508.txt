Partial hearing recovery after cochlear nerve surface transplantation of hair follicle‐derived neural crest stem cells in neural hearing loss rat
Authors: Huidong Chen, Yunlong Zhang, Huanzhi Wan, Bingqian Yang, Tianyi Liu, Jie Wang, Hua Liao, Yuanyuan Zhang, Zhijian Zhang, Qingquan Hua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStem cell therapy offers promise for the neurodegenerative diseases and has been explored for sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL). However, effective cell delivery strategies remain a critical challenge for SNHL treatment.MethodsTo address this need, we established an ouabain‐induced SGN injured hearing loss model in rat and evaluated a novel transplantation strategy targeting the cochlear nerve surface via posterior occipital approach, designed to minimize cochlear structural damage and facilitate targeted cell delivery to Rosenthal's canal (RC). The temporal changes in glial cell densities within RC revealed a progressively deteriorating neural microenvironment, supporting early‐stage intervention. Accordingly, hair follicle‐derived neural crest stem cell (HFNCSC) transplantation was performed 3 or 4 days after modeling via two approaches: cochlear nerve surface transplantation (CNT) and round window transplantation (RWT).ResultsCNT resulted in significant improvements in auditory function, as evidenced by reduced auditory brainstem response (ABR) thresholds, shortened wave I latencies, and preserved wave I amplitudes post‐transplantation. Transplanted cells were distributed along the nerve trunk and within RC. In contrast, RWT failed to improve auditory function and caused cochlear structural damage, with widespread cell dispersion in cochlear fluids. Notably, the CNT group exhibited significantly higher densities of TUJ1‐positive neuron‐like cells and glial cells in the RC, accompanied by enhanced myelin basic protein expression suggestive of remyelination. No such improvements were observed in the RWT group.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that cochlear nerve surface transplantation enhances stem cell survival and auditory function recovery, and represents a promising delivery approach for stem cell‐based therapy of SGN‐related hearing loss.Keywords: cochlear nerve surface approach, hair follicle‐derived neural crest stem cell, neural hearing loss, spiral ganglion neuron, stem cell transplantation

INTRODUCTION

Hearing loss is one of the most common sensory disorders that severely impacts quality of life. It is estimated that by 2050, 2.5 billion people (1 in 4) worldwide will experience hearing loss, with 700 million suffering from moderate or higher levels of hearing impairment requiring rehabilitation. 1 Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) accounts for a substantial proportion of these cases. Many cases result from cochlear hair cell dysfunction and can be effectively treated with cochlear implants or emerging gene therapies targeting hair cell‐specific mutations. 2 However, a significant subset of SNHL is characterized by degeneration of spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs), including hereditary auditory neuropathies and age‐related hearing loss. Unlike hair cells, SGNs reside deep within Rosenthal's canal (RC) and are anatomically isolated from the round window, making them difficult to access via conventional intracochlear injection routes.

For these conditions, stem cell‐based therapy aimed at replacing damaged SGNs and reconstructing auditory neural pathways represents a promising therapeutic strategy. However, translating this approach into clinical reality faces several critical challenges: (1) achieving efficient and targeted delivery of stem cells to the spiral ganglion region; (2) directing donor cell differentiation toward functional SGN‐like phenotypes; and (3) ensuring long‐term survival, integration, and synaptic connectivity within the hostile microenvironment of the injured cochlea. 3 Among these, the lack of an optimal transplantation route that can deliver therapeutic cells directly to the target neural structures while minimizing trauma remains a major bottleneck.

To date, most inner ear cell therapy studies have relied on direct injection through the round window. However, this approach results in extensive cell dispersion within cochlear fluids and limited survival within RC. 4 , 5 More invasive strategies such as direct transplantation into the modiolus, auditory nerve, or RC, improve cell localization but often cause uneven distribution and tissue damage due to surgical trauma. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Importantly, only a few studies have reported meaningful auditory function recovery following transplantation. 11 , 12 Among these, Sekiya et al. 12 adopted an innovative approach of transplanting cells onto the surface of the cochlear nerve, which enabled autonomous migration, integration, and functional recovery in a rat model of auditory nerve injury. Based on this conceptual advance, we hypothesized that a cochlear nerve surface transplantation approach might similarly facilitate targeted delivery of stem cells to the spiral ganglion region while minimizing additional trauma to the cochlea.

An additional important consideration is the choice of donor cell population. Hair follicle‐derived neural crest stem cells (HFNCSCs) are readily accessible, and share molecular and functional characteristics with neural stem cells, including expression of nestin and Sox10. 13 Moreover, HFNCSCs have been shown to differentiate into neurons 14 and Schwann cells 15 , 16 and to promote functional recovery in models of spinal cord and peripheral nerve injury. 17 , 18 , 19 Based on these properties, we selected HFNCSCs as the donor cell population for the present study and, to our knowledge, we are the first to apply them to the treatment of neural hearing loss.

We therefore established a rat model of ouabain‐induced SGN injury and investigated the therapeutic outcomes of round window injection and cochlear nerve surface transplantation using HFNCSCs. By systematically evaluating hearing function and cochlear pathology, we aimed to identify a more effective strategy for stem cell‐based therapy of SGN‐related hearing loss.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Subjects and experimental design

A total of 55 female Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (8 weeks old) were used in this study. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Wuhan University and were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines.

To characterize the temporal progression of ouabain‐induced cochlear injury, 24 rats were used. Ouabain (10 mmol/L) was applied to the round window membrane of the right ear, while the left ear received physiological saline and served as the control. Auditory function tests and cochlear tissue analyses were performed before treatment and at 2 days (n = 8), 7 days (n = 8), and 30 days (n = 8) after ouabain application.

For stem cell transplantation experiments, 31 rats were randomly assigned to four groups: normal control (NC group, n = 8), ouabain‐injured without transplantation (OI group, n = 8), round window transplantation (RWT group, n = 7), and cochlear nerve surface transplantation (CNT group, n = 8). Hearing loss was confirmed by auditory brainstem response (ABR) testing 2 days after ouabain treatment. Stem cell transplantation was performed 3 or 4 days after model induction. ABR measurements were conducted before modeling, 2 days after modeling, and at 10 and 30 days after transplantation. Cochlear tissues were harvested 30 days post‐transplantation for histological and immunofluorescence analyses. All outcome assessments (ABR measurements, histological analyses, cell counting) were performed by investigators blinded to group allocation. The blinding was maintained until all data were collected and analyzed.

Isolation, culture, and induction of HFNCSCs

Hair follicle‐derived neural crest stem cells (HFNCSCs) were isolated from the whisker hair follicles of SD rats. 20 Following euthanasia, the whisker pad region was disinfected, and intact hair follicles were dissected and immersed in Hanks' balanced salt solution (HBSS). Under a stereomicroscope (Olympus, Japan), hair follicles were separated from surrounding skin, dermis, and adipose tissue. The bulge region was carefully excised, rinsed three times with HBSS, and plated in six‐well plates pre‐coated with fibronectin (25 μg/plate; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). After adhesion, cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% N2, 2% B27, 1% GlutaMAX, 0.25% ITS+3, epidermal growth factor (20 ng/mL), basic fibroblast growth factor (20 ng/mL), and Normocin (100 μg/mL). Cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2, and half of the medium was replaced every other day. After 4 days, the bulge tissue was removed, and adherent cells were dissociated using StemPro Accutase (Gibco, USA). Cells were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min, resuspended, and subcultured at a density of 1000 cells per well. For neuronal differentiation, retinoic acid (2000 nmol/L) and sonic hedgehog (500 ng/mL) were added to the culture medium. For glial differentiation, neuregulin‐1 (125 ng/mL) was used. Directed differentiation was performed for 7 days before further analysis.

Immunofluorescence identification and GFP labeling of HFNCSCs

For immunofluorescence staining, cultured cells were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 5 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‐100 for 15 min, and blocked with 10% normal goat serum for 30 min. Cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against nestin (mouse, 1:100; Abcam) and Sox10 (rabbit, 1:300; Abcam). Differentiated cells were stained with TUJ1 (mouse, 1:100; R&D Systems) or S100B (rabbit, 1:300; Abcam). Corresponding Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

For cell tracking, HFNCSCs were labeled with green fluorescent protein (GFP) using a lentiviral transduction system (Gigascreen). Optimal infection conditions were determined in preliminary experiments. Cells exhibiting stable GFP expression were expanded and cryopreserved for subsequent transplantation.

Surgical procedures

Ouabain application through round window

Rats were anesthetized with avertin (2.5 mL/100 g, intraperitoneally) and maintained at 37°C. A postauricular incision was made to expose the auditory bulla. A small opening was drilled to visualize the round window niche, and 5 μL of ouabain solution (10 mmol/L) was applied to the round window membrane using a microsyringe. The solution was refreshed every 30 min for 1 h. The bulla was sealed with bone wax, and the incision was closed in layers (Figure S1).

Round window approach for transplantation

Using the same surgical approach, 1 × 104 GFP‐labeled HFNCSCs suspended in 10 μL PBS were injected into the cochlea through the round window membrane using a microsyringe. Prior to transplantation, cell viability was assessed by trypan blue exclusion and consistently exceeded 95%. The surgical site was sealed and closed as described above.

Cochlear nerve surface approach for transplantation

Based on the method of Sekiya et al., 12 rats were placed in a prone position, and a posterior occipital incision was made to expose the occipital bone. A 3–4 mm craniotomy was performed, and the dura mater was carefully opened. Partial cerebellar tissue was gently aspirated to expose the cochlear nerve near the internal auditory canal. 1 × 104 GFP‐labeled HFNCSCs suspended in Matrigel (10 μL) were injected onto the surface of the cochlear nerve using a micropipette. After confirmation of gelation, the incision was closed layer by layer.

Auditory brainstem response (ABR) testing

ABR testing was performed in an anechoic chamber using the TDT RZ6 system (Tucker‐Davis Technologies, USA). Subdermal needle electrodes were placed at the vertex (recording), ipsilateral mastoid (reference), and contralateral thigh (ground). Tone‐burst stimuli (8, 16, and 32 kHz; 3 ms duration) were delivered, with sound intensities ranging from 90 to 10 dB SPL in 5 dB steps. ABR threshold was defined as the lowest stimulus level that elicited a reproducible wave I response.

Distortion product otoacoustic emission test (DPOAE)

Distortion product otoacoustic emissions (DPOAEs) were recorded using the TDT RZ6 system with an f2/f1 ratio of 1.2. The f1 level ranged from 20 to 80 dB SPL. DPOAE amplitudes at 2f1‐f2 frequencies (4, 8, 16, and 32 kHz) were measured, and thresholds were defined as the stimulus level required to produce a 0 dB SPL response.

Tissue preparation

After ABR and DPOAE tests, the rats were euthanized. The temporal bones and adjacent brainstem tissues on both sides were removed intact. These were placed in 4% PFA and dissected under a stereomicroscope (Olympus, Japan). Perfusion of 4% PFA through the round window and oval window was used to fix the internal cochlear tissues. The cochlear tissue with the brainstem was immersed in 4% PFA and fixed for 24 h at 4°C and decalcified for 9–10 days in 10% EDTA on a shaker at room temperature with the solution changed every 3 days. Following PBS washing, the tissue was successively dehydrated in 15% and 30% sucrose solutions, and then embedded in O.C.T. frozen tissue embedding agent (Sakura, USA).

Cryosectioning and immunofluorescence staining

Cryosetions were made with a cryostat (Leica, Germany) to prepare 10 μm thick cochlear tissue sections. During embedding, the tissue was rotated 90° horizontally left or right from the conventional cochlear sectioning direction with the cochlear axis as the longitudinal axis, to achieve a cross‐section where the “cochlea‐temporal bone‐cochlear nucleus” were in the same field of view. This allowed observation of the cochlear nerve extending into the cochlear nucleus. Cryosections were then air‐dried using cold air, followed by PBS washing for 5 min, fixed in 4% PFA for 5 min, washed with PBS, and 0.5%Triton X‐100 for 15 min. After washed in PBS 3 times, 10% normal goat serum was added for 30‐min blocking, and then appropriate amounts of primary antibody incubation solution were applied. Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C. Primary antibodies included mouse anti‐TUJ1 (1:100; MAB1195; R&D Systems, USA), rabbit anti‐S100B (1:300; ab52642; Abcam, Ambridge, MA, USA), and rat anti‐MBP (1:200; ab7349; Abcam). After primary antibody incubation, the primary antibody solution was carefully removed, followed by three PBS washes. The corresponding secondary antibody solution (Alexa Fluor 488 or 594, Invitrogen, USA) was added and the section was incubated in a dark, moist incubator at room temperature for 1 h. The slides were washed with PBS three times. Nuclear staining with DAPI (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) was performed, and the slides were then covered. Fluorescence images were captured using a microscope (Olympus BX53, Japan).

Surface preparation and staining

Under a stereomicroscope (Olympus, Japan), the bulla was removed to expose the cochlea. The cochlear tissue was perfused and fixed with 4% PFA at 4°C for 24 h. After fixation, it was washed three times with PBS and decalcified for 4 days in 10% EDTA at 4°C. The decalcified rat cochlea was dissected, and the basilar membrane was separated and divided into apical, middle, and basal turns. The tissues were incubated in phalloidin (diluted 1:500; P5282; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate in the dark at room temperature for 2 h. The basilar membrane stained with phalloidin was carefully removed, placed on a slide and positioned under the microscope before covering with a coverslip. Under the microscope, any missing hair cells or acicular cilia were meticulously examined.

Cell counting

Immunofluorescence staining was performed on cochlear cryosections from each group to quantitatively analyze TUJ1 and S100B‐positive cells. Five 10 μm sections were collected from each cochlea, with at least 30 μm spacing between sections. The area was calculated using Image‐Pro Plus 6.0 software, and both immunofluorescence‐positive SGNs and glial cells were counted. Cell density was determined by dividing the total cell count by the area available for counting.

Data analysis

All data values are presented as mean ± standard error (mean ± SEM). Statistical analysis and graph generation were performed using Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA). Two independent samples t‐test, one‐way ANOVA, and two‐way ANOVA were employed to assess statistical differences in ABR test results and immunofluorescence staining between groups. A p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Auditory characteristics of the ouabain‐induced hearing loss model

Following round window application of ouabain, significant shifts in ABR thresholds were observed in treated ears compared with controls. At 2 days post‐treatment, ABR thresholds were significantly elevated across all tested frequencies. By 7 days post‐treatment, threshold elevations persisted at 16 and 32 kHz, while lower frequencies showed partial recovery. At 30 days post‐treatment, ABR thresholds at 16 and 32 kHz remained significantly elevated, whereas thresholds at 8 kHz were no longer significantly different from controls (Figure 1A).

(A) Mean ABR thresholds (mean ± SEM, n = 8/group) of groups treated with 10 mM ouabain through round window membrane on days 0, 2, 7, and 30; (B) Mean DPOAE thresholds (mean ± SEM, n = 8/group) of groups treated with 10 mmol/L ouabain on days 0, 2, 7, and 30. ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

In addition to threshold shifts, ABR waveforms from ouabain‐treated ears exhibited prolonged wave I latencies compared with controls (Figure S2). DPOAE thresholds were significantly increased at 2 days post‐treatment but returned to control levels at 7 and 30 days post‐treatment (Figure 1B). Phalloidin staining of the basilar membrane revealed no substantial loss of cochlear hair cells (Figure S3).

Ouabain induces degeneration of SGNs and glial cells in rat cochlea

To quantitatively assess the degenerative effects of ouabain on spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs) and glial cells, we performed immunofluorescence staining on cochlear sections at 2, 7, and 30 days post‐treatment (Figure 2A).

(A) Spiral ganglion neurons (TUJ1 green fluorescence), glial cells (S100B red fluorescence), and nuclei (DAPI blue fluorescence) in the apex, middle, and base turns of the cochlea from the control group and groups treated with 10 mmol/L ouabain on days 2, 7, and 30. The scale bar represents 50 μm. (B, C) Density (mean ± SEM, n = 8/group) of spiral ganglion neurons and glial cells in the apex, middle, and base turns of the cochlea from the control group and groups treated with 10 mmol/L ouabain on days 2, 7, and 30. *p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

In control cochleae, SGN densities in the apical, middle, and basal turns were 4213 ± 470, 4822 ± 432, and 3737 ± 296 cells/mm2, respectively. In ouabain‐treated cochleae, following ouabain treatment, SGN densities in the apical turns remained comparable to controls at all time points. In contrast, SGN densities in the middle turns decreased to approximately 52%–54% of control levels at 2–30 days post‐treatment, while those in the basal turns were markedly reduced to only 8%–10% of control levels (Figure 2B). These results indicate that ouabain induces region‐specific SGN degeneration, with the basal and middle turns being most severely affected.

Glial cell densities showed a different temporal pattern. After ouabain treatment, glial cell densities in the apical turns remained unchanged. However, in the middle turns, glial cell densities progressively declined to 79%, 69%, and 57% of control levels at 2, 7, and 30 days, respectively. In the basal turns, the decline was even more pronounced, falling to 38%, 35%, and 13% of control levels at the corresponding time points (Figure 2C). Notably, while SGN loss stabilized within the first 2 days and remained constant thereafter, glial cell densities continued to decrease progressively, with significantly lower values at 30 days than at 2 days post‐treatment (Figure 2B,C).

Isolation and induction of hair follicle‐derived HFNCSCs

Following 4 days of in vitro culture, spindle‐shaped cells migrated from the hair follicle bulge region and adhered to the fibronectin‐coated culture surface, exhibiting active proliferation (Figure 3A,B). Following lentiviral transduction and selection, HFNCSCs exhibited stable green fluorescent protein (GFP) expression (Figure 3C). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the migrating and proliferating cells expressed nestin (Figure 3D) and Sox10 (Figure 3E), confirming their identification as HFNCSCs.

Under directed differentiation conditions, HFNCSCs cultured with retinoic acid and sonic hedgehog expressed TUJ1 after 7 days (Figure 3F), while those cultured in neuregulin‐1‐containing medium expressed S100B (Figure 3G).

Hearing improvement after HFNCSC transplantation in CNT group

All animals subjected to stem cell transplantation survived the surgical procedures and showed no adverse effects including tumor formation, throughout the 30‐day observation period.

Electrophysiological assessments showed that in the RWT group, ABR testing at 10 and 30 days post‐transplantation revealed no significant improvement in thresholds at 8, 16, or 32 kHz compared with the ouabain injured group (Figure 4A). ABR wave I latencies at 90 dB SPL were significantly prolonged at 8 and 16 kHz, and wave I amplitudes were significantly reduced across all tested frequencies (Figure 4B,C).

The asterisk indicates statistically significant differences between the transplantation groups and the ouabain injured group. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

In contrast, CNT resulted in functional improvement in most animals. Seven of eight rats exhibited improved ABR thresholds, with five showing improvements across all tested frequencies. At 10 and 30 days post‐transplantation, ABR thresholds at 16 and 32 kHz were significantly reduced compared with the ouabain injured group, while thresholds at 8 kHz showed no significant difference (Figure 4A). ABR wave I latencies were significantly shortened compared with the ouabain injured group, and wave I amplitudes were preserved (Figure 4B,C). These electrophysiological findings provide direct evidence of partial functional recovery following cochlear nerve surface transplantation (Figure S4).

Round window transplantation causes structural damage to the cochlea

Nissl staining revealed morphological and structural damage in the cochlea of the RWT group. The two transplantation approaches (RWT and CNT) are illustrated schematically in Figure 5A. Six out of seven animals showed evidence of cochlear structural damage (clustered cells in scala media, basilar membrane deformation, or osseous spiral lamina fractures) upon histological examination. In multiple cochlear sections, transplanted cells were observed to aggregate into clusters within the scala media (arrows in Figure 5B), partially obstructing the duct. The vestibular membrane showed marked deformation and disruption (arrowheads in Figure 5B). Furthermore, fractures were detected within the osseous portion of RC at the basal turns of the cochlea (arrowheads in Figure 5C), accompanied by evident deformation of the basilar membrane (arrowheads in Figure 5D). In contrast, the morphology of the cochlear structure after cell transplantation in the CNT group remained intact and normal (Figure 5E–G).

Survival and distribution of transplanted HFNCSCs

At 30 days post‐transplantation, GFP‐labeled HFNCSCs in the RWT group were predominantly distributed within the scala tympani (ST), scala vestibuli (SV), and scala media (SM), with some cells forming clusters along the lateral cochlear wall (Figure 5B,H,I). GFP‐ and TUJ1‐positive cells were rarely detected within the RC.

In the CNT group, GFP‐labeled cells were observed along the cochlear nerve trunk and migrating toward the cochlea. GFP‐ and TUJ1‐positive cells were detected within the cochlear modiolus and RC (Figure 5J,K).

Increased densities of TUJ1‐positive cells and glial cells following cochlear nerve surface transplantation

The immunofluorescence staining of cryosections of the normal cochlea, the ouabain injured group, and the 30 days post‐transplantation cochleas of the RWT and CNT groups were analyzed, and densities of the TUJ1‐positive cells and glial cells in different groups were compared (Figures 6A, B and 7A, B). Compared with normal cochlea, the densities of TUJ1‐positive cells and glial cells in the middle and basal turns of the RC in the ouabain injured group were significantly reduced, which was consistent with the results in the previous modeling experiment (Figures 6B and 7B). In the RWT group, the densities of TUJ1‐positive cells in the apical, middle and basal turns of the cochlea were significantly lower than those of the normal cochlea, and the densities of glial cells in the basal turns were also significantly lower (Figures 6B and 7B). While in the CNT group, the densities of TUJ1‐positive cells in the apical and middle turns of the cochlea showed no significant difference from normal cochlea. However, the TUJ1‐positive cells in the basal turns remained significantly reduced compared with normal cochlea but significantly higher than those in the ouabain injured group and RWT group. The glial cell densities in all cochlear turns showed no significant difference from normal cochlea, with the basal turn glial cell densities significantly higher than both the ouabain injured group and RWT group (Figures 6B and 7B). Notably, GFP‐labeled cells within the RC co‐localized with TUJ1‐positive neuron‐like cells and were surrounded by S100B‐positive glial cells (Figure 7A). In conclusion, compared with the ouabain injured group and the RWT group, the densities of TUJ1‐positive neuron‐like cells and glial cells in the cochlear RC of the CNT group were significantly higher after the transplantation of HFNCSCs.

Enhanced MBP expression following cochlear nerve surface transplantation

Previous studies indicated that myelin basic protein (MBP) immunostaining is a sensitive, reliable, and effective method for assessing auditory nerve myelin changes. 21 To further explore the alterations in the microenvironment of the cochlear SGNs in ouabain‐induced hearing loss models and post‐transplantation rats, we conducted TUJ1 and MBP staining on all groups. The staining results revealed MBP expression around peripheral processes of SGNs within the cochlea of normal rats, with additional MBP expression observed in the RC. In the ouabain‐induced models, SGNs in the RC showed minimal survival by day 30, and peripheral nerve fibers and myelin sheaths were nearly absent. After 30 days of round window cell transplantation, the cochlea exhibited only sparse TUJ1‐positive cells and MBP‐positive areas. Conversely, extensive MBP‐positive areas appeared in the RC and peripheral processes regions, accompanied by significantly higher TUJ1‐positive neuron‐like cell survival in the RC 30 days post‐transplantation in the CNT group (Figure 8A). Immunofluorescence intensity analysis demonstrated that MBP expression in the ouabain injured group and RWT group was markedly lower than in the normal cochlea. However, MBP expression in the CNT group was significantly higher than in the ouabain injured group and RWT group and did not differ significantly from normal controls (Figure 8B).

DISCUSSION

Optimal timing for stem cell transplantation in neural hearing loss

In this study, we systematically characterized the temporal progression of ouabain‐ induced neural hearing loss in rats and identified an optimal therapeutic window for stem cell transplantation. ABR testing demonstrated acute hearing impairment across all tested frequencies shortly after ouabain exposure with time‐dependent recovery at lower frequencies but persistent high‐frequency hearing loss persisted for at least 30 days. These functional changes were consistent with the spatial pattern of cochlear pathology: SGN degeneration was most pronounced in the basal and middle turns, while apical turn neurons were relatively preserved—consistent with prior reports. 22 , 23 SGN densities in the injured cochlea declined rapidly within the first 2 days after ouabain treatment and remained largely stable thereafter, whereas glial cell densities continued to decrease progressively over time, reaching significantly lower levels at 30 days compared with the early post‐injury stage. This progressive loss of glial cells suggests a gradual deterioration of the neural microenvironment within RC.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the survival, differentiation, and functional integration of transplanted stem cells are highly dependent on the host tissue microenvironment. 24 , 25 Lang et al. 26 reported that embryonic stem cell engraftment in an auditory neuropathy model was most successful when transplantation was performed during the early post‐injury period, when supportive cellular and molecular cues were still present. Consistent with these findings, our data indicate that early intervention—specifically 3–4 days after neural injury—provides a more favorable environment for stem cell survival and integration, likely due to the relative preservation of glial support cells within the RC.

The survival and distribution of the transplanted cells were highly related to the transplantation approach

Efficient delivery of therapeutic cells to the target neural structures is another major challenge. Most previous studies have relied on round window injection, which typically results in widespread cell dispersion within cochlear fluids and limited engraftment in RC. 3 These findings were consistent with our RWT group. More invasive strategies, such as direct transplantation into the modiolus or auditory nerve, improve localization but are often associated with uneven distribution and tissue damage. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10

The two transplantation approaches used in the present study resulted in different patterns of cell distribution. Following round window injection, transplanted cells were predominantly distributed within the scala tympani, scala vestibuli, and scala media, with minimal localization to the RC. In contrast, cochlear nerve surface transplantation enabled transplanted cells to migrate along the nerve trunk toward the cochlea, resulting in targeted localization within the cochlear and RC. This pattern closely resembles the findings of Sekiya et al. 12 who demonstrated that cells placed on the surface of the injured auditory nerve could autonomously migrate into the parenchyma, guided by structural cues within the glial scar. Together with emerging evidence from gene therapy studies showing axonal transport‐mediated delivery of viral vectors to SGNs, 27 our results suggest that SGN‐targeted therapies should leverage neural pathways rather than rely solely on cochlear fluid‐based delivery methods.

Clinical relevance and translational potential of cochlear nerve surface transplantation

In this study, partial hearing recovery after cochlear nerve surface transplantation of HFNCSCs was observed. These results indicate that cochlear nerve surface transplantation may offer a realistic clinical opportunity for patients with various etiologies of auditory nerve degeneration, including auditory neuropathy, hereditary polyneuropathies, head trauma, and vestibular schwannoma (VS) treated surgically or with radiotherapy. 28

Beyond direct hearing restoration, cochlear nerve surface transplantation could also increase the number of functional auditory neurons and thus a patient's candidature for cochlear implantation. The minimum number of functional auditory neurons required for successful CI performance is estimated to be only 5%–10% of the normal number. 29 However, many patients with profound SGN loss fall below this threshold. Refilling auditory neurons through cell transplantation could therefore expand CI candidacy and improve outcomes, particularly for patients with neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2), who experience progressive hearing decline as VS grows. 30

The clinical feasibility of cochlear nerve surface transplantation is supported by its alignment with existing surgical procedures such as resection of acoustic neuroma via retrosigmoid approach. The practical application of surface transplantation is further facilitated by endoscopic techniques, which offer minimally invasive procedures and proven safe maneuverability. 31 , 32 Therefore, we consider that cochlear nerve surface transplantation holds a great potential for clinical translation.

Possible mechanism underlying cell survival and partial hearing recovery

Although numerous studies have reported survival and differentiation of transplanted stem cells in the cochlea, only a few have demonstrated meaningful improvements in ABR thresholds. 11 , 12 Restoration of hearing requires not only cell survival but also functional integration of transplanted cells into the auditory neural network. In this study, partial functional recovery was observed exclusively in the cochlear nerve surface transplantation group. Rats receiving cochlear nerve surface transplantation exhibited significant improvements in ABR thresholds at middle and high frequencies, along with shortened ABR wave I latencies. In contrast, round window transplantation failed to produce functional improvement despite successful cell delivery.

The neuronal microenvironment is crucial for the survival and function of neurons, with glial cells surrounding neurons being the most important component. Glial cells play a central role in maintaining neuronal function, providing metabolic support, regulating synaptic activity, and forming myelin sheaths. In neural injury models, satellite glial cells surrounding SGNs undergo a series of changes, including apoptosis 23 and transdifferentiation, 33 which may play important roles in responses to neural injury and protection of neurons.

Our data demonstrate that glial cells exhibit substantial plasticity following ouabain‐induced injury. While SGN loss stabilized early after injury, glial cell densities continued to decline over time, indicating progressive microenvironmental deterioration. Notably, cochlear nerve surface transplantation significantly increased glial cell density within the RC and achieved MBP expression levels comparable with normal cochleae. The increased MBP expression observed in the CNT group is consistent with enhanced remyelination of auditory nerve fibers, which may be associated with the observed improvements in ABR thresholds and wave I latencies. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that glial cells can undergo phenotypic changes and support axonal regeneration following neural injury. 34 This aligns with the concept that the glial scar, when preserved, provides structural and molecular cues that guide donor cell migration and axon elongation. 12

Limitations and future directions

In summary, this study establishes a reproducible rat model of neural hearing loss and provides long‐term functional and histological evidence supporting early intervention with stem cell therapy. By identifying HFNCSCs as a viable donor cell population and demonstrating the superiority of cochlear nerve surface transplantation, our findings highlight the importance of aligning the delivery route with the specific pathophysiology of SGN‐related hearing loss.

Despite the promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the ouabain‐induced model represents an acute SGN injury, which differs from the chronic, multifactorial nature of most clinical SNHL cases. Future studies in progressive or age‐related hearing loss models, as well as human‐relevant platforms such as inner ear organoids, 35 will be important for translational validation. Second, we acknowledge the lack of quantitative stereological counting of surviving GFP+ cells. In the RWT group, transplanted cells were widely dispersed within cochlear fluids and formed small clusters, making accurate quantification challenging. Recent advances in molecular imaging, such as radionuclide‐labeled antibodies targeting SGN‐specific markers like VGLUT1, offer potential solutions for tracking and quantifying transplanted cells in future studies. 36 Third, the structural damage observed in the RWT group is likely technique‐related, resulting from manual microsyringe injection in the small animal model. Fortunately, such damage does not necessarily predict clinical risk, as current practice for round window injection employs high‐definition endoscopic visualization, precision pumps, and experienced surgeons. 37 Future directions for minimally invasive delivery include combined microscopic and endoscopic approaches, image‐guided injection using MRI or CT fusion, and small‐molecule tracers capable of penetrating the blood‐labyrinth barrier. 36 Finally, the precise molecular mechanisms by which transplanted HFNCSCs promote repair remain to be elucidated, and the long‐term fate and functional durability of transplanted cells require further evaluation. Addressing these questions will guide the rational development of this strategy toward clinical application. Future studies should focus on donor–host interactions, optimization of cell dosage and scaffold materials, and long‐term safety and efficacy. Ultimately, combining targeted neural delivery with biologically appropriate stem cell sources may pave the way for effective regenerative therapies for auditory neuropathy and other forms of sensorineural hearing loss.
