Coal-Derived Carbon Materials: Pathways to Graphene, Carbon Nanotubes, Carbon Quantum Dots, and Nanodiamond for Energy and Environmental Solutions
Authors: Nazimul Hoque, Binoy K Saikia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Carbon materials have garnered significant interest due to their exceptional physicochemical properties, making them suitable for a wide range of high-end applications. However, the synthesis strategies and large-scale production of these materials remain challenging, primarily due to the limited availability of suitable precursor materials and the complexity of the synthesis processes. Recent advancements have demonstrated that coal, an abundant and inexpensive carbon-rich feedstock, can be effectively utilized to develop high-quality carbon materials, such as graphene and its derivatives, carbon quantum dots, and graphene quantum dots. This review primarily emphasizes the transformation of coal into advanced carbon nanostructures such as graphene, carbon nanotubes, nanodiamonds, and carbon quantum dots, while providing brief coverage of other coal-derived carbons, including graphitic, mesoporous, heteroatom-doped, fibrous, and sheet-like carbon materials obtained from this abundant carbon-rich feedstock. It critically evaluates the advantages and limitations of these techniques and explores their technological applications, particularly in energy storage systems such as batteries and supercapacitors. Furthermore, the review highlights their environmental applications, including biosensing, metal ion detection, photocatalytic dye degradation, CO2 reduction, and hydrogen generation. By providing a comprehensive overview of the current state of research and future prospects, this review emphasizes the significant potential of coal-derived graphene and related materials for sustainable applications in energy and environmental domains.

Introduction

Coal has served as a fundamental energy source for human civilization since ancient times. Early societies used coal for heating and metalworking, recognizing its ability to produce intense heat. By the Industrial Revolution, coal had become the backbone of global energy production, powering steam engines, factories, and power plants. As one of the most abundant fossil fuels, its extensive use has played a significant role in driving global economic growth. However, with growing concerns over environmental sustainability and the depletion of fossil fuel reserves, research has increasingly focused on exploring alternative uses for coal beyond just burning it. One such possibility is the transformation of coal into high-value carbon materials, particularly graphene and its derivatives, which hold immense potential for energy storage, catalysis, and environmental applications. ,

Chemically, coal is a complex heterogeneous material mainly composed of C, H, O, N, and S, along with traces of various inorganic minerals. , The chemical structure of coal varies depending on its rank/type, with classifications ranging from lignite (low rank coal) to anthracite (high rank coal). , Thus, the chemistry of coal is crucial for determining its potential as a precursor for carbon-based materials. However, the higher carbon content and degree of aromaticity (sp2 hybridized carbon) in the coal structure make it more suitable for producing advanced carbon material. ,

Carbon material exists in various allotropes, each with distinct structural and electronic properties. Traditional forms of carbon, such as graphite and diamond, have been extensively studied and utilized for effective industrial purposes. , However, recent advancements in nanotechnology have brought novel carbon allotropes into attention such as graphene, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), carbon dots, nanodiamonds, and fullerenes, out of which, graphene, a single layer of sp2 hybridized carbon atoms arranged in a honeycomb lattice, is particularly noteworthy due to its exceptional mechanical strength, high electrical conductivity, and superior thermal stability. − These properties make graphene one of the highly desirable carbon materials for various energy and environmental applications. ,

The synthesis of graphene and its derivatives from coal precursor has emerged as a cost-effective and scalable approach. Unlike traditional methods for graphene synthesis that rely on expensive graphite precursors, coal offers a naturally abundant and economical alternative. Additionally, using coal for graphene production provides an alternative application for coal feedstock, thereby reducing its use in combustion. Several techniques have been explored for coal-derived materials synthesis, including pyrolysis, chemical vapor deposition (CVD), and chemical and thermal treatment methods. − Each approach offers distinct advantages in terms of yield, structural integrity, and functionalization possibilities.

Apart from graphene, coal can also be processed to produce hybrid carbon materials and carbon nanotubes. − Functionalization of coal-derived graphene with heteroatoms such as nitrogen (N), sulfur (S), phosphorus (P), and boron (B) enhances its electronic and catalytic properties, expanding its utility in energy storage and conversion devices. , Also, coal-based graphene hybrid materials also demonstrate superior performance in supercapacitors, lithium-ion batteries, sodium-ion batteries, and fuel cells, offering a sustainable alternative to conventional electrode materials. −

The environmental applications of coal-derived carbon materials are equally promising. These materials have been successfully employed in bio and metal sensing, photocatalytic dye degradation, and photocatalytic CO2 reduction coupled with hydrogen generation. , Such advancements highlight the dual role of coal-based carbon materials in mitigating environmental pollution while contributing to the development of clean energy solutions.

Over the past few years, a large number of articles have been published on coal-derived carbon materials with diverse applications (Figure ). However, a systematic review on the broader spectrum of coal-derived carbon materials, including nanodiamonds and their applications in energy and environmental fields such as photocatalysis and sensing, has not yet been reported.

Therefore, this review attempts to provide a comprehensive overview of various coal-derived carbon products by discussing their structural and chemical characteristics, synthesis methods, functionalization strategies, and potential applications in both energy and environmental fields. It focuses on the synthesis approaches, including thermal treatment, chemical vapor deposition, chemical processing, and electrochemical methods, while critically evaluating their advantages and limitations for the way forward. Moreover, the review explores their technological applications in energy storage systems such as batteries and supercapacitors, as well as their environmental applications in biosensing, metal ion detection, photocatalytic dye degradation, CO2 reduction, and hydrogen generation (Figure ).

Structural Integrity and Chemical Composition of Coal

The structural integrity of coal is determined by its internal arrangement of organic macerals and inorganic minerals, which form a complex porous network. , Coal has basic structural differences depending on its rank, indicating the degree of transformation that undergone in the process of coalification. Subbituminous coals and lignite are comparatively softer and have a fibrous structure, hence have lower densities and mechanical strengths. It is clearly seen that with the increase in organics and carbon content and decrease in the moisture and volatile matter, the coals become more compact, harder, and brittle due to a more cemented structure resulting from the higher organic contents. Compaction and molecular cross-linking also rise while making the coals greater mechanical strength and at the same time making it brittle. ,

Thus, the major constituent in the coal is carbon, and its amount can vary from as low as 60% in the low rank coals to as high as more than ∼90% in anthracite coal. , Others are H, O, N, and S, and their percentages also depend on the type/rank of coal. Hydrogen is mainly incorporated in hydrocarbons while oxygen is an element inherent in water and carbonyl compounds in the coal matrix. , The amount of N is low, while S-containing compounds that formed sulfur dioxide (SO2) during the process of combustion are also available in both organic and inorganic forms. The other traces of constituents include quartz-type minerals, clay minerals, carbonates minerals, and pyrites, which are major sources of ash after burning of coal. − These minerals are distributed within the coal structure and determine the mechanical strength of coal, together with its reaction characteristics during combustion and other industrial applications.

Brown coal, or lignite, generally contains 60–70% carbon and has a relatively high moisture (25–45%) and impurity content compared to those of higher rank coals. It also has impurities like S and O, complicating the conversion process. − These characteristics make it challenging for direct conversion into high-purity graphene. However, the feasibility of the production of graphene from lignite cannot be determined only by its carbon content or purity. Several studies have demonstrated that lignite, particularly through its humic acid fraction or via tailored chemical and thermal treatments, can serve as an effective precursor for high-quality graphene. In contrast, subbituminous coal has 71–77% carbon and lower moisture levels, making it more efficient for graphene production. , However, it still requires purification due to impurities. Bituminous coal, with 77–87% carbon, is a higher rank coal ideal for graphene production due to its compact structure and low impurities. , It produces high-quality graphene with fewer defects via methods like pyrolysis. Anthracite, the purest coal with >87% carbon, is perfect for high-purity graphene production due to its very low impurities. , It was used in applications requiring top-notch graphene, despite its scarcity and cost. Inclusive, different types of coal offer varying carbon content and suitability for graphene production, with anthracite being the best for high-quality applications. Although graphene and its derivatives are emphasized in this section, it is important to note that coal feedstock can also serve as a precursor for other carbon materials such as carbon nanotubes, carbon quantum dots, and nanodiamonds, which are discussed in subsequent sections.

Synthesis and Functionalization of Coal-Based Carbon Materials

Synthesis of Coal-Based Carbon Materials

Several techniques have been developed to synthesize carbon derivatives from coal, utilizing its carbon-rich content as a precursor material. These methods primarily focus on thermal and chemical processes for converting coal into high-purity graphene/graphene derivatives or other carbon materials. The most prominent techniques include pyrolysis, chemical vapor deposition (CVD), and chemical/electrochemical methods.

Pyrolysis

Pyrolysis is a thermal process that involves heating coal >700 °C in the absence of oxygen, and as a result, volatile matters are driven off, leaving a carbon-rich residue that graphitizes into graphene/graphene-like materials. It is a simple, cost-effective, and large-scale synthesis method. However, the quality of the graphene/carbon materials varies depending on the type of coal chemistry and the process conditions. Higher rank coals like bituminous and anthracite yield better results. Coal-derived carbons face the challenge of tuning the microstructure for different uses. Li et al. addressed the pyrolysis of anthracite at 1000–1400 °C and observed the reduced interlayer spacing and surface area on increasing the temperature. To simplify the synthesis, Lu et al. used a one-step carbonization of subbituminous coal at different temperatures, offering a straightforward route to control properties. Abou-Rjeily et al. tackled the influence of coal rank, showing that volatile-rich bituminous coal provided greater disorder than semi-anthracite. Thus, the carbonization temperature plays a key role in tuning interlayer spacing and crystallite size in coal-derived carbon materials. Together, these studies highlight how coal rank and processing temperature can be strategically adjusted to overcome microstructural limitations and improve the electrochemical performance. Liu et al. synthesized a pitch-derived graphene oxide (pGO) from coal tar pitch using a ZnO template and KOH, followed by heating to 800 °C under N2 atmosphere. This process not only enhances the surface area of pGO but also improves the structural regularity and coke yield of modified coal tar pitch during pyrolysis, increasing coke yield from 43.14 to 53.17%. Purwandari et al. introduced a pyrolysis synthesis process for graphene nanosheets using coal, particularly with the aid of biocatalysts, which offers an efficient and environmentally friendly approach. This method not only produces high-quality graphene but also aligns with sustainable practices in coal utilization, making it a viable option for industrial applications.

Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD)

Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) is a common method for producing high-quality graphene/graphene analogies from coal-derived carbon gases. These gases, generated by thermal decomposition above 1000 °C, are deposited on a metal surface, where they form graphene-like layers. Therefore, coal-derived gas can be a viable precursor for nanostructured carbons. Qiu et al. first synthesized single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWNTs) from coal gas using catalytic CVD with ferrocene. Structural characterization (SEM, TEM, XRD, Raman) can be satisfactorily applied to confirm the SWNT formation and also to evaluate the catalyst properties and coal gas composition, influencing the growth, solving the problem of identifying coal gas as a practical carbon source for SWNTs. Later on, Qiu et al. advanced to magnetic nanostructures, synthesizing iron carbide-oxide-filled carbon nanotubes and nanocapsules (CNCs) via CVD at 950 °C. Nano-sized carbide-oxide particles encapsulated by graphitic shells exhibited high magnetic coercivity, corrosion resistance, and stability in acid, addressing the problem of multifunctionality by integrating magnetic and structural features. This opens routes for magnetic and protective applications. The focus then shifted to improving fiber morphology and electronic functionality. Liu et al. synthesized longer and oriented vapor-grown carbon fibers (VGCFs) from coal pitch via CVD with ferrocene and hydrogen. With lengths up to 6 cm and ∼1 μm diameters, these fibers showed higher purity, graphitic features, and nonlinear resistance, solving the problem of scale and orientation for conductive applications, thereby extending coal-derived nanocarbons toward electronics. In order to increase the diversification of coal waste use, Xiao et al. fabricated CNF/carbon foam composites from coal liquefaction residue (CLR). The template-derived foam (95% porosity) provided support for Fe-catalyzed CNF growth (20–100 nm). These composites demonstrated excellent oil–water separation via hydrophobicity and capillary action, solving the problem of functionalizing coal residues into selective absorbents beyond energy storage.

The focus then shifted toward low-cost catalysts and a sustainability approach for manufacturing coal-based carbon materials. Hintsho et al. showed that South African coal fly ash could act as a natural catalyst for CNFs and CNTs via acetylene CVD at 400–700 °C. Iron species (cementite) in fly ash enabled high-yield nanofiber formation, solving the problem of catalyst cost and turning coal combustion waste into a valuable precursor for nanocarbons. Also, advanced functionality can be achieved by synthesizing N-doped CNTs (NCNTs) using CFA as a catalyst with melamine as a combined C/N source. The morphology varied with temperature (multiwalled CNTs at 800 °C, chain-like at 850 °C, bamboo-like at 900 °C). While 800 °C gave the most crystalline, stable CNTs, 850 °C provided the highest nitrogen incorporation. This solved the problem of heteroatom doping, tailoring the conductivity, stability, and electrochemical behavior of coal-derived nanocarbons for the sustainable utilization of coal feedstock.

The CVD process produces a large scale of lower defect graphene with excellent conductivity because it can control graphene thickness and quality precisely. In spite of its effectiveness, it is more complex and costly than pyrolysis. It is found that Wang et al. utilized the CVD process for synthesizing layered graphene involving multiple steps, beginning with system evacuation and backfilled with hydrogen. The furnace is then heated to 1070 °C, followed by annealing of the copper substrate. Afterward, coal char is introduced for 30 min to facilitate graphene growth on the copper foil (Figure a,b). Finally, the setup is cooled, and graphene is transferred for characterization.

Later on, Vijapur et al. developed a two-step CVD process on copper substrates for the synthesis of graphene. The method begins with annealing copper substrates, followed by flash pyrolysis of coal in a hydrogen atmosphere. In this procedure, carbon species are first adsorbed onto copper, forming an amorphous carbon film, which is subsequently graphitized to create graphene domains.

Chemical/ Electrochemical and Thermal Treatment Methods

Chemical methods involve breaking down coal into graphene particles using chemical reactions or electrical energy. One common chemical method is the oxidation of coal, where coal is treated with strong oxidizing agents such as KMnO4, H2SO4, HNO3, etc., to produce graphene oxide (GO). The GO is then reduced to graphene through thermal or chemical reduction, although this process often introduces defects in the graphene structure.

For example, Lee et al. utilized a one-pot method using HNO3 to convert coal into graphene oxide (Coal-GO), followed by thermal reduction to obtain reduced graphene oxide (Coal-rGO). In this typical procedure, anthracite coal is powdered, purified with HCl and HF, and then oxidized using 80 mL of concentrated HNO3 (16 M) at 120 °C for 5 h. The mixture is washed with deionized water, exfoliated by ultrasound in an ice bath, and centrifuged to separate unreacted particles. The resulting solution is lyophilized to obtain Coal-GO powder. Subsequently, reduced graphene oxide (Coal-rGO) is produced by heating coal-GO at 800 °C under a hydrogen/argon atmosphere (Figure a–c). Further, TEM analysis along with SAED patterns confirmed the crystalline nature of Coal-GO with curved nanoscrolls and Coal-rGO with 6-fold symmetry (Figure d,e). Pakhira et al. developed a facile method for the synthesis of graphene oxide (GO). In this method, GO can be made from low-grade coal using a simple process involving cold HNO3, which helps separate GO sheets. The synthesized GO exhibits pH-dependent structural changes, allowing it to act as a carrier for drug molecules. This property is crucial for applications in drug delivery systems, where the release of the encapsulated drug can be controlled by changing the pH.

There is an established report on a greener and more affordable way for the synthesis of graphene using humic acid sourced from low-grade coal. Since humic acid has a structure similar to graphene oxide (GO) and is rich in oxygenated groups, it can be transformed into graphene through a combination of chemical and heat treatments. This process eliminates the need for graphite and harmful chemicals, significantly lowering the environmental impact and production costs. In another report, GO is synthesized from sub-bituminous coal using a method that involves several wet-chemical steps. Initially, the coal was demineralized to remove mineral matter, which included treating the coal with hydrochloric acid (HCl) followed by hydrofluoric acid (HF) to eliminate silica. After demineralization, the coal was treated with concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4) in the presence of sodium nitrite (NaNO2) while being subjected to ultrasonication at 80 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, concentrated nitric acid (HNO3) was added to the mixture. The resulting product was then washed and centrifuged to obtain the desired graphene oxide. This method is noted for being cost-effective and suitable for bulk production of coal-based graphene in material science and technology. Leandro et al. synthesized stable GO powder from low-grade subbituminous coal and coal char using a modified Hummers’ method. Their study revealed that coal char-derived GO exhibited a higher degree of graphitization and showed favorable electrical properties. A correlation between electrical conductivity and reduced GO quality was confirmed through Raman, XRD, and XPS analyses, and emphasizes its potential in energy storage applications. Also, low-grade coals were converted into graphene-like carbon nanosheets (GCNSs) using such a wet chemical method. Zhamu and Jang developed a method to produce graphene sheets from coke or coal powder by electrochemical intercalation in a reactor. The intercalated material is then exfoliated by using ultrasonication, thermal shock, or shearing, yielding isolated graphene sheets from petroleum coke, coal, synthetic coke, or natural coal powders. Coal-based coke is also used to develop multicolored graphene quantum dots (GQDs) by electrochemical stripping using a methanol–water electrolyte. In this method, a platinum plate and a coke lump were used as the counterelectrode and the working electrode, respectively (Table ).

Coal-Based Graphene/Graphene-Derived Functional and Hybrid Materials

Surface Functionalization with Heteroatoms Like N, S, P, and B

Surface functionalization is used to further enhance its chemical reactivity, thereby improving compatibility with other materials. Such modification of coal-based graphene involves introducing functional groups such as −OH, −COOH, or −NH2 in its surface, by doping it with atoms like N, O, S, B, P, etc. These processes typically employ oxidation, reduction, or chemical treatments under different conditions. Functionalization significantly enhances the dispersion of graphene in solvents and composites, which makes it more suitable for use in polymer blends. These modifications also improve its interaction with other materials, boosting its performance in a wide range of applications.

Thomas et al. developed an eco-sustainable method to convert fossil fuel coke into highly fluorescent N-doped graphene for bioimaging. The material showed superior photophysical properties, strong fluorescence, and biocompatibility (≥85% cell viability) in cancer and normal cell imaging, demonstrating the potential for cancer diagnostics and biomedical applications. On the other hand, Li et al. emphasized the importance of nitrogen (N) and oxygen (O) doping in enhancing the electrochemical performance of hierarchical porous carbons (HPCs) derived from Xinjiang (China) low rank coals. The introduction of N and O atoms created additional active sites on the carbon surface, improving ion interaction, electrical conductivity, pseudo capacitance (PC), ion transport, surface wettability, electrolyte access, capacitance, stability, and overall electrochemical performance of the HPCs. Likewise, N and O self-doped hierarchical porous carbons (HPCs) have been synthesized from low-cost coal tar pitch (CTP) modified by fluid catalytic cracking oil (FCCO) via a coactivation and graphitization strategy using KOH and Ni. On the other hand, nitrogen-doped graphene quantum dots (N-GQDs) derived from coal to enhance the performance of carbon-based perovskite solar cells (C-PSCs). These N-GQDs contain pyridinic nitrogen, which behaves like a Lewis baseforming bonds with lead ions in the perovskite layer. This interaction helps reduce structural defects and suppresses the nonradiative recombination, resulting in smoother charge transport across the device. On a structural level, these N-GQDs were impressively smallmainly in the range of 2–4 nm, with an average diameter of about 3.55 nm. Their HRTEM images revealed distinct crystalline lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.24 nm, indicating their well-defined nanostructure (Figure ).

In another innovation, such cost-effective coal-derived nanographene oxide functionalized with octadecylamine was applied to polyethylene terephthalate (PET) fabrics. The resultant matrix had not only durable antiviral activity but also liquid repellent properties that effectively inhibit viruses such as human adenovirus type 5 and betacoronavirus. Mohanta et al. also synthesized a surface-functionalized coal with aniline that significantly increases differential charging and separation in an electric field. FTIR spectroscopy confirmed the aniline adsorption on coal, reducing the work function by 3.464%. Further, Density Functional Theory (DFT) simulations also validated the observed reduction in work function, supported by experimental data. These approaches offer a method to modify the surface properties of coal in order to make it suitable for industrial applications. Further, N, P, and S codoped graphene quantum dots (NPS-GQDs) were synthesized using anthracite coal through a simple one-pot hydrothermal and dialysis method, in which the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy confirmed the successful doping of N, P, and S atoms. The NPS-GQDs exhibited excitation-dependent luminescence and were employed for sensitive Pb2+ detection, with a detection limit of 0.75 μM in a linear range of 1–20 μM, demonstrating the potential of coal-derived NPS-GQDs for heavy metal detection, with ascorbic acid used to eliminate Fe3+ interference during Pb2+ sensing. Liu et al. developed a N and S-co-doped carbon nanotubes (NS-CNTs) using coal tar, using Dicyandiamide (DICY) as an evocating agent and cobalt chloride (CoCl2) as a catalyst. These NS-CNTs exhibit an ORR performance highly comparable to that of commercial platinum-based catalysts (Pt-C) in both acidic and alkaline media. Coal-Based Graphene/Graphene-Derived Hybrid Materials

Coal-based graphene hybrid materials have combined the properties of graphene, such as high conductivity, mechanical strength, and large surface area, with abundant and cost-effective coal as a carbon source. Hybrid materials are increasingly used in applications such as batteries and energy storage devices, including supercapacitors, due to their excellent electrochemical performance. There are few latest developments in the integration of coal-derived graphene with metals, metal oxides, or polymers, significantly enhancing composite performance, leading to superior functionality in sensors, catalysts, and environmental remediation. Such an innovative approach not only utilizes the economic advantages of coal but also fosters sustainable material development. Zhang et al. developed a novel CuO/Cu2O/coal-based reduced graphene oxide (CCO/CRGO) composite using a solvothermal self-assembly method (Figure a). Although CuO boasts high theoretical capacity, the unique porous structure and high conductivity of the composite enhance ion diffusion and reduce volume expansion, and consequently help to achieve a stable capacity of 586 mAh g–1 at 1 A g–1. This innovative CCO/CRGO composite offers a green, scalable solution for high-performance lithium-ion battery (LIB) anodes. Coal-based carbon nanomaterials have also been used for Fenton-like photocatalytic degradation of organic pollutants, and a noteworthy study revealed that coal-derived graphene oxide (CDGO) combined with silica (SiO2), i.e., GO/SiO2 nanosheet loaded with ZnO nanorods to form ZnO-GO/SiO2 composite. The resulting composite material acts as a photocatalyst that exhibits better methylene blue (MB) degradation in 40 min in the presence of H2O2 under UV light. Such property highlighted the efficiency of the material in environmental pollutant removal applications such as wastewater treatment, which will greatly help to produce cleaner ecosystems. Likewise, a TiO2/coal-based graphene aerogel (TCGA) was synthesized for efficient dye removal, which has a 3D porous structure with TiO2 nanoparticles, high strength, and low density. TCGA-40% shows excellent dye adsorption and photocatalytic decolorization, good recyclability, and it is potential for large-scale wastewater treatment. Gao et al. obtained a byproduct-free, green strategy for synthesizing reduced coal-derived graphene oxide/Mn3O4 (RCDGO/Mn3O4) composites using coal-derived graphite (CDG) through a modified Hummers method. The resulting composite, with 86% Mn3O4, exhibited high specific capacitance and energy density in the K2SO4 electrolyte (Figure b). It also showed excellent electrochemical stability, which makes it a promising supercapacitor electrode material.

The vitrinite-rich variant (NCH-V) of N-doped nanocarbon hybrids can be obtained from anthracite using graphene oxide-Co-melamine via pyrolysis, which exhibits better performance, with a graphenized structure, rich mesopores, ideal for supercapacitor electrodes and metal catalyst applications. Zhang et al. developed a porous 3D graphene silicon composite anode using humic acid (HA) from coal as a graphene precursor. The graphene foam supports silicon, offering a promising design for high-performance lithium-ion battery anodes with high conductivity. Thus, coal-based graphene hybridized materials have enough potential for the future in advanced energy device applications.

Other Coal-Derived Carbon Nanomaterials

Carbon Nanotubes (CNTs) from Coal

In 1991, Iijima made the groundbreaking discovery of carbon nanotubes (CNTs), which come in two main types, i.e., single-walled (SWCNTs) and multi-walled (MWCNTs). Since then, researchers have spent three decades developing different ways to create CNTs using coal and coal-derived materials. Methods like arc discharge, laser ablation, and chemical vapor deposition (CVD) have all been explored. Coal, being an affordable and abundant carbon source, holds great promise for the large-scale production of CNTs.

Qiu et al. synthesized high-quality single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWNTs) from cheap coal-based materials using arc discharge with iron as a catalyst. By adding a simple wire cage around the electrodes, the purity of the SWNTs improved significantly, reducing contaminants like soot. The same research group, on the other hand, produced CNTs by using the DC arc-discharge method, with factors like buffer gas pressure and coal’s carbon content affecting growth, and later on, catalytic CVD of coal gas also led to the synthesis of iron carbide oxide-filled CNTs and CNCs, which exhibited good magnetic properties and stability. , They also explored the synthesis of large-scale high-quality double-walled carbon nanotubes (DWNTs) from anthracite coal using Fe catalyst in H2-free atmosphere. Macroscopic rope-like DWNTs with well-aligned tubes with an outer diameter of 1.0–5.0 nm and an interlayer spacing of 0.41 nm. These properties demonstrate the potential of coal as an economical and effective precursor material for the large-scale synthesis of double-walled carbon nanotubes (DWNTs). In another study, super clean coal was utilized in a direct current arc technique to synthesize CNTs, instead of high-purity graphite with metal catalysts. The elimination of the costly metal catalytic process produced CNTs with a small diameter in the range 6 to 8 nm. Xu et al. synthesized Single-, double-, and triple-walled carbon nanotubes (SWNTs, DWNTs, and TWNTs) using an arc discharge method with asphalt as the carbon source and iron as a catalyst. The results showed that the buffer gas significantly affected the number of layers in the nanotubes: SWNTs were produced in a He gas atmosphere, TWNTs in an Ar gas atmosphere, and both SWNTs and DWNTs in a N2 atmosphere. This study indicates that asphalt is a promising industrial carbon source for the production of various carbon nanotubes. A scalable method for synthesizing CNTs via coal pyrolysis is also shown in Figure . A Gibbs minimization model accurately predicts the composition of CH4 and CO gases produced during pyrolysis as primary carbon sources for CNT formation. The research finding indicates that higher pyrolysis temperatures decrease the yields of CH4 and CO, which are crucial for CNT growth. Accordingly, an optimal temperature range of 400–500 °C was determined for effective CNT synthesis. Different types of coal have also been attempted for CNT synthesis, e.g., a high-quality SWCNTs in large quantities can be produced using the arc discharge method from bituminous coal with Fe/Ni–Y catalysts. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) confirmed the diameter of SWCNTs in the range of 1.2 to 1.8 nm. Besides, multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) and graphene-like nanosheets were synthesized for the first time from a coal-based electrode without any catalysts in a H2 and Ar atmosphere. Song et al. developed a large-scale production of high aspect ratio MWCNTs using a Co-catalyzed pyrolysis method with coal tar pitch in an Ar atmosphere. Their work demonstrated that the MWCNTs, with lengths around 200 μm, had an outer diameter of 40 nm and a wall thickness of 10 nm, as confirmed by SEM and TEM analyses. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations predicted that the in situ reduced Co-catalyst played a key role in facilitating the cleavage of CC and C–H bonds in ethylene (C2H4) molecules, which drove the growth of these straight nanotubes. Zhang et al. proposed a new pyrolytic method for the synthesis of CNT from coal using KOH as a catalyst precursor. In their study, FTIR analysis indicated a decrease in the number of −CH3 and −CH2 groups in coal during pyrolysis. High graphitization was observed in Raman analysis with an I G/I D ratio of 3.0, and TEM confirmed a layered structure with a diameter range of 20 to 155 nm. On the other hand, Reddy et al. demonstrated that microwave pyrolysis of coal at 560 W efficiently produces high-quality CNTs (20–120 nm) and carbon nanospheres (130–190 nm). Higher Fe content promotes ordered growth. Compared with traditional methods, microwave pyrolysis offers faster synthesis with comparable or improved yields of carbon nanostructures. Chen et al. developed CNT clusters using Fe­(NO3)3 or ferrocene with Zhundong coal/char via microwave irradiation. Fe­(NO3)3 converted to Fe2O3 and then to Fe through carbothermal reduction, while ferrocene released Fe upon sublimation at high temperatures, enabling CNT growth from the Fe-doped carbon matrix.

Plasma catalytic pyrolysis has also been effectively utilized to produce low-purity multiwalled CNTs from lignite with a yield up to ∼5% by using 5% Cu catalyst. Wu et al. synthesized “bamboo-like” CNTs from Xinjiang Kuche coal using a Ni-FeSO4 catalyst, achieving yields of 10.36, 12.08, and 22.64% in Ar, N2, and He atmospheres, respectively. On the other hand, it was illustrated that Co particles affect local electric fields, promoting carbon pentagonal structures and influencing CNT yield from coal. During the pyrolytic process of coal, KOH broke down into K2CO3, which, along with metals like Fe, Co, and Ni in the coal, helped produce coal-based CNTs. The addition of more KOH reduced the amount of tar produced and increased alkane gas, both of which were conducive to the synthesis of CNTs.

Nanodiamond from Coal

Nanodiamonds, as another variety of nanocarbon, consist of a rigid sp3-bonded diamond lattice, whereas CDs and GQDs are mainly sp2-carbon domains, with CDs being quasi-spherical amorphous clusters and GQDs as few-layer graphene fragments with hexagonal network structures, which catch the eye of researchers. Nanodiamonds (NDs) are a unique class of nanocarbons with promising biomedical applications due to their small size (∼4–5 nm), high biocompatibility, and ease of surface functionalization. Its synthesis becomes worthy when it is enriched from cost-effective sources like coal.

Xiao et al. utilized coal, the most abundant and inexpensive carbon source, to synthesize nanodiamonds via a simple, green, top-down laser ablation method in liquid at room temperature and atmospheric pressure. This process yields 3 nm cubic phase nanodiamonds with strong, stable fluorescence in water and alcohol. The study also explores the phase transformation mechanisms from coal to nanodiamonds through nucleation thermodynamics and growth kinetics. Saikia’s group at CSIR-NEIST, Jorhat, on the other hand, successfully converted the low-grade Northeast Indian coal as a cheap feedstock to synthesize nanodiamonds through low-power ultrasonic-assisted treatment in hydrogen peroxide, followed by dialysis. Characterization techniques confirm the formation of 4–5 nm carbon nanocrystals with a lattice spacing of 2.0–2.3 Å that matches diamond phases. The resulting nanodiamonds exhibit bright blue fluorescence, showing promise for bioimaging and optoelectronic applications. In another study, biocompatible nanodiamonds (NDs) from coal washery rejects (CWRs) were synthesized with a 2–3.5 nm range that showed blue fluorescent particles with a cubic diamond structure and oxygen-rich surface functionalities. These NDs demonstrated strong antiviral activity (99.3% inhibition, IC50 = 7.664 μg mL–1) and moderate antioxidant properties. Toxicity tests on wheatgrass showed minimal impact, highlighting the potential of CWR-derived NDs for biomedical and antiviral applications.

Applications of Coal-Derived Carbon Materials

Application in Energy Sectors

With coal being abundant and affordable, it has become an attractive carbon source for graphene and graphene derivative synthesis. Graphene derived from cheap coal precursor stands out for its excellent electrical conductivity, large surface area, and strong chemical stability, making it perfect for various energy storage applications such as supercapacitors, hybrid capacitors, fuel cells, or batteries; coal-based graphene can significantly boost performance. Interestingly, it can be used in both anodes and cathodes, making it a key material in the development of more efficient and sustainable energy storage solutions for the future.

Supercapacitors

Supercapacitors, also called ultracapacitors, are energy storage devices that bridge the gap between capacitors and batteries. , They have rapid charge-discharge cycles, high power density, and a long cycle life. A supercapacitor stores energy through electrostatic charge separation, so it can deliver energy more quickly than batteries. Electronics devices and renewable energy systems use these supercapacitors for quick bursts of power. ,

He et al. synthesized morphologically honeycomb-like porous carbons (HPCs) from coal tar pitch utilizing melamine as a soft template with KOH activation, as shown in Figure (I)­a,b. The HPCs exhibit an excellent surface area of 2038 m2 g–1. As supercapacitor electrodes, they show a specific capacitance of 221 F g–1 at 0.05 A g–1 in 6 M KOH electrolyte, with 81.0% capacitance retention at 20 A g–1 and excellent cycle stability of 95.3% retention even after 5000 cycles (Figure (II)­a–d). On the other hand, an acid-free method was reported for synthesizing 3D, N,O-co-doped egg-box-like carbons (EBCs) from coal tar pitch (CTP) for supercapacitors. The EBCs have a 3D interconnected structure with open pores and N, O functional groups, achieving an areal capacitance of 39.8 μF cm–2 at 0.106 mA cm–2 in an alkaline KOH electrolyte. As a supercapacitor electrode, EBC shows high capacitance and excellent cycle stability, with only 1.9% decay after 50,000 cycles, making it promising for sustainable energy storage solutions. Mesoporous carbons (MCs) can also be successfully synthesized from coal tar pitch using a microwave-assisted one-step method integrating KOH activation and MgO as a template. The MC achieved 224 F g–1 capacitance in 6 M KOH after 1000 cycles. In a related study, they also employed a similar method for the synthesis of porous carbons (PCs) that significantly reduced heating time and KOH use and achieved superior supercapacitor performance compared to conventional methods. The same research group, otherwise, utilized a novel hydroxide template strategy with in situ chemical activation and produced porous carbon nanosheets (PCNSs) from coal tar that show better supercapacitor performance with capacitance up to 296.2 F g–1 in 6 M KOH electrolyte. Likewise, Geng et al. synthesized a 2D hierarchical porous carbon sheets (HPCs) using a nano-MgO template and coal tar pitch with KOH activation. Their study revealed that the pitch to KOH weight ratio significantly influences the porosity of the carbon matrix. FHPCs exhibit a high specific surface area (1454–1604 m2 g–1) and excellent electrochemical performance, achieving capacitance up to 290 F g–1.

The combination of coal tar and melamine has been used in a novel strategy involving space confinement and self-sacrificed template coupling with in situ chemical activation for synthesizing layered microporous carbon materials (LMPCs). Melamine and 2D g-C3N4 function as templates to facilitate this conversion. The resulting LMPCs exhibited a layered microporous structure that enhances ion diffusion and accessibility to electrolytes with a high capacitance (274 F g–1), excellent rate performance, and remarkable cycle stability. This approach offers industrial potential for low-cost supercapacitor materials. Wang et al. have established a novel method for synthesizing mesoporous carbon sheet-like frameworks (MCSFs) using mesoporous SiO2 nanosheets and coal tar pitch. The resulting MCSFs exhibit a high specific surface area of 582.7 m2 g–1 and a maximum capacitance of 264 F g–1, as well as excellent cycling stability. The unique structure of MCSFs enables rapid ion diffusion and electron transport, making them an attractive material for supercapacitor applications. A novel method was established to synthesize asphaltene supermolecules from coal, leveraging their condensed polyaromatic units and aliphatic chains as a high-carbon natural source. This breakthrough demonstrates the potential of asphaltene as a carbon precursor for nanosheets through self-assembly with graphene oxide, with thicknesses of 13 ± 3 to 41 ± 5 nm, which exhibit electrical conductivity of around 450 S m–1 and a specific capacitance of 163 F g–1, highlighting their suitability for energy material preparation. On the other hand, an innovative approach has demonstrated the potential of asphaltene from coal liquefaction residue as a precursor for carbon materials via urea thermal polymerization and subsequent KOH activation. The resulting porous carbon nanosheets exhibit a graphitized-like structure, making them suitable for supercapacitor applications. Interestingly, 3D porous carbon materials derived from 2D sheets show promise as supercapacitor electrodes. This study introduces 3D interconnected sheet-like porous carbons (ISPCs) created from inexpensive coal tar using a confined ionic liquid soft template method with KOH activation. These interdigitated Supercapacitor Cells (ISPCs) exhibit thin nanosheets and a high specific surface area of 1593 m2 g–1. Their excellent electrochemical performance is evident in their specific capacitance of 314 F g–1 and their outstanding cycle stability. Microporous carbon/graphene composites (MPC/Gs) from coal tar pitch also showed excellent electrochemical performance and are ideal for electrode material for supercapacitors. In our study, coal-derived porous carbon material was prepared via simple chemical activation for high-performance pouch cell supercapacitors. The material synthesized showed high surface area (1480 m2 g–1) and excellent electrochemical performance across various types of electrolytes. Subsequently, the fabricated pouch cell achieved 4.5 Wh kg–1 energy and 736 Wk g–1 power, with 90% stability over 10,000 cycles. In a similar way, Wang et al. developed a self-template method to synthesize HPCs from bituminous coal. The heavy component kaolinite forms natrolite, acting as a template, and the dense medium component enhances its utilization. Optimal HPC-0.4 has a high surface area of 3851 m2 g–1, excellent conductivity, and impressive supercapacitor performance with 436 F g–1 at 0.1 A g–1 (Figure ). The method provides an affordable method for producing HPCs from coal. A hybrid flexible supercapacitor electrode was also reported to be produced from coal-based carbon nanofiber (CBCNF) paper and polyaniline (PANI) nanowires through rapid mix polymerization. The best electrode prepared with 0.15 M aniline exhibits a specific capacitance of 458 F g–1 at 0.5 A g–1 and retains 87% capacitance after 1000 cycles at 2 A g–1. This performance is due to the synergistic effects and nanostructure of CBCNFs and PANI nanowire arrays. Biocoal was also effectively utilized in a sustainable approach to convert into graphitized activated carbon (BCA) for supercapacitors, which showed a high specific capacitance (153.33 F g–1) and low ESR (0.28 Ω), with pouch cells retaining 90% capacitance and lighting 20 LEDs for 21 min. The same research group at CSIR-NEIST, on the other hand, developed a high-performance coin cell supercapacitor using coal-based carbon and molasses as binder. The resultant material exhibited 127 F g–1 capacitance and 92% retention over 10,000 cycles. Low-grade coal from Northeast India was proven to be suitable for conversion into valuable graphene derivatives using a simple chemical method and ultracentrifugation. The synthesized few-layered graphene showed excellent electrochemical performance, including a specific capacitance of 95.65 F g–1 .

Coal-derived carbon materials, such as hard carbon, soft carbon, and carbon nanosheets, have been extensively investigated as high-performance anodes for lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). For instance, Fujimoto et al. investigated hard carbon derived from oxygen-containing coal tar pitch, revealing a poorly ordered framework that required further optimization. Guo et al. then prepared hard carbon via a simple oxidation-carbonization route, producing microporous structures with graphite-like microcrystallites. This material showed 576 mAh g–1 with 69.5% initial efficiency, retained 350.8 mAh g–1 after 30 cycles, and significantly outperformed commercial graphitized mesocarbon microbeads at high rates. To improve conductivity and rate capability, soft carbon development from coal-based mesophase pitch under vacuum conditions was also reported. The resulting material, with smaller nanocrystallites, higher porosity, and defect-rich frameworks, showed enhanced energy density and rate performance compared to the nitrogen carbonized analogy. Liu et al. further explored hard–soft carbon composites by introducing coal tar pitch into polyacrylonitrile-based nanofibers. The resulting composite balanced electron transport and Li+ diffusivity, achieving 53.7% capacity retention from 0.1 to 5 A g–1 and exceptional long-term stability with 99.7% retention after 1000 cycles, demonstrating the importance of optimizing microstructure for high rate performance. More recently, attention has shifted toward two-dimensional nanosheets with heteroatom doping. Wang et al. prepared carbon nanosheets (CNSs) by a space-confined carbonization method using expanded vermiculite as a template from glucose and coal tar pitch. The CNSs have structures of multilayer graphene, hierarchical pores, and a rich in heteroatoms. As the anodes for LIBs, the CTP-CNSs present 1147 mAh g–1 at 0.05 A g–1, 510 mAh g–1 at 2 A g–1, and 623 mAh g–1 with retention of 99.7% Coulombic efficiency after 500 cycles, as depicted in Figure a–e.

Wang et al. reported nitrogen-doped porous carbon nanosheets from Victorian brown coal, achieving 604.7 mAh g–1 at 50 mA g–1 with 97.4% capacity retention over 1000 cycles. Sun et al. further advanced this approach with sulfur-doped nanosheets from lignite, delivering 1620 and 901 mAh g–1 after 500 cycles, aided by their high surface area and active sites. Extending applications beyond LIBs, Li et al. employed asphaltene from coal liquefaction residue to construct a sandwich-like SC@EG composite, which demonstrated high capacity and cycling stability in both lithium and potassium ion batteries.

Graphite is the most widely used variety of anode material for lithium-ion batteries (LIBs). It is stable, efficient for lithium intercalation, and relatively affordable. However, natural graphite has drawbacks, and petroleum-based synthetic graphite raises cost and supply concerns. This has turned attention to coal, an abundant and inexpensive carbon source, as a promising feedstock for synthetic graphite. Over the years, research has moved from simple high-temperature graphitization to catalytic, green, and composite-based approaches, each improving performance and sustainability. A subsequent study in 2010 and 2011 by Cameán et al. used anthracite and fly ash subjected to high temperature treatment (2400–2800 °C). , This process produced graphite with ∼250–310 mAh g–1 capacity and stable cycling, confirming the feasibility of coal residues as anode materials. Coke powder can be improved by boron doping and graphitization, enhancing reversible capacity (360.3 mAh g–1 vs 292.9 mAh g–1 for undoped). Han et al. further boosted rate capability using coal tar pitch coatings, reducing charge transfer resistance. Zhong et al. utilized anthracite and semicoke to develop a scalable, eco-friendly graphitization strategy to convert open-shell hollow multilayer graphene spheres using nickel as a catalyst at low temperatures. Subsequently, these graphene spheres exhibit high reversible capacities of 389.8 and 401.4 mAh g–1 at 0.1 A g–1 and show excellent cycling stability as lithium-ion battery anodes. The study also proposes underlying graphitization mechanisms and offers an efficient route for large-scale graphene-based anode production from carbonaceous materials. Xing et al. developed a cost-effective, eco-friendly method to produce porous graphene from anthracite using graphitization, liquid oxidation, and rapid thermal reduction. The synthesized graphene consists of uninterrupted corrugated nanosheets with hierarchical porous morphology, large surface area (640 m2 g–1), and large pore volume (3.792 cm3 g–1). Such features provide more active sites and channels for fast lithium-ion transfer. As a lithium-ion battery anode, it provides a large reversible capacity of 770 mAh g–1 at 0.1 C and maintains 224 mAh g–1 at 20 C. It also exhibits strong cycling stability with a 98% capacity retention for 110 cycles (Figure a–e).

Catalytic graphitization is currently catching the eye of researchers. Since it can significantly lower energy barriers, thereby lowering the graphitization temperature. For instance, rare earth oxides were used for reducing graphitization temperature and delivering 337 mAh g–1 with excellent cycling. Shi et al. used a catalyst that promotes aromatic layer growth, reduces cross-linking, and lowers the energy barrier for graphitization. As a Li-ion anode, it supplied 369 mAh g–1 (FCE 85.3%) at 0.05 C and 160 mAh g–1 at 3 C. At 0.5 C, it retained over 300 mAh g–1 with a 98% capacity after 500 cycles. Li et al. applied Si powder as a catalyst, producing graphite with 91.5% graphitization and 92% retention after 200 cycles. Similarly, bituminous coal-derived graphite with high order, mesoporosity, and 310 mAh g–1 capacity was also used in a lithium-ion battery. Han et al. removed nanopores through catalytic graphitization and produced 347 mAh g–1 capacity with no fading over 1000 cycles. Wang et al. compared raw vs demineralized coke, with raw coke showing superior performance (301 mAh g–1 at 0.1 C).

Green and sustainable routes were also established through converting the inferior coal into crystalline graphite via molten CaCl2 electrolysis, delivering 510 mAh g–1. Paul et al. highlighted domestic coal for synthetic graphite, coupling performance evaluation with multi-species, multireaction modeling to predict behavior and address supply chain security. Beyond graphite, coal-derived composites extend applications. Zhang et al. developed 3D graphene-Si foams from humic acid, achieving high capacity (656 mAh g–1) and 90% retention after 300 cycles, which signified the versatility of coal-derived graphite for advanced anodic materials applications.

Besides, composite and morphologically designed carbon materials using coal-based feedstock were also studied for LIB. A core-shell humic-acid-based carbon composite (CGHA) via liquid phase coating and carbonization at 900 °C reveals the lithium ion intercalation, conductivity, and structural stability and exhibits excellent electrochemical performance with a discharge capacity of 597.1 mAh g–1 and a charge capacity of 463.0 mAh g–1 at 0.1 C, with an initial Coulombic efficiency of 77.5%. It maintains a stable specific capacity of 251.7 mAh g–1 at 2 C after 50 cycles and 173.5 mAh g–1 at 5 C, indicating its excellent rate capability and durability. This makes CGHA a promising and cost-effective LIB anode material. Qiu et al. synthesized morphologically onion-like porous graphite from anthracite for LIB anode applications. Their study revealed that the graphite exhibited outstanding electrochemical performance, as long as it is cost-effective for the utilization of anthracite. Besides, the structure properties were developed by Fe2O3, and porosity and lithium-ion diffusion were increased by oxidation, heat treatment, and KOH activation. A unique 3D-flower-like heterostructure was developed by intercalating graphene derived from coal into MoS2 through hydrothermal processing and calcination. The resultant materials, as lithium ion node materials, showed remarkable activity with an initial discharge capacity of ∼929 mAh g–1 and a capacity of ∼813 mAh g–1 with a Coulombic efficiency of ∼99% after 150 cycles at a constant current density of 100 mAh g–1. Recently, Xing et al. fabricated a scalable method to simplify a binder-free LIB anode from coal-derived carbon fibers (CCFs) via electrospinning and carbonization of bituminous coal and polyacrylonitrile. At 700 °C, CCFs showed a dense, smooth structure with a diameter of 177 nm and a surface area of 7.2 m2 g–1 surface area. The 3D framework enhanced flexibility, strength, and conductivity. CCFs-700, rich in oxygen and nitrogen functional groups, accomplished a reversible capacity of 631.4 mAh g–1 and retained 400.1 mAh g–1 after 120 cycles.

Therefore, coal-derived carbons are emerging as strong alternatives for LIB anodes. Hard as well as soft carbons from coal feedstock improved the capacity and rate performance through microstructural tuning. Catalytic and green graphitization has lowered energy costs and made production more scalable. Nanosheets with heteroatom doping offered more active sites and better stability. Binder-free fibers and hybrid structures add flexibility and high capacity. These studies demonstrate coal as a cheap, abundant, and sustainable feedstock for use in advanced and high-performance lithium-ion batteries.

Sodium Ion Batteries

On the other hand, sodium ion batteries (SIBs) offer a cost-effective, safe, and abundant alternative to lithium ion batteries, with potential for large-scale energy storage in recent years. However, the high cost of hard carbon, the primary anode material, limits its application. Therefore, coal, with its global availability and diverse structural characteristics across metamorphic grades, presents a low-cost alternative for anode materials in SIBs (Table ).

Moving toward structural optimization, Wang et al. synthesized porous carbon and coal-based graphene from anthracite using NaCl templating, NaOH activation, and high temperature annealing. These porous frameworks enabled efficient ion transport and delivered long-term cycling stability, with capacities above 150 mAh g–1 after 1000 cycles. This established the porosity and graphitic character as key design parameters. To solve the conductivity and ion diffusion, Gao et al. developed hierarchically porous nanofibers (HPCCNFs) with expanded interlayer spacing (0.386 nm) and nitrogen doping. These structural features improved electron/ion transport and active site density, giving excellent rate performance (121.7 mAh g–1 at 5 A g–1) and stability across 1000 cycles. Complementing this, Jiang et al. introduced anthracite-based expanded graphite (AEG) with capacitive sodium storage behavior. AEG delivered 326 mAh g–1 at low rates, maintained 88 mAh g–1 even at 10 A g–1, and retained 85% capacity after 1000 cycles, solving the challenge of fast kinetics.

Flexibility and binder-free electrode design became the next challenge. Guo et al. incorporated coal-based carbon dots into electrospun nanofiber films, creating a 3D conductive framework. These binder-free anodes delivered 223 mAh g–1 with 96% retention over 400 cycles, enabled by strong ion/electron transport. Zhang et al. further solved the bottleneck of low softening point coal pitch by developing Biassisted electrospun hard carbon nanofibers, which remained flexible and stable with 78.4% retention after 1500 cycles in sodium dual-ion batteries. Similarly, Qin et al. introduced CoO x into coal-derived nanofibers, achieving 320 mAh g–1 with strong cycling durability, addressing active material utilization.

At the same time, hybrid and graphene-based strategies emerged. Wang et al. synthesized coal-derived reduced graphene oxide (CrGO) for sodium ion batteries (SIBs) as an anode using a modified Hummers high-temperature method. The synthesized higher rank coal-based graphite (CG) showed better graphitization and easier exfoliation, and CrGO exhibited an initial capacity of 331 mAh g–1 at 0.03 A g–1 and retained 123 mAh g–1 after 1000 cycles at 1 A g–1 with 91.8% retention (Figure a–f).

Liu et al. developed a graphene-coated coal liquefaction residue-derived hard carbon (HC@G), where wrinkled graphene sheets enhanced ion transport and cycling stability, maintaining 83% capacity after 2000 cycles. Porous carbons (PCs) derived from anthracite further demonstrated stable performance, maintaining 175.3 mAh g–1 after 100 cycles and minimal fading over 1000 cycles. Li et al. prepared hard carbon from Shenfu bituminous coal by one-step pyrolysis and used coal-based graphene oxide (CGO) as a conductive binder. Compared with traditional binders, CGO improved reversible capacity and rate capability. This dual coal utilization offers a simple and efficient strategy for advanced sodium ion battery anodes.

Moreover, coal-derived carbons have recently attracted attention due to their dual behavior as potential anode materials for both lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) and sodium-ion batteries (SIBs). This versatility is mainly attributed to their adjustable microstructure, interlayer spacing, and defect density, which can be tailored through carbonization and surface modification. Abou-Rjeily et al. demonstrated that raw and annealed high volatile bituminous coal could serve as low-cost anodes for both SIBs and LIBs. Thermal annealing between 800 and 1200 °C altered the morphology and structure, with nitrogen-treated samples at 800 °C showing the best results. Electrochemical tests confirmed reversible capacities of 223 mAh g–1 for sodium and 409 mAh g–1 for lithium, proving that even mining residues could be repurposed into effective battery materials. Tian et al. studied low-temperature carbonization of bituminous coal to understand structural evolution in disordered carbons. Below 1000 °C, interlayer spacing and defects increased, while at 1000–1600 °C, both decreased. Optimal performance was achieved at 1000 °C for LIBs (384 mAh g–1) and at 1200 °C for SIBs (270 mAh g–1, 86.8% ICE).

Thus, Graphitic carbons derived from coal offer promising anode candidates for both LIBs and SIBs because of their tunable structural features, including porosity and interlayer spacing. With the advancement of doping, hybrid frameworks, and flexible electrode architectures, the electrochemical performance of these materials has been further enhanced. With its dual applicability across Li+ and Na+ battery systems, coal displays potential as a next-generation, sustainable energy storage precursor.

Fuel Cells

A direct carbon fuel cell (DCFC) is an advanced electrochemical device that directly converts solid carbon fuels like coal into electricity with high efficiency (70–80%). It operates by oxidizing carbon at the anode and reducing oxygen at the cathode, producing only CO2, which can be easily captured. The DCFC holds great potential for cleaner power generation in sustainable energy systems. The use of coal materials in fuel cell research is very frightening; however, it holds excellent potential for the future.

The influence of thermally decomposed gases on the anodic reaction of DCFC using three raw coal samples and their corresponding coal chars was studied by Eom et al. At temperatures above 500 °C, pyrolysis or partial gasification releases hydrogen and hydrocarbons, enhancing the electrochemical reactions. In a study, Australian coals were evaluated in a carbon fuel cell (CFC) without chemical pretreatment, achieving power densities over 290 mW cm–2 using a scandia–zirconia electrolyte with Ce0.9Gd0.1O2–Ag electrodes. The cell operated for over 10 h with no observed reactivity between electrodes and fuel, highlighting the potential for cost-effective coal utilization in CFCs. The performance of a DCFC using Shenfu bituminous coal at 650–850 °C reveals that coal composition, microstructure, and ash content significantly impact the efficiency of fuel cells. It is to be mentioned that the pyrolysis gases like H 2 , CO, and short hydrocarbons improve the performance of the material, while a higher ash content reduces it, with a peak power density of 31.3 mW cm–2 achieved without a carrier gas. The use of a ternary carbonate-samarium-doped ceria (LNK-SDC) as electrolyte and as LiNiCuZnFeO (LNCZFO) electrode in a direct carbon-based fuel cell (DCFC) was satisfactorily explored for efficient energy conversion. Among the tested fuels, sub-bituminous coal achieved the highest power density of 58 mW cm–2, with higher carbon and lower ash content being key factors for improved performance.

Environmental Applications

Bio and Metal Sensing

Coal-based graphene and carbon derivatives hold great potential for both biosensing and environmental applications due to their unique structural and electrochemical properties. In biosensing, coal-derived graphene oxide (GO) and reduced graphene oxide (rGO) offer high surface area, conductivity, and available functional groups for interaction with biomolecules, which makes them suitable for rapid and sensitive detection. For environmental applications, coal-based carbon materials exhibit excellent adsorption capabilities for pollutants, efficient photocatalytic dye degradation, and water purification potential. Apart from these, their cost-effectiveness, scalability, and eco-friendly synthesis methods make them ideal candidates for sustainable biosensors, wastewater treatment, and air purification technologies.

Lee et al. presented a one-pot process using HNO3 to synthesize graphene oxide (Coal-GO) from coal, effectively removing aliphatic compounds while preserving graphene domains. Coal-GO was reduced to Coal-rGO, yielding nanosheets (300–700 nm). Surface analysis showed nitrogen retention and a high COOH content. Oxidative scissoring mainly affects coal edges with minimal c-direction swelling. Compared to graphite-GO, Coal-GO exhibits superior interaction with DNA aptamers, enhancing biosensor sensitivity. In their study, Huang et al. demonstrated that coal could serve as a new cost-effective alternative to graphene electrode material for electrochemical sensing, with performance comparable to graphene. The resultant edge-plane-like defective sites enhance electron-transfer efficiency and exhibit strong electrocatalytic activity for detecting ruin, with a broad linear range (0.001–10 μmol dm–3) and a low detection limit (0.2 nmol dm–3). Moreover, it effectively detected biomolecules, such as ascorbic acid, dopamine, uric acid, guanine, and adenine. CQDs were also synthesized via green ozone oxidation of lignite coal, producing well-dispersed particles with an average diameter of ∼2.8 nm. The CQDs, rich in oxygen functional groups, exhibited good water solubility and a selective quenching effect on Fe3+. Their fluorescence intensity showed a linear response to Fe3+ (10–150 μmol/L) with a detection limit of 0.26 μmol/L. Likewise, carbon quantum dots (CQDs) were also synthesized via ozone treatment of coal tar pitch, with a smaller size range of 3–7 nm and strong luminescence properties. The material acted as fluorescent probes for Fe3+ detection with high sensitivity (0–9 μM range and 0.02 μM limit). Besides, CQDs also showed low toxicity and strong fluorescence in cell imaging, making them suitable for biosensing applications. Kuma et al. developed graphene oxide from bituminous coal via modified Hummer’s method with a graphene modified electrode (GME) for sensitive caffeine detection. The electrochemical studies showed excellent electrocatalytic activity for caffeine oxidation, with a detection range of 0.2–120 μmol/L and a 90 nmol/L limit. GME demonstrated high sensitivity and selectivity for caffeine in soft drinks, making it suitable for sensor applications. Coal-derived humic acid-based carbon dots (HA-CDs) were prepared for Cu2+ detection in water. These resultant materials with nanodot-like (size ∼7.6 nm) morphology have well-dispersed, high sp2 carbon content, and maintain stable fluorescence with different pH levels. They selectively detect Cu2+ with a linear response (0–40 μmol/L) and a 0.014 μmol/L detection limit, making them ideal for water monitoring (Figure ).

Coal-based fluorescent carbon dots (CDs) produced by using carbonization and acidic oxidation etching result in two emission modes, with short wavelength fluorescence enhanced by partial reduction. Fluorescence originated from sp2 carbon domains and surface defects. The reduced CDs showed high selectivity for Cu (II) detection with a limit of 2.0 nM, highlighting their promising sensing potential. Also, top-down strategies were adopted to produce lignite-based fluorescent nanocarbons using acidic oxidation and chemical reflux to form oxygenated nanocarbon dots and graphene sheets. These structures exhibit stable fluorescence across all pH conditions and enable label-free, selective glucose detection with a 0.125 mM limit.

Photocatalytic Dye Degradation

Coal-derived carbon-based materials, such as activated carbon, graphene, carbon dots, and carbon nanotubes, offer a cost-effective and sustainable platform for photocatalytic degradation of toxic dyes in wastewater. Due to their higher surface area, excellent conductivity, and tunable surface chemistry, coal-based carbon materials are effective either as standalone photocatalysts or as supports for metal-/semiconductor-based systems. Upon light irradiation, these catalysts generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) that break down dye molecules into harmless byproducts. This approach not only utilizes low-cost coal precursors but also provides an eco-friendly solution for industrial wastewater treatment.

Shaida et al. explored an alternative use of pyrite-rich coal resources for sustainable hydrogen peroxide and hydroxyl radical generation via a solar light-induced photo-Fenton process, facilitating azo-dye degradation. Their study revealed that Basic Blue 41 showed a higher degradation rate (∼99%) under the optimized conditions. Coal-based carbon membrane anode was coupled with an electric field in order to efficiently remove malachite green via electrochemical anodic oxidation. This process enhances permeate flux, with degradation occurring through N-demethylation and cleavage of the conjugated structure, ultimately converting intermediates into CO2 and H2O. Coal-based magnetically activated carbons (MACs) prepared by using a two-stage carbonization and activation process with Fe2O3 are reported to achieve a high surface area (2075 m2 g–1) and strong magnetization (15.02 emu g–1). These MACs efficiently adsorb methylene blue (MB) with a maximum capacity of 871 mg g–1, following pseudo-second order kinetics and the Freundlich model. Their magnetic properties enable easy separation and recovery, making them a promising, low-cost adsorbent for organic dye removal. In another finding, coal-based carbon dots (C-Dots) with a graphene-like structure were developed from Xinjiang Wucaiwan coal using an acid/ultrasonic oxidation technique and combined with in situ synthesized TiO2 to form C-Dots/TiO2 nanoparticles. The composite exhibited a reduced band gap (2.9 eV), enhanced visible light photocatalytic activity, and superior degradation of organic pollutants compared to pure TiO2 due to the synergistic effect of C-Dots. Thus, coal can be a new carbonaceous raw material to synthesize eco-friendly and cost-effective nano-activated carbon (NAC) for efficient methylene blue (MB) dye removal in industrial applications. The NAC exhibited a high adsorption capacity (28.09 mg g–1), spherical morphology (38 nm), and excellent reusability, making it a promising material for water purification applications. It was highlighted that porous coal-based activated carbon (AC) with a higher surface area of 3452.8 m2 g–1 can be used for efficient azo dye adsorption, achieving a maximum uptake of 333 mg g–1 under acidic conditions. The adsorption process was spontaneous, endothermic, and followed pseudo-second order kinetics, with DFT analysis confirming the most effective adsorption configuration through enthalpy change and atomic charge transfer. Jawad et al. upgraded Malaysian low rank Merit Kapit (MK) coal into mesoporous activated carbon (MKAC) via microwave-assisted H3PO4 activation, achieving a surface area of 848.7 m2 g–1. The optimized MKAC exhibited an 86.6% methylene blue removal efficiency, following the Freundlich isotherm and second-order kinetics, with adsorption driven by electrostatic, hydrogen bonding, and π–π interactions. The synthesis of coal tar pitch-derived carbon quantum dots (CQDs) has been reported via ball milling-assisted H2O2 oxidation and combined with Ag3PO4 and g-C3N4 to form Ag3PO4/g-C3N4/CQDs composites for efficient dye degradation. The optimized catalyst exhibited an 11-fold increase in the methyl orange degradation rate compared to g-C3N4, attributed to a Z-scheme heterostructure with CQDs as an electron bridge, enhancing surface area and band gap properties for improved visible light photocatalysis. Coal-based activated carbon (CBAC) satisfactorily adsorbs methylene blue (MB) from high salt printing and dyeing wastewater, achieving a maximum removal rate of 90% at optimal conditions. While high salt concentrations (20 g L–1 NaCl) reduced the adsorption capacity by 1.8 mg g–1, the presence of Cu2+ and Fe3+ had a greater impact on MB removal, though their inhibitory effect decreased in high salt environments. Notably, Hu et al. developed a green and cost-effective top-down approach to synthesize fluorescent carbon dots (CDs) from coal, avoiding hazardous oxidizing acids and complex purification steps. In addition to conventional combustion-based applications, these high-yield CDs exhibited strong photocatalytic activity for organic dye degradation under visible light, demonstrating the potential for coal to be used for environmental remediation, solar energy conversion, and energy storage beyond traditional combustion-based applications. On the other hand, coal tar pitch-derived carbon quantum dots (CQDs) prepared via ball milling were incorporated into Ag3PO4/Ni-MOF/CQDs composites for efficient phenol degradation. The optimized Ag-Ni-70/CQDs-0.45 catalyst achieved a 98% degradation rate in 30 min, benefiting from a Z-scheme heterojunction that enhanced charge separation and electron transport, ensuring high photocatalytic performance and stability across various conditions. Coal-based carbon membrane was developed and integrated with sulfate radical-based advanced oxidation processes (SR-AOPs) for efficient phenol degradation, achieving 100% removal under optimal conditions. The process utilized both radical and nonradical mechanisms, offering a green and effective approach for wastewater treatment, applying such coal-derived carbon membrane. It was observed that the N and F codoped TiO2 via the solvothermal method and then loaded with coal-based hierarchical porous carbon foam (HPCF) enhanced the photocatalytic degradation of phenol. In this case, N doping enhanced the photoresponse of TiO2 toward visible light, while F doping improved UV-driven activity. The macroporous HPCF structure maximized light exposure, and its mesopores enhanced adsorption capacity. The (N, F)–TiO2/HPCF composite exhibited the highest photocatalytic activity and stability, demonstrating great potential for wastewater treatment. In a study, CdS was loaded with coal-based activated carbon nanofibers (CBACFs) via electrospinning and solvothermal methods for the enhancement of photocatalytic degradation of methyl blue under visible light. Their characterization confirmed the homogeneous dispersion of CdS on CBACFs, which served as a low-cost support material with improved photocatalytic efficiency compared to pure CdS. Liu et al. also introduced coal-based rGO-hTiO2 composites for degrading Rhodamine B and methyl orange under visible light. The superior performance was attributed to enhanced adsorption, a narrowed TiO2 band gap, and efficient electron–hole separation due to the Ti–O–C bond formation. These findings extensively highlight the potential of coal-derived rGO-TiO2 composites for photocatalytic wastewater treatment. Hydrothermal reduction and freeze-drying techniques are also utilized to develop cost-effective TiO2/coal-based graphene aerogel (TCGA) in order to use in photocatalytic applications. The 3D porous structure with well-distributed TiO2 enhances dye adsorption and photocatalytic activity, and the optimized TCGA-40% achieved high dye removal capacities and maintained 81.0% efficiency after four cycles. This study offers a scalable approach to wastewater treatment using TiO2/graphene aerogel composites. Zhang et al. introduced a novel CTP/TiO2 composite photocatalyst by using coal tar pitch (CTP) as a natural alternative to carbon quantum dots (CQDs) through a simple solvothermal method. The composite exhibited significantly superior visible-light-driven photocatalytic degradation of Rhodamine B, with an apparent degradation rate constant 23 times higher than that of pure TiO2 (Figure ).

Photocatalytic CO2 Reduction and H2 Generation

The use of coal-derived carbon materials will be one of the smart, low-cost options for clean energy applications in the coming years. These materials, including graphene, carbon dots, carbon nanotubes, and reduced graphene oxide derived from coal, also exhibited a large surface area and conduct electricity well. They are also efficient in absorbing light and separating charges efficiently. This makes them also great utility for photocatalytic CO2 reduction and hydrogen production. They can work alone or support other catalysts such as TiO2 or g-C3N4. Together, they help turn CO2 into fuels, such as CO, methane, or methanol. Incorporating dopants or cocatalysts (e.g., transition metals) further improves activity and selectivity.

Maimaiti et al. synthesized coal-based carbon dots and further modified with N and S codoped aminated carbon nanoparticles (NH2-CNPs) for efficient photocatalytic CO2 reduction. The NH2-CNPs exhibited greater electron generation and charge separation, achieving a 76.6% selectivity for CH3OH with a total product yield of 807.56 μmol g–1 after 10 h under visible light. Likewise, Dedong et al. utilized coal-based carbon nanoparticles (CNPs) and integrated with Cu2O to form a composite photocatalyst for CO2 reduction to methanol under visible light (Figure ). The CNPs improved charge separation and adsorption, improving photocatalytic efficiency.

Coal-based luminescent carbon dots (CDs) integrated with Ag nanoparticles are useful to enhance photocatalytic CO2 reduction into hydrocarbons. The Ag/CDs composite showed better CO2 adsorption, charge separation, and photocatalytic efficiency under visible light. Coal-based carbon quantum dots (CQDs) synthesized via ultrasound-assisted oxidation were integrated with CoMoO4 and g-C3N4 to enhance photocatalytic hydrogen evolution, achieving a high hydrogen production rate of 4916.63 μmol g–1 h–1. The CQDs facilitated electron transfer and charge separation, forming an S-type heterojunction and providing a sustainable and cost-effective approach for coal utilization in water splitting. The same research group, on the other hand, developed a novel N and S codoped coal-based carbon quantum dot (NSCQDs/Co-MOF/CN) composite for efficient hydrogen evolution. The resultant material not only exhibited a high production rate of 6254 μmol g–1 h–1) at pH = 9 but also showed high visible light absorption capability and better charge separation, thereby forming a stable Z-type heterojunction between graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4, CN) and cobalt-based metal organic framework (Co-MOF). The catalyst demonstrated good stability over multiple cycles, with Mott–Schottky analysis confirming its n-type semiconductor behavior and favorable band structure for photocatalysis. Likewise, NS-CQDs were synthesized from high sulfur lignite and used to modify Ni-MOF and Co/Zr-MOF for improved photocatalytic hydrogen production. The 5%NS-CQDs/NCZ2 composite achieved a high hydrogen evolution rate of 3662.68 μmol g–1 h–1 due to the formation of a double S-type heterojunction. Thiophene and pyrrole nitrogen in NS-CQDs facilitated electron transport and reduced charge recombination, which improved the photocatalytic efficiency. A composite catalyst was synthesized by Sun et al. using coal-based N-doped carbon dots and copper nanorods (N-CDs/Cu NRs) for simultaneous photoelectrocatalytic (PEC) degradation of cotton pulp black liquor (CPBL) and hydrogen (H2) production. In an H-type photoreactor, CPBL degradation reached 94.33% while H2 yield reached 272.0 mmol in 60 min. GC-MS analysis confirmed the breakdown of various organic compounds, demonstrating an efficient dual-function PEC process. The same research group, in contrast, developed a morphologically lotus-shaped MoS2/SiO2/GO composite photocatalyst using coal-based SiO2/GO for wastewater degradation and hydrogen production. In another study, coal-derived CNTs are combined with Ti3+-doped TiO2 (brookite phase) to form Ti3+-TiO2/Fe-CNTs, which were reported to be efficient for the photocatalytic coreduction of N2 and CO2 in water to produce urea. The Ti3+ sites and oxygen vacancies on Ti3+-TiO2 played key roles in activating N2 and CO2, forming six-membered cyclic intermediates that converted into urea. Interestingly, the excellent photoreduction of CO2 to CH3OH is found by using a 2D/2D BiVO4/coal-based graphene oxide (CGO) nanostructure catalyst. The better photocatalytic performance is attributed to broadened visible light absorption, improved charge separation, and abundant active sites facilitated by CGO as a cocatalyst. Carbon dots with tunable size were also synthesized from coal tar pitch with higher solar to hydrogen efficiency by 70% with Pt support, demonstrating their potential for enhanced photocatalysis.

Future Prospects and Challenges

Coal-derived carbon nanomaterials, including graphene and its derivatives, are emerging as a sustainable and economical alternative to graphite-based nanomaterials. Their successful synthesis through pyrolysis, chemical vapor deposition, electrochemical methods, and green chemical approaches demonstrates the potential of coal as a versatile feedstock. The derived carbon materials already show promise in energy storage, catalysis, environmental remediation, and sensing, indicating that coal can be repositioned from a traditional fuel to a strategically valuable precursor for advanced functional carbon materials. The abundance of coal, together with the possibility of utilizing low-grade varieties and waste residues, also aligns with circular economy goals by turning underutilized resources into high-value nanocarbons. Opportunities also exist for expanding applications into biomedicine, electronics, and quantum technologies. Therefore, the future of coal-based nanocarbons will depend on connecting laboratory innovation with industrial scalability through interdisciplinary collaboration and sustainable processing. Besides, coal-derived graphene can be positioned within a broader class of advanced 2D materials. Other functional materials, such as MXenes, Prussian blue frameworks, covalent organic frameworks (COFs), metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), and hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks (HOFs), can also be explored for future synthesis and offer unique advantages.

Despite these advances, challenges remain (Figure ) in achieving consistent quality, scalable production, and environmentally safe synthesis routes. The intrinsic heterogeneity of coal chemistry makes structural control over graphene layers, defects, and functionalization difficult, while conventional oxidation/reduction methods rely heavily on hazardous reagents. Future research must therefore prioritize greener, energy-efficient synthesis strategies, a deeper understanding of heteroatom doping effects, and systematic integration into commercially relevant high-end devices.

Conclusions

This extensive and distinctive review highlights the emergence of diverse coal-derived carbon nanomaterials for advanced energy and environmental applications. Different studies concluded that these materials possess high surface area, excellent electrical conductivity, and remarkable structural adaptability, making them highly promising for advanced energy applications such as supercapacitors, lithium-ion batteries, sodium-ion batteries, and fuel cells. Apart from the energy sector, coal-based carbons have shown significant promise in environmental applications such as biosensing of metal ions such as Fe3+ and Cu2+, pollutant adsorption, water purification, and air quality improvement. Coal-derived carbon materials as photocatalysts effectively degrade organic dyes such as methylene blue and methyl orange through Z-scheme heterostructures and efficient electron–hole separation. The materials are also applied in CO2 reduction to hydrocarbons and in hydrogen evolution reactions (HER), indicating their versatility as functional materials and photocatalysts. As a result, these advancements indicate that coal is no longer confined to its conventional role as a fuel but is established as a valuable precursor for next-generation carbon nanostructures that enable sustainable energy and environmental solutions.
