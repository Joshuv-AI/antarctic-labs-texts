Assessment of Left Atrial Fibrosis by Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging in Ischemic Stroke Patients Without Atrial Fibrillation: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Jin Hean Koh, Lincoln Kai En Lim, Ying Kiat Tan, Claire Goh, Yao Hao Teo, Jamie Sin Ying Ho, Mayank Dalakoti, Mark Yan Yee Chan, Ching‐Hui Sia, Leonard Leong Litt Yeo, Benjamin Yong Qiang Tan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft atrial (LA) fibrosis is a marker of atrial cardiomyopathy and has been reported to be associated with both atrial fibrillation and ischemic stroke. Elucidating this relationship is clinically important as LA fibrosis could serve as a surrogate biomarker of LA cardiomyopathy. The objective of this study is to investigate the association of LA fibrosis and embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS) using cardiac magnetic resonance imaging.Methods and ResultsFollowing an International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews‐registered protocol, 3 blinded reviewers performed a systematic review for studies that quantified the degree of LA fibrosis in patients with ESUS as compared with healthy patients from inception to February 2024. A meta‐analysis was conducted in the mean difference. From 7 studies (705 patients), there was a significantly higher degree of LA fibrosis in patients with ESUS compared with healthy controls (MD, 5.71% [95% CI, 3.55%–7.87%], P<0.01). The degree of LA fibrosis was significantly higher in patients with atrial fibrillation than healthy controls (MD, 8.22% [95% CI, 5.62%–10.83%], P<0.01). A similar degree of LA fibrosis was observed in patients with ESUS compared with patients with atrial fibrillation (MD, −0.92% [95% CI, −2.29% to 0.44%], P=0.35).ConclusionsA significantly higher degree of LA fibrosis was found in patients with ESUS as compared with healthy controls. This suggests that LA fibrosis may play a significant role in the pathogenesis of ESUS. Further research is warranted to investigate LA fibrosis as a surrogate biomarker of atrial cardiomyopathy and recurrent stroke risk in patients with ESUS.

Ischemic stroke is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally. 1 The association between atrial fibrillation (AF) and stroke is well established, with AF‐related embolic stroke events accounting for up to 1 in 4 ischemic strokes in the United States. 2 Embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS) is a subset of ischemic stroke, representing 17% of all patients with ischemic stroke. 3 This term is used to describe patients with a nonlacunar ischemic stroke in the absence of obvious cause after initial workup. 3 Key sources of ESUS include the left atrium, left ventricle, and nonstenotic carotid plaques. 4 , 5 Classically, occult paroxysmal AF has been thought to be one of the key underlying mechanisms of ESUS. 6 , 7 , 8 However, newer evidence has called into question this causal association, particularly in short‐lasting and subclinical bouts of AF. Although patients with atrial cardiomyopathy may not have AF, these patients are still at risk of ESUS. 9 Other pathologies such as left atrial (LA) fibrosis have therefore been suggested as a possible underlying precipitating cause of ESUS. 10

Atrial fibrosis is the result of fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition, which promote anisotropic conduction and reentry. This has been proposed as an independent pathway toward thromboembolism, rather than a precursor to AF. Hirsch et al. suggested that atrial fibrosis leads to alterations in atrial cardiomyocyte interactions, predisposing to the formation of thrombogenic substrate. This leads to inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and stasis in the left atrium, leading to subsequent thromboembolism. 11

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging can be used for the quantification of LA fibrosis and evaluation of cardiomyopathies. An established technique is late‐gadolinium enhancement magnetic resonance imaging (LGE‐MRI), which hinges on the washout kinetics of the extracellular contrast gadolinium. The degree of LA fibrosis as identified by LGE‐MRI is closely related to ventricular myocardial pathology, with atrial fibrosis detected by LGE‐MRI being significantly higher in patients with previous stroke and new major adverse cardiovascular events such as ischemic stroke. 12 , 13 , 14 LGE‐MRI has been shown to be an effective tool for detection of atrial fibrosis. 15 , 16 The DECAAF (Delayed‐Enhancement MRI Determinant of Successful Radiofrequency Catheter Ablation of Atrial Fibrillation) study also demonstrated that atrial tissue fibrosis estimated by delayed enhancement MRI was independently associated with likelihood of recurrent arrhythmia. 17 Furthermore, a higher degree of LA fibrosis has been observed in patients with LA appendage thrombi found on transesophageal echocardiographic evaluation. 18

Several cohort studies have demonstrated that LA fibrosis is more pronounced in patients with ESUS. However, further investigation is warranted to confirm the relationship between LA fibrosis and ESUS. An assessment of this relationship can better inform clinical decision‐making in the management of patients with atrial fibrosis in view of their possibly increased risk of AF and stroke. Therefore, the aim of this study is to evaluate the association between LA fibrosis and ESUS.

Methods

All studies reviewed in this article are available from Medline, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health.

Study Design

The protocol for this review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42023423421). With reference to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta‐Analyses guidelines, a search was conducted on Medline/PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Register for Controlled Trials, and Cumulative Index of Nursing and Allied Health databases for studies published from inception to February 2024. 19 The search strategy used a combination of free text words and medical subject heading terms (Data S1). The reference lists of systematic reviews, included articles, and the gray literature were also screened manually to identify additional studies for a comprehensive search. Contact with authors of included studies was made where feasible to collect supplementary data. Institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required for this study.

Study Selection

Three blinded reviewers independently screened abstracts to check the eligibility for inclusion, with disputes being resolved through consensus from a fourth independent reviewer. Reviewers then assessed the full texts of shortlisted studies against the following predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were (1) clinical studies that evaluated LA fibrosis using CMR in patients with ESUS, (2) full‐text studies, (3) published in a peer‐review journal, and (4) written in English. The exclusion criteria were (1) clinical studies that did not have a control group, (2) animal studies, (3) case reports, (4) in vitro studies, and (5) reviews.

Data Extraction

Data from included articles were collected by 2 blinded, independent reviewers (J.H.K. and L.K.E.L.) in duplicate onto a structured proforma specifically designed for the study and piloted beforehand on a sample of selected studies. Disagreement was resolved by discussion and consensus with a third reviewer (Y.K.T.). The data collection sheet contained key characteristics of studies, according to the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study Type framework (Data S2). 20 , 21

Assessment of Evidence and Publication Bias

Two blinded, independent reviewers conducted the risk of bias assessment of included articles using the Risk of Bias in Non‐Randomized Studies of Interventions tool. 22 This tool rates the risk of bias of cohort studies on the premises of bias due to confounding, selection of participants into the study, classification of interventions, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported result.

Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of the respective funnel plots. 23 The asymmetry of funnel plots was further assessed using Egger's linear regression method and Begg's test. 24 Missing studies were imputed using the trim‐and‐fill method. 25

Statistical Analysis

The meta‐analysis was conducted using R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with the meta package extension (version 5.0.1; Balduzzi 2019). 26 Descriptive statistics were calculated for all continuous and categorical variables. A conventional meta‐analysis was done in the mean difference (MD) and ratio of means. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed via I 2, Cochran Q and τ2 test values, where an I 2 value of 25%, 50%, and 75% represented low, moderate, and high degree of heterogeneity respectively, and a P value of ≤0.10 for the Cochran Q test represented statistically significant heterogeneity. 27 , 28 τ2 values and their corresponding CIs were also calculated, where a smaller τ2 value represents a lower degree of between study variance. 29

Additional analyses that were planned a priori were conducted to evaluate potential sources of heterogeneity between studies. 30 Apart from subgroup analyses, univariate random‐effects meta‐regression were conducted, and effect modifiers were confirmed using permutation testing with 1000 iterations to eliminate spurious results. 31 , 32 Briefly, permutation testing is used to assess the robustness of a statistical model, against the potential effects of chance, by providing it with different data sampled from the same source or generative process, hence minimizing the risk of false positives when fitting meta‐regression models. The process of permutation testing involves randomization of exposure group, which will be repeated 1000 times, followed by calculation of effect size and to subsequently construct a distribution of pooled effect sizes expected by chance. This will be compared with the observed effect size to determine the P value, which indicates how often an observed effect size could occur by chance alone. 33 Statistical significance was considered for outcomes with a P value ≤0.05. Leave‐out‐one influence analyses were performed to examine the influence of individual studies on the overall findings. Cumulative meta‐analyses were performed ranked by year published, to examine the stability of published data over time.

Certainty of Evidence

The quality of pooled evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluations framework. 34 This framework rates each study on the basis of study design, consistency, directness, risk of bias, precision, and publication bias. For each outcome, the level of evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low.

Results

Literature Search

The literature search based on the search strategy yielded 2192 studies, of which 557 were removed as duplicates. A further 1595 studies were excluded after screening by title and abstract. A total of 40 studies were included for full‐text review and 7 studies fulfilled our inclusion/exclusion criteria. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 The interrater reliability as assessed by Cohen's kappa was 0.98. 42 The study identification and selection process is reflected in Figure 1.

Study Characteristics

The total sample size was 705 with a mean age of 61.71±6.62 years. 398 (57%) patients were male, and 307 (43%) patients were female. The MRI scanners used include the Siemens Avanto, Phillips 1.5T Achieva, and Philips Achieve 3 Tesla System. In all included studies, the imaging modality used was LGE‐MRI. The mean LA size was 114.55±5.55 cm2 in patients with ESUS and 108.50±30.3 cm2 in healthy controls. The mean LA volume index was 37.70±16.76 mL/m2 in patients with ESUS and 35.70±1.10 cm2 in healthy controls. All mean LA sizes reported were indexed to body surface area. Within the pooled cohort, 226 patients had ESUS, 273 patients had AF without ESUS, 90 patients had AF without ESUS, 90 patients were healthy controls, and 90 patients had strokes with causes. ESUS was defined using the published criteria of the ESUS construct, which entailed (1) stroke detected by computed tomography or MRI that is not lacunar, (2) absence of extracranial or intracranial atherosclerosis causing ≥50% luminal stenosis in arteries supplying the area of ischemia, (3) no major‐risk cardioembolic source of embolism, and (4) no other specific cause of stroke identified (eg, arteritis, dissection, migraine/vasospasm, drug misuse). 3 One study could not be included in the meta‐analysis due to the lack of other studies reporting the same outcomes. 35

Using the Risk of Bias in Non‐Randomized Studies of Interventions tool, the risk of bias was found to be moderate in 2 studies 38 , 39 and low in 3 studies. 36 , 37 , 41 (Figure S1). The risk of bias could not be assessed in the 2 abstracts. Details on study and patient characteristics are shown in Tables 1 and 2.

LA Fibrosis in Patients With ESUS Versus Healthy Controls

The degree of LA fibrosis among patients with ESUS and healthy controls was reported in 3 studies. 38 , 39 , 41 The pooled MD between the group with ESUS and the control group was 5.70% (95% CI, 3.71%–7.69%; P<0.1) and 5.71% (95% CI, 3.55%–7.87%; P<0.01) in the fixed and random effects models respectively, showing that the degree of LA fibrosis was significantly higher in patients with ESUS than healthy controls (Figure 2). In terms of relative measures, the pooled ratio of means in the fixed and random effects models was 1.79 (95% CI, 1.44%–2.23%; P<0.01), demonstrating that the degree of LA fibrosis was on average 79% higher in patients with ESUS compared with patients without ESUS (Figure 3). There was low evidence of heterogeneity observed for the MD (I 2=0%, τ2=0.4838 [95% CI, 0.0000–88.8087]; P=0.40) and ratio of means (I 2=0%, τ2=0 [95% CI, 0.0000–0.3749]; P=0.76). Furthermore, there is a directionally similar trend across studies favoring a higher degree of LA fibrosis in patients with ESUS and the range of effect sizes (MD, 4.13–7.10 and ratio of means, 1.58–1.90) are not clinically significant/practically different. The overall quality of evidence was moderate as assessed by the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluations framework (Table S1).

LA Fibrosis in Patients With ESUS Versus AF

The degree of LA fibrosis among patients with ESUS and patients with AF was reported in 5 studies. 36 , 37 , 38 , 40 , 41 The pooled MD between the groups with ESUS and AF was −0.92% (95% CI, −2.29% to 0.44%; P=0.19) in both fixed and random effects models, showing that the degree of LA fibrosis was comparable in patients with AF and patients with ESUS patiens (Figure 5). There was low evidence of statistical heterogeneity observed (I 2=0%, τ2=0 [95% CI, 0.0000–18.2477]; P=0.80). The overall quality of evidence was moderate as assessed by the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluations framework (Table S1).

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta‐analysis, LA fibrosis was significantly higher in patients with ESUS compared with healthy controls. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 Likewise, there was a higher extent of LA fibrosis in patients with AF when compared with healthy controls, whereas the degree of LA fibrosis appeared to be comparable between patients with ESUS and patients with AF. At present, most clinical studies on this topic are small, uncontrolled studies. By pooling data in this meta‐analysis, it is possible to draw stronger conclusions on the association of LA fibrosis with ESUS. Elucidating this relationship is clinically important as LA fibrosis could serve as a surrogate biomarker of LA cardiomyopathy. Patients with ESUS with a high degree of LA fibrosis may potentially benefit from prolonged cardiac monitoring to capture occult paroxysmal AF.

The association of AF and embolic stroke is well established in the literature. Classically, AF has been proposed to accelerate LA thrombus formation by means of Virchow's triad of stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability. 43 However, the mechanism linking thrombus formation and other clinical risk factors is yet unclear. Data from patients with implantable cardioversion devices revealed a temporal dissociation between instances of AF and ESUS, in addition to other embolic pathologies. 11 Furthermore, only 3 in 10 patients with ESUS were diagnosed with AF after 3 years of follow‐up, demonstrating that 7 in 10 patients with ESUS do not necessarily have AF. 7 This calls into question the direct role of AF in cardioembolic stroke and prompts the question as to whether there are alternative causal mechanisms that explain the development of ESUS independent of AF. Atrial fibrosis is the result of fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition, which promote anisotropic conduction and reentry, hence showing histological and imaging features of atrial cardiomyopathy. Previous literature has suggested that cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and obesity may accelerate the development of atrial fibrosis; therefore, control of these risk factors may have the benefit of slowing the progression of atrial fibrosis. 44

The link between LA fibrosis and ESUS raises considerations for clinical practice, including the potential use of anticoagulation to prevent recurrent stroke in patients with ESUS. It is noteworthy that a pair of randomized controlled trials (NAVIGATE‐ESUS [Rivaroxaban Versus Aspirin in Secondary Prevention of Stroke and Prevention of Systemic Embolism in Patients With Recent Embolic Stroke of Undetermined Source] and RESPECT‐ESUS [Dabigatran Etexilate for Secondary Stroke Prevention in Patients With Embolic Stroke of Undetermined Source]) of direct oral anticoagulants versus aspirin for patients with ESUS have failed to demonstrate a reduced risk of recurrent stroke following anticoagulation. 45 , 46 NAVIGATE‐ESUS enrolled 7200 patients and compared rivaroxaban to aspirin therapy but was terminated prematurely for a lack of efficacy in preventing stroke. RESPECT‐ESUS compared dabigatran to aspirin and showed similar results. These findings suggest that the causes of ESUS, including patent foramen ovale and nonstenosing internal carotid artery lesions, are complex and multifactorial, where some causes may respond to anticoagulation and others do not. 47 , 48 However, a post hoc analysis of the NAVIGATE‐ESUS trial showed that rivaroxaban was beneficial in preventing recurrent stroke in patients with ESUS who had an increased LA surface area, indicating that patients with atrial cardiomyopathy may benefit from anticoagulation. 49

Several trials have investigated or are investigating a selection of patients for anticoagulation based on atrial myopathy. One such trial is the WARSS (Warfarin‐Aspirin Recurrent Stroke Study) trial, which randomized patients with ischemic stroke in the absence of AF to warfarin versus aspirin therapy, finding that patients with a serum NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) exceeding the 95th percentile benefited from anticoagulation. This benefit was not seen in patients with NT‐proBNP below the 95th percentile. 50 NT‐proBNP is a biological marker closely associated with atrial fibrosis, thus demonstrating that patients with ESUS and advanced LA fibrosis may benefit more from anticoagulation compared with patients with an embolic origin outside the left atrium. In the recently published ARCADIA (Atrial Cardiopathy and Antithrombotic Drugs in Prevention After Cryptogenic Stroke) and ATTICUS (Apixaban for Treatment of Embolic Stroke of Undetermined Source) trials, both studies found no benefit of apixaban over aspirin in patients with ESUS with LA cardiopathy. The 2 clinical trials employed different biomarkers of atrial cardiopathy. 51 , 52 ARCADIA randomized patients with ESUS with LA enlargement on echocardiography, high P‐wave terminal force in lead V1 on ECG or elevated plasma NT‐proBNP, whereas ATTICUS randomized patients with ESUS with at least 1 risk factor for AF/cardiac thromboembolism, such as LA size >45 mm, spontaneous echo contrast in LA appendage, LA appendage flow velocity ≤0.2 m/s, atrial high‐rate episodes, CHA2DS2‐VASc ≥4, or patent foramen ovale. As demonstrated in these biomarker‐stratified randomized controlled trials, current biomarkers do not seem to be precise enough to identify patients with atrial cardiopathy who would benefit from anticoagulation. Cardiac MRI‐measured atrial fibrosis may therefore be an alternative and more precise biomarker of atrial cardiopathy than the echocardiographic features and other biomarkers employed in ARCADIA and ATTICUS, although the prognostic implications of these biomarkers requires further investigation.

Studies are also ongoing to investigate the use of CMR in patients with ESUS. The CARM‐AF (Atrial CMR in Patients With CVA of Unknown Source and No Known AF) trial will enroll 92 patients to undergo a CMR scan for features suggestive of LA remodeling. 53 The degree of atrial fibrosis will then be quantified using LGE‐MRI. The COAST (Atrial Cardiomyopathy in Patients With Stroke of Undetected Mechanism) study is a cross‐sectional and prospective cohort study which will enroll 100 patients with recent ischemic stroke and 50 healthy controls. 54 Patients will undergo CMR to assess LA structural and functional abnormalities. The primary outcomes of these studies are the extent of CMR‐assessed atrial fibrosis in the LA at baseline and the incidence of recurrent ischemic stroke, AF, and cardiovascular death. Although these studies are currently ongoing and no published data are available, they reflect the growing interest in this field to quantify the relationship between atrial fibrosis and ESUS.

A limitation of this systematic review and meta‐analysis is the small sample size, as only 7 studies could be included. There was also only 1 study that compared the degree of atrial fibrosis in patients with ESUS versus other stroke subtypes; therefore, a meta‐analysis could not be performed. 40 However, this reflects the paucity of literature rather than a deficit in the methodology of this systematic review and meta‐analysis. Several new studies are in the pipeline, which will strengthen the current literature pertaining to atrial fibrosis in patients with ESUS. This study therefore serves to provide preliminary evidence suggesting a significant relationship between LA fibrosis and ESUS. This could thus serve as the foundation for future longitudinal studies that will assess the use of CMR for quantifying atrial fibrosis in patients with ESUS.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrated that the degree of atrial fibrosis was significantly higher in patients with ESUS compared with healthy controls. Cardiac MRI may be useful to evaluate atrial fibrosis in patients with ESUS without AF. Elucidating this relationship is clinically important as LA fibrosis could serve as a surrogate biomarker of LA cardiopathy, and this warrants further investigation, as prolonged cardiac monitoring in patients with ESUS with high degrees of LA fibrosis may be required to capture AF. Further prospective studies are necessary to evaluate the prognostic implications of LA fibrosis.
