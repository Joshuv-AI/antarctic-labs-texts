Introduction

Intracardiac masses may present as primary or secondary tumours, infection, and/or thrombus. Tumours are typically attached to the endocardium with a stalk, and surgery is the default therapy. As percutaneous technologies evolve, non-surgical percutaneous techniques for mass removal have become an option. In this case report, we present a case of a woman with a large right atrial mass that was percutaneously removed en bloc without surgery.

Case summary

A 77-year-old woman with bronchiectasis, ulcerative colitis, immune thrombocytopenia (platelets: 105 000) and rheumatoid arthritis presented with a transient ischaemic attack (TIA) in 2012 and had a right atrial mass (2.5 × 2.4 cm; no PFO) identified by transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE). A confirmatory cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) suggested a probable myxoma, but the patient refused surgery, so based on the presenting symptoms of TIA, anticoagulation was initiated and the mass was followed with serial imaging.

Surveillance echocardiography in 2022 suggested growth, and a TEE now showed a mobile 4 cm mass that appeared to be attached to the right atrium along the lateral wall near the superior vena cava (SVC) ostium (Figure 1). The patient once again refused surgery despite the growth of the mass and was referred for percutaneous extraction.

Transoesophageal echocardiogram of right atrial mass (bicaval view).

The plan for removal was based on the use of a large 22 F AngioVac (Angiodynamics) cannula to provide suction and engage the mass, followed by endoscopic snaring of the stalk (Boston Scientific) and finally electrocautery applied to the snare to free the mass from its attachment to the right atrial wall.

The procedure was performed under general anaesthesia with TEE guidance. A 26 F sheath was placed in the right femoral vein. An 18 F return cannula was placed in the left femoral vein. A second access in the left femoral vein was obtained to insert a 24 mm gooseneck endoscopy snare, which was then deployed in the inferior vena cava (IVC). From the right groin, we placed a 0.035″ wire through the IVC snare and advanced the AngioVac cannula over the wire, through the snare and into the IVC. The system was primed and flows were gradually increased to 4 L/min. The cannula was then advanced into the right atrium and the mass was engaged. Although the mass was at the tip of the cannula, the stalk prevented the mass from being removed. Next, we then advanced the snare over the cannula and the tumour under TEE guidance (Figure 2). The snare was tightened over the stalk at the base of the tumour and 50 W of electricity was delivered via a cautery pen from the handle of the snare (Figure 3). This resulted in the trans-section of the stalk and the tumour was freed from the right atrial wall and captured at the tip of the cannula. Given the size of the tumour, the flow was increased to 6 L/min to provide enough force to pull the mass into the circuit and trap it in the in-line filter (Figure 4). After tumour excision, the patient developed a pericardial effusion and hypotension. The anticoagulation was reversed, and a pericardial drain was placed with bloody drainage noted. Her haemodynamics improved, but the drainage continued to accumulate. The blood from the pericardium was re-infused to the patient using cell salvage. Due to recurrent effusion, 40 cc of Surgiflow (Ethicon) was infused into the pericardial space. After injection of the haemostatic agent, the effusion did not re-accumulate and the patient remained haemodynamically stable (Figure 5). Repeat imaging after 30 min confirmed no recurrent effusion and no surgical intervention was required with a final 48 h echo showing no effusion. The patient was discharged on post-operative day 3. The gross pathology of the mass suggested a papillary fibroelastoma (Figure 6).

Discussion

The differential diagnosis for an intracardiac mass includes thrombus, vegetation, primary cardiac tumour, or metastatic disease. This patient had no symptoms or clinical evidence of infection or cancer, making vegetation or metastatic disease improbable. The mass grew larger on anticoagulation, and thus, thrombus was an unlikely diagnosis. The mass clinically was presumed to be a benign tumour, either a myxoma or fibroelastoma with the final gross morphology highly suggestive of a fibroelastoma.

Intracardiac tumours are typically removed using open surgical resection.1 As technology has evolved, less-invasive strategies may become options to remove intracardiac tumours in the right atrium. Catheter-based removal of a right atrial myxoma was described by Konecny et al.2 using radio-frequency ablation and a snare to remove the mass in fragments. El-Sabawai, et al.3 reported the largest right atrial tumour removed percutaneously at 4.7 cm. This angiosarcoma was debulked in fragments that were removed using the AngioVac system. In an effort to percutaneously remove the tumours en bloc, McCabe et al.4 utilized electro-surgical techniques to sever the stalk of a 1.7 cm right atrial myxoma. This SEATTLE (Simplified Extraction of Atrial Tumor with Targeted Loop Electricity) procedure used a snare to deliver electricity that cuts the tumour stalk. The mass was removed using the ONO retrieval system. This device has a 35 mm basket that accommodates foreign bodies for removal. For our case, we modified this procedure and replaced the retrieval system with the 22 F AngioVac cannula. This device is a large suction cannula with a funnel tip, creating a venous—venous bypass circuit with an in-line filter. The system creates powerful suction to engage and remove thrombus, vegetations, or masses. We coupled this with an endoscopy snare as a modified technique, modelled after the SEATTLE procedure. This modification of McCabe’s procedure allowed us to successfully cut the stalk and remove a 4 cm tumour en bloc. We call this modification the Transcatheter EXtraction of an Atrial masS (TEXAS) technique.

This case is the largest right atrial tumour removed percutaneously, in one piece. Despite snaring the tumour stalk near the base of the tumour, the right atrium was still injured resulting in a haemopericardium and tamponade. The large size of this tumour and its stalk likely contributed to this atrial injury. Although atrial injury management was not the focus of this manuscript, a few points should be emphasized. The use of cell salvage/autotransfusion is valuable in the management of ongoing haemopericardium. In addition, instillation of a haemostatic matrix into the pericardium can result in haemostasis in cases of cardiac injury without the need for open surgery. These techniques were critical in the outcome of our case.

Conclusion

In this case, the TEXAS technique utilized a modification of the SEATTLE procedure to allow for percutaneous, en bloc removal of a large right atrial papillary fibroelastoma. This case was complicated by a haemopericardium from right atrial injury that was managed with cell salvage and infusion of a haemostatic matrix into the pericardium. Percutaneous removal of right atrial tumours appears feasible, although this may be limited to smaller tumours based on the results of our case.
