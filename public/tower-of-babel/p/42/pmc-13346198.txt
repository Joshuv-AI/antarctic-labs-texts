Compliance with the Netherlands’ vitamin D supplementation recommendation for three at-risk groups: young children, women over 50 years, and elderly
Authors: M. R. Verbakel, M. H. de Jong, J. Verkaik-Kloosterman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo prevent vitamin D deficiency subgroups are advised to daily take a vitamin D supplement. Knowledge on the compliance with this recommendation is limited. For young children, women over 50 yrs, and elderly, the supplementation recommendation is independent of sunlight exposure. This cross-sectional study aimed to investigate compliance with the supplementation recommendation for these groups.MethodsData on young children (1–3 yrs, n = 703), women (50–69 yrs, n = 451), and elderly (70–79 yrs, n = 331) from the Dutch National Food and Consumption Survey (DNFCS) 2019–2021 were analysed for vitamin D-containing supplement use. With a general questionnaire (GQ) information was collected on frequency of use of sub-types of supplements (e.g. vitamin D, vitamin D & calcium, multivitamin, multivitamins-minerals). In two 24-h dietary recalls, use of supplements was reported in detail; e.g. type and dosage.Results71% of young children, 38% of women, and 40% of elderly reported use of vitamin D-containing supplements (GQ: 80%, 55%, and 50%, respectively). Among supplement users, 70% of young children, 30% of women, and 35% of elderly used exactly the advised dosage.ConclusionCompliance with vitamin D supplementation recommendation was high in young children and could be improved most among women over 50 years and elderly. Further research is needed to identify effective strategies to improve compliance in these groups. In addition, to ensure that the supplementation recommendations are effective in achieving adequate vitamin D status, regular monitoring of vitamin D status and related health outcomes in all target groups is warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00394-026-04052-1.

Introduction

Vitamin D plays an important role in bone health and contributes to other physiological processes, including the immune system [1]. It can be obtained through the diet and by synthesis in the skin after exposure to sunlight. The amount of vitamin D synthesized in the skin depends on factors such as latitude, season, duration of exposure, skin colour and use of sun protecting agents [2]. The Health Council of the Netherlands (HCNL) established dietary reference values for vitamin D, and, in addition, the HCNL recommends vitamin D supplementation for certain subgroups of the population (shown in Table 1) [3–5]. For young children, this protects against rickets, and because it is advised to protect young children from direct sunlight exposure, they rely mostly on diet for an adequate vitamin D intake [3]. People with a dark skin colour or those who are not sufficiently exposed to sunlight (due to staying indoors or covering their skin) have low vitamin D production in the skin and also rely mostly on diet for vitamin D intake. For women ≥ 50 years old and men ≥ 70 years old extra vitamin D can help to protect against bone loss and fractures [3], and for pregnant women it may reduce the risk of low birthweight, gestational diabetes, and asthma-like symptoms or wheezing in the offspring [5, 6]. Recommendations for vitamin D supplementation are not unique to the Netherlands; similar guidelines exist in other countries and by international organizations [7, 8]. Although specific subgroups and dosages may vary, the underlying rationale and targeted populations are broadly comparable.

There is limited information on the compliance with these supplementation recommendations among different population subgroups in the Netherlands. Existing studies in Dutch children and elderly are often regional with data collected between 8 to 17 years ago, and report inconsistent results. Two studies found relatively low adherence, with 33 or 50% of young children receiving a daily vitamin D supplement [10, 11], while other studies described higher percentages ranging from 61 to 99% [12–14]. A similar difference is observed in studies among elderly; two studies described that 17–20% of elderly reported use of a vitamin D supplement [15, 16], while in another study reported use was 44–55% [17]. Higher reported vitamin D supplement use in children compared to older adults is reflected in vitamin D status measurements, with lower deficiency in young children (0–5% < 25 nmol/L) [11] and higher deficiency in older adults ≥ 65 years (10–14% < 30 nmol/L [15, 16]; 45–52% < 50 nmol/L [15, 16, 18]). However, higher deficiency among elderly may also partly result from a higher cut-off value for deficiency set by the HCNL. For individuals over 70 years old, deficiency is defined as a serum 25(OH)D concentration < 50 nmol/L, whereas for younger individuals the threshold is set at < 30 nmol/L [3]. Given the inconsistent findings regarding vitamin D supplement use, compliance with supplementation recommendations may be suboptimal. There is a need for nationally representative data to accurately assess compliance with recommendations and to determine whether improved education or policy interventions may be required.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate compliance with the Netherlands’ vitamin D supplementation recommendations among young children, women over 50 years, and elderly. Building on previous work that provided a preliminary overview of vitamin D supplement use among groups for which there are age-based recommendations [19], we now conduct a more in-depth analysis of compliance with supplementation recommendation, specifically examining whether individuals consumed the correct dosage and in the correct frequency.

Methods

Survey population

For this study, data from the DNFC 2019–2021 were used. The DNFCS is a cross-sectional study conducted in the Dutch population (N = 3,570; 1–79 yrs). A detailed description of the study design and data collection methods can be found elsewhere [19, 20]. Briefly, for the DNFCS, a representative sample of the Dutch population was selected from a consumer panel: Kantar TNS (net response rate 37%). Sampling was adjusted for gender, age, region of residence, degree of urbanization, educational level, and size of family. Only people with adequate command of the Dutch language were included. In addition, people living institutionalized were excluded, due to the limited freedom of food choice. For this study, data from three subgroups were analysed: young children 1–3 years old, women 50–69 years old, and elderly individuals 70–79 years old. Throughout this paper, the terms young children, women and elderly will refer to these subgroups. Data in this study were pseudonymised to protect participant privacy.

Questionnaires

As part of the DNFCS, two non-consecutive 24-h recalls were collected by trained dieticians using the interview program Globodiet (IARC; formerly EPIC-Soft). For children 1 to 3 years, the parents or caretakers were interviewed about their child’s food consumption, including use of dietary supplements. Adults were interviewed by phone, while elderly participants were interviewed at home and/or by phone (with limited home visits in periods with COVID-19 pandemic restrictions). Parents/caretakers of young children were asked to complete a food diary preceding the interviews, to capture consumption outside of home, such as food provided at daycare or other childcare settings. Elderly were also asked to complete a food diary to use as a memory aid. Interviewers were instructed to specifically ask about the intake of dietary supplements (supplement brand, used quantity and dosage) during the 24-h recalls. To estimate the amount of vitamin D intake from dietary supplements, information from DNFCS was combined with information (based on label information only) on supplement composition available in the Dutch Supplement Database (NES) [21].

In addition to the two 24-h recalls, a general and lifestyle questionnaire (GQ) provided information on e.g. background characteristics (e.g., education, smoking) and frequency of use of specific dietary supplements. For children, the educational level was defined as the level of the highest-educated parent or caretaker. Region was based on the Nielsen CBS division: northern, eastern, southern, and western regions (including the three largest cities Amsterdam, Rotterdam, and The Hague). Participants were classified into a season based on the date of their first recall day. Frequency of supplement use was reported separately for winter (Dec, Jan, Feb) and the rest of the year, within six frequency categories (i.e. less than 1 day per month, 1–3 days per month, 1 day per week, 2–3 days per week, 4–5 days per week, and 6–7 days per week). For vitamin D, relevant supplement types included vitamin D, multivitamins, combinations of multivitamins and minerals (multivitamins-minerals), and combinations of calcium and vitamin D. In this study, we assumed that all these supplement categories contain vitamin D.

Statistical analysis

To correct for small differences between the study population and the Dutch population, analyses were weighted for socio-demographic factors, season and day of the week. Participants were considered supplement users if they reported using a vitamin D (containing) supplement a) in the GQ (any frequency in the winter, rest of the year, or both) or on at least one of the 24-h recall days (percentage presented is average of two recall days). For both data sources, the number of types of supplements consumed per participant were counted and compared between the GQ and 24-h recall. The comparison was descriptive; only the percentages were compared and no statistical tests were performed. As the 24-h recall only contains two days of observation, subjects were assigned to use supplements potentially containing vitamin D a) during winter period only, b) only during the rest of the year or c) throughout the whole year (i.e. both winter and rest of the year) based on the GQ. As dosage information was only present in 24-h recall data, proportion of subjects using vitamin D supplements below, at exactly or above the recommended dosage was calculated from the 24-h recalls (average percentage of two recall days shown). For participants with higher vitamin D intake from dietary supplements than recommended, it was assessed whether this was due to the use of multiple supplements or a single high-dose supplement.

Differences in the proportion of supplement users within each subgroups across age (years/groups), gender, educational level, and region were tested using chi-square tests for both the GQ and each 24-h recall day. When chi-square tests could not be performed (data contained values equal to zero), t-tests or ANOVA were used. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to adjust p-values for multiple testing, with the false discovery rate set at ≤ 5% [22]. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS Studio 381 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Supplement users, number and type of supplements

According to the GQ, 80% of the young children, 55% of women and 50% of elderly reported to use a vitamin D-containing supplement in any frequency during the winter period, the rest of the year, or throughout the whole year (supplement user, see Table 3). Compared to the GQ, the self-reported actual use in the 24-h recall data showed slightly lower percentages, namely 71%, 38%, and 40%, respectively. Infrequent supplement use and supplement use in a specific season only, was not captured for all in the 24-h recall. GQ data indicated that children of higher age reported less frequent (almost) daily use of vitamin D-containing supplements (from 83% in 1-year olds to 72% in 3-year olds; p = 0.0025). This lower frequency of use among children of higher age was also reflected in the 24-h recall data, with supplement use decreasing from 82% in 1-year olds to 61% in 3-year olds (p < 0.0001). Moreover, GQ data showed that children of highly educated parents were more likely to report use of vitamin D-containing supplements (p < 0.0001). Among elderly, GQ data showed that more women reported use of a vitamin D-containing supplement than men (GQ: 61% in women, 41% in men, p < 0.001). For exact p-values see Supplementary Table 1. Among users of vitamin D-containing supplements, most participants reported use of one type of supplement (74–96%; Table 3). No more than 24% reported use of two types of supplements and almost no one reported use of three or more types of supplements (≤ 2%). Vitamin D-only supplement use was the most commonly reported type of supplement (50–78%). Multivitamins-minerals use was reported by a smaller part (14–35%). A combination of two supplements was mostly a vitamin D-only supplement combined with a multivitamins-minerals. Dosage of supplement intake

Of the young children using vitamin D supplements, 70% received the exact recommended dosage of 10 µg on a consumption day, while 14% used a higher dosage and 16% a lower dosage (Table 5). About 50% of the young children using a higher dosage reported this on both recall days. High intakes in this group were mostly due to administering more droplets than the advised amount of droplets on the packaging of the product (57%, Supplementary Table 3). For the adult groups, 30% of women and 35% of the elderly used the exact recommended dosage of respectively 10 and 20 µg on a consumption day. About half of women and 28% of elderly used a higher dosage vitamin D, of these almost 70% did this on both recall days. Discussion

The present study assessed the compliance of young children, women over 50 years, and elderly in the Netherlands with national vitamin D supplementation recommendations. The majority of young children (80%) and half of women over 50 yrs and elderly used vitamin D-containing supplements at any frequency and in any period of the year (i.e. winter, rest of year, whole year). Among vitamin D-containing supplement users, the exact recommended dosage was used by 70% of the young children and by 30% of women and 35% of elderly. For young children, the number of children using vitamin D-containing supplements decreased with age. In elderly more women used vitamin D-containing supplements than men.

Our findings on the high proportion of vitamin D-containing supplement users among young children are in accordance with earlier Dutch studies. For example, Gommans et al. and Eslami et al. reported even higher rates, likely due to their inclusion of younger children (< 1 year) [24] and data collection at child health clinics where social desirable answers might be given [12]. Consistent with Van der Meer et al., we observed a decline in supplement use with increasing age among young children [14]. Other studies, such as De Nooijer et al. and Hoevenaar et al., reported lower proportions, possibly due to stricter definitions of supplement user [10] or a smaller sample size, which may have limited the ability to accurately reflect the total Dutch population for this age group [11]. Similar or lower supplement use has been reported for older women and elderly. However, as these data were collected a considerable time ago (between 1998–2004) direct comparisons are difficult, since changes in supplement guidelines over time may have influenced supplement use [15, 17, 25, 26].

Compliance to recommendations in Dutch young children appears to be higher compared to several other countries, while adherence among women and elderly is comparable to that observed in other nations and could be improved. A review from Eslami et al. reported lower compliance with guidelines among young children in other countries compared to our results (Ireland (23–46%), UK (14%), USA (27–57%), Denmark (54%) and Canada (61%), and Sweden (74%)) [24]. A Canadian study reported similar results to ours for breastfed infants ≤ 1 year (83%), and adherence also decreased with higher age [27]. For women and elderly, vitamin D-containing supplement use in Canada and Denmark was similar to our study [28, 29]. In addition, comparable to our study, these studies also observed that more elderly women were supplement users compared to men [28, 29]. Rates of supplement use were lower in the United States, probably due to the absence of an official supplementation recommendation [30]. Differences in the specific content of the recommendation (e.g., whether certain subgroups are advised to use vitamin D supplement in one country but not in another) and how it is communicated may partially explain the international variation.

Our study showed that overall supplement use reported in the general questionnaire corresponded reasonably well with data derived from the 24-h dietary recalls, supporting the credibility of participant responses and data quality. For example, a lower frequency of supplement use among children of higher age in the GQ was reflected in a lower supplement use in the 24-h recall data. However, overall, a higher prevalence of supplement users was identified through the GQ than with the 24-h recalls. This may be because some individuals use supplements sporadically (≤ 7–15%) or only during specific periods, such as winter (≤ 6%), which makes it less likely that their supplement use was captured on a specific recall day.

In the Netherlands, vitamin D supplements for young children are most often provided in the form of a liquid suspension and dosed in droplets rather than as tablets. When examining vitamin D intake among young children, our study found that vitamin D intake above the recommended dosage among young children was primarily attributable to the administration of a greater number of droplets than advised on the product packaging to reach the recommended (daily) dosage. This may, in part, be explained by the variation in the number of droplets required to achieve the recommended daily dose across different products available on the market. Such inconsistencies may lead to confusion among caregivers, particularly when switching between brands or formulations, potentially resulting in under- or overdosing. Alternatively, it is possible that, due to limitations in data collection or processing, the reported supplement was accidentally linked to an incorrect supplement with a different droplet regimen for achieving the recommended daily dose. Additionally, accidental deviations from the recommended dosage, such as inadvertently administering too many or too few droplets, may also occur. Compared to young children and elderly, women more frequently used vitamin D supplements with a higher dosage than the recommended 10 µg/d.

Our study showed that most women (85%) used only one supplement per day, indicating that these supplements contain higher vitamin D level per daily dosage (usually a single tablet) than recommended for women aged 50–69 years. However, most used supplements contained only slightly higher amounts than advised. These intakes rarely surpassed the established upper intake level (100 µg/d [23]) or the legal maximum daily dosage of vitamin D for supplements (75 µg/d [31]), suggesting that the risk of excessive vitamin D intake in this population remains low. Possible explanations for using a supplement with a higher-than-recommended dosage include confusion regarding the differing recommendations for women aged ≥ 70 years versus those aged 50–69 years and the wide variety of supplements on the market. To illustrate, the NES database of 2020 showed that approximately 850 out of the total of 2800 products contained vitamin D, with dosages ranging from 0.06 to 2500 µg per unit (mean: 15 µg per unit) [21], which may complicate choosing a supplement with the recommended dosage. Notably, nine products exceeded the legal maximum daily dosage, but four of these were classified as medicines for use under medical supervision and some products were intended for weekly rather than daily use. It remains unclear which barriers and opportunities exist to enhance compliance with supplementation guidelines across various population subgroups.

The high adherence observed among young children may be attributable to the Dutch healthcare system, in which parents receive recommendation to administer vitamin D supplements during maternity care and subsequently at regular check-ups at the child health clinic, which are more frequent at younger ages [32]. For women and the elderly, no comparable system exists in the Netherlands to provide such structured supplementation recommendation. (Scientific) literature showed financial constraints [33], lack of information and/or knowledge (about the recommendation/healthcare system) [25, 33, 34], or health (absence of health issues/medication use/life expectancy) [33, 34] could be reasons for not adhering to the guidelines. A deeper understanding of the behavioural patterns and decision-making processes related to the compliance with supplementation guidelines is needed to find ways to improve compliance. Existing evidence suggests that simply providing information is insufficient to achieve meaningful changes in health-related behaviours [35]. This underscores the importance of addressing additional factors beyond information provision when seeking to improve compliance with supplementation guidelines. Additionally, alternative approaches to increasing vitamin D intake, such as food fortification, may warrant consideration, taking into account the potential benefits and drawbacks of such strategies. Further, the aim of the supplementation recommendation is to improve the vitamin D status. Insight in the vitamin D status and the association with supplement use and sunlight exposure is needed to evaluate the successfulness of the supplement recommendation, as well as to get insights that could improve the supplement recommendation.

A strength of our study is the use of nationally representative data for the Netherlands to investigate compliance with the HCNL recommendation, which improves the generalizability of our findings compared to previous, non-representative studies. In contrast, a limitation in this study associated with the general questionnaire is the aggregation of potential vitamin D-containing supplements into four categories, two of which (multivitamins and multivitamin with minerals) may, but do not always, contain vitamin D. However, based on information from the 24-h recall, in our study, the use of multivitamins (with or without minerals) supplements not containing vitamin D was rare (n = 3). Another limitation is that the NES database does not include all dietary supplements available on the Dutch market, but only those reported in the 24-h recalls. NES is updated with new supplements for each survey cycle. Due to unstructured reporting within the recalls, not all reported supplement intakes could be matched exactly by product and dosage in NES. In such cases, intakes were linked to the most comparable available supplement based on expert opinion. Furthermore, information in NES is based on the label of the supplement, and previous research has shown that the actual dosage can deviate from the dosage on the label [36]. Lastly, we did not assess total vitamin D intake from all sources, including food, supplements, and sunlight exposure, as our aim was to monitor compliance to the supplementation recommendation. This, in itself, ensures that the recommended intake is met for each target group.

Conclusion

To conclude, the compliance to the vitamin D supplementation recommendation for young children 1 to 3 years old is high, however, the percentage decreases with higher age. With only half of the women over 50 and elderly using vitamin D-containing supplements, and even fewer adhering to the recommendations in terms of dosage and frequency, compliance is low and could be improved. We advise conducting further research to identify effective strategies for improving compliance with supplementation recommendations and to better understand the relationship between vitamin D status, supplement use, and sunlight exposure. These insights are essential for evaluating current guidelines and considering alternative approaches, such as food fortification.
