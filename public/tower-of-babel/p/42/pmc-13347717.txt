Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a heterogeneous and broad group of acute and chronic lung diseases of known and unknown causes, with pulmonary hypertension (PH) often developing as a sequela of chronic ILDs with a reported prevalence as high as 86% in patients with end-stage disease [1–5]. PH associated with ILD (ILD-PH) is a common complication of fibrotic ILD, independently associated with worse outcomes, including reduced exercise capacity, increased risk for hospitalisations, poor health-related quality of life and decreased survival [5–13]. The diagnosis of PH is often delayed in ILD largely due to non-specific and overlapping symptoms in both ILD and PH. The diagnosis of specific ILD subtypes is often challenging, especially at earlier stages, further complicating identification of comorbid PH and treatment regimens for specific ILDs [14].

This state-of-the-art review offers a comprehensive overview of ILD-PH building upon the proceedings of the 7th World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension (WSPH) [15]. We discuss the clinical characteristics, prevalence, prognosis, impact of comorbidities, pathogenesis, current treatment strategies and future therapeutic directions for ILD-PH.

Clinical characteristics of ILD-PH

PH is a haemodynamic condition defined by an elevated mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) >20 mmHg on right heart catheterisation (RHC). Using pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP) and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), PH can be further divided into pre-capillary PH (PAWP ≤15 mmHg and PVR >2 WU), post-capillary PH (PAWP >15 mmHg and PVR ≤2 WU) or combined post- and pre-capillary PH (PAWP >15 mmHg and PVR >2 WU) [3]. Although post-capillary PH due to cardiovascular comorbidities may coexist in patients with ILD, this review focuses primarily on isolated pre-capillary PH [2, 3]. Some guidelines have proposed using PVR >5 WU to define severe ILD-PH [2, 9]. Table 1 lists forms of ILD that have a higher likelihood of developing PH. Presenting symptoms of PH, including dyspnoea, fatigue and low exercise tolerance, overlap with the symptoms of underlying ILD or associated comorbidities, and early findings from pulmonary function testing (PFT) in ILD and ILD-PH may be indistinguishable [16–19]. Clinical features that may indicate development of PH in a patient with underlying ILD include unexplained worsening dyspnoea, exercise capacity, exertional hypoxaemia and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO) in a patient with otherwise stable PFT and parenchymal imaging [20–22]. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) imaging of the chest may show an enlarged main pulmonary artery or an increased main pulmonary artery/aorta diameter ratio, findings that should prompt further evaluation for PH [23, 24]. If the pre-test probability is higher based on the clinical evaluation that includes elevated serum natriuretic peptide (brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) and N-terminal pro-BNP (NT-proBNP)) levels, patients should proceed with transthoracic echocardiography and be referred to a PH centre for further evaluation with RHC if indicated [3].

Common interstitial lung disease (ILD) subtypes associated with pulmonary hypertension (PH)

Clinically, PH is categorised into five World Health Organization groups, based on pathophysiology, clinical features and therapeutic approaches: pulmonary arterial hypertension (group 1), primarily affecting small pulmonary arterioles without left heart disease (pre-capillary PH characterised by increased pulmonary vascular resistance in the absence of other causes of pre-capillary PH, i.e. lung diseases or thromboembolic events); PH associated with left heart disease (group 2), including valvular disease, where elevated left heart pressures lead to increased pulmonary pressures; PH associated with lung diseases and/or hypoxia (group 3, which includes ILD-PH); PH associated with pulmonary artery obstructions (group 4) caused by chronic blood clots in the lungs; and PH with unclear and/or multifactorial mechanisms (group 5) [3].

The severity of ILD based on radiography or PFTs may not correlate well with the severity of ILD-PH, suggesting that mechanisms beyond parenchymal fibrosis contribute to the development of PH [25]. A recent publication by Kim et al. [26] highlights the heterogeneity observed in ILD. Their single-centre study, which included 371 patients with RHC data available, highlighted that >50% of patients with DLCO ≤40% predicted or forced vital capacity (FVC)/DLCO % predicted ratio >1.5 still had potentially treatable PH (PVR >3 WU) [26]. This underscores the need for more refined models and methods to predict the presence of PH in the setting of ILD.

Another challenge is determining whether pre-capillary PH arising in a patient with ILD solely represents ILD-PH (WSPH group 3) versus coincident pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH; WSPH group 1). This is particularly relevant in systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases (connective tissue diseases) such as systemic sclerosis, where ILD and PAH frequently coexist. In such cases, we suggest that a careful evaluation of PH and ILD be undertaken at a PH centre. Differentiating between groups 1 and 3 is vital for PH management decisions, as their treatment approaches vary markedly. Patients with PAH who have DLCO <45% predicted or mild interstitial changes on HRCT exhibit a poorer prognosis when compared with classical PAH, similar to that of ILD-PH [27–30]. The recent 7th WSPH proceedings recommend considering the extent of lung disease using PFT and HRCT images of the chest to distinguish between WSPH groups 1 and 3 PH, although it is challenging to define specific thresholds that indicate one group over the other [3]. The 6th WSPH Task Force recommended a multiparametric approach to discriminate between group 1 versus group 3 PH [31].

Prevalence of ILD-PH

Estimating the prevalence of PH in ILD is challenging due to the lack of a standardised approach in screening for PH in patients with ILD. In the reported literature, prevalence of ILD-PH varies significantly across studies. PH has been reported as high as 86% in patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) at the time of lung transplantation [32, 33]. Demographic factors including race, sex and ethnicity may influence the development of ILD-PH, but their impact is largely unknown [34]. Furthermore, estimates of PH in ILD subgroups may vary by as much as 20% depending on the diagnostic cut-off applied [35, 36]. A recent physician survey in Europe indicated that fewer than half of patients with suspected ILD-PH may be referred for confirmatory tests (i.e. echocardiography or RHC), leading to significant underestimation of disease prevalence [16]. Further systematic studies are warranted with a standardised approach to diagnosis to estimate the prevalence of ILD-PH. The results of cohort studies such as DeciPHer-ILD (NCT06388421), a real-world patient registry study, are eagerly awaited for insights into the clinical variables, treatments and clinical outcomes of patients with ILD-PH.

Early detection of ILD-PH

Currently, there is no established screening approach for early detection of PH in the setting of ILD. Traditionally, PH in ILD has been diagnosed at advanced stages, often when patients present with clinical signs of right heart failure or during evaluation for lung transplantation. The absence of a validated screening tool presents a significant challenge for clinicians. To address this, the prospective PHINDER study (NCT05776225) has recently expanded participant recruitment to explore early detection strategies for ILD-PH [37–40]. Lately, some have derived and validated non-invasive screening tools, but they need to be further validated in a larger population [41, 42].

A 2022 Delphi consensus involving US pulmonary specialists strongly supported the importance of early diagnosis of PH in ILD [43]. Several clinical features should prompt diagnostic evaluation for PH (figure 1) [43–45].

Echocardiography is a key PH screening tool. An estimated tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV) on echocardiography can be used to estimate right ventricular (RV) systolic pressure and, when significantly elevated, TRV has a high positive predictive value for the true presence of PH on RHC [46]. At the same time, TRV may be unmeasurable or underestimated, particularly in ILD where acoustic windows may be poor [2–4, 47]. In one study of ILD patients, ∼40% of patients labelled “low probability” based on TRV were later found to have PH on RHC [47]. Therefore, ancillary signs of PH or right heart dysfunction should be considered as well, and several validated scoring systems incorporating these additional parameters have been developed for the detection of ILD-PH [41, 42].

Other imaging modalities are also gaining relevance for PH detection. A pulmonary artery/aorta ratio >1 (figure 2) on non-gated CT is more reliable than pulmonary artery diameter alone. When combined with echocardiographic estimates of systolic PAP, the predictive value improves [48, 49]. Gated CT and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging can also show enlargement of the right heart chambers, which is indicative of PH [50]. BNP and NT-proBNP levels are particularly valuable to provide an early clue in screening and are routinely used in PH detection and management [6, 51]. Prognosis of ILD-PH

ILD-PH carries significantly higher morbidity and mortality than ILD alone, and outcomes are generally worse than those reported for other causes of PH, including PAH [52, 53]. Five-year survival rates for ILD-PH range from 22% to 38% compared with 52% to 66% for PAH [54–57]. Among group 3 PH subtypes, ILD-PH has the poorest prognosis [58].

Disease severity, particularly elevated PVR, further impacts outcomes. Data from the COMPERA registry show that patients with PVR >5 WU had markedly worse survival than those with PVR ≤5 WU [9]. Based on these findings, the European Society of Cardiology (ESC)/European Respiratory Society (ERS) guidelines distinguish non-severe ILD-PH (PVR ≤5 WU) from severe ILD-PH (PVR >5 WU), although this classification is not formally used in the 7th WSPH guidelines [3, 15]. More recent data from the Pulmonary Vascular Research Institute (PVRI) GoDeep meta-registry also showed that PVR >5 WU was associated with worse survival in patients with PH associated with idiopathic interstitial pneumonias (IIPs) and in patients with IPF-PH, further suggesting this may be a useful threshold to define severe ILD-PH [59].

In IPF, mPAP >20 mmHg is independently associated with increased mortality [53]. A large international retrospective study also confirmed that mortality risk in ILD is closely linked to PH severity as measured by mPAP [8]. Despite the heterogeneity of ILD subtypes, the development of PH consistently portends a poor prognosis [20].

Impact of comorbidities

Multiple comorbidities, such as coronary artery disease, arrhythmias, emphysema, thromboembolic disease, sleep apnoea, atrial fibrillation and heart failure, can coexist with or worsen ILD-PH [15, 60–64]. These are more common in older patients, particularly in those with fibrotic ILD of unknown cause. Patients with ILD-PH are also at increased risk for complications, including infections, gastro-oesophageal reflux and lung cancer [65]. Immunosuppressive therapies may heighten infection risk, and overlapping radiological features between infection and ILD progression complicate diagnosis [65, 66]. In the absence of infection, new ground-glass opacities may signal acute exacerbation of fibrotic ILD [67].

Pathogenesis of ILD-PH

ILD is characterised by inflammatory and fibrotic damage to the lungs, including excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) in the parenchyma (figure 3) [68–70] and ultimately, architectural destruction (figure 4). PH development in ILD typically progresses over time, although with considerable variability across patients [35]. The relationship between levels of fibrosis and PH severity is not linear, as indicated by a lack of clear correlation between lung function and CT fibrosis extent with invasive haemodynamics, suggesting independent mechanisms are also relevant. Vessel loss is observed in patients who have coexistent emphysema, further reducing the alveolar surface area for effective gas exchange, hence the compound impact on oxygenation, pulmonary haemodynamics and poor outcome in patients with combined pulmonary fibrosis and emphysema (CPFE) [71].

The impact of ILD-PH on the pulmonary vasculature extends beyond what is seen at the alveolar–capillary interface, and heterogeneous vascular changes are seen throughout the lungs of patients with ILD-PH [72, 73]. This might suggest vascular involvement and its progression are not necessarily homogenous and linear but may be spatially and temporally heterogenous, while haemodynamic changes reflect the summary effect of vascular changes. Most vascular changes in ILD-PH are seen in small pulmonary arteries and arterioles, but remodelling can also involve pulmonary capillaries, veins and lymphatics to varying degrees throughout the lungs [74–77]. Pulmonary venous involvement may occur in ILD-PH and may result in poor tolerance to pulmonary vasodilators. A final vascular histological observation is that of neoangiogenesis in patients with ILD-PH with variable pulmonary–systemic anastomoses, which likely contributes to hypoxaemia [78, 79].

Acute exacerbations of lung disease can worsen pulmonary haemodynamics through hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction as well as alveolar injury and decreased vascular compliance [80, 81]. Chronic hypoxia leads to both medial hypertrophy in muscular small pulmonary arteries, alongside neomuscularisation in previously non-muscular arterioles [82–84]. This usually leads to a mild degree of pulmonary vascular remodelling with non-severe PH and is well studied in terms of implicated mediators (through the transcription factor hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1α). Given that the lung is unlike other organs that vasodilate with hypoxia, this mild increased pulmonary vascular tone probably protects ventilation/perfusion matching in patients with lung disease. In addition, intermittent hypoxia [85, 86] and hypercapnia are likely to be relevant in the onset of ILD-PH, e.g. in those with associated sleep disorders [87]. Finally, exercise-induced hypoxia likely has an influence on ILD-PH onset [88], as this condition involves both increased flow and tone, recognised cofactors in PH onset.

Severe pulmonary vascular remodelling can occur in ILD-PH which, in rare cases, may include PAH-like plexiform lesions. Histopathological studies have shown that the extent of remodelling correlates with haemodynamic severity (figure 3) [76]. Onset of a more severe pulmonary vasculopathy is likely to share mediators relevant in PAH. Additional driving factors in patients with ILD may include cigarette smoke [89–92] and viral infections. Genetic predisposition is also likely but not well studied in ILD-PH (e.g. mutations in BMP receptors) [93]. Relevant circulating mediators include pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. interleukin (IL)-1, IL-6, tumour necrosis factor-α, RANTES (CCL5)), and pro-remodelling and profibrotic signalling molecules (e.g. platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), endothelin-1, fibroblast growth factor (FGF) 2, transforming growth factor-β, HIF-1α and vascular endothelial growth factor) [94–104]. This pro-inflammatory, pro-proliferative, anti-apoptotic and profibrotic environment drives both endothelial dysfunction and ultimately remodelling of the pulmonary smooth muscle vasculature, with these pathways being potential treatment targets for ILD-PH.

Ultimately, the overall reduction in perfusable vasculature in patients with ILD-PH, driven by remodelling of pulmonary arteries, loss of pulmonary vessels and impaired vasodilatory capacity, results in increased PVR. This causes exercise limitation in patients with ILD-PH more due to cardiovascular dysfunction than ventilatory impairment [105–107]. In time, reduced pulmonary arterial compliance and increasing pulmonary arterial resistance impose limits on RV cardiac output and increase RV wall stress and consequently, myocardial oxygen demand. Initially, adaptation of the right ventricle leads to hypertrophy. Eventually, the right ventricle dilates, establishing a maladaptive process resulting in RV uncoupling and failure, significant exercise limitation, and increased mortality. The poorly compliant lung parenchyma also limits RV diastolic filling and therefore any adaptive increases in stroke volume. Together, these effects may lead to RV–pulmonary arterial uncoupling and progressive RV failure.

Therapeutic strategies

Overview

Treatment strategies targeting the inflammatory, fibrotic and vascular components of the abnormal distal pulmonary parenchyma are appealing. In the early 2000s, researchers began exploring whether drugs approved for PAH may benefit patients with fibrotic lung disease due to the potential antifibrotic effects of these drugs, as well as their potential for treating PH associated with fibrotic lung disease (table 2). Clinical trials of PAH drugs had mixed results and were largely proven unsuccessful in IPF and ILD-PH [108–114], until positive results were seen with inhaled treprostinil in the phase 3 INCREASE study of inhaled treprostinil [12, 21, 115, 116]. Prostacyclin analogues

Prostacyclin analogues have drawn interest for their ability to regulate fibrotic pathways and increase blood flow by stimulating the production of cAMP. While the pharmacological profile of prostacyclin class therapies such as iloprost and treprostinil differs considerably depending on the receptor being targeted, they can lead to vasodilation and reduced vascular remodelling, antifibrotic effects via reduced fibroblast activity and proliferation and deposition of collagens, and reduced lung inflammation [117–131].

Prior to the 2021 INCREASE trial discussed below, data for prostacyclin analogues in ILD-PH were mixed. In an early physiological study, inhaled iloprost at the time of RHC was shown to acutely reduce mPAP and PVR in a small cohort of patients with pulmonary fibrosis [132]. However, a subsequent randomised double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of inhaled iloprost in 51 patients with IPF and PH did not show benefit in 6-min walk distance (6MWD), New York Heart Association Functional Class, dyspnoea scores or exertional oxygenation when compared with placebo [133]. A small 2014 open-label study assessed the impact of parenteral treprostinil in pulmonary fibrosis patients with an advanced PH phenotype, and found improvements in right heart haemodynamics and echocardiographic function, although robust randomised trial data on parenteral prostacyclins in ILD-PH are lacking [134]. A randomised trial of inhaled treprostinil in ILD-PH (INCREASE) met its primary end-point, demonstrating a significant improvement in 6MWD, with findings published in 2021 [115]. Regarding secondary end-points for efficacy, a lower risk of clinical worsening, as well as NT-proBNP reduction, were seen with inhaled treprostinil during the 16-week randomised study period. Further, in a post hoc analysis of the INCREASE study, patients who received inhaled treprostinil had fewer ILD exacerbations and clinical worsening events and decreases in NT-proBNP levels [115, 135]. Along with continued safety, reduction in risk of underlying lung disease exacerbation was also found in the phase 3 open-label extension over 52 weeks [136]. A Japanese single-arm trial of inhaled treprostinil at the same dose and duration as that in the INCREASE trial demonstrated improvements in haemodynamics and a safety profile consistent with prior studies [137]. The INCREASE trial showed a placebo-corrected improvement in FVC, which suggested inhaled treprostinil has the potential of being an antifibrotic drug, a concept being pursued in the TETON studies in patients with IPF and progressive pulmonary fibrosis (PPF) [138, 139]. Positive results of the TETON-2 trial were recently reported, suggesting possible direct antifibrotic effects in IPF [140].

Inhaled treprostinil can be administered as a nebulised formulation or a dry powder inhaler. For patients with significant respiratory muscle dysfunction associated with ILD-PH, high-resistance, low-flow dry powder inhalers may provide a greater probability of achieving effective doses compared to other dry powder inhalers [141, 142]. The most common side-effects of inhaled treprostinil are cough (43.6%), headache (27.6%) and nausea (15.3%). The duration and severity of these side-effects may change over time, particularly during the initial titration period as patients establish a stable, effective dose [143]. Effective side-effect management and necessary treatment adjustments may support treatment adherence [143, 144].

Phosphodiesterase 5 inhibitors

Clinical trials of several phosphodiesterase 5 inhibitors (PDE5i), including sildenafil, have been performed in ILD and ILD-PH [19, 108, 109, 113, 145–153]. Observational studies have shown that PDE5i may improve survival outcomes, particularly if started early in ILD-PH disease progression [59, 150], before the onset of RV dysfunction [150]. A recent analysis of the PVRI GoDeep meta-registry suggested that PDE5i might improve survival in severe IIP-PH and IPF-PH [59]. Clinical improvements have been seen in patients with IPF and likely ILD-PH in some sildenafil studies [19, 147–151, 153], but small sample sizes and lack of placebo groups limit interpretation [21]. No prospective randomised placebo-controlled study has yet been performed in patients with RHC-confirmed ILD-PH using sildenafil. Neither the STEP-IPF trial nor the INSTAGE trial, which evaluated sildenafil alone or in tandem with the antifibrotic nintedanib, or the SP-IPF trial, which investigated sildenafil on top of pirfenidone, met their primary end-points [109, 113, 145, 154].

Endothelin receptor antagonists

Endothelin-1, a potent vasoconstrictor and growth factor, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis [5, 155]. Endothelins have been implicated in fibrosis, particularly in the stimulation of myofibroblast differentiation and ECM production. Biological samples from patients with ILD have indicated that endothelins are overexpressed in fibrotic lung tissue and may be associated with ILD severity [156]. As such, endothelin receptor antagonists (ERA) were considered promising for the treatment of IPF and ILD-PH because they targeted both vasodilation and antifibrotic pathways [5].

The trials of the oral dual ERA bosentan in subjects with IPF did not meet their primary end-points [111, 112], and a trial of the single ERA ambrisentan was terminated due to increased adverse events [157]. The ARIES and ARTEMIS trials of ambrisentan had mixed results. In the open-label ARIES-3 study evaluating efficacy and safety of ambrisentan in patients with various PH aetiologies and background PH medications, the primary end-point of improvement in 6MWD was met; however, in the ILD-specific subgroup, 6MWD decreased [158]. Improvements in 6MWD were not seen in ARTEMIS-IPF or ARTEMIS-PH, and risk of disease progression and respiratory hospitalisations were found to be increased, suggesting harm [157, 159].

Patients with IPF similarly saw no clinical improvement in PFT, disease progression or survival with macitentan in the phase 2 MUSIC trial [160]. ERA did not demonstrate significant clinical improvements in IPF or in ILD-PH and may even be harmful, and as such ambrisentan is contraindicated in patients with IPF [15].

Nitric oxide

Nitric oxide (NO) is a well-documented vasodilator due to its role as a stimulator of the cyclisation of guanosine triphosphate to cGMP; cGMP production also stimulates the relaxation and dedifferentiation of myofibroblasts, thereby reducing fibrosis [161–163]. Inhaled NO has been shown to protect against the development of PH in rats [164]. Lower levels of exhaled NO have been associated with PH and ILD [165], although NO was not found to be predictive of PH or mortality [166]. However, raising NO levels in patients with ILD-PH may not be a feasible, long-term option for most patients. While some studies have indicated that inhaled NO is beneficial to patients, clinical trials of inhaled NO in ILD-PH have been unsuccessful [167, 168]. Recently, the REBUILD trial investigating inhaled NO was terminated due to lack of efficacy [168].

Soluble guanylate cyclase stimulators

An alternative method to increase cGMP levels for the treatment of ILD-PH is the use of soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) stimulators, such as riociguat. The phase 2b RISE-IIP study of riociguat in patients with PH associated with IIPs or ILD-PH was terminated early due to adverse events [114]. On the basis of the results from RISE-IIP, riociguat is contraindicated in PH associated with IIP [15].

Treatment considerations and knowledge gaps

In patients with ILD and suspected PH, proper diagnosis and individualised management of ILD-PH at PH centres is recommended [2, 3, 21]. Treatment of ILD-PH includes optimised treatment of the underlying lung disease and, where indicated, hypoxaemia, sleep disordered breathing and/or alveolar hypoventilation [2]. In cases of hypoxaemia (partial pressure of oxygen <8 kPa (60 mmHg)) at rest and in patients who desaturate to ≥88% during 6-min walk tests, supplemental oxygen is recommended, although the evidence of supplemental oxygen improving quality of life or survival is limited [2, 5]. Acknowledging the evolving data, the 2022 ESC/ERS PH guidelines recommend that inhaled treprostinil may be considered in patients with ILD-PH, where available (level B), and consideration of PDE5i in patients with severe ILD-PH (PVR >5 WU; individual decision making in PH centres: level C) [2]. The 7th WSPH proceedings suggest parameters favouring treatment of PH in patients with ILD include the presence of systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease, haemodynamics (mPAP ≥25 mmHg and PVR ≥4 WU), mild-to-moderate ventilatory impairment or the presence of pulmonary vascular limitation to exercise of cardiopulmonary exercise testing, higher NT-proBNP, less extensive fibrotic ILD and the availability of therapies [15]. Less severe PH associated with ILD

Current evidence from randomised clinical trials does not directly address the patient population with mPAP between 21 and 24 mmHg and/or PVR between 2 and 4 WU. The INCREASE trial of inhaled treprostinil, for example, enrolled patients with mPAP ≥25 mmHg and PVR ≥3 WU according to the contemporary haemodynamic definition of PH [115]. The 7th WSPH recommends the updated haemodynamic definition (discussed above) for diagnosis but suggested that clinical trial inclusion and treatment decisions in practice be restricted to patients with PVR ≥4 WU, partially based on prespecified subgroup analysis of patients with PVR <4 WU in the INCREASE trial showing no improvement in 6MWD [15, 115]. However, the INCREASE study was not powered to detect 6MWD improvement in this subgroup, and the patient population with less severe haemodynamics in INCREASE potentially had benefit in other arenas in post hoc analyses, such as lung disease exacerbations and clinical worsening events [135].

This creates an important “grey zone” of patients with mild elevations, namely those with mPAP 21–24 mmHg and PVR 2–4 WU, who are increasingly recognised in clinical practice but have not been adequately studied. Whether these patients should receive pulmonary vasodilator therapy is unclear.

Another key uncertainty is the natural history of less severe PH in ILD. It remains unclear whether all such patients inevitably progress to more advanced disease if left untreated. Although no current guidelines recommend PH drugs for this population, observational data suggest that mild haemodynamic impairment may progress, sometimes rapidly, with notable increases in mPAP and PVR over time. In a cohort analysis, Nathan et al. [35] showed that in patients with mild PH associated with IPF, the median (interquartile range) rate of change in mPAP and PVR was 3.8 (−0.9–11.8) mmHg·year−1 and 0.8 (−0.2–2.4) WU·year−1, respectively. In the recent analysis from the PVRI GoDeep registry, the most recent definition of pre-capillary PH (mPAP >20 mmHg, PVR >2 WU and PAWP ≤15 mmHg) was applied. PDE5i treatment was associated with improved survival in patients with severe PH for IIP-PH, IPF-PH and IIP-PH with nintedanib or pirfenidone background therapy, but not in patients with less severe haemodynamic impairment (PVR ≤5 WU) [59]. This study suggested a survival advantage with inhaled prostacyclin analogues in patients with IIP-PH. Furthermore, the study indicates that combined PDE5i and inhaled prostacyclin analogue therapy might confer greater survival benefit than PDE5i monotherapy, an observation of particular interest given the widespread use of PDE5i as background therapy in ILD-PH patients, despite its exclusion from the INCREASE trial [59].

These findings highlight a critical knowledge gap: even if we lack the evidence supporting treatment of these patients, close clinical follow-up is therefore essential, integrating symptom evolution, gas exchange abnormalities, biomarkers such as NT-proBNP and repeat haemodynamic assessment when clinically indicated. For patients with PVR in the 3–4 WU range, an individualised approach may be warranted. Treatment decisions in these patients should be made within PH centres, balancing haemodynamic severity with clinical burden, comorbidities and the extent of underlying parenchymal disease. Further, it will be crucial to define clinically meaningful treatment response in these patients. Regardless of haemodynamic severity, eligible patients with ILD and PH should be referred for lung transplantation evaluation, given the overall poor prognosis and the limited availability of evidence-based therapies [35]. While patients with ILD-PH who receive lung transplants often experience an improvement in quality of life and survival, the median survival after lung transplant with IPF is only ∼5 years [21, 169], highlighting the need for better therapies to prevent or delay need for transplant.

Challenges associated with PH associated with CPFE

PH associated with CPFE (CPFE-PH) represents a complex and often under-recognised clinical entity characterised by coexisting upper-lobe emphysema and lower-lobe fibrosis, most commonly in heavy smokers (figure 6), as well as in non-smokers with scleroderma. Patients with CPFE frequently develop PH, which significantly contributes to morbidity and mortality in this population. In a cohort study, the risk of developing PH was much higher in CPFE than in IPF without emphysema [170]. The prognosis of CPFE is worse than that of IPF without emphysema, an outcome determined by severe PH and not only by the presence of associated emphysema. The pathogenesis of PH in CPFE is multifactorial and not very well understood, but it likely involves vascular remodelling due to chronic hypoxia, destruction of the pulmonary vascular bed by emphysematous changes, inflammation and fibrotic obliteration of pulmonary capillaries [171]. CPFE is often associated with severe gas exchange abnormalities, including a marked reduction in DLCO, severe hypoxaemia and preserved lung volumes on spirometry, which can delay diagnosis if not carefully assessed.

Management of CPFE-PH includes optimising the underlying lung disease, smoking cessation, supplemental oxygen, pulmonary rehabilitation and management of comorbidities [2]. HRCT is essential for the identification of this phenotype, and it should be correlated with another physiological evaluation such as PFT, which generally shows preserved lung volumes but markedly reduced DLCO [172].

Prognosis is generally poor, especially once PH develops, with median survival significantly shortened [173]. Hence, early recognition and comprehensive multidisciplinary management are critical.

CPFE represents a unique entity with important treatment considerations, as the extent of emphysema may affect how patients respond to PH drugs [19, 174]. A post hoc analysis of the RISE-IIP study suggested that patients whose HRCT of the chest had more emphysema than fibrosis may have contributed to the negative outcomes of the trial [174]. This is consistent with the PERFECT study testing inhaled treprostinil in patients with PH, which was terminated early due to suggestive evidence of increased risk of serious adverse events and mortality in COPD-PH [175, 176]. In the INCREASE trial, there appeared to be attenuated efficacy of inhaled treprostinil in the subgroup with CPFE with respect to 6MWD and disease progression events, although such subgroup analyses are underpowered and must be interpreted with caution [12, 115]. Therefore, given the data from RISE-IIP, INCREASE and PERFECT trials, future studies on CPFE-PH should target patients with predominantly fibrotic and less emphysematous phenotypes. In addition, future registries should collect high-quality HRCT data to better understand whether there is a difference in response to treatment based on a particular CPFE phenotype. Lung transplantation remains a definitive option in eligible patients with progressive disease despite optimal therapy. Ongoing research is focused on better characterising the pulmonary vascular phenotype not only in CPFE but in smokers who present with PH for evaluation [92]. With emerging treatments such as sotatercept and seralutinib being investigated in other PH groups, or mosliciguat in ILD-PH, future trials may provide insights into their applicability in CPFE-PH [177–179].

Future directions

Registry efforts to characterise ILD-PH

The use of registry data, rather than claims data, has been proposed as a more accurate means of characterising ILD-PH and its long-term outcomes [180]. The Spanish Registry for PH Associated with Respiratory Diseases (REHAR) collected data from across 13 PH centres, with the aim of better defining the characteristics of PH associated with respiratory diseases. REHAR includes patients with COPD, ILD and CPFE [181]. A registry of patients with group 3 ILD-PH (DeciPHer-ILD) is currently actively recruiting subjects in the USA. Multiple European registries are actively enrolling patients with ILD-PH [9, 55, 58, 59, 150, 182]. ASPIRE (Assessing the Spectrum of PH Identified at a REferral centre) is unique in maintaining the imaging database and providing insightful information in relation to cardiac, pulmonary and pulmonary vascular imaging in patients with PH [183].

Therapies currently in development

There is a lack of information about the potential effect of currently approved antifibrotic therapies on the development of PH [21]. Furthermore, given the positive results of TETON-2, a novel question is whether prostacyclin therapies might reduce the risk of incident PH in patients with fibrotic ILDs such as IPF. However, this hypothesis should be considered with great caution and warrants further research. Several therapies currently being evaluated or approved in PAH may also be promising candidates for the treatment of ILD-PH.

Since inhaled medications have fewer systemic side-effects and decreased probability of potential ventilation/perfusion mismatches, inhaled therapeutics are an attractive option for ILD-PH. Inhaled treprostinil has demonstrated efficacy in ILD-PH [184], and inhaled formulations of the sGC stimulator fraspaciguat [185–187] and the PDE5i vardenafil (RT234) (NCT04266197) are also currently in clinical trials for COPD-PH and PAH, respectively. Recently, positive results from the phase 1B ATMOS trial investigating the inhaled sGC activator mosliciguat in ILD-PH were announced, and the phase 2 PHocus trial (NCT06635850) has recently completed enrolment (table 3) [188]. Additionally, a new, inhaled liposomal formulation of treprostinil (L606) as well as treprostinil palmitil (a long-lasting formulation for once-daily inhalation) are being evaluated in phase 2B/3 trials (NCT04691154).

Overexpression of growth factors, including FGF, epidermal growth factor and PDGF (figure 3), is well documented in ILD-PH [189]. Nintedanib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), is currently approved for the treatment of various forms of ILD [190–193]. Limited data suggest that nintedanib does not have direct benefits on pulmonary vascular disease [194]. Inhaled seralutinib, a TKI signalling through PDGF and colony-stimulating factor 1 receptors, met the primary outcome of change in PVR in the phase 2 TORREY study in PAH [179] and is expected to be investigated in a pivotal phase 3 study (NCT07181382) in ILD-PH [178].

Sotatercept, an activin signalling inhibitor, has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of PAH on the basis of the outcomes of the pivotal phase 3 STELLAR trial, which demonstrated improvements in the primary end-point of 6MWD [177]. Sotatercept has not been assessed in patients with ILD-PH, and there are no trials announced in ILD-PH to date [177].

Clinical trial end-points

End-points for registrational phase 3 trials in ILD-PH, like those for any condition, must reflect how a patient feels, functions or survives. A recent consensus statement from the PVRI's Innovative Drug Development Initiative provides some guidance for determining successful study end-points in ILD-PH [195]. 6MWD has been routinely used as a primary end-point in PH clinical studies, including ILD-PH (table 2), and change in 6MWD was the basis of a FDA approval for ILD-PH therapy [115, 196]. Other measures of exercise capacity and function in daily living using accelerometry devices hold promise and have been used in previous ILD trials [168, 195, 197]. Haemodynamic end-points, such as change in PVR, are commonly used but are appropriate only for early-stage trials, as in PAH [198, 199]. However, end-points may vary depending on the purpose of the study demonstrating preventative or reparative efficacy.

Composite end-points of two or more efficacy outcomes may be more appropriate primary outcomes, especially for phase 3 trials [195]. Recently, the “win ratio” has been proposed as a new method of evaluating composite end-points based on a hierarchy of components according to clinical relevance [198, 200–202], which may be attractive for future ILD-PH trials. Additionally, patient-reported outcome measures could be considered in clinical trials as primary or secondary outcomes and can also be included as components in composite end-points [195].

ILD-PH trial designers must consider their dosing strategy when determining duration. However, clinical trials that are too long run the risk of significant patient dropout, which may be due to compliance or mortality. For phase 2 or 3 studies with functional end-points, an intermediate length (12–26 weeks) may be appropriate, but studies >26 weeks and event-driven are needed for clinical end-points such as clinical worsening or mortality [195]. Ultimately, innovations to ILD-PH clinical trial end-points should strive to improve trial efficiency, clinical meaningfulness of the measurements or patient experience during trial participation.

Conclusions

The identification and management of ILD-PH are challenging, in part due to the complex interactions between the vascular and interstitial components of the disease, inevitable progression of both components, and the challenges of therapeutic thresholds. Recent improvements in the early detection of PH in ILD, new understanding of the pathogenesis of ILD-PH, the ability to access more robust patient data from registries and recent evidence-based recommendations have advanced our understanding and management of this serious disease [2, 3, 15, 21]. It is hoped that patients with ILD-PH will feel and function better and survive longer, with inhaled treprostinil in clinical use and with other drugs in development for treatment of ILD-PH.
