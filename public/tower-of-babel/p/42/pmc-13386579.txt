The salient role of childhood neglect in single and multiple suicide attempts among adult psychiatric inpatients
Authors: Noémi Szeifert, Balázs Fábián, Xenia Gonda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Background: A growing body of evidence identifies childhood trauma, encompassing both abuse and neglect, as a potent predictor of suicide attempts across the lifespan. Clear delineation of its mechanisms is essential for advancing trauma-informed psychiatric care. Objective: This study examined the role of childhood maltreatment in single and multiple suicide attempts among adult psychiatric inpatients. Method: In this cross-sectional study, 300 adult psychiatric inpatients, with and without a history of suicide attempts, were assessed using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire – Short Form (CTQ-SF), alongside the collection of sociodemographic and clinical data. Binary logistic regression was employed to evaluate the associations between childhood trauma, psychiatric diagnoses, and suicide attempt history, distinguishing between single and multiple attempts. Results: All CTQ-SF domains were significantly intercorrelated (p < .01), with the strongest associations between emotional neglect and physical neglect (ρ = 0.708) and between emotional neglect and emotional abuse (ρ = 0.683). Emotional neglect was the most prevalent trauma type (93%), whereas sexual abuse was the least common (27%); 90% of participants reported two or more trauma types. Emotional abuse was associated with increased odds of any suicide attempt [odds ratio (OR) = 1.04], while physical neglect was associated with increased odds of multiple attempts (OR = 1.14). Conclusion: Our findings indicate that most forms of childhood trauma may contribute to repeated suicide attempts. Neglect exerts a heightened effect when co-occurring with other maltreatment types, suggesting a dose–response relationship. This synergistic impact underscores the need for comprehensive trauma assessments that capture both the severity and multiplicity of adverse experiences.KEYWORDS: Childhood maltreatment, trauma, neglect, borderline personality disorder, mental disorders, suicidality

Introduction

Both suicide and child maltreatment are major global public health concerns. Suicide accounts for approximately 720,000 deaths annually (WHO, 2025) and remains one of the leading causes of premature mortality, particularly among individuals with psychiatric disorders. Although over 90% of those who die by suicide have a diagnosable mental disorder – most commonly, major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, or substance use disorders – the aetiology of suicidal behaviour is complex and extends beyond diagnostic categories (Balázs et al., 2000; Rihmer et al., 2017; Szeifert, Bálint, et al., 2025). Suicidal acts are shaped by a dynamic interplay of distal factors, such as genetic vulnerability, impulsivity, and early-life adversities; and proximal factors, such as acute psychiatric symptoms, interpersonal crises, and access to means (Joiner, 2005; Osvath et al., 2004; Turecki & Brent, 2016).

Among distal risk factors, childhood maltreatment – including physical, sexual, and emotional abuse and neglect – is consistently associated with suicidal behaviour across the lifespan (Angelakis et al., 2019, 2020; Felitti et al., 1998; Norman et al., 2012). Such adversities can disrupt emotional regulation, attachment, and stress-response systems, impair immune function, and increase vulnerability to both psychiatric and somatic disorders (Bruffaerts et al., 2010; Kovács-Tóth & Kuritárné Szabó, 2023; van der Kolk, 2005). Trauma exposure is highly prevalent in clinical populations, with estimates ranging from 30% to 80%, and is linked to earlier illness onset, greater symptom severity, higher comorbidity, and poorer treatment outcomes in mood, anxiety, psychotic, and personality disorders (Bozzatello et al., 2021; LeMoult et al., 2020; Varese et al., 2012). Global retrospective data indicate that 23% of adults report physical abuse, 36% emotional abuse, and 16% physical neglect during childhood, while 20% of women and 5–10% of men report sexual abuse (WHO, 2024). These figures are likely to underestimate true prevalence owing to cultural norms and underreporting.

The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) framework highlights the cumulative impact of early adversities on health and mortality, demonstrating a strong graded relationship between ACE scores and suicide risk (Dube et al., 2001; Felitti et al., 1998). Meta-analyses confirm that all forms of abuse elevate suicide risk, particularly when multiple adversities co-occur (Angelakis et al., 2019; Enns et al., 2006). However, neglect remains underrecognized despite evidence of its profound impact on self-concept, emotional regulation, and the development of essential skills, all of which may significantly increase vulnerability to suicidal behaviour (Horváth et al., 2024).

In Hungary, suicide rates were among the highest in Europe during the 1980s. Although they have declined by more than 50% since then – attributed to advances in mental healthcare, pharmacological treatment, and socioeconomic improvements – rates remain high relative to European averages (Almasi et al., 2009; Hungarian Central Statistical Office, 2025; Rihmer et al., 2013). Cultural acceptance of physical punishment and household dysfunction historically hindered early detection of child maltreatment, and recognition of its psychological impact has only gained traction in recent decades (Kovács-Tóth & Kuritárné Szabó, 2023). Recent national statistics indicate that neglect and emotional abuse are the most prevalent forms of child maltreatment; however, reporting remains substantially incomplete (Ujhelyi Nagy et al., 2019). Estimates suggest that only approximately 3% of maltreatment cases are officially recorded, indicating that roughly one in 30 incidents is captured in official statistics (Kovács-Tóth & Kuritárné Szabó, 2023).

A previous suicide attempt is the strongest predictor of future attempts or death by suicide (Hom et al., 2016 ). Multiple attempters often present with greater psychiatric comorbidity, higher trauma exposure, and more severe clinical profiles compared to single attempters (Angelakis et al., 2019; Beghi et al., 2013; Osváth et al., 2003). Despite the relevance of this distinction, large-scale Hungarian studies comparing trauma histories between these groups are scarce (Rihmer et al., 2009).

The present study aims to examine the predictive role of childhood trauma in differentiating single from multiple suicide attempts among adult psychiatric inpatients in Hungary, with a particular focus on neglect as a potential key risk factor.

Method

Participants and procedure

Only inpatients aged 18 years and older were included in the study. Participants were recruited from the Crisis Intervention and Psychiatric Ward of Péterfy Sándor Hospital in Budapest, Hungary, between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2021. This cohort has been described previously in our earlier publications (Szeifert, Bálint, et al., 2025; Szeifert, Oláh & Gonda, 2025; Szeifert, Olah, et al., 2025); however, the present study addresses a distinct research question and reports novel analyses. Psychiatric diagnoses were determined using the Structured Clinical Interview for the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th Edition (DSM-IV) and the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10), administered by psychiatrists and trained clinical psychologists. Exclusion criteria included acute psychotic episodes, current alcohol or substance abuse (including acute intoxication), schizophrenia, a history of traumatic brain injury or neurological disorders (e.g. dementia), and serious medical conditions.

Ethical approval

All participants took part in the study voluntarily and provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Péterfy Sándor Hospital and Outpatient Center (approval number 25/2016). The authors affirm that all procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the relevant national and institutional review boards and with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1975), as revised in 2013.

Measures

After providing written informed consent, participants completed the assessments in paper-and-pencil format. Demographic data were collected through self-report questionnaires and supplemented with information from the hospital’s electronic records, following patients’ informed consent. Collected variables included age, gender, number of children, marital status, place of residence, educational level, employment status, financial status, and psychiatric history. Admission diagnoses were established based on detailed clinical assessments. Additional data were gathered on the circumstances of suicide attempts, including the number of previous attempts, age at first attempts, and motivational factors. Suicide attempts were assessed using a combination of self-report questionnaires, in which inpatients were asked about any history of suicide attempts and structured clinical interviews conducted by psychiatrists and trained clinical psychologists. Cases involving non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) were excluded from the suicide attempt group.

The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire – Short Form (CTQ-SF) is a widely used self-report instrument designed to assess the severity and frequency of childhood abuse and neglect across five domains: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect. It consists of 28 items rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never true) to 5 (very often true), providing both a total trauma score and individual subscale scores for each trauma type. The CTQ has demonstrated strong internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values typically ranging from 0.70 to 0.95 across subscales (Bernstein et al., 2003). In the present sample, internal reliability ranged from Cronbach’s α = 0.630 to 0.914, indicating acceptable to excellent reliability. The Hungarian adaptation of the CTQ was developed by Csernela et al. (2021).

The Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV Axis I Disorders (SCID-I) was used as a semi-structured interview to diagnose major DSM-IV Axis I disorders. The Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV Axis II Disorders (SCID-II) was employed to assess personality disorders (First et al., 1997).

This study does not provide a detailed analysis of gender differences, because of the unequal distribution of male and female participants. The role of psychiatric diagnosis in relation to childhood trauma was also not examined, as these findings have been reported in a previous publication (Szeifert et al., 2026).

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Kruskal–Wallis tests, Mann–Whitney U-tests, and chi-squared (χ2) tests were applied to examine group differences, as appropriate. When multi-group comparisons were significant, Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc tests were conducted for pairwise comparisons. Spearman’s rank correlation was applied to assess associations between continuous variables. Binary logistic regression analyses were performed to determine whether independent variables differentiated between (1) patients with no suicide attempts versus those reporting one or multiple attempts, and (2) patients with a single attempt versus those with multiple attempts. Independent variables entered into the initial models included all available demographic (age, gender, residence, family status, working status, financial status, number of children) and clinical variables (diagnosis, CTQ subscales). A backward stepwise (likelihood ratio) procedure was applied, with all variables entered simultaneously at the initial step. Variables were then sequentially removed based on the likelihood ratio criterion, with a retention threshold of p < .05. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), applying a conservative threshold of 4. No evidence of problematic multicollinearity was observed, with VIF values ranging from 1.106 to 2.925. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported as measures of association. Missing data were minimal (< 5% for all variables) and were imputed using the participant’s median score for continuous variables. Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Results

In total, 300 inpatient participants (27.7% male, 72.3% female) aged 18–85 years (M = 37.99, SD = 13.27) were included in the study. Diagnoses (including major and comorbid conditions) comprised anxiety disorders (53.0%), major depressive disorder (46.7%), borderline personality disorder (29.7%), personality disorder not otherwise specified (26.3%), and bipolar disorder (16.7%). Additional comorbidities were also common: 30.3% had alcohol use disorder, 15.0% illicit drug use disorder, and 24.0% prescription drug misuse. Nicotine dependence was reported by 35.3% of participants.

Among participants with a history of suicide attempts (48.7%), 27.7% reported a single attempt, 21.2% reported two attempts, and 22.0% reported three or more. The age at first attempt varied: 11.0% occurred before the age of 18 years, 33.6% between 18 and 29, 28.1% between 30 and 44, 16.4% between 45 and 59, and 4.1% at 60 years or older. Post-discharge follow-up indicated that among individuals with prior attempts, 24.7% made another attempt within 1 month, 11.0% within 3 months, 5.5% within 6 months, and 12.3% within 1 year of discharge. Among those with a history of attempts, 27.5% were male and 72.6% female, consistent with evidence that non-violent suicide attempts and psychiatric service utilization are more common among women.

The most frequently reported reasons for the most recent attempt were relationship conflict (37.0%), divorce or break-up (31.5%), and financial hardship (15.8%). Additional motives included chronic illness (17.1%), job loss (13.3%), bereavement due to the loss of a close relative (8.9%), and other unspecified reasons (37.0%), such as loneliness, social isolation, abortion, gambling addiction, situational crises (e.g. increased workplace or school stress), harassment, and violence.

Emotional neglect was the most prevalent trauma type (93%), while sexual abuse was the least common (27%). Other trauma types included emotional abuse (83.3%), physical neglect (74.3%), and physical abuse (54.7%). Only nine participants (3.0%) reported no trauma, 26 (8.7%) reported one type, and the vast majority (271 participants, 90.3%) had experienced two or more trauma types, confirming the high co-occurrence observed in the correlation analysis. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to examine the differences in CTQ total and subscale scores across the three subgroups (no attempt vs single attempt vs multiple attempts). Significant overall group differences were observed for physical abuse (H = 6.93, p = .031), emotional abuse (H = 8.46, p = .015), physical neglect (H = 12.11, p = .002), emotional neglect (H = 6.05, p = .049), and CTQ total score (H = 10.38, p = .006). No significant differences were found between patients with a single suicide attempt and those with multiple attempts or between patients with no attempts and those with a single attempt (p > .05) (Table 4).

Predictors of suicide risk

Binary logistic regression was conducted to identify predictors of having at least one suicide attempt compared to no suicide attempt. As shown in Table 5, in Model 1, a family history of suicide (OR= 1.99, 95% CI 1.21–3.30, p = .007), medication dependency (OR = 2.05, 95% CI 1.16–3.64, p = .014), and a diagnosis of personality disorder not otherwise specified (OR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.32–0.94, p = .029) were significant predictors. The emotional abuse subscale of the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ-EA) showed a trend-level association (p = .066). In Model 2, after removing the family history of suicide variable, CTQ-EA emerged as a significant predictor (OR = 1.04, 95% CI 1.00–1.09, p = .049), along with medication dependency (OR = 2.14, 95% CI 1.22–3.78, p = .008) and personality disorder not otherwise specified (OR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.32–0.95, p = .031). These findings suggest that the predictive effect of emotional abuse may be suppressed when family history of suicide is included in the model.

Repeated suicide-related factors

No significant differences were observed in age at first suicide attempt between patients with single and multiple attempts (U = 2057, p = .44). Age at suicide attempt was significantly correlated with CTQ emotional abuse scores (Spearman’s ρ = –0.29, p = .001), but not with other CTQ subscales or the total score. When asking the patients about the motivational background behind their suicide attempt, some gender differences were identified. More men reported job loss (25%) compared to women (9.3%) (Fisher’s exact test, p = .013), while fewer men reported relationship issues (22.5%) than women (42.5%) (Fisher’s exact test, p = .034). No significant gender differences were found regarding existential, divorce, death of a relative, or chronic illness-related motivations. Patients with a family history of suicide had significantly higher scores on CTQ physical neglect (U = 1940, p = .008), emotional neglect (U = 2021, p = .021), and the CTQ total score (U = 1986, p = .014) compared to those without such a history.

Discussion

This study found that nearly half (48.7%) of adult psychiatric inpatients reported at least one suicide attempt, with a relatively even split between single (27.7%) and multiple attempts (21.2%). Previous studies have identified specific psychiatric disorders as significant risk factors for repeated suicide attempts (Angelakis et al., 2019; Beghi et al., 2013; Osváth et al., 2003), which is consistent with our findings that major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, and borderline personality disorder were significant predictors of suicide attempts. In addition, multiple attempters demonstrated greater psychiatric complexity, characterized by higher levels of comorbidity and more pronounced functional impairment. Whereas personality disorder not otherwise specified was more prevalent among single attempters, borderline personality disorder was significantly more common among multiple attempters, suggesting a more severe and pervasive pattern of psychopathology. This finding is in line with evidence indicating that repeated suicidal behaviour is associated with greater clinical severity, chronicity, and psychosocial dysfunction. Major depressive disorder had the highest prevalence in our sample (46.6%) and significantly predicted both any suicide attempt (OR = 1.89) and multiple attempts (OR = 3.67), reinforcing its central role in suicidality (Hawton et al., 2012; Mann et al., 2005; Rihmer et al., 2006, 2007). Repeated attempts were also strongly linked to bipolar disorder (OR = 4.00) and borderline personality disorder (OR = 2.97), which are characterized by affective instability, impulsivity, and chronic suicidal ideation (Osváth et al., 2003; Rihmer et al., 2013).

Notably, a diagnosis of personality disorder not otherwise specified was associated with reduced odds of suicide attempts (OR = 0.42), possibly reflecting comparatively milder pathology and better psychosocial functioning. However, many mental disorders – particularly in subthreshold forms – remain undiagnosed and untreated. When combined with chronic stress or adverse life events, these conditions often lead to self-medication through alcohol or substance use, thereby increasing suicide risk (Balázs et al., 2000; Szeifert, Olah, et al., 2025). Psychiatric care frequently begins only after a first attempt, underscoring the need for proactive screening and early intervention. Individuals with trauma histories and severe psychiatric comorbidities are especially vulnerable to chronic suicidality, characterized by persistent suicidal ideation, recurrent attempts, and resistance to remission due to the combined effects of enduring trauma and treatment-resistant illness.

Our findings confirm the high co-occurrence of childhood adversities, consistent with research showing that maltreatment rarely occurs in isolation (Angelakis et al., 2019; Felitti et al., 1998). Emotional neglect was the most prevalent trauma subtype (93%), followed by emotional abuse (83.3%), physical neglect (74.3%), and physical abuse (54.7%), while sexual abuse was the least common (27%). Strong associations between emotional neglect, emotional abuse, and physical neglect underscore the interconnected nature of caregiving failures and suggest that neglect often coexists with other forms of maltreatment. These patterns align with growing evidence that neglect, although less visible than overt abuse, has profound and enduring effects on emotional regulation, interpersonal functioning, and skill development (Herpay et al., 2024; Horváth et al., 2024). Notably, sexual abuse did not significantly differentiate the no-attempt and multiple-attempt groups. Given that the no-attempt group also consisted of psychiatric inpatients, sexual abuse may represent a shared risk factor for psychopathology across clinical populations rather than a factor specifically associated with repeated suicidal behaviour. In contrast, chronic forms of interpersonal trauma, such as emotional abuse and neglect, may be more closely linked to the emotional dysregulation and interpersonal difficulties that contribute to recurrent suicide attempts.

Physical neglect emerged as the most consistent trauma-related factor, distinguishing individuals with any suicide attempt from those without (post-hoc Kruskal–Wallis pairwise comparison: z = − 3.04, p = .002) and predicting repeated attempts compared to a single attempt (OR = 1.14, p = .012). Unlike overt abuse, physical neglect – defined as persistent failure to meet basic needs for food, shelter, hygiene, and medical care – often goes unnoticed, yet its impact on psychological development is profound. It undermines the sense of safety, self-worth, and trust in caregivers, leading to chronic impairments in attachment and stress regulation (Angelakis et al., 2019; Felitti et al., 1998). These deficits may foster hopelessness and maladaptive coping strategies, thereby increasing vulnerability to depression, feelings of worthlessness, and suicidal behaviour. The frequent co-occurrence of neglect with other maltreatment types suggests a cumulative effect, consistent with ACE research demonstrating a graded relationship between adversity and suicide risk (Angelakis et al., 2019; Felitti et al., 1998).

Patients with a family history of suicide reported significantly higher levels of physical neglect, emotional neglect, and overall trauma exposure. This pattern may reflect the combined effects of genetic vulnerability, shared environmental stressors, and intergenerational transmission of impaired caregiving. Parental suicide or untreated psychiatric illness can diminish emotional availability and caregiving capacity, contributing to neglect and household dysfunction. Early parental loss, especially when combined with other trauma types, has been shown to significantly increase suicide risk later in life (Jeon et al., 2014). Such circumstances can destabilize family systems, compromise emotional and practical support, and reinforce maladaptive coping mechanisms, amplifying long-term vulnerability. The observed change in the significance of CTQ-EA between models, depending on the inclusion of family history of suicide, probably reflects shared variance between these variables rather than problematic multicollinearity. Although no evidence of multicollinearity was detected, the attenuation of the CTQ-EA effect in the full model suggests that part of its association with suicide attempts may overlap with familial suicide history. This may indicate that these variables capture partially related underlying risk environments, such as intergenerational transmission of vulnerability and adverse family contexts, rather than independent effects. However, given the cross-sectional and retrospective nature of the data, causal interpretations or mediation effects cannot be inferred.

Nicotine dependence (OR = 1.90, p = .031) and prescription drug misuse (OR = 2.74, p = .036) were significantly associated with suicide attempts, indicating that substance-related factors act as additional risk markers. Nicotine use has been linked to increased impulsivity, dysregulated neurotransmitter systems, and higher psychiatric comorbidity, all of which may heighten suicide risk (Bohnert et al., 2017; Döme et al., 2011; Rihmer et al., 2007). These behaviours may also reflect maladaptive coping in trauma-exposed individuals, reinforcing emotional dysregulation. Importantly, both nicotine and prescription drug misuse are modifiable risk factors, highlighting their relevance for prevention and intervention.

From a developmental perspective, childhood maltreatment predisposes individuals to cyclothymic or irritable temperament and impulsive–aggressive traits, which are recognized psychological correlates of suicidality (Mann et al., 2005). At the clinical level, major depressive episodes remain the most important proximal mediator of suicidal acts. This trajectory often begins with NSSI, progressing to non-violent attempts and, in some individuals, escalating to more lethal methods (Walsh et al., 2017). Early identification and intervention during the NSSI phase are crucial for preventing this escalation.

Finally, our findings raise the possibility that the profile of childhood adversity may be evolving over time. While overt forms of abuse, such as corporal punishment and sexual abuse, may have declined with increasing social awareness and public advocacy, more subtle, relational, and chronic adversities – particularly emotional neglect – may remain prevalent and underrecognized. Although we did not assess societal or family-level determinants of neglect in the present study, future research could examine whether sociodemographic changes, such as parental work-related stress, economic migration, family instability, or reduced caregiver availability, contribute to emotional neglect in clinical populations. Such hypotheses could be tested by incorporating detailed assessments of family functioning, caregiving quality, parental stress, and attachment-related experiences alongside traditional measures of childhood trauma.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The use of a validated and widely employed instrument (the CTQ) enhances measurement reliability, and adjustment for key sociodemographic variables strengthens internal validity by reducing potential confounding. The relatively large clinical sample and the differentiation between single and multiple suicide attempters provide nuanced insights into trauma-related risk profiles. In addition, the inclusion of multiple psychiatric diagnoses and substance use factors offers a more comprehensive understanding of the complex interplay of risk factors for suicidal behaviour in psychiatric populations.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, and reliance on retrospective self-report for sensitive topics such as childhood trauma may introduce recall bias or underreporting. The study did not include a healthy control group, limiting comparisons with the general population, and did not assess trauma-related syndromes such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) or complex PTSD, which could mediate or moderate observed associations. The lack of representativeness restricts generalizability beyond similar inpatient settings. Gender differences were not examined owing to unequal distribution, and the correlation between childhood maltreatment and psychiatric diagnoses was not analysed, as this was addressed in a previous study. The timeframe between trauma exposure and the first suicide attempt was not recorded, which limits conclusions about temporal sequencing.

Conclusion

Maltreatment in childhood induces chronic stress that can disrupt early brain development, impairing both nervous system and immune system functioning. Such neurobiological alterations, compounded by adverse psychosocial environments, substantially increase the likelihood of diverse negative outcomes in adulthood, including victimization or perpetration of violence, depression, nicotine dependence, obesity, high-risk sexual behaviours, unintended pregnancy, and alcohol or drug misuse. Importantly, suicide risk factors do not operate in isolation; rather, they interact in a synergistic and cumulative manner, whereby one vulnerability often precipitates or exacerbates another. For example, family dysfunction may contribute to childhood trauma, which fosters chronic stress, aggressive–impulsive personality traits, social dysfunction, and increased susceptibility to both psychiatric and somatic disorders. These, in turn, heighten the likelihood of maladaptive coping behaviours, such as nicotine and other substance misuse, ultimately amplifying suicide risk.

Overall, these findings highlight the urgent need for comprehensive, trauma-informed suicide prevention strategies that: (1) recognize neglect as a critical and often overlooked risk factor; (2) address the escalation from self-harm to increasingly lethal attempts; (3) integrate early screening for subthreshold psychiatric disorders; and (4) target modifiable behaviours such as nicotine dependence and prescription drug misuse. A proactive, multifaceted approach could substantially improve both the detection of at-risk individuals and the prevention of initial and repeated suicidal behaviours in psychiatric populations.

Our research also supports the view that suicide is a highly complex, multifactorial phenomenon. Accordingly, suicide prevention must operate on multiple levels, ranging from universal public health interventions and social policies aimed at reducing child maltreatment to targeted clinical strategies for high-risk groups and timely, personalized interventions following early warning signs such as NSSI or a first suicide attempt. Recognizing and addressing this layered, interacting complexity is essential for advancing truly preventive, lifespan-oriented mental healthcare.

Finally, it would be valuable for future research to examine whether the profile of childhood trauma is changing over time. Although the present study was not designed to assess temporal trends in maltreatment, heightened public awareness and social movements such as #MeToo may have contributed to greater recognition and prevention of some overt forms of abuse, including physical and sexual abuse. At the same time, emotional abuse and neglect may remain highly prevalent, or may simply have been historically underrecognized despite their substantial psychological impact. Future longitudinal and population-based studies are needed to determine whether the relative prevalence and developmental consequences of different trauma types are shifting across generations. These ‘invisible’ forms of trauma are often difficult to detect and may remain unnoticed for years. Physical neglect outside the home may sometimes be identified by general practitioners, health visitors, or teachers, whereas emotional abuse and neglect are more likely to be inferred from behavioural disturbances, learning difficulties, or social withdrawal. This underscores the importance of proactive, multi-agency collaboration and vigilant observation by professionals in education, healthcare, and child protection to ensure that even subtle forms of maltreatment are recognized early and addressed effectively.
