Establishment and application of a new one-step multiplex PCR for the accurate detection and differentiation of Salmonella Gallinarum biovars Pullorum and Gallinarum
Authors: Dan Xiong, Li Yuan, Xiao Fei, John Elmerdahl Olsen, Zhiming Pan, Xinan Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSalmonella enterica serovar Gallinarum biovars Pullorum and Gallinarum lead to pullorum disease and fowl typhoid in avian species and have been of substantial economic significance to the poultry industry. Serotyping and biochemical assays are complicated for use in the differentiation of these two biovars owing to their antigenic similarity. The aims of this study were to establish a new multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) method to detect and differentiate the two Salmonella biovars, Gallinarum and Pullorum.ResultsThe novel multiplex PCR was established on the basis of three genes, SG1445, I137_14465, and stn. In silico analysis indicated the SG1445 gene exists in all Salmonella serovar, apart from S. Pullorum, which allowed the use of this gene for S. Pullorum identification. The I137_14465 gene is absent in S. Gallinarum and present in all other Salmonella serovars, allowing this gene to be utilized for S. Gallinarum detection. stn was employed as a control gene for the Salmonella genus. The multiplex PCR could identify and distinguish the two pathogens accurately from 74 strains of Salmonella, including 29 different serovars and without false positive reactions from 44 non-Salmonella bacteria. The specificity was verified with a broad collection of Salmonella isolates. The detection limit of the multiplex PCR was as low as 22.5 pg/μL genomic DNA of the two pathogens and 100 CFU of bacterial cells. Detection of clinical isolates from a chicken farm was carried out using this method with good agreement with detection based on traditional Salmonella serotyping and biochemical identification.ConclusionsThe findings of this study showed that the developed multiplex PCR technique could sensitively detect and specifically differentiate the two biotypes of S. Gallinarum in laboratory and clinical samples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05408-9.

Background

Salmonella enterica is responsible for salmonellosis in both human and animal populations globally [1]. There are currently 2650 recognized Salmonella serovars based on 114 flagellar (H) and 46 lipopolysaccharide antigens (O) [2]. Sick birds are still considered a significant source of infection in poultry, and likewise foodborne salmonellosis arising from contaminated chicken and poultry goods remains a significant concern for human health [3–5].

Some Salmonella serotypes are important bacterial pathogens in both animal and human populations, while others show host specificity [6]. Among the latter group, S. enterica serovar Gallinarum biovars Pullorum and Gallinarum (from hereon denoted S. Gallinarum and S. Pullorum) are the causative agents of Pullorum disease (PD) and Fowl typhoid (FT), respectively, affecting only chickens and of great economic concern for the poultry sector globally [7]. S. Pullorum infection causes a significant incidence and fatality rate in young chickens [8]. This Salmonella type can colonize the reproductive system, resulting in vertical transfer and egg-associated salmonellosis and longtime persistence in the infected bird [9]. S. Gallinarum, on the other hand, causes severe, systemic infection in birds all ages. The disease, Fowl typhoid, may cause death with flock mortalities ranging from 40 to 80% [10, 11].

To prevent spread of these host-specific Salmonella types in the poultry production system, a rapid characterization of Salmonella isolates to serovars and biovar levels is important. Traditionally, serotyping is done using the White-Kauffmann-Le Minor approach to identify the somatic (O) and flagellar (H) antigens [12], however, this has several limitations. It requires several hundred high-quality typing sera and may take 3 days for completion [13], which limits its application efficiency as a tool for large-scale screening [14, 15]. Furthermore, in the specific case of Pullorum and Gallinarum disease, it cannot distinguish between the two biotypes, since they have the same O antigens 1, 9, and 12 and both lack flagella antigens [16]. Biochemical properties, including ornithine decarboxylation and dulcitol fermentation, have been utilized to distinguish among them [17, 18]. However, biochemical identification is also labor-intensive and requires 2 to 3 days for a definitive answer [19]. In consequence of this, DNA analysis techniques have been developed and are being employed extensively.

The ability to determine the entire bacterial genome of every isolate and compare the results with gene databases has recently made Salmonella sequence analysis highly effective for strain typing [20]. Unfortunately, whole genome sequencing techniques are still costly, and the results need to be thoroughly and intricately analyzed [21]. Consequently, rapid detection methods based on polymerase chain reaction (PCR) have been commonly applied [22, 23], due to the high sensitivity and specificity of this method [24–26].

Several PCR/qPCR-based assays have already been published for differentiating S. Gallinarum biovars Pullorum and Gallinarum. Representative approaches include SNP- or RoD-based conventional PCR followed by post-PCR processing, such as fimH PCR–RFLP [27], as well as duplex PCR assays targeting polymorphic regions of glgC/speC [28] or ratA-associated genomic differences [10, 29]. Real-time methods have also been developed, including SYBR Green-based multiplex qPCR for differential diagnosis and quantification [11], and HRM assays that discriminate the two biovars by melting profiles [30]. In addition, single-gene PCR assays (e.g., ipaJ) can identify only one biovar S. Pullorum [13]. While these assays have advanced rapid diagnosis, some require additional steps (restriction digestion, sequencing/HRM analysis), specialized real-time PCR instrumentation, or have been validated with narrower panels of serovars and non-Salmonella organisms. In routine veterinary diagnostic laboratories and in resource-limited settings, there remains a need for a simple, one-tube, gel-based assay that provides an unambiguous banding pattern and enables reliable identification across a broad diversity of Salmonella serovars and non-Salmonella bacteria.

In relation to problems in poultry industry caused by S. Gallinarum and S. Pullorum, there is an utmost need for a PCR-based method for fast, accurate, and sensitive identification of the strain of the two biovars. In the present study, a reliable multiplex PCR assay was developed to identify and differentiate S. Pullorum and S. Gallinarum. The new multiplex PCR was based on primers that specifically amplify unique targets in a one-step PCR differentiation of the two biovars. The specificity and sensitivity of the multiplex PCR assay were evaluated on broad strain collections as well as on field samples of poultry egg samples.

Methods

Bacterial strains and traditional strain characterization

Eighty nine Salmonella strains comprising strains of 29 different serovars and 44 non-Salmonella pathogens were employed (Supplementary Table S1 and S2). Strains originated from the strain collections of Jiangsu Key Laboratory of Zoonosis, Yangzhou University, Yangzhou, China and the Department of Veterinary and Animal Sciences, University of Copenhagen, Denmark. Strains were originally collected from routine veterinary laboratories or commercially available. Biochemical typing employing API-20E (bioMerieux, France) was utilized to confirm the identity of all Salmonella strains, followed by confirmation of the strains via serotyping with diagnostic antisera (Tianrun Bio-Pharmaceutical, Ningbo, China) based on rapid agglutination according to the White–Kauffmann–Le Minor scheme. Ornithine decarboxylation and dulcitol fermentation were employed to confirm whether strains belonged to S. Pullorum or S. Gallinarum [17, 18].

Bacterial growth and genomic DNA isolation

Recovery of all strains was conducted overnight at 37 °C using either Luria–Bertani (LB) agar (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) or brain heart infusion (BHI) agar (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Sparks, MD, United States) followed by culture of one colony into BHI or LB liquid broth at 37 °C for 14 h with an agitation speed of 180 rpm. A DNA extraction kit (Tiangen, Beijing) was employed for the isolation of the genomic DNA following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. Concentration of DNA and purity control was determined using NanoDrop 1000 (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE). The DNA suspended in sterile water was then stored at −20 °C until the PCR amplification was conducted.

Bioinformatics analysis and specific primer design

Target selection was guided by comparative genomics and regions-of-difference (RoD) between S. Pullorum and S. Gallinarum. Briefly, we prioritized RoD genes showing a stable presence/absence pattern between the two biovars (i.e., present in one biovar but absent in the other), because such markers are less sensitive to single-point mutations and are well suited for a gel-based multiplex PCR. Accordingly, SG1445 (GenBank acc. no. AM933173.1, region 1,515,365–1,516,222) and I137_14465 (GenBank acc. no. CP006575.1, region 3,092,853–3093389) were selected as candidate biovar-discriminatory targets, and stn was included as a genus-level internal control.

To ensure that the selected targets and primer binding sites were specific and broadly applicable, an extensive sequence identity assessment was performed. SG1445 and I137_14465 were queried using the web BLASTn against the NCBI nucleotide (nr/nt) database, and up to 5,000 aligned sequences per target (the maximum display limit) were inspected. Sequence comparisons were used to verify that (i) SG1445 is absent from SP but present in SG and other Salmonella serovars, and (ii) I137_14465 is absent from SG but present in SP and other Salmonella serovars (Supplementary Table S3).

Three sets of primers were designed for SG1445, I137_14465, and stn. The first primer set, SG1445-F/R, was designed for the purpose of amplifying an 835 bp fragment for specific identification of S. Pullorum. The second primer pair, I137_14465-F/R, was designed to amplify a 167 bp fragment to identify S. Gallinarum from other serovars. stn-F/R was used to generate a 357 bp product to detect all strains of the Salmonella genus as the reference gene (Table 1). The positions of the designed primers were based on the primer specificity and the length of the three products to clearly distinguish the three bands. Primer specificity was first evaluated using NCBI BLAST/Primer-BLAST to exclude off-target homologies. During multiplex assay optimization, primer-primer interactions were assessed by iterative adjustment of reaction conditions. Optimizations included primer concentrations and evaluation of annealing temperature gradients to obtain robust amplification of each target. Multiplex PCR process

The established multiplex PCR was conducted in a 25 μL reaction volume. This mixture contained 12.5 μL of 2 × Taq Master mix (Vazyme, China), 60 nM of SG1445 primers, 60 nM of I137_14465 primers, 40 nM of stn primers, bacterial genomic DNA (100 ng), and sterilized water for making up the final mixture to 25 μL. Cycling was done under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles at 94 °C for 45 s, 56 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 60 s, with a final step of 72 °C for 10 min. Electrophoresis with a 1% agarose gel was employed to analyze the products of PCR. The GelDoc XR Gel Documentation System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) was employed for the visualization of the amplified products of PCR.

Specificity and sensitivity of the multiplex PCR method

To test the specificity and sensitivity of the established multiplex PCR method, 89 Salmonella strains of 29 serovars and 44 different strains of non-Salmonella pathogens were subjected to detection by the multiplex method, carried out on pure cultures of bacteria as described above.

Detection level of the developed PCR method

The detection level of the multiplex PCR method was using S. Pullorum strain S06004 and S. Gallinarum strain SG9 overnight culture in the LB medium. DNA extraction was conducted as described. The genomic DNA was consecutively diluted from 225 ng/μL to 2.25 pg/μL in sterile water and served as the templates of the multiplex PCR. In parallel, a tenfold serial dilution of the overnight culture of the two strains was carried out in sterile water (ranging from 2 × 108 CFU to 2 × 102 CFU/mL). The culture was boiled for 10 min in a water bath to release the bacterial genome DNA. The multiplex PCR was performed using 5 μL of genomic DNA from each concentration that had been centrifuged.

Application of the multiplex PCR system on naturally contaminated samples

Isolates of Salmonella from eggs of a chicken farm were used to evaluate the established multiplex PCR technique under field condition. Salmonella isolates from samples of chicken eggs were isolated and characterized according to published methods [31, 32]. Briefly, samples were pre-enriched in buffered peptone water (50 mL) (Difco, BD, Sparks, MD, United States) for 24 h at 37 °C. Xylose lysine tergitol 4 agar (Difco, BD) was employed to streak the bacterial culture, followed by incubation for 16 h at 37 °C. Colony morphology on selective media and biochemical characteristics were used to identify Salmonella. Traditional serovar identification was performed via slide agglutination test with specified O and H antisera per the latest version of the White-Kaufmann-Le Minor method. Meanwhile, the DNA was extracted from the presumptive Salmonella colonies and analyzed by the developed multiplex PCR method.

Specificity and sensitivity of the multiplex PCR assay

To test the specificity of the primers for the two biovars, the three primers pairs of SG1445, I137_14465, and stn were used on the genomic DNA from 89 Salmonella strains of 29 serovars, and 44 strains of different non-Salmonella pathogens. With the three pairs of primers, all (n = 12) S. Pullorum strains produced two bands of 357-bp stn and 167-bp I137_14465, while all (n = 4) S. Gallinarum strains generated two bands of 357-bp stn and 835-bp SG1445. At the same time, other serovars of 58 Salmonella strains produced all three fragments. Bacterial genomic DNA from 44 non-Salmonella pathogens showed no amplification products with the three specific pairs of primers. Besides, across all strains tested in this study, no non-specific or unexpected bands were observed when the three primer pairs were combined in a single multiplex reaction (Fig. 2). These results showed that the multiplex PCR had no cross-reactions to other Salmonella serovars and to non-Salmonella pathogens (100% specificity), in accordance with the indications based on BLAST search. In addition to the strains of S. Gallinarum and S. Pullorum tested above, a larger collection of strains with broad geographic origin was tested. Discussion

Chickens, turkeys, and certain other avian species can contract fowl typhoid and Pullorum illness from S. Gallinarum biovars Gallinarum and Pullorum, respectively [33]. Although these two pathogens cause distinct diseases, S. Pullorum and S. Gallinarum belong to group D (both possess O antigens 1, 9, and 12) and depict antigenic and biochemical similarities [34]. Hence, standard serotyping cannot distinguish between the two biovars while isolating serovar Gallinarum [35].

It is well known that molecular assays may distinguish between strongly linked bacterial strains and the agents that cause infectious illnesses in a very sensitive and particular manner [36]. Utilizing comparative genomics to benefit from novel serovar-specific gene sequences is becoming a more crucial strategy as the BLAST program continues to improve. For example, by employing this technique, the ssaN gene was used to efficiently detect S. Enteritidis [37]. Here, three genes of SG1445, I137_14465, and stn were evaluated for use in the identification and separation of S. Pullorum and S. Gallinarum. The gene stn has been used widely as the reference control of the Salmonella genus [38, 39].

Accurate Salmonella identification and serotyping are necessary to offer more details for determining the origins of infection at the time of epidemic investigations and to identify strains [40]. In the present study, we established a one-step multiplex PCR using two previously unexploited genomic regions of difference, SG1445 (absent in S. Pullorum) and I137_14465 (absent in S. Gallinarum), together with the Salmonella genus control gene stn. This design enables simultaneous genus confirmation and biovar differentiation in a single reaction using standard thermocyclers and agarose gel electrophoresis, without restriction digestion, probes, or HRM analysis. The assay was validated using a broad strain collection (29 serovars) and non-Salmonella bacteria, with a detection limit of 22.5 pg/μL genomic DNA and 100 CFU cells. This is the first report of the use of genes SG1445 and I137_14465 as specific diagnostic markers for the biovars Pullorum and Gallinarum. Based on reactions with the primer SG1445-F/R and I137_14465-F/R, the two new genes were capable of being utilized independently to differentiate the two biovars.

The established multiplex PCR had a minimum limit of DNA templates of 22.5 pg/μL, making it more sensitive than the HRM-PCR melting assay, which has previously been suggested for discrimination between the two serovars and which has a detection level of 126.2 pg/μL [30]. The limit of bacterial cells in the developed PCR technique was comparable to previously published multiplex PCR method for separation of animal important group-D Salmonella based on the genes of flhB, lygD and tcpS (100 CFU) [41] and the ipaJ-based PCR designed to detect both S. Gallinarum and S. Pullorum (100 CFU) [13]. The findings indicated a high detection limit with the PCR method that it allowed the detection of the two biovar cells in even low concentrations.

Animals and poultry are known to be substantial reservoirs of Salmonella [14, 42]. Salmonellosis outbreaks may be quickly identified, and their sources can be tracked owing to Salmonella serotyping, which is still a crucial component of Salmonella surveillance efforts [43]. As these two Salmonella biovars Gallinarum and Pullorum are the leading pathogenic causes of fowl diseases, the primary objective of this research was developing a quick and accurate method capable of distinguishing the two biovars. Effective control of PD and FT in chickens requires timely detection of infected animals and removal of infected animals [11]. A variety of techniques based on nucleic acids and antigens, including PCR, ELISA, microarray, and next-generation sequencing (NGS) have been used [44, 45], and among these, PCR methods are attractive, due to easiness of procedures, high specificity and speed of analysis, which guided our choice of method in the current study. No strains were shown to belong to SG in the field-testing, and further studies should focus on demonstrating that more field isolates will react as expected in the multiplex PCR method.

Traditional detection methods involve time- and labor-intensive biochemical evaluation, non-selective and selective enrichment processes, and serological detection [46]. This newly designed PCR technique may detect the two Salmonella biovars within 2 h in the enriched samples, considerably reducing the time needed to identify serotypes and biovars. The results described in this study provide a proof of concept and demonstrate the feasibility of using this PCR method to screen S. Pullorum and S. Gallinarum. Future studies will investigate different approaches to isolate DNA directly from infected animals and determine if it can be applied in the field.

Although the multiplex PCR showed clear and consistent discrimination, several limitations should be noted. First, the workflow relies on enrichment and culture steps prior to DNA extraction, which supports sensitivity and specificity but does not enable rapid on-site detection without laboratory processing. Nevertheless, the biovar-discriminatory targets and primer sequences established here provide a practical foundation for future rapid point-of-care platforms (e.g., isothermal amplification or CRISPR-based assays) by supplying experimentally validated loci for amplicon and guide design. Second, the assay uses presence/absence of SG1445 and I137_14465 as discriminatory markers, and rare genomic variation (e.g., deletions, horizontal gene transfer, or future evolution) could potentially affect marker distribution. Thus, continued surveillance and periodic in silico re-evaluation against newly deposited genomes are therefore warranted. Third, field validation for S. Gallinarum is inherently constrained because SG is infrequently detected in many clinical samples and its prevalence varies by region. Thus, availability of SG-positive clinical specimens was limited. Finally, while our validation panel included laboratory isolates, clinical specimens, and isolates from different geographic settings (including independent testing in China and Denmark), the overall number of strains remains limited and may not fully capture global diversity. Future studies with larger and internationally representative collections will further strengthen generalizability.

Conclusions

In summary, a highly accurate biovar-specific multiplex PCR was developed to rapidly detect and differentiate the two Salmonella biovars associated with PD and FT. The multiplex PCR was established based on previously untested targets namely SG1445 and I137_14465. The technique was capable of correctly identifying laboratory strains of SP and SG and clinical isolates of SP with 100% specificity and sensitivity. This highly accurate multiplex PCR assay will be helpful for quickly differentiating between the two biovars. The established multiplex PCR method may be applied in epidemiological research as well as in eradication programs for PD and FT.
