Substrate heterogeneity outweighs colour in shaping thermal environment and intertidal barnacle recruitment on artificial surfaces
Authors: Nelson A Lagos, Marco A Lardies, Claudio García-Herrera, Nicolás M Leppes, Felipe Moscoso, Diego Herrera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding how physical attributes of artificial substrates influence ecological processes is essential for the design of habitat-enhancing structures in coastal ecosystems. Here, we examine how structural heterogeneity (flat vs. heterogeneous tiles) and colour (black vs. white) of 3D-printed substrates are associated with the thermal environment and recruitment of two intertidal barnacle species, Notochthamalus scabrosus and Jehlius cirratus. Surface temperature during low tide was quantified using infrared thermography. Black tiles heated significantly more than white tiles, with temperature increases of ∼6–12 °C and 1–4 °C, respectively. However, surface heterogeneity produced measurable thermal buffering, with crevices on black tiles remaining 3–6 °C cooler than adjacent ridges, generating fine-scale thermal refugia whose magnitude varied with substrate colour. Barnacle recruitment was primarily associated with habitat structure, with high recruitment on heterogeneous tiles, especially within crevices where recruitment was two orders of magnitude greater than ridges for both species. Species-specific responses to colour were limited: J. cirratus responded only to microhabitat identity, whereas N. scabrosus exhibited additional sensitivity to substrate colour. These results suggest that structural heterogeneity can mitigate thermal stress by providing stable microhabitats that may enhance post settlement survival and recruitment, even on thermally extreme artificial substrates. Introduction

The intertidal zone is a thermally dynamic environment where small-scale substrate properties such as colour and material composition strongly influence species distribution, physiological performance, and community assembly1–5. These variations are also driven by the structural complexity of habitats at several spatial scales6–9, and can mediate colonization success and survival, especially for sessile invertebrates and algae that are vulnerable to desiccation and thermal stress3,10–13. Recent experimental warming studies show that even subtle, substrate-driven temperature differences can mediate ecological processes and reorganize intertidal communities by reducing thermally sensitive taxa and limiting the replacement by warm-adapted species14.

Ecological studies have emphasized the benefits of introducing microhabitat heterogeneity (e.g., crevices, pits) into artificial substrates to enhance biodiversity15–19. However, the thermal dimension of habitat heterogeneity has often been overlooked, even though structurally complex substrates generate strong temperature contrast at small scales. Studies have shown significant differences in the thermal environment provided by artificial coastal infrastructure compared to natural rocky platforms, which led to a lower presence of herbivores and a decrease in species richness12. These contrasts underscore the need to disentangle the physical mechanisms operating at small spatial scales on artificial substrates. Thermal variations at the patch scale (centimetres) in rocky shore habitats are governed by a heat transfer process that depends on the thermophysical properties, colour, and texture of the material. This micro-scale thermal process influences larval settlement and survival in species with narrow thermal limits12,20,21. Some studies have demonstrated that barnacle settlement is influenced by the thermal properties of substrate materials22, and that barnacle recruitment survival improves in cooler, thermally buffered microhabitats23. In this study, we focus on recruitment, defined as the net outcome of larval settlement and post-settlement survival, evaluating how substrate colour and structural heterogeneity shape recruitment outcomes.

In addition, it is well established that macroalgae reduce thermal stress for intertidal understory invertebrates by ameliorating temperatures and dessication24, thereby enhancing thermal resilience and facilitating community recovery following heatwaves25. Likewise, intertidal areas with low barnacle densities may be more thermally stressful than areas with high densities, with implications for the survival of barnacle recruits23,26. Despite this evidence, thermal heterogeneity remains less integrated into eco-engineered design than structural complexity, highlighting the need to understand how material and microtopographic features interact to create structurally functional microhabitats and to establish mechanistic baselines that can inform, but not directly replicate, applied contexts.

Beyond structural heterogeneity, substrate colour also modulates surface temperature and larval behaviour at settlement. For instance, photonegative invertebrates such as barnacles tend to avoid highly reflective or illuminated surfaces, preferring darker substrates27,28. The physical property most directly influencing surface heating is spectral reflectance, which determines the proportion of incident solar radiation reflected or absorbed, while other elements, such as surface texture, biofilms, and chemical cues also play a role in microhabitat selection during settlement7,28. Dark-coloured substrates exhibit low reflectance and high absorptance, resulting in elevated surface temperatures compared to lighter surfaces7,28. Thus, substrate colour acts both as a behavioural cue for larval settlement and as a physical determinant of thermal environment, depending on its interaction with material composition and surface heterogeneity. Disentangling these optical and thermal effects under controlled conditions is a necessary mechanistic step before extrapolation across materials, scales, or applied contexts29,30.

The extensive Chilean coastline, together with rapidly expanding coastal infrastructure, offer a relevant setting to examine these dynamics under intensified radiation and warming conditions. In northern Chilean cities, such as Antofagasta (approximately 23 °S), artificial substrates often lack the thermal buffering and microhabitat diversity of natural rocky shores, potentially impairing key ecological processes such as barnacle recruitment12. Despite their ecological relevance, the combined effects of substrate structural heterogeneity and colour-driven thermal variations on surface temperature and invertebrate recruitment remain understudied. Here, artificial substrates are used as a controlled model to isolate colour and structural heterogeneity on thermal microhabitats and recruitment. Specifically, we investigate how substrate structure (flat vs. structurally heterogeneous) and colour (white vs. black, differing in reflectance and thermal conductivity) influence surface temperature and recruitment (integrating settlement and post-settlement survival) of two ecologically important barnacle species, Jehlius cirratus and Notochthamalus scabrosus (Darwin, 1854). Both species are abundant foundation invertebrates that dominate space and shape succession in mid- to high-intertidal rocky shores of northern Chile31,32, and are strongly influenced by the thermal properties of their habitats33. For instance, J. cirratus maintains a relatively constant upper thermal limit (CTmax) along its latitudinal range in Chile, while the lower thermal limit (CTmin) decreased towards higher latitudes21, indicating asymmetric vulnerability to warming, which may be mitigated by thermally buffered microhabitats such as dark, shaded crevices provided by heterogeneous natural substrates.

Using two species as model systems, together with 3D-printed artificial substrates and infrared thermography (IRT), we test the hypothesis that structural heterogeneity mitigates the thermal stress associated with low-reflectance, dark surfaces, thereby promoting higher recruitment rates in thermally buffered microhabitats. The 3D-printed substrates were manufactured from polylactic acid (PLA), differing only in black and white pigments, thereby maintaining constant material composition while isolating the effects of colour and structural heterogeneity on thermal environment and recruitment. Combining laboratory measurements with field experimentation, we quantified the thermal and chemical properties of the 3D-printed substrates, evaluated their thermal performance in the field using IRT, and assessed barnacle recruitment across substrate types and microhabitats. Our findings provide mechanistic insight into how microhabitat-scale structural heterogeneity shapes the coupling between abiotic conditions and ecological processes, contributing to emergent patterns of thermal functional heterogeneity in intertidal ecosystems.

Results

Material characterization of 3D-printed artificial substrates

Under controlled laboratory conditions, the thermal properties of the 3D-printed material varied with both internal infill and colour. Test probes with different infill densities (solid vs. 25% infill, hereafter referred to as porous) showed subtle but detectable variations in thermal conductivity (Fig. 1A). Substrate colour was the primary driver of thermal conductivity variations, with black, solid 3D-printed probes reaching significantly higher temperatures than white, porous ones (Two-way ANOVA: colour × density interaction, F(1, 16) = 13.13, p = 0.002; Fig. 1A, see Table S1). Reflectance profiles also differed between colours, with white surfaces reflecting ∼70% of incident light, and black surfaces reflecting ∼10%, and closely matching the reflectance of the natural rocky substrate (Fig. 1B). Spectral analyses (ATR-FTIR) revealed pigment-associated characteristic features in both PLA filaments (Fig. 1C). Black PLA displayed slightly higher absorbance in the 1000–1600 cm⁻¹ region, whereas white PLA showed a secondary absorption feature around 690–710 cm⁻1. However, both filaments exhibited highly correlated spectra (r = 0.998, p < 0.001; inset Fig. 1C). The FTIR spectra of natural rocky substrate showed a broad band between 1420 and 870 cm⁻¹. Based on these laboratory results, 3D tiles printed with the porous infill (Fig. 2A) were subsequently deployed in the intertidal.

Thermal performance of 3D-printed artificial substrates

Under field and low tide conditions, surface reflectance revealed the expected pattern: white tiles consistently showed higher surface reflectance than both black tiles and the surrounding natural rocky substrate (Fig. 2D). Infrared thermography confirmed these optical differences, with black tiles consistently heated more than white tiles, independent of surface structure (Table 1; Fig. 3A-C). At the beginning of the experiment (March), temperature variations between 2 h prior to MLLW and MLLW itself, increased by 6–10 °C on black tiles compared with 3–4 °C on white tiles over the course of two hours. By the end of the 29 days of exposure period, these ranges shifted to 3–12 °C and 1–3 °C, respectively. Notably, surface heterogeneity buffered the temperature increase on black tiles, reducing surface heating by approximately 3–6 °C (Fig. 3C), an effect that likely arises due to the increase in the area that transfers heat created by heterogeneous surfaces, producing a significant interaction between colour and surface structural heterogeneity (Table 1). Microhabitat identity also influenced temperature variations: crevices and ridges, within heterogeneous tiles, exhibited thermal differences that were modulated by colour, especially toward the end of the experimental period (Fig. 3D; Table 1). In heterogeneous black tiles, crevices remained ∼2.4 °C below adjacent ridges, a difference that is reduced within white tiles (Fig. 3D). Finally, significant differences among tiles within colours (F(10, 10) = 30.29, p < 0.001) indicate that fine-scale geometry further modulated heating, while the non-significant MHI × Tiles(C) term showed that crevice–ridge differences were consistent across tiles (Table 1).

Barnacle recruitment under variable habitat structures and surface colour

Recruitment rates of the studied barnacle species were primarily influenced by habitat structure, with heterogeneous tiles supporting higher recruitment than flat ones, while surface colour alone had no detectable effect, and the interaction between habitat structure and colour was non-significant (Table 2). Species-specific analyses revealed a similar pattern in the recruitment of Jehlius cirratus (Fig. 4A). In contrast, Notochthamalus scabrosus responded significantly to both habitat structure and surface colour (Table 2; Fig. 4B), exhibiting highest recruitment on heterogeneous black tiles and lowest recruitment on flat white tiles, with intermediate values in the remaining combinations (H/W and F/B, Fig. 4B). These differences illustrate a greater sensitivity of N. scabrosus to colour-associated thermal environment compared to J. cirratus. Despite this, interaction terms remained non-significant for both species (Table 2), suggesting that structural and colour effects on recruitment operated additively rather than synergistically.

Barnacle recruitment across microhabitat identity and surface colour

Variations in barnacle recruitment rates were driven by microhabitat identity, rather than by surface colour within heterogeneous tiles (Table 3). Recruitment of both species was significantly higher in crevices than ridges (Table 3; Fig. 5A-B). The magnitude of the microhabitat effect can be further appreciated by examining the back-transformed LSMeans values: independent of colour, crevices supported ~ 23× more recruits in J. cirratus, and ~ 21× more recruits in N. scabrosus (Fig. 5A-B). Together, these results confirm that fine-scale structural heterogeneity (within tiles), rather than surface colour, determines the likelihood of recruitment. Crevices provide thermally buffered microhabitats that may enhance early survival, explaining the strong and consistent contrast observed between ridges and crevices across treatments.

Discussion

Our findings illustrate that the physical properties of artificial substrates, particularly thermal conditions, which are primarily driven by surface colour at the tile level, but temperature increases are buffered by structural heterogeneity (crevices) at small-scale. Also, the recruitment rates of sessile barnacle species, Notochthamalus scabrosus and Jehlius cirratus, were primarily driven by structural heterogeneity with no influence of surface colour. These results support the notion that microclimatic conditions in the intertidal zone, shaped by structural heterogeneity and surface reflectance, play a key role in recruitment dynamics2,7,34. Because recruitment integrates larval settlement and post-settlement survival, the patterns reported here reflect recruitment outcomes rather than settlement alone.

Laboratory characterization of the 3D-printed materials provides mechanistic insight into why colour dominated the thermal behaviour of the experimental substrates. Although infill-density–related differences in thermal conductivity were detectable, their influence was negligible compared to the pronounced, colour-driven contrast in solar energy absorption. ATR-FTIR analyses showed strongly similar, pigment-related spectral features for carbon-black and TiO₂35, the pigments incorporated in black and white PLA filaments, respectively, confirming that the PLA tiles differ primarily in optical rather than chemical properties. In contrast, the natural rocky substrate displayed broad carbonate- and silicate-related spectral bands, typical of coastal lithologies36, highlighting its distinct composition. Together, these results identify substrate colour, rather than pigment chemistry or material density, as the dominant physical driver of surface temperature and, the resulting thermal microhabitats.

In field conditions, black tiles exhibit a ∼ 6–12 °C heat gain relatives to white ones. This result is consistent with previous studies showing that darker surfaces absorb more solar radiation37, intensifying thermal stress for intertidal organisms22. Some studies indicate thermally extreme black microhabitats may represent a selective filter38, limiting recruitment success on dark, hotter substrates, as shown in thermal manipulation experiments in Tesseropora rosea, where lower recruitment was recorded on darker, warmer surfaces7. However, in our study, despite the increased heat on black tiles, only the recruitment of N. scabrosus was partially affected by colour, indicating broad thermal tolerances of both studied species.

However, despite differences in surface temperatures of black and white tiles, similar recruitment rates of both species occurred independently of colour effect, and the increase occurs within crevices where temperature increases were buffered. This pattern may reflect the combined influence of physical and behavioural processes. For instance, physically, shaded microhabitats enhance heat dissipation and reduce desiccation creating thermal refugia that promote settlement38. Behaviourally, barnacle larvae exhibit negative phototaxis39, favouring darker, less reflective background39, as reported for Amphibalanus amphitrite27. In addition, heterogeneous tiles, especially in shaded microhabitats such as crevices, may facilitate the formation of biofilms40, which are known to enhance larval settlement. Through their influence on settlement, these mechanisms may contribute to recruitment patterns. Together, these processes may explain the elevated recruitment on heterogeneous substrates, while also emphasizing that, in some taxa (e.g., ascidians and bryozoans), brightness or reflectance, rather than colour alone, can influence larval settlement28. highlighting that marine invertebrates may respond to multiple optical cues.

A central outcome of our study is that microhabitat identity, within heterogeneous tiles, emerged as the dominant predictor of barnacle recruitment, surpassing the effects of substrate colour and the thermal regimes generated by it33,41,42. Across species and treatments, crevices consistently supported more recruits than adjacent ridges, demonstrating that fine-scale topographic complexity may exert a stronger influence on early colonization and survival of barnacles than broader-scale thermal or optical differences43,44. This pattern underscores how microscale refugia modulate ecological processes in physically stressful environments44, providing shelter from heat, desiccation, and hydrodynamic exposure45–47. Importantly, these effects were evident even on black tiles, which reached the highest surface temperatures, indicating that thermal buffering created by structural complexity can offset the disadvantages of warmer substrates.45.

At our study site, natural rocks are dark-coloured. Indeed, the rocky platforms in the area consist of reddish-brown sedimentary rocks, belonging to the Coloso formation (upper Jurassic – lower Cretaceous), with some granitic outcrops48. Spectral analyses of the natural substrate evidenced reflectance values comparable to those of the printed black tiles. This correspondence suggests that the high recruitment rate on dark backgrounds could also reflect a possible evolutionary adaptation of barnacle populations to local habitat conditions, reinforcing the idea that high recruitment on black plates may not only reflect phototactic preference but also long-term selection for optimal substrate recognition49. Supporting the role of light environment in settlement behaviour, Manríquez et al., 202150, working at the same location studied here, showed that artificial light at night (ALAN) negatively affects the settlement of J. cirratus and N. scabrosus barnacles, suggesting that the disruption of natural darkness interferes with larval behaviour at settlement. This sensitivity to light is consistent with greater settlement within crevices, which offers reduced light exposure and darker microhabitats, suggesting that shaded refugia may function as both thermal and optical shelters during settlement. However, even if light-mediated preferences may influence settlement and recruitment at broader scales, our results show that these optical effects remain secondary to the influence of microhabitat identity49.

Despite the elevated temperature on black tiles, the addition of surface heterogeneity, such as ridges and crevices, partially mitigated the thermal stress, reducing surface temperatures and increasing recruitment of benthic organisms30. These findings are consistent with recent studies emphasizing that the role of small-scale surface roughness can create thermal refuges and enhance colonization in intertidal organisms, including barnacles51,52. In particular, crevices created localized cool zones, buffering organisms from extreme thermal conditions and reducing post-settlement mortality. Such effects have been identified as a key mechanism for maintaining community persistence under climate change scenarios7,12,13,16. These buffering effects were substantially stronger than the influence of colour or absolute surface temperature variations on recruitment, further highlighting microhabitat identity as the dominant environmental determinant.

Although both barnacle species showed similar general responses to microhabitat identity (i.e., preferential recruitment in crevices regardless of colour), N. scabrosus exhibited a significant effect of substrate colour when comparing flat and heterogeneous tiles, thus recruiting more strongly on black surfaces than on white ones. This pattern suggests that colour-mediated cues may also influence recruitment at coarse spatial scales, possibly linked to optical contrast or thermal conditions experienced at the tile level. However, when recruitment was examined within heterogeneous tiles only, both species responded strongly and significantly to microhabitat identity, with crevices supporting 20–35 times more recruits than ridges. This preference for more protected microhabitats is likely due to reduced desiccation stress and lower exposure to extreme temperatures53, supporting the importance of structural and functional refuges in enhancing juvenile survival in exposed intertidal zones54. Similarly, Aguilera et al. (2014)12 reported higher barnacle cover on natural reefs with greater small-scale spatial heterogeneity compared to adjacent artificial breakwaters, highlighting the ecological relevance of fine-scale structural complexity for intertidal recruitment. These results indicate that although substrate colour can influence recruitment at broader structural scales (> tile-level), particularly for N. scabrosus, the dominant mechanism shaping barnacle recruitment is the presence of microhabitat refugia. Previous studies reporting the thermal sensitivity of N. scabrosus in shading experiments involving growth and reproduction33, suggest that this species may have a narrower thermal tolerance, which can increase reliance on colour-driven cues when selecting settlement substrates. In contrast, J. cirratus showed higher recruitment on heterogeneous black tiles, in crevices and even in ridges, suggesting a broader tolerance or different larval behavior under thermal stress. These contrasting responses at scales of structural heterogeneity and microhabitat identity highlight subtle interspecific variability in how environmental cues are integrated during post-settlement process until recruitment.

From an applied perspective, our findings underscore the need to incorporate both optical (e.g., reflectance, colour) and topographic (e.g., surface roughness and microstructure) features into the design of eco-engineered coastal infrastructure. Recent studies have emphasized that mimicking natural structural complexity and selecting materials with low thermal conductivity and high reflectance can be effective strategies for maximizing biodiversity and ecological resilience in urbanized coastal environments55,56. Although PLA differs from marine concrete in thermophysical properties, our findings reveal mechanistic links between colour-driven heat absorption and heterogeneity-mediated thermal buffering, and recruitment. Absolute temperatures of PLA surfaces differ on concrete due to material properties, yet the thermal processes underlying recruitment responses are general and relevant for artificial coastal structures design. Recent advances in shell-enhanced, bio-receptive cementitious materials show reduced thermal conductivity and increased thermal inertia while maintaining mechanical performance57, suggesting that surface tone and microhabitat configuration can be strategically incorporated into eco-engineered, artificial coastal structures to create thermal refugia and enhance recruitment under warming conditions. Our results indicate, however, that microhabitat-scale structural design is likely to exert a far greater ecological impact than substrate colour alone.

We acknowledge several limitations in our study. We did not directly assess the effects of other biotic factors such as competition or predation, nor did we account for the influence of biofilms, all of which may also affect larval settlement58–60. In addition, because this experiment was conducted at a single site, and with limited degrees of freedom, the results should be interpreted with caution, and further spatially replicated studies are needed to confirm the generality of these patterns. Future research should address these factors, exploring species-specific physiological responses to thermal gradients, and examining microclimatic variations at finer spatial and temporal scales, to disentangle whether the dominance of microhabitat identity over colour persists across broader environmental gradients and substrate materials. In summary, barnacle recruitment emerges from the interplay of thermal and optical mechanisms, but fine-scale refugia exert the strongest influence in intertidal habitats. Beyond thermal modulation, larval responses to light and shade likely contribute to these patterns. Recognizing and harnessing both processes can guide ecological design strategies that enhance the effectiveness of restoration and conservation initiatives amid accelerating climate change and coastal development.

Methods

Material characterization of 3D-printed artificial substrata

Under laboratory conditions, we assessed the effect of PLA colour and material density or internal infill on thermal conductivity. Standard cylindrical test probes (60 mm in diameter, 120 mm in height; ASTM D5334-08) were printed using Polylactic acid (PLA) filaments (1.75 mm, Cicla, Chile), a compostable, cellulose-derived material known for its biocompatibility in biological applications61. Test probes were printed at two different densities or internal infill: 100% (solid) and 25% (porous). A two-way ANOVA was used to compare the effect of colour and material density on the thermal conductivity of test probes. Thermal conductivity was measured using KD2-Pro equipment, with two perforations (1.4 mm diameter, 3 mm depth) placed 6 mm apart on the surface of each probe. In addition, surface reflectance (%) in the visible spectral range (400–700 nm) of black and white flat tiles, and the rocky substrata of the study site was measured using a portable colourimeter (3NH; PS2040). Finally, to evaluate if PLA colour pigments may introduce chemical differences beyond thermal effects, we performed an ATR-FTIR (Attenuated Transmitted Reflectance-Fourier Transformed Infrared Spectrometry, Jasco 4600, Japan) analysis. A correlational analysis was used to evidence similarities between the FTIR spectra of both PLA filaments. Then, 3D-tiles were printed with a 25% internal infill density to support the structure. We designed 10 × 10 × 2 cm tiles (L×W×H) representing flat and heterogeneous habitat structures and two colours (black and white) (Fig. 2A)61. The heterogeneous surface featured two distinctive microhabitat identities: four curved crevices (61.8 cm2) and 5 ridges (62.7 cm2), which increased the available surface area to 130.7 cm2 (Fig. 2A).

Thermal characterization of artificial substrates, experimental design, and environmental variability

The study was conducted in northern Chile (approximately 23°S), within Antofagasta Bay, an embayment area characterized by high larval retention and relatively stable recruitment of intertidal invertebrates throughout the year, with locally increased recruitment due to water retention occurring inside the bay32. A total of 32 tiles, combining habitat structures (heterogeneous/flat) and colours (black/white) were used, with 8 replicates per treatment. The tiles were deployed in the mid-upper intertidal zone (approximately 0.7 m above mean lower low water, MLLW) on a rocky platform at the study site (El Way, Antofagasta city; Fig. 2D) using stainless steel screws and a rotary hammer. The tiles were arranged following a systematic and interspersed design62, allowing replicates from all treatment to be positioned adjacent to each other along the same tidal level to ensure comparable environmental exposure. The position of each tile within a sequence was randomized, preventing clustering by treatment while maintaining a consistent spatial arrangement across the rocky platform (see Fig. 2D). The installation was completed during low tide on March 22, 2023 (16:00–18:00 h UTC; 0.3 m MLLW), and thermal variability on the tile surfaces was assessed the following day. Infrared thermography (IRT) was used to characterize the thermal environment on both flat and heterogeneous tiles, employing a FLIR E5 camera (FLIR Systems, Inc., Wilsonville, OR). The first IR measurements were performed during the ebb tide on March 23, under clear-sky, sunny conditions typical of the region, with solar irradiance ranging from 616 to 913 W m⁻². Infrared thermographic (IRT) images were taken twice on March 23: first, approximately 2 h prior to maximum low tide (0.6 m MLLW; 15:30 UTC), and again at maximum low tide (0.3 m MLLW; 17:50 UTC). IRT images were taken from ca. 1 m above the 3D tiles to ensure a perpendicular field of view and minimize thermal interference from surrounding rock surfaces and seawater splashes, thereby reducing background reflectance effects. The FLIR E5 camera has a temperature range of -20 °C to + 250 °C, infrared resolution of 120 × 90 pixels, thermal sensitivity of < 0.15 °C, and a measurement accuracy of ± 2 °C. Consistent with values commonly applied to wet and natural surfaces in intertidal environments, an emissivity value of 0.95 was applied to account for atmospheric effects on the artificial substrata63. In the case of PLA, previous studies have adopted emissivity values around 0.9264. Although surface colour and finish can introduce minor variation, emissivity in the infrared range is largely independent of colour and only weakly affected by surface texture, particularly in high-emissivity materials, such as organic filaments63. On April 20, 2023, after 29 days of exposure, the IRT imaging process was repeated at two h prior to MLLW (13:53, 0.6 m MLLW) and at the maximum MLLW (15:53 h; 0.3 m MLLW). After this period, the tiles were collected, transported to the laboratory, and stored at -20 °C for further processing. IRT images of the tiles were taken only at the start and end of the experiment to provide baseline and final reference values, as the objective was to quantify fine-scale thermal variation on tile surfaces. Six tiles were lost at the end of the experiment (three H/W; One H/B, one F/B, and 1 H/W). During both the beginning and end sampling periods, additional environmental data were collected using continuous temperature sensors (Tidbit, Onset Corp., USA) anchored in the mid intertidal zone, and radiation sensors (W/m²) installed along the low tidal period of sampling (ca. 6 h). Both sensors recorded data at 5-minute intervals.

Estimates of barnacle recruitment rate and surface temperature of 3D–tiles

We investigated the recruitment of two chthamalid barnacle species, Jehlius cirratus and Notochthamalus scabrosus, which are common invertebrates in the intertidal habitats of northern Chile. These species exhibit continuous year-round recruitment, with higher rates during the austral spring–summer period32, in contrast to the seasonal recruitment reported for central Chile and southern Argentina65,66. Recruits corresponded to post-metamorphic individuals present at the time of sampling, which in these species typically range from ~ 0.5 to 1 mm in size. Barnacle recruitment on each 3D–tile was observed and counted under a dissection microscope (Olympus SZ30). Recruits were identified to species level based on early post-metamorphic morphological traits, with J. cirratus exhibiting a more elongated, golden-toned shell, and N. scabrosus showing a more rounded shape with a grey-green coloration. These counts were recorded with respect to habitat structure treatments (heterogeneous/flat– black/white tiles) and the specific microhabitat identities where settlers were found (crevices vs. ridges) within heterogeneous 3D-tiles. Macroalgae or other sessile invertebrates do not colonize the tiles, likely due to high desiccation stress in the mid-high intertidal zone31. The surface area of the flat tiles was 100 cm², while the heterogeneous tiles had a larger surface area (130.7 cm²) due to the presence of crevices; thus, the recruitment rate was standardized as the number of barnacles per day per unit area. However, this metric should be interpreted with caution, as it does not represent ecologically equivalent recruitment area due to differences in surface orientation and exposure across microhabitats. Surface temperatures of the 3D–tiles were extracted from infrared thermographic images using FLIR Tools software (FLIR Systems, Inc., Wilsonville, OR). To characterize the surface thermal environment, two straight transects (ca. 3 cm in length) were randomly placed over each tile image. For flat tiles, the transects were drawn on opposite sides, while for heterogeneous tiles, the transects were positioned across randomly selected areas of two microhabitat identities: crevices and ridges67. Each transect provided an average of ca. 43 data points, which were then averaged to describe the temperature associated with the habitat structure (flat vs. heterogeneous) and, in the case of heterogeneous tiles, the corresponding microhabitat types (crevices vs. ridges) for each colour treatment. Temperature variations occurring on the surface of the artificial substrates along the ebb tidal period were estimated as the difference between the temperature recorded ca. 2 h before the maximum MLLW with the temperature recorded at maximum MLLW.

Statistical analysis

First, we evaluated the effects of Habitat Structure (HS; flat vs. heterogeneous) and Colour (C; black vs. white) on temperature variation and barnacle recruitment using factorial two-way ANOVA, with both factors treated as fixed effects and Tukey HSD used as post-hoc comparison. Temperature analysis was performed using the surface temperatures recorded at the beginning (March) and end (April) of the experimental period. Normality was evaluated using Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests and homogeneity of variances using Bartlett’s test. When required, temperature data were log10(y) transformed to meet ANOVA assumptions (see Supplementary Material, Table S2). Recruitment analyses were conducted separately for each barnacle species using loge(y+1)-transformed data to accommodate zero counts and linearize multiplicative ecological processes underlying population abundances65. Secondly, to evaluate temperature and recruitment differences between microhabitat identities (MHI: crevices vs. ridges) within heterogeneous 3D-tiles, we fitted a mixed two–way ANOVA in which colour and MHI were treated as fixed factors and tiles as a random effect. Because crevices and ridges represent repeated measures within each tile, MHI was modelled as a repeated measure (i.e., non-independent replicates), nested within tiles. Because this mixed design does not yield an independent residual term suitable for classical post hoc procedures, pairwise contrasts for MHI and colours were estimated using Least-Squares Means (LSMeans), and interpreted after back-transformation to the original scale of recruitment. Due to imbalanced sample sizes, all models were fitted using type III sums of squares, which are robust to moderate differences in sample replication. For recruitment analyses, residuals normality was evaluated using Anderson–Darling tests, which is robust under small sample sizes and moderate departures from normality. Homogeneity of variances across factor levels was assessed using Levene’s test, which is less sensitive to non-normal error distribution, and offers good power and performance under small sample sizes and moderately skewed datasets. Given the limited degrees of freedom, we focused on simple, biologically interpretable contrasts, and avoided overinterpretation of cross-treatment comparisons. Although some recruitment models did not fully meet normality assumption, the loge(y+1) transformation effectively reduced skew and ensured homogeneity. Because ANOVA is robust to moderate departures from normality, but sensitive to heteroscedasticity68, analyses were retained once variance homogeneity was satisfied (see Table S2 in supplementary material).
