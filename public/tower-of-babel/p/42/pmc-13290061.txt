Evaluation of Pharynx in Isolated Cleft Palate and Pierre-Robin Syndrome With Cleft Palate
Authors: Baihui Liu, Jun Li, Shuyang Dai, Chenbin Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the difference between the 3-dimensional structure of the cavity in patients with isolated cleft palate and Pierre-Robin syndrome with cleft palate. Retrospective measurements of high need ratio (NR), velopharyngeal gap (VPG), soft palate length (SPL), and long velopharyngeal depth (VPD) were taken from lateral and anteroposterior (AP) cephalometric radiographs of isolated cleft palate and PRS with cleft palate. A total of 29 isolated cleft palate and 18 PRS patients with cleft palate were included. The VPG, SPL, and VPD of cleft palate were higher than those in the PRS group. But there is no statistically significant difference of the NR values between the 2 groups (P=0.509). NR and long VPD were contributing factors to velopharyngeal insufficiency (VPI) occurrence. The higher VPD in isolated cleft palate patients compared with PRS may reflect age-related assessment variability. However, the 2 groups show no significant difference in NR values, suggesting comparable velopharyngeal insufficiency between PRS and isolated cleft palate patients.

Cleft palate (CP) is the most common congenital malformation of the oral and maxillofacial region, characterized by a gap in the palate present at birth. Surgery is the major treatment. To promote normal speech development, most cleft palate centers recommend primary palatoplasty between 6 and 18 months of age. However, despite timely primary repair, 20% to 30% of patients still develop velopharyngeal insufficiency (VPI) postoperatively, which refers to the inability of the soft palate to form a seal with the posterior pharyngeal wall during speech, resulting in air escape into the nasal cavity.1 Pierre-Robin sequence (PRS) consists of a clinical triad of micrognathia, glossoptosis, and airway obstruction. Its incidence in newborns is ∼1 in 14,000 to 8000.2 More than 3/4 PRS infants were associated with cleft palate.3 Many studies have reported worse speech development in PRS children after cleft palate repair. It is assumed that PRS is associated with a wide, often U-shaped cleft palate, even though severity can vary.4 We performed this study to investigate the difference between the 3-dimensional structure cavity in patient with isolated CP and PRS with cleft palate, aim to provide another valuable insight for postoperative speech development assessment.

METHODS

Patient

Medical and imaging records of 29 patients with CP and 18 patients with PRS at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University between 2020.1 and 2024.6 were collected. All patients underwent lateral and anteroposterior cephalometric radiographs.

Measurements

The measures were analyzed in sagittal views of the X-ray. We measured the velopharyngeal gap (VPG), soft palate length (SPL), and velopharyngeal depth (VPD), and calculated a quantitative ratio between VPD and SPL, which is known as the need ratio (NR) (Fig. 1). The ratio is known to be between 0.6 and 0.7 in healthy individuals aged 6 months to 15 years.5 In other words, it is considered normal when the VPD is about 60% to 70% compared with the SPL.

Sagittal views of the X-ray: (1) soft palate length; (2) velopharyngeal depth; (3) velopharyngeal gap.

Statistical Analysis

The t test was performed to compare groups. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 23; IBM). A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

The CP group consisted of 29 patients (female 17, male 12), and 18 in the PRS group (female 8, male 10). The median age of the 2 groups at examination were 23.7 months (range: 8–73 mo) and 3.8 months (range: 1 d–19 mo), respectively (Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/SCS/J175). In the CP group, X-ray examinations were performed preoperatively for 10 children, while the remaining 10 underwent X-ray imaging between 1 month and 3 years postoperatively. All PRS group children underwent X-ray imaging within 1 week postoperatively. The Sommerlad method was used for palatoplasty, and MDO for PRS correction, all operated by one surgeon.

We identified significant intergroup differences in SPL, VPD, and VPG (P<0.0001, P<0.0001, P=0.048) but not in NR values (P=0.509) between isolated cleft palate and PRS-associated cleft palate cohorts (Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/SCS/J175).

DISCUSSION

To compare the 3-dimensional pharyngeal structures between children with isolated CP and those with PRS combined with cleft palate, we measured the NR, SPL, VPD, and VPG using lateral cephalograms. Previous studies have suggested that high NR, wide VPG, and long VPD were contributing factors to VPI occurrence (P<0.05). Our findings revealed a statistically significant difference in both SPL, VPD and VPG between the CP group and PRS group (P<0.05), whereas no significant difference was observed in NR values across the 2 groups. We thought the observed differences in SPL, VPD, and VPG are primarily age-dependent, with the CP group having a mean age of 23 months compared with 3.8 months in the PRS group. Although no significant difference was found in NR values (0.728±0.088 vs. 0.748±0.118, respectively), both values were notably higher than those in normal controls.

Cleft palate surgery aims to achieve dual goals not only anatomic closure of the palatal cleft but also the optimization of speech development. Numerous factors influence speech outcomes, including surgical timing, surgical technique, and velopharyngeal function. Therefore, comprehensive preoperative assessment is critical. Key determinants such as nasopharyngeal anatomy and velopharyngeal closure patterns significantly influence both the choice of surgical approach and postoperative rehabilitation outcomes.

A robust body of evidence indicates that PRS-associated cleft palate patients exhibit significantly higher rates of postoperative VPI (OR: 2.3, 95% CI: 1.7–3.1) and impaired speech acquisition compared with isolated cleft palate cases.6,7 Potential contributing factors include the severity and width of the cleft, maxillary anatomic structure, surgical techniques, and their association with underlying syndromes. However, Goudy et al8 reported comparable velopharyngeal function between the 2 groups, with no significant differences in reoperation rates due to either primary or secondary VPI. They used the 3-flap technique for cleft palate repair, achieving complete velopharyngeal closure rates of 76.74% and 78.13% in PRS patients and isolated cleft palate patients, respectively. Khosla et al9 applied the Furlow technique and found that postoperatively, nonsyndromic PRS patients showed no significant differences compared with isolated cleft palate patients in terms of velopharyngeal function, hypernasality, nasal air emission, and articulation. They further concluded that surgical age, cleft characteristics, and surgeon experience had no significant impact on speech outcomes. In addition, Naros et al10 found no significant intergroup differences in speech development among children aged 5 to 6 years postoperatively when comparing isolated CP and PRS-associated cleft palate cases. All children studied showed good to very good speech development without serious impairment, which suggested isolated PRS may not necessarily lead to speech development disorders.

Our study primarily aims to measure the 3-dimensional pharyngeal structure in both groups of children during the initial phase of treatment, in hopes of enabling early prediction and evaluation of speech development prognosis by excluding factors such as the surgeon, surgical technique, and age at surgery. This study similarly demonstrated comparable NR values without significant differences between the 2 groups, providing evidence that postoperative speech outcomes may not differ substantially between isolated CP and PRS-associated cleft palate patients.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small. More notably, a key limitation arises from the age disparity between the cohorts, as all radiographs were obtained perioperatively. This age difference likely explains the significantly smaller SPL, VPD, and VPG measurements observed in the PRS group compared with the isolated CP group. We aim to address these limitations in future research.

CONCLUSION

Overall, our results demonstrate no significant differences in NR values between the groups, suggesting through preoperative assessment that children with PRS-associated cleft palate may not necessarily have poorer long-term speech development outcomes compared with those with isolated CP.
