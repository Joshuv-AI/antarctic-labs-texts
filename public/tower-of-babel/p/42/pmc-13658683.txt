A review of mental health and psychosocial support needs in Central Africa with emphasis on service gaps and opportunities for action

Introduction

Mental health and psychosocial well-being are fundamental components of overall health, yet they remain critically underprioritized in many regions, particularly in Central Africa. The region faces a confluence of factors, including conflict, displacement, poverty, and fragile health systems, that exacerbate the burden of mental health disorders and impede access to appropriate care [1, 2]. Despite these challenges, global and regional efforts to understand and address mental health needs in Central Africa have been limited, often overshadowed by other urgent health priorities such as infectious diseases [3, 4].

The importance of integrating mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) into primary health care and humanitarian responses has been increasingly recognized. The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes that mental health is a vital aspect of health that deserves comprehensive attention, especially in settings affected by conflict and crises [1]. Nevertheless, the scope and scale of mental health needs in Central Africa remain insufficiently documented, hindering effective policy formulation and intervention development [2, 5].

Central Africa is characterized by persistent instability, armed conflicts, and humanitarian crises that significantly impact mental health [1]. Prolonged exposure to violence, displacement, and loss of livelihoods contributes to a heightened prevalence of depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and other psychological conditions. A significant barrier to addressing these issues is the scarcity of mental health infrastructure, trained personnel, and culturally appropriate services. Studies have highlighted that existing health systems are often ill-equipped to meet the psychosocial needs of affected populations, especially in rural and conflict-affected areas [6, 7].

Moreover, social determinants such as poverty, limited education, and stigma further hinder help-seeking behaviors and the utilization of mental health services. The intersection of cultural beliefs and mental health perceptions also influences help-seeking and treatment adherence, necessitating culturally sensitive approaches to intervention [8].

Recent research efforts have begun to shed light on the mental health landscape in Africa. For instance, studies underscore the high prevalence of mental health disorders among conflict-affected populations, emphasizing the urgent need for scalable, contextually tailored interventions [9, 10]. Several investigations have explored various models of care, including community-based and task-shifting approaches, to bridge the gap caused by human resource shortages [11, 12].

However, despite these advances, significant gaps persist. There remains a paucity of longitudinal data on treatment outcomes, a limited understanding of culturally specific expressions of mental distress, and inadequate integration of mental health into broader health and development agendas [13, 14]. Furthermore, most existing studies focus on specific populations or regions, lacking a comprehensive overview of the diverse needs across Central Africa.

Given the complexity and heterogeneity of mental health needs across Central Africa, this narrative review synthesizes existing knowledge, identifies gaps, and informs future research and policy. Understanding the regional landscape will facilitate the development of culturally appropriate, sustainable, and scalable mental health interventions [15].

Methods

Literature search strategy

This narrative literature review synthesized existing evidence on MHPSS needs across Central Africa. A comprehensive systematic search of electronic databases was performed to identify relevant peer-reviewed articles, reports, and grey literature published between January 2010 and December 2025. The primary databases searched included PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Both English and French language sources were included.

The search strategy employed a combination of keywords and MeSH terms: (“mental health” OR “psychosocial support” OR “PTSD” OR “depression” OR “anxiety” OR “trauma”) AND (“Central Africa” OR “Cameroon” OR “Chad” OR “Rwanda” OR “Democratic Republic of Congo” OR “Gabon” OR “Republic of Congo” OR “Equatorial Guinea” OR “Burundi” OR “Central African Republic”) AND (“refugee” OR “displaced” OR “conflict-affected” OR “humanitarian”). Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used. The search was supplemented by manual screening of reference lists.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they: (1) were published in peer-reviewed journals or reputable organizational reports; (2) were conducted within Central African countries or relevant refugee settings; (3) focused on MHPSS needs, interventions, or service delivery; (4) employed qualitative, quantitative, or mixed-methods approaches; and (5) were published in English or French. Exclusion criteria included studies with a primary focus outside the region, non-peer-reviewed opinion pieces, and articles lacking sufficient methodological detail.

Study selection process

An initial screening of titles and abstracts was performed independently by two reviewers. Full-text articles were then retrieved and assessed against inclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. A total of 515 records were identified; after deduplication (n = 412), title/abstract screening (n = 68), and full-text review (n = 23), nine articles met inclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

Data extraction and synthesis

A standardized data extraction form collated key information: study setting, population, methodology, key MHPSS findings, and identified gaps. Data were synthesized narratively to identify common themes, regional variations, and evidence gaps.

Quality appraisal

Each included study was critically appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) version 2018. All nine studies met at least 4 of 5 quality criteria; none were excluded based on quality appraisal.

Ethical considerations

As this review involved synthesis of publicly available literature, ethical approval was not required.

Results

Overview of included literature

Nine studies met inclusion criteria (Table 1). These encompassed quantitative surveys (n = 4), qualitative studies (n = 3), and mixed-methods or systematic reviews (n = 2). Geographically, studies covered Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) [16, 17], Central African Republic (CAR) [18], Chad [19], Rwanda [20, 21], and Cameroon [22]. Populations included internally displaced persons (IDPs), refugees, conflict-affected communities, and post-genocide survivors.

Characteristics of included studies

Prevalence and spectrum of mental health disorders

A recurring theme is the high prevalence of mental health disorders among conflict-affected populations. A large-scale systematic review in DRC reported PTSD prevalence exceeding 30% among IDPs, with depression rates similarly elevated [16]. Among female DRC refugees in Uganda, conflict-related violence was associated with PTSD in 35% and major depression in 42% [23]. In CAR, studies documented pervasive anxiety and depression linked to ongoing insecurity and displacement [18]. Among Darfuri refugees in Chad, trauma exposure was nearly universal, with over 50% meeting criteria for probable PTSD [19]. In post-genocide Rwanda, elevated rates of PTSD and substance abuse persisted decades after the conflict [20].

Mental health issues are compounded by ongoing insecurity, poverty, and social disintegration. Several studies emphasize that symptoms are frequently underdiagnosed and undertreated due to limited infrastructure and stigma [16, 24]. Comorbidity between PTSD, depression, and anxiety is common, with women and children disproportionately affected [25, 26].

Barriers to accessing MHPSS

Multiple barriers impede access. Key obstacles include inadequate mental health infrastructure, scarcity of trained professionals, and limited funding. In CAR and DRC, mental health services are predominantly concentrated in urban centers, leaving rural and conflict-affected communities underserved [16, 18, 23].

Stigma remains a pervasive barrier. Qualitative studies describe community perceptions of mental illness as linked to spiritual or supernatural causes, leading to reliance on traditional healers rather than formal health services [5, 14]. In Cameroon, the WHO-AIMS assessment revealed fewer than one psychiatrist per million population, with mental health budgets below 1% of health expenditures [24]. Logistical challenges—insecurity, displacement, transportation difficulties—further hinder service utilization [25].

Existing MHPSS interventions

The literature documents various MHPSS interventions. Task-shifting approaches involving training community health workers to deliver basic psychosocial support have shown promise in improving service coverage [11, 12]. In Rwanda, community-based social healing models incorporating Ubuntu principles demonstrated reductions in PTSD symptoms and improved psychosocial functioning [20]. In Chad, culturally adapted trauma counseling for Darfuri refugees reduced distress symptoms [19].

Several studies evaluate psychosocial interventions such as group therapy, trauma counseling, and psychological first aid [7, 16, 19, 20, 27]. Findings suggest that culturally adapted interventions delivered through community engagement can reduce trauma symptoms and improve well-being. However, sustainability and scalability remain constrained by resource limitations and a lack of policy support [18, 28].

Cultural and social contexts

Understanding local cultural contexts is critical. Many studies emphasize integrating traditional beliefs into formal MHPSS responses. Collaborations with traditional healers and community leaders are highlighted as strategies to enhance acceptability. In Rwanda, post-genocide programs successfully partnered with local healers and incorporated community reconciliation practices [20, 28]. In CAR, engagement of community leaders facilitated MHPSS delivery in highly insecure settings [18].

Social determinants such as poverty, gender inequality, and social exclusion exacerbate mental health issues. Women and children experience higher rates of trauma and limited access to support [25, 27]. Addressing these determinants is essential for comprehensive MHPSS strategies.

Gaps and recommendations

Notable gaps persist. First, there is a paucity of longitudinal studies tracking mental health outcomes over time; most evidence is cross-sectional [16, 24]. Second, intervention research remains limited, with few randomized controlled trials to definitively establish efficacy; exceptions include the Rwanda community healing trial [20]. Third, systemic issues, including policy gaps, funding shortages, and workforce deficits, hinder sustainable MHPSS programs [18, 25].

Recommendations emerging from the literature include: increased investment in mental health infrastructure [24]; integration of culturally sensitive approaches [19, 20]; enhanced community engagement [18, 28]; task-shifting to non-specialist workers [11, 12]; and strengthened partnerships between governmental agencies, NGOs, and local communities [25].

Discussion

Synthesis of key findings

This review underscores profound MHPSS needs in Central Africa. Across studies, there is a consistent elevation of trauma-related disorders, including PTSD, depression, and anxiety, particularly among IDPs, refugees, women, and children. These conditions are compounded by complex trauma, gender-based violence, and social dislocation, impairing individual functioning and community cohesion [19, 23, 24].

Mental health services are grossly inadequate, with significant disparities between need and access. Infrastructure is concentrated in urban areas; rural and conflict-affected zones are underserved. Systems are underfunded, poorly integrated into primary healthcare, and reliant on limited specialized personnel. Consequently, many populations do not receive needed care, exacerbating cycles of suffering and social exclusion [16, 28].

Interpretation and implications

The mental health crisis in Central Africa is intertwined with broader social and political issues: ongoing conflict, insecurity, and weak health systems. The high prevalence of trauma-related disorders among displaced populations indicates a pressing need for culturally sensitive, contextually appropriate interventions that address both individual symptoms and social determinants.

Evidence supporting community-based and task-shifting approaches highlights their potential to expand access and foster resilience. Programs involving non-specialist health workers have shown promise [11, 12], but scaling up requires attention to cultural relevance, local capacities, and sustainability. Stigma and misconceptions remain significant barriers, underscoring the need for community education and stigma reduction campaigns [20, 25].

Challenges and gaps

Persistent challenges impede progress. Political instability and limited governmental commitment restrict resource allocation and policy development [25]. Security concerns restrict access to vulnerable populations. Lack of locally adapted, culturally sensitive frameworks hampers effective implementation.

A notable gap is the paucity of longitudinal research evaluating the sustainability and long-term impact of psychosocial interventions. Most programs are short-term and lack rigorous monitoring [16, 24]. Despite recognition of the importance of integrating mental health into primary healthcare, operationalization remains limited. Many interventions focus predominantly on trauma symptoms, neglecting broader psychosocial issues such as social cohesion and livelihood support.

Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, only nine studies met the inclusion criteria, reflecting the true paucity of MHPSS research in Central Africa, but also limiting generalizability. Second, heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and outcomes precluded meta-analysis. Third, exclusion of non-English/French literature and unpublished grey literature may have introduced language and publication bias. Fourth, the lack of longitudinal studies prevents the assessment of causal relationships.

Recommendations for future action

To address these gaps, the literature advocates a multi-sectoral approach combining mental health services with social and economic development initiatives. Strengthening community engagement and culturally sensitive practices is vital. Capacity-building targeting local health workers, community leaders, and traditional healers should be prioritized [20, 28].

Policy reforms are essential to increase political commitment and allocate resources for mental health. Integrating mental health into universal health coverage frameworks can facilitate access, especially in rural and underserved areas [24]. Establishing rigorous monitoring and evaluation systems will be crucial for measuring impact and refining intervention strategies.

Addressing stigma through community education campaigns and involving local stakeholders in program design can foster greater community ownership. Emphasizing resilience, social cohesion, and psychosocial well-being, alongside symptom reduction, aligns with culturally appropriate care models and promotes long-term recovery [19, 20].

Conclusion

This narrative review underscores significant MHPSS needs in Central Africa, a region characterized by complex socio-political dynamics, economic challenges, and ongoing conflicts. Mental health issues are pervasive yet underrecognized and undertreated due to limited healthcare infrastructure, pervasive stigma, and insufficient integration of mental health into primary healthcare. Vulnerable populations, including refugees, IDPs, women, and children, are disproportionately affected.

The reviewed studies consistently emphasize culturally sensitive, context-specific approaches to MHPSS delivery. Despite recognition of these needs, substantial gaps persist in data availability, mental health workforce capacity, and sustainable funding mechanisms.

Addressing MHPSS needs in Central Africa requires a concerted, sustainable effort rooted in local realities and driven by a commitment to human rights and social justice. Future efforts must focus on enhancing service accessibility, reducing stigma, and building resilient health systems capable of responding to the region’s unique challenges.
