Comparative analysis of chlamydia pneumoniae pneumonia and Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in children
Authors: Yongkang Wang, Tianyu Huang, Weikang Gao, Yun Liu, Wenna Shen, Tingting Yang, Hongchuan Yu, Xinrong Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChlamydia pneumoniae pneumonia (CPP) in children often presents with mild clinical manifestations, leading to less clinical attention. This study aimed to compare the clinical features of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) and CPP in pediatric patients and to identify risk factors for lobar involvement in CPP.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 145 children with CPP and 145 contemporaneously hospitalized children with MPP. Clinical characteristics were compared between the two groups. Patients with CPP were further stratified into lobar pneumonia and bronchopneumonia subgroups to assess risk factors for lobar consolidation.ResultsChildren with CPP were significantly older than those with MPP 11.00(8.33–12.42)years vs. 6.20 (4.00–8.00)years, p < 0.05). The CPP group exhibited lower peak fever, shorter febrile duration, a higher incidence of chest pain, and lower rates of tachypnea and hypoxemia (all p < 0.05). Laboratory findings showed significantly higher eosinophil counts and lower levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in CPP patients compared to MPP patients (all p < 0.05). Lobar pneumonia accounted for 61.4% of CPP cases. On binary logistic regression analysis, decreased breath sounds was identified as a risk factor for lobar involvement in CPP.ConclusionsCompared to MPP, CPP patients is characterized by more frequent chest pain, lower inflammatory marker, and higher eosinophil counts. The presence of decreased breath sounds may serve as a clinical indicator for lobar pneumonia in children with C. pneumoniae infection.

Background

Atypical pathogens, including Chlamydia pneumoniae are often underestimated in children due to diagnostic challenges, variable prevalence, and heterogeneous clinical severity [1]. The reported incidence of C. pneumoniae infection in children with community-acquired pneumonia ranges from 3.5% to 16.2% [2–4], highlighting its role as a relevant etiological agent. While Mycoplasma pneumoniae is highly prevalent in China and has been extensively studied clinically, research on C. pneumoniae pneumonia remains relatively limited, In China, while Mycoplasma pneumoniae has been widely investigated given its high prevalence, clinical studies focusing on C. pneumoniae pneumonia remain relatively scarce-partly owing to its milder presentation. Moreover, the common empirical use of macrolide antibiotics may obscure the true epidemiological burden of C. pneumoniae infection. Both M. pneumoniae and C. pneumoniae share overlapping clinical features as atypical pathogens further complicating their differential diagnosis.

A study of 393 hospitalized children with MPP reported that lobar or segmental consolidation was the most frequent imaging finding, occurring in 37%of cases [5]. In contrast, a study of CPP reported patchy high-density opacities in 84.6% of pediatric patients, with air bronchograms present in 71.8% [6]. Meanwhile, a Korean study noted lobar pneumonia in 38.1% of children with CPP [2], suggesting that C. pneumoniae infection is not always mild. Accurate recognition of lobar pneumonia is essential for identifying potentially severe cases and facilitating timely, individualized clinical management. Owing to the nonspecific clinical manifestations of C. pneumoniae infection, recent literature on this pathogen in children has been scarce. Therefore, in clinical practice, doctors pay less attention to Chlamydia pneumoniae.

The greatest feature of our study is that it simultaneously analyzes and compares the clinical characteristics of Chlamydia pneumoniae (CP) and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) to highlight the differences between the two pathogens. Notably, the number of cases of Chlamydia pneumoniae included in this study is relatively large. This study has enriched the clinical research evidence for pediatric chlamydial pneumonia. The aim of this study is to identify these two atypical pathogens. Additionally, the study sought to identify risk factors associated with lobar involvement in CPP, in order to support early recognition of severe cases and guide clinical management.

Materials and methods

Subjects

This retrospective case-control study included 145 children with CPP hospitalized to Xi’an Children’s Hospital from January 2025 to June 2025, as well as 145 MPP patients were randomly selected from concurrent admissions during the same period serve as control. Data on demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, physical signs, laboratory results, chest imaging findings, and treatment were collected from the included children. The primary objective was to compare clinical and paraclinical features between the CPP and MPP groups.

Treatment regimen

The indications for bronchoscopy include: 1. Severe pneumonia patients with excessive thick sputum and inability to expectorate; 2. Large area of pulmonary consolidation, persistent unabsorption of pulmonary consolidation or presence of atelectasis. The main purpose is to drain airway secretions and promote lung re-expansion through bronchoscopy.

All patients received inhaled glucocorticosteroids: budesonide at a dose of 1 mg per dose, twice daily for patients over 1 year old; and 0.5 mg per dose, twice daily for patients under 1 year old. Some patients received systemic glucocorticosteroids: methylprednisolone at a dose of 2 mg/kg per day. The indications were: 1. Severe mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia: presence of tachypnea, dyspnea, and hypoxemia; 2. Refractory mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia: persistent high fever or worsening clinical symptoms and imaging findings after more than 72 h of standardized macrolide treatment; 3. Excessive systemic inflammatory response: such as significant elevation of inflammatory indicators including CRP, LDH, and ferritin. All patients received intravenous azithromycin during hospitalization, followed by sequential oral azithromycin after discharge.

Cases were defined as lobar pneumonia based on the *Guidelines for the Management of Community-Acquired Pneumonia in Children (2024 Revision)* [9]. Radiological criteria for defining lobar pneumonia: In this study, chest imaging included chest X-ray and low-dose chest CT. Lobar pneumonia was defined according to the pediatric pulmonary imaging consensus: Unilateral or complete consolidation involving one or more whole pulmonary lobes, with uniform increased opacity in the corresponding lung field, visible air bronchogram inside the lesion, and no scattered patchy infiltrates limited to local lung segments. Cases with patchy, segmental or peribronchial opacities were classified as bronchopneumonia and excluded from the lobar pneumonia group. This criterion was strictly implemented throughout the study. All imaging examinations and image interpretations were performed following unified operating and diagnostic standards formulated by our radiology department: Standardized imaging protocol: All children received routine chest radiography or low-dose chest CT with consistent scanning parameters, positioning and image acquisition standards.

Dual independent review

All imaging images were firstly interpreted by two attending radiologists with more than 5 years of pediatric imaging experience, who were blinded to the patients’ etiological results and clinical data. If the two radiologists reached consistent conclusions, the result was adopted.

Adjudication procedure

In case of inconsistent judgments between the two radiologists, a senior chief radiologist conducted a third review to make the final diagnosis. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xi’an Children’s Hospital, Xi’an Jiaotong University. (2026-009-01).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted on available data; no imputation was performed for missing data. For the analysis of peak body temperature and duration of fever, only children who exhibited fever were included. To reduce the influence of potential confounding factors and balance baseline characteristics between the Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) group and Chlamydia pneumoniae pneumonia (CPP) group, 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching (PSM) was performed. The covariates included age, gender, disease course, pre-admission use of antibiotics, pre-admission use of glucocorticoids and co-infection. The caliper value was set at 0.2 times the standard deviation of the propensity score, and sampling without replacement was adopted. Standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to evaluate the between-group balance of baseline variables; an SMD < 0.1 was defined as good balance. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (S.D.) and compared with independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median with ranges (IQR) and analyzed using Mann–Whitney U-test or non-parametric methods. Categorical variables are reported as percentages and were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Variables meeting significant differences (p < 0.05) between lobar and non-lobar subgroups within the CPP and MPP cohorts, along with variables considered potential clinical confounders, were entered into a binary logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors for lobar involvement in CPP group. Statistical significance was defined as a two‑sided p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.

Results

Among 145 pediatric CPP patients, 143 (98.6%) underwent respiratory nucleic acid testing for respiratory pathogens via pharyngeal swabs on the day of admission, of whom 84 (57.9%) tested positive for CP nucleic acid. Additionally, 115 children (79.3%) underwent CP-specific antibody testing, with 81 (55.9%) showing positive antibody results. One patient received bronchoalveolar lavage fluid high-throughput testing during hospitalization (Table 1).

The CPP group was significantly older than the MPP group, with median ages of 11.00 (8.33–12.42) years versus 6.20 (4.00–8.00) years (p < 0.05). The proportion of male patients was 61.4% in the CPP group and 42.8% in the MPP group (p < 0.05). The proportion of patients with expectoration was lower in the MPP group (p < 0.05). The incidence of fever was significantly higher in the MPP group than in the CPP group (94.5% vs. 44.8%, p < 0.05). The highest body temperature in the MPP group was significantly higher than that in the CPP group, with median values of 39.2 (38.8–39.7) °C and 38.5 (38.0–39.0) °C, (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the duration of fever was longer in the MPP group (7 days in the MPP group vs. 3 days in the CPP group, p < 0.05). Chest pain was more prevalent in the CPP group (11 patients, 7.6%), while no cases were reported in the MPP group (p < 0.05). Additionally, the proportion of patients with shortness of breath was higher in the MPP group than in the CPP group (6.9% vs. 0%, p < 0.05). Hypoxemia was also more prevalent in the MPP group (8 cases, 5.5%) compared to the CPP group (0 cases, p < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of symptoms and signs such as cough, headache, dyspnea, and decreased breath sounds. Laboratory analysis showed that levels of CRP, PCT, LDH, and CK-MB were significantly lower in the CPP group than in the MPP group (p < 0.05). In contrast, eosinophil count was higher in the CPP group than in the MPP group (0.19 × 10⁹/L vs. 0.05 × 10⁹/L, p < 0.05). No significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, lymphocyte percentage, NLR, ALT, or the proportion of co-infections. Moreover, the proportion of patients with pleural effusion and lobar consolidation on imaging was higher in the MPP group than in the CPP group (11.7% vs. 0.7% and 75.9% vs. 61.4%, p < 0.05). Significant differences were also observed in treatment. The proportion of patients who received antibiotics and glucocorticoids prior to admission was higher in the MPP group than in the CPP group. After admission, the MPP group had a higher rate of bronchoscopic lavage (53.1% vs. 24.8%, p < 0.05) and glucocorticoid treatment (21.4% vs. 2.8%, p < 0.05). Although the overall disease course did not differ significantly between the groups, the length of hospital stay was slightly longer in the MPP group than in the CPP group (7 days vs. 6 days, p < 0.05) (Table 2).

Within the CPP cohort, 89 children (61.4%) were classified as lobar pneumonia and 56 children(38.6%) as bronchopneumonia. Discussion

It was observed that a significant increase in the number of CPP cases from January to June 2025 compared to previous periods, suggesting that C. pneumoniae may exhibit epidemiological cyclicity, warranting further investigation into its transmission patterns. Currently, the etiological diagnosis of C. pneumoniae relies on serological methods and nucleic acid detection. In this study, the positive rate for nucleic acid testing of throat swabs was 57.9%, while the seropositivity rate was 55.9%. Nucleic acid testing may yield false-negative results due to factors such as sampling technique, whereas serological testing is limited by the timing of antibody production and potential cross-reactivity with other pathogens [10, 11]. Therefore, a combined approach utilizing both serology and nucleic acid testing is recommended for the etiological diagnosis of C. pneumoniae in clinical practice [12].

The results showed that compared to children with MPP, those with CPP were older, had lower peak fever, shorter duration of fever, and lower levels of inflammatory markers. A Chinese study reported that children with CPP had a mean age of 10.9 years, a peak fever of 38.5 °C, and normal levels of CRP and PCT, which is consistent with our findings [6]. In contrast, a Mexican study showed a mean age of 4.7 years among children with CPP, with 32% requiring intensive care unit admission, indicating differences in affected populations and disease severity across regions [4]. Another study on MPP reported a mean age of 4.9 years, fever in 81.5% of cases, CRP of 11 mg/L, and PCT of 0.11 ng/ml, aligning with our results [13]. Huang Y et al. also demonstrated that lactate dehydrogenase levels were significantly higher in MPP compared to CPP [14]. M. pneumonia infection can activate the NF‑κB and TGF‑β pathways, leading to the release of pro‑inflammatory cytokines such as IL‑6, IL‑1β, and TNF‑α [15–17], thereby promoting the elevation of inflammatory markers including CRP. In our study, children with CPP exhibited higher eosinophil counts than those with MPP, which may suggest the possible involvement of eosinophilic inflammation. Accumulated studies have demonstrated that Chlamydia pneumoniae infection tends to induce a Th2-skewed immune response [18]. This pathway involves the secretion of IL‑5, which directly activates and recruits eosinophils, as well as the release of IL‑4 and IL‑13, which promote IgE production and further eosinophil activation. This Th2‑driven eosinophilic inflammatory mechanism shares notable similarities with the pathophysiology of asthma [19]. Previous studies indicate that C. pneumonia can promote Th2 immune responses and elevate IgE levels, triggering allergic reactions in asthma patients. C. pneumoniae‑specific IgE has been proposed as a potential clinical biomarker for chronic C. pneumonia infection in children with asthma [18, 20].Taken together, we speculate that a Th2-driven eosinophilic inflammatory process might be implicated in CPP pathogenesis. However, since we did not detect relevant immune indicators in this study, this interpretation remains speculative.

The lower rate of glucocorticoid use in patients with CPP in this study may reflect fewer immune dysregulation events compared to MPP. This finding aligns with our observation of lower inflammatory marker levels in the CPP cohort. Therefore, a combination of age, fever pattern, CRP, PCT, LDH, and eosinophil count may help differentiate between M. pneumoniae and C. pneumoniae pneumonia. While lobar pneumonia was more common in children with MPP, chest pain was more frequently reported in those with CPP, possibly due to the predominance of subpleural patchy high‑density opacities in C. pneumoniae infection, leading to more pronounced pleural traction and irritation [21].

Conventional understanding regards CPP as a mild respiratory infection, which aligns with its clinical presentation. However, in this study, imaging findings consistent with lobar pneumonia were observed in 61.4% of the affected children. Therefore, C. pneumoniae infection holds an important place in the differential diagnosis of CAP in older children, particularly when fever is not prominent, inflammatory markers remain unremarkable, and other etiological tests yield negative results. In this study, lobar pneumonia in C. pneumoniae infection was associated with diminished breath sounds, underscoring the significance of accurate pulmonary physical examination in assessing disease severity in these patients. When decreased breath sounds are detected, a high index of suspicion for progression to lobar pneumonia should be maintained, and timely chest imaging is warranted to avoid missed diagnoses. In the future, investigations may be pursued to identify biomarkers capable of predicting lobar pneumonia progression.

Limitations

First, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias and limits our ability to establish causality between the identified predictive factors and outcomes. Second, the single-center study may restrict the generalizability of the results to broader populations. Third, other potential biomarkers such as interleukins and D‑dimer were not included. Fourth, the lack of post‑discharge follow‑up data—including serial chest imaging, repeat laboratory testing during convalescence, and long‑term outcome assessment—constitutes a shortcoming of this study.

Recommendations for further research

First, multi-center studies can be conducted in the future to increase the sample size and enhance the persuasiveness of the conclusions. Second, the pathogenesis of the lobar manifestations of CPP can be further explored. Third, long-term follow-up observations of the prognosis for patients can be carried out.

Conclusions

Fever, chest pain, tachypnea, dyspnea, and levels of CRP, PCT, LDH, and eosinophil count provide important clues for distinguishing between C. pneumoniae and Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia. The presence of decreased breath sounds may serve as a clinical indicator for lobar pneumonia in children with C. pneumoniae infection. Clinicians can make better differential diagnoses of these two atypical pathogens based on these characteristics.
