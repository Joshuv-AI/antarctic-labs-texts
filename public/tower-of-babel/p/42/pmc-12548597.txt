Assessing the feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of a school-based digital intervention for enhancing emotion regulation in Chinese children (CHEER): protocol for a feasibility cluster randomised controlled trial
Authors: Zhou Xiong, Jing Li, Mengru Ji, Fang-biao Tao, Ying Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost mental health disorders are first experienced during childhood. There are evidence-based treatments, but it is difficult to reach all children in need. Schools have long been recognised as an ideal setting to provide mental health interventions and services for children. There is growing evidence of the effectiveness of school-based digital mental health interventions. Emotion regulation (ER) is central to psychopathology across childhood and adolescence. Cultivating ER can help build resilience and foster long-term well-being. We developed an app (‘CoaCH Effective Emotion Regulation’, CHEER) designed to promote ER, eventually aiming to reduce broader mental health disorders in children. Introduction

Mental disorders have become a serious public health problem, causing an enormous burden for children, adolescents and society in recent years.1 2 Adolescence is a critical developmental period for mental illness, with 50% of all mental disorders appearing before the age of 14 years.3 However, most children and adolescents with mental disorders do not access or receive formal help, and engaging them in prevention and early intervention programmes is challenging.4

In China, the prevalence of mental disorders among individuals aged 6 to 16 years has reached 17.5%.5 However, there is a shortage of child and adolescent psychiatrists (CAPs) in China, with fewer than 500 full-time CAPs nationwide.6 More than 30 million children and adolescents under 17 years have behavioural and emotional problems in China, of which over 50 per cent may need but are not receiving mental health services.7 Despite the considerable burden, mental health services access is further impeded by inconvenient appointment times, transportation challenges, stigma, long waiting lists and high thresholds for specialist referral.8

Schools have long been recognised as an ideal setting to provide mental health interventions and services for children because they typically spend a significant amount of their daily time at school.9 It has been demonstrated that school-based mental health interventions are necessary and effective.10 However, the current school-based mental health services are insufficient to meet the needs of children.11

Digital mental health interventions (DMHIs) have received increasing attention in recent years, as they minimise costs, time and personal resources in comparison with face-to-face interventions.12,14 Due to the rapid and consistent advancement of technology, children are increasingly using mobile devices, and digital interventions are readily acceptable to them.15 There is growing evidence of the effectiveness of DMHIs designed for children with specific symptoms or disorders in school settings.16,18 However, these DMHIs interventions follow a wait-to-act model, providing mental health support only after observing certain behaviours or students’ self-disclosures.19 Universal interventions are applied to all students regardless of their risk level, with the aim of promoting mental health and preventing the onset of mental health issues before they arise.10 It is more indispensable to shift the direction of DMHIs to the school-based mental health promotion and prevention of children.20

Emotion regulation (ER), which encompasses the process wherein an individual shapes the emotions they have, when they have them and how they experience or express them, is a foundational skill to target as it is critical for healthy development.21 22 According to Gross’s process model of ER, an individual recognises an ER goal, selects and implements specific ER strategies, and observes whether these were successful.23 ER strategies can be used to regulate both negative and positive emotions, as both may have adverse impacts on behaviour.24

Evidence suggests that ER is malleable, and this malleability is particularly pronounced from late childhood to adolescence.25 26 Recent evidence indicates that enhancing ER in children and adolescents is correlated with improvements in mental health.27 Equipping children with ER skills can help them navigate current challenges, build resilience in the face of adversity and establish a foundation for long-term mental well-being.28 A number of systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown that positive changes in ER are associated with significant decreases in symptoms related to anxiety, depression, substance abuse, eating disorders and borderline personality disorder.29 30 In addition, evidence suggests that ER is a crucial transdiagnostic mechanism underlying a wide range of mental health disorders and predicting subsequent levels of psychopathology.28

To our knowledge, there is currently no digital intervention in China that specifically targets ER in late childhood, despite this period being recognised as a critical stage for achieving maximum impact in terms of youth mental health prevention.31 Moreover, in China and many other countries, late childhood is characterised by the transition from primary to secondary school. The school transition period represents a critical developmental juncture for children, characterised by concurrent transformations in both objective educational environments and subjective physiological-psychological states. The transition constitutes a significant ecological shift, marked by multidimensional environmental novelty encompassing unfamiliar campus architecture, expanded class sizes and complete reorganisation of established social networks. This transition period demonstrates significant volatility in academic performance, emotional and school belonging. This transition is one of the most stressful events in Chinese children. Therefore, it is necessary to take measures to promote the ER ability among children before entering secondary schools. Furthermore, in health education or mental health education curricula in China, schools are encouraged to use digital interventions to promote students’ psychological well-being, indicating that the implementation of DMHIs in classroom settings holds considerable promise.

Objectives

There is a notable shortage of school-based DMHIs targeting ER in children.26 The development of an acceptable, user-friendly and absorbing digital platform for ER, which can be implemented successfully in the school setting for all students, would be highly necessary for supporting children’s mental health. We developed an ER promotion app (‘CoaCH Effective Emotion Regulation’, CHEER), eventually aiming to prevent mental health disorders in children. Based on the process model of ER,23 we developed an app (CHEER) designed to promote ER, eventually aiming to reduce broader mental health disorders in children. The aims of the proposed study are threefold (1) To examine the effectiveness of CHEER app intervention in enhancing emotional awareness and intentions for use of ER strategies, (2) To test the preliminary effects of CHEER app in reducing symptoms of anxiety and depression compared with a wait-list control group and (3) To investigate the feasibility of CHEER app in school settings, focusing on adherence to the CHEER app, its usability and potential to deliver a full trial in the future.

Methods and analysis

Trial design

The trial is a single-blinded, two-arm parallel assignment feasibility cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT) with treatment and wait-list control group. The RCT will be conducted among several independent, public primary schools in Anhui province of China in 2025. Schools will be randomly assigned to either the intervention group or a wait-list control group in order to minimise contamination between the intervention and control arms. The outcomes will be assessed at baseline, postintervention and 1-month follow-up in both study groups by assessors blinded to treatment allocation. Figure 1 provides an overview of the recruitment, randomisation and participation process for this trial. The trial has been registered in chictr.org.cn (Project Number: ChiCTR2400091728) and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Project Number: 82240119) in October 2024.

Participants

Participants are grades 4–5 students (ages 9 to 12 years) enrolled in the selected primary schools. Students will be included if they (1) Can complete questionnaires independently, (2) Have access to a tablet or mobile device with internet access and (3) Provide written informed consent by parents and children. Additionally, individuals engaged in other forms of mental health treatment or presenting with a group of psychiatric problems (eg, bipolar disorder, clinical anxiety or depression disorder) will be excluded. The exclusion criteria were established to minimise the risk of exacerbating severe mental health issues and to protect participants, while also accurately evaluating the safety, feasibility and effectiveness of the intervention.

Sample size

This study is planned with a total of 400 participants. The sample size was calculated using G*Power V.3.1.9.7 for a mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA) design with two groups and three measurement points.32 In this study, the estimated effect size for our primary outcomes (ER skills) is assumed to be small (F=0.1), with an α of 0.05, power of 0.90, and the correlation between measurements will be taken as 0.5. According to these parameters, a total sample size of 214 is required. Based on the development and early evaluation work of previous digital mental health studies, we anticipate a dropout rate of approximately 40%.33 We aim to recruit at least 400 participants.

Recruitment of schools

All schools will be recruited across three sites in Anhui province: (1) Tianchang, (2) Bengbu and (3) Wuhu. This study aims to enrol four schools, with each school enrolling two classes. Eligible schools across the three cities will be approached by publicly available contact details. School principals and mental health education staff will receive an invitation outlining the study aims and requesting permission to conduct the study. Schools will also receive follow-up communication from the research staff via phone calls and emails. Schools will be informed that the intervention is available to students in any year group but is considered by the research team to be well-suited to students in grades 4–5 (ages 9 to 12 years), as late childhood is characterised by the transition from primary to secondary school, which is one of the most stressful events in Chinese children. To ensure comparability between the intervention and control groups, this study will select schools of similar size and level. Recruitment will begin on 20 January 2025.

Informed consent

Paper-informed consent forms are provided for children to complete before completion of baseline questionnaires. Parents are provided with an opt-out consent process, with schools informing them about the study 2 weeks before the baseline school visit. Dual assent and consent will be required for participation. The template of the informed consent form can be found in the online supplemental material.

Patient and public involvement

Children were consulted at every stage of the design process to help guide the creation of the CHEER Initiative. User testing was conducted with students and teachers from one primary school to provide feedback about the uptake of the intervention, prior to its formal implementation in schools. Based on this feedback, changes were made to the CHEER intervention. Complete design specifications appear in the ‘Intervention development and design process’ section with accompanying design flow in figure 2. Additionally, the programme was reviewed by experts to make sure it mirrored the most recent scientific findings.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval for the study has been granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Project Number: 82240119). Each child and their parent/guardian must provide signed informed consent before study enrolment, with the right to withdraw at any time. The results of the study will be disseminated through publications in scientific articles and presentations at relevant conferences and/or public events.

Data collection, management and statistical methods

All raw data will be securely collected and archived at the School of Public Health, Anhui Medical University. Access is restricted to authorised personnel to ensure confidentiality and compliance with data protection standards. The statistical analysis plan will be jointly developed by the study statistician and principal investigator in accordance with the trial protocol.

Randomisation

The randomisation scheme will be generated using random allocation software. Following consent and baseline assessment procedures, schools will be randomised 1:1 to either the intervention or control arms. When evaluating outcomes at postintervention and follow-up, the assessors won’t know which participants were assigned to which groups.

Intervention and control condition

Intervention development and design process

CHEER is a digital app programme to support children with their ER, involving a set of multiple, interconnected and interacting components. It is designed to engage children by using developmentally appropriate language, illustrations, animations and interactive components. Our design and development process consisted of three stages, with each stage using a unique set of objectives, methodologies and stakeholders (figure 2).

The CHEER intervention was developed by a multidisciplinary team, each contributing to their area of expertise: child psychology professors (led evidence-based content design (eg, process model of ER techniques for children) and validity checks), child psychiatrists (ensured clinical safety and reviewed risk management protocols), primary school teachers (provided pedagogical context and usability feedback for classroom integration) and graduate students in child psychology (conducted pilot testing and data collection under supervision.)

Stage I: clarify the requirement

This stage focuses on understanding the current problem and clarifying the requirements of children. The research team identified initial ideas through evidence-based literature and workshops with key stakeholders and evaluated the feasibility of research activities for the subsequent stage. Additionally, the study team synthesise the existing evidence to develop a foundational logic model and framework for the intervention, highlighting its key components and anticipated outcomes.

Stage II: design and development

This stage emphasised the involvement of children and other key stakeholders, including teachers, researchers, the app developer and designers, in the design and modelling process. This involved identifying the key modules in the app and developing the content and design for each module, such as daily monitoring of emotional states, mindfulness exercises and ER exercises. We conducted 3 participatory workshops across two primary schools with 23 children and 11 teachers. Each workshop, each lasting 2 hours, was conducted by the lead author and accompanied by a professor of child psychology. Each workshop event included an icebreaker exercise, an introduction to the topic (eg, mental health, digital interventions, how can technology support children’s ER or mental health, and what kind of digital intervention do children like). Data collection comprised real-time observational notes during workshops and posthoc reflective memos authored by the lead researcher.

Stage III: feasibility trial

Once the digital APP is developed, the research team intends to carry out a feasibility cluster RCT. This trial evaluates the feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of a school-based digital ER intervention for the student population, enhancing their ER. Additionally, this study will identify relevant challenges preventing the implementation of the app within the school ecosystem. If the digital intervention and procedures prove feasible and acceptable, we plan to assess their efficacy in a definitive controlled trial.

Intervention description

This APP was designed to offer children a personalised and interactive approach to managing and understanding their emotions. It includes four main elements: (a) Daily monitoring of emotional states, (b) A self-assessment module, (c) A relaxation module and (d) A library of exercises placed in ER. The details of each of these app functionalities are provided below.

Delivery method

Participants randomised to the intervention condition receive a self-guided, 4-week CHEER app intervention and have complete access to the app. The intervention will be integrated into regular school hours, taking place in the classes and being compatible with the normal school curriculum. Each participant will be allowed to use the APP via a tablet two times per week in class for 40 min each session. The intervention will be administered by two lay counsellors who have undergraduate degrees in public health. In the first session, the counselor will play a video to all participants that outlines the app’s objectives and demonstrates its functionalities. Children are encouraged to explore every part of the app.

The intervention sessions followed a standardised procedure comprising these essential steps. Before sessions, counsellors prepared materials and recorded attendance to track participant compliance. Each intervention session commenced with a structured review of assigned homework, which primarily reinforced ER skills learned from the previous session. After problem-solving issues that occurred with the homework, children first engaged in tablet-based learning of the prescribed curriculum, followed by self-directed exploration of the app. The concluding segment of each session incorporated gamified activities and situational simulations to reinforce acquired knowledge and skills.

In order to enhance the engagement of the digital intervention, we informed participants that each class would include a question-and-answer session designed to review and reinforce the content covered in the CHEER program that day. Students who actively participated in answering questions would receive badges. On accumulating a certain number of badges, students could select from a range of small rewards, such as picture books, comics and stationery. Additionally, each session concluded with gamified activities and situational simulations related to the CHEER curriculum to further enhance engagement.

At the beginning of the trial period, all participants within schools allocated to the control group will be provided with a resource guide listing relevant resources that included: (1) Clear instructions stating ‘If you feel you need psychological support, please consider the following options’, (2) Specific local resources (eg, the school counselling centre with operating hours and location, designated psychiatric hospitals with contact information) and (3) Emergency contact numbers for immediate crisis. All participants in the control condition will be provided with access to the intervention once their participation in the trial has concluded (ie, 6months post-baseline).

Measures

Student-reported questionnaires would be used. The questionnaire surveys would be conducted in the participants’ classrooms at baseline, postintervention and 1-month follow-up (table 2). The head teachers of each class would distribute questionnaires and supervise students to complete them. After completion, the head teacher would collate the questionnaires, and any missing items would be requested to be refilled by the students before the final collection. The head teacher would submit all the collected questionnaires to the school management staff, who would then send them to the researchers via mail. The researchers would meticulously check each questionnaire and eliminate those containing obvious logical errors, a high number of consistent answers or too many missing details. Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study are the ER abilities and strategies of children.

How I feel—Questionnaire (HIFQ)

The HIFQ is a 10-item multidimensional self-report scale designed to evaluate children’s emotional arousal and regulation abilities. Items range from 1 (‘not at all true of me’) to 5 (‘very true of me’).35,37 The items evaluate the frequency, intensity and regulation of five distinct emotions: happiness, fear, anger, sadness and excitement. There are three subscales: (1) A positive emotion subscale with higher scores indicating that happiness and excitement are experienced with high frequency and intensity, (2) A negative emotion subscale, where high scores reflect that fear, anger and sadness are experienced with high frequency and intensity and (3) An ER subscale, where high scores show a powerful ability to regulate the frequency and intensity of either positive or negative emotions. The HIFQ has been identified to be a reliable and valid measure for both research and school interventions targeting students’ ER.

Chinese version of the emotion regulation questionnaire for children and adolescents (ERQ-CA-C)

The ERQ-CA-C is comprised of 30 items that assess the ER strategies of expressive suppression (4 items) and cognitive reappraisal (6 items).38 39 The items are rated on a 5-point scale, which ranges from 1 (‘strongly disagree’) to 5 (‘strongly agree’). Higher scores on each subscale indicate a greater level of implementation of the corresponding ER strategy. The ERQ-CA-C has been reported to demonstrate acceptable internal consistency.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes of this study are well-being, depressive symptoms and anxiety symptoms.

Satisfaction with life scale adapted for children (SWLS-C)

The SWLS-C is a 5-item self-report measure designed to assess life satisfaction and well-being of children and adolescents (ages 9 and above).40 All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (‘disagree a lot’) to 5 (‘agree a lot’) and have been reported to have good psychometric properties. Higher scores indicate higher levels of satisfaction with life and well-being.

Mood and feelings questionnaire (MFQ)

The MFQ consists of 33 items assessing the presence of depressive symptoms in children and adolescents.41 Each item has a severity index ranging from 0 (‘not true’) to 2 (‘true’). The scores range from 0–66, with higher scores indicating more severe levels of depression. The clinical cut-off is set at 26. The validity and reliability of the measure have been established in a wide range of clinical and non-clinical samples.

Screen for child anxiety-related emotional disorders (SCARED)

The SCARED is a 41-item multidimensional self-report scale designed to evaluate children’s anxiety disorders.42 The SCARED includes 5 factors: somatic/panic (13 items), generalised anxiety (9 items), separation anxiety (8 items), social phobia (7 items) and school phobia (4 items). Each item has a severity index ranging from 0 (‘not true or hardly ever true’) to 2 (‘true or often true’). This study adopts the SCARED-GAD subscale, where higher scores indicate higher levels of generalised anxiety.

Feasibility and acceptability

The APP feasibility and acceptability will be assessed by analysing children’s objective engagement conditions alongside their subjective experience with the APP.

System usability scale (SUS)

The SUS is composed of 10 statements that are designed to measure the usability of the apps.43 All responses are rated on a 5-point scale from 0 (‘strongly disagree’) to 4 (‘strongly agree’). The SUS scores are summarised and then multiplied by 2.5 to transform the SUS score into a 0 to 100 interval scale. APPs with scores of 70 and above are accepted as highly usable, and scores between 50 and 70 indicate acceptable usability. APPs with scores of 50 and below are subject to concerns about their usability by the target population and should be investigated further.

Mobile application rating scale (MARS)

The acceptability of the app is assessed with items from the quality rating section of the MARS.44 45 MARS consists of 23 items divided into 5 subscales to assess engagement (section A), functionality (section B), aesthetics (section C), information (section D) and subjective quality ratings (section E). To adapt the scale to children, we only include questions that we expect to be easy for them to answer and that we believe would give us a good indication of important aspects of the acceptability of the APP. Hence, we incorporate and modify 10 items from the original scale for our study. The children could also comment on the acceptability and quality of the APP in a supplementary open comment field at the end of the questionnaire.

Objective engagement conditions

Objective engagement conditions were derived from app usage analytics (ie, log data) and included metrics such as the duration of time children spent on the APP, the number of module accesses, exercise completion rate, average time spent per exercise, the number of exercises started but not completed, and engagement with the self-assessment module. To address potential measurement bias in app engagement, a dedicated tablet ‘children’s mode’ was implemented during classroom sessions. This restricted environment allowed access only to pre-installed apps, with the CHEER app being the sole app available in this mode. The system prevented background app switching and blocked access to other apps, thereby ensuring that recorded usage time accurately reflected active user interaction.

Subjective experience

We will use in-depth semi-structured interviews to explore implementation constructs around trial participants’ experiences of participating in the trial and their views of the CHEER app. This included assessing app acceptability (eg, What features did you use and what features did you not use?), appropriateness (eg, Do you remember what you first thought of the app when you first used it?), usability (eg, How did you find the app to use in general?), barriers to engagement and feedback about intervention content and app features (eg, What parts of the CHEER app do you not like/find unhelpful? Did you experience any problems while using the app? The qualitative research team will be led by a Professor of Adolescent Psychiatry and Consultant in Child and Adolescent Psychiatry. In order to collect as many opinions as possible, especially to ensure that underrepresented and minority opinions are included, we set the sample size at 20. The 20 participants for the post-trial interviews were selected through purposive sampling to ensure diversity in perspectives. We included students representing different engagement levels (high/medium/low based on classroom participation and app log data) and genders. Interviews will be conducted after the intervention is completed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will primarily be descriptive in nature, with the aim of providing estimates of key feasibility parameters and informing power calculations for a future definitive trial. A description of the sample will be presented by reporting means and SD for continuous data. Frequencies and proportions will be used for the analysis of categorical variables.

In order to assess the preliminary evidence of intervention efficacy, generalised linear mixed models (GLMM) will be conducted in SPSS using an intent-to-treat analysis approach. GLMM will be used to analyse the outcomes related to ER ability, anxiety, depression and well-being by comparing effect sizes between groups to evaluate the interaction effects between the intervention group and the waitlist control group, as well as the effects of time, group and their interaction between the preintervention and postintervention phases in the intervention and waitlist control groups. The study involves three time points: preintervention, postintervention and 1-month postintervention follow-up. The main effect of time will be examined to determine if any intervention effects observed at post-treatment are maintained during follow-up.

Semi-structured acceptability interviews will be recorded and transcribed, while excluding personal privacy data. Recorded files will be deleted after transcription. Two independent coders conducted a thematic content analysis, during which they identified, described and subsequently categorised themes. Acceptability will also be assessed by reporting descriptive statistics for the objective completion condition of the module.
