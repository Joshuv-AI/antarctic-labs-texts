Probing amikacin interaction with albumin: A combined multispectral and molecular docking investigation
Authors: Fatemeh Javaheri-Ghezeldizaj, Maryam Azimirad, Neda Ghafouri, Mohsen Zaheri, Reza Yekta, Masoud Moghadaszadeh, Maryam Zeinolabedini, Jafar Ezzati Nazhad Dolatabadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The fate of a drug administered to a living organism depends on the pharmacological and pharmacokinetic behavior of drugs. Biological macromolecules like serum albumins have a crucial role in the design and biological safety assessments of drugs. Amikacin as an example of the most used semi-synthetic aminoglycosides antibiotics, is included in the crucial drug list of the World Health Organization. This in vitro study aimed to explore Amikacin's binding specification with bovine serum albumin (BSA) through a computational and experimental approach. According to fluorescence spectra, it was declared that the innate fluorescence emission of serum albumin was quenched by the Amikacin via a static quenching mechanism. Meantime, the binding constants and thermodynamic parameters of Amikacin with BSA were calculated at various temperatures. Based on this calculation, negative ΔG°, ΔH°, and ΔS° values showed that spontaneous binding process occurred via hydrogen bond and van der Waals interaction. Additionally, based on UV–vis absorption, Amikacin leads to conformational modifications of BSA with the construction of a ground state complex, which may affect the physiological functions of this serum albumin. The results of FTIR spectroscopy showed that the binding of amikacin led in the change of BSA secondary structure and confirmed the possibility of changing the physiological functions of BSA. It is anticipated that this research will supply significant insight into the pharmacological properties of Amikacin and its related effects on human health in vivo.

Introduction

Antibiotics, the most current type of modern medication prescribed, are extensively used against infectious diseases in humans, animals, and livestock medicine [1]. Antibiotics have become a global concern that has increased significantly due to their use in humans, animals, food, and the environment [2,3].

Amikacin (AMK; Fig. 1), as one of the most successful semi-synthetic aminoglycosides, is a first-line drug to treat Gram-negative bacterial infections [4]. This water-soluble antibiotic with empirical formula C22H43N5O13 arose in 1977 and is still used to treat various kinds of infections, such as fatal nosocomial infection as well as strains of E. coli, Serratia, Proteus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [5]. AMK can be synthesized by modification of kanamycin A and is the most preferred aminoglycoside due to potent bactericidal effects, synergistic actions with beta-lactam antibiotics, low resistance, and low cost [6,7]. AMK induces changes in cell wall composition upon binding to bacteria, while decoding the A-site located in the 16S rRNA causes misreading of the t-RNA and inhibits the synthesis of proteins that are critical for bacterial growth [8,9].Fig. 1Chemical structure of AMK.Fig. 1

Chemical structure of AMK.

It is well-defined that the pharmacology and pharmacokinetics properties of a drug are impacted by plasma protein binding. The most important protein in this respect is serum albumin (SA) [10]. Serum albumin proteins, constitute the most plentiful serum transporter protein in the plasma which accounts for 60 % of all plasma proteins [11]. Albumin is responsible for transporting and disposition of numerous external and endogenous compounds, including drugs, amino acids, fatty acids, hormones, foods, etc. to specific tissues [[12], [13], [14]].

Human serum (HSA) and bovine serum albumins (BSA) as transporters in blood plasma are increasingly used as model proteins for drug-protein interaction investigations. SA has disulfur bridges and cysteine residues in its structure, hence, indicating eight and a half loops [15,16]. Needless to say that these loops in both proteins (BSA, HSA) are grouped in three homologous alpha-helices domains (Ι, ΙΙ, and ΙΙΙ). Each domain is composed of six-helix and four-helix sub-domains (A and B) [17]. The binding sites of serum albumins for drugs are located in these domains, within specialized hydrophobic cavities present in subdomains IIA or IIIA of the protein [17,18]. Since BSA's triangular structure is resembling HSA (roughly 76 %), BSA has been one of the most evaluated cases of serum albumins [19]. Crystal structure analyses specified that BSA, which contains 582 amino acids, has two tryptophan residues (134 and 214) and its molecular weight is around 66.3 kD. The Tryptophan residues are mostly in contact with the hydrophilic environment (domain I) and extremely buried in the hydrophobic loop (domain II), respectively [20]. During the past periods a wide number of research studies have been conducted on various drug-protein interactions [21,22]. It has been shown that drug-protein interaction in the bloodstream could be impacted on physiological fate, distribution, metabolism, and efficacy of drugs [23]. Also, this can affect the conformation and stability of the protein. Thus, the pharmacological study of binding phenomena between protein and drug molecules is imperative and fundamental [24,25]. AMK at dosage of 15–20 mg/kg once-daily regimen could possibly led in toxicity in severe renal dysfunction stages when associated with low ALB (low plasma concentrations of albumin) levels, and could enhance the risk of lack of efficacy in patients with hypoalbuminemia [11]. In addition, in another study it has been indicated that low serum albumin concentrations were due to the increased amikacin trough levels in plasma, which also was independent of age, sex, initial renal function, and dose of amikacin administered. It can be inferred that the concentration of serum albumin is the effective predictor of amikacin-induced nephrotoxicity [12].

It is noteworthy that this study is a pioneering research study since investigating the binding properties of AMK to BSA via fluorescence quenching procedure and UV–Vis absorption spectra has not been conducted till now. In the meantime, while the thermodynamic properties were discussed, recognition of the amino acid residues that participated in the binding of AMK to BSA was also performed by computational molecular modeling.

Material and methods

Materials

All starting reagents utilized were of spectroscopic or the highest-purity analytical grade and were utilized without any additional purification as well. BSA, AMK, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) applied in this study were supplied from Sigma Aldrich Co (Poole, UK). Stock solution (250 μM) of BSA was prepared by dissolving the protein in PBS solution and was maintained in the dark and low temperature (277 K). The experimental stock of AMK drug (2 mM) was dissolved in 2 mL of PBS solution.

Intrinsic fluorescence measurements

Tryptophan fluorescence analysis were done by a Jasco FP-750 spectrofluorimeter (Kyoto, Japan) with a 1.0 cm path length quartz. In these experiments, a 2.0 mL of BSA solution with a constant concentration of 250 μM was titrated by varying concentrations of AMK (10–60 μM). Various thermodynamic parameters were investigated at five temperatures (283, 289, 298, 303, and 310 K). Under 290 nm excitation, the emission wavelength for the BSA solution were carried out from 300 to 450 nm. The spectral resolution of both emission and excitation was fixed at 5 nm [26].

Ultraviolet–Visible absorption spectroscopy

BSA solution (2 mM) UV–Vis spectra in the lack and existence of several concentrations of AMK (10–70 μM) was recorded by PG Instrument Ltd spectrometer (Leicestershire, UK). The absorption spectrum of the BSA and BSA-AMK complex was taken in the region of 200–350 nm at room temperature (298 K).

FT-IR spectroscopic measurement

FT-IR spectroscopy of native BSA powder and dry powder of BSA-AMK complex was measured through a Bruker Tensor27 spectrometer (Ettlingen, Germany). 1:1 BSA-AMK solution was incubated for 30 min and then dried and powdered at room temperature. BSA and BSA-AMK complex were combined with KBr powder at a ratio of 1:100 and became a compressed tablet. The spectra were recorded with a resolution of 2 cm−1 and 100 scans per second in the range of 400–4000 cm−1. To investigate the structural changes after the interaction, the recorded spectra of native BSA and BSA-AMK complex were compared.

Docking studies

To understand the possible binding site of AMK onto BSA (bovine serum albumin, PDB code: 3V03, https://www.rcsb.org/), the molecular docking simulation was run with Autodock4.2 software. As the first step, the molecular 3D structure of AMK (PubChem CID: 37768) was downloaded from the PubChem website (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), and then adjusted with Gaussian 03 program. Then, Chain A of the BSA structure was selected as a target for docking study after applying polar hydrogens, Kollman, and Gasteiger charges through the AutoDock Tools-1.5.6 package. The blind docking study was performed to comprehensively search the binding domain of AMK on BSA. The grid box for blind docking was set as 126 × 126 × 126 Å with a spacing parameter of 0.598 Å, and all other settings were default. After finding the binding domain, a further molecule docking run was conducted with the selected domain to study the interaction more strictly. In this run, the grid box was determined as 70 × 70 × 70 Å with the spacing parameter of 0.375 Å. All docking simulation runs were accomplished with the Lamarckian genetic algorithm with a GA number of 200. The minimal binding score was selected as the best docking position between AMK and BSA. The data was analyzed and presented with UCSF Chimera 1.14 program.

Result and discussion

Steady-state fluorescence spectroscopy

In evaluating the BSA binding properties to the AMK antibiotic, the inherent fluorescence intensity of BSA allowed the researchers to assay the compound interaction with BSA in the existence and lack of AMK. Steady-state fluorescence spectroscopy provides some information about the interaction of ligands and proteins, such as accessibility and quenching mechanism [27]. Generally speaking, while the intrinsic fluorescence of BSA is owing to tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine residues, the main contributor residue to the steady-state fluorescence quenching of protein is attributed to tryptophan [28]. There are various mechanisms for quenching, which are categorized into static, dynamic, and hybrid [29]. Variables such as temperature, viscosity dependence, and lifetime measurements can affect the type of quenching mechanism [29]. The collisional process depends on diffusion. Higher temperatures lead to the contact of the fluorophore and the quencher during the ephemeral presence of the excited state and higher amounts of collisional quenching [30]. Therefore, the bimolecular quenching constants would be larger for dynamic quenching [20]. Howbeit, in static quenching, when the temperature rises, the basis state complex is formed between the fluorophore and the quencher and leads to a decline in the stability of the complexes, which is expected to be lower bimolecular quenching constants in static quenching [19]. In the study conducted by Thorve and Kishore, it was found that the binding interactions between BSA and gentamicin, an aminoglycoside drug, resulted in fluorescence quenching of BSA upon drug addition. Needless to say that strong binding can cause a long lifespan or strong dispersal with a decrease in the concentrations of free drugs in plasma, while weak binding has poor distribution. Frequently, the affinity of the drug to protein will become more powerful when the Kb value increases [33].

The thermodynamic analysis and type of binding forces

In the interaction of small molecules with biological macromolecules there are four main binding forces. These forces include hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, electrostatic bonding, and hydrophobic interaction in the bonding process. The magnitude and sign of the temperature-dependent thermodynamic parameters, provide the principal evidence to confirm the interaction forces. According to Ross and Subramania's theory, the sign of thermodynamic parameters can account for the principal forces concerned in the protein and ligand binding, as characterized below [36]:

(a) ΔH° <0 and ΔS° <0 are continually accepted as evidence for van der Waals and hydrogen bonding formation. (b) ΔH° > 0 and ΔS° > 0 propose the occurrence of the hydrophobic interaction. And, (c) ΔH° < 0 and ΔS° > 0 are associated with contribution of electrostatic bond formation. From Table 2, it can be seen that the values of ΔH° and ΔS° were −329.4 kJ mol−1 and -1054 J mol−1 K−1, respectively. Negative ΔH° and ΔS° values describe that the binding of AMK and BSA were especially based on van der Waals and hydrogen bonding formation in an aqueous solution. Eventually, the negative value of free energy (ΔG°), reinforces the spontaneity binding occurring between AMK and BSA in the temperature range studied [37,38].

In the case of the tobramycin drug, thermodynamic and docking studies showed that the interaction between HSA-tobramycin was governed by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions [32]. Also, from docking and literature study it has been confirmed that hydrophobic characteristic of gentamicin led in strong interaction with the hydrophobic side chain of the BSA [31].

UV–Vis spectrophotometry assessment

UV–Vis spectrophotometry is an important and effective experimental approach for probing protein structural change with various exogenous compounds. The UV–Vis spectra of proteins are generally split into two wavelength ranges, a strong peak (210 nm) which is due to the framework conformation of BSA, and other is a weak peak (280 nm) which reflects the existence of three critical aromatic amino acid residues [39]. As displayed in Fig. 6, upon addition of AMK (10–70 μM) into BSA solution, there is a slight decrease in absorbance intensity of BSA with a dose-response relationship. Accordingly, the AMK-BSA complex formation was also obvious from UV–Vis spectral data. Thus, the interaction of AMK with BSA might cause to structural modifications in proteins. FT-IR studies

Infrared spectrometry is a high-resolution technique for studying the interaction and structural changes of proteins. Intermolecular forces that are responsible for maintaining the secondary and tertiary structure of proteins may undergo changes in composition and ratio after binding with different molecules [40]. To obtain detailed information about the binding of AMK to BSA, FT-IR experiments were performed at room temperature.

Fig. 7 shows the FT-IR spectrum of BSA (a) and BSA-AMK complex (b). As seen in Fig. 7a in the FTIR spectrum of native BSA, there is a broad band in the spectral range of 3000–3600 cm−1 and a small band in the region of 2900–3000 cm−1. The wide band is related to O-H stretching along with symmetric and asymmetric N-H stretching, and the smaller band is attributed to C-H stretching in aliphatic compounds. Also, a number of amide bands that represent different vibrations of the peptide part are observed in the range of 1400–1700 cm−1: amide I band in the spectral range of 1650 cm−1, which is related to a strong carbonyl signal and mainly shows C-O stretching; Amide II band around the spectrum of 1550 cm−1 is related to C-N stretching along with N-H bending; Amide III band around the spectral region of 1450 cm−1 is corresponded to C-H bending [41,42]. Among the amide bands, amide I and II are related to the secondary structure of the protein, and amide I is more sensitive to structural changes than amide II [43].Fig. 7FT-IR spectra of native BSA (a), and the interacted AMK-BSA (b), at 298 K.Fig. 7

FT-IR spectra of native BSA (a), and the interacted AMK-BSA (b), at 298 K.

Fig. 7b shows the FT-IR spectrum of BSA after interaction with AMK. As can be seen in the figure, the peak intensity of the spectral region of 3000–3600 cm−1 has increased and its width has decreased. Also, its peak position has moved from 3440 to 3445 cm−1. The peak of the spectral region 2900-3000 cm−1 has also decreased and its position has changed from 2926 to 2920 cm−1.

In the spectral range of 1400–1700 cm−1, after the interaction, the position of the band peak of amide I has changed from 1653 to 1645 cm−1 and amide II has changed from 1540 to 1548 cm−1. The Amide III band has also disappeared. Other marked bands and peaks have undergone various increasing or decreasing changes in intensity and position. The noted changes in the shape and position of the peaks indicate that the secondary structure of BSA has changed after the interaction with AMK. Due to the changes in amide bands, AMK interacted with C-O and C-N groups in the BSA protein structural subunits, which led in the rearrangement of the polypeptide carbonyl hydrogen bonding pattern and ultimately altered the secondary structure of BSA [38,42].

Molecular docking results

BSA as a known binding target model in blood plasma has a similarity of more than 70 % with human serum albumin and contains 583 residues with three homologous domains I-III (subdomain A and B). The interaction of AMK with BSA was determined in this study by the Autodock4.2 program to analyze the binding domain, binding affinity, and type of interaction. The blinding molecular docking calculations displayed that AMK can firmly bind to the IIA subdomain (Sudlow's binding domain I) as an important binding domain in BSA (Fig. 8A and B). This domain is a well-known binding pocket that many drugs such as warfarin can favorably interact with it. Several studies have reported that the amino acids in this binding pocket reveal some flexibility for ligand binding, and also an ionization capacity to maintain the ligand. It is also shown that the trapped water molecules in the Sudlow I site are essential in ligand recognition and consequence binding. The further docking study (focused on Sudlow I site) showed that Glu100, Cys101, Ile202, Gln203, Gly206, Glu207, Lys242, and His246 play essential roles in the binding process of AMK onto BSA (Fig. 9). The binding score for this interaction was estimated to be −12.63 kJ mol-1, representing its high binding affinity onto Sudlow I site. Conclusion

In this examination, fluorescence and UV–Vis spectroscopy coupled with molecular docking techniques were employed to delineate the binding of AMK to BSA. The results of fluorescence spectroscopy revealed that quenching mode for BSA-Amikacin interaction was static. UV–Vis spectra of BSA presented slight changes implying the formation of the BSA-AMK complex. It could be concluded that the interaction mode in the formation of AMK-BSA complex was mostly hydrogen bonding and van der Waals. The negative value of free energy (ΔG°) implied the reaction is thermodynamically favorable and spontaneous. FTIR spectroscopy confirmed that the secondary structure of the BSA protein changes after interaction with AMK. Molecular docking results showed that AMK binds to the IIA subdomain through amino acids Glu100, Cys101, Ile202, Gln203, Gly206, Glu207, Lys242, and His246, which is a known binding site for drugs such as warfarin. The results of UV–vis, fluorescence and FT-IR spectroscopy showed that the binding of AMK to BSA induced conformational changes in BSA.

The conducted research is expected to give us useful information about the pharmacological and pharmacokinetic properties of AMK antibiotics through in vitro data. It is recommended to provide a comprehensive evaluation on the interaction between Amikacin with other serum proteins or biomolecules. Also, the physiological relevance of Amikacin-serum albumins interactions in a living organism remains uncertain due to the absence of such data. This limitation underscores the importance of further research aimed at elucidating the role of these interactions in the body's complex biological systems. Further research is required to determine how these in vitro findings translate to actual physiological conditions and their relevance to clinical scenarios.
