The integrin α2-osteoclast axis: a key driver of bone destruction and therapeutic target in osteosarcoma
Authors: Hongxiang Wei, Kai Shi, Daoxiang Huang, Huangfeng Lin, Shenglin Wang, Xuanwei Chen, Wenming Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteosarcoma is an aggressive bone malignancy with elusive core driving mechanisms. Dysregulated expression of integrin α2 (ITGA2) promotes malignant progression in multiple cancers, yet its role in osteosarcoma and correlation with bone destruction remain poorly understood. This study investigated the potential role of ITGA2 in osteosarcoma progression and therapeutic targeting.MethodsIntegrating bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation, we explored ITGA2 dysregulation and its clinical significance in osteosarcoma. Public databases were analyzed to confirm ITGA2 overexpression. CCK-8 assay, colony formation, Transwell migration/invasion assays, wound healing, and cell transcriptomics were employed to assess ITGA2’s impact on tumor proliferation, migration, and invasion. Xenograft mouse models evaluated ITGA2-driven tumor growth and osteolytic damage. WB analyzed ITGA2-mediated modulation of osteoclast differentiation-related molecules (RANKL, MMP9, OPN). ITGA2 knockdown and inhibitor (E7820) intervention were applied, with Western Blot (WB)/ Immunohistochemistry (IHC) validating ITGA2-MMP9 interactions.ResultsClinical cohorts and bioinformatics revealed ITGA2-specific overexpression in osteosarcoma tissues/cells (P < 0.0001), correlating with poor prognosis (HR = 3.392). Pathway analysis linked ITGA2 to bone remodeling and osteoclast differentiation. Functional assays showed that ITGA2 inhibition decreased cell proliferation and migration/invasion by 2–threefold (all P < 0.001), and reduced tumor volume and decreased osteoclast numbers (TRAP staining) in mouse models. Mechanistically, ITGA2 blockade downregulated MMP9 (pro-osteoclast) and upregulated OPN (pro-osteogenesis), indicating its role in exacerbating bone destruction via osteoclast differentiation cascades. ITGA2 emerged as an independent prognostic biomarker.ConclusionThis study uncovered that ITGA2 drives osteosarcoma progression and aggravates osteolysis via the “ITGA2-osteoclast axis”, with high expression predicting poor outcomes. Mechanistically, ITGA2 promoted tumor invasion and bone metabolism imbalance by regulating osteoclastogenic signaling, while its targeted inhibition synergistically suppresses tumor growth and restores bone homeostasis, highlighting ITGA2 as a pivotal therapeutic target for osteosarcoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-06906-4.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma is an aggressive primary bone malignancy that predominantly affects adolescents and is primarily managed through neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgical resection [1, 2]. Despite these interventions, the prognosis remains poor, with 5-year survival rates lingering below 70%, and dropping to less than 20% in cases of metastatic or recurrent [3, 4]. While advances in molecular-targeted therapies and immunotherapy have been achieved, persistent challenges continue to impede clinical success such as chemoresistance and tumor microenvironment-driven osteolytic destruction [5–7]. Mounting evidence has highlighted the critical role of pathological osteoclast hyperactivation in disease progression [8, 9]. Specifically, osteosarcoma cells secrete osteoclastogenic mediators, including RANKL and IL-6, to fuel osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption. This process releases tumor-promoting factors like TGF-β from the degraded bone matrix, establishing a self-perpetuating "tumor-osteoclast vicious cycle" that exacerbates tumor invasion and metastasis [10, 11]. Consequently, identifying novel therapeutic targets and molecular networks governing osteosarcoma-associated bone destruction may represent a dual strategy to disrupt tumor progression and improve patient outcomes.

ITGA2, a transmembrane collagen-binding α-subunit receptor [12], typically pairs with β1-integrin to form the α2β1 heterodimer, a key mediator of cell-extracellular matrix adhesion [13]. ITGA2 is notably overexpressed in several malignancies, including colorectal [14], gastric [15], prostate [16], and breast cancer [17], where it promotes tumor progression by modulating cancer cell migration, invasion, and metastatic dissemination [18, 19]. Its overexpression amplifies proliferative and invasive phenotypes through PD-L1 activation and induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathways [20, 21]. ITGA2 further contributes to tumor angiogenesis [22] and chemoresistance [23]. In glioblastoma, surface-specific ITGA2 overexpression enables antibody-based targeting to suppress tumor migration, demonstrating therapeutic potential [24]. Nonetheless, the role of ITGA2 in osteosarcoma-associated osteolysis and its mechanistic basis remains underexplored.

Osteosarcoma prognosis correlated strongly with the severity of osteolytic bone destruction [25, 26]. Pathological crosstalk between osteosarcoma cells and osteoclasts fueled tumor progression and metastatic bone resorption [25]. Excessive osteoclast activation perpetuated a feedforward loop, aggravating skeletal degradation and creating a permissive niche for tumor invasion and metastasis [25, 27]. Elucidating how osteosarcoma cells regulate osteoclastogenic signaling pathways is thus critical for dual-target therapies that concurrently mitigate bone destruction and inhibit tumor growth, ultimately improving patient outcomes [27, 28]. Targeting these mechanisms may unlock novel therapeutic paradigms to enhance survival rates and quality of life in osteosarcoma.

Our findings established that ITGA2 overexpression robustly correlated with enhanced osteosarcoma cell viability, migration, invasion, and adverse clinical prognosis. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that ITGA2 modulated the tumor microenvironment by orchestrating osteoclast differentiation. Mechanistically, we identified a functional interaction between ITGA2 and MMP9, demonstrating that ITGA2 drives osteoclastogenesis through MMP9 upregulation in tumor cells. Collectively, these results unveiled ITGA2 as a dual-functional therapeutic target, offering promise for concurrently curbing osteoclast-mediated bone destruction and amplifying antitumor efficacy.

Materials and methods

Data collection and ITGA2 expression analysis

The fresh osteosarcoma tissues and clinical information in this study was collected from patients at the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University from September 1, 2019 to September 1, 2024 as experimental group. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants and all procedures followed ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. The GEO (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) and TARGET (https://ocg.cancer.gov/programs/target) databases are both free public gene-expression data repositories containing RNA sequencing data and clinical data of osteosarcoma cases. As the test cohort, gene expression datasets GSE21257, GSE19276, GSE126209, and GSE42352 were obtained from GEO. In addition, clinical information and gene expression profiles of 85 osteosarcoma patients were acquired from TARGET database as validation dataset, referred to as TARGET-OS (http://xena.ucsc.edu/).

Functional enrichment analysis

The list of characteristic genes from cell clusters, or those most related with ITGA2, was uploaded to the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID, v6.8). The official gene symbol for the identifier was chosen, and Homo sapiens was selected as the species to obtain enrichment results of Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. The adjusted P-value (P < 0.05) was considered statistically significant and the top ten results of GO and KEGG in ascending order were visualized in this study.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

The gene list of osteoclast differentiation was obtained from the AmiGO 2 portal (http://amigo.geneontology.org/amigo). The functional enrichment scores of each osteosarcoma sample was calculated using R software’s GSVA package and the enrichment results were visualized using the pheatmap package in R.

Cell culture

The human osteosarcoma cell line 143B, U2OS, MG63, SAOS as well as the human osteoblast cell line hFOB, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and cultured in DMEM (Biological Industries, Israel) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone Thermo Scientific, USA) and 1% antibiotics. All OS cell lines were incubated at an atmosphere containing 5% CO2 and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified chamber (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). hFOB cells were cultured separately at 34 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator.

Transfection of cells

In order to knock down the ITGA2 gene in 143B cells, the plasmids containing each target shRNA sequence (shITGA2: 5ʹ- GCTAGTATTCCAACAGAAAGA -3ʹ) along with the corresponding negative control vectors, were designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotechnology (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., and transfected into 143B cells according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Forty-eight hours after transfection, stably transfected clones were screened using puromycin, and the resulting stable cell line was designated as 143B ITGA2-KD. The relative expression of ITGA2 measured by Quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Wstern blot (WB) analysis to confirm transfection efficiency.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined using Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, Co., Ltd, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Herein, 143B and U2OS cells were maintained overnight at a density of 2000 cells/ well in a 96-well plate, followed by treatment with various concentrations of ITGA2 inhibitor (E7820, TargetMol Co., Ltd., China, Cas. 289483–69-8) for 72 h. Subsequently, 10% CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured after 2 h of incubation.

Migration and invasion assay

For migration assay, the monolayer 143B and U2OS cells in 6-well plate were stratched in a straight line to create a wound. Detached cells were washed away with PBS, and incubate the cells in serum-free medium containing different concentrations of E7820 for 24 h before taking photographs of the scratch. For invasion assay, 143B cells were first treated with different concentrations of E7820 for 72 h, and then seeded (10000 cells/well) into the upper matrigel-coated (Corning Life Sciences, catalog no. 354483) chamber containing serum-free culture medium. Then, 600 µl of 10% foetal bovine serum medium was added to the lower chamber, and incubated for 8 h in a Transwell system. Finally, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal indigo at room temperature for 10 min. The invasion and migration ability of tumor cells were evaluated by Nikon inverted microscope.

Cell apoptosis detection

Cell apoptosis was assessed by flow cytometry. 143B and U2OS cells in log-phase were harvested with trypsin, washed twice in PBS, and resuspended at 1 × 106 cells/mL. Following the manufacturer's protocol, cells were dual-stained with Annexin V-FITC/PI (15 min, RT, dark). Apoptotic fractions (early + late) were discriminated by flow cytometry (BD FACSVerse™), with cell percentages calculated using FlowJo v10.8 software.

Immunofluorescence

Upon attaining 50% confluency, 143B cells underwent sequential processing: fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), permeabilization with 0.2% Triton X-100 (20 min), and blocking in 5% BSA (30 min, RT). Primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer were incubated overnight at 4 °C: Rabbit anti-ITGA2 (1:200, Immunoway #YT5588), Mouse anti-CD29/Integrin β1 (clone TS2/16, 1:200, Invitrogen #14–0299-82), and Phalloidin-AF488 (1:200, Immunoway #YS0016). After PBS washes, specimens were incubated with goat secondary antibodies (1:200, 1 h, RT, dark): Anti-Mouse IgG(H + L)-AbFluor 555 (Immunoway #RS3408) and Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L)-AbFluor 647 (Immunoway #RS3811). Nuclei were finally counterstained with DAPI (5 μg/mL, 10 min) prior to confocal imaging (Zeiss LSM 880, 40 × oil objective).

qRT-PCR and WB analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and cDNA was synthesized using the First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific). qRT-PCR was performed on an ABI 7500 Real-time PCR System, with reactions including SYBR Green and specific primers below (hITGA2-F: 5ʹ-TGTGGTGAGGACGGACTTTG-3ʹ; hITGA2-R: 5ʹ-AACCGGCAGGGAGAATGATG-3ʹ). GAPDH gene served as an internal control for data normalization. The relative mRNA expression levels of target genes were calculated by the comparative CT method.

Samples from whole-cell lysate were extracted by RIPA lysis buffer (Epizyme Biotech Co., Ltd.), and the protein content was quantified using the BCA method (Epizyme Biotech Co., Ltd.), then subjected to SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The PVDF membranes were then blocked with a blocking reagent (5% skimmed dried milk) for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies used were Rabbit Polyclonal Antibody ITGA2 (1:1000, Immunoway Technology Inc, China, No: YT5588), Rabbit Polyclonal antibody RANKL (1:1000, Immunoway Technology Inc, China, No: YT5404), Rabbit Polyclonal antibody MMP9 (1:1000, Immunoway Technology Inc, China, No: YT1892), Rabbit Monoclonal antibody Osteopontin (1:1000, Immunoway Technology Inc, China, No: YM8281), and Rabbit Monoclonal antibody β-actin (1:1000, Immunoway Technology Inc, China, No: YM8343). The membranes were incubated with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C on a shaker, followed by incubation with HRP Goat anti-rabbit antibodies (1:5000, Epizyme Biotech, China, no. LF102) for 1 h. The antibody-bound proteins were detected by a gel imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

IHC staining

Tissues were obtained from patients during surgical excision or from tumor-bearing nude mice, were paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into 4 μm serial sections. Following deparaffinization with xylene and rehydration with graded alcohols, the sections were repaired with sodium citrate antigen, blocked with goat serum and hydrogen peroxide, then incubated with primary antibodies ITGA2 Polyclonal antibody (1:400, Immunoway Technology Inc, China, No: YT5588); MMP9 Polyclonal antibody (1:500, Immunoway Technology Inc, China, No: YT1892); TRAP Monoclonal antibody (1:500, Immunoway Technology Inc, China, No: YM6106) and the second antibody IGg polymer (1:200, bioss, Beijing, China, No: bs-0295G) under high-pressure conditions, and finally stained with DAB. With the help of hematoxylin, performed blue reverse staining, followed by dehydration and fixation. In order to identify immunoreactions, two pathologists independently participated in the evaluation of tumor tissue specimens. Brown granules appearing in the nucleus or cytoplasm of tumor cells were identified as ITGA2 and MMP9 positive expression under light microscope. Cells with ≤ 50% positive staining in the field were considered weakly positive, while > 50% were considered as strongly positive.

Animal studies

In accordance with international standards, all animal care and experiments were approved by our animal protection and use committee (IACUC #FJMU 2025-Y-1082). Female BALB/c mice were purchased from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd. at 4 to 5 weeks of age, mean weight 16.7 g, and were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with five mice per cage in a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. 143B cells or 143B ITGA2-KD cells were washed, resuspended in PBS (1 × 10⁸ cells/mL), and injected into the tibia of anesthetized mice (buprenorphine, 2 mg/kg s.c.; 3% isoflurane inhalation). After disinfecting the left hind limb with 70% ethanol, the knee joint was exposed via flexion and lateral rotation. A 28-G ½-inch needle was used to penetrate the patellar ligament into the tibial intercondylar region. A Hamilton syringe then delivered 1 × 10⁶ cells or 20μL PBS (as sham group or control group) into the bone tract. Postoperatively, mice received analgesics and were monitored for weight/pain daily (3 days) then biweekly. Approximately 10 days after inoculation, E7820 (50 mg⁄kg) or PBS (as control group) were administered orally twice daily for 3 weeks. All experiments concluded by 4 weeks post-injection due to tumor burden (weight loss, morbidity).

X-Ray examination and micro-computed tomography (μCT) analysis

X-ray radiography of tibial osteosarcoma progression was conducted pre- and post-intervention via prone-position imaging using a dedicated small-animal X-ray system. Excised tibial specimens from each cohort were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, washed in PBS and ethanol preservation prior to μCT analysis. High-resolution μCT scanning (Skyscan 1172, Bruker, Kontich, Belgium) focused on tibial destruction as a key indicator of tumor-mediated bone destruction. Cross-group image reconstruction using standardized thresholds facilitated comparative 3D modeling of osseous architectures. Quantitative bone volume assessment targeted the tibial epiphysis (distal end of the tibial growth plate), objectively measuring tumor-induced skeletal erosion.

Statistical analysis

For the 62 cases of osteosarcoma collected from our hospital, Kaplan–Meier (KM) analysis was performed using survival and survminer package in R to determine whether ITGA2 expression accurately predicted PFS and OS time. In addition, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted on the clinical characteristics and ITGA2 expression of osteosarcoma patients. In the external validation cohort, the KM analysis was also used to analyze the survival prognosis of ITGA2 in osteosarcoma patients with high and low ITGA2 expression, and univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were constructed to determine whether ITGA2 was an independent prognostic factor. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to evaluate the prediction efficiency of ITGA2. Besides, a prognostic nomogram was performed using the R software’s rms package. All statistical analyses were two-sided, and p-values < 0.05 were considered significant.

Result

ITGA2 is abundantly expressed in osteosarcoma.

Despite well-documented ITGA2 overexpression and association with adverse prognosis in multiple malignancies, its role in osteosarcoma remains uncharacterized. We therefore established ITGA2 expression patterns in human osteosarcoma specimens. Three independent GEO datasets (GSE19276, GSE126209, and GSE42352) consistently confirmed ITGA2 upregulation of in osteosarcoma tumor tissues (Fig. 1A). IHC staining (Fig. 1B, C) demonstrated strong positive expression of ITGA2 in osteosarcoma tissues, in contrast to relatively weak staining in normal bone controls. WB analysis of ITGA2 protein levels across distinct cell lines (Fig. 1D) showed pronounced elevation in osteosarcoma cell lines (143B, U2OS, SAOS) compared to normal human osteoblasts (hFOB), with the highest expression observed in 143B and U2OS cells. In order to investigate the critical role of ITGA2 in osteosarcoma, we constructed ITGA2-knockdown models using 143B cells. qPCR results indicated that all three shITGA2 constructs (TSH159-V1) effectively silenced ITGA2 expression (Figure S1A). Fluorescence microscopy confirmed that the transfection efficiency of all three types of lentiviruses was above 83% (Figure S1B). Both qPCR and WB analyses validated the significant downregulation of ITGA2 by shITGA2 (Figures S1C, S1D), confirming successful model establishment. Integrin α₂β₁ heterodimer formation, resulting from ITGA2 complexation with the β₁ subunit (ITGB1), was specifically enriched on the osteosarcoma plasma membrane as evidenced by immunofluorescence co-localization (Figure S1E, arrowed). This membrane-centric distribution aligns with canonical collagen receptor topology. Intriguingly, ITGA2 knockdown concurrently reduced ITGB1 protein levels (Figure S1D)—indicating potential co-regulatory mechanisms.

ITGA2 serves as a potential prognostic biomarker for osteosarcoma

To evaluate the prognostic significance of ITGA2 expression in osteosarcoma, patients were categorized into high- and low-expression groups. Survival analysis based on ITGA2 expression (Fig. 2A–C) demonstrated significantly shorter progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in the high-ITGA2 expression group across datasets (GSE21257 and TARGET-OS) compared to the low-ITGA2 expression group (Log-rank test, p < 0.001), indicating a significant association between elevated ITGA2 and poor prognosis. Time-dependent ROC curves (Fig. 2D–F) demonstrated moderate predictive power of ITGA2 expression for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival, with the highest AUC observed at the 5-year mark, suggesting better performance in long-term prognostication. Multivariate Cox regression analysis (Fig. 2G–H) further supported ITGA2 as an independent risk factor for OS. Although the p-value marginally exceeded 0.05, a consistently elevated hazard ratio (HR > 1) suggested a strong trend toward poor survival in patients with high ITGA2 expression. ITGA2 is associated with osteoclast differentiation

To explore the biological functions associated with ITGA2, we analyzed identified significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between high and low ITGA2 expression groups in osteosarcoma. Volcano plots (Fig. 3A, 3D) revealed 152 upregulated and 462 downregulated genes in the TARGET-OS dataset, and 28 upregulated and 59 downregulated genes in GSE21257, in the high-ITGA2 expression group compared to the low-expression group. GO enrichment analysis (Fig. 3B, 3E) indicated that upregulated DEGs were enriched (blue sections) in skeletal development, bone remodeling, and osteoclast differentiation, indicating that ITGA2 may play a role in bone metabolism during osteosarcoma progression. Conversely, downregulated DEGs were predominantly enriched (green sections) in energy metabolism-related processes, including oxidative phosphorylation. KEGG pathway analysis (Fig. 3C, 3F) further confirmed enrichment of upregulated DEGs in PI3K-Akt, Rap1, and osteoclast differentiation pathways, suggesting that ITGA2 may regulate osteosarcoma progression through these signaling cascades. Downregulated DEGs showed enrichment (green sections) in pathways related to DNA/RNA replication, repair, and energy metabolism. Transcriptional profiling of osteosarcoma cells after ITGA2 inhibition

To elucidate the mechanistic cascade whereby ITGA2 governs osteosarcoma pathogenesis, we performed transcriptomic profiling of human 143B osteosarcoma cells following ITGA2 suppression via E7820, an integrin-specific aromatic sulfonamide that transcriptionally downregulates ITGA2 expression. Principal component analysis (PCA) (Figure S2A) showed a clear separation between E7820-treated and control groups, indicating that E7820 profoundly altered gene expression profiles. Bar chart analysis (Fig. 4A) confirmed a significant reduction in ITGA2 expression following E7820 treatment (p = 0.03). The volcano plot (Fig. 4B) displays 2092 up-regulated and 2333 down-regulated genes in the E7820-treated group compared to the control. Heatmap analysis (Figure S2B) showed that E7820 upregulated key pro-osteoclastogenic genes (e.g., TNFRSF11A, TNFRSF11B, MMP9) and downregulated anti-osteoclastogenic genes (e.g., TGFB1, WNT10B, GADD45B). The heatmap (Fig. 4C) shows differences in osteoclast—differentiation—related gene expression patterns among groups, with significant variations in the E7820—treated group compared to the control. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses (Fig. 4D, E) revealed significant enrichment of DEGs in pathways related to electron transport, ATP synthesis, carcinogenesis, and DNA repair, suggesting E7820 may exert its effects by modulating these key processes. Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 4F) compares the overlap of ITGA2-related genes in different datasets. Collectively, these results suggest that E7820 may inhibit osteosarcoma cell growth and invasion by regulating specific gene expression and related signaling pathways. E7820‑mediated inhibition of ITGA2 attenuates osteosarcoma cell proliferation and tumor growth

Given that elevated ITGA2 correlates with poor prognosis in osteosarcoma, we further investigated its biological roles in osteosarcoma pathogenesis. ITGA2 was inhibited using E7820 in 143B and U2OS cell lines. CCK-8 assay results (Fig. 5A) demonstrated that E7820 treatment significantly inhibited the viability of 143B and U2OS cells in a dose-dependent manner, with calculated IC50 values of 3.45 μM (143B) and 2.90 μM (U2OS). In addition, E7820 treatment significantly increased the percentage of cells undergoing apoptosis in both 143B and U2OS cell cultures (Fig. 5B, C). Colony formation assays (Fig. 5D) showed a progressive decline in colony numbers with increasing E7820 concentrations, indicating suppressed proliferative capacity. Transwell assays (Fig. 5E) revealed that ITGA2 inhibition markedly reduced the migratory and invasive capacities of both osteosarcoma cell lines, evidenced by decreased numbers of membrane-traversing cells. Scratch wound healing assays (Fig. 5F) further confirmed that E7820 significantly impaired cell migration, as reflected by reduced wound closure rates. Additionally, ITGA2 knockdown in 143B cells led to fewer colonies (Figure S3A), diminished migration (Figure S3B), and lower wound healing rates, corroborating the critical role of ITGA2 in sustaining malignant phenotypes. In vivo experiments (Fig. 5G, H) revealed that E7820-treated groups exhibited significantly smaller tumor volumes and weights compared to controls, indicating potent growth suppression in osteosarcoma xenografts. Similarly, the ITGA2-knockdown (ITGA2-KD) group (Figure S3C) demonstrated markedly reduced tumor growth relative to controls, underscoring the functional contribution of ITGA2 to tumor progression. Collectively, these in vitro and in vivo findings demonstrated the potential therapeutic value of E7820 in osteosarcoma.

The impact of ITGA2 inhibition or knockout on osteolytic lesions in osteosarcoma.

Radiographic analysis (Fig. 6A) demonstrated significantly reduced osteolytic bone destruction in both the ITGA2-KD and E7820-treated groups compared to controls Micro-CT and histological evaluations (H&E staining, TRAP staining) (Fig. 6B) further confirmed that inhibition or knockdown of ITGA2 effectively attenuated bone degradation and osteoclast activity. Osteosarcoma cells promote bone destruction primarily by activating osteoclasts and releasing osteolytic mediators. [29]. RANKL plays a pivotal role in osteoclast differentiation and activation, whereas MMP9 facilitates bone matrix degradation and exacerbates osteolysis [10, 30]. Conversely, OPN inhibits osteoclast function [31]. To explore the interplay between ITGA2 and these factors, we analyzed their expression in osteosarcoma cells. WB results (Fig. 6C, D) revealed that in E7820-treated 143B and U2OS cells, as well as ITGA2-KD 143B cells, RANKL levels exhibited a downward trend, MMP9 and MMP13 expression was markedly suppressed, and OPN expression was upregulated. These findings suggest that ITGA2 critically influences osteosarcoma-mediated osteolysis. Mechanistically, suppressing ITGA2 regulates osteoclast activity by modulating RANKL, MMP9, MMP13, and OPN expression, thereby mitigating bone destruction. After ITGA2 inhibition or knockout, the expression of MMP9 in the supernatant of 143B and U2OS cell cultures also decreased, as quantified by ELISA (Fig. 6E). Validate ITGA2 expression/prognostic value and investigate ITGA2-MMP9 correlation/interaction.

To evaluate the prognostic value of ITGA2 in osteosarcoma, we performed IHC staining to assess its protein expression in 62 osteosarcoma patient tissues. Our findings revealed that ITGA2 was predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of osteosarcoma cells (Fig. 1B). Among the 62 osteosarcoma tissue samples, high ITGA2 expression was observed in 30 cases, while low expression was detected in 32 cases (Table 1, Fig. 7A). Subsequent analysis showed a strong correlation between high ITGA2 expression and tumor metastasis (Table 1). Univariate Cox regression analysis (Table 2) identified ITGA2 (HR = 3.975, p = 0.001), recurrence (HR = 2.619, p = 0.012), and metastasis (HR = 10.849, p < 0.001) as significant prognostic factors for OS. Multivariate Cox regression (Table 2) further confirmed that ITGA2 (HR = 3.392, p = 0.012) and metastasis (HR = 5.741, p = 0.007) were independent risk factors for overall survival. Kaplan–Meier survival curves (Fig. 7B, C) indicated that high ITGA2 expression correlated with significantly shorter OS and PFS (p < 0.0001), suggesting ITGA2 as a robust marker of poor prognosis. IHC analysis (Fig. 7D) showed that in the ITGA2 high expression group, the co-expression of ITGA2 and MMP9 was enhanced. Correlation analysis (Fig. 7E) showed a strong correlation between their expression (p < 0.001, R = 0.86), indicating a potential synergistic effect in the prognosis of osteosarcoma. To define the ITGA2-MMP9 binding interface, structural models were generated through molecular docking (Fig. 7F, G). Interaction analysis was performed using PDBePISA (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/msd-srv/prot_int/pistart.html) to evaluate protein binding energy. The binding energy between ITGA2 and MMP9 was determined to be − 9.7 kcal/mol. Based on our findings, we hypothesize that ITGA2 may promote osteolytic destruction by upregulating MMP9, thereby enhancing osteoclast maturation and differentiation. Consequently, dual-targeting of ITGA2 and MMP9 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for osteosarcoma. Conclusion

Elevated ITGA2 expression is significantly associated with poor prognosis in osteosarcoma patients, underscoring its value as a potential prognostic biomarker. Our findings indicated that ITGA2 played a key regulatory role in osteoclast differentiation, and that its inhibition significantly suppressed osteosarcoma cell proliferation, migration, and tumor volume. Mechanistically, ITGA2 might exacerbate osteolytic destruction by upregulating MMP9 expression, thereby facilitating osteoclast maturation and differentiation. Collectively, these findings identified ITGA2 as a critical driver of osteosarcoma progression and bone resorption via the “ITGA2-osteoclast axis”, highlighting the therapeutic promise of targeting ITGA2 for novel treatment strategies.

Discussion

Osteosarcoma, the most common primary malignant bone tumor [1, 2], remains clinically challenging, with lung metastasis and osteolytic destruction reducing the 5-year survival rate to below 20% [3, 4], despite improved outcomes in localized cases due to neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Notably, osteolytic lesions in osteosarcoma were a critical pathological hallmark [32], contributing to severe pain, pathological fractures, and poor prognosis. Although integrins such as ITGAV and ITGB3 have been implicated in tumor-induced bone homeostasis disruption [32], the precise contribution of ITGA2 to osteosarcoma-associated osteolysis remains poorly defined. This study identified ITGA2 as markedly overexpressed in osteosarcoma, with its levels inversely correlating with osteolytic severity and patient survival, suggesting ITGA2 as a potential driver of bone destruction. This aligns with previous reports linking ITGA2 to poor prognosis in breast and pancreatic cancers via stromal invasion [33, 34], yet our findings uniquely revealed its bone-specific mechanism through regulation of the “osteoclast axis”. Strikingly, targeted ITGA2 inhibition suppressed tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, while in an orthotopic tibial osteosarcoma model, it not only impeded tumor growth but also markedly reduced osteolytic lesions. This dual effect might stem from ITGA2’s paracrine regulation of osteoclast precursors within the tumor microenvironment, likely mediated through upregulation of the MMP9/RANKL pathway [35], highlighting its dual role: directly fueling tumor aggressiveness and indirectly disrupting bone homeostasis via the osteoclast axis.

This study provided the first evidence that ITGA2 drives osteoclast maturation and differentiation by upregulating MMP9 expression, consequently exacerbating osteolytic destruction. Mechanistically, ITGA2 may promote MMP9 transcription through activation of downstream autophagy signaling pathways (although this has not yet been experimentally validated) [36], while MMP9, as a key matrix metalloproteinase, degraded the bone matrix and released osteoclastogenic factors such as TGF-β, establishing a self-amplifying vicious cycle [37]. Furthermore, RNA-seq analysis of public datasets revealed elevated RANKL expression in ITGA2-high osteosarcoma cells, corroborating its role in osteoclast activation [38]. These findings provide experimental support for the proposed “ITGA2-osteoclast axis” concept and suggested that targeting ITGA2 could simultaneously suppress tumor progression and bone loss, offering dual therapeutic potential. Studies revealed that silencing ITGA2 in periodontal ligament stem cells inhibited the ITGA2/FAK/AKT signaling axis [39]. In oral cancer stem cells, ITGA2 activated stemness-associated pathways including PI3K-AKT, MAPK, and Hippo [40]. Furthermore, suppression of ITGA2 inhibited cervical tumorigenesis and metastasis by targeting the AKT/mTOR pathway [41]. Collectively, ITGA2 emerges as a critical regulator across cell types, driving stemness and tumor progression through an AKT-centered signaling network, highlighting its therapeutic targeting potential. Recognizing the significance of this signaling cascade, we believed that the crosstalk between ITGA2 targeted therapy and key signaling nodes such as FAK/AKT deserves further investigation. Additionally, our results demonstrated significantly higher ITGA2 expression in osteosarcoma tissues compared to normal bone tissues, with its overexpression in osteosarcoma patients showing no association with gender, age, tumor size, or location. We confirmed that a high percentage of ITGA2-positive tumor cells in osteosarcoma serves as a critical independent predictor of poor prognosis, underscoring ITGA2 overexpression as a promising prognostic marker for osteosarcoma progression and metastasis.

While this study achieved significant breakthroughs, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the relatively small clinical cohort (n = 62) may constrain the statistical power of prognostic analyses; expanding clinical validation cohorts could consolidate ITGA2’s prognostic value. Second, despite E7820’s potent antitumor efficacy, the potential toxicity of long-term administration—such as hematopoietic or hepatorenal impacts—has yet to be assessed. Additionally, the current orthotopic tibial osteosarcoma model inadequately replicated the intricate immune-osteoclast interaction network within human bone microenvironments, necessitating conditional knockout models to clarify ITGA2’s role in tumor-specific osteolysis. Further mechanistic studies are also required to clarify how ITGA2 regulates of MMP9 expression and influences osteoclast differentiation, strengthening the theoretical foundation for clinical translation. Lastly, evaluating E7820’s synergism with other chemotherapeutics and its systemic toxicity profile is critical for developing safer therapeutic regimens.

Building on these findings, we proposed two translational strategies: first, combinatorial therapy pairing ITGA2 inhibitors (e.g., E7820) with anti-osteolytic agents (e.g., denosumab or bisphosphonates) may synergistically disrupt the “tumor-osteoclast axis” and enhance therapeutic efficacy [42, 43]; second, developing nanodrug delivery systems targeting the ITGA2-MMP9 axis could improve bone tumor specificity while minimizing off-target toxicity. Collectively, the discovery of the ITGA2-osteoclast axis provided novel mechanistic insights and a framework for precision medicine in osteosarcoma, underscoring its untapped clinical potential.
