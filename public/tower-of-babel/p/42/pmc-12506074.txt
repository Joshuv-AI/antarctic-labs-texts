Candidiasis knowledge, Self-Reported infection history, preventive practices, and associated factors among female tertiary students in Ghana
Authors: Elsie Fafa King, Petra Osei Amankwah, Emmanuel Abu Bonsra, Awolu Adam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCandidiasis, a prevalent fungal infection caused by Candida species, poses significant health challenges, particularly for females who are more vulnerable, in environments like tertiary institutions where students are exposed to various risk factors. This study aimed to assess the knowledge, infection history, and prevention practices related to candidiasis among female tertiary students in Ghana.MethodsA cross-sectional study involving 788 female tertiary students from Kumasi Metropolis and Hohoe Municipality, Ghana, was conducted using multi-stage sampling. Data on socio-demographics, knowledge of candidiasis, infection history, and preventive practices were collected through structured questionnaires and analysed with Stata 17.0. Bivariate and Binary logistic regression analyses were employed to identify factors linked to knowledge and history of candidiasis.ResultsOf the 788 students, 52.2% demonstrated a high level of knowledge about candidiasis, while 42.4% reported poor preventive practices. Additionally, 51.4% indicated a history of candidiasis, with 31.1% experiencing recurrent infections. A significant association was found between knowledge level and prevention practices (p < 0.001). Multivariable logistic regression shows that students in Kumasi were 10 times more likely to have high knowledge of candidiasis compared to those in Hohoe (AOR: 10.2, 95% CI: 6.87–15.4, p < 0.001). Binary logistic regression shows that students in Kumasi had 4.99 times higher odds of having a history of candidiasis compared to those in Hohoe (AOR: 4.99, 95% CI: 3.42–7.28, p < 0.001). Those with unemployed parents had 49.6% lower odds of past infection compared to those whose parents were artisans (AOR: 0.504, 95% CI: 0.26–0.95, p = 0.037).ConclusionThis study highlights a significant gap in the knowledge and prevention practices related to candidiasis among female tertiary students in Ghana. Despite awareness levels, over half of the participants had inadequate knowledge of preventive measures, leading to high rates of recurrent infections. Ghana Health Service and Ministry of Education should focus on enhancing knowledge, promoting good hygiene practices, and improving access to healthcare services to reduce the burden of candidiasis in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04042-1.

Introduction

Background

Candidiasis is a fungal infection caused by the overgrowth of Candida species, particularly Candida albicans, a yeast that naturally inhabits various parts of the human body, including the mouth, throat, gut, skin, and vagina without causing harm under normal conditions [1]. When Candida grows uncontrollably or penetrates deeper tissues, it can lead to infections such as oral thrush, vaginal candidiasis, and systemic infections like candidemia [2, 3]. Candida species are unicellular fungi with a diverse morphology, including pseudohyphae and true hyphae forms [3]. These fungi exhibit virulence factors like dimorphism, enzyme secretion, biofilm formation (surface-attached microbial communities), and phenotypic switching (reversible changes aiding adaptation and immune evasion) [4]. Although more than 200 Candida species exist, only a few are commonly implicated in human infections, with C. albicans accounting for the majority [3]. Vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC) is a frequent manifestation among women [5] and can present with symptoms such as itching, burning, abnormal vaginal discharge, and pain during intercourse, although some cases remain asymptomatic [6].

Globally, approximately 75% of women experience VVC at least once, with 40–50% encountering recurrent episodes [5, 7, 8]. Candida species also account for up to 15% of nosocomial bloodstream infections, with candidemia being one of the leading causes of systemic fungal infections and hospitalizations [9–11]. Notably, C. albicans is responsible for about 85% of VVC cases, whereas C. glabrata, C. krusei, and C. tropicalis are less commonly involved [12, 13]. The condition is particularly concerning among female tertiary students due to several contributing risk factors, such as hormonal fluctuations, antibiotic use, weakened immunity (when present), poor hygiene, dietary habits, and psychosocial stress, which can collectively increase susceptibility to candidiasis [5, 7]. Environmental factors such as hot climates and certain behavioral practices like douching, the use of perfumed soaps, and herbal vaginal preparations further contribute to increased susceptibility [14].

In sub-Saharan Africa, Candida infections remain a significant public health concern, with prevalence rates ranging from 1% to 35% depending on demographic, environmental, and clinical factors [15, 16]. Cultural practices such as vaginal douching and the use of herbal or perfumed feminine hygiene products have also been shown to alter the vaginal pH and microbiota, creating favorable conditions for Candida proliferation [17]. Nutritional habits often characterized by high sugar intake and poor dietary balance can also promote Candida overgrowth, as the yeast thrives in high-glucose environments [18]. Moreover, behavioral and psychosocial factors, such as multiple sexual partners, unprotected sex, and high levels of academic or social stress, may impair immune function and contribute to recurrent infections [19].

In West Africa, including Ghana, Candida species have been isolated in up to 21% of clinical specimens, indicating a considerable disease burden [20]. Various factors contribute to this high prevalence [19, 20]. The warm and humid climate of Ghana creates an environment conducive to yeast overgrowth, particularly in areas of the body prone to moisture and limited ventilation. Socio-behavioural and economic factors also play a key role, especially among tertiary students who are often navigating new environments, dietary changes, and increased exposure to stressors [2]. Among female tertiary students in Ghana, specific risk factors have been identified that elevate susceptibility to candidiasis. These include hormonal fluctuations due to menstruation and the use of high-estrogen oral contraceptives, frequent antibiotic use that disrupts normal vaginal flora, and weakened immune responses due to stress or underlying health conditions [18]. Poor personal hygiene, irregular bathing, and the wearing of tight, synthetic clothing are additional contributors to local irritation and fungal overgrowth [7]. Despite these known risk factors, awareness and knowledge about candidiasis among tertiary students remain limited. Misconceptions about causes, symptoms, and treatments often lead to delayed diagnosis, self-medication, and inappropriate use of antifungals, which can worsen outcomes and increase the risk of recurrence or resistance. Preventive practices such as maintaining personal hygiene, avoiding indiscriminate use of antibiotics, wearing breathable clothing, and seeking initial treatment are not consistently practiced among young women in Ghana.

The burden of candidiasis on women’s reproductive health, academic performance, and daily quality of life highlights its public health importance. Despite its prevalence, evidence suggests that young women in tertiary institutions often have limited awareness and may engage in practices that increase their risk of infection. Identifying gaps in knowledge, infection history, and preventive practices and understanding the factors associated with them is therefore critical. This study provides context-specific evidence that can inform campus-based health promotion initiatives, integration of reproductive health education into curricula, and targeted counselling services for female students. The findings of this study have the potential to guide interventions that will not only improve knowledge and preventive practices but also contribute to reducing the burden of candidiasis among young women in Ghana. Health-seeking behaviour is further influenced by stigma, lack of access to accurate sexual health education, and the perception that candidiasis is a minor or normal condition. Therefore, this study sought to assess candidiasis knowledge, infection history, preventive practices, and associated factors among female tertiary students in Ghana. To guide this investigation, the following research questions were formulated:

Methodology

Study design

The researchers employed a cross-sectional research design to investigate candidiasis knowledge, infection history, and prevention practices among tertiary students in Ghana.

Study period

Data collection was carried out over a four-month period, from February 2024 to June 2024.

Study site description

The study was conducted in two major academic centres in Ghana: Hohoe Municipality in the Volta Region and Kumasi Metropolis in the Ashanti Region. Hohoe Municipality has a population of approximately 114,472 and hosts the University of Health and Allied Sciences (UHAS), St. Francis College of Education, St. Theresa’s College of Education, and the Nursing and Midwifery Training College, Hohoe (NMTC). These institutions are state-owned and collectively accommodate a student population of over 10,000 learners across various health, education, and allied disciplines. The student body is demographically diverse, typically ranging between 18 and 35 years, with a mix of male and female students, and representing different ethnic and religious groups across Ghana. UHAS, in particular, is a public health-focused university, offering health related programmes. NMTC and the Colleges of Education largely train nursing and teacher education professionals. Sanitation facilities are available in the form of hostels with shared bathrooms, toilets, and water points, but occasional water shortages and poor hygiene practices among students have been reported.

Kumasi Metropolis is a highly urbanized and cosmopolitan setting, with a population exceeding 3 million, and it hosts major tertiary institutions such as Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST), Kumasi Technical University, and the Akenten Appiah-Menka University of Skills Training and Entrepreneurial Development (AAMUSTED). These are all public institutions, with KNUST alone accommodating over 80,000 students across multiple faculties. The student population in Kumasi is highly diverse, representing all 16 regions of Ghana, with age ranges typically between 17 and 35 years, a nearly balanced gender ratio, and varied religious affiliations (Christian, Muslim, and traditional). KNUST and Kumasi Technical University have well-established campus hospitals and clinics, providing general medical and reproductive health services, including testing and treatment for fungal infections, STIs, and health education programmes. Student exposure to SRH information is relatively higher in Kumasi due to frequent collaborations with NGOs, peer health educators, and health science faculties. Both sites were selected because they represent contrasting urban (Kumasi) and semi-urban (Hohoe) environments, with differences in institutional size, health service availability, sanitation facilities, and student sociodemographic, allowing for a comparative understanding of candidiasis knowledge, infection history, and prevention practices among tertiary students.

Study population

The target population was female students enrolled in selected public tertiary institutions located in Hohoe Municipality and Kumasi Metropolis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Female tertiary students at the Kumasi and Hohoe Municipalities available at the time of the study were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

This study excluded female students not enrolled at the specified universities selected in the study. Females who were not there at the time of administering the questionnaire were excluded from this study, and students who were not willing to participate.

Sampling strategy

A multi-stage sampling technique was employed to select participants for this school-based cross-sectional study, targeting female tertiary students in the Ashanti Region (Kumasi Metropolis) and the Volta Region (Hohoe Municipality) of Ghana. A list of all accredited public universities in the two selected regions was compiled. Using simple random sampling, three universities were selected from the Kumasi Metropolis (Ashanti Region) and one university from the Hohoe Municipality (Volta Region). This ensured adequate geographic and institutional representation. In the Hohoe Municipality, the University of Health and Allied Sciences (School of Public Health) was selected. In the Kumasi Metropolis, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (Departments of Biological Sciences, Nursing, and Midwifery), Kumasi Technical University (Departments of Applied Sciences and Health Sciences), and Akenten Appiah-Menka University of Skills Training and Entrepreneurial Development (Faculty of Applied Sciences and Mathematics Education) were included. Departments were chosen through simple random sampling without restriction to any field, thereby ensuring diversity in academic backgrounds and enhancing the generalizability of findings.

From each selected department, a list of female students was obtained and used as a sampling frame where available. Systematic random sampling was then applied to recruit participants. To implement this, the total number of female students in each department (N) was divided by the required sample size (n) to determine the sampling interval (k = N/n). A random starting point between 1 and k was chosen using a lottery method, after which every kth student on the list was selected until the required sample size was reached. In departments where no complete list of students was available, a simple lottery method was employed: slips marked “Yes” and “No” were distributed randomly among female students, and those who selected “Yes” were enrolled in the study. All slips were thoroughly mixed to ensure fairness and randomness in participant selection.

Data collection methods and instruments

Study instrument

The primary data collection tool used in this study was a structured questionnaire designed by the principal investigator (PI). The development of the instrument was guided by a comprehensive review of existing literature and standardized tools related to fungal infections, with a specific focus on candidiasis knowledge, infection history, and preventive behaviors among women. The questionnaire was constructed to align closely with the study objectives and consisted of four sections: Section A: Captured socio-demographic characteristics such as age, level of study, program of study, marital status, and residential status. Section B: Assessed participants’ knowledge about candidiasis, including its causes, symptoms, risk factors, and modes of transmission. Section C: Explored respondents’ personal or observed history of candidiasis infection, including frequency, diagnosis, and treatment. Section D: Focused on current preventive practices employed by respondents to avoid candidiasis, such as hygiene practices, diet, and use of antibiotics or antifungals. The questionnaire included both closed-ended questions (e.g., yes/no, multiple choice) to gather both factual data and subjective perceptions. The instrument was originally drafted in English, as all participants were tertiary students proficient in the language.

Data collection methods

Data collection was conducted face-to-face using a structured questionnaire administered in both self-administered and interviewer-administered formats. The questionnaire was deployed electronically via Kobo Collect on tablets to enhance data accuracy and efficiency. The process was led by the principal investigator and supported by six trained field assistants with prior experience in institutional surveys. To ensure consistency and minimize bias, the research team received a three-day training covering ethical data collection, study objectives, interviewing techniques, and handling sensitive questions. For students able to read and complete the tool independently, the questionnaire was self-administered on the tablet in a private setting to maintain confidentiality. Participants who required support were assisted by trained field assistants under the supervision of the PI. All participants were first briefed on the study objectives, and informed consent was obtained prior to participation. The study was conducted between February 2024 and June 2024.

Pretesting and validation of the instrument

The data collection instrument (a structured questionnaire) was developed by the principal investigator (PI) based on an extensive review of relevant literature and existing tools on candidiasis knowledge, infection history, and prevention practices [22]. To ensure content validity, the draft questionnaire was reviewed by three public health experts and one microbiologist for clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness.

The instrument was then pretested among 30 female tertiary students from a public university in the Volta Region who were not part of the main study. This pretest helped assess the clarity of questions, the appropriate response options, and the average time required for completion [22, 23]. Feedback from participants was used to refine ambiguous items and restructure the flow of sections for better coherence. Reliability testing was conducted using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded a Value of 0.791, indicating high internal consistency. The refined instrument was then finalized for data collection.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study received ethical approval from the University of Health and Allied Sciences Research Ethics Committee (UHAS-REC) under reference number UHAS-REC A.4 [040]23–24. Before initiating data collection, formal permission was sought from each university. Consent was documented via signatures or thumbprints, with verbal consent encouraged to address any uncertainties. Participants’ confidentiality was protected using pseudonyms, and all data were securely stored in an encrypted, password-protected database, accessible only to the Principal Investigators and the research supervisor. This approach adhered to ethical standards, ensuring participant protection and data integrity throughout the research process.

There were no identified risks associated with participation in this study.

While there were no direct benefits to participants, the findings were expected to influence policies to the benefit of individuals at each university. To maintain participant confidentiality, names and details of participants were not linked to the data analysis and findings to ensure anonymity. Furthermore, data management, storage, analysis, and reporting were conducted using codes to avoid exposing the details of the participants, thus ensuring anonymity. Confidentiality was strictly maintained, and information about participants was not disclosed to anyone.

Knowledge variables

The overall knowledge level of candidiasis was determined using a composite score, based on responses to questions such as “Candidiasis is a. (Bacterial infection, Fungal infection, not sure, Viral infection),” “Candidiasis can be transmitted through sexual intercourse (Yes/No),” and “Maintaining good hygiene can prevent candidiasis (Yes/No).” Participants also answered questions about preventive measures, such as “Adherence to hand hygiene,” “Antifungal medication,” and “Avoiding douching” (Yes/No). The overall knowledge score was calculated by summing correct responses for each participant. Participants were classified as having ‘low’ knowledge if their score was below the mean, and ‘high’ knowledge if their score was equal to or above the mean. This approach provides a simple and interpretable measure of relative knowledge among students [24, 25].

Self-Reported infection history

The past history of candidiasis infection was assessed using a series of questions related to previous experiences with the infection. These included “Ever had candidiasis before?” (Yes/No), “How many times have you experienced candidiasis in the past year?” (1 time, 2–3 times, more than 3 times), and “Did you seek medical treatment for your candidiasis infection?” (Yes/No). The responses to these questions were scored, and a mean score was calculated to categorize participants into two groups: “No History” (those with a mean score below a predetermined threshold) and “History” (those with a mean score at or above the threshold). This classification helped to distinguish between those with a past history of candidiasis and those without.

Prevention practices variables

The overall prevention level of candidiasis was determined based on the variable “personal hygiene,” which measured the frequency of practicing good personal hygiene to prevent candidiasis. The mean value derived from this variable was used to classify the responses into two categories: “Good Practice” and “Bad Practice.”

Independent variables

This study considered several independent variables, including age, level of study, program of study, and region (Hohoe or Kumasi). Other factors such as marital status, religious affiliation, parental occupation, sexual activity, history of antibiotic use, and personal hygiene practices (e.g., vaginal douching, type of underwear) were also examined. These variables were assessed to determine their associations with candidiasis knowledge, infection history, and prevention practices among female tertiary students.

Statistical analysis

Data collected from participants were first captured using Kobo Collect and subsequently exported into Microsoft Excel for initial cleaning, where incomplete and inconsistent responses were removed. The cleaned dataset was then imported into STATA version 17 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize socio-demographic characteristics and key study variables. To ensure data quality, a double-entry approach was used to resolve discrepancies during entry. Descriptive statistics, including means or medians (depending on data distribution), frequencies, and proportions, were used to summarize socio-demographic characteristics and the prevalence of key variables. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using Chi-square tests. Variables significant at the bivariate level (p < 0.05) were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to determine independent predictors. A p-value < 0.05 at a 95% confidence interval was considered statistically significant. Results were presented in tables and figures.

Handling of missing data

During the data cleaning process, all responses were carefully reviewed for completeness. Incomplete or inconsistent responses were identified and excluded from the final analysis to ensure data integrity. During this stage, all 798 questionnaires were reviewed for completeness. Ten questionnaires (1.25%) were identified as incomplete or inconsistent and were excluded, leaving 788 valid responses for analysis. Given the minimal proportion of missing data, no imputation techniques were applied.

Discussion

This study was a school-based cross-sectional study with a quantitative approach to assess the knowledge, infection history, and prevention practices related to candidiasis among female tertiary students in Ghana. The study revealed that 52.2% female students demonstrated a high level of knowledge about the condition. This indicates generally good awareness among the participants. However, a significant number of respondents in this study (47.8%) still had low knowledge, highlighting a need for improved education and awareness campaigns. The findings contradict previous studies that have reported varying levels of knowledge about candidiasis among female tertiary students. For instance, a study by Gour et al. found that more than half (51%) of adolescents had below-average knowledge, with only 1% demonstrating good knowledge [22]. Similarly, Murad [23] reported that 66.8% of female nursing students at King Abdulaziz University had poor knowledge about candidiasis prevention, and only 29.9% had good knowledge [23]. In another study conducted at Assiut University, more than half (56.5%) of pregnant women demonstrated a moderate level of knowledge about vaginal yeast infections [24]. These findings highlight the persistent knowledge gaps regarding candidiasis, particularly among women of reproductive age. This supports the argument that, despite general awareness, a lack of comprehensive knowledge about candidiasis is prevalent, which could contribute to higher infection rates.

Our study highlighted a significant regional disparity in knowledge levels, with students from Kumasi in the Ashanti Region being more knowledgeable about candidiasis compared to those from Hohoe in the Volta Region. This disparity could be due to several factors, including differences in educational resources, healthcare outreach programs, and cultural factors between the two regions. This regional disparity mirrors findings from other studies in different geographic contexts. For instance, A study conducted in Cape Coast, Ghana by Ayebi-Arthur, among adolescent girls in senior high schools, revealed that while 93% of respondents knew that vulvovaginal candidiasis could affect the reproductive system, 77% did not know the causative organism or appropriate medications [2]. This highlights a gap in understanding despite awareness of its impact [25, 26]. Such disparities could indicate that access to information and education on health matters is not uniform, which might be influenced by local resources and infrastructure.

Moreover, Muslims were found to have over twice the odds of a past candidiasis infection compared to followers of African Traditional Religion. This may indicate cultural or behavioral factors that influence the prevalence of candidiasis in different religious communities. Those with unemployed parents had lower odds of past infection, which might seem counterintuitive but could suggest differences in lifestyle or healthcare access.

Additionally, individuals with Valid health insurance had 1.88 times higher odds of reporting a history of candidiasis, possibly due to increased healthcare utilization among insured individuals and the ability to access care due to active health insurance coverage. Socioeconomic factors have been recognized in other studies as well. For instance, Hussen et al. reported that participants demonstrated varying levels of understanding of Vulvovaginal Candidiasis (VVC), which could be influenced by their socioeconomic background, including educational attainment and access to healthcare resources [25]. The study by [23] also highlights the impact of socioeconomic status, where more than two-thirds (66.8%) of the students have poor knowledge regarding the prevention of vaginal candidiasis [23]. This suggests that, beyond regional differences, individual socioeconomic factors are critical in determining health knowledge and practices.

Our study revealed that 51.4% of the students had experienced candidiasis at least once, with a notable recurrence rate: 44.4% had experienced it once in the past year, 31.1% had it 2–3 times, and 20.6% had it more than three times. This high recurrence rate suggests that a significant number of students may not be effectively managing the infection, possibly due to inadequate treatment, poor personal hygiene, or other underlying factors. A recent study and a study conducted at the outpatient department of the University of Cape Coast Hospital in Ghana found a high prevalence of Candida infection, particularly among female subjects, where 97.1% of infections were among women [27, 28]. Similarly, a study in Nigeria found that the prevalence rate of Candida albicans was 37.7% among young women, with higher rates observed in older students [29]. The recurrence rates in our study suggest that certain groups of students may be more prone to recurrent infections, possibly due to similar risk factors identified in these studies, such as poor hygiene or the use of antibiotics. This finding is consistent with findings from studies within the West African sub-region.

A study conducted in Nigeria also observed regional differences in the prevalence of candidiasis, with higher rates in certain regions, attributed to variations in hygiene practices and healthcare access [29]. The significant regional difference in our study suggests that students in Kumasi may be exposed to different risk factors, possibly related to urbanization, Lifestyle, or healthcare availability, compared to their counterparts in Hohoe. Our study also found that students with unemployed parents were 50% less likely to have high knowledge about candidiasis compared to those whose parents were artisans. This suggests that parental occupation may influence students’ access to information or education on health matters. Additionally, those with unemployed parents had lower odds of past infection, which could reflect differences in lifestyle or healthcare access. In another study conducted by [27, 30], it was found that socio-economic factors, such as parental occupation, played a significant role in students’ health outcomes, particularly in their knowledge and management of candidiasis [27, 30].

In assessing the prevention practices among female tertiary students, our study found that over half of the students (51.0%) were not familiar with preventive measures for candidiasis. Among those who were aware, the most common practices included adherence to hand hygiene (45.0%) and practicing safe sex (26.0%). This indicates a significant gap in both knowledge and application of preventive measures, emphasizing the need for more comprehensive education on candidiasis prevention. Our study also revealed a significant association between knowledge of candidiasis and familiarity with preventive measures. Students with higher knowledge levels were more Likely to engage in good personal hygiene and understand the importance of wearing breathable clothing, both critical in preventing candidiasis. Additionally, preventive behaviours such as changing damp or sweaty clothes regularly, 74.2% of participants reported doing so twice a day, were significantly associated with a lower history of infection. Several studies support our findings. For example, A study conducted by Adjei and Samono, among female nursing and midwifery students in Berekum, revealed that the majority of students practiced good personal hygiene and were aware that frequent bathing helps prevent candidiasis [26]. This is similar to findings by Mbim et al. at the University of Calabar, where students reported regular hygiene behaviours such as changing underwear and avoiding tight clothing to reduce heat and moisture buildup [31].

Additionally, Umeh et al. [32]. also found that female secondary school students in Nsukka demonstrated awareness of proper genital hygiene and recognized that douching and scented products could increase the risk of infection, aligning with the current study’s observations on harmful hygiene practices [32].

Furthermore, Maikenti et al. noted that misconceptions still exist, with many students incorrectly identifying candidiasis as a bacterial infection, a finding mirrored in the present study, where similar misconceptions were noted [33]. Gudia et al. and Osei-Tutu et al. both recommended structured educational interventions to improve awareness and correct myths, a recommendation that strongly supports the need for campus-wide health education campaigns highlighted in this study [34, 35]. This current study found that out of the total students (788), 454(57.61%) engage in good practices for preventing candidiasis, while 334(42.39%) practice poor prevention practices. This implies that although a majority of female tertiary students are engaging in good preventive practices against candidiasis, a significant proportion still exhibit poor prevention behaviours, which could increase their risk of infection. The statistical level of prevention practices of candidiasis among female tertiary students, based on the study at Holy Family Nursing and Midwifery Training College in Berekum, shows that 92% of respondents bathe twice daily, which is a positive hygiene practice contributing to candidiasis prevention, 5% bathe once daily, and 3% bathe thrice daily [26]. It suggests a gap in consistent practice despite existing knowledge, highlighting the need for strengthened health education interventions.

Our study also highlighted that most respondents (70.30%) learned about preventive measures through Internet research, suggesting that digital platforms could serve as effective channels for health education. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) emphasizes that knowledge and awareness are pivotal in preventing candidiasis, with an understanding of the causes and symptoms being the first step [35]. Educating students about risk factors such as weakened immune systems, prolonged antibiotic use, and underlying health conditions is essential for effective prevention [26]. A balanced diet, which strengthens the immune system, is also recognized as a preventive measure, with recommendations to limit sugar and yeast-containing foods that promote Candida growth [28]. Moreover, maintaining good personal hygiene, including regular hand washing, is crucial to preventing the spread of germs, as highlighted by the CDC [36]. These multifaceted approaches contribute to a comprehensive strategy for preventing candidiasis among female tertiary students, ensuring their optimal physical health and overall well-being. Adopting safer and hygienic practices is paramount for protecting against candidiasis infection, ensuring optimal physical health, and overall well-being [24]. The gap in knowledge and preventive practices identified in our study indicates a need for targeted health education interventions. By leveraging digital platforms for education and promoting comprehensive prevention strategies, including proper hygiene, safe sex, and balanced diets, we can effectively reduce the incidence and recurrence of candidiasis among female tertiary students.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study benefits from a large sample size of 788 female tertiary students, enhancing the reliability of its findings. It provides a comprehensive overview by assessing candidiasis knowledge, infection history, and prevention practices, while also comparing data across two regions, Kumasi Metropolis and Hohoe Municipality, to highlight geographical differences. The inclusion of socio-demographic variables such as age, ethnicity, and parental employment further strengthens its relevance for targeted health interventions. However, the study’s cross-sectional design restricts its ability to determine causality. Data were self-reported, which may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Additionally, the exclusion of male students and individuals outside the tertiary education system limits the generalizability of the findings. Lastly, the absence of clinical or laboratory confirmation of candidiasis cases reduces the accuracy of reported infection histories.

Conclusion

This study highlights a significant gap in the knowledge and prevention practices related to candidiasis among female tertiary students in Ghana. Despite awareness levels, over half of the participants had inadequate knowledge of preventive measures, leading to high rates of recurrent infections. These findings align with Sustainable Development Goal 3, which emphasizes ensuring healthy lives and promoting well-being through awareness and disease prevention. Ghana Health Service and the Ministry of Education should focus on enhancing knowledge, promoting good hygiene practices, and improving access to healthcare services to reduce the burden of candidiasis in this population.
