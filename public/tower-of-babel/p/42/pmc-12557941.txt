Effects of iron replacement on electrocardiogram parameters in breath-holding spells patients with iron deficiency
Authors: Gamze Aslan, Fatos Alkan, Onur Bircan, Senol Coskun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to examine electrocardiogram parameters in patients with breath-holding spells accompanied by iron deficiency before iron treatment and after three months of iron supplementation therapy. A total of 195 children were included in the study, consisting of 95 patients with breath-holding spells and 100 age- and gender-matched controls. Patients were evaluated for iron deficiency at admission. Iron deficiency was associated with 48 patients who had breath-holding spells, while it was not associated with 47 of them. When comparing the ECG parameters of patients with breath-holding spells (BHS) and the control group, the QRS and QT intervals were found to be significantly longer in patients with BHS (p<0.05). However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of PHR, Pmax, Pd, QTd, QTc, QTcd, Tp-e, Tp-e/QT, and Tp-e/QTc (p>0.05). In BHS patients with iron deficiency, QRS, QT, and QTc durations were observed to shorten after iron supplementation therapy, with statistically significant reductions (p=0.036, p=0.011, p=0.008, respectively). Additionally, BHS did not recur in 45.23% of patients who received iron supplementation therapy. Our study found that QRS, QT, and QTc durations shortened following iron supplementation in pediatric patients with BHS and iron deficiency anemia. This highlights the beneficial effects of iron on ventricular depolarization and repolarization parameters. Therefore, ECG parameter evaluation should always be considered in the management of patients with breath-holding spells, particularly those with iron deficiency.Keywords: Breath-holding spell, Iron deficiency, Electrocardiography

Introduction

Breath-holding spells (BHS), which are relatively common during childhood, were first described by Nicholas Culpeper [1]. These episodes are characterized as recurrent and involuntary events, typically precipitated by emotional triggers such as frustration or crying, and are accompanied by changes in skin color [2]. BHS most frequently occurs between the ages of 6 months and 6 years, although cases have been reported up to 8 years of age [3].

While the exact pathophysiological mechanisms of BHS remain incompletely understood, several contributing factors have been proposed. These include genetic predisposition, emotional stress, parental psychosocial background, imbalance in autonomic nervous system regulation, and iron deficiency anemia (IDA) [4, 5]. Iron deficiency has been shown to increase irritability and crying in children, potentially predisposing them to BHS episodes [5–7]. Furthermore, decreased hemoglobin levels in IDA result in reduced tissue oxygenation, which may contribute to cerebral hypoxia and trigger BHS attacks [8, 9].

Adequate iron stores are also essential for proper autonomic nervous system function. Iron deficiency may disrupt autonomic regulation, potentially leading to subclinical cardiovascular effects in affected children [10]. In particular, abnormalities in ventricular repolarization and atrial conduction have been reported in children with BHS. Prolongation of the QT interval and the associated risk of ventricular arrhythmias are major clinical concerns, given their association with increased morbidity and potential mortality [5, 6, 11].

Several studies have demonstrated subclinical cardiac involvement in children with BHS, including QTc prolongation, repolarization abnormalities, and arrhythmias, as well as evidence of autonomic imbalance [12–14]. These findings underscore that BHS may not be entirely benign and highlight the importance of ECG evaluation in affected children. Establishing whether iron supplementation can favorably modify these ECG parameters provides a strong rationale for the present study.

These considerations highlight the importance of electrocardiographic (ECG) evaluation in children with BHS, particularly those presenting with frequent or cyanotic episodes.

Although prior studies have examined the relationship between iron deficiency and BHS frequency or severity [10, 11, 15, 16], there is a paucity of research focusing on the effects of iron supplementation on ECG parameters in this population.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the changes in ECG findings before and after iron supplementation therapy in pediatric patients with BHS and IDA, and to compare these results with those of children without iron deficiency and with healthy controls.

Study design and population

This prospective observational study was conducted at the Pediatric Cardiology Outpatient Clinic of Manisa Celal Bayar University (MCBU) Hospital between June 2022 and July 2023. A total of 95 children aged 6 months to 8 years who presented with episodes initially diagnosed as seizure, convulsion, or syncope were screened. Children whose parents consented to participation were enrolled. At admission, all patients were evaluated for iron deficiency using laboratory testing. Of these, 48 patients were diagnosed with iron deficiency and 47 had normal iron profiles. Iron deficiency was defined as hemoglobin below − 2 SD for age, serum iron and ferritin below reference thresholds, and elevated total iron-binding capacity (TIBC). Reference intervals used were: Serum iron: 60–180 µg/dL, Ferritin: 11–306 ng/mL, TIBC: 250–450 µg/dL, Iron-binding capacity (IBC): 155–300 µg/dL. Patients whose values fell below or above these ranges were categorized accordingly.

Control group

A control group of 100 age-matched children was recruited from the same clinic over the same period. These children presented for reasons unrelated to syncope (e.g., chest pain, innocent murmur), had normal ECGs and iron profiles, no known cardiac or neurological disease, and no history of breath-holding spells, seizure, or syncope.

Iron therapy protocol

Patients with iron deficiency received oral ferrous sulfate at a dose of 4 mg/kg/day (elemental iron), divided into two doses, administered between meals for 3 months. Caregivers were instructed regarding adherence and side effect monitoring. After the treatment period, iron studies (hemogram, iron, TIBC, IBC, ferritin) and ECG were repeated to evaluate treatment response.

Electrocardiographic (ECG) evaluation

All ECGs were obtained in the supine position after a 10-minute rest using 12-lead systems (Nihon Kohden Cardiofax and GE Healthcare MAC 2000), with standard settings of 25 mm/sec speed and 10 mm/mV amplitude. Measurements were performed manually using an electronic digital caliper (WOZLO W5506-008) with a resolution of 0.1 mm/0.01 inch. In ECGs, P, QRS, T wave, PR interval (from the beginning of the P wave to the beginning of the QRS complex), QT interval (from the beginning of the Q wave to the end of the T wave), QTc (calculated by Bazett’s formula: QTc = QT/√RR,) QTc = QT/√RR. T peak (Tp-e, the interval between the peak and end of the T wave), QTd (QT dispersion; the difference between the minimum and maximum QT intervals measured in all leads in each lead), QTcd (QTc dispersion; measured in all leads of the ECG and calculated by Bazett’s formula), Pd (P dispersion; the difference between the minimum and maximal P intervals measured in all leads of each patient’s ECG), Tp-e/QT ratio, Tp-e/QTc ratio measurements were made. To minimize inter-observer variability, all ECG measurements were performed by a single trained observer blinded to clinical data. Measurements were primarily taken from lead V5; if not interpretable, lead II (DII) was used. Tp-e interval was preferentially measured from lead V6, as recommended.

Ethics approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics in Research Committee of the School of Medicine of the Celal Bayar University of Manisa (05.30.2022/286).

Statistical analysis

Statistical data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). In descriptive analyses, nominal variables are represented by number and percentage, and continuous variables are represented by mean and standard deviation. The suitability of continuous data for normal distribution was evaluated by the Shapiro-Wilk test. In analytical analyses, paired t-test (t-test in dependent groups) was used to compare two consecutive measurements in dependent groups, Wilcoxon signed rank test when parametric conditions were not met, Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables in independent groups, Student’s t-test was used to compare the measurement values of two independent groups, Mann Whitney U test was used in nonparametric conditions, ANOVA test was used to compare the mean of the measurement values of more than two groups, and Kruskal-Wallis variance analysis was used in nonparametric conditions. In the analytical analyses, type 1 error was accepted as 0.05. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study evaluated ECG changes in children with BHS, particularly in those with concurrent IDA, and examined the potential reversibility of these changes following iron supplementation. To the best of our knowledge, no prior studies have evaluated ECG parameters in children with BHS associated with iron deficiency before and after iron therapy. Therefore, direct comparisons with existing literature were not possible, underscoring the originality and novelty of our study.

Among the 48 patients diagnosed with iron deficiency and BHS, 42 completed three months of oral iron therapy. Following treatment, 19 (45.2%) patients achieved complete remission of spells. This clinical improvement aligns with previous reports demonstrating reduced BHS frequency after iron supplementation.

When comparing ECG parameters before and after treatment in patients with IDA-associated BHS, we observed a statistically significant shortening in QRS, QT, and corrected QT (QTc) durations (p = 0.036, p = 0.011, and p = 0.008, respectively). These findings suggest that iron plays a favorable role in cardiac conduction and ventricular repolarization. Prolonged QT intervals have been associated with an increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias, thus the improvement observed may reflect reduced arrhythmic potential post-treatment.

Consistent with our results, studies by Akalın et al. and DiMario et al. reported prolonged QT intervals in children with BHS compared to controls [19, 20]. Additionally, anemia has been shown to affect ventricular repolarization markers, including QT and QRS duration [21, 22]. Our finding that QRS duration shortened after iron supplementation supports the hypothesis that iron improves myocardial electrophysiologic properties in this population.

Although QRS duration was statistically longer in the BHS group compared to controls, it remained within age-specific normal limits. This suggests that the observed difference, while significant, may not reflect overt conduction pathology. Similar findings have been noted in other pediatric studies evaluating anemia-related ECG changes [21].

In contrast to some reports, we found no significant difference in QTd, QTcd, or Tp-e interval between BHS patients and healthy controls [23, 24]. These parameters did not significantly change after treatment either. Variability in findings across studies may stem from differences in patient selection, anemia severity, or underlying autonomic dysfunction.

Pd, a marker of atrial conduction heterogeneity and predictor of atrial arrhythmias, was evaluated but showed no significant differences between groups or after treatment, consistent with some previous pediatric reports [23, 25], though others have noted increased Pd in BHS or IDA populations [26, 27]. Regarding repolarization heterogeneity, Tp-e/QT and Tp-e/QTc ratios were likewise not significantly altered, suggesting a limited role for these markers in iron-related modulation despite occasional reports of elevation in BHS [26, 28].

A study by Karadeniz et al. found that low ferritin levels in healthy children were associated with prolongation of QT, QTc, and Tp-e intervals, and recommended ECG monitoring in such cases [29]. Our study supports this recommendation, particularly in children with frequent or severe BHS episodes and coexisting iron deficiency.

Limitations

The most important limitation of our study was the lack of long-term follow-up, which prevented the evaluation of delayed or persistent ECG abnormalities and arrhythmias. Additionally, 24-hour Holter monitoring was not performed, which could have enhanced the detection of subclinical or paroxysmal arrhythmias and provided a more robust correlation with standard ECG parameters.

Furthermore, although autonomic dysregulation is considered a potential mechanism in BHS, objective assessment of autonomic function—such as heart rate variability analysis—was not included.

All ECG measurements were performed by a single trained observer to minimize variability; however, inter-observer variability was not assessed, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.

Lastly, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to infer temporal relationships or establish causality between iron supplementation and ECG changes. Future prospective longitudinal studies are needed to validate these findings and explore the underlying mechanisms more comprehensively.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that children with BHS and IDA exhibit subtle but significant prolongation of QRS, QT, and QTc intervals, which improve following iron supplementation. These findings suggest a reversible effect of iron on cardiac conduction and repolarization, with potential implications for arrhythmia risk. Routine ECG monitoring may therefore be warranted in BHS patients with IDA, and larger prospective studies are needed to confirm and extend these observations.
