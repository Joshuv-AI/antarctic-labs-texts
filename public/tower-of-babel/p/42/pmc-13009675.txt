Pediatric COVID-19 in Korea: Lessons and Strategies for Future Disease-X Preparedness
Authors: Young June Choe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has had distinct public health and societal impacts on children worldwidely, prompting calls to prepare for the next pandemic by incorporating children’s needs. Republic of Korea’s experience provides insights into pediatric-focused pandemic response. This review analyzes the impact of COVID-19 on children in Korea and evaluates the national response. This review encompasses the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency COVID-19 response white paper, National Medical Center response report, Ministry of Education and Seoul Metropolitan Office of Education white papers, focusing on pediatric data and policies. Key findings were supplemented with international studies on pediatric COVID-19 epidemiology, vaccination, and educational impacts. Children in Korea accounted for a substantial number of COVID-19 cases during omicron wave, yet severe outcomes remained rare. Surveillance adaptations included dedicated monitoring of pediatric multisystem inflammatory syndrome through antibody testing. The healthcare system rapidly adjusted to pediatric needs by allowing home isolation for mild cases and by permitting caregiver accompaniment during pediatric hospital isolation. Vaccine rollout for adolescents began in 2021 and for ages 5–11 in 2022, with an initial policy focusing on high-risk children and voluntary uptake for others. In the education sector, Korea implemented remote learning infrastructure, distributing devices and expanding internet access to bridge the digital divide. Korea’s pandemic response illustrates the importance of pediatric-specific strategies: surveillance, child-friendly healthcare protocols, risk communication to improve vaccine acceptance, and treating schools and child services as essential infrastructure.Keywords: COVID-19, Pediatric, Children, Korea

INTRODUCTION

Children have generally experienced milder direct health effects from coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) than adults, but the pandemic’s indirect impacts on children have been profound.1 Globally, the indirect impacts of prolonged school closures and social distancing have been profound, resulting in significant learning deficits, worsening mental health outcomes, and increased obesity rates.2

In Republic of Korea, as in other countries, the early pandemic response largely prioritized reducing overall transmission and protecting high-risk elderly populations.3,4 Over time, it became clear that a balanced approach was needed—one that also mitigated harm to younger age groups.5

In Korea, children initially comprised a small fraction of COVID-19 cases, but the omicron wave in 2022 saw infections in pediatric age groups surge.5,6,7 Despite high case counts, severe illness in children remained fortunately rare—pediatric hospitalizations and fatalities were only a small proportion of overall COVID-19 burden.8 Korea recorded very few pediatric deaths from COVID-19, and analysis of those cases underscored the critical importance of early detection and management of infections in children.9 These observations align with international findings that, even with omicron’s generally milder course, infants and children with comorbidities were at somewhat higher risk, though absolute rates of severe outcomes stayed very low.10

Beyond clinical outcomes, the pandemic disrupted nearly every aspect of children’s lives—from routine healthcare such as delayed vaccinations and check-ups to daily education and social development. Korea’s comprehensive pandemic response included measures aimed at children’s welfare, implemented by agencies such as the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA; public health surveillance and vaccination),11 the National Medical Center (clinical coordination),12 and the Ministry of Education along with regional offices (school operations and student support).13,14

This review covers Korea’s pediatric COVID-19 experience in four key domains, drawing lessons to guide preparations for a future novel pathogen (“Disease-X”) that might affect children more severely. These domains correspond to: 1) Epidemiologic Surveillance—how pediatric cases and outcomes were monitored, and where data gaps exist; 2) Healthcare System Response—pediatric preparedness in hospitals and public health such as intensive care unit capacity, treatment protocols, mental health considerations; 3) Vaccine Strategy—policies for vaccinating children and adolescents, and public acceptance; and 4) Educational Continuity—ensuring learning and psychosocial support during school closures or other disruptions (Fig. 1).

EPIDEMIOLOGIC SURVEILLANCE OF PEDIATRIC COVID-19

Effective surveillance is the foundation for mounting a timely pandemic response. This surveillance faced the universal challenge that children often have asymptomatic or mild infections, which likely led to under-detection in the early phase. However, Korea’s robust contact tracing system was a key strength that partially mitigated this specific challenge, successfully identifying many pediatric cases that would have otherwise been missed. In Korea, COVID-19 surveillance was comprehensive, but it faced challenges in capturing pediatric infections and outcomes.15 Initially, testing and case investigations focused on identified clusters and symptomatic individuals; given that children often have asymptomatic or mild infections, early surveillance likely under-detected pediatric cases.9,16

Nonetheless, Korea’s vigorous contact tracing system did include children, which helped in identifying pediatric cases that might have otherwise been missed.15 As the pandemic evolved, Korea adjusted its surveillance strategy, especially once case numbers exploded with omicron. In early 2022, with increased daily cases at the peak, universal case investigation became impractical and a transition was made to more targeted approaches including monitoring high-risk groups and using sentinel sites.17 By this stage, the clinical consensus was that most pediatric cases were mild, so intensive contact tracing for every child case was less critical for clinical management.

One notable enhancement was the surveillance of Multisystem Inflammatory Syndrome in Children (MIS-C), a rare but serious post-COVID complication.18 In May 2020, as MIS-C reports emerged globally, the KDCA introduced a neutralizing antibody testing protocol for suspected MIS-C cases.19 This allowed confirmation of past severe acute respiratory syndrome-coronavirus-2 infection in children presenting with MIS-C symptoms. By confirming MIS-C cases serologically, Korea could accurately track this pediatric complication and provide appropriate treatment (Fig. 2). The proactive use of serology for MIS-C surveillance demonstrated a nimble adaptation of epidemiologic methods to pediatric needs, ensuring cases did not go unrecognized. MIS-C incidence in Korea was low, mirroring the small number of pediatric COVID-19 deaths.20

Despite these efforts, certain data gaps in pediatric surveillance became apparent. Detailed age-stratified data including infection rates in daycare vs. elementary vs. teens were not always readily available to the public, which at times made risk communication to parents more difficult. Additionally, surveillance systems initially did not systematically capture secondary impacts on children’s health.

HEALTHCARE SYSTEM RESPONSE: PEDIATRIC PREPAREDNESS

Korea’s healthcare system had to adapt rapidly to the influx of COVID-19 patients.6 Although children fortunately made up a small fraction of severe cases, ensuring capacity and appropriate care for pediatric patients was an important part of the response. Early in the pandemic, most designated COVID-19 treatment hospitals and wards were adult-focused. As pediatric cases appeared, however, hospitals mobilized pediatric COVID-19 units within major medical centers. Pediatric infectious disease specialists in Korea collaborated to share treatment protocols and clinical experiences.

Guidelines for treating children with COVID-19 were developed in parallel with adult guidelines, covering use of antivirals, managing MIS-C, albeit with recognition that evidence was limited and mostly extrapolated from adult data.12 Importantly, the healthcare response evolved to address the unique needs of children and families in isolation. Hospitalization of a child for an infectious disease raises concerns about both medical care and emotional support.21 In the initial phase, strict infection control rules often meant family members could not accompany patients in isolation. However, pediatricians recognized that young children suffer trauma if separated from parents.11 The National Medical Center’s records show that policies were reviewed to allow a caregiver to stay with an isolated child patient under safe conditions.12 If a parent had recovered from COVID-19 or was fully vaccinated, they could be permitted as an attendant in the child’s negative-pressure room with appropriate personal protective equipment, to provide comfort and basic care. Flexible approaches like this helped mitigate the stress on hospitalized children and were facilitated by coordination between infectious disease experts and hospital administrators.

At the community level, when case numbers grew, Korea moved mild pediatric cases out of hospitals entirely—implementing home isolation (“home treatment”) for children.22 Starting in October 2020, the KDCA established a home care protocol for pediatric patients who had minimal symptoms and no risk factors (Fig. 2). This policy was driven by reports that children in prolonged hospital isolation experienced emotional difficulties. By allowing low-risk children to quarantine at home under remote monitoring, the system improved the pediatric patient experience while reserving hospital beds for more serious cases. The “COVID-19 Pediatric Home Care Guide” was issued to ensure infection control at home and to advise parents on managing the illness.11 Local health teams were assigned to check on these children’s health status daily. This innovation not only addressed psychological well-being but also hinted at a broader shift toward community-based care models that could be very useful in future pandemics.

Another aspect of healthcare preparedness is critical care capacity for children. A potential Disease-X, unlike COVID-19, may cause severe illness preferentially in the young—hence assessing pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) capacity is a key lesson.23 COVID-19’s mild nature in children meant PICUs in Korea were not overwhelmed by pediatric cases; in fact, during surges, many PICU beds remained available and some were even repurposed for adult use in emergencies. However, this should not breed complacency. Healthcare planning documents now emphasize expanding surge capacity for pediatric care in referral centers.24 This might include cross-training pediatric staff so that they can support PICU care if needed.

Notably, during COVID-19, the sharp reduction in other pediatric respiratory illnesses (due to social distancing) led to an unusual situation in which pediatric hospitals had very low census in 2020–2021.25 But once social distancing eased, a rebound of endemic viruses (respiratory syncytial virus, influenza) caused spikes in pediatric hospital admissions globally, testing capacity.13,26 This underscored that pandemic response and routine pediatric care are interlinked—a holistic preparedness strategy must account for collateral effects so that children’s hospitals can manage both pandemic and non-pandemic illnesses.

VACCINE STRATEGY FOR CHILDREN AND ADOLESCENTS

Vaccination has been the cornerstone of COVID-19 exit strategies globally. However, the inclusion of children in those strategies came with unique challenges. In Korea, as in many countries, initial vaccine roll-out in early 2021 focused on adults, especially healthcare workers, the elderly, and vulnerable groups.27,28 Clinical trials for pediatric COVID-19 vaccines were still ongoing at that time, so vaccines for adolescents (12–17 years) were authorized later in 2021 and for younger children (5–11 years) only by early 2022.29 Korea’s approach to pediatric COVID-19 vaccination was cautious and phased (Fig. 2). Once vaccines were approved for adolescents in mid-2021, Korea began vaccinating 16–17 year-olds and subsequently 12–15 year-olds, largely through school-based coordination and local clinics.30,31

By early 2022, with the omicron wave driving up child infections, attention turned to younger children. The vaccine policy for ages 5–11 deliberately prioritized safety and parental choice. Citing the worsening outbreak and rising pediatric cases including some severe cases among unvaccinated children with risk factors, authorities recommended vaccination strongly for high-risk children while leaving the decision optional for healthy children. The rationale was that while every child could benefit from vaccination in terms of reducing infection and rare severe outcomes, the risk–benefit calculus was most clear-cut for children with underlying comorbidities. Outreach efforts included distributing informational letters through schools and holding webinars for parents to explain the vaccine’s safety and efficacy. For adolescents 12–17, a booster (third dose) campaign was also launched in March 2022, again focusing on those with medical vulnerabilities while making it available to all teens on a voluntary basis.32

Despite these efforts, public uptake of pediatric vaccination in Korea was disappointingly low. By January 2023, only 1.1% of children 5–11 had received two vaccine doses, and even among children with comorbidities, uptake was just 2.4% in this age group.31 In adolescents 12–17, who had more time and a more routine school-based campaign, coverage was better—about 66% had two doses.11 Still, this lagged adult vaccination rates.33 The contrast revealed significant vaccine hesitancy among parents.34 Surveys in 2022 found only around 6% of Korean parents of children were willing to vaccinate their child, primarily due to concerns about safety and the perception that COVID-19 infection was mild for their children.14 These perceptions were reinforced by the fact that by the time pediatric vaccines became available, omicron was dominant and widely seen as less severe.

EDUCATIONAL CONTINUITY AND PSYCHOSOCIAL SUPPORT

To maintain educational continuity during the initial pandemic response in spring 2020, Korea implemented nationwide school closures and transitioned to an online opening of schools (Fig. 2). By April 2020, all Korean schools had launched remote classes, leveraging digital platforms to livestream lessons. An enormous effort was made to build a remote education infrastructure, including distributing digital devices to bridge the digital divide. As a result, surveys indicated that nearly 99% of schools were able to provide alternative learning materials during closure periods. By late 2020, Korea further enhanced these efforts by developing specialized online learning platforms to support interactive classes.

Despite the rapid transition to remote learning, prolonged school closures and social distancing measures led to adverse impacts on the physical and mental health of Korean children and adolescents. Regarding education, while overall learning loss was relatively modest, a notable widening of the learning gap based on socioeconomic status occurred, with a significant increase in the proportion of lower-performing students failing to meet basic academic standards.35 In terms of physical health, a sharp decline in outdoor activities triggered a surge in pediatric obesity. A study from a tertiary hospital in Seoul reported that after six months of social distancing, the mean body mass index z-score of children rose by 0.219 (P < 0.001), and the prevalence of overweight or obesity increased from 23.9% to 31.4%, alongside worsened vitamin D deficiency.36 Furthermore, a mental health crisis termed ‘Corona Blue’ emerged, characterized by significant increases in depressive symptoms (P = 0.010) and anxiety, particularly among adolescents.37,38

CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

Korea’s experience with COVID-19 in the pediatric population yields valuable insights for improving preparedness for a future “Disease-X”—a hypothetical novel pathogen that could cause the next pandemic. Key lessons include the importance of tailoring public health and healthcare strategies to the needs of children, who are often not the face of a pandemic yet bear significant hidden burdens. Children must be “seen and heard” in pandemic planning, as neglecting their needs can lead to lifelong consequences. Drawing on the analyses above, we propose the following recommendations to strengthen Korea’s pediatric preparedness.

First, we need to develop dedicated surveillance mechanisms for early detection of outbreaks in children. This could involve sentinel reporting from pediatric clinics and schools, routine analysis of school absenteeism for illness trends, and deployment of wastewater surveillance in schools or childcare centers. Ensure surveillance data is stratified by age to identify at-risk pediatric subgroups quickly.39 Also track indirect health indicators such as immunization coverage, mental health consults, child abuse reports during pandemics to promptly address secondary effects on children.

Second, increasing the surge capacity for pediatric care, including critical care is essential. Prepare protocols for rapid conversion of beds or staff from adult to pediatric care if needed. Stockpile pediatric-specific medical supplies. Incorporate pediatric specialists in pandemic response leadership so that guidelines consider developmental and psychological needs of minors. Continue policies allowing family presence safely during a child’s hospitalization, and expand home-based care options with telehealth support for cases that can be managed outside hospital.

Third, we should include children in vaccine R&D from the outset of an outbreak so that effective vaccines for all ages are available with minimal delay. Once vaccines are available, initiate clear and transparent risk–benefit communication targeting parents, using trusted messengers like pediatricians and educators. Aim to achieve high coverage in children, especially those with high-risk conditions, before a large wave hits. Vaccine strategy should also encompass maintaining routine childhood immunizations during pandemics to prevent other outbreaks.

Lastly, recognizing schools as essential services that merit the utmost priority in pandemic plans is important. Develop robust protocols to keep schools open safely—improve ventilation in school buildings, have stockpiles of masks and rapid tests for school use, and establish threshold criteria for shifting to remote learning that are used sparingly. When closures or hybrid models are necessary, ensure continuity of learning by having pre-prepared online curriculum resources and training for teachers in digital instruction.

Importantly, the severe, long-term collateral harms documented—ranging from learning loss to mental health crises—imply that future pandemic plans must treat school closures as an extreme measure of last resort, not a primary containment tool. By implementing these recommendations, we may provide readiness to safeguard children in the face of the next pandemic. COVID-19 taught us that while children were not the most clinically vulnerable group for that particular disease, they were profoundly affected in other ways. A future Disease-X might pose greater direct risks to children; even if not, the indirect effects of any pandemic on the young are too significant to ignore.
