Racial and ethnic disparities in pediatric autoimmune encephalitis: A systematic review
Authors: Jennifer H. Yang, Kristen S. Fisher, Julia Dumcum, Sophie Zacharek, Alexandra B. Kornbluh, Kelsey Poisson, Mary C. Karalius, Leigh Sepeta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Health disparities and social determinants of health are increasingly recognized as important predictors for clinical outcomes in autoimmune disease. However, this is poorly studied in pediatric autoimmune encephalitis (AE).Methods:We conducted a systematic literature review in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Searches included Medline (Pubmed), EMBASE, and Web of Science databases from inception years to October 4, 2024. Inclusion criteria were: 1) Patients diagnosed with AE ≤ 18 years old; 2) any report of race and/or ethnicity, 3) report of specific diagnostics and disease outcomes, 4) observational cohort studies, epidemiological studies or case series/cohort studies of n ≥ 5 participants. We excluded studies describing only AE associated with viral encephalitis, case reports or case series with <5 cases, papers without clear racial or ethnicity demographics, no report of clinical outcomes, and non-English studies. Study screening, scoring, and data extraction used the Covidence platform. Study quality utilized the Newcastle-Ottawa scale for case-control and cohort studies, and the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for epidemiological studies. Descriptive analyses were used to summarize study findings.Results:A total of 35 studies were included in the study: 74 % retrospective cohort, 14 % prospective cohort, 8.6 % epidemiological, and 1 case-control study. Study quality fell mostly into “good” (45.7 %) or “fair” (37 %), and the remaining 17 % were given “poor” quality scores. Among the included studies, 94 % of the studies had pediatric-specific data from which demographic and clinical information could be extracted. Only 12 (34 %) of studies reported outcomes specific to a racial and/or ethnic group – 7 cohort studies from homogenous populations, 3 epidemiological studies, and 2 reported racial frequencies in cognitive outcomes only. No studies compared clinical outcomes among different racial or ethnic groups.Discussion:There is an overall paucity in rigorous studies on health disparities in pediatric AE. Future studies should be aimed at determining individual, social, and structural risk factors that may impact treatment and long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Social determinants of health (SDOH) are circumstances that people are either born into or live in that positively or negatively impact their health and welfare.(Robers and Amezcua, 2024) This includes, but is not limited to, socioeconomic status, education, access to healthcare, race and ethnicity, and environment.(Amezcua et al., 2021; Robers and Amezcua, 2024) Although race and ethnicity are social constructs that have no direct scientific or biological significance, many researchers utilize these demographic variables as proxies for socioeconomic or environmental SDOH variables in statistical models. The roles of health disparities and SDOH in determining clinical outcomes are increasingly recognized in neuroinflammatory disorders such as multiple sclerosis (MS) and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders (NMOSD) (Amezcua et al., 2021, 2018). Observational studies on MS and NMOSD demonstrate that patients who identify as Black or Hispanic/LatinX experience more disability due to a combination of structural, social, and biological risk factors (Alsalek et al., 2024; Graus et al., 2016; Telesford et al., 2023). Furthermore, while observational studies for anti-NMDARE, one of the most common AE subtypes in pediatric populations, demonstrate better clinical outcomes with earlier immunotherapy (Nosadini et al., 2021; Titulaer et al., 2013), there are no guidelines for therapeutic approaches in specific subpopulations with increased societal or socioeconomic vulnerabilities in pediatric anti-NMDARE or other pediatric onset AE. Additionally, studies suggest some patients suffer persistent cognitive and neuropsychological symptoms despite treatment with early immunotherapy, though information regarding which clinical predictors at disease onset predispose to long-term neurocognitive sequelae is lacking (Nguyen et al., 2022; Yeshokumar et al., 2017).

While the role of health disparities is being studied in MS, NMOSD, and adults with AE, the demographic and social risk factors associated with treatment outcomes and cognitive deficits in pediatric AE are poorly characterized. Improved understanding of disparities in AE is the first step toward developing meaningful and accessible interventions to improve health outcomes and cognitive recovery across all affected children. Thus, there is a critical need to better understand these disparities since studies in MS and NMOSD have shown that genetic risk factors, gene-environment interactions, and lifestyle and socioeconomic factors affect disease burden (Rivas-Rodríguez and Amezcua, 2018; Yang et al., 2022). The purpose of this systematic review is to synthesize current understanding of the racial and ethnic health disparities as a proxy for health disparities in pediatric AE epidemiology, treatment response, and neuropsychological outcomes.

Methods

We conducted a systematic literature review in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. We searched Medline (Pubmed), EMBASE, and Web of Science databases from inception years to October 4, 2024. We employed two search criteria for Pubmed to best capture all relevant literature on AE in children. The first search was “autoimmune encephalitis OR NMDA encephalitis OR limbic encephalitis OR paraneoplastic encephalitis AND children and race”; the second search was “autoimmune encephalitis AND pediatric”. The search string for EMBASE was “autoimmune encephalitis’/exp OR ‘autoimmune encephalitis’) AND (‘childhood disease’/exp OR ‘childhood disease’) AND (‘race’/exp OR ‘race’). For web of science, we used “autoimmune encephalitis or NMDA encephalitis or limbic encephalitis or paraneoplastic encephalitis and children and race.”

Inclusion criteria were: 1) any study that included patients diagnosed with AE ≤ 18 years old; 2) any report of race and/or ethnicity, 3) report of specific diagnostics and disease outcomes (electroencephalogram [EEG], magnetic resonance imaging [MRI], immunotherapy, treatment outcomes, modified Rankin Scale (mRS), neuropsychological testing, seizures), 4) observational studies (retrospective or prospective cohorts), epidemiological studies, or case series/ cohort studies of n ≥ 5 participants. We excluded studies describing only AE associated with viral encephalitis (eg. herpes simplex virus [HSV] related anti-NMDARE), papers without clear data on race or ethnicity in demographics, lack of clinical outcomes in the results (eg. methodology papers), studies that did not primarily focus on incidence/prevalence of AE or demographic/clinical characteristics, and studies not written in English.

Following database searches, the reference lists were downloaded into Zotero reference manager and uploaded into the Covidence systematic review platform for screening, quality assessment, and data extraction. Titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers (JHY, JD). Full text articles were reviewed by the primary author (JHY) for inclusion into the systematic review. Study quality assessments were conducted by two independent reviewers (JHY, KF) using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale for case-control and cohort studies (Stang, 2010) and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist (“Critical Appraisal Tools | JBI, 2017.) for studies reporting prevalence, and any discrepancies were discussed to achieve consensus.

Data extraction from selected studies included methodology, study population, clinical phenotypes, immunotherapy, clinical outcomes, and whether outcome measurements were analyzed within racial or ethnic subgroups. Demographics and clinical characteristics were summarized using means and medians as appropriate, and proportions were used for discrete variables. Analyses were conducted in R and R Studio.

Results

Study characteristics

A total of 35 studies were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1) including 26 (74 %) retrospective cohort, 5 (14 %) prospective cohort, and 3 (8.6 %) epidemiological studies (Table 1). The study quality fell mostly into “good” (45.7 %) or “fair” (37 %) categorizations, and the remaining 17 % were given “poor” quality scores. Studies that were deemed “fair” were due to inadequate statistical methods and not controlling for appropriate confounders, and studies that were deemed “poor” lacked appropriate statistical methods and follow-up data (Table 1, S1A, S1B). Among the included studies, 94 % had pediatric-specific data from which demographic and clinical information could be extracted. Only 12 (34 %) of studies reported outcomes specific to a racial and/or ethnic group, three of which were the epidemiologic studies. Most of the studies reported demographics, incidence, or prevalence for anti-NMDARE alone or anti-NMDARE and other antibody-positive AE. The racial and ethnic demographics for pediatric participants extrapolated from each of the studies are shown in Table 2, and the key clinical characteristics for each study are summarized in Table 3.

Anti-NMDARE demographics

For anti-NMDARE, there were demographic differences in disease incidence and prevalence. A study conducted through Kaiser Permanente Southern California found that among 70 pediatric and adult patients with anti-NMDARE, the age and sex adjusted standardized incident rates of disease were higher in Black, Asian/Pacific Islander (API), and Hispanic individuals than White individuals (Alsalek et al., 2024). The overall incidence of anti-NMDARE in cases per million in person-years was 4.84, 95 % CI 1.81–7.86 (Black), 3.17, 95 % CI 0.97–5.37 (API), and 2.51, 95 % CI 1.52–3.49 (Hispanic), versus 0.25, 95 % CI 0.00–0.60 (White). The sex and age-standardized incidence of anti-NMDARE per one million person-years was higher in non-White populations compared to White individuals. Approximately 58.3 % of Black females were found to have ovarian teratomas compared to 10–28 % in the other groups.(Alsalek et al., 2024) In a large anti-NMDARE cohort study from Spain with 556 pediatric and adult patients, Asian and Black adult patients had a higher frequency of teratomas than White and Hispanic patients (31 % White, 45 % Asian, 27 % Hispanic, 47.5 % Afro-American, 37.5 % Other [native American Indians and Pacific Islanders]) (Titulaer et al., 2013). A study examining 86 cases at Texas Children’s Hospital evaluated infectious exposures in children diagnosed with anti-NMDARE (Sandweiss et al., 2023). Over two-thirds of their cohort were Hispanic, which is 1.6-fold greater than the county’s population (Sandweiss et al., 2023).

Two epidemiologic studies in the API population suggest higher incidence of anti-NMDARE than previously published in other populations. In a study by Jones et al. conducted on 16 Māori and Pacific Islander children with anti-NMDARE in New Zealand, the incidence of anti-NMDARE in Māori children was 3.4 per million per year (95 % CI 1.4–7.0) while the incidence in children of other Pacific Island descent was 10.0 per-million-year (95 % CI 4.3–19.8), and incidence in children without Māori or Pacific Island ancestry was 0.2 per million children per year (95 % CI 0.0–1.0) (Jones et al., 2017). A study by Wong et al. evaluated frequency of anti-NMDARE in the Austronesian population including 18 pediatric cases in the entire population of Sabah, Malaysia. (Wong et al., 2021) The study reported a high annualized incidence among all Austronesians (2.56 persons per million, 95 % CI 1.72 – 3.66), and in the pediatric population, the calculated annualized incidence was even higher (3.63 persons per million, 95 % CI 2.13 – 5.75) (Wong et al., 2021). These incidences are higher than those reported in predominantly White European populations: 0.85 per million children per year in a study conducted in the United Kingdom (Wright et al., 2015), 0.7 per million children in Denmark (Boesen et al., 2019), and 1.54 per million children in the Netherlands (de Bruijn et al., 2020).

Other autoantibody positive AE demographics

Two studies reported demographic data on pediatric-onset stiff person syndrome (SPS) due to anti-GAD65 or anti-GlyR antibodies (Clardy et al., 2013; Yeshokumar et al., 2021). In the study evaluating clinical outcomes and treatments in 15 patients with pediatric onset, 87 % of participants were White and no race-specific outcomes were reported (Yeshokumar et al., 2021). A study from the Mayo Clinic reported clinical outcomes in 8 anti-GAD65 positive SPS cases, 7 (87.5 %) of whom were White and 1 patient was Black (Clardy et al., 2013). A study from France reported that among the 46 cases of pediatric GFAP antibody positive AE, 64 % were White without description of other demographic breakdown (Gravier-Dumonceau et al., 2022). One study from the United States (U.S.) of 12 cases of voltage-gated potassium channel antibody positive AE reported 92 % White and 8 % Asian subjects in their cohort without any race-specific outcome measures (Dhamija et al., 2011).

Long-term outcomes based on race and ethnicity

Twelve studies had race-specific data beyond demographic descriptions, including 3 epidemiological studies and 9 cohort studies. Of the 9 cohort studies, 7 reported clinical outcomes in anti-NMDARE in a homogenous racial or ethnic group: 51 in Saudi Arabia (Abu Melha et al., 2024), 25 in India (Datta et al., 2021), 20 in Malaysia (Galardi et al., 2023), 111 in Han Chinese (Li et al., 2021), 17 from Thailand (Saengow and Bhudhisawasdi, 2022), 6 from the Philippines (Reyes et al., 2021), and 16 in New Zealand (Jones et al., 2017). The remaining 2 studies from France (Flet-Berliac et al., 2023) and the U.K.(Sabanathan et al., 2022) were from heterogenous populations (Table 3).

Of the 9 cohort studies, 8 reported use of acute immunotherapies (Abu Melha et al., 2024; Datta et al., 2021; Flet-Berliac et al., 2023; Galardi et al., 2023; Jones et al., 2017; Li et al., 2021; Sabanathan et al., 2022; Saengow and Bhudhisawasdi, 2022). All 8 cohorts used intravenous (IV) corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) as first line agents. Second-line immunotherapy (rituximab, cyclophosphamide) use varied among studies. Long-term outcomes measured by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) were reported in 6 studies with overall good recovery. Among 25 anti-NMDARE patients from India with average follow-up time of 7 months, 28 % had complete recovery (mRS 0 to 1), 48 % had partial recovery (mRS 2 to 3), and 16 % were disabled (mRS 4 to 5) (Datta et al., 2021). Among 20 anti-NMDAE patients evaluated in Malaysia with limited follow-up data due to the COVID-19 pandemic and regional political unrest, 87.5 % of patients had an mRS of 2 after acute immunotherapy, and no patient had an mRS >2 (Galardi et al., 2023). Among 16 anti-NMDARE patients from the Māori and Pacific Island population with mean 38 month follow-up, 67 % participants had mRS ≤ 2 at the last reported follow-up (Jones et al., 2017). The Māori population trended toward a more severe clinical course (presenting with higher mRS and requiring intensive care admission) compared to non-Māori patients. This risk may be independent of socioeconomic factors since the total number of Pacific Islander cases is still higher than expected when factoring in low socioeconomic status (Jones et al., 2017). Among a large cohort of 111 anti-NMDARE cases in Han Chinese patients with a median follow-up of 10 months (IQR 6 – 16 months), 81.7 % had mRS ≤2 at the latest follow up (Li et al., 2021). Among 6 anti-NMDARE patients from the Philippines, 83.3 % had an mRS from 0 to 2, and 16.7 % had an mRS 3–6 though follow-up time frame was not reported (Reyes et al., 2021). In a cohort of 17 anti-NMDARE cases from Thailand with a mean follow-up period of 12 months, 78.5 % of patients had complete recovery (mRS = 0) at last follow-up (Saengow and Bhudhisawasdi, 2022).

Neuropsychological outcomes

Eight studies evaluated long-term cognitive outcomes, though data on disparities is conflicting among them. Both Flet-Berliac et al. and Sabanathan et al. reported cognitive outcomes based on race and ethnicity. Flet-Berliac et al. in France conducted a retrospective study of 81 anti-NMDARE pediatric participants who underwent formal neuropsychological testing at least 2 years from disease onset testing working memory, verbal and visuospatial abilities, attention, executive function, language, and fine motor dexterity (Flet-Berliac et al., 2023). At >2 years follow-up, 45 % of the participants had persistent deficits in attention (53 %), language (30 %), memory (21 %), executive functioning (41 %), praxis (34 %), and psychomotor impairment (38 %) (Flet-Berliac et al., 2023). The authors compared the demographics for participants (White, Black, and Asian groups) with and without long-term cognitive outcomes, and found no specific racial disparities (Flet-Berliac et al., 2023).

Sabanathan et al. reported neuropsychological outcomes for 9 patients (38 %) within a multicenter study of 25 participants enrolled in the UK Childhood Neuroinflammatory Disease Network (Sabanathan et al., 2022). The median time from disease onset was 12 months. Four of the 9 were White, 2 Indian, 1 Black African, 1 mixed Asian/White, and 1 “other ethnic group.” The proportions for the group undergoing neuropsychological testing were similar to the demographics of the whole cohort. On formal testing, 89 % had below average scores in memory, reasoning, or processing speeds, and 67 % had very low scores (< 5 %ile) compared to age-normative data in memory (Sabanathan et al., 2022). Differences between racial groups were not compared.

In a prospective cross-sectional study of 23 pediatric anti-NMDARE patients in the U.S. using a standardized neuropsychological battery for executive function, memory, and language, the authors found that 92 % of the participants were non-White, which is disproportional to the general demographics of the study region (Wilkinson-Smith et al., 2022). Yang et al. reported long-term outcomes in 30 pediatric AE patients (59 % Hispanic, 16.7 % Asian, 20 % White, 6.7 % Black) – 75 % reported executive function problems, 88 % with visuospatial problems, and 63 % with memory complaints, though no race or ethnicity-specific data were reported (Yang et al., 2023). Hageboutros et al. reported neuropsychological outcomes based on the Wechsler scales for 15 anti-NMDARE cases (60 % White; no other reported demographic frequencies) – 50 % had delayed word recall, 30 % had delayed story recall, 22 % had immediate recall problems, and 25 % had visual set shifting difficulties (Hageboutros et al., 2023). No race or ethnicity-specific cognitive outcomes were reported.

Discussion

The results from this systematic review demonstrate an overall paucity of rigorous data focused on health disparities in pediatric AE. Epidemiological studies suggest a higher incidence of anti-NMDARE in non-White populations. Although most of the studies scored “fair” or “good” on quality assessment based on study design, there were significant degrees of heterogeneity in sample size and demographic reporting. Additionally, there were no studies that evaluated the individual, structural, or systemic levels of disparity that may influence clinical outcomes. The only insight into specific racial and ethnic groups were studies that selected for a particular geographic area or ethnic minority. All studies from heterogenous populations reported demographics in frequencies or reported race and ethnicity-specific incidences but did not further evaluate clinical outcomes within those groups. The 8 studies that reported acute treatments align with current practices in treating pediatric AE (Nosadini et al., 2021). The ranges for mRS outcomes in the studies with race-specific populations also align with larger multi-racial cohorts (Titulaer et al., 2013). While cognitive dysfunction persisting beyond the initial disease phase is well recognized particularly in anti-NMDARE, the epidemiological and social risk factors for neuropsychological sequelae were not well studied.

Racial and ethnic disparities in other neurological autoimmune diseases are well described in adult populations, mostly in multiple sclerosis (MS). Observational data from adult MS studies demonstrate that Black and Hispanic/Latinx patients experience greater disease burden and more severe morbidity compared to their White counterparts (Williams et al., 2023). A study utilizing electronic health record data from 810,504 Black and White people diagnosed with MS showed that poor or unstable socioeconomic status is a risk factor for MS and an indicator of worse disease burden (Briggs and Hill, 2020). A study from France examining the impact of racial and socioeconomic disparities on disease burden of 4498 adult patients living with MS showed that patients living in socioeconomically deprived areas reached disability milestones earlier and had a greater number of hospital admissions than those living in more socioeconomically secure areas (Calocer et al., 2020). Additionally, this study found that first- and second-generation African immigrants living with MS reached a higher Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) score of 4 to 6 earlier in their disease course than patients of European lineage, suggesting earlier disability accrual in the immigrant population (Calocer et al., 2020).

More recent work has found that pediatric patients may experience the same socioeconomic and racial disparities as their adult counterparts. A retrospective study of 138 pediatric onset MS (POMS) patients showed that high social vulnerability index, race/ethnicity, low parental education and public insurance were associated with worse MRI brain lesion burden (Ross et al., 2024). Another retrospective study of 31 POMS patients using census tracking as a proxy for socioeconomic status showed that Black children with POMS had higher disability scores than White children at first presentation (Poisson et al., 2024). Census tracking also showed that Black patients lived in more underprivileged areas and had significantly more hospital admissions compared to their White counterparts (Poisson et al., 2024). A study at the same center examined racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic influences on clinical outcomes in 38 children and adolescents diagnosed with pediatric aquaporin-4-positive neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (AQP4+ NMOSD). This study compared EDSS scores, hospital admissions rates, relapse rates, and other relevant clinical outcomes measures between Black and non-Hispanic White patients (Poisson et al., 2024b). The study showed that Black participants had higher disability scores, more hospital visits, longer hospitalization stays, and a trend toward longer duration between symptom onset and diagnosis compared to White participants (Poisson et al., 2024).

While prior studies have begun to identify disparities within AE populations (Alsalek et al., 2024), research on epidemiologic risk factors and health disparities in the pediatric populations is sparse. The population-based studies in this review showed that there is likely a disproportionate incidence of anti-NMDARE in Black, Asian/Pacific Islander and Hispanic groups. However, there is an overall lack of clarity on whether this observed difference in incidence translates to differences in disease outcomes. The major limitation in interpreting studies that only report race or ethnicity-specific outcomes is the focus on race and ethnicity as the sole metric for health disparity. Focusing on race and ethnicity alone can be misleading as both are social constructs different from genetic ancestry or biological risk factors (Keeys et al., 2021). Understanding of health inequities and disparities requires identification of different levels of structural, intermediary, and individual SDOH (Griffith et al., 2023). Thus, studies on health disparities ought to take these factors into account but by no means draw conclusions solely from racial and ethnic identity. For example, while API is often a heterogenous minority group in the U.S., the Han ethnic group is the major ethnicity in China. Thus, grouping these as the same race would be problematic when interpreting disease outcomes. Factoring race and ethnicity in the larger societal context is critical to determine causal inferences drawn from observed health disparities. Evaluating disparities among neuropsychological outcomes is particularly challenging in pediatric rare diseases. There is inherent bias in reporting data available only for participants who have access to formal neuropsychological testing from referrals to a pediatric neuropsychologist and accessibility to the testing center, which is often in a metropolitan academic center. Retrospective data would not be able to affirm whether the observed differences is a result of disease pathology affecting disparate populations or a consequence of multifactorial socioeconomic and/or cultural influences.

Due to the lack of adequate assessment of the full spectrum of these disparities, there is a large gap in the current understanding of how SDOH impacts long-term outcomes in pediatric AE. The influences of race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, neighborhood resources, and social networks have not been studied and remain critical research gaps. Studying SDOH impacts on disease outcomes in children is informative due to the general lack of co-existing medical comorbidities that may confound disease outcomes and closer proximity to early childhood stressors and adverse events that may reflect environmental influences on health outcomes. Determining the consequences of SDOH factors is even more important as high socioeconomic deprivation is known to be associated with increased mental health problems, lower cognitive performance, and overall worse physical health in children (Xiao et al., 2023). This can in turn significantly impact a child’s academic trajectory and quality of life and increase caregiver burden. It is likely that the drivers of disease outcomes are multifactorial, including genetic risk factors, direct consequences of neuroinflammation, and interactions between disease pathology and social/environmental risk factors. Future studies in pediatric AE should focus on clinical and treatment outcomes capturing more granular aspects of biological, environmental, and systemic risk factors to better prognosticate and develop therapeutic strategies for all children impacted by neuroinflammation.
