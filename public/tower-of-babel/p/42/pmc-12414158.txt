Effects of etomidate versus propofol for total intravenous anaesthesia on postoperative quality of recovery in patients undergoing day-case laparoscopic cholecystectomy: protocol for a multicentre, randomised controlled non-inferiority trial
Authors: Shouqiang Zhu, Mimi Wu, Xiahao Ding, Wei Zhang, Tianjiao Xia, Jinhua Bo, Xiaoping Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe choice of anaesthetic agents may influence specific aspects of postoperative recovery, such as haemodynamic stability, recovery times and the incidence of adverse events, in patients undergoing day-case laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Propofol is widely used in total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) for its favourable recovery profile, while etomidate, valued for its haemodynamic stability, is less commonly used due to concerns about adrenal suppression. This study aims to compare etomidate-based and propofol-based TIVA on postoperative quality of recovery in patients undergoing day-case laparoscopic cholecystectomy, hypothesising that etomidate is non-inferior to propofol.Methods and analysisA multicentre, double-blind, randomised controlled non-inferiority trial was conducted to compare the effects of etomidate vs propofol for TIVA on postoperative quality of recovery in patients undergoing day-case laparoscopic cholecystectomy. A total of at least 336 participants were enrolled and randomly assigned to either the etomidate or propofol group in a 1:1 ratio, stratified by site. The primary outcome was quality of recovery on postoperative day 1, quantified by the Quality of Recovery-15 questionnaire. Non-inferiority between the groups was determined using a margin of −6 points and a 95% CI. Secondary outcomes included perioperative haemodynamic stability, recovery times (eg, response to verbal commands, time to extubation and duration of postanaesthesia care unit stay), postoperative complications (eg, hypoxaemia, nausea/vomiting, delirium) and patient-reported outcomes such as pain, satisfaction and quality of sleep. Long-term outcomes included quality of life at 6 and 12 months, assessed using the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey.Ethics and disseminationThis study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanjing University Medical School, Drum Tower Hospital (No. 2024-371-04) and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry.Trial registration numberChiCTR2400087413.Keywords: Propofol, Adult anaesthesia, Hepatobiliary surgery

Introduction

Accelerating postoperative recovery is a key goal in enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols, particularly for day-case surgeries. Day-case laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC), aimed to obtain rapid discharge, alleviate suffering and improve patient satisfaction, is a common intra-abdominal surgical procedure globally.

However, it is well known that temporary side effects such as nausea, vomiting, pain, drowsiness and fatigue during the first postoperative day significantly delayed postoperative recovery. Therefore, methods of anaesthesia in an effort to improve the postoperative quality of recovery (QoR) in patients undergoing day-case LC are of great importance.1 2

Recent cohort studies have suggested that propofol-based total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) provides superior QoR compared with inhalational anaesthesia. Moreover, propofol is the most widely used agent due to its favourable recovery profile, including reduced postoperative nausea, vomiting and faster recovery times.3 4 However, propofol-based TIVA is also associated with drawbacks, such as risks of hypotension, injection pain and higher costs, which limit its utility in certain patient populations.5 6 In contrast, etomidate offers advantages in terms of haemodynamic stability, minimal respiratory depression and rapid recovery, making it a potential alternative for TIVA, especially in high-risk patients.7,9 Despite these benefits, concerns about adrenal suppression and associated outcomes, such as postoperative infections, have limited its broader adoption.9 10 Data on the comparative effects of etomidate-based versus propofol-based TIVA on postoperative QoR remains scarce. Additionally, most existing studies focus on inpatient populations or high-risk surgeries, leaving a significant gap in evidence for day-case procedures.

This study aims to address this gap by comparing the postoperative QoR of etomidate-based TIVA to propofol-based TIVA in patients undergoing day-case LC. QoR will be assessed using the validated QoR-15 questionnaire, which measures multiple dimensions of recovery, including pain, physical comfort, emotional state and psychological support.11 12 We hypothesise that etomidate-based TIVA will demonstrate non-inferiority to propofol-based TIVA in terms of recovery quality, providing a viable alternative for day-case surgical anaesthesia. This investigation seeks to provide new insights into anaesthetic choice and its impact on short-term and long-term outcomes in the context of modern ERAS protocols.

Method and analysis

Study design, approval and registration

The planned study is a multicentre, prospective, single-blinded, randomised controlled, non-inferiority trial designed to evaluate whether TIVA with etomidate is non-inferior to propofol in terms of postoperative QoR (assessed using QoR-15) in patients undergoing day-case LC. Given the differences in dosing and administration between propofol and etomidate, only outcome assessors and the data analyst were blinded to the treatment groups. The trial has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nanjing University Medical School, Drum Tower Hospital (Ethics Approval Number: 2024-371-04). The protocol follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines.13 The study is planned to start on 25 October 2024 and is expected to be completed by 31 October 2026, including patient recruitment, follow-up and data analysis.

Study aim

The primary objective of this study is to determine whether TIVA with etomidate is non-inferior to propofol in terms of postoperative QoR, as measured by the QoR-15 score on postoperative day 1, in patients undergoing day-case LC. Additionally, the study aims to evaluate the safety and comfort associated with the use of etomidate in this surgical context.

Randomisation, allocation and concealment, and blinding

Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio through a centralised electronic data capture (EDC) system. Block randomisation was stratified by center, with a fixed block size of four to ensure balanced allocation within each center. The preparation of trial drugs was carried out by a designated investigator who remained unblinded throughout the study. This individual did not participate in participant enrolment, anaesthesia administration or postoperative follow-up. The trial drugs were prepared at the following concentrations: etomidate at 2 mg/mL (20 mg/10 mL) and propofol at 10 mg/mL (200 mg/20 mL). The unblinded investigator responsible for drug preparation had no involvement in other aspects of the study, such as anaesthesia implementation, data collection or outcome evaluation.

Interventions

Participants were randomised to receive either etomidate or propofol for general anaesthesia induction and maintenance (figure 1). Anaesthesia induction was performed with an initial bolus dose of either etomidate (0.2–0.4 mg/kg) or propofol (1.5–2.5 mg/kg), followed by continuous intravenous infusion of the respective drug to maintain a bispectral index (BIS) range of 40–60.

Study flow chart. PP, per-protocol.

All patients fasted for 6–8 hours before surgery. On arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was implemented, including ECG, heart rate, non-invasive blood pressure (BP), peripheral oxygen saturation, capnography for respiratory monitoring, BIS for anaesthesia depth monitoring, and nasopharyngeal temperature for core temperature measurement. Invasive monitoring, such as direct arterial pressure or central venous pressure, was applied based on clinical requirements. Anaesthesia was induced and maintained with either etomidate or propofol according to the group allocation. Midazolam (0.015 mg/kg) and sufentanil (0.3–0.5 µg/kg) were administered intravenously during induction, followed by continuous intravenous remifentanil infusion (0.2–0.4 µg/kg/min) for maintenance. Non-depolarising muscle relaxants were used to ensure adequate muscle relaxation. At our centre, vecuronium (0.1 mg/kg, intravenous) was administered, while other centres used either vecuronium or rocuronium based on availability. A single dose of dexamethasone (5 mg) was given prophylactically to prevent postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV). Airway management involved tracheal intubation, and no other anaesthetic techniques, including nerve blocks, dexmedetomidine, nitrous oxide, scopolamine or penehyclidine, were used during the procedure. The target anaesthetic depth was maintained at a BIS of 40–60, with BP fluctuations limited to within 20% of baseline values. Active warming measures, such as a warming device or adjusted operating room temperature, were applied to maintain core temperature between 36°C and 37°C.

Rescue analgesia with sufentanil (5–10 µg per dose) was administered as needed to address inadequate pain control, and total intraoperative doses of etomidate, propofol, sufentanil, remifentanil and muscle relaxants were recorded. Before the end of surgery, a 5-HT₃ receptor antagonist could also be administered for additional PONV prophylaxis if deemed necessary, and it was used as rescue therapy to manage PONV when it occurred, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs were used as adjunctive analgesics. Neuromuscular blockade was reversed as necessary, and flumazenil was administered to reverse sedation if clinically indicated. Following surgery, patients were extubated in the post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU), with discharge from the PACU determined by predefined recovery criteria assessed by the attending anaesthesiologist.

Data collection and management

Data were collected prospectively at multiple time points throughout the study to ensure comprehensive coverage of preoperative, intraoperative and postoperative variables. Participants were screened in the day-case surgery assessment centre based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Authorised research staff, including physicians or nurses familiar with the protocol, conducted visits in a quiet environment, ideally with family members present. The study’s purpose, methods, potential benefits and risks were thoroughly explained to participants and their representatives, allowing sufficient time for consideration before obtaining written informed consent.

Preoperative data included demographics and baseline characteristics: name, gender, birth date, education level, years of education and body mass index. Medical history data included comorbidities, medication use, smoking and alcohol history, allergies, and surgical or anaesthetic history. Clinical assessments comprised New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification, Charlson Comorbidity Index, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status (I–II required for inclusion), Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) score, cognitive status [assessed using the Mini–Mental State Examination (MMSE)], sleep quality [Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)], and pain severity [Numeric Rating Scale (NRS)].

Detailed intraoperative information was collected, including study drug administration (dosage and duration); anaesthetic details such as preoperative medications, induction and maintenance drugs with dosages, and total anaesthesia time; surgical parameters including procedure type, surgical site and duration; and mechanical ventilation parameters such as minimum oxygen saturation. Haemodynamic and oxygenation data—BP, heart rate (HR) and SpO2—were measured at specific time points (eg, 5 min before induction, 1 min after induction, 1 min after intubation and surgical start). Baseline BP was defined as the mean of measurements taken 5 min before and immediately prior to induction, with BP variability defined as deviations exceeding 20% of baseline. Hypoxia was defined as SpO2 <90%. Fluid management data included blood loss, transfusion volume (with storage time) and fluid balance. Emergence agitation was defined as any Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS) score ≥+1 from anaesthetic cessation to departure from the operating room. Intraoperative adverse events recorded included the incidence of hypotension, hypertension, hypoxia and use of vasoactive drugs. Awareness assessment was performed using the modified Brice interview to evaluate intraoperative awareness and drowsiness.

Data were collected and synchronised across multiple centres using a proprietary Medical Data platform (https://login.medbit.cn/pdmsApp/CaseMgr) to ensure consistency and reliability. The platform incorporated automated quality checks to minimise errors and ensure high data integrity.

Postoperative endpoints and safety outcomes

Primary postoperative endpoint

The primary short-term endpoint is the postoperative recovery quality on day 1 (QoR-15). QoR-15 is a globally recognised measure for assessing postoperative recovery, focusing on patient-centred outcomes (online supplemental figure). It is a validated scale with excellent reliability, responsiveness and clinical acceptability.14 The QoR-15 score ranges from 0 (poor recovery) to 150 (excellent recovery), with scores ≥118 indicating good recovery. In this study, patients will be evaluated using the QoR-15 scale between 8:00 and 10:00 on postoperative day 1.

Sample size estimates and statistical analysis

A seasoned epidemiologist with extensive experience in designing, conducting and analysing clinical trials will oversee the statistical analysis. This individual will remain blinded to the specific interventions and will not be involved in patient management.

Data will be electronically stored using a web-based case report form and analysed using the SAS V.9.4. Missing data for the primary outcome will not be imputed, while other critical missing covariates will be appropriately imputed. This study is designed as a non-inferiority comparison between two groups: the experimental group receiving etomidate-based TIVA and the control group receiving propofol-based TIVA. The primary endpoint is the postoperative recovery quality, measured by the QoR-15 score.

Based on prior literature and pilot studies, the SD is assumed to be 15,12 14 18 and the non-inferiority margin is set at 6.19 Statistical parameters include a power of 0.9, a one-sided type I error (alpha) of 0.025, and a 1:1 allocation ratio between the groups. Using PASS V.15.0 software, the minimum required sample size is calculated to be 133 per group. Considering an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, the final sample size is determined to be at least 168 participants per group, totalling 336 participants.

Statistical analyses will be performed using SAS V.9.4 software. The primary analysis will be conducted on the Full Analysis Set (FAS), which is consistent with the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle and includes all randomised participants who received at least one dose of study medication and had available outcome data. Missing data will be addressed using appropriate imputation methods (eg, multiple imputation if >5%). A per-protocol (PP) analysis will also be performed, including only those participants who completed the intervention as assigned without major protocol violations, to assess the consistency of the findings. Statistical significance will be determined at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables will be reported as means, SD, medians, IQRs, and minimum and maximum values, while categorical variables will be summarised as frequencies and percentages. The method of analysis for between-group comparisons will depend on the type of data, using independent t-tests (for quantitative data with homogeneity of variance and normal distribution), Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (for non-normally distributed quantitative data) and χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests (for categorical data).

The enrolment status and demographic characteristics of the study population across centres will be summarised, including the list of dropouts and excluded cases, along with comparisons of the number of subjects in each group and centre. The reasons for dropout and non-completion will be detailed. The baseline analysis will be conducted using the full analysis set (FAS) and will include demographic information, medical history and other relevant baseline characteristics. The statistical methods for comparing the two groups will follow the general principles outlined above.

The primary outcome will be the postoperative QoR-15 score on day 1. Descriptive statistics will be used to summarise the scores, and between-group comparisons will be performed using either the independent t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on the normality of the data distribution. Non-inferiority testing will be used to compare the QoR-15 scores on day 1 between the experimental group (etomidate TIVA) and the control group (propofol TIVA). The null hypothesis for the non-inferiority test is as follows:

H0: μT−μC≤−6

H1:μT−μC>−6

Where μT and μC represent the estimated mean QoR-15 scores of the experimental and control groups, respectively, and −6 is the non-inferiority margin. A 95% CI for the difference in scores between the two groups will be calculated. Non-inferiority will be concluded if the lower bound of the 95% CI is greater than −6 in both the FAS and PP set (PPS).

Secondary outcomes will be analysed using both the FAS and the PPS. For binary outcomes, generalised linear models will be used, with appropriate distributions for the data. For continuous variables, linear regression or quartile regression will be applied, depending on the skewness of the distribution. All models will be adjusted for stratification variables, including ASA score, anaesthesia time and surgery duration. For repeated measures of continuous variables, repeated measures analysis of variance will be employed. Subgroup analyses will be conducted based on ASA classification, anaesthesia time and surgery time. The primary outcome will be analysed using a two-sided significance level of 0.05. Secondary outcomes will be considered exploratory, and no formal multiplicity adjustment will be made unless otherwise specified. To address the risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons, we will: Report all secondary outcome p values with 95% CIs. Interpret findings cautiously, especially for outcomes not strongly supported by prior evidence. Apply the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction for sets of related outcomes (eg, adverse events, QoR scores), where applicable.

This thorough monitoring ensures the integrity and quality of the study.

Ethical considerations

The administration of etomidate and propofol for (TIVA) in this study is considered clinically safe and will be performed by trained anaesthesiologists experienced in advanced anaesthesia techniques. To minimise any potential complications, including respiratory or cardiovascular issues, patients will be continuously monitored throughout the procedure. The administration of these agents will adhere to established safety guidelines to prevent any adverse reactions. Although etomidate has been associated with a transient suppression of adrenal function, this effect is typically short-lived and reversible upon discontinuation. Propofol, commonly used in clinical practice, is well-tolerated, with side effects being rare when administered according to standard protocols.

This study will be conducted in full accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. It will adhere to the International Conference on Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Registration of Pharmaceuticals for Human Use–Good Clinical Practice guidelines, as well as relevant regulations by the China National Drug Administration. The collected data are essential for evaluating the efficacy, safety and overall impact of TIVA on the postoperative recovery of patients undergoing LC. Patient confidentiality will be rigorously maintained in compliance with applicable laws and regulations, ensuring protection of personal data. Written informed consent (online supplemental material) will be obtained in line with the Declaration of Helsinki, applicable laws and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Participants have the right to withdraw from the study at any point, at which stage all their data will be excluded from further analysis. All personal information will be anonymised before statistical analysis to ensure privacy and confidentiality.

Discussion

The significance of this randomised controlled trial lies in its ability to provide evidence-based guidance for anaesthesiologists in selecting optimal anaesthetic agents for improving postoperative QoR in day-case LC. As day-case surgeries necessitate rapid recovery and minimal postoperative complications to facilitate early discharge, the findings of this study could play a pivotal role in shaping perioperative management strategies. By directly comparing the effects of etomidate and propofol in this specific surgical context, the trial addresses a critical knowledge gap, offering insights that could refine anaesthetic practices and enhance patient outcomes. This research is especially relevant in the current healthcare environment, where minimising hospital stays and optimising resource utilisation are priorities.

The administration of TIVA with either etomidate or propofol provides a valuable strategy for optimising perioperative care in patients undergoing day-case LC. Both anaesthetics are well-established for their safety and efficacy profiles; however, their distinct pharmacological characteristics may influence postoperative recovery outcomes. The ability to deliver stable haemodynamic control with etomidate, alongside its non-inferiority to propofol in terms of recovery quality, represents a significant advantage in this surgical context. Should the findings of our study confirm that etomidate is non-inferior to propofol based on the QoR-15 scores, this would underscore its viability as an alternative to propofol, particularly in patients at risk for haemodynamic instability. Furthermore, the implications of our study extend beyond the direct comparison of these agents; they provide insights into refining perioperative management strategies that prioritise rapid recovery, minimal adverse events and safe discharge protocols in day-case surgeries. These results could inform evidence-based anaesthesia practices and ultimately improve patient outcomes in routine clinical settings.

The findings of Lu et al9 in the EPIC trial provide a pivotal foundation for evaluating the safety and efficacy of etomidate in older surgical patients. Their demonstration of non-inferiority between etomidate and propofol for anaesthesia induction and maintenance, particularly concerning major in-hospital complications, underscores the potential applicability of etomidate in broader surgical settings. Building on these insights, our study aims to assess the effects of etomidate and propofol TIVA on postoperative recovery quality in patients undergoing day-case LC. By focusing on a non-inferiority framework and a distinct surgical context, our investigation seeks to expand the evidence base, with specific attention to postoperative recovery quality and the unique demands of outpatient surgery.

The haemodynamic stability of etomidate during induction of anaesthesia has been consistently demonstrated in prior studies, particularly in cardiac surgery patients. Soleimani et al21 and Bendel et al,22 using blinded, randomised designs, reported that etomidate induction was associated with significantly less reduction in mean arterial pressure (MAP) compared with propofol, findings corroborated by other trials in both cardiac and non-cardiac surgical populations. Notably, propofol-induced reductions in MAP ranged from 15% to 15 mm Hg at single time points after induction, while Soleimani et al observed increased vasopressor use in the propofol group, further underscoring its impact on haemodynamic stability. Importantly, a more robust approach by Bendel et al, incorporating baseline MAP and continuous analysis over a 10 min period postinduction, revealed that propofol led to a 34% greater reduction in MAP area below baseline compared with etomidate, a clinically meaningful difference that supports the prevailing notion of etomidate’s superior haemodynamic profile. In our study, which examines the effects of etomidate and propofol TIVA on postoperative QoR in day-case LC, these findings on haemodynamic stability are particularly relevant. Day-case surgery demands not only effective anaesthesia but also rapid and uncomplicated recovery, where intraoperative haemodynamic stability plays a critical role. Etomidate’s ability to minimise haemodynamic fluctuations may contribute to improved intraoperative management and potentially faster postoperative recovery, aligning with our objective of evaluating recovery quality. By building on this established evidence, our study will provide further insights into how the choice of induction agents impacts both intraoperative stability and postoperative outcomes in outpatient surgical settings.

This study employs a pragmatic trial design to minimise disruptions to routine clinical practice while ensuring broad applicability of the findings. Flexibility in the selection of anaesthetic agents, anaesthetic techniques and modes of respiratory management was allowed to better reflect real-world clinical settings. This approach not only enhances the external validity of the study but also permits subgroup analyses to explore the potential impact of these variables on postoperative recovery quality. If significant differences are observed between groups, adjustments will be made through sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of the results. Moreover, no restrictions were placed on patient demographics or minor surgical variations, making the findings generalisable to a wider surgical population undergoing day-case LC. Given the focus on improving postoperative recovery quality, this design facilitates a more comprehensive understanding of how etomidate and propofol influence recovery outcomes in diverse clinical scenarios. The trial’s practical orientation ensures relevance for anaesthesiologists, providing evidence-based insights to guide anaesthetic management in similar settings.

Strengths and limitations

One potential limitation of this study, which can also be viewed as a strength, is the absence of a strictly standardised anaesthetic protocol regarding the selection of specific anaesthetic agents, intraoperative fluid management and ventilatory strategies. Instead, the choice of anaesthetic agents and management approaches was based on the clinical judgement of the anaesthesiologists and the best available practices at each participating centre. This flexibility in treatment protocols enhances the external validity of our study by reflecting real-world clinical practices and the variability in anaesthetic techniques commonly encountered in surgical settings. However, this variability may introduce potential confounding factors, limiting our ability to draw definitive conclusions about the individual effects of the anaesthetic agents used. Despite this, our approach provides valuable insights into the broader application of anaesthetic strategies in clinical practice.

Another limitation relates to the potential for selection bias in our patient cohort. Given that only patients undergoing LC were included, the findings may not be directly applicable to other types of surgeries, particularly those involving higher risks or different physiological challenges. However, we have designed this study as a pragmatic, multicentre trial that can potentially be generalised to a wide range of patients within the scope of day-case surgery. Furthermore, the trial’s design accommodates the possibility of variations in surgical techniques across sites, which enhances the real-world applicability of the results.

Additionally, while the study aimed for non-inferiority between etomidate and propofol in terms of postoperative recovery quality, there remains a possibility of residual confounding due to the lack of strict control over all perioperative variables. Although the primary endpoint, the QoR-15 score, is a validated measure of postoperative recovery, the influence of unmeasured factors, such as patient comorbidities and intraoperative complications, could affect the generalisability of our findings. However, by stratifying randomisation and adjusting for known confounders, we have minimised potential biases and aimed to provide a robust comparison between the two anaesthetic agents.

Despite these limitations, this study has significant strengths. The multicentre design ensures a broad range of patient characteristics and clinical settings, enhancing the generalisability of the findings. Moreover, by investigating the non-inferiority of etomidate compared to propofol for day-case LC, we provide crucial evidence that can inform clinical practice regarding the selection of anaesthetic agents in this specific patient population. Furthermore, the flexible and pragmatic design ensures that the study reflects current clinical practices and provides insights that are applicable in a variety of settings.
