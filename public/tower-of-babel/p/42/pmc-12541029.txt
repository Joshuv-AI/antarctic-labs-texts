Prediction of the short-term prognosis of acute ischaemic stroke in patients with high treatment platelet reactivity using explainable machine learning
Authors: Jiaming Liu, Yumeng Gu, Dongliang Wang, Xiaoshuang Xia, Xin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study is to establish and validate an optimal explainable prediction model based on a machine learning (ML) approach to predict the short-term prognosis in high on-treatment platelet reactivity (HTPR) individuals with acute ischaemic stroke (AIS). Using individual basic characteristics, blood test indices, and the CYP2C19 genotype, a model to predict a poor functional prognosis (modified Rankin scale score ≥ 3) was constructed based on ML models, including logistic regression, support vector machine, decision tree, random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting, and light gradient boosting machine. On this basis, global and local interpretability techniques were used to interpret selected ML models and explore the risk factors affecting the short-term prognosis of AIS in patients with HTPR. In this study, the performance of the model was futher evaluated through sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis. A total of 515 AIS patients with HTPR were retrospectively enrolled, and approximately 129 (25%) had a poor outcome in the short term. Among the 6 ML models, RF performed best in discriminative ability in terms of area under the curve (0.84 [0.71–0.97]), accuracy (0.80 [0.71–0.89]), and precision (0.71 [0.61–0.81], which are far superior to the other models. Interpretability techniques showed that high levels of diastolic blood pressure, blood urea nitrogen, homocysteine, C-reactive protein, white blood cells, and CYP2C19 poor metabolizers were significant predictors of a poor prognosis of AIS in patients with HTPR. The risk prediction model for AIS patients with HTPR based on RF algorithms has high predictive power. Introduction

Stroke, the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability worldwide, has long been a major challenge to public health1. Acute ischaemic stroke (AIS) accounts for more than 87% of strokes2, and owing to its rapid onset, rapid progression, and difficulty in prediction, it is associated with high mortality and disability3. Arterial thrombosis caused by platelet adhesion and aggregation is a key step in the development of AIS4. Antiplatelet agents, such as clopidogrel, are commonly used to reduce the risk of AIS by inhibiting platelet aggregation5. However, the inhibition of platelets among different individuals might be limited or variable, which has been associated with high on-treatment platelet reactivity (HTPR)6. The estimated HTPR for the Asian population is 17.2–81.6%7. HTPR is influenced by multiple factors, including genetic polymorphisms and drug interactions; however, the mechanism remains poorly understood, and research on the prognosis, diagnosis, and treatment of AIS in HTPR patients is limited8–10.

Real-time and early estimates of the prognostic status of AIS can be used to assist physicians in developing targeted care and treatment programs. Traditional methods such as logistic regression (LR) and Cox models often struggle with nonlinear relationships and imbalanced data, which are common in clinical settings. In contrast, machine learning (ML) algorithms can handle large, heterogeneous datasets and learn from them to improve prediction accuracy11. Despite their success in predicting AIS outcomes12–14, most existing studies focus on the general population, without addressing the unique challenges posed by patients with HTPR.

Motivated by the above discussion, an ML algorithm for predicting the short-term prognosis of AIS in patients with HTPR is proposed in this work. By using clinical data from 515 patients, we constructed and validated six ML models, including LR, support vector machine (SVM), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM) models, and evaluate them on the basis of multiple performance metrics. Additionally, this study integrated interpretability techniques to help clinicians understand the impact of predictive factors on individual patient outcomes, providing valuable insights for personalized treatment strategies, as shown in Fig. 1.

Materials and methods

In this section, the pipeline of our prediction method for the short-term prognosis of AIS in patients with HTPR using ML algorithms is introduced. The implementation process of our prediction method is shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S1 and consists of four sequential flows, namely, the feature preprocessing layer, data preprocessing layer, model training layer, and analysis layer. Cooperation of the above processes can achieve high-precision prediction of the short-term prognosis of AIS in patients with HTPR and provide specific analysis references for various influencing factors in AIS prognostication.

Feature preprocessing layer

Participants and study design

The baseline variables and clinical outcome registry data for AIS patients admitted to the Second Hospital of Tianjin Medical University from April 2019 to August 2021 were used in this study. In the data used, all patients were older than 40 years and were tested for adenosine diphosphate-induced platelet aggregation and the CYP2C19 genotype at admission. Notably, platelet function testing results were obtained from blood samples collected 7 days after clopidogrel administration for platelet aggregation testing. In addition, the critical threshold for HTPR was defined as 5 mM ADP-induced platelet aggregation > 42.9%15. The CYP2C19 genotype was investigated by extracting DNA from the patient’s venous blood, performing polymerase chain reaction amplification, detecting mutation sites, and visualizing DNA bands using agarose gel electrophoresis.

To make the experimental research more scientific, it was necessary to partially screen the original data. Patient records meeting the following criteria were removed: (1) AIS cases attributed to cardiogenic causes. (2) Missing modified Rankin scale (mRS) score at 2 or 3 months pre-stroke. (3) Patients who had taken antiplatelet aggregation drugs within the past 14 days or were currently undergoing anticoagulation treatment and intravenous thrombolysis. (4) Patients with a history of intracranial haemorrhage, coagulation disorders, or thrombocytopenia. (5) Patients with severe renal or hepatic insufficiency. Finally, the remaining data that met the usage criteria included information on 515 AIS patients.

Feature factor statistics and filtering

The 42 feature factors included in the model analysis were identified by combining clinical experience, previous literature reports, and data collection (missing values < 30%) for this study, including individual basic features, blood testing indicators, and CYP2C19 genotypes. The CYP2C19 genotypes were then selected and classified according to CYP2C19 loss-of-function alleles16. The specific classification principle is as follows: (1) Normal metabolizers (NMs) are CYP2C19 * 1/* 1. (2) Intermediate metabolizers (IMs) include CYP2C19 * 1/* 2 and CYP2C19 * 1/* 3. (3) Poor metabolizers (PMs) include CYP2C19 * 2/* 2, CYP2C19* 2/* 3, and CYP2C19 * 3/* 3. Moreover, a 3-month follow-up was completed through telephone or outpatient clinic visits. The patient outcome was divided into two groups on the basis of the mRS score of the patient 3 months after AIS onset: (1) good outcomes (mRS < 3). and (2) poor outcomes (mRS ≥ 3).

On the other hand, to ensure that the high correlation of multiple feature factors does not lead to a decrease in the accuracy of AIS prognostication, it is often necessary to use correlation analysis to test the independence of the studied feature factors. This study used the common Pearson correlation analysis method, specifically the use of Pearson correlation coefficients for actual screening. It is generally believed that when the Pearson correlation coefficient is > 0.6, there is a high correlation between the selected feature factors17. Therefore, this study conducted a Pearson analysis on the selected feature factors, removed features with collinear threats, and finally screened a total of 38 features, the specific results of which are shown in Fig. 3.

Data preprocessing layer

Data preprocessing is a necessary condition for ML model training. In this work, data preprocessing involved a series of processes such as feature factor screening, incomplete data supplementation, data variable encoding, data normalization, and balancing positive and negative sample data. The specific details are described in the subchapters.

Data cleaning

The purpose of data cleaning is to remove outliers and smooth out noisy data. In this work, it is believed that outliers are values that are far from the rest of the values. Specifically, outliers are checked through the interquartile range (IQR), and only data points located between the Q1-1.5 IQR and the Q3 + 1.5 IQR were considered for preservation, whereas other data were subjected to nonzero processing.

Incomplete data supplementation

Owing to the incomplete data collection process and abnormal data cleaning, there were missing data values, which will affect subsequent implementation (Supplementary Table S2). Therefore, the mean-filling method was utilized in this study, which uses the mean of the column data to fill in missing parts. A sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation as an alternative imputation method was also conducted (Supplemental Fig. S6).

Data variable encoding and data normalization

The discontinuity of certain feature data, such as gender and medical history, can affect the processing of ML model training. Ordinal categorical variables were coded as integers. Categorical variables that were not ordinal were coded using one-hot encoding (Supplementary Table S3). Continuous variables remained continuous. In addition, because of the differences in the magnitude of feature data expression, the accuracy of ML prediction models trained on raw data is low. The numerical variables were normalized with the standard deviation.

Balancing positive and negative sample data

After the data were processed and analysed, approximately 389 patients were found to have a good outcome, which was much lower than the number of patients with a poor outcome. The imbalance between these data classes is a typical problem in practice and can result in biased algorithms that perform poorly on the minority class18. This occurs because ML models may learn to classify the majority class correctly while ignoring or incorrectly classifying the minority class19. Therefore, to address this issue and improve the effectiveness of the model, the Tomek Links method was ultimately chosen to resample the data in this study. In addition, the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) and random undersampling (RUS) were also used.

Machine learning model training layer

We developed and compared 6 MLs to predict the AIS prognostic status, namely, LR, SVM, DT, RF, XGBoost, and LightGBM20. LR is a generalized linear regression analysis model that converts the predicted values obtained from linear regression through a sigmoid function to obtain classification probability values. LR is simple and easy to understand, implement, and interpret. SVM is a binary classification model based on statistical learning theory, which completes the classification task of data by finding the optimal hyperplane. The advantages of SVM include good performance in high-dimensional space, suitability for small sample datasets, and the ability to handle nonlinear problems. The core idea of DT is to divide the dataset into subsets with increasing purity through a series of judgement conditions until the stopping condition is met. Its advantages include ease of understanding and interpretation, ability to handle nonlinear relationships, insensitivity to missing and outliers, etc. RF is an ensemble learning algorithm that improves overall prediction accuracy and stability by constructing multiple decision trees and combining their prediction results. This approach also compensates for the shortcomings of overfitting in a single DT and reduces variance. XGBoost is an efficient gradient-boosting decision tree algorithm that iteratively adds weak learners to gradually optimize the model. XGBoost performs well when processing structured data and large-scale data datasets, with efficiency, accuracy, and flexibility. LightGBM is a distributed gradient-boosting framework based on DT algorithms. Its capabilities include fast, efficient, low memory usage, high accuracy, and support for parallel and large-scale data processing.

In addition, to optimize the performance of the models, we used cross-validation (CV) to search for and adjust the parameters separately for each model. First, for each parameter, iterative debugging was carried out within its possible value range, and a learning curve graph was used to assist in selecting the optimal parameter value, to achieve local optimization of model performance. We subsequently repeated this process for each parameter in the model to determine the optimal range of values for each parameter. We subsequently employed a 5-fold CV combined with a grid search method to explore and determine the optimal parameter combination for the model systematically. This method can comprehensively evaluate the performance of the model under different parameter combinations by dividing the dataset into five parts and using different subsets of data for training and validation multiple times. By conducting 5-fold CV tests on the training set, we can effectively avoid overfitting issues in the model and evaluate its stability. Furthermore, decision curve analysis (DCA)21 was used to assess the performance of the model.

Feature analysis layer

The Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) method is a game theory method used to rank the importance of input features and explain the prediction results of MLs. Specifically, SHAP allocates “benefits” by calculating the marginal contribution of each feature to model predictions. This marginal contribution is calculated considering all possible combinations of features, thus reflecting the interactions between features. The calculation of SHAP values takes into account all possible combinations of features rather than just the influence of individual features, thus providing a more comprehensive and accurate explanation. Unlike other techniques, SHAP stands out for its ability to offer both local interpretability and global interpretability concurrently while providing a solid theoretical foundation.

In addition, local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) are also locally interpretable models whose core idea is to use a trained local surrogate model to interpret individual samples. Owing to its independent structure, LIME can be applied to any ML classifier and can determine which variables affect the degree and direction of each prediction in the model or which variable has a greater impact on the outcome of each prediction than the other variables do.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were implemented with SPSS 25.0 and Python (version 3.9.1). Numerical variables conforming to a normal distribution are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, and intergroup differences were evaluated using independent samples t tests; those not conforming to a normal distribution are expressed as the median and interquartile range, and intergroup differences were assessed by the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical variables are represented as percentages, and intergroup differences were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A P value < 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered statistically significant.

Model performance and evaluation

Receiver operating characteristic curves were selected to represent the results of model training, as shown in Fig. 4a. The area under the curve (AUC) of the six MLs is above average, indicating that they are effective models. The RF model performed best (AUC = 0.84 [0.71–0.97]).

Comparison of performance evaluation indicators for 6 MLs. (a) ROC curves of the MLs. (b) AUC, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score of the ML model with varied numbers of features. (c) Confusion matrices across all algorithms.

For model evaluation, metrics such as accuracy, recall, precision, the F1 score, the precision‒recall curve and the confusion matrix were used. The XGBoost model achieved the highest accuracy (0.82 [0.73–0.91]), followed by the RF model (0.80 [0.71–0.89]), with the LR model having the lowest accuracy (0.69 [0.59–0.79]). RF performed best in terms of precision (0.71 [0.61–0.81]), followed by XGBoost (0.7 [0.6–0.8]), with the other models having lower results. The XGBoost and LightGBM models had the highest recall (0.47 [0.37–0.57]), whereas the RF and DT models had the lowest recall (0.33 [0.24–0.42]). The top three models in terms of the F1 score were XGBoost (0.56 [0.45–0.67]), LightGBM (0.52 [0.41–0.63]), and RF (0.45 [0.35–0.55]). The F1 scores of the other models ranged from 0.3 to 0.4. Detailed information is shown in Fig. 4b and Supplementary Table S4. RF had the highest average precision value (AP = 0.73, Supplementary Figure S1). The confusion matrix indicates that the RF model performed significantly better than the other models in identifying true positive and true negatives. The DCA demonstrated net benefits for all the models within the probability threshold range of 0.5–0.8 (Supplementary Figure S2), confirming their clinical utility and robustness across different probability thresholds.

The sensitivity analysis results revealed that for the SMOTE (AUC = 0.85 [0.78–0.92], AP = 0.87) and RUS (AUC = 0.79 [0.68–0.89], AP = 0.64) methods, the RF still performed well (Supplementary Figure S3–4).The recall rate has been increased to 0.75 (Supplementary Table S5). Overall, on the basis of the AUC and evaluation metrics of these models, the best-performing model is RF, followed by XGBoost and LightGBM, and the worst-performing model is LR. Therefore, the subsequent analysis of the interpretability of the eigenvalues in this study focuses on the RF model.

Interpretability results of the model

For the RF model with the best-predicted performance among the 6 MLs, global interpretability techniques at the feature level (feature importance22, partial dependence plots (PDPs)23 and individual conditional expectation plots (ICEs)24 and local interpretability techniques at the individual level (LIME and SHAP values) were used to interpret the prediction results.

Feature importance

SHAP analysis is a method used to explain the predictions of black-box models such as ML, with its core idea originating from Shapley values in cooperative game theory25. SHAP plots show the contribution of each patient to the variable outcome in the modelling data, with dots of different colours representing a single patient, red representing a high risk of a poor outcome, and blue representing a low risk. Therefore, according to the SHAP summary plots derived from the RF model (Fig. 5a and b), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), BUN, homocysteine (HCY), C-reactive protein (CRP), WBC, and the CYP2C19 genotype were the strongest predictors. As shown in Fig. 5, the classification of NM and IM had a positive effect on AIS patients with HTPR. A 5-fold cross-validation was performed to assess SHAP-based feature importance. The results showed consistent ranking of key features (including DBP, BUN, HCY, CRP, WBC, and genotype) across all folds, further validating the reliability of the model interpretation (Supplementary Table S6, Supplementary Figure S5).

Partial dependence plots and individual conditional expectation plots

The top 6 most important features were selected for analysis. Figure 6 shows the PDPs and ICEs of the CRP, DBP, BUN, WBC, HCY, and CYP2C19 genotype on the risk of a poor outcome. The light blue thin line represents the ICE of poor outcome risk probability for an individual, which is based on 90 randomly selected samples. For example, higher levels of HCY are associated with higher mRS scores and a worse outcome, especially when HCY exceeds 27 µmol/L, where the mRS score increases substantially. In addition, when DBP approaches 80 mmHg, the mRS increases and then stabilizes, with DBP = 78 mmHg being an important marker for predicting AIS prognosis. For the CYP2C19 genotype, PMs tend to have a poor outcome, whereas NMs and IMs are associated with good outcomes.

Local interpretability techniques

LIME and SHAP are instance-based interpreters, and the following evaluation is based on two randomly selected instances from the test dataset. One instance was correctly predicted as a ‘poor prognosis’ in the TP group, and the other was correctly predicted as a ‘good prognosis’ in the TN group.

Figure 7a shows that this patient has a 90% probability of being classified in the ‘poor prognosis’ according to the prediction model. The values of CRP, WBC, BUN, DBP, and HCY pushed the decision towards a ‘poor prognosis’, whereas NMs decreased the probability of a poor outcome. Figure 7b, which uses Shapley values, also indicates that CRP, WBC, BUN, DBP, and HCY increase the risk of a poor AIS outcome, while NMs decreases the risk of a poor outcome.

Local model explanation by the SHAP and LIME method. (a-d) Waterfall plot and evolution of risks contributed by each feature for HTPR individual at high (a, b) or low (c, d) risk of poor prognosis.

The correctly predicted TN cases are shown in Fig. 7c and d, with HCY = 1.4 µmol/L, WBC = 5 × 109/L, CRP = 0.27 mg/L, BUN = 5.4 mmol/L, and gene classification as NMs. Figure 7c shows that this patient has a 94% probability of ‘good prognosis’. Both the LIME and Shapley values indicate explain that low HCY and NMs increase the probability of a good outcome.

Additionally, sensitivity analysis was performed using multiple imputation as an alternative method. The results indicated that with multiple imputation, the predictive performance (AUC = 0.83 [0.73–0.94]) and interpretability of the RF model were similar to those of the original results (Supplementary Figure S6). The most important features were WBC, HCY, DBP, CRP, and CYP2C19 genotype, although there were some variations in feature importance rankings under different imputation methods. Moreover, the importance factor of using the SMOTE and RUS methods remains consistent with the results of the aforementioned methods (Supplementary Figure S7–12).

Performance of the RF model under different subgroups

The diagnostic performance of the RF model in specific patient populations was determined through subgroup analysis. The performance of the RF model in predicting the prognosis of AIS in patients with HTPR across different subgroups (sex, CYP2C19 genotype, hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, atrial fibrillation, and myocardial infarction) was studied. Consistent findings across different subgroups were observed, demonstrating the universality and stability of the RF model. Notably, the highest AUC (0.87 [0.79–0.95]) was shown by the RF model in diabetic patients (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

The ML algorithm is a data-driven intelligent algorithm that makes comprehensive predictions or decisions by learning data patterns. Compared with the traditional regression method, the ML algorithm can extract the key features of complex datasets more accurately and solve the problems of multivariable interactions and collinearity more effectively. In this study, we developed and validated 6 MLs for predicting the short-term functional prognosis of AIS in patients with HTPR. The diagnostic prediction model exhibited accuracy in both the training and validation datasets and remained stable across different groups. In terms of the predicted values, the RF model typically has a high AUC of 0.84. These findings indicate the accuracy and stability of the model in predicting the prognostic status of AIS. Furthermore, the confusion matrix was used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the RF model. Our research suggests that the RF model has the potential to predict the 3-month functional prognosis of AIS in patients with HTPR, which may aid clinicians in quickly assessing disease severity for further examination and assisting in treatment decision-making.

In previous studies, the application of ML models in predicting the prognosis of AIS has been widely discussed, with many studies reporting the success of using RF and XGBoost models. One ML model that was developed to predict early outcomes of AIS showed that the RF model performed well, with an AUC of approximately 0.8026. Similar results were achieved in our study, with an AUC of 0.84, further validating the effectiveness and reliability of the model in predicting AIS prognosis. In addition, Zuo et al. used a similar approach to predict environmental exposure factors in stroke patients and employed the SHAP method to interpret the model and assess the contributions of different features27. Similarly, the interpretability of our model’s predictions was also emphasized, ensuring transparency and providing strong support for clinical decision-making. Although the recall rate and F1 score in the external validation cohort were low, this might be attributed to the lower prevalence of AIS patients with HTPR in this group, who had a good functional outcome in the short term. After SMOTE sensitivity analysis, the AUC and AP values of the model improved, indicating the robustness of the random forest model. Unfortunately, the SMOTE method is an oversampling technique that amplifies the data volume of patients with poor outcomes, which may result in the training samples losing the authenticity of the original data samples. Therefore, to make the model more closely match the features reflected in real sample data, the Tomek Links method is still used.

To help doctors understand the underlying mechanisms linking various clinical features and disease occurrence, an interpretable analysis of the optimal ML predictive model, the RF model, was used in this work. The conclusion that DBP, BUN, HCY, CRP, and WBC levels are important predictive factors for poor prognosis was demonstrated through global feature importance analysis. Notably, the CYP2C19 genotype was also an important factor affecting the prognosis of AIS in patients with HTPR.

The CYP2C19 genotype, particularly in PMs, is considered to play a negative role in influencing the prognosis of AIS in individuals with HTPR, primarily because of its close relationship with drug metabolism. In the metabolism of drugs, the conversion of certain medications, such as clopidogrel, is mediated by the protein encoded by the CYP2C19 gene. Specifically, clopidogrel is a prodrug that requires biotransformation to an active metabolite by metabolizing the enzyme CYP2C1928. The genes encoding CYP2C19 are polymorphic, and among the genetic variants of CYP2C19, CYP2C19*2 and *3 are known as LoF, leading to reduced enzymatic function, which directly diminishes antiplatelet effects in clopidogrel­treated patients6. In CYP2C19 PMs receiving clopidogrel treatment, platelet inhibition is reduced, resulting in an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events. Therefore, the metabolic status of CYP2C19 has been recognized as an important factor in personalized treatment. For AIS patients, understanding their CYP2C19 genotype can help optimize antiplatelet therapy, ensuring that the medication effectively achieves its intended outcome and reduces the risk of thrombosis and stroke recurrence.

In addition, BUN, HCY, CRP, and WBC are the laboratory indicators. HCY is a recognized risk factor for stroke with HTPR. A cohort study of 5935 patients with hypertension revealed that the risk of stroke in patients with HCY > 30 µmol/L is 4.96 times greater than that in patients with HCY < 15 µmol/L30. In this study, the calculated SHAP values confirmed that HCY values exceeding 27 µmol/L were associated with an increased risk of a poor outcome of AIS in patients with HTPR. HCY levels play a crucial role in oxidative stress, which damages endothelial cells and leads to dysfunction, increasing vascular permeability and promoting thrombus formation29. HCY enhances the expression of thrombus-regulating factors, activates protein C and coagulation factors XII and V, and increases the production of thromboxane B2 and prostacyclin, thereby accelerating platelet aggregation and thrombus formation30. Overall, these factors collectively lead to vascular dysfunction and thrombosis, resulting in poor short-term prognosis of AIS in patients.

Data from several studies suggest that high WBC counts are related to a poor patient outcome31,32. A study on percutaneous coronary intervention patients revealed thaat increased WBC counts were associated with HTPR33. In line with these previous studies, our study suggested that increased WBC counts were correlated with a greater risk of a poor prognosis in the HTPR population. Furthermore, BUN, a protein metabolic waste produced by the liver and excreted by the kidney, is also considered a vital predictor of a poor prognosis. Previous research has demonstrated that the BUN level is a critical predictive factor for a poor prognosis of AIS, with a higher BUN predictive of higher in-hospital mortality for AIS patients and higher risks of AIS34,35. Similarly, our research revealed that higher BUN leads to a greater risk of a poor prognosis of AIS in HTPR patients. SHAP analysis revealed that higher CRP levels are associated with a poor prognosis of AIS in patients with HTPR. CRP is one of the most commonly used biomarkers for assessing the level of inflammation. Relevant studies suggest that the level of CRP, a marker of inflammation, is significantly correlated with a poor prognosis, with higher CRP levels typically indicating a greater risk of poor outcomes36,37. In AIS patients with HTPR, elevated CRP levels may indicate a more severe inflammatory response, which is closely related to platelet activation and thrombosis formation.

In the form of SHAP, the best-performing model was embedded using interpretability methods, which shows that high DBP levels are associated with a poor prognosis. Many published studies have shown that hypertension has negative effects on cerebral circulation, including increasing oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and vasoconstriction38. Accordingly, high SBP is considered a major risk factor for most cardiovascular diseases, including stroke39. In contrast, relatively little research has been conducted on the relationship between DBP and a poor prognosis40. A prospective cohort study suggested that DBP, as measured through dynamic blood pressure monitoring, is a better predictor of stroke than is systolic blood pressure41. High DBP may indicate the presence of small artery and arteriolar remodelling, which could lead to lumen abnormalities and ischaemia, thereby exacerbating the progression of stroke and negatively affecting the nervous system’s recovery capacity, ultimately resulting in a worse clinical outcome. The results of our study further indicate that, in AIS patients with HTPR, an increase in DBP is associated with a significantly greater risk of a poor outcome. Therefore, DBP should not be overlooked and should be given sufficient attention.

On the basis of the results of locally interpretable analysis, we can focus more on individual samples and analyse the relationships between various characteristics and the prognosis of AIS patients from an individual perspective. On the basis of PDPs and ICEs, the prognosis of AIS patients with HTPR varies with specific trends in different characteristics. The phenomena reflected in the global interpretable technology have also been demonstrated. Moreover, we evaluated these two interpreters by randomly selecting two instances from the test dataset, and the results showed that both interpreters were able to reflect the prognosis of AIS in patients well.

The global interpretation method can enable clinical doctors to understand the response trend of the model throughout the entire feature space. In contrast, local interpretation methods can provide feature-based decision interpretation for specific individuals. In practice, both methods can assist clinical doctors in making effective decisions during the medical process. In summary, this article discusses the interpretability of MLs currently used in clinical environments. The prognostic status of AIS in patients with HTPR was used as a research example to compare the predictive performance of different types of MLs. ML interpretation methods were subsequently used to interpret the models on the basis of population and individuals.

In addition, in our study, the performance of the RF model was evaluated across different subgroups to detect any potential biases in the dataset. The consistent results across the subgroups demonstrate the universality and stability of the RF model. The highest AUC value was observed in the diabetic subgroup. Owing to poor blood sugar control, diabetic patients are prone to vascular damage, particularly arteriosclerosis in small and large vessels, which significantly increases the incidence of stroke42. Therefore, improving blood sugar management in HTPR patients is crucial.

The strengths of this study include a comprehensive evaluation, as multiple MLs were employed to predict AIS prognosis, allowing for a robust comparison and the identification of the most effective model. The use of 5-fold CV ensures that the models are trained and validated on different subsets of data, minimizing overfitting and enhancing generalizability. Furthermore, this study provides in-depth performance metrics, offering a well-rounded view of model effectiveness. The focus on AIS prognosis using MLs in clinical settings also provides valuable insights for potential real-world applications in health care decision-making. Notably, several limitations exist in our study. First, the sample size of this study was limited, particularly with a small number of positive cases, which may have resulted in a wide AUC confidence interval and affected the reliability of the findings. Although SMOTE was employed for sensitivity analysis to support the robustness of the model, further external validation with larger samples is still needed to strengthen the validity of the model. Second, this was a single-centre retrospective study. Single-centre data may carry biases specific to the region or patient population, and the use of retrospective data may limit the applicability of the model in other settings. Additionally, the lack of external validation limits the ability to assess the model’s performance in real-world settings outside the dataset used in this study.

Conclusion

In this study, RF models were used to predict the short-term prognosis of AIS in patients with HTPR and were analysed for interpretability. Through the exploration of various clinical features, we found significant associations between high levels of DBP, BUN, HCY, CRP, WBC, and the CYP2C19 genotype and poor AIS outcomes in the HTPR population. Our findings enhance the understanding of the risk factors of AIS patients with HTPR, providing valuable insights for clinical practice and public health interventions.
