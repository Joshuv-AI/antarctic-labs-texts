Evidence for the Stability Selection Mechanism in a Live Predator–Prey System
Authors: John P DeLong, Kyle E Coblentz, Kristi L Montooth, Qingqing Yang, Dinelka Thilakarathne, Francis Biagioli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStability selection is the process by which species are lost from a community due to a structural susceptibility to extinction. Stability selection is non‐adaptive because it does not lead to the evolution of traits that increase individual fitness. However, stability selection could still drive evolutionary change because the stability of populations is linked to heritable traits. Here we demonstrate both phenomena with a live predator–prey system. We show that the stability properties of a predator–prey pair vary with prey genetics, indicating the potential for differential extinction to influence the genotypic makeup of populations. Second, we show that the loss of unstable predator–prey pairs in subpopulations from the overall population can lead to trait evolution in the aggregate population, providing empirical support for the stability selection mechanism. Our results indicate that community‐level processes such as predator–prey interactions can generate eco‐evolutionary change at the population scale.Keywords: didinium, eco‐evolutionary dynamics, paramecium, predator–prey, stability, stability selection

Introduction

Stability selection is the process by which species engaged in unstable interactions within ecological communities such as food webs are lost, leaving the more stable pairs in place (Borrelli et al. 2015; Damuth and Ginzburg 2025; Johnson and Amarasekare 2015). For example, strong predator–prey interactions have the potential to lead to low abundances of predators and their prey during the troughs of population cycles (Holyoak and Lawler 1996; Rosenzweig 1971), potentially leading to the stochastic or deterministic extinction of the predator, the prey, or both. What remains in the community, then, would be some other set of less extinction‐prone predator–prey interactions. This process is thought to generate a pattern of attrition that can increase the stability of the system overall despite the inherent destabilisation generated by negative species interactions (Allesina and Pascual 2008; May 2001; Yodzis 1981).

Stability selection determines species composition of communities and is not considered a form of adaptive selection operating at the level of populations (Borrelli et al. 2015). However, we propose that if stability selection operates at the level of sub‐populations of interacting species that combine to form a larger aggregate population, and those sub‐populations vary in heritable traits, it is possible that stability selection could alter the allele frequencies of the remaining aggregate population. This alteration could arise randomly or directionally through a link between phenotypes and stability. This would lead to evolution per se (change in the allele frequencies in the aggregate population) that did not arise through among‐individual differences in fitness.

Persistence due to stability seems logical—after all, what we see in nature is at least stable enough to persist up to the point of our observation. Stability selection also appears consistent with some macroecological patterns and the underlying mechanisms that may drive them in consumer‐resource systems (Coblentz and DeLong 2023; Johnson and Amarasekare 2015; Otto et al. 2007). However, it is empirically unclear if the stability selection mechanism can operate at the level of populations by the differential loss of sub‐populations and subsequent trait evolution. Theoretical work has shown that evolving consumer populations may go extinct due to instability (specifically, stochastic extinctions) when under strong selection, leaving us to see only a subset of trajectories that happened to not go extinct by random chance (Coblentz and DeLong 2023). However, we lack empirical evidence for the stability selection mechanism, either at the population level or the community level.

Here, we show that the loss of some sub‐populations subject to negative species interactions alters the distribution of phenotypes across the remaining aggregate of sub‐populations, providing direct empirical evidence for the stability selection mechanism. We use the classic Didinium–Paramecium predator–prey system in an experiment designed to elucidate the phenotypic outcome of population extinctions. Experiments with Didinium and Paramecium have repeatedly revealed strong predator–prey interactions that can drive cycles and cause extinctions, but the cycles can be stabilised through a variety of mechanisms, making this system ideal for investigating stability selection (DeLong and Vasseur 2013; Gause 1935; Luckinbill 1973). Our results clearly show variation in predator–prey stability linked to underlying genetic differences, generating a scenario in which the frequency of heritable traits underlying stability could be altered by the loss of unstable sub‐populations. We found that the loss of unstable predator–prey pairs within sub‐populations changed the distribution of phenotypic values across the remaining population, but this effect was transient and linked temporally to the strongest period of the predator–prey interaction. Our results thus provide support for the mechanisms of stability selection at the population level, demonstrate that stability selection can shape trait evolution, but also suggest that its effect may be transient.

Materials and Methods

We produced outcrossed cell lines of Paramecium caudatum by collecting cells from three local ponds and lakes, isolating individual cells from these collections, and then combining isolate cultures to encourage conjugation (Coblentz et al. 2024). From a mass‐conjugation event, we collected and isolated multiple pairs of actively conjugating cells. Because ex‐conjugates from P. caudatum are identical but different from either cell parent (Bell 2008), isolating the pair generates a new outcrossed genetic line of cells that will then propagate asexually.

We phenotyped a total of 52 of these paramecium cell lines by tracking cells in short (~25 s) video clips taken with a microscope (Leica 165C) (Coblentz et al. 2024). To take the videos, we washed 20 cells in autoclaved pond water filtered with 0.2 μm pore size Whatman mixed cellulose ester filters and suspended them in a 0.1 mL drop of water in a Petri dish. We flattened the drop slightly with a small Petri dish lid. This generated a standardised density, volume, and water condition for observing the phenotypes. We used the BEMOVI package in R to extract morphological (cell length and width) and behavioural (a range of movements speeds and variabilities) phenotypes (Pennekamp et al. 2015). From a principal components analysis (PCA), we selected five lines to use in the experiment based on position in the PCA space. We chose one cell line with traits near the origin and one from each quadrant of the PCA space, referred to hereafter as lines 9, 33, 34, 56 and 57. These choices represent a set of slow and small cells, slow and large cells, fast and small cells, fast and large cells, and cells of average speed and size, creating variation among lines that would be expected to influence predation risk (Coblentz et al. 2024).

We assembled replicate 15.5 mL microcosm communities containing P. caudatum as the prey and Didinium nasutum as the predator. We acquired Didinium cultures from Carolina Biological Supply (Burlington, NC, USA). We provided microcosms with protozoa medium (Carolina Biological Supply) diluted 1:10 into filtered pond water (using Whatman qualitative 1 filters with ~11 μm pore size) that was autoclaved and then inoculated with a mix of bacteria extracted from the collection ponds and grown on standard agar plates. The filtering removed small protist‐sized particles and larger ones, while the autoclaving killed smaller organisms that passed through the filter. We put one autoclaved brown rice grain into each microcosm as a slow‐release source of carbon. We made five replicates for microcosms that had one of the five separate Paramecium genetic lines, and we made 25 replicate microcosms that had an even mix of all five lines to start. This approximates equal starting genotypic frequencies in each microcosm. All communities started with four Didinium cells and ~2500 Paramecium cells (~161 per mL). We maintained the microcosms at 23°C in Percival incubators with no light and 60% relative humidity.

We sampled microcosms for Didinium and Paramecium every day during the first week and about every other day after that. We used a scaled sampling regime, scanning the whole dish for cells when they were rare, scanning half a dish when they got a little more abundant, and counting a subsample when they were abundant and too numerous to count. We removed 0.2 mL every day throughout the experiment, and we counted the cells in this 0.2 mL subsample when the paramecia were too numerous to count in the whole dish. We maintained volume in the microcosms by topping them up to a pre‐marked fill line with autoclaved pond water prior to sampling. We then replaced the withdrawn 0.2 mL of media with fresh protozoan media inoculated with the same bacterial mixture used to initiate the populations. This approach, combined with the observation that predation suppressed the population abundance of Paramecium, helped to ensure that bacterial abundances were not a limiting factor for Paramecium growth. We continued sampling each dish until the extinction of Paramecium and/or Didinium or through 3 weeks if both species were still extant. For completeness, in six cases, missing density values were interpolated from the estimates immediately before and after the missing datum. To estimate different facets of population stability (Donohue et al. 2013), we calculated the time to extinction and the coefficient of variation of abundance for each species within each replicate microcosm.

Every 2–3 days, we also resampled the Paramecium populations for morphological and behavioural phenotypes, using the same standardising approach as above with 20 rinsed cells and video tracking. We therefore generated trajectories of both species' abundances and paramecia traits through time for all 50 microcosms. We conducted a PCA of these traits across the entire sampling period, including seven key traits that are heritable in P. caudatum (mean cell area, mean major axis length, mean minor axis length, mean aspect ratio, the standard deviation of turning angles, net displacement, and gross speed; Coblentz et al. 2024). The first PCA axis roughly corresponded to displacement, speed, and cell length and width, and the second PCA axis corresponded to turning variability and aspect ratio (Figure S1). Cell area was associated with both axes. For simplicity, we report the trajectories of phenotypes using the two main PCA axes and the mean value across the 20 cells in each video.

We divided the time series into early (Days 0–10), middle (Days 11–20), and late (Days 21–24) periods. The early period roughly corresponds to population growth of Paramecium and the beginning of growth for Didinium. The middle period is the most intensive period of Didinium foraging, driving the Paramecium population down. The middle period also should represent the most intensive period of selection by Didinium on Paramecium traits. The late period is the period with minimal Didinium abundances and, in some cases, recovery of Paramecium populations. We then fit a linear model with the time to extinction as the dependent variable against the two PCA axes as predictors, checking for an interaction between the PCA axes. This analysis tests the hypothesis that Paramecium phenotypes are linked to variation in stability among the microcosms, a critical component for within‐species evolution via stability selection. We conducted this analysis in Julia v1.10 using the lm function in the GLM package. Data and code are available from the public GitHub Repository (https://github.com/jpdelong/Paramecium‐didinium‐predator‐prey‐dynamcis.git).

Results

The dynamics of the Paramecium–Didinium system showed clear differences among genetic lines (Figure 1). Predator–prey pairs using line 33 went extinct early, while all replicates with line 9 persisted to the end of the experiment (Figures 1 and 2). The other three Didinium–Paramecium pairs showed a variety of qualitatively different dynamics, with some replicates persisting and others going extinct. The mixed‐line microcosms showed dynamics and stability properties that were roughly the average of the five component lines (Figures 1 and 2).

For the mixed‐line microcosms, the trait trajectories in PCA space showed substantial variation (Figure 3). There was a strong tendency for the traits to move left on the PC1 axis from the starting values (thus lowering displacement, speed, and cell length and width relative to the starting values; Figure 3A) but move widely up and down along the PC2 axis (thus showing little consistent change in turning variability, aspect ratio). In the early period, there was a positive effect of traits (only on the PC1 axis) on the time to extinction of the populations, using a linear model including (t = 2.87, p = 0.01) or not including (t = 2.75, p = 0.01) an interaction between the PC1 and PC2 axes as a predictor (Figure 3A). In the middle period, a significant effect of PC1 on persistence remained (Figure 3B), again whether an interaction between the PC axes was included (t = 3.58, p = 0.002) or not (t = 3.65, p = 0.001). By the later period, however, this effect was gone (without interaction: t = 1.59, p = 0.13; with interaction: t = 1.17, p = 0.26; Figure 3C). Thus, only when Didinium populations were growing and the predation pressure was strong was there a clear signal of trait differences among Paramecium subpopulations that influenced their persistence.

All microcosms started with a calculated average phenotype based on the equal contributions of each line to the starting population (blue star). The trajectories include longer time periods from the top to the bottom, illustrating that the separation in trait space for populations that went extinct by the end of the experiment or persisted (seen in separation along the PCA1 axis in the top two panels) was a transient phenomenon. In microcosms with only a single outcrossed line of Paramecium, cells traversed much the same expanse of trait space covered by cells in the mixed line microcosms (Figure S2). However, these trait changes did not have the same effect on persistence as we observed with the mixed line microcosms. Using the same linear model to predict the days to extinction given both PCA axes, we found no significant effect of either trait dimension (up to 10 days, with interaction: t = 1.54, p = 0.14 and without interaction: t = 1.75, p = 0.09; up to 20 days, with interaction: t = 1.54, p = 0.14 and without interaction: t = 1.55, p = 0.13; up to 24 days, with interaction: t = 0.71, p = 0.49 and without interaction: t = 0.71, p = 0.48). Thus, trait changes generated by phenotypic plasticity were not enough to yield an effect on population stability, which contrasts with the results in the mixed‐line communities where the added potential trait change coming from shifts in allele frequencies did lead to a stability selection outcome.

Discussion

The interplay of ecological processes driving population abundances and evolutionary processes determining the genotypic makeup of populations can have profound implications for community composition and trait distributions across species (Fryxell et al. 2019; Post and Palkovacs 2009; Schoener 2011). Typically, however, ‘eco‐evolutionary dynamics’ refers to the change in population abundances and traits that emerge from the demographic consequences of traits, placing adaptive outcomes on the same timescale as ecological outcomes (DeLong et al. 2016; Hairston Jr. et al. 2005). It has been less clear that interactions occurring between species can drive trait evolution without invoking the among‐individual differences in fitness that drive natural selection. Yet, the idea of stability selection has persisted due to the logical supposition that the interactions we see in nature must have persisted long enough for our observation. That notion implies that unstable between‐species configurations in ecological communities should eventually be pruned from the system, potentially changing genetic variation within species if this variation is associated with the persistence of these between‐species interactions.

Despite theoretical and circumstantial evidence for stability selection (Coblentz and DeLong 2023; Johnson and Amarasekare 2015; Otto et al. 2007), there has been little empirical evidence that stability selection can actually occur or that stability selection can lead to trait evolution. Our results demonstrate both possibilities. In our experiment, the loss of unstable predator–prey pairs led to a shift in heritable phenotypes across the aggregate population of prey that remained, providing direct empirical support for the idea that the mechanisms of stability selection can shape trait distributions of species. Specifically, populations that were further to the left on the PCA1 axis (i.e., slower cells) were more likely to go extinct earlier. This pattern was only observed in communities that contained multiple lines that differed in these trait values and not in genetically homogenous populations, indicating that this is not the consequence of trait plasticity. Our results thus indicate that community‐level processes can generate eco‐evolutionary change. This outcome, however, may change with different predator behaviours. When paired with copepod predators, P. caudatum showed a decrease in predation susceptibility when slower, which would, all things being equal, lead to a more stable predator–prey interaction (Coblentz et al. 2024).

Referring to the process as a type of selection implies some analogy to different types of natural selection (e.g., directional), but stability selection does not operate on individual fitness differences. The phenomenon is perhaps better described as ‘differential sub‐population attrition’ (Figure 4). The process can operate because traits are the direct drivers of ecological functions that determine population stability such as demography and foraging interactions (i.e., traits are ‘functional’, Violle et al. 2007). Losing pools of individuals through sub‐population extinction, then, can alter the composition of the remaining larger aggregate of individuals across other sub‐populations (Figure 4). Critically, however, the process also requires there to be some reason that trait trajectories differ among sub‐populations. In theory, and in our observations here, stochastic variation among sub‐populations can produce such variation (Coblentz and DeLong 2023), meaning that the evolutionary outcome of stability selection can be analogous to drift‐like effects with some populations entering more stable states by chance. Stability selection, however, is not equivalent to group selection, as there is no expectation that the differences in persistence are generated by the positive fitness effects of interacting as a group (Nowak et al. 2010; Williams 1966).

Conceptual figure of stability selection and its relation to other processes. For a species that occurs across a range of subpopulations (grey circles), the selective loss of some subpopulations due to trait‐induced instability can lead to a shift in the allele frequency across the whole species. In this case, unstable starburst subpopulations eventually go extinct (marked by an X), leaving the whole species with a higher frequency of grey circles. This may occur even when evolutionary adaptation processes occur within subpopulations. We suggest dispersal among subpopulations (gene flow) could disrupt the stability selection process even as it may undermine local adaptation as well.

Our experiment leveraged the famously unstable Didinium–Paramecium predator–prey system. Prior experiments with this system have revealed highly variable stability properties that depend, in part, on the relative magnitude of foraging parameters. Almost a century ago, Gause (1935) used the Didinium–Paramecium system to test the theoretical prediction of population cycles proposed by Lotka and Volterra (Berryman 1992; Lotka 1909). Over time, similar experiments demonstrated that stabilisation of predator–prey cycles in Didinium‐ciliate systems could be achieved by changing environmental conditions (Luckinbill 1973), time lags between foraging and reproduction (Maly 1978), the presence of interference competition (DeLong and Vasseur 2013; Salt 1974), the presence of lower quality prey such as Colpidium campylum (Luckinbill 1979), dispersal among sub‐populations (Holyoak and Lawler 1996), and cooler temperatures (DeLong and Lyon 2020). Our results add to this series of insights by demonstrating the potential for stochastic differences in trait change to lead to the selective persistence of sub‐populations. This detail is important here as predation by Didinium did not consistently move all populations to the same trait location. Instead, random differences among replicates also contribute to differences in stability (Figure 3).

A major caveat here is that in true metapopulations, dispersal among sub‐populations may stabilise the overall system, both removing the stability selection process and increasing genotypic similarity of sub‐populations through gene flow (Hanski 1999; Huffaker 1958; Figure 4). Indeed, dispersal among subpopulations stabilised the predator–prey metapopulations of Didinium foraging on another ciliate, Colpidium striatum, in a connected set of microcosms (Holyoak and Lawler 1996). Thus, the level of dispersal also may play a role in creating conditions appropriate for the operation of stability selection. This might require that the trait‐environment interactions that lead to stability vary in space, which is more likely for sub‐populations that are spatially separated.

In conclusion, our results lend empirical evidence to the idea of stability selection and our extension that stability selection can actively lead to the evolution of traits in the remaining aggregate population. We see, however, many impediments to its operation in nature including (1) the transient dynamics often associated with strong predator–prey interactions, (2) gene flow across subpopulations that could alleviate instability and reset allele frequencies and (3) direct selection happening within populations that drive traits in a specific direction unlinked to population stability, suggesting that it remains difficult to determine whether stability selection can operate in more complex natural communities.
