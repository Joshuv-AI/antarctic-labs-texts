Post-translational weapons of microbial warfare: how bacterial effectors hijack host cell death and xenophagy
Authors: Kun Meng, Liuliu Shi, Xianglan Fang, Jin Yang, Yan Ding, Zheng Cao, Shan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The intricate interplay between pathogenic bacteria and host cellular processes represents a dynamic molecular battlefield during infection. Mammalian hosts employ two critical cell-autonomous defense mechanisms, regulated cell death (RCD) and xenophagy, to combat invading pathogens. RCD eliminates infected cells through programmed self-destruction, thereby removing bacterial replication niches and limiting pathogen spread. Concurrently, xenophagy sequesters intracellular bacteria within autophagosomes for subsequent lysosomal degradation. To subvert these host defenses, bacterial pathogens have evolved sophisticated effector proteins that mimic eukaryotic functional domains, enabling them to catalyze diverse post-translational modifications (PTMs) of host targets. While these PTM-mediated strategies are crucial for bacterial survival and persistence, current understanding of their specific mechanisms in modulating RCD and xenophagy remains fragmented. This review systematically examines the host–pathogen interaction landscape, with particular emphasis on how bacterial effector-driven PTMs disrupt these key defense pathways to establish infection. Elucidating the regulatory roles of bacterial PTMs in these processes is essential for advancing our knowledge of microbial pathogenesis and informing the development of innovative anti-infective therapies.

Introductions

Pathogenic bacterial infections represent a major global health burden, contributing substantially to worldwide morbidity and mortality through diverse infectious diseases [1]. In response to microbial invasion, the host immune system deploys a sophisticated multilayered defense network. The initial line of protection involves membrane-anchored or cytosolic pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that detect conserved pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). PAMPs, among other triggers such as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and host-associated molecular patterns (HAMPs), can induce potent innate immune signaling cascades [2, 3]. For intracellular bacterial pathogens that evade extracellular defenses, mammalian cells activate two fundamental cell-autonomous immunity pathways: regulated cell death (RCD) and xenophagy. These mechanisms synergistically function to eradicate intracellular bacterial replication niches, establish cellular containment barriers to prevent pathogen spread, mobilize and activate immune cells to infection foci, and directly execute antimicrobial effector programs against cytosolic invaders [4, 5].

Among the well-characterized forms of RCD, apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis represent the most comprehensively studied paradigms. Apoptosis, a non-lytic and immunologically silent process, is executed through caspase activation in response to intrinsic (mitochondrial) or extrinsic (death receptor) stimuli. This pathway manifests distinct morphological features including membrane blebbing, chromatin condensation, and internucleosomal DNA fragmentation [6]. In contrast, pyroptosis constitutes a lytic, pro-inflammatory cell death modality initiated by inflammasome activation upon PAMP detection [7, 8]. Upon recognizing invading PAMPs by specialized sensors, pyroptosis is initiated by assembling inflammasome platforms. For example, bacterial flagellin and cytosolic LPS trigger the assembly of canonical NAIP-NLRC4-caspase-1 [9] and non-canonical caspase-4/5/11 inflammasome complexes, respectively [10]. These molecular platforms catalyze the proteolytic activation of gasdermin family proteins (GSDMs), which have a pore-forming activity that drives pyroptotic cell rupture and cytokine release [11]. Necroptosis represents an alternative lytic death pathway with inflammatory consequences, typically engaged when apoptotic signaling is compromised [12, 13]. The activation of necroptosis involves the formation of necrosome complexes through homotypic interactions with the RIP homotypic interaction motif (RHIM)-containing proteins [14]. Although the morphological characteristics of these three types of death are distinct and their molecular machinery has traditionally been considered independent, increasing evidence proves significant crosstalk among them (Fig. 1) [15]. Complementing these cell death mechanisms, xenophagy functions as a specialized antimicrobial autophagy pathway that selectively targets intracellular pathogens. Upon cytosolic invasion, intracellular bacteria are recognized by PRRs, which recruit the LC3 (microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3) lipidation machinery to the bacterial phagosome. This triggers autophagy initiation and delivery of the cargo to lysosomes for degradation [16, 17]. Xenophagy can be induced via two distinct mechanisms: the selective canonical autophagy pathway, which involves the de novo formation of a double-membrane autophagosome around the target [18], and the non-canonical CASM (conjugation of ATG8 to single membranes) pathway which directly labels damaged, pathogen-containing vacuoles with LC3 for lysosomal degradation (Fig. 3) [19].Fig. 1Bacterial effectors directly regulate host cell death via PTMs targeting core pathway components. Given the critical roles of RCD and xenophagy in controlling intracellular bacterial infections, pathogenic bacteria have evolved sophisticated mechanisms to manipulate these host defense pathways, thereby facilitating their survival and proliferation. Gram-negative pathogens such as Shigella flexneri (S. flexneri), Salmonella enterica, and Legionella pneumophila (L. pneumophila) employ specialized secretion systems—needle-like nanomachines that deliver effector proteins directly into host cells [20, 21]. These bacterial effectors frequently exhibit diverse enzymatic activities and exploit post-translational modifications (PTMs), both reversible and irreversible, to hijack host cellular processes. While some effectors mimic eukaryotic enzymes, others possess unique biochemical properties that enable them to subvert host immunity in unprecedented ways [22–25].

In this review, we systematically summarize recent advances in understanding how bacterial virulence factors exploit PTMs to disrupt host cell death and xenophagy, thereby facilitating infection. Additionally, we explore the translational potential of these PTMs in biomedical applications and propose novel therapeutic strategies targeting these microbial virulence mechanisms for future intervention.

Bacterial modulation of host cell death via PTMs of cell death signaling proteins

This section will first detail the sophisticated molecular mechanisms by which these effectors target and alter core components of apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis pathways through unique PTMs, effectively neutralizing the host's defensive cell death machinery.

Bacteria evade death receptor-mediated cell death by arginine glycosylation

Death receptors (DRs), key members of the tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) superfamily, are defined by their characteristic cytoplasmic death domains (DDs). Ligand binding induces receptor trimerization and subsequent recruitment of DD-containing adaptor proteins (e.g., TRADD), initiating downstream apoptotic signaling [29]. Pathogenic bacteria have evolved a sophisticated countermeasure via the type III secretion system (T3SS)-delivered NleB-family effectors. Unlike conventional glycosylation that targets serine/threonine/tyrosine or asparagine residues, these unique glycosyltransferases specifically catalyze GlcNAcylation of conserved arginine residues within host DD-containing proteins [30, 31]. This modification disrupts both homotypic and heterotypic DD interactions, thereby preventing the assembly of the canonical death-inducing signaling complex (DISC) and consequently interfering with TNF, FAS ligand, and TRAIL-induced cell death. Notably, this glycosyltransferase activity is indispensable for bacterial virulence and establishment of persistent infections in vivo [32, 33].

The NleB effector family exhibits both structural conservation and functional diversity. While NleBc (Citrobacter rodentium), NleB1/2 (EPEC/EHEC), and SseK1/2/3 (Salmonella enterica) share significant sequence homology, they display remarkable substrate specificity [31, 34]. NleB1 demonstrates broad targeting of host DD-containing proteins, whereas SseK1 shows a preference for TRADD and SseK3 primarily modifies TNFR1 during Salmonella infection [35, 36]. NleB2 represents a notable exception among family members, featuring a serine residue outside the Asp-X-Asp catalytic motif that confers preferential utilization of UDP-glucose rather than UDP-GlcNAc as its sugar donor [37].

Beyond subverting apoptosis through death receptor modification, proteomic analyses have revealed that NleB-family effectors target an extensive range of non-death domain proteins for GlcNAcylation, including both host factors (e.g., GAPDH, HIF1α, Rab GTPases, and SNAREs) and bacterial components (e.g., GshB, OmpR, NagC, and GlmR) [38–45]. Intriguingly, these effectors also undergo autoglucosylation, which enhances their enzymatic activity [46]. Such widespread Arg-GlcNAcylation demonstrates bacterial hijacking of host signaling via atypical modifications.

Pathogens disrupt caspase-dependent cell death via arginine ADP-riboxanation/ADPR-deacylization

Caspases, a family of evolutionarily conserved cysteine proteases, serve as central regulators of programmed cell death pathways. Their activation triggers proteolytic cascades that eliminate infected cells, forming a crucial antimicrobial defense mechanism [47, 48]. In response to this host protection strategy, pathogenic bacteria have developed sophisticated countermeasures. Specifically, T3SS effectors OspC3 from S. flexneri and CopC from Chromobacterium violaceum (C. violaceum) subvert host defenses by catalyzing a unique post-translational modification called ADP-riboxanation (or ADPR-deacylization) on caspase proteins [49, 50]. Distinct from traditional protein ADP-ribosylation, OspC3/CopC-catalyzed ADPR-deacylization requires Ca2+-free calmodulin (apo-CaM) as the co-factor and proceeds through a two-step biochemical reaction: (1) initial ADP-ribosyl transfer from NAD⁺ to target arginine residues, followed by (2) deamination between the ribosyl 2'-OH and adjacent guanidine group [51, 52]. This modification occurs on caspases at conserved arginine residues crucial for substrate recognition, leading to the suppression of their proteolytic activity and the limitation of host cell death [53].

The virulence functions of CopC and OspC3 have been firmly established in mouse infection models, where both effectors prove indispensable for bacterial pathogenicity. These effectors exhibit distinct mechanisms of caspase inhibition: OspC3 specifically targets inflammatory caspases-4/11 to block GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, while CopC demonstrates broader substrate specificity, simultaneously disrupting apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis through its action on caspases-3/7/8/9 [49, 50]. Phylogenetic analysis reveals the widespread distribution of CopC/OspC homologs across diverse bacterial species, including pathogens, commensals, and symbionts, with conserved catalytic residues indicating an evolutionarily ancient origin for this cell death subversion strategy [49, 50]. In addition to executing cell death, these effectors are now known to target multiple non-caspase substrates and modulate diverse cellular processes to promote pathogenesis. Newly identified functions include subverting the IFN signaling pathway [54], inactivating host translation [55], and manipulating ISGylation [56], suggesting they may function as global modulators of host–pathogen interactions through their versatile ADPR-deacylase activity.

Pathogens evade pyroptosis signaling by ubiquitination

Ubiquitination is a crucial post-translational modification in eukaryotic cells, involving the covalent attachment of ubiquitin molecules to target proteins via a cascade of E1 (activating), E2 (conjugating), and E3 (ligase) enzymes. This process regulates protein stability, localization, and function, playing pivotal roles in inflammation and programmed cell death. Notably, many bacterial pathogens exploit this system by encoding effector proteins that mimic host E3 ubiquitin ligases, thereby hijacking the host ubiquitination machinery to their advantage.

A striking example is Shigella, which delivers a repertoire of T3SS effectors known as invasion plasmid antigen H (IpaH) proteins. These bacterial effectors represent a unique class of NEL (Novel E3 Ligase)-type ubiquitin ligases that are structurally distinct from their eukaryotic counterparts. The IpaH family members share a conserved architecture featuring an N-terminal leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain, which mediates substrate recognition, and a C-terminal NEL catalytic domain responsible for their E3 ligase activity [57–59]. The interferon-induced GBP family plays an essential role in anti-bacterial defense by detecting cytosolic pathogens [60–62]. In IFNγ-activated epithelial cells, GBP1 initiates pathogen recognition through direct LPS binding, nucleating subsequent GBP assembly on bacterial surfaces [63]. This GBP complex disrupts the bacterial outer membrane integrity, liberating LPS into the cytosol, where it directly activates caspase-4 to induce noncanonical pyroptosis [64–67]. Through mutagenesis screening, both Li et al. and Wandal et al. (from two independent studies) identified IpaH9.8 as the essential virulence factor mediating hGBP1 degradation in S. flexneri infection [68, 69]. Structural analysis revealed that IpaH9.8's LRR domain engages the GBP1 GTPase domain while its NEL domain catalyzes K48-linked polyubiquitination, targeting GBP1 for proteasomal degradation [68–70]. This degradation mechanism proves critical for pathogenesis, as IpaH9.8 deletion mutants exhibit reduced hepatic bacterial loads and enhanced host survival in murine infection models [68]. By eliminating GBP1, IpaH9.8 effectively blocks LPS liberation and consequent caspase-4 activation, thereby facilitating bacterial dissemination [67]. Recent work by Cao et al. shows that Yersinia pestis has evolutionarily acquired two functionally redundant homologs of IpaH9.8—YspE1 and YspE2 [71]. These two T3SS effectors target a broad range of human and murine GBPs for proteasomal degradation, facilitating Y. pestis survival through inhibition of inflammasome activation and potentially pyroptosis. Shigella effector IpaH7.8 orchestrates a sophisticated immune evasion strategy by simultaneously targeting both GSDMD and GSDMB for K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation [75–79]. Structural studies demonstrate that IpaH7.8 utilizes its LRR domain to specifically recognize the NTDs of both GSDMD and GSDMB, while the NEL domain mediates K48-linked polyubiquitination. This dual targeting strategy effectively neutralizes two distinct antimicrobial mechanisms: it suppresses GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis (including both caspase-1-dependent canonical and caspase-4-dependent noncanonical pathways) [75], while also blocking GSDMB's direct bactericidal pore-forming activity [76] and cytotoxic pyroptosis that would otherwise be activated by granzyme A (GZMA) from cytotoxic lymphocytes [77–80].

GSDMD exists as a single isoform, whereas multiple variants have been reported for GSDMB. Different GSDMB isoforms may exhibit distinct lipid-binding profiles and pyroptotic activities owing to their interdomain linker (regulated by alternative splicing) [77, 79]. One isoform, containing an insertion at the start of exon 6, selectively binds bacterial membrane phospholipids to kill bacteria without damaging host cells [76]. In contrast, isoforms with an intact exon 6 form pores at plasma membrane and trigger pyroptosis in mammalian cells when activated. IpaH7.8 exhibited comparable binding affinity to these GSDMB isoforms and efficiently induced their degradation, thereby potently inhibiting the diverse functions of GSDMB [77, 79].

Conflicting findings have been reported regarding the role of IpaH7.8 in regulating pyroptosis between human and murine cells. In human cells, IpaH7.8 effectively suppresses pyroptosis, primarily by mediating the degradation of GSDMD and GSDMB. By contrast, murine GSDMD lacks the critical α1–β1′ loop necessary for IpaH7.8-induced degradation [75, 78], and the GSDMB gene is completely absent in mice [81]. Instead, IpaH7.8 ubiquitinates mouse Glomulin and NLRP1B-NTD and elicits their degradation, triggering inflammasome activation and pyroptotic cell death in macrophage [82–84]. However, neither of their human orthologs is affected by IpaH7.8 [75]. In the natural context of human pathogenesis, Shigella ulitilizes IpaH7.8 as a key molecular determinant of host specificity during infection.

Pathogens subvert host cell death by phosphorylation regulation

Phosphorylation is a fundamental PTM in eukaryotic cells, involving the covalent addition of phosphate groups to target proteins through the coordinated action of kinases (phosphate donors) and phosphatases (phosphate removers) [85]. This reversible process regulates protein activity, interactions, and localization, serving as a critical switch in signal transduction and cellular homeostasis. Remarkably, numerous bacterial pathogens have evolved sophisticated mechanisms to manipulate host phosphorylation networks, either by secreting effector proteins with kinase/phosphatase activity or by modulating endogenous host enzymes. Two paradigmatic examples of this evolutionary arms race are Legionella kinase LegK3 and Mycobacterium phosphatase PtpB, which modulate host cell death pathways.

The L. pneumophila effector LegK3 is a eukaryotic-like Ser/Thr kinase recently shown to suppress host cell death [86]. Mechanistically, LegK3 directly phosphorylates key apoptotic caspases, including Caspase-3, Caspase-7, and Caspase-9, at specific residues within their prodomains or interdomain linkers. These modifications do not impair the proteolytic activity of the caspases but instead reduce their suitability as substrates for upstream initiator caspases or regulatory proteins. LegK3 is dispensable for normal intracellular replication but becomes essential for optimal bacterial growth under VP-16-induced cell death conditions. These findings demonstrate that LegK3-mediated inhibition of host cell death supports L. pneumophila proliferation during apoptosis stress.

Mycobacterium tuberculosis employs the effector PtpB, a unique phospholipid phosphatase, to suppress host inflammasome-pyroptosis pathways. PtpB does not affect inflammasome assembly but still suppresses GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis [87]. PtpB specifically targets and dephosphorylates PI4P and PI(4,5)P2 on the plasma membrane. By depleting these signaling lipids, PtpB blocks the membrane translocation of GSDMD-NTD and disrupts the assembly of GSDMD-NTD pore-forming polymers, thereby inhibiting pyroptosis [88–91]. PtpB’s phosphatase activity is strictly dependent on mono-ubiquitin (Ub) as a cofactor. Upon binding with Ub through its Ubiquitin Interacting Motif (UIM)-like region, PtpB undergoes a conformational change to expose its active site for substrate binding, and efficient dephosphorylation [87, 92]. The phosphatase activity of PtpB is required for mycobacterial survival in both macrophages and the mouse infection model [90, 93]. The ptpB-deficient mutation infection attenuates bacterial load in lungs and spleens and significantly increases the amounts of inflammatory cytokines in a Gsdmd-dependent manner [87]. Thus, the PtpB-ubiquitin interaction is essential for Mtb to evade host GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis.

Pathogens block necroptosis by proteolysis of the RHIM-containing proteins

Proteolysis, an irreversible post-translational modification, involves the enzymatic cleavage of peptide bonds by specialized enzymes known as proteases or peptidases. Based on their catalytic mechanisms, proteases are classified into six major categories: serine, cysteine, aspartic, glutamic, threonine, and metalloproteases [94–97]. Bacterial pathogens have evolved a diverse array of proteases that exhibit significant structural, mechanistic, and functional differences compared to their mammalian counterparts. Through their proteolytic activity, these bacterial enzymes can either suppress host cell death to facilitate successful infection or induce cell death to provoke pro-inflammatory signaling that alerts the host to the infection [98, 99]. To illustrate these dual roles, we highlight two representative examples of bacterial proteases that differentially regulate host cell death during infection.

RHIM-containing proteins are essential mediators of antimicrobial defense, triggering inflammatory necroptosis [100, 101]. Upon sensing necroptotic signals, RHIM adaptors (e.g., TRIF and ZBP1) activate and recruit the kinases RIPK1 and RIPK3. These kinases form necrotic signaling complexes (necrosomes) through homotypic interactions, initiating a phosphorylation cascade that activates the Mixed Lineage Kinase Domain-Like (MLKL) protein. Once phosphorylated, MLKL oligomerizes and translocates to the plasma membrane, where it drives membrane disruption and necroptotic cell death [14, 102, 103].

During EPEC infection, the T3SS effector EspL targets and degrades multiple RHIM-containing proteins, including RIPK1, RIPK3, and TRIF [104]. Remarkably, although EspL shares no sequence homology with canonical cysteine proteases, it contains a functional Cys-His-Asp catalytic triad that directly cleaves the RHIM motif. This proteolytic activity disrupts RHIM-mediated homotypic interactions, thereby preventing necrosome assembly and effectively suppressing TNF-induced necroptosis signaling. Importantly, EspL's cysteine protease activity is essential for the persistent colonization of Citrobacter rodentium (an EPEC-like mouse pathogen) in murine hosts, underscoring its critical role in bacterial pathogenesis [104].

The evolutionary conservation of EspL-family effectors among enteric pathogens (including Shigella, Salmonella, and Yersinia) underscores their biological significance. Structural and functional analyses demonstrate that Shigella OspD3, an EspL homolog, similarly targets RHIM domains in RIPK1 and RIPK3, resulting in suppressed MLKL activation and blockade of necroptosis [105]. These findings collectively identify a family of bacterial cysteine proteases that have evolved to efficiently disrupt host necroptotic defense mechanisms.

Bacterial modulation of host cell death via PTM of host MAPK pathway

The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling cascade serves as a critical regulator of diverse cellular processes, including immune responses and programmed cell death, through its control of gene transcription [106, 107]. The MAPK pathway is a phosphorylation cascade that involves three central kinases: MAPK kinase kinase (MAPKKK, such as TAK1 and RAF) that initiates the cascade, MAPK kinase (MAPKK, such as MEKs and MKK4/7) that relays the signal, and MAPK (including ERK, p38, JNK) that phosphorylates downstream effectors [108]. Metalloprotease

NleD, a zinc-dependent metalloprotease effector conserved in attaching and effacing (A/E) pathogens (such as EPEC, EHEC, and Citrobacter rodentium), inhibits host apoptosis by selectively cleaving JNK and p38 MAPKs [109, 110]. During infection, NleD is translocated into the host cytoplasm where it specifically recognizes and cleaves the conserved TXY motif within the activation loop of these kinases, resulting in their permanent inactivation [111]. By ablating JNK activity, NleD efficiently inhibits apoptosis during infection. Mechanistically, NleD inactivates JNK and blocks the activation of AP-1 [110], a transcription factor responsible for the gene transcription of many apoptotic factors [112]. Furthermore, given that JNK enhances p53-dependent apoptosis by promoting its phosphorylation and stabilization [113], NleD may also indirectly compromise p53 activity and thus attenuate this alternative apoptotic pathway. However, NleD is dispensable for A/E lesion formation and shows no significant contribution to bacterial colonization or virulence in vivo [114]. This lack of phenotypic impact may be explained by functional redundancy among T3SS effectors, such as NleB [115].

Acetyltransferases

Salmonella T3SS effector AvrA is an acetyltransferase that suppresses host cell death by disrupting MAPK signaling. AvrA specifically modifies lysine and threonine residues on MAPKK proteins MKK4, preventing their phosphorylation and subsequent activation. This inhibition attenuates the expression of pro-apoptotic genes, thereby blocking host cell death [116, 117]. Furthermore, this acetylation-mediated inhibition affects other JNK-dependent signaling pathways, including stabilizing the intestinal Tight-Junction to attenuate bacterial invasion [118], and reducing Beclin-1 expression to attenuate autophagic responses [119].

Phosphothreonine lyase

Another mechanism by which bacteria subvert the MAPK-mediated host death is through a cohort of bacterial phosphothreonine lyases. Among these, the Salmonella effector SpvC serves as a paradigm, directly catalyzing the irreversible dephosphorylation of phosphothreonine residues in MAPKs. SpvC overexpression broadly inactivates ERK1/2, p38, and JNK, yet during infection, it selectively targets ERK1/2 [120–122]. By suppressing MAPK signaling, this effector attenuates the expression of pro-apoptotic genes, thereby blocking host cell death pathways and creating a permissive intracellular environment for bacterial replication. For example, SpvC decreases the expression levels of NLRP3 and NLRC4 through phosphothreonine activity, subsequently dampening the GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis and inflammation to aggravate systemic infection [123–125]. The evolutionary conservation of this virulence mechanism is underscored by the presence of SpvC homologs in other pathogens, including Shigella OspF [122, 126, 127], Edwardsiella ictaluri EseN [128], and Pseudomonas syringae HopAI1 [129, 130].

Emerging bacterial PTMs that regulate host xenophagy

Xenophagy is another type of host defense that involves the selective recognition of intracellular bacteria by the autophagic machinery for degradation [16–18]. Nonetheless, it has been noted that only a tiny portion of bacteria are arrested by the host's autophagic machinery during natural infections [131]. Growing evidence suggests that bacterial PTMs disrupt this antibacterial degradation pathway at various steps, including xenophagosome initiation, biogenesis, and maturation. Salmonella blocks xenophagy initiation via ARF1-dependent ADP-ribosylation of V-ATPase

During early infection, intracellular bacteria disrupt their vacuolar membranes to escape into the cytosol, where they can establish a hyper-replicative niche. To counteract this bacterial strategy, host cells initiate xenophagy-a selective autophagy pathway that targets invading pathogens. Upon vacuole rupture, the host V-ATPase complex detects the damaged membrane and recruits ATG16L1, which catalyzes LC3 lipidation with phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). This non-canonical CASM/VAIL (V-ATPase–ATG16L1-induced LC3 lipidation) pathway targets the damaged bacteria-containing vacuole, promoting lysosomal degradation and restricting bacterial proliferation [132–134].

The Salmonella SPI-1 T3SS effector SopF mediates xenophagy evasion through a unique ARF1-dependent ADP-ribosylation mechanism [134, 135]. By specifically modifying a key glutamine residue on the V-ATPase subunit ATP6V0C, SopF disrupts the critical ATG16L1-V-ATPase interaction required for LC3 recruitment, thereby effectively neutralizing the host's xenophagic defense [134]. This enzymatic activity strictly requires GTP-bound ARF1 as a cofactor, which induces conformational changes in SopF to expose its catalytic site and enhance ADP-ribosyltransferase efficiency [135]. SopF inhibits xenophagy in a bacterial species-independent manner, as its ectopic expression effectively blocks autophagy induced by diverse bacterial mutants, including S. flexneri ΔvirAΔicsB, L. monocytogenes, B. thailandensis ΔbopA, and Y. enterocolitica ΔHOPEM. Furthermore, SopF does not impair canonical autophagy triggered by mTORC1 inhibition, PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, or LC3-associated phagocytosis, demonstrating its specificity for bacterial xenophagy [134]. This ADP-ribosylation explains SopF's essential role in intracellular proliferation. While dispensable for initial epithelial cell invasion, the effector becomes crucial when bacteria need to counteract xenophagy-mediated clearance during infection [134].

Shigella disrupts xenophagy through synergistic fatty acylation and GTPase inactivation during cell-to-cell spread

The intracellular infection cycle of S. flexneri involves several key stages: (1) bacterial uptake by host cells, (2) rupture of the internalization vacuole, (3) cytosolic proliferation, and (4) cell-to-cell spread for dissemination. During cell-to-cell spread, S. flexneri induces the formation of membrane protrusions that encapsulate the bacteria, which are subsequently internalized by neighboring host cells [136, 137]. Notably, LC3 is recruited to S. flexneri-containing vacuoles formed during both initial bacterial entry and protrusion-mediated spread, marking initiation of xenophagy [138, 139].

To counteract host xenophagy, S. flexneri employs the T3SS effector IcsB, which localizes to Shigella-containing vacuoles [140]. IcsB functions as an 18-carbon fatty acyltransferase, catalyzing Nε-fatty acylation of lysine residues on host proteins. During infection, IcsB modifies multiple membrane trafficking proteins, including CHMP5—a core component of the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) machinery. Critically, IcsB-mediated Nε-fatty acylation of CHMP5’s polybasic region enables S. flexneri to evade LC3 sequestration [140].

Functionally, IcsB acts synergistically with another T3SS effector VirA [141]. While distinct from the PTM-mediated mechanisms, VirA exhibits GAP (GTPase-activating protein) activities that inactivate Rab1 to disrupt autophagosome formation. Through the combined actions of IcsB and VirA, S. flexneri effectively evades capture by LC3 and lysosomal LAMP2-positive compartments, thereby promoting bacterial cell-to-cell transmission [139].

Pathogens evade complement-dependent xenophagy through conserved omptin protease activity

The complement system consists of a cascade of serum proteins that become activated upon pathogen recognition. During physiological infection, invasive bacterial pathogens are typically opsonized by complement protein C3 activation fragments prior to host cell entry [142, 143]. Recent studies have demonstrated that C3 fragments accompany invading bacteria into the host cytoplasm, where they function as xenophagy initiation signals. Specifically, C3-coated bacteria directly recruit ATG16L1 through physical interaction, which in turn positions the LC3 lipidation machinery (including ATG5 and ATG12) in proximity to the bacterial surface, driving autophagosome biogenesis for pathogen containment [144].

Sorbara et al. discovered that intracellular bacteria such as S. flexneri and S. Typhimurium could efficiently evade C3-dependent xenophagy-mediated growth restriction by omptin-family proteases (IcsP and PgtE, respectively) [144]. Omptin proteins, though not effectors in the traditional secretory system sense, are typically bacteria-specific proteases anchored in the outer membrane and have been implicated in bacterial pathogenesis [145]. These bacterial enzymes can target and cleave surface-bound C3 fragments before they can recruit ATG16L1, thereby interrupting the xenophagy initiation cascade [144]. Structural analyses reveal that omptins achieve this through a conserved catalytic triad that recognizes C3's activation domain, explaining their effectiveness across Enterobacteriaceae [144–146]. This proteolytic interference represents a precise evolutionary adaptation where pathogens exploit extracellular protease activity to control complement-dependent autophagic clearance.

Legionella subverts autophagy via rewiring ubiquitin signaling and unleashing hydrolases

L. pneumophila employs a sophisticated DUB system to remove ubiquitin modifications from the Legionella-containing vacuole (LCV) that may target it for xenophagic clearance, utilizing specialized effector proteins delivered through its T4SS. These include the OTU-family DUBs LotA, LotB (also known as Ceg23), LotC (also known as Lem27), LotD and OTULIN-family RavD, which localize to LCV through PI(3)P-binding motifs and remove various poly-Ub signal types from the LCV [153–155]. Given the crucial role of host ubiquitin signaling in xenophagy, L. pneumophila evolves an extraordinary ubiquitination bypass system mediated by its SidE family effectors (SidE, SdeA/B/C). These T4SS effectors contains at least three enzymatic domains: an N-terminal canonical DUB domain, a phosphodiesterase (PDE) domain, and a mono-ADP-ribosyltransferase (mART) domain. The DUB domain cleaves polyubiquitin chains and is critical for the removal of K63-linked polyubiquitin chains from the LCV [173]. The PDE domain and mART domain are responsible for catalyzing a unique phosphoribosyl (PR)-ubiquitination pathway that bypasses canonical E1-E2-E3 machinery [174, 175]. Using NAD+ as an energy source, the mART domain ADP-ribosylates ubiquitin at Arg42, which is then converted to PR-Ub by the PDE domain and transferred to serine/tyrosine residues on host proteins [176–178]. This non-canonical modification generates ubiquitin tags that autophagy adaptors fail to recognize, effectively masking the LCV from xenophagic clearance [179]. For example, the host protein USP14 localizes to the membrane of the LCV during infection. SidEs subsequently target USP14 and catalyze its PR-Ub modification, which prevents p62 recognition and inhibits the recruitment of autophagosomes to LCV [180]. (2) MavL specifically reverses ubiquitin ADP-ribosylation at Arg42 [187], and (3) DupA/B function as PR-ubiquitin erasers by cleaving phosphoribosyl modifications from target proteins [188, 189]. Moreover, certain bacteria, such as the Chlamydia-related species Simkania negevensis, encode Josephin-like DUBs that catalyze a unique destructive deubiquitination reaction. Unlike conventional DUBs, these enzymes cleave ubiquitin upstream of the C-terminal diGly motif, truncating the ubiquitin moiety and leaving a residual fragment on the substrate. Although this reaction irreversibly destroys ubiquitin, its functional consequences for autophagy or xenophagy during infection remain to be determined [172].

L. pneumophila excludes autophagy adaptor recruitment by phosphoribosyl (PR) ubiquitination. Given the crucial role of host ubiquitin signaling in xenophagy, L. pneumophila evolves an extraordinary ubiquitination bypass system mediated by its SidE family effectors (SidE, SdeA/B/C). These T4SS effectors contains at least three enzymatic domains: an N-terminal canonical DUB domain, a phosphodiesterase (PDE) domain, and a mono-ADP-ribosyltransferase (mART) domain. The DUB domain cleaves polyubiquitin chains and is critical for the removal of K63-linked polyubiquitin chains from the LCV [173]. The PDE domain and mART domain are responsible for catalyzing a unique phosphoribosyl (PR)-ubiquitination pathway that bypasses canonical E1-E2-E3 machinery [174, 175]. Using NAD+ as an energy source, the mART domain ADP-ribosylates ubiquitin at Arg42, which is then converted to PR-Ub by the PDE domain and transferred to serine/tyrosine residues on host proteins [176–178]. This non-canonical modification generates ubiquitin tags that autophagy adaptors fail to recognize, effectively masking the LCV from xenophagic clearance [179]. For example, the host protein USP14 localizes to the membrane of the LCV during infection. SidEs subsequently target USP14 and catalyze its PR-Ub modification, which prevents p62 recognition and inhibits the recruitment of autophagosomes to LCV [180]. Legionella inhibits autophagosome formation through targeted hydrolase effectors. Beyond its manipulation of ubiquitin signaling, Legionella has evolved a remarkable arsenal of hydrolytic effectors that directly target and inactivate core autophagy machinery. Two particularly well-characterized examples, the cysteine protease RavZ and serine protease Lpg1137, exemplify the pathogen's sophisticated strategies for autophagy disruption.

The effector RavZ orchestrates an irreversible blockade of autophagosome formation through its unique proteolytic activity against lipidated LC3 [192]. Unlike the host protease ATG4 which cleaves LC3-PE after the C-terminal glycine, RavZ specifically recognizes PE-conjugated LC3 through its N-terminal interaction domain and hydrolyzes the amide bond preceding glycine. This distinctive cleavage mechanism, coupled with RavZ's PI3P-binding mediated localization to autophagosomal membranes, creates an irreparable defect in LC3 recycling [192–194]. The resulting inhibition of autophagosome biogenesis not only protects Legionella but also provides cross-protection against xenophagic clearance of other intracellular pathogens including Salmonella, Coxiella and Listeria, demonstrating the broad-spectrum impact of this virulence strategy [195–197].

Complementing the action of RavZ, the effector Lpg1137 executes a dual sabotage of host defenses through precise targeting of syntaxin 17 (STX17) at mitochondria-ER contact sites [198]. Lpg1137's C-terminal phosphatidic acid (PA)-binding domain directs its localization to mitochondria-associated membrane (MAM), where its serine protease activity not only disrupts STX17-ATG14L complexes to abort autophagosome nucleation, but also interferes with STX17-DRP1 interactions to suppress mitochondrial fission and BAX-mediated apoptosis [199, 200]. This coordinated disruption of both autophagy initiation and apoptotic signaling highlights Legionella's ability to simultaneously neutralize multiple host defense pathways through a single effector protein.

Conclusion and further perspective

This review highlights the complex interplay and outcomes of the host–pathogen evolutionary arms race. Xenophagy and regulated cell death represent critical cell-autonomous defense mechanisms that eliminate intracellular bacterial replication niches. In response, bacterial pathogens have evolved sophisticated countermeasures, deploying secretion system effectors with diverse biochemical activities to subvert these host defenses. Notably, many pathogens employ multiple effectors targeting the same host pathway, creating redundant protective layers. For instance, Shigella utilizes several effectors to inhibit non-canonical pyroptosis in human cells: IpaH9.8 prevents GBP1-mediated LPS release [67, 68], OspC3 to block caspase-4 inflammasome activation [49], and IpaH7.8 to degrade GSDMD [75]. Similarly, L. pneumophila deploys an array of effectors to evade xenophagic capture, including Lot DUBs and RavD to remove ubiquitin signals [153–155], SidEs to displace autophagy adaptors via non-canonical ubiquitination [179, 197], and RavZ to cleave PE-LC3 [192]. Meanwhile, some effectors can manipulate cellular defenses by catalyzing PTM towards different host substrates. A notable example provided by our group and others is Salmonella SseK3. SseK3 can efficiently inhibit cell death by targeting TNFR1 [35, 36], impede cytokine release via Rab1 modification [40, 41], and contribute to the intracellular replicative structure maturation by modifying SNAREs [42]. Furthermore, investigations into bacterial interference strategies have not only revealed novel enzymatic activities of effectors but also elucidated fundamental host defense mechanisms. For example, Xu et al. identified the critical role of V-ATPase in sensing membrane damage and initiating autophagosome formation via ATG16L1 interaction, a discovery made possible by characterizing the Salmonella effector SopF [134].

Beyond the RCD pathways discussed, other forms of cell death like ferroptosis, excitotoxicity, and entosis, as well as selective autophagy (e.g., mitophagy, reticulophagy, ribophagy) may influence intracellular bacterial replication [201, 202]. Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent lipid peroxidation-driven cell death process, exhibits context-dependent roles in infection [203]. While Mycobacterium tuberculosis exploits ferroptosis to enhance pathogenicity and dissemination in macrophages [204], ferroptotic stress conversely strengthens macrophage defenses against Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Salmonella pullorum [205]. Additionally, mitophagy, which removes damaged mitochondria to regulate immune responses, is increasingly recognized as a target for bacterial manipulation [206]. Several pathogens induce host mitophagy to suppress mitochondrial-mediated inflammation or evade oxidative stress, thereby promoting intracellular survival [207–210]. However, the specific role of bacterially encoded effector proteins and their novel PTMs in modulating these forms of cell death and autophagy remains poorly understood, warranting further mechanistic investigation.

This review primarily focuses on virulence factors that inhibit host cell death for the benefit of the pathogen. However, it should be noted that certain bacterial virulence factors can trigger effector-triggered immunity (ETI) by the PTMs of host cell death pathways [211]. This mechanism enables the host to detect pathogens and distinguish them from non-pathogenic microorganisms, thereby providing a protective benefit. For example, the group A streptococcal virulence factor SpeB, although not a classical effector, triggers host cell pyroptosis through a unique proteolytic mechanism [212]. Specifically, SpeB cleaves GSDMA at Gln246, generating an active GSDMA-NTD that induces pyroptosis in keratinocytes and promotes acute inflammation. Gsdma1-deficient mice exhibit increased susceptibility to GAS infection in a SpeB-dependent manner, underscoring the protective role of GSDMA in sensing SpeB and initiating pyroptosis [213, 214]. Another example is YopJ from Yersinia, an AvrA homolog, which selectively targets TAK1 during infection. By acetylating TAK1, Yersinia initiates a signaling cascade involving TNF Complex IIb assembly, caspase-8 activation, and GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, ultimately eliciting a host-protective immune response [215]. These findings highlight bacterial PTMs function as key elements in the evolutionary arms race between host and pathogen.

This review summarizes unconventional PTMs catalyzed by bacterial effectors, including Arg-GlcNAcylation, ADP-riboxanation, PR-ubiquitination, and atypical proteolysis. Deciphering how pathogens manipulate cell death and xenophagy through these PTMs not only advances our understanding of infection mechanisms but also opens avenues for clinical and biotechnological applications. 1) Antibacterial Drug Development. Given the direct link between bacterial PTMs and virulence, targeting these modifications via small-molecule inhibitors or effector degradation could yield novel therapeutics. Such strategies may circumvent antibiotic resistance by specifically disabling virulence mechanisms. 2) Diagnostic Biomarkers. Bacterial effectors and their unique PTMs are structurally distinct from host proteins and often irreversible. Antibodies against pathogen-specific PTMs or PCR-based effector detection could enable highly specific diagnostics for infectious diseases. 3) Precision Medicine Applications. Bacterial effectors with immunomodulatory or cell death-regulatory properties offer promising therapeutic leads. For example, E. coli NleB, an NF-κB pathway inhibitor, might ameliorate autoimmune disorders like amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), while CopC, a broad-spectrum cell death suppressor, could mitigate chemotherapy-induced tissue damage in cancer patients. By unraveling the molecular strategies of bacterial effectors, this field holds immense potential for revolutionizing infectious disease management, diagnostics, and beyond.
