A standardized manual goniometry protocol to quantify joint range of motion in women with rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: M Lozada-Mellado, JM García-Morales, JA Pineda-Juárez, L Castillo-Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatoid arthritis causes persistent synovial inflammation, pain, and progressive joint damage that limits the range of motion. Although ROM is clinically relevant, in routine practice it is often assessed incompletely or only qualitatively. The technologies available to quantify it, such as digital inclinometers, electrogoniometers, and motion capture systems, are frequently limited by their cost, the infrastructure they require, and the difficulty of measuring impaired or deformed joints. This protocol describes a standardized manual goniometry procedure to quantify the ROM in six joint regions (hip, knee, ankle, shoulder, elbow, and wrist) in women with established RA, using an accessible, portable, and inexpensive instrument. It covers participant recruitment and selection, preparation before the assessment, evaluator standardization, general measurement principles, and measurement of each joint, complemented by the calculation of the expected ROM percentage. The protocol allows joint impairment to be characterized by anatomical region and movement pattern, which supports clinical evaluation and the design of targeted interventions.•A standardized and reproducible manual goniometry protocol to quantify joint ROM in women with rheumatoid arthritis.•It covers six major joint regions (hip, knee, ankle, shoulder, elbow, and wrist) using an inexpensive and portable universal goniometer.•It includes standardization of the evaluator and calculation of the expected ROM percentage, which allows comparison between joints and between individuals.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Range of motion, Goniometry, Joint mobility, Functional assessment

Background

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by persistent and sustained inflammation of the synovial membrane [1]. This inflammatory process causes progressive deterioration of the synovial joints, leading to chronic pain, structural damage, and even other systemic alterations resulting from the persistent inflammatory state [2]. Several studies have documented the impact on quality of life and physical function in these patients, highlighting limitations in joint mobility. Reduced range of motion (ROM) impairs the ability to perform activities of daily living and contributes to premature disability, a common complication in RA; these mobility limitations are particularly evident in older patients with RA, in whom age-related musculoskeletal changes and comorbidities further restrict physical function [3,4]. Assessing ROM allows us to understand musculoskeletal function in women with long-term RA.

Despite its clinical relevance, joint mobility is often assessed incompletely or only qualitatively in daily clinical practice. Instruments such as the Disease Activity Score (DAS) and the Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ) provide general information on disease activity and functional status; however, they do not allow identification of the joints with the greatest impairment or the extent of structural and functional compromise [5].

Multiple technologies have been used to assess movement, such as digital inclinometers, two-dimensional photogrammetry, three-dimensional motion analysis, sensor-based systems, and mobile applications. These tools offer advantages in terms of accuracy and dynamic analysis during functional tasks [[6], [7], [8]]. However, their implementation is not always possible in clinical settings because they require expensive infrastructure, highly trained personnel, complex calibration procedures, and controlled conditions that are not always available. In patients with joint impairment, such as RA, assessing mobility in small joints or in those with significant structural deformities presents measurement challenges. Furthermore, fluctuating inflammation or pain can interfere with proper assessment, reducing its reliability [9,10].

Therefore, manual goniometry remains the most widely used method in clinical practice. It is an accessible, simple, low-cost, and portable tool with good validity when applied using standardized techniques, enabling quantification of joint mobility. Despite this, its use and reporting in the RA population are limited, restricted to a few joints, and omit relevant movements [[11], [12], [13]]. The literature commonly evaluates only one or two joints, which does not provide a comprehensive overview of joint involvement.

On the other hand, there is limited information describing the ROM profile in patients with RA in detail. Having a description of the ROM would allow us to assess joint status, identify priority joints, predict potential functional limitations, and understand the impact on daily living activities [14]. This information is fundamental for designing more precise rehabilitation interventions and individualizing exercise plans.

In this context, it is necessary to establish a clear, reproducible method to quantify ROM and facilitate its clinical application. Describing ROM in women with long-term RA will contribute to characterizing the functional status of this population and will provide information for the assessment and design of interventions.

The importance of this work come from providing a single, standardized, and reproducible manual goniometry protocol. This protocol assesses joint range of motion in six major joint areas: the hip, knee, ankle, shoulder, elbow, and wrist in women with RA. Unlike earlier reports that usually focus on just one or two joints and leave out important movements, this protocol offers a complete functional profile. It uses an easy-to-carry, affordable universal goniometer that does not need expensive infrastructure or complicated calibration.

One of its key features is the use of the expected ROM percentage (%ROM). This normalizes the measurements against reference values, allowing direct comparisons between different joints, movements, and individuals. On the other hand, standardization, low cost, and comparability make the protocol effective in identifying the most affected and preserved joints. It can guide personalized rehabilitation and exercise plans, as well as provide a consistent baseline for monitoring disease progress and treatment outcomes.

Description of protocol

This protocol provides a clear and reproducible procedure for quantifying joint ROM using manual goniometry in women with rheumatoid arthritis. The validation data came from a sample made up only of women with established RA. Therefore, the reference values reported here should be used with caution when considering men or other disease stages. Additionally, the protocol should not be used for patients with severe pain, acute synovitis, or those who cannot assume the positions needed for measurement. These conditions affect both the safety and reliability of the assessment.

Protocol validation

The protocol was applied to 120 women with long-standing RA. Before data collection, a single physiotherapist was standardized by measuring 10 patients who were not included in the final sample, and ICC was confirmed to be acceptable for each joint and movement, supporting the consistency of the measurements obtained with this procedure.

The clinical and demographic characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 47.6 ± 11.7 years, and the median disease duration was 10 years (6 - 20). Most participants were in functional class I (67.5%), followed by class II (25%) and class III (7.5%). The median body mass index was 26.6 kg/m² (24.2 to 29.2), and handgrip strength had a median of 14 kg (10 - 18.6). Disease activity was low to moderate, with a DAS28 of 2.8 ± 1.1. The median SF-36 quality of life questionnaire was 55.6 (37.4- 76.8), and the median HAQ-DI score was 0.88 (0.38 - 1.25). The most frequent comorbidities were hypertension (19.2%), dyslipidemia (17.5%), and hypothyroidism (14.2%).

Fig. 1 shows the %ROM with its 95% confidence interval, enabling identification of the movements with the highest and lowest degrees of preservation. The highest %ROM values were observed in hip adduction, hip extension, knee flexion, ulnar deviation, elbow flexion, and elbow supination, all of which reached or exceeded 90% of the expected ROM. In contrast, ankle dorsiflexion showed one of the lowest percentages, close to 50% of the expected value. Reductions were also observed in hip rotation, hip abduction, and shoulder extension. At the wrist, palmar flexion showed a moderate degree of preservation, while ulnar deviation remained close to the reference values.

The %ROM showed significant negative correlations with the HAQ in the hip (r= −0.43), shoulder (r= −0.43), wrist (r= −0.39), ankle (r= −0.29), and elbow (r= −0.24), indicating that greater preserved mobility was associated with lower disability.

Significant correlations were found with the SF-36 in the hip (r = 0.33), shoulder (r = 0.30), ankle (r = 0.22) and wrist (r = 0.22), indicating that greater joint mobility was associated with better quality of life.

These data show that the protocol can quantify ROM and identify joint involvement by anatomical region and movement, confirming that it provides a functional characterization of mobility in this population.

Limitations

Manual goniometry has limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The identification of anatomical landmarks can be less precise in patients with marked joint deformities, periarticular edema, or excess adipose tissue, which may affect measurement accuracy. In addition, although intra-rater reliability is good, inter-rater reliability can be lower, ranging from moderate to excellent (ICC 0.62 to 0.95) depending on the joint and the movement assessed; for this reason, all measurements for the same patient should be performed by a single standardized evaluator [12,13,15]. Recent AI assisted methods have been suggested to reduce evaluator dependency. However, they still show only moderate agreement with manual goniometry and require additional validation before routine clinical use. Therefore, standardized manual goniometry continues to be the reference method in this context [16].

The protocol focuses on the large joints of the upper and lower limbs and excludes the metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints, which are frequently affected in rheumatoid arthritis. Adding these joints in future versions of the protocol would provide a more complete characterization of the functional movement pattern.

Finally, the validation data were obtained from a sample of women only, so the values should be extrapolated with caution to men or to other stages of the disease. The protocol should also not be applied to patients with severe pain at rest, acute synovitis, or an inability to adopt the positions required for measurement
