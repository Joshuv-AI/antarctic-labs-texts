How accurately does L band vegetation optical depth predict aboveground biomass?
Authors: Yuan Zhang, Philippe Ciais, Jean-Pierre Wigneron, Jérôme Chave, Nan Cong, Xiaojun Li, Yan Yang, Sassan Saatchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The performances of different methods to predict AGB using L-VOD are investigated.•Adding tree cover remarkably improves L-VOD-based AGB prediction.•All methods fail to capture AGB spatial variation in dense rainforests, where reference data also show large discrepancies.•The temporal relationships between AGB and L-VOD differ from the spatial relationships.Keywords: L-VOD, Aboveground biomass, Space-for-time, Tree cover

Introduction

Biomass is one of the most important carbon pools in the global carbon cycle due to its large stock and vulnerability to climate and land use changes (Liu et al., 2013, Yang et al., 2023). Despite its critical role, large-scale biomass remains poorly quantified due to methodological constraints in spatiotemporal monitoring. Recent advances in vegetation optical depth (VOD) have provided new opportunities to characterize large-scale biomass changes (Frappart et al., 2020). Although widely adopted in recent studies (Fan et al., 2019, Wigneron et al., 2021, Wigneron et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2022), the capabilities of VOD-derived biomass estimates remain insufficiently explored. Therefore, a systematic evaluation of VOD's predictive capacity for biomass is urgently needed to enhance the reliability of this proxy and reduce uncertainties in biomass predictions.

VOD does not directly measure biomass but quantifies the extinction of microwave radiation propagating through vegetation canopies, resulting from absorption, scattering, and emission processes. It is reported to be sensitive to the total water content of aboveground living wood and leaf tissues (Jackson and Schmugge, 1991). This total water content can be further decomposed into the product of the living biomass and the water content in vegetation tissues (Frappart et al., 2020; Schmidt et al., 2023). Assuming a constant water content in the living biomass, during the wet season when tree water content is maximal (Yang et al., 2022), or averaged at the yearly scale, VOD has been found effective for estimating AGB and inferring its spatiotemporal changes (Liu et al., 2015, Fan et al., 2019, Moesinger et al., 2020).

Current VOD products span multiple microwave frequencies, including Ku-band (19 GHz), X-band (10.7 GHz), C-band (6.9 GHz), and L-band (1.4 GHz) (Moesinger et al., 2020, Wigneron et al., 2021, Li et al., 2022). Among these products, L-VOD exhibits superior sensitivity to total AGB, as longer wavelengths penetrate vegetation more effectively (Chaparro et al., 2019; Schmidt et al., 2022). Because of this advantage, L-VOD has become the most used proxy for investigating AGB changes. For instance, Fan et al. (2019) used it to investigate tropical AGB changes during 2010–2017, and detected an increase during 2010–2012 La Niña event, and a decrease during the 2015–2016 El Niño event. Wigneron et al. (2020) applied it to characterize AGB changes after the 2015–2016 El Niño and found a slow recovery. Yang et al. (2022) further investigated the factors regulating forest recovery using the same L-VOD data. L-VOD was also used in the high northern latitudes, where it indicated a decrease in living biomass in northern Siberian forests (Fan et al., 2023). These applications demonstrate L-VOD's capacity to resolve spatiotemporal AGB dynamics at large scales.

The methodological divergence implies a large fundamental gap in understanding L-VOD-biomass relationships. It causes difficulties in comparing among studies and attributing AGB changes, and propagates model-specific biases through AGB estimates. Therefore, a rigorous evaluation of model performance with identification of model limitations is a crucial step to improve the accuracy of AGB estimation.

Besides the empirical parametric models, previous studies on L-VOD-based AGB variation also rely on a space-for-time assumption, such that temporal variation in AGB is well reflected in the spatial variation of a single-time dataset (Fan et al., 2019, Wigneron et al., 2021, Yang et al., 2022). This assumption is classically made in chronosequence approaches (Johnson and Miyanishi, 2008) but has never been carefully tested in L-VOD. L-VOD signals contain not only information on biomass, but also on canopy structure, canopy and soil water content, which the space-for-time assumption might fail to account for. An invalid space-for-time assumption may result in an artificial temporal change of biomass, leading to consequently biased understanding on the carbon cycle. Therefore, it is also crucial to examine to what extent this assumption is valid for L-VOD-biomass relationships.

Evaluating L-VOD-based AGB predictions require reliable AGB references. While field measurements traditionally serve as validation benchmarks, recent analyses have found that AGB changes from forest inventories are underestimated by all large-scale AGB datasets (Araza et al., 2023). This implies a large bias due to the scale mismatch between the 0.1-ha inventory plots and remote sensing datasets that have much coarser resolutions (typically 25 km for L-VOD). Therefore, the spatial consistency must be considered in evaluating L-VOD-based AGB predictions.

In order to understand how well L-VOD can predict biomass, we evaluated the methodology to infer AGB from L-VOD in this study. We first aim to evaluate the existing fitting equations used to infer AGB from L-VOD, and propose new methods for improving the fitting accuracy. To match the coarse resolution of L-VOD, we use five reference AGB datasets derived from remote-sensing measurements (Saatchi et al., 2011, Xu et al., 2021, Yu et al., 2023, Dubayah et al., 2022, Santoro and Cartus, 2023). Second, we test the validity of the space-for-time assumption in inferring AGB changes. Our study addresses the following questions: (1) Which of the existing methods for fitting the AGB-L-VOD spatial relationship performs the best? (2) Can the AGB model be improved by new models or by including additional information? (3) What is the capacity of L-VOD to infer temporal variation in AGB based on a space-for-time inference method?

Data and methods

L-VOD, AGB and additional factors

We utilized the 25-km monthly L-VOD data from January 2010 to May 2021, retrieved using the SMOS (Soil Moisture and Ocean Salinity)-IC (Institut National de la Recherche Agronomique and Centre d’Etudes Spatiales de la BIOsphère) algorithm based on the Level-3 brightness temperature product (Version RE06) from the Soil Moisture and Ocean Salinity mission. As the latest SMOS product, SMOS-IC was developed using an optimized inversion of the L-MEB (L-band Microwave Emission of the Biosphere) model (Li et al., 2020; Wigneron et al., 2021) and serves as the reference L-VOD dataset in the European Space Agency Climate Change Initiative Project ‘REgional Carbon Cycle Assessment and Processes’, Phase 2 (RECCAP2) (https://climate.esa.int/en/projects/reccap-2/). The high accuracy of SMOS-IC L-VOD compared to other counterparts has been demonstrated in several evaluation studies (Li et al., 2021; Cui et al., 2023). We restricted our analysis to the ascending observations of the SMOS satellite, as they correspond to mornings when the vegetation is less water-stressed. To minimize the impact of short-term (<2 months) anomalies in L-VOD data, we employed a filter that de-seasonalizes and smooths the data in time at each pixel, as commonly used to smooth atmospheric CO2 measurements by NOAA (Thoning et al., 1989). Due to a significant amount of missing data in 2010 and incomplete time series in 2021, all analyses were conducted using yearly average L-VOD from 2011 to 2020.

The first reference dataset is a 1-km AGB map for 2015 updated from the tropical biomass product of Saatchi et al. (2011) (available from S.S.S. (sasan.s.saatchi@jpl.nasa.gov)), which has been used in Wigneron et al. (2021) and Fan et al. (2019). Two additional datasets are global biomass maps from Xu et al. (2021) (https://zenodo.org/records/4161694) covering the period 2000–2019 at a resolution of 0.1-degree, and a newly developed 100-m global biomass dataset for the period 2014–2021 (Yu et al., 2023) (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7583611). These two datasets are globally available biomass datasets with yearly time series. Here we converted them to AGB based on the map of aboveground to total biomass ratio (related to the root:shoot ratio) published by Spawn et al. (2020) (https://doi.org/10.3334/ORNLDAAC/1763). For convenience, these AGB datasets are referred to as “Saatchi2015”, “Xu2021” and “Yu2023” in this study. These AGB datasets are all based on machine-learning models trained on local field measurements and using optical and high-frequency microwave datasets for upscaling. Besides the three machine-learning-based AGB datasets, we also used the 2017 100-m AGB map from the ESA CCI dataset v4 (hereafter referred to as “CCI”) (https://climate.esa.int/en/projects/biomass/data/), which uses more physics-based models to retrieve AGB from remote sensing observations (Santoro and Cartus, 2023). Finally, we included the Global Ecosystem Dynamics Investigation (GEDI) L4B AGB product, which provides lidar-based 1-km AGB estimates between 52°S and 52°N as supplementary (Dubayah et al., 2022, hereafter referred to as “GEDI”) (https://daac.ornl.gov/GEDI/guides/GEDI_L4B_Gridded_Biomass.html). For consistency and comparability, all datasets were remapped to a resolution of 0.1-degree using area-weighted average except for GEDI data due to its larger proportion of observation gaps. For GEDI data, we averaged the valid AGB in each 0.1-degree grid, and removed grids without valid measurements. Additionally, pixels with more than half of the five reference datasets showing AGB values below 1 MgC ha−1 were excluded from the analyses, which accounted for 28.8 % of the land pixels and are mainly distributed in deserts.

The relationships between AGB and L-VOD are expected to vary with vegetation types and cover, as these affect the microwave emissivity signals from L-VOD. In order to investigate how these vegetation properties influence the relationship between AGB and L-VOD, we integrated additional factors into the models. Specifically, we described canopy structure and phenology by selecting the yearly mean and standard deviation from the 0.05-degree monthly Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI_mean and EVI_std) derived from the MODIS dataset (MYD13C2, Didan, 2021) (https://lpdaac.usgs.gov/products/myd13c2v061/), the 30-m tree cover (Hansen et al., 2013) (https://glad.umd.edu/dataset/global-2010-tree-cover-30-m) and the 1-km global map of tree density (Crowther et al., 2015) (https://elischolar.library.yale.edu/yale_fes_data/1/). To ensure spatial consistency across datasets, we aggregated them to a resolution of 0.1 degrees.

Fitting methods

In addition to equations (1–3), we utilized a method called Multivariate Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS) to fit the AGB-L-VOD relationship. Unlike parametric equations, MARS searches for the optimal breakpoints to divide the predictor space and fits a linear model to each segment (Friedman, 1991). This approach enables the creation of a flexible model capable of capturing nonlinear relationship between the predictor and the response variable, making it suitable for investigating the potential of fitting with a single predictor. For the fitting of AGB using two explanatory variables (L-VOD and an additional factor), we used random forest models as there was no prior knowledge on how these factors influence AGB. We randomly sampled 40,000 data points from the global datasets and allocated 90 % of the data for training and 10 % for validation. This methodology ensured robust model calibration with minimal overfitting (Fig. S1, S2).

Models built using random forests are highly flexible but challenging to interpret. To understand how the factors influence AGB values, we used SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), an established method (Lundberg and Lee, 2017, Zhang et al., 2022), to represent how each factor influences the model's AGB predictions. In a complex model, the SHAP values for each factor are calculated at each sample, representing the expectance of how much the factor value pushes the sample prediction away from the model's average prediction. Analyzing the distribution of SHAP values of all factors across all samples allows to visualize how each factor generally affects AGB predictions, identify which factors are most important overall, and understand how factors interact. In this study, SHAP was specifically used to investigate the interactions between L-VOD and other factors in the RF model.

Sensitivity analyses

We analyzed how the sensitivity of AGB to L-VOD varied across different regions. For the sensitivity quantification, we performed a linear regression between AGB and L-VOD for the 5-degree window centered at each pixel. The resulting slope of this regression represents the spatial sensitivity at the pixel scale. Similarly, we calculated the temporal sensitivity by estimating the slope of a linear fit between AGB and L-VOD annual time series (only for the Xu2021 and Yu2023 datasets). Because the sensitivity of AGB to L-VOD depends on the absolute value of L-VOD, we grouped the pixel-level sensitivities into different L-VOD bins for comparison. To ensure statistical validity, pixels with insignificant fits (p > 0.1) were excluded from the comparison.

Results

Performance of different fitting methods

The L-VOD fittings of AGB using different methods are shown in Fig. 1a-c, g, h. We found that the parametric equations (Eqs. 1–3) explain 69 %–77 % of the AGB variation depending on the reference maps, with the three fitting curves being very similar when L-VOD is smaller than 1.1. This indicates generally good and similar performances of these methods. However, for values of L-VOD higher than 1.1, Eq. (3) results in larger estimates of AGB than the other equations. In comparison, the curves and R2 values of MARS models are similar to those of the three parametric equations. The minimal improvement from using the non-parametric method implies that the uncertainties in AGB fittings are unlikely to be due to the choice of equation.

To further investigate the sources of uncertainty, we calculated the correlation coefficients (R) between predicted AGB and reference AGB per L-VOD bin of width 0.3 (Fig. 1d-f, i, j). The results indicate that regardless of the reference AGB dataset used, all fits exhibit a largely decreasing R as L-VOD increases. In areas where L-VOD is less than 1, the fits for all references by all methods generally display R values of 0.35–0.6 in each bin. However, when L-VOD is over 1, all methods fail to predict reference AGB accurately and R values drop below 0.3. Exceptions are found in Saatchi2015 AGB fitted by MARS and Yu2023 AGB fitted by all the methods, where R increases to 0.35 when L-VOD > 1.2. However, this does not significantly improve the overall fittings due to the limited number of samples in high L-VOD regions. The better performance of MARS compared to other equations at high L-VOD levels in the Saatchi2015 dataset is probably because the complex patterns in Saatchi2015′s model, arising from sparse training data with high variability, cannot be represented in simple parametric equations.

The similar performance of MARS and the parametric equations implies that using L-VOD alone is insufficient to further improve AGB estimates. Hence, we investigated the potential of including additional information to improve predictions (Fig. 2). Our results show that incorporating information on greenness (EVI_mean), phenology (EVI_std), tree cover, and tree density all leads to better models than using L-VOD alone, with R2 increasing by −0.02–0.04 (EVI_mean), −0.01–0.02 (EVI_std), 0.05–0.11 (tree cover) and 0.01–0.04 (tree density). The models combining L-VOD and tree cover achieved superior performance across all AGB references (Fig. 2 compared with Fig. 1). Compared with the models built on L-VOD alone, adding tree cover information explained the scatter around the fitting curves, highlighting the contribution of tree cover to the prediction of AGB. In spite of the large improvement from tree cover, substantial unexplained variance persists in regions with L-VOD > 1 (Fig. 2).

Uncertainty in AGB reference maps

We examined the R between three AGB references in 2015 at different L-VOD levels (Fig. 3) and found similar decorrelation at large L-VOD levels. When L-VOD is > 1.1, there is almost no correlation between the Saatchi2015 and the Yu2023 datasets. This inconsistency suggests large uncertainties in the existing AGB reference datasets, which may further result in considerable uncertainties in L-VOD based estimates. When comparing different models, we found that the correlations between L-VOD + Tree cover models and the AGB reference maps are comparable to those between AGB references. It should be noted that all the three AGB reference datasets are estimated from non-parametric model predictions using remote sensing observations (but no L-VOD data), and the true AGB remains unknown. When performing similar analyses using the data from 2017 and 2019 with CCI and GEDI references, we found similar large declines of R in high L-VOD regions, except for the relationships between CCI and Xu2021 (Fig. S3) and between GEDI and Yu2023 (Fig. S4), with smaller declines of R at high L-VOD level. These results indicate that one of the current main factors limiting AGB prediction is the AGB reference accuracy.

Spatial vs temporal sensitivities

The key assumption for predicting AGB temporal variation using models built from a spatial analysis is the spatiotemporal consistency of AGB-L-VOD sensitivity. To test this assumption, we calculated both spatial and temporal sensitivities between AGB and L-VOD across L-VOD levels using different references and predictions (Fig. 5). The spatial sensitivity derived from the AGB references (dotted lines in Fig. 5) shows an increasing trend when L-VOD is smaller than 0.8, with values from 40 to 100 MgC ha−1 per L-VOD unit in regions with L-VOD < 0.1 to 120–170 MgC ha−1 per L-VOD unit in regions with 0.6–0.8 L-VOD. The sensitivity then plateaus and slightly decreases at the highest L-VOD levels. This trend is generally captured by the fits with Eqs. (1), (2) and the MARS models, except for a significant overestimation of AGB sensitivity in regions with L-VOD between 0.6 and 1, where the model-based sensitivity exceeds 200 MgC ha−1 per L-VOD unit. At higher L-VOD level, the sensitivity based on Eq. (3) shows a continuous increasing trend, which is different from those obtained from the AGB references, implying that the power function (Eq. (3) may have a risk of overestimating AGB in dense forests. Compared with the models built using L-VOD alone (thin lines in Fig. 5), adding tree cover into the model (thick black line in Fig. 5) significantly reduces the sensitivity in medium L-VOD levels and shows better consistency with the references (dotted lines in Fig. 5), highlighting the benefit of including tree cover information to make predictions more consistent with AGB reference maps. We found similar results using GEDI data in mid- and low-latitude (Fig. S7), indicating the robustness of benefits from tree cover.

Compared with the spatial sensitivities, the temporal AGB sensitivities to L-VOD show large uncertainties at all L-VOD levels (Fig. 5, S6). This may indicate large variations in temporal sensitivity among regions, but could also be due to the limited time series of L-VOD and AGB datasets. The temporal sensitivities range between 0 and 30 MgC ha−1 per L-VOD unit in the Yu2023 dataset and 0–80 MgC ha−1 per L-VOD unit in the Xu2021 dataset, which are smaller than the spatial sensitivities estimated using the same AGB dataset. Similar to the spatial sensitivities, the temporal sensitivities also exhibit a peak at medium L-VOD levels (0.6–0.8).

Interannual AGB changes

Using different models, we predicted AGB interannual variations and compared them with the Xu2021 and the Yu2023 datasets in five regions with large forest areas over the globe (Fig. 6). The results reveal marked discrepancies between the Xu2021 and the Yu2023 in representing AGB changes, especially in tropical regions. The Xu2021 dataset shows an increase of AGB in all the three tropical regions after 2015, while the Yu2023 dataset indicates a loss of biomass in the same regions during the same period.

For the L-VOD based predictions, the models built with L-VOD alone estimate very similar AGB changes in each region, with AGB increases by 0.3–3 MgC ha−1 in different regions between 2015 and 2020. For the models built using the (L-VOD + tree cover) model, assuming constant tree cover over the period yields similar AGB interannual variation to the L-VOD alone models but with smaller biomass changes. However, when considering the tree cover changes, a decreasing trend of AGB is found in all three tropical regions. It is important to note that the Hansen et al. (2013) dataset currently only provides yearly tree cover loss, while for tree cover gain, only the total gain between 2000–2012 is available. In the prediction with varying tree cover, we have to assume a constant gain rate, which may not match the reality.

Although our results show a significant improvement on the AGB fit by combining L-VOD and tree cover, an accurate prediction of AGB changes remains a large challenge. One limitation is the lack of high-frequency tree cover gain information, which limits the application of well-trained models. Unlike tree cover loss, which is detectable from space, tree cover gain occurs slowly, and it may take years before young forests become detectable from space. The Tropical Moist Forests (TMF) product provides alternative tree cover data in tropical rainforests (Vancutsem et al., 2021). This dataset has annual maps of forest disturbance and regrowth. Therefore, it better indicates the changes of tree cover. Using this dataset, we predict a similar AGB interannual variation to the predictions based on the Hansen tree cover (with changes) in tropical rainforests (Fig. S8). The only limitation is that the TMF dataset focuses on tropical forests only, and does not have the information of other forest types, thus it cannot be used in predicting global AGB changes.

Discussion

Choice of fitting methods

Accurately fitting the relationship between AGB and L-VOD is a key step to predict the biomass changes. Our results indicate that once well calibrated, all the existing methods generally predict the overall spatial variation of AGB. However, their poor performance in high-L-VOD regions (predominantly tropical rainforests) introduces substantial uncertainty. An earlier study has investigated the dependence of AGB on L-VOD and found that in less dense forests, the relationship is slightly concave, while in very dense forests, a saturation of L-VOD with AGB is detected (Mialon et al., 2020). The three equations evaluated in this study can all represent the concave relationship in less dense forests, while they differ remarkably in dense forests. The similar performance of the three equations implies that the concave relationship in less dense forests is more important in capturing global AGB variation. Nevertheless, since the forests with large L-VOD account for only 10 % of the vegetation area but 30 % of the AGB between 20oS and 20oN, it is still important to consider these large uncertainties when explaining previous L-VOD based results in the tropics (Fan et al., 2019, Yang et al., 2022). Considering the concave power equation may result in too large AGB variation at high L-VOD levels, we do not recommend using Eq. (3) in dense rainforests.

The marginal improvement from non-parametric methods underscores the necessity of incorporating additional information into L-VOD-based AGB estimates. Our results suggest that tree cover demonstrates robust complementary value. Tree cover was not used as a predictor in generating the machine-learning-based AGB references in previous studies (Saatchi et al., 2011, Xu et al., 2021, Yu et al., 2023). Therefore, the improvement in model performance by incorporating tree cover information is not an artifact. To further investigate how adding tree cover improved the prediction, we analyzed the SHAP values in the random forest models (Fig. 7). The results indicate a strong dependence between the L-VOD SHAP values and tree cover. At low L-VOD levels, larger tree cover corresponds to smaller SHAP values, i.e., smaller contribution from L-VOD to AGB. At high L-VOD levels, most fittings also show smaller SHAP values when tree cover is larger, except for the fitting for Yu2023 and CCI AGB, which show an opposite pattern. The dependence of L-VOD SHAP values on tree cover suggests that the information in L-VOD differs with canopy structure. Therefore, it is a necessary step to include such information to achieve better biomass predictions based on L-VOD.

Uncertainties in AGB

The uncertainties in L-VOD-based AGB prediction come from multiple sources. The first source is related to the fitting methods. Our analyses reveal inherent imperfections in existing fitting equations and identify a way to improve the model. However, current AGB reference uncertainties (Fig. 3), particularly in dense forests, impose fundamental accuracy ceilings. The AGB references are based on machine-learning algorithms or semi-empirical models and remote sensing signals, with training samples from inventory plots (Saatchi et al., 2011, Xu et al., 2021, Yu et al., 2023, Dubayah et al., 2022, Santoro and Cartus, 2023). Although the training samples have overlaps among these AGB products, the machine-learning algorithms are different. Since non-parametric fittings always have the risk of extrapolation of data and the dense forest is at the high end of AGB distribution, large uncertainties appear (Fig. 3). Furthermore, although L-band microwave can penetrate deeper in the canopy than X- and C- bands (Frappart et al., 2020), it remains unclear whether L-VOD is affected by saturation in very dense and tall forests. The uncertainties in dense forests from both AGB and L-VOD may cause the fitting failure of all methods at high L-VOD levels. The failure across all methods at high L-VOD (non-parametric included) suggests signal-to-noise degradation rather than methodological bias. Due to the lack of other reliable benchmark datasets, discerning whether signal-to-noise degradation stems from possible L-VOD saturation, AGB uncertainty, or both remains challenging. Because this fitting uncertainty in dense forests is currently unavoidable and can consequently propagate into inaccurate AGB prediction, any attempt to explain the L-VOD-based AGB in dense forests needs to be cautious.

To further improve model prediction, the limitation of current AGB references in dense forests must be addressed. First, to reduce uncertainties from data extrapolation in AGB references, more in situ AGB measurements are needed in the highly dense forests, especially in underrepresented regions such as African and Southeast Asian rainforests. To address uncertainty from scale mismatch (Araza et al., 2023), AGB upscaling based on high-density plot networks can be used. Second, features more sensitive to AGB variations are needed in generating the AGB references. The Saatchi2015 and Xu2021 datasets have shortwave signals (visible and infrared bands depending on the dataset), and Ku-band signal in their model. The shortwave bands cannot penetrate deep in very closed canopy while Ku-band signal may also saturate if the forest is too dense (Frappart et al., 2020). The Yu2023 dataset used ALOS L-band backscatter besides near infrared and shortwave infrared signal. L-band radiation has a longer wavelength than Ku band, but the L-band backscatter may also saturate in dense forests (Mermoz et al., 2015). Similarly, the usage of ALOS L-band data in the CCI algorithm may be insufficient in mapping AGB in dense forests. As vegetation with different structures may show different responses to environmental changes, including proxies for vegetation physiology (e.g. vegetation sensitivity to precipitation) with field validation might be a direction to explore for further improving the quality of AGB datasets.

Besides the fitting process, AGB predictions may also inherit uncertainty from predictor datasets. For instance, the choice of tree cover changes resulted in remarkable differences in the L-VOD + TreeCover −based AGB predictions (Fig. 6), even reversing AGB trends in some tropical regions if tree cover changes are omitted. Therefore, the variables incorporated into the L-VOD-AGB model must be reliable enough to improve AGB prediction without introducing large uncertainties.

Space-for-time assumption

Besides well-characterized L-VOD-AGB relationships, accurate prediction of AGB interannual variations also relies on the validity of the space-for-time assumption. Unfortunately, we detect a significant discrepancy between spatial and temporal AGB sensitivities to L-VOD. These findings highlight the need for caution when interpreting the results from previous studies relying on the space-for-time assumption (Fan et al., 2019, Wigneron et al., 2021, Yang et al., 2022).

Because L-VOD is decomposed into the products of AGB and water content in vegetation tissues, the failure of the space-for-time assumption detected in this study could arise from the two mechanisms. On the one hand, the AGB reference datasets may inadequately capture variations in space and time. In this study, both the Xu2021 and the Yu2023 datasets are machine-learning-generated. Although their models are trained with data from different years, the major variation in the training samples is spatial information. Therefore, these datasets inevitably introduced spatial information to the prediction of temporal AGB changes. However, the mechanisms driving AGB spatial and temporal variations might be different. At the scale of this study, the spatial variation in AGB is mainly due to long adaptation of vegetation to climate. The structural differences between different vegetation types and spectral difference among species lead to variation in remote sensing signals. Temporally, AGB changes are mainly short-term changes contributed by slow forest growth, and sudden forest loss due to dieback and logging. The first process does not essentially change forest structure, and all these processes result in much smaller changes in species composition compared to spatial variations. Therefore, the machine-learning models may predict biased AGB temporal changes. In contrast to the AGB references, the L-VOD data is based on direct observation, its time series is independent of the spatial pattern. The discrepancies in the information contained in the observed L-VOD and AGB references may lead to differences in spatial and temporal sensitivities.

On the other hand, predictions of AGB changes rely on the space-for-time assumption which assumes that the water content of vegetation tissues remains constant over a period of time—typically one year or over wet period (Wigneron et al., 2024). The validity of the space-for-time assumption needs to be considered at different time scales. At the seasonal scale, precipitation and soil moisture can vary remarkably, which causes large variations in leaf water potential. Meanwhile, deciduous vegetation undergoes significant changes in leaf biomass. Due to the relatively slow growth of wood, the physiological and structural changes of leaves together dominate VOD seasonal variations. Momen et al. (2017) showed that neither phenology nor water stress can be neglected in explaining X-VOD seasonal dynamics. At the interannual scale, the seasonal variations of leaf phenology and leaf water potential no longer dominate. Instead, the variations in stem biomass and water content explain more VOD variation. This ensures that VOD interannual variation is more related to the entire AGB, but the constant water content remains to be justified. One justification is provided by demonstrating similar parameters in fitting equations using multi-year spatial data (Brandt et al., 2018, Fan et al., 2019). However, the analyses primarily reflect spatial variation exceeding annual changes. Therefore, they are incapable of indicating whether the spatial relationships can represent the temporal changes. Another justification from Yang et al. (2022) showed that leaf water content has relatively small contribution to L-VOD variation compared to greenness, using remotely sensed vegetation and water index (NDWI). This result is in line with other analyses on two X-band VOD products in temperate ecosystems (Zhang et al., 2019). However, even by including leaf structural information, the overall explanatory capacity for temporal VOD variation remains low (30 % in Yang et al., 2022; 33 % and 51 % in Zhang et al., 2019). This poor explanatory capacity indicates that only considering leaf water alone is insufficient to justify the application of L-VOD to infer interannual AGB changes.

In this study, we only used the observations from the ascending orbit in the morning (∼6 am local time) when vegetation is less water-stressed. This minimized the signal from varying water content. We also used the yearly averaged value to further reduce water content impacts. However, our results show much smaller temporal than spatial sensitivity of AGB to L-VOD (Fig. 5). This difference probably indicates that the interannual changes in water content cannot be fully removed by these methods. On the one hand, canopy water extends beyond plant tissue water. Interception of rainfall, or dew condensed on canopy may also affect L-VOD signals. These meteorologically modulated factors have larger chance to happen during relatively wetter periods or in wetter years. On the other hand, while long-term averaging of L-VOD smoothed out short-term vegetation water content fluctuations, persistent hydrological events may still imprint on annual signal. If vegetation experiences long term water stress during a prolonged drought, the yearly average of L-VOD may capture a more water-stressed vegetation water content than in a normal year. Given discrepancies in existing AGB datasets (Fig. 6), further investigations on these hypotheses with more reliable observations remain needed.

In contrast to a recent study that treated the AGB changes from Xu et al. (2021) as the truth and attributed the collapse of temporal VOD-AGB relationships to soil moisture effects on VOD (Konings et al., 2021), we show that uncertainties in the machine-learning-based AGB datasets should not be omitted (Fig. 6). This finding aligns with another recent evaluation (Li et al., 2025). It remains too early to conclude whether L-VOD-based AGB changes or the existing AGB datasets are closer to the reality. The difference in sensitivities detected in this study (Fig. 5) could also stem from the fact that temporal variation is driven by vegetation physiological changes and biomass changes under a relatively stable canopy structure (except for stand-replacing disturbances), whereas the spatial variation also includes the long-term adaptation of canopy structure. Nevertheless, the sensitivities estimated in this study still have large uncertainty due to the limitation of data. To further quantify the difference, field data, especially in dense forests, where the saturation of L-VOD and vegetation index may occur, is needed. With more data gathered and new methods developed, the impacts of water content on L-VOD-based AGB estimates are expected to be further constrained and corrected (Wigneron et al., 2024).

Future improvements on AGB predictions

Accurately predicting large-scale AGB is challenging for remote sensing. The existing machine-learning-based predictions using multi-wavelength backscatter have already made significant progress (e.g. Saatchi et al., 2011, Xu et al., 2021, Dubayah et al., 2022, etc.), while the long-term L-VOD observations provide a new opportunity for temporal change detection (Li et al., 2020; Wigneron et al., 2021). However, both approaches exhibit large uncertainties as shown in this study especially in dense forests. To reduce extrapolation uncertainties in very dense forests, either more representative training data or a more physics-based model is required. Although Lidar observations address structural gaps in dense forests, Lidar-derived AGB estimates suffer from poorly calibrated allometric equations and limited understory detection. The decomposition of L-VOD signals is more physics-based, but to what extent it saturates and how to exclude the effects water content changes in dense forests remain to be further investigated. Long-term in situ observations are essential to understand these processes. Besides, including large-scale vegetation structure information such as tree cover is another complement to existing data. Next-generation biomass monitoring should synthesize these information streams through comprehensive data assimilation systems.

Conclusion

In this study we evaluated the existing methods that have been used to fit AGB with L-VOD data, and explored the potential of L-VOD in predicting the spatial and temporal variations of AGB. Our findings revealed that once well calibrated, all the existing methods perform similarly in capturing the relationships between AGB and L-VOD, with the R2 between 0.69–0.77. Moreover, we found that tree cover information can complement L-VOD data in explaining AGB variations, especially in regions with low and medium L-VOD. In spite of these promising results, all empirical models failed to reproduce AGB variation well in dense forests. We show that one of the main factors limiting AGB prediction in these forests is the large uncertainties in current AGB reference datasets, since the fitting errors are not larger than the discrepancies among AGB datasets. These uncertainties mainly stem from three factors. First, the saturation of remote sensing signals in dense forests dampens their sensitivity to high AGB values. Second, scale mismatches between field observations and satellite footprints in highly heterogeneous forests introduce unquantified errors. Finally, data extrapolation at the upper range of the AGB distribution results in predictions that are highly sensitive to the choice of model structure and training samples. Reliable AGB benchmark datasets in dense forests are urgently needed to further attribute and constrain the uncertainties.

Previous studies have relied on the space-for-time assumption when investigating biomass changes. However, our results show large discrepancies between the spatial and temporal AGB sensitivity to L-VOD. These results highlight the need for caution when interpreting AGB changes based on models built on spatial data. Due to the lack of reliable references and the poor understanding of the true AGB, it remains difficult to attribute the difference in spatial and temporal sensitivities. A priority would be to rely on long-term observational biomass datasets to better understand L-VOD-AGB relationships, especially for their temporal variations.

For AGB prediction, we demonstrate significant improvement through the integration of tree cover into L-VOD-AGB models. However, this advancement marks a starting point rather than an endpoint. Future improvements in AGB prediction rely on integrating multiple data sources, including traditional multi-frequency backscatter observations, more physically based proxies (e.g., VOD, Lidar, and tree cover), and reliable in situ observations.
