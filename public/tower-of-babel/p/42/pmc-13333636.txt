Congenital Hypoglossia With Craniofacial Anomalies Without Limb Defects: A Rare Phenotypic Variant of Oromandibular–Limb Hypogenesis Syndrome
Authors: Abraham Kwadzo Ahiakpa, Liza Georgina Agyapong‐Quaittoo, Emmanuella Haluri Ayeh, Precious Akosua Raphaelson, Dorothy Sackey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCongenital hypoglossia can present with life‐threatening airway compromise even in the absence of limb anomalies or classical syndromic features. Early airway assessment, feeding support, and timely referral from resource‐limited settings are critical for survival. Such cases may represent a broader phenotypic spectrum of oromandibular–limb hypogenesis syndrome.Keywords: airway obstruction, craniofacial abnormalities, hypoglossia, newborn, oromandibular–limb hypogenesis complex

Introduction

Congenital anomalies of the tongue are rare developmental disorders that can significantly impair feeding, airway protection, and speech. These include ankyloglossia, lingual thyroid, bifid tongue, accessory tongue, aglossia, and hypoglossia [1]. Hypoglossia refers to a markedly small or rudimentary tongue, whereas aglossia denotes complete congenital absence [2].

Severe underdevelopment or absence of the tongue is exceedingly rare and most often occurs within syndromic contexts, particularly oromandibular–limb hypogenesis syndrome (OLHS), a heterogeneous group of disorders involving varying degrees of tongue, mandibular, and limb abnormalities, first described by Hall in 1971 [3]. Truly isolated hypoglossia without limb defects is uncommon, with fewer than 30 cases reported in the literature as of 2022 [4].

The etiology of hypoglossia remains uncertain. Proposed mechanisms include genetic factors, maternal illness, teratogenic exposures, and early vascular disruption affecting first branchial arch derivatives [4]. Management typically requires multidisciplinary care involving neonatologists, otolaryngologists, pediatric surgeons, speech therapists, and nutrition specialists [5, 6].

Reports from sub‐Saharan Africa are extremely limited, likely reflecting both the rarity of the condition and underreporting in resource‐limited settings. To our knowledge, no published cases have been documented from Ghana. We, therefore, present a case of congenital hypoglossia with craniofacial anomalies in a term neonate that may represent an atypical phenotypic variant not fully captured by existing OLHS classifications.

Case Presentation

A female neonate was delivered via spontaneous vaginal delivery at 42 weeks and 3 days of gestation to a 19‐year‐old primigravida at a district hospital in Ghana. The pregnancy was uneventful, with no maternal chronic or febrile illness during pregnancy, teratogenic drug exposure, radiation exposure, or alcohol use. There was no family history of congenital anomalies or consanguinity. A routine antenatal anomaly scan performed at 21 weeks of gestation detected no structural abnormalities. Preconception counseling was not performed because the pregnancy was unplanned.

At birth, the infant weighed 3.3 kg with a length of 51 cm, head circumference of 37 cm, and chest circumference of 33 cm. Apgar scores were 3/10, 6/10, and 8/10 at the first, fifth, and tenth minutes, respectively. The neonate required initial resuscitative support via drying, stimulation, and suctioning of oral and nasal secretions.

Physical Examination

Shortly after birth, the neonate was noted to have a markedly reduced tongue size consistent with hypoglossia. On crying, there was a rhythmic vertical elevation and depression of the floor of the mouth, with no obvious tongue protrusion, suggesting the presence of rudimentary lingual musculature beneath the mucosa (Video S1). There was asymmetry of the alveolar crest with mild misalignment on the left side. The mandible appeared posteriorly positioned relative to the maxilla, consistent with retrognathia. The nose was bulbous with depressed/flat nasal bridge, broad nasal base, and long and flat philtrum. She had epicanthal folds and arched eyebrows. Both ears were small and low‐set, with auricular length (supra‐aurale to sub‐aurale) measuring approximately 2.5 cm bilaterally, which is below the typical physiognomic auricular length of about 3.0–3.5 cm in term neonates [7]. They were cup‐shaped with antihelix and earlobe hypoplasia.

No cleft palate or cleft lip was identified. Examination of the limbs revealed no limb reduction defects, limb length discrepancies, or digital anomalies (Figure 1). Investigations

Basic imaging, including abdominopelvic ultrasonography and skeletal radiographs, revealed no abnormalities. Whole‐body anteroposterior and lateral radiographs confirmed normal limb morphology (Figures 2 and 3).

Although hypoglossia may fall within the spectrum of OLHS, the absence of limb defects and other classical features made definitive classification challenging. Aglossia‐adactylia syndrome was considered a part of the differential diagnoses because of similar craniofacial anomalies, including a severely small tongue, retrognathia, and gingival asymmetry. However, the absence of digital anomalies renders it less fitting. Also, Pierre Robin sequence was considered due to mandibular retrognathia and airway compromise, although the absence of cleft palate and glossoptosis made the diagnosis less likely. Initial Management

The neonate was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit for monitoring and management of mild birth asphyxia. Neurological assessment using the Thompson score yielded a score of 0, indicating no evidence or risk of hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy. Feeding difficulties were evident due to ineffective suckling and inadequate management of oral secretions. An orogastric tube was therefore passed to facilitate feeding and reduce aspiration risk.

Clinical Deterioration

On the second day of life, the infant developed progressive respiratory distress characterized by increased work of breathing and intermittent oxygen desaturation. Supplemental oxygen was administered via nasal prongs with no significant improvement.

Referral and Outcome

Given the complexity of the anomaly, persistent feeding difficulty, and worsening respiratory compromise, the infant was referred to a tertiary hospital for multidisciplinary evaluation on day four of life.

During follow‐up communication with the family, it was reported that the infant experienced cardiorespiratory arrest but was successfully resuscitated. Due to persistent airway compromise, the infant subsequently underwent emergency tracheostomy and is currently clinically stable in the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit at the time of reporting.

Discussion

Hypoglossia is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by a markedly reduced tongue, an organ essential for feeding, airway protection, and speech. The tongue develops from the first, third, and fourth branchial arches between the fourth and eighth weeks of embryogenesis; disruption during this period may result in hypoglossia or, in severe cases, aglossia. The associated craniofacial anomalies observed in our patient, including retrognathia, small, low‐set ears, and gingival ridge asymmetry, likely reflect disturbances in first branchial arch derivatives. A similar phenotypic variant, seen in a child with aglossia, has been reported by Gupta et al. [8].

Most reported cases of hypoglossia occur within the spectrum of OLHS [2, 4, 5]. Hall's classification categorizes OLHS based on combinations of oromandibular and limb anomalies; however, cases with isolated craniofacial involvement and no limb defects present a diagnostic challenge. Our patient demonstrated hypoglossia with craniofacial anomalies but lacked limb defects, intraoral fusion abnormalities, and features of other OLHS subtypes, making precise classification difficult. This supports previous observations that existing classification systems may not fully capture the phenotypic variability of hypoglossia [9].

Clinically, hypoglossia in the neonatal period often manifests with feeding difficulty, aspiration risk, and airway obstruction [10, 11]. Reduced tongue bulk impairs suction and swallowing, while associated mandibular anomalies such as retrognathia may further compromise the oropharyngeal airway. In this case, ineffective suckling necessitated enteral feeding, and progressive respiratory distress ultimately required advanced airway intervention.

Management requires a multidisciplinary approach involving neonatologists, otolaryngologists, pediatric surgeons, nutrition specialists, maxillofacial surgeons, pediatric dentists, and orthodontists [9]. Early airway assessment and feeding support are critical, particularly in resource‐limited settings where access to specialized care may be constrained. This underscores the importance of early recognition and timely referral.

This report adds to the limited literature on hypoglossia from sub‐Saharan Africa and highlights the need for continued documentation of such cases. Broader case accumulation and molecular characterization may help clarify whether isolated hypoglossia represents a distinct developmental entity or a milder phenotypic expression within the OLHS spectrum.

Limitations

Advanced imaging and genetic testing were not performed due to resource constraints, limiting the ability to exclude subtle syndromic associations. In addition, long‐term follow‐up data on feeding, speech, and neurodevelopmental outcomes are currently unavailable, as the infant remains under specialist care. Despite these limitations, this report provides valuable insight into the early presentation and management challenges of hypoglossia in a resource‐limited setting.

Conclusion

Congenital hypoglossia is a rare anomaly that may present with significant feeding difficulties and airway compromise in the neonatal period. The presence of craniofacial anomalies without limb defects, as seen in this case, may represent a broader phenotypic spectrum of OLHS. Early recognition and prompt referral are essential to optimize airway management and feeding support, particularly in resource‐limited settings. Continued reporting of such cases is needed to refine existing classification systems and improve clinical understanding.
