Advanced seismic resilient performance of steel MRF equipped with viscoelastic friction dampers
Authors: Jaehoon Bae, Xiameng Huang, Ziwen Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper demonstrates the enhanced resilience performance of steel structures with viscoelastic friction dampers (VEFDs) based on numerical simulations of building responses. Velocity-dependent dampers, which are widely used to increase seismic resilience, may increase the axial force of the column under strong earthquake conditions because the generated force depends on the interstory velocity. This often leads to plastic hinges being placed on the columns of the structure, which can lead to structural collapse via weak-layer failure. In addition, while viscoelastic dampers are effective in reducing story drift, peak acceleration, and peak velocity, the proposed hybrid VEFD offers the additional benefit of reducing base shear via the friction damper. Simulation results for 10- and 20-story buildings with the novel VEFDs show that the proposed dampers can control drift and plastic deformation in structural members. Nonlinear dynamic analysis of 20 far-fault seismic ground motion records conducted using OpenSees also reveals lower peak absolute floor acceleration and velocity. Overall, the results suggest that the proposed VEFD has excellent potential for use in the performance-based seismic design of structures because it can reduce both structural and nonstructural damage. The results verify the damper's effectiveness in controlling story drift without a significant increase in the base shear. Introduction

The resilience-based design of buildings is vital to minimize the socio-economic losses incurred by natural disasters such as earthquakes. In particular, to reduce the carbon emissions from buildings, which account for more than 39% of global carbon emissions due to earthquakes, concerted efforts to enhance the resilience of buildings from a life-cycle perspective after an earthquake are required1,2. This includes the development of advanced technologies to reduce residual deformation and increase the elastic recovery capability of buildings after seismic events3. Researchers have thus investigated a variety of approaches to increase the resilience of buildings against winds or earthquake loads4–9. For example, resilient performance can be improved using various passive damper systems, such as self-centering structures and pre-tensioned wires10–12, as verified by a number of studies13–19. However, Tse et al.13 reported the use of a passive damper system can increase total construction costs for a building by 2%, with maintenance costs of around 0.2% of the total budget that depend on the damper type, the conditions under which the damper is employed, and the target performance. However, the additional costs of passive dampers can be offset by the reduced weight of the structural components as a result of the use of the damper system20.

One of the most effective uses of a passive damper system is that it emulates the reduction of spectral acceleration by adding supplementary damping to the structure. Another positive effect associated with the use of damper systems is a reduction in displacement, drift, velocity, and acceleration, which is crucial for nonstructural components21. For example, Christopoulos and Montgomery22 studied viscous dampers to determine whether they can increase the seismic performance of tall buildings against wind and seismic loads. The results of their study showed that modular viscoelastic coupling dampers can play a major role in sustaining large shear deformation and reducing torsional velocity and lateral acceleration. The most important finding of their study was that viscous dampers help increase the stability of buildings and retrofitted designs without altering the original dynamic periods. In another study, Dong et al.23 developed a high-performance acrylate viscoelastic damper (HAVED) suitable for low-frequency vibration control. The dynamic mechanical performance and energy dissipation capacity of the HAVED were strongly dependent on the temperature, frequency, and displacement amplitude. Metallic dampers have also been widely used because of their cost-effectiveness and ease of replacement24. According to Chen et al.25, metallic dampers can also reduce acceleration and dissipate low levels of energy. They concluded that a metallic damper acts in a manner similar to an isolation device by ensuring that damage is localized within the damper so that the main structure remains undamaged. In addition to passive seismic dampers, a number of previous studies have also investigated the use of innovative materials for earthquake resilience. For example, shape memory alloys (SMAs) have been proposed as a new approach to enhance building resilience26–29. Collectively innovative-material based dampers are used in both new constructions and retrofitted buildings, employing techniques such as rocking walls, metal shear panels, and steel dampers to improve seismic performance30–37.

Viscous and viscoelastic velocity-dependent dampers have also been explored to improve seismic performance and increase seismic resilience without significantly increasing overall construction costs21. However, velocity-dependent dampers may increase the axial force of the column in the event of a strong earthquake because the force produced is dependent on the interstory velocity38. Therefore, a major problem that arises when a velocity-dependent damper is used in conjunction with moment-resisting frames (MRFs) is that plastic hinges may be placed on the column, which can lead to structural collapse as a result of weak-story failure39. To mitigate this, one approach to control the induced force on the column is to employ a hybrid damper that is capable of imposing a limit on the peak force40. In particular, while viscoelastic dampers are effective in reducing story drift, peak acceleration, and peak velocity, a hybrid damper that employs viscoelastic friction would offer the additional benefit of reducing base shear.

This study thus designs a hybrid viscoelastic friction damper (VEFD) for MRF buildings and seeks to demonstrate that it can meet the required design criteria for both design-basis earthquake (DBE) and maximum considered earthquake (MCE) levels, while simultaneously controlling base shear. In particular, the seismic performance of 10- and 20-story MRF buildings fitted with the proposed VEFDs was assessed for 20 scaled seismic ground motion records. The main goal of using VEFDs was to reduce the use of plastic hinges in the columns to enhance the ductility and strength of the buildings when exposed to a strong earthquake. Because the use of plastic hinges is directly related to an increase in the story drift, while base shear at the design level greatly affects total building costs, this study attempted to devise a means to control story drift without compromising the reduction in the base shear.

A high-ductile steel MRF was designed as a prototype building. The bare MRF was then equipped with viscoelastic and VEFDs and the seismic performance of 10- and 20-story buildings was investigated. For the VEFDs, parametric designs with different λ values (i.e., the ratio of the peak force of the VEFDs to the peak force of viscoelastic dampers at the DBE intensity level that activates the friction device) were considered. To explore the effects of a higher vibration mode due to variation in building height, the results were compared with those for viscoelastic dampers in 10-story and 20-story buildings. Nonlinear time-history analysis was conducted using OpenSees with 20 far-fault seismic ground motion records scaled to the DBE and MCE intensity levels. The base shear according to λ was also determined to ensure that it was controlled in retrofitted schemes. The residual drift and collapse probability were also calculated to evaluate the multidimensional resilience performance of MRFs fitted with VEFDs.

Viscoelastic friction dampers (VEFDs)

The proposed VEFD consisted of a viscoelastic damper and a friction device. Figure 1a presents a basic model of the VEFD based on a Kelvin model of a dashpot and a spring connected in parallel. The VEFD utilizes viscoelastic and steel or friction devices connected in series. Figure 1b shows a potential setup for the VEFD. A conventional viscoelastic damper was employed, using an elastomeric material bonded to two steel plates and gusset plates to connect to strong braces within the frame, while the friction device acted through pre-tensioned bolts and long-slotted holes at the bottom part of the flange of the beam.

Steel MRFs equipped with viscoelastic dampers

Viscoelastic dampers were designed for high-ductile steel MRFs in the 10- and 20-story buildings in accordance with the process reported by Lee et al.43 and Bae and Karavasilis9 (Fig. 4).

Viscoelastic damper design process43.

Step 1 In this step, the target performance was defined in terms of the drift ratio (%). The performance targets for the 10- and 20-story MRFs with viscoelastic dampers were set using response spectral analysis at 1.0% and 0.6%, respectively; these settings corresponded to an effective dampening of 20% (e.g., the reduced target drift using the Bs,1 factor is 1.5 based on Ramirez et al. 44). The damping coefficient B corresponded to the value of the total damping ratio β* (Table 1). In this step, the target damping corresponding to the target displacement was selected by reducing the response spectra using an MRF without dampers using Eq. (1).

Step 2 The β factor (β = Ki/Ko) was assumed to range from 0.3 to 0.43 for the 10-story building and was set at 0.42 for the 20-story building to match the target drift, where Ki is the stiffness of the viscoelastic damper, and Ko is the lateral stiffness of the frame.

Step 3 Using modal analysis for the MRFs with viscoelastic dampers, the reduced fundamental period (T1) for 5, 10, and 20 stories was 1.13 s, 2.25 s, and 3.32 s, respectively. Table 2 lists the fundamental period comparisons between MRFs with and without viscoelastic dampers and the properties of these dampers.

VEFD design based on λ values

Figure 7 describes the design process for the VEFDs, which were based on viscoelastic dampers connected to friction dampers in series to control the induced force. In this process, it was necessary to identify the maximum viscoelastic force based on pushover analysis. Various analysis methods for pushover analysis are available46–48 but, in the present study, pushover analysis was conducted for MRFs fitted with viscoelastic dampers with reverse triangular loads. The force of each story damper (F*1, F*2… F*10) was then extracted at the maximum drift setting for the DBE level in accordance with the pushover analysis. The activation forces for the damper friction devices (Fy1, Fy2…Fy10) were calculated using the λ factor by multiplying the viscoelastic damper forces for each story. Lowering λ can reduce the force on the column but inevitably increases the drift ratio due to the series connection of the friction dampers. Therefore, it was necessary to determine the target drift ratio and identify an appropriate λ value to satisfy the target performance. The λ factor was thus considered an unknown and set at 1.0, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, and 0.2 for analysis. Because the viscoelastic and friction devices were connected in series, the overall force could be controlled by the friction damper force.

Design process for steel MRFs equipped with VEFDs.

Nonlinear dynamic analysis

Description of the nonlinear analysis model

Nonlinear analysis models for steel MRFs equipped with viscoelastic dampers or VEFDs were constructed using OpenSees. Fiber beam-column elements were used for the columns given the interaction between the axial force and bending moment. Concentrated plastic rotational springs were used at the end of the beams, which can lead to deterioration under cyclic loading 49. Rotation of the beam occurred at the plastic hinges at both ends of the beam and was expressed as a bilinear backbone curve whose equation was adapted from the Lignos deterioration model. The cyclic deterioration of the beam strength can be expressed in terms of the beam rotation.

For the panel zones, 12 nodes and 8 elastic beam–column elements were strongly connected to create a rigid zone to resist the shear force caused by the interaction of opposite moments between the beams and columns. To consider the effects of P-Δ, a lean-on column was connected with the diaphragm of the frames, and 3.0% Rayleigh damping was considered. A Kelvin model was used for viscoelastic damper modeling in which a spring and dashpot were connected in parallel (Fig. 1a). Reliable deterioration beam spring models were calibrated based on more than 300 experiments that considered variation in the steel wide-flange beams49. Figure 8a presents the monotonic backbone curve and cyclic deterioration model used in the present study, while Fig. 8b displays the viscoelastic-friction damper model. For the friction device, bilinear elastic, perfectly plastic model was considered with an initial stiffness equal to 1000 times that of the viscoelastic damper to emulate the closely rigid behavior of the friction device before sliding, while a yield force equivalent to the activation force of the friction device was applied9.

Figures 11, 12, 13 present the roof drift (%), peak acceleration (m/s2), and peak velocity (m/s) for the evaluated seismic ground motions. In terms of roof drift, the residual deformation was significant for MRFs without dampers, while this deformation was minimal for MRFs with viscoelastic dampers and VEFDs, except from the Friuli (1976) earthquake. The peak acceleration and peak velocity, which affect the usability of housing, were significantly lower for the MRFs with viscoelastic dampers and VEFDs than for the MRFs without dampers. Figure 14 displays the mean peak story drift (θmax) for MRFs equipped with viscoelastic and VEFDs at DBE and MCE intensity levels for λ = 1.0, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4, and 0.2. VEFDs designed with λ values of 0.4–0.6 were found to satisfy strict peak story drift under the DBE whilst also achieving a reduction in the peak base shear force of 17%. For the 10- and 20-story buildings, the optimal λ was found to be 0.4–0.6 and 0.8–1.0, respectively. In particular, VEFDs designed for λ values of 0.4–0.6 were found to satisfy the strict peak story drift of the DBE, while they reduced the peak base shear force by 11–18% in the 10-story building. Similarly, λ values within the range of 0.8–1.0 were found to satisfy the strict peak story drift of the DBE while also reducing the peak base shear force by 13–15% in the 20-story building (Fig. 15).

To evaluate the vulnerability to collapse, IDA was conducted on the MRFs with viscoelastic and viscoplastic dampers using a set of 20 far-field seismic records. Figure 18 presents the fitted collapse probability curves for MRFs with viscoelastic friction dampers (λ = 1.0–0.2). The probability of collapse curve is a cumulative distribution using calibrated mean and standard deviation values for logarithms of each Sa value. Spectral acceleration was normalized at the MCE intensity level, and Sa was obtained from IDA. The ratios from 0 to 1 represent the number of collapses for Sa arranged in ascending order. The fitted data yielded similar trends to the fragility curve. The results showed that the collapse resistance fell as λ decreased from 1.0 to 0.2, indicating that the probability of collapse increased as λ decreased. The results also indicate that the proposed VEFD system achieves a high collapse probability with a 50% collapse probability for Sa(T1)/Sa,MCE(T1) in the range of 3.2–3.8. In particular, when λ = 0.2, the collapse probability is advantageous compared with the Sa(T1)/Sa,MCE(T1) value of 3.15 when MRFs were used alone, thus representing sufficient collapse resistance against earthquakes.

Conclusions

This study designed and tested hybrid VEFDs for use with MRFs with different stories. The main goal for the use of VEFDs in resilience-based seismic design was the reduction of story drift without increasing the base shear of the structure, which is difficult to achieve using conventional viscous or viscoelastic dampers. The appropriate activation force range of VEFDs was identified to satisfy the seismic performance by reducing story drift and residual drift. A simplified design process using pushover analysis for MRFs fitted with viscoelastic dampers that explored the activation force of the friction devices for each story was also presented. Despite the contributions of this study, some limitations should be noted. For example, the present study only analyzed the effect of VEFDs in 10- and 20-story buildings. Additional research is thus necessary for high-rise structures. In addition, experiments and finite element method analysis are required to extend the analytical results presented in this study. Nevertheless, A number of important generalizable conclusions can be drawn from this study.

First, the results highlighted the advantages of VEFDs and identified suitable activation forces for the friction device that simultaneously reduce drift and control the peak base shear. When VEFDs were used in MRFs, only a small reduction in the base shear was observed under DBE-level intensity. However, when the seismic intensity was higher (e.g., during an MCE), a significant reduction in the base shear was observed. The energy dissipation device was used to control the maximum value of the friction device, which was adjustable based on the torque of the friction bolt. The force applied to the column can thus be controlled, allowing the number of plastic hinges on the column to be reduced, meaning that the overall base shear can be controlled.

Secondly, in terms of the residual drift (θr), the use of VEFDs yielded values below 0.19% and 0.14% for λ = 1.0–0.2 in the 10- and 20-story buildings, respectively. These values were below the 0.5% limit for economically replaceable residual drift. These results suggest that MRFs fitted with VEFDs offer cost benefits when improving resilience performance.

Third, parametric analysis was conducted using different λ values. Numerical analysis revealed that λ values within a certain range were suitable for reducing drift with no significant increase in the base shear of the structure. In particular, for the VEFDs used in the 10-story and 20-story buildings, the appropriate λ was 0.4–0.6 and 0.8–1.0, respectively. However, there are limitations regarding how λ can be applied to other buildings with different heights and structural systems. It is therefore necessary to conduct additional parametric analysis using more specific λ values.

A collapse fragility curve for the MRFs with viscoplastic dampers was used to estimate the probability of collapse when the normalized cumulative distribution was fitted as a function of the Sa(T1) value associated with collapse using 20 seismic ground motions. The results showed that the collapse resistance decreased as λ decreased from 1.0 to 0.2, thus indicating that the probability of collapse increased as λ decreased.
