Ruxolitinib inhibits CaMKII-γ to reverse bortezomib resistance in multiple myeloma
Authors: Ruqi Liang, Hong Liu, Xushan Zhong, Yizhao Chen, Jianru Tian, Chaofeng Zhang, Mingdi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple myeloma (MM) is the second most common hematologic malignancy, with increasing incidence and earlier onset. Despite the therapeutic efficacy of proteasome inhibitors and other strategies, nearly all patients eventually develop resistance, making the discovery of novel drug targets and innovative therapeutic approaches an urgent clinical need. A critical knowledge gap exists regarding the molecular mechanisms underlying bortezomib (first-line MM drug) resistance, which hinders the development of effective resistance-reversing strategies. Here we show calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II gamma (CaMKII-γ) regulates bortezomib-resistant MM (BRMM) via AMPK-ULK1-autophagy axis. High-throughput screening identified ruxolitinib as a selective CaMKII-γ inhibitor, which reverses BRMM resistance in vitro (using U266 and KMS11 bortezomib-resistant cell lines) and in vivo (using female BALB/c nude mouse model), with efficacy comparable to genetic CaMKII-γ ablation, providing a potential therapeutic strategy for BRMM with broader implications to improve patient outcomes in the future.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) has become the second most common hematologic malignancy with the infinite clonal proliferation of malignant plasma cells in the bone marrow and the secretion of monoclonal immunoglobulin.1,2 The symptoms include skeletal destruction, infections, anemia, hypercalcemia, and renal failure3. For the treatment of MM, proteasome inhibitors (bortezomib, carfilzomib and ixazomib) and other first-line strategies in clinics have prolonged the survival time of patients by 2–3 times4,5. However, almost all patients develop resistance after treatment, leading to eventual treatment failure and recurrence, which severely affects the survival of MM patients6,7. Therefore, novel drug targets and innovative therapeutic strategies are in great need for drug-resistant MM patients. Among the treatment drugs for MM, bortezomib (BTZ) is the most recommended and commonly used one. So, bortezomib resistance of MM (BRMM) has attracted widespread attention.

CaMKII-γ is a member of the CaMKII family (calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II), which belongs to the serine/threonine kinase family. There are four subtypes in the CaMKII family, including CaMKII-α, CaMKII-β, CaMKII-γ, and CaMKII-δ, regulated by calcium ions and calmodulin. Members of the CaMKII family play important roles in signal transduction, neurotransmitter release, synaptic plasticity, gene regulation and other biological processes8,9. Numerous studies have shown that aberrant activation of CaMKII-γ is closely related to a variety of tumors, such as T-cell lymphoma, myeloid leukemia, and colon cancer10–14. In particular, studies also found that CaMKII-γ was aberrantly activated in MM and its expression level was positively correlated with malignant progression and poor prognosis11, suggesting that CaMKII-γ could be a potential therapeutic target for MM. However, the relationship between CaMKII-γ and drug-resistant MM has not been reported, so in this study, we explored whether CaMKII-γ is a potential therapeutic target for drug-resistant MM, especially BRMM.

AMPK-ULK1 axis-mediated autophagy pathway is an important cellular self-protective and metabolic regulation mechanism, which not only maintains cell homeostasis, but also solves cell stress by degrading and recycling cell components, thereby preventing diseases and maintaining cell function. Multiple studies have shown that the AMPK-ULK1 axis-mediated autophagy pathway plays a complex dual role in tumors. On the one hand, autophagy can promote the survival and proliferation of tumor cells; on the other hand, excessive autophagy will lead to resource depletion and inhibit tumor growth15,16. Importantly, studies have found that bortezomib resistance is induced by activation of the AMPK-ULK1 axis-mediated protective autophagy in MM cells, in which NCX 1/Ca2+ triggers the autophagy flux of MM cells through the non-classical NF-κB pathway, thereby reducing the sensitivity of MM to bortezomib17. In addition, CaMKII, as an important regulator of Ca2+ signaling, has also been found to significantly enhance autophagy flux to protect cell survival under short-term starvation conditions. Therefore, we hypothesized that BRMM cells may also achieve cell survival by activating the CaMKII-γ-AMPK-ULK1 axis to enhance the autophagy pathway.

Ruxolitinib, as a small-molecule inhibitor of JAK1/2, is the first drug approved by the FDA for the treatment of moderate or high-risk myelofibrosis18. It mainly plays a variety of roles such as anti-inflammatory, immune regulation and inhibition of abnormal cell growth, which is widely used in the treatment of myelofibrosis, polycythemia vera, and corticosteroid-refractory acute graft-versus-host disease19. Recent studies have found that ruxolitinib also has a role in reducing arthritis symptoms and cardiac protection20.

Results

Overexpression of CaMKII-γ is associated with poor prognosis of BRMM patients

Studies have revealed that dysregulation of CaMKII-γ is closely associated with various hematological malignancies, including MM, CML (chronic myeloid leukemia) and TCL (T-cell lymphoma)11–14. Moreover, previous studies and our analysis (with MMRFCoMMpass database & GEO database) have both revealed that CaMKII-γ (encoded by CAMK2G) was aberrantly overexpressed in MM and its expression level was positively correlated with malignant progression and poor prognosis11 (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating that CaMKII-γ plays a crucial role in the progression of MM. In order to investigate whether CaMKII-γ is related to the drug resistance of MM, we analyzed the MMRFCoMMpass database containing 764 bone marrow samples derived from primary MM patients (newly diagnosed and treatment-naive MM patients) and 80 bone marrow samples derived from recurrent MM patients (relapsed MM patients following prior therapy). The result showed that compared with primary MM patients, the expression level of CaMKII-γ (encoded by CAMK2G) was significantly upregulated in recurrent MM patients (Fig. 1A). To assess the clinical relevance of CaMKII-γ (encoded by CAMK2G), we stratified recurrent MM patients into high- and low-expression groups based on the median CAMK2G mRNA level. Survival analysis revealed that recurrent MM patients with low expression of CAMK2G were remarkably better than those with high expression of CAMK2G (Fig. 1B). Then, to verify the results of the above bioinformatics analysis, we induced drug resistance in MM cell lines (U266 and KMS11) using bortezomib, the most common and representative first-line clinical treatment drug for MM. Two bortezomib-resistant MM (BRMM) cell lines, U266.BR and KMS11.BR, were obtained through the drug concentration escalation method, with IC50 values increased by 13.25-fold and 6.45-fold, respectively (Fig. 1C, D and Fig. S1C, D from Supplementary Data 2). In both BRMM cell lines, we observed elevated protein levels of CaMKII-γ compared to their non-resistant counterparts (Fig. 1E, F, Supplementary Fig. 2A, B, and Fig. S1E, F from Supplementary Data 2). Then, to validate the differential expression of CaMKII-γ in clinical samples, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining on bone marrow tissue sections collected from newly diagnosed MM patients and relapsed/refractory MM patients. The results revealed markedly elevated protein levels of CaMKII-γ in bone marrow tissue sections from recurrent/refractory MM patients compared with primary MM patients (Fig. 1G). Highly expressed CaMKII-γ is a potential target for BRMM

To further address the role of CaMKII-γ in BRMM, we knocked down CaMKII-γ in U266.BR and KMS11.BR cells using siRNA (Fig. 2A, B, Supplementary Fig. 3A, B, and Fig. S2A, B from Supplementary Data 2). The result showed that CaMKII-γ depletion caused a notable reduction in IC50 values of bortezomib against BRMM cells (from ~120 to ~60 nM) (Fig. 2C, D and Fig. S2C, D from Supplementary Data 2), demonstrating a significant restoration of drug sensitivity. Moreover, knock-down of CaMKII-γ significantly inhibited the proliferation of BRMM cells (Fig. 2E, F and Fig. S2E, F from Supplementary Data 2). Furthermore, combined CaMKII-γ depletion and bortezomib treatment exerted more potent growth inhibition on bortezomib-resistant cells than on their parental counterparts, supporting the role of CaMKII-γ in BRMM (Fig. 2G, H and Fig. S2G, H from Supplementary Data 2). In addition to the in vitro assays, we further evaluated the effect of CaMKII-γ depletion on BRMM in vivo using a xenograft mouse model. U266.BR cells transfected with either si-CaMKII-γ or si-NC were subcutaneously injected into BALB/c nude mice, and then bortezomib treatment was carried out (Fig. 2I). Similar to the in vitro data, knock-down of CaMKII-γ not only restored the sensitivity of BRMM to bortezomib but also effectively inhibited tumor growth in vivo, while body weights of mice in each group remained unaffected throughout the experimental period. Especially, the combination of CaMKII-γ depletion and bortezomib treatment showed the best anti-tumor effect among all the groups of BRMM mice (Fig. 2J–M and Fig. S2K–M from Supplementary Data 2). Then, the histological analysis of excised tumors showed a notable decrease in tumor cell density and increased necrosis in both the si-CaMKII-γ group and the si-CaMKII-γ + bortezomib group, as revealed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining for the proliferation marker Ki-67 demonstrated a markedly lower proliferation index in tumors of both the si-CaMKII-γ group and the si-CaMKII-γ + bortezomib group (Fig. 2N). Autophagy pathway plays a key role in the regulation of BRMM

To further explore the mechanism by which CaMKII-γ mediates drug-resistant MM, we conducted the GO (Gene Ontology) enrichment analysis on the MMRFCoMMpass database. The results revealed that compared with primary MM patients, the bone marrow samples of recurrent MM patients were significantly enriched in primary lysosomes, special granules and other cellular components closely related to autophagy (Fig. 3A). While compared with recurrent MM patients with low expression of CaMKII-γ, recurrent MM patients with high expression of CaMKII-γ had significantly enriched biological processes related to calcium signaling pathways, such as calcium-mediated signal transduction and positive regulation of cytoplasmic calcium ion concentration (Fig. 3B). In addition, earlier research has indicated that CaMKII promotes autophagic flux to mediate the drug resistance and maintain the survival of tumor cells21–23 Based on the above bioinformatics analysis results and relevant research reports, we speculate that CaMKII-γ may mediate the proliferation of BRMM cells through regulating autophagy pathway. To verify our hypothesis, we performed monodansylcadaverine (MDC) staining to label autophagic vacuoles in U266.BR and KMS11.BR cells. Both cell lines were transfected with either si-CaMKII-γ or si-NC. The fluorescent signals representing autophagic vesicles were markedly reduced in CaMKII-γ knockdown cells compared to controls (Fig. 3C and Fig. S3C from Supplementary Data 2). This morphological evidence confirmed that CaMKII-γ depletion suppressed autophagy in BRMM cells. Then, to further investigate the relationship between CaMKII-γ and autophagy in BRMM, we observed that knock-down of CaMKII-γ led to a significant reduction of LC3-II protein level in BRMM cells (Fig. 3D, E, Supplementary Fig. 4A, B, and Fig. S3D, E from Supplementary Data 2). This finding suggests that the high level of autophagic flux in BRMM cells is dependent on the presence of CaMKII-γ. Autophagy is primarily regulated by AMPK activation and mTOR inhibition24, with AMPK serving as a critical signaling molecule in the calcium-mediated pathway influencing autophagy25. Given this context, we examined the impact of CaMKII-γ on AMPK and its downstream targets. To explore whether CaMKII-γ directly interacts with AMPK, we first performed protein-protein docking analysis in silico. The result revealed that CaMKII-γ and AMPK could form a stable complex with a favorable binding interface and complementary spatial conformation with a Z-score of −1.9 (Fig. 3F), suggesting a probability of direct interaction between CaMKII-γ and AMPK (Z-score less than −1 indicates a direct interaction theoretically). Prompted by the computational prediction, we next conducted a co-immunoprecipitation assay (Co-IP) in U266.BR cells. Immunoprecipitation of endogenous CaMKII-γ resulted in the co-precipitation of AMPK, and reciprocal IP using anti-AMPK also pulled down CaMKII-γ (Fig. 3G and Supplementary Fig. 4C). These results provide direct biochemical evidence that CaMKII-γ physically associates with AMPK in BRMM cells. Furthermore, western blotting analysis revealed that knock-down of CaMKII-γ not only reduced the phosphorylation levels of AMPK but also decreased the phosphorylation levels of ULK1 (Fig. 3H, I, Supplementary Fig. 4D, E, and Fig. S3H, I from Supplementary Data 2). Ruxolitinib is a selective small-molecule inhibitor of CaMKII-γ

Despite KN-93 being recognized as a specific inhibitor of CaMKII26, its inhibitory efficacy is suboptimal, and it cannot differentiate between the four CaMKII isoforms. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop selective inhibitors targeting CaMKII-γ. To address this, we established an HTS platform using recombinant CaMKII-γ protein to screen a library of 2665 FDA-approved drugs for their potential to inhibit CaMKII-γ activity (Fig. 4A). In the initial screening, 8 compounds that inhibited CaMKII-γ activity by more than 80% at a concentration of 10 μM were identified (the complete HTS results are presented in Supplementary Data 1). These compounds were then subjected to secondary screening to determine their half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50). Based on these results, we further evaluated their selectivity towards different CaMKII isoforms. Among these, ruxolitinib (Fig. 4B) exhibited significant inhibitory activity against CaMKII-γ with an IC50 of 109 nM, along with notable isoform selectivity, demonstrating at least a 6-fold higher potency against CaMKII-γ compared to other isoforms (Fig. 4C, D and Fig. S4C from Supplementary Data 2). Ruxolitinib selectively inhibits CaMKII-γ via binding to its ATP-binding pocket

To explore the interaction between ruxolitinib and CaMKII-γ, as well as to elucidate the underlying mechanism, molecular docking simulations were conducted to model the binding mode of ruxolitinib (PubChem CID: 25127112) and CaMKII-γ (PBD ID: 2V7O). The docking result indicated that the optimal docking pose of ruxolitinib fitted well into the ATP-binding site, interacting with the V93 residue and C65 residue of CaMKII-γ through the formation of two hydrogen bonds. At the same time, ruxolitinib formed hydrophobic interactions with residues L20, V28, A41, V74, F90, L92, L143, L155, A156, and F158 of CaMKII-γ (Fig. 5A–C). Ruxolitinib reverses the resistance of bortezomib by down-regulating the autophagy pathway

We have confirmed that ruxolitinib is a potent and selective inhibitor of CaMKII-γ. To further investigate its effects on BRMM cells, the pharmaceutical inhibition of CaMKII-γ using ruxolitinib exerted more potent growth inhibition on BRMM cells than on their parental counterparts (Fig. 6A, B and Fig. S6A, B from Supplementary Data 2). Next, we cotreated BRMM cells and MM cells with ruxolitinib and bortezomib. The results demonstrated that combined treatment achieved remarkable growth inhibition in the resistant cells (Fig. 6C, D and Fig. S6C, D from Supplementary Data 2). In addition to biochemical validation, we used MDC staining to visualize autophagic vacuole formation after ruxolitinib treatment. U266.BR and KMS11.BR cells were treated with 10 μM ruxolitinib or vehicle (DMSO) for 24 h and subjected to MDC staining. The results revealed that ruxolitinib-treated BRMM cells displayed significantly fewer MDC-labeled autophagic vesicles compared to vehicle-treated BRMM cells (Fig. 6E and Fig. S6E from Supplementary Data 2), further supporting the conclusion that ruxolitinib suppresses autophagy in BRMM cells through CaMKII-γ inhibition. Then we treated BRMM cells with 10 μM ruxolitinib and observed no significant change in total CaMKII-γ levels, but a notable reduction in phosphorylated CaMKII-γ. Consistent with the knock-down results, the total levels of AMPK and ULK1 remained unchanged, while their phosphorylation levels were significantly reduced (Fig. 6F, G, Supplementary Fig. 5A, B, and Fig. S6F, G from Supplementary Data 2). Additionally, levels of the autophagy-related protein LC3-II were markedly decreased. Ruxolitinib significantly reverses the resistance of bortezomib in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of ruxolitinib on BRMM, we established the xenograft model of U266.BR cells in BALB/c nude mice (Fig. 7A). The results demonstrated that combined treatment of ruxolitinib + bortezomib and ruxolitinib treatment exhibited remarkable anti-tumor activity in the BRMM mouse model. Tumors in the vehicle-treated control group and bortezomib-treated group continued to grow steadily throughout the study period, whereas the combined treatment of ruxolitinib + bortezomib and ruxolitinib treatment resulted in a notable inhibition of tumor progression, supporting that ruxolitinib reversed the resistance of bortezomib effectively (Fig. 7B–D and Fig. S7C, D from Supplementary Data 2). And neither group showed significant weight loss during the study period (Fig. 7E and Fig. S7E from Supplementary Data 2). After a designated period, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were excised for further analysis. The H&E results showed structural disruption and increased necrosis in the combined treatment group and ruxolitinib treatment group compared to the vehicle control group. In addition, Ki67 staining revealed markedly reduced proliferative activity in the combined treatment group and ruxolitinib treatment group, aligning with the observed decrease in tumor volume (Fig. 7F). These immunohistochemical results are consistent with the observed reduction in tumor volume, confirming the reversal of ruxolitinib on bortezomib resistance. Moreover, Western blotting analysis of the tumor tissues confirmed the findings from the in vitro experiments. Discussion

Based on the previous study, which found that CaMKII-γ was a potential therapeutic target for MM, our research further explored the relationship between CaMKII-γ and drug-resistant MM. In this study, CaMKII-γ was identified as a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of BRMM. Given that bortezomib is widely used as a first-line agent in MM, our work mainly focuses on the mechanism underlying BRMM. Our results showed that the expression level of CaMKII-γ was significantly elevated in BRMM cells, and the silencing of CaMKII-γ via si-RNA significantly inhibited the proliferation of BRMM cells. We also found that the autophagy pathway mediated via the CaMKII-γ-AMPK-ULK1 axis regulated the proliferation of BRMM cells, as verified by western blotting. Then, through high-throughput screening, we identified ruxolitinib, a small-molecule inhibitor with high selectivity to CaMKII-γ among 2665 compounds, which effectively inhibited the proliferation of BRMM both in vitro and in vivo by suppressing the autophagy pathway via the CaMKII-γ-AMPK-ULK1 axis. Notably, the combination of ruxolitinib and bortezomib further enhanced the inhibitory effect in resistant cells, supporting its potential to reverse bortezomib resistance. Moreover, molecular docking simulation was employed to elucidate the interaction mechanism between ruxolitinib and CaMKII-γ. Our findings suggest that ruxolitinib, an FDA-approved drug, can be repurposed as a selective CaMKII-γ inhibitor for the treatment of BRMM, offering a promising therapeutic strategy. The existing clinical safety profile of ruxolitinib may also accelerate its translational potential in patients with bortezomib-resistant disease. This research highlights the importance of identifying novel therapeutic targets and developing targeted therapies to improve the outcomes for BRMM patients.

Based on our research findings, targeting CaMKII-γ in BRMM treatment shows certain feasibility and potential clinical value. Its core advantages lie in: a clear drug resistance driving mechanism (mediated by the CaMKII-γ-AMPK-ULK1 axis and autophagy), readily available highly selective drugs (ruxolitinib), and proven effects of drug resistance reversal and combined enhancement. Targeting CaMKII-γ not only provides new treatment options for BRMM patients but also enables individualized treatment through precise stratification, improving patient prognosis. Although there is a risk of off-target effects related to the multi-target nature of ruxolitinib, this can be effectively managed through dose optimization, precise delivery, and dynamic monitoring. In the future, relying on drug repositioning strategies to accelerate the clinical trial process, while improving the detection standards for CaMKII-γ, optimizing combination regimens, and exploring drug resistance mechanisms, will be key to promoting targeted CaMKII-γ treatment for BRMM from the laboratory to clinical practice.

Importantly, studies have found that bortezomib resistance is induced by activation of the AMPK-ULK1 axis-mediated protective autophagy in MM cells, in which NCX 1/Ca2+ triggers the autophagy flux of MM cells through the non-classical NF-κB pathway, thereby reducing the sensitivity of MM to bortezomib17. In addition, CaMKII, as an important regulator of Ca2+ signaling, has also been found to significantly enhance autophagy flux to protect cell survival under short-term starvation conditions. Therefore, we hypothesized that BRMM cells may also achieve cell survival by activating the CaMKII-γ-AMPK-ULK1 axis to enhance the autophagy pathway, which was verified in this study.

Most studies have focused on the crucial role of ruxolitinib in reversing immune modulator resistance, which makes it particularly advantageous in the treatment of RRMM. Specifically, MM cells with immune modulator resistance often exhibit abnormal activation of the JAK/STAT pathway, leading to enhanced cell proliferation and resistance to apoptosis. Ruxolitinib can precisely inhibit this abnormally activated pathway, reverse the malignant phenotype of resistant cells, and restore their sensitivity to immune modulators. For example, multiple in vitro experiments have confirmed that ruxolitinib combined with lenalidomide can significantly increase the apoptosis rate of RRMM cell lines, showing a significant synergistic effect compared to the single-agent treatment group27. Moreover, clinical studies have also observed that for MM patients who have failed immune modulator treatment, ruxolitinib alone or in combination regimens can effectively control disease progression and prolong the progression-free survival of patients28,29. These research results not only provide a solid basis for the application of ruxolitinib in RRMM treatment but also offer new therapeutic ideas for solving clinical resistance problems.

One limitation of this study is that the binding mode of ruxolitinib and CaMKII-γ was obtained by molecular docking simulations, which is not exact enough. Future studies can perform X-ray diffraction to resolve the true co-crystal structure of ruxolitinib and CaMKII-γ. Another limitation of the current work is the reliance on subcutaneous tumor models. Although these models offer practical advantages for measuring tumor volume and response, they lack the anatomical and physiological context of the original tissue, which can influence drug delivery, immune infiltration, and metastatic behavior. To overcome this constraint and strengthen the translational impact of our research, subsequent investigations will utilize orthotopic implantation models to achieve a more faithful representation of human disease. These improvements will further clarify the therapeutic value of targeting CaMKII-γ in BRMM.

For the treatment of MM, drug resistance remains a significant challenge, with mechanisms including gene mutations, epigenetic alterations and influences from the tumor microenvironment30. Although traditional chemotherapy remains the primary treatment, its efficacy is limited. And novel therapeutic strategies such as chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy, monoclonal antibody therapy and bispecific antibody therapy have shown promising results in clinical treatment31–33. Future research will focus on elucidating resistance mechanisms, developing new targeted drugs, and optimizing combination therapy strategies to improve treatment outcomes and survival rates for patients with drug-resistant MM. Our study provides a basis for such efforts by identifying CaMKII-γ as a potential target and suggesting that ruxolitinib-based combinations may offer therapeutic benefit in BRMM. As the research progresses, the potential of combining ruxolitinib with proteasome inhibitors, immunomodulators, CD38 monoclonal antibodies, and CAR-T cells also deserves exploration to achieve the multi-level synergy of “pathway inhibition + targeted killing + immune remodeling”. This is expected to build a more comprehensive and effective treatment system for patients with RRMM, ultimately breaking through the current treatment limitations.

Materials and methods

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis was conducted using R 4.3.2, and the MM Research Foundation CoMMpass database (MMRFCoMMpass), which included bone marrow microarray data from 764 primary MM patients and 80 recurrent MM patients, was studied. Informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to the collection and use of their tissue samples. Here, recurrent MM patients and primary MM patients were defined according to the clinical annotations provided in the database. According to the CoMMpass clinical annotation, primary MM patients represent newly diagnosed and treatment-naive MM patients, whereas recurrent MM patients represent relapsed MM patients who previously received standard therapies, including proteasome inhibitor-based regimens such as bortezomib, dexamethasone, and melphalan. Firstly, primary and recurrent MM patients in the dataset were grouped, and log2 conversion on the expression level of CAMK2G was performed. Subsequently, “ggplot2” package was used to draw violin plots, and “ggpubr” package was used to add statistical significance markers to assess the differential expression levels of CAMK2G in different groups.

Survival analysis

Survival analysis of high and low expression of CAMK2G was conducted on recurrent MM patients, with the median expression level set as the threshold, and the samples were divided into high CAMK2G group and low CAMK2G group. Furthermore, the “survfit” function of the “Survival” package was used to analyze the survival differences between the two groups, and the log-rank test method was used to evaluate the significance of survival differences between samples from different groups.

Functional enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis was conducted using R 4.3.2, and bone marrow microarray data of primary MM patients and recurrent MM patients from MMRFCoMMpass database were studied. Firstly, a differential expression analysis method based on linear models was adopted to identify the genes with significantly differential expression between the recurrent group and the primary group using “limma”. The target comparison group was defined using the “makeContrasts” function to quantify the expression differences between the two groups. The “lmFit” function was employed to conduct linear regression modeling on the gene expression matrix, correlating the expression levels with the grouping variables. Using the “eBayes” function to conduct “Empirical Bayes moderation” on the residual variance of the model, thereby enhancing the robustness of variance estimation in small sample data. The adjusted P-value was less than 0.05, which was used to control the false positive rate in multiple comparisons. Based on the strict criteria mentioned above, 995 genes with significant differences in the recurrent MM group were identified. To further explore the functions of these differentially expressed genes, GO enrichment analysis was conducted using the “enrichGO” function of the “clusterProfiler” package. Subsequently, the differentially expressed genes were mapped to the GO database to calculate the significance of enrichment for each type of function. To visually display the results of GO enrichment analysis, the “dotplot” function was adopted to draw the bubble chart, thereby facilitating the identification of functional genes closely related to recurrent MM patients. Then, the “read” function was used to load the preprocessed expression matrix. To ensure data reliability, the first column gene names were set as matrix row names using the “row. names = 1” parameter, resulting in a standardized expression matrix. Using the median expression value of CAMK2G as the threshold, the samples were divided into the high-expression group and the low-expression group. And factor-type variables were adopted for grouping, which was used for subsequent difference analysis. A framework for differential analysis was constructed based on the “linear model”. The linear model was fitted by the “lmFit” function of the “limma” package, and Bayesian smoothing correction was performed by the “eBayes” function. To analyze the biological functions of differentially expressed genes, based on the above differential gene analysis, the “enrichGO” function of the “clusterProfiler” package was subsequently used for GO enrichment analysis. In order to visually display the results of GO enrichment analysis, the bubble chart using the “dotplot” function was also generated to help identify functional genes closely related to high expression of CAMK2G in recurrent MM patients.

Cell culture

U266 and KMS11 cell lines were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Cat#C11875500BT) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Cat#10099141C) and 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin Solution (Gibco, Cat#15140122). All cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Establishment of the BRMM cell line

U266 and KMS11 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were selected and placed in RPMI 1640 complete medium containing the first-line treatment drug for multiple myeloma, bortezomib. The concentration gradient of bortezomib was set at 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, and 160 nM, starting from the lowest concentration of 5 nM and increasing in a stepwise manner. After treating the cells with bortezomib for 24 h, bortezomib was removed and replaced with fresh complete medium, and the cells were continued to be cultured. Then, the concentration of bortezomib was gradually increased, usually once every 4 weeks. In the bortezomib medium at a concentration of 160 nM, the cells proliferated stably and were identified as bortezomib-resistant cell lines, named U266.BR and KMS11.BR respectively.

Western blotting analysis

Cell specimens were washed three times with PBS buffer, and tissue samples were ground using a mortar. Then, the total protein from the cell and tissue was extracted using Radio-Immunoprecipitation Assay buffer (RIPA) (Servicebio, G2002). Lysates from cells and tissues were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE; 10% polyacrylamide gels), and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Servicebio, Cat#G6044-0.45) and blocked with Protein Free Rapid Blocking Buffer (Servicebio, Cat#G2052). The membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with TBST, membranes were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h, and detected with Super-Sensitive ECL chemiluminescent substrate (Biosharp, Cat#BL520A). The antibodies used were described before. Quantification of western blotting was performed using the ImageJ software. The antibodies used in this study are as follows: CaMKII-γ (Abcam, Cat#AB201966, 1:2,000); p-CaMKII-γ (CST, Cat#181602, 1:1,000); GAPDH (Abcam, Cat#AB181602, 1:12,000); p-ULK1 (Ser555) (CST, Cat#5869, 1:1,000); ULK1 (CST, Cat#8054, 1:1,000); p-AMPK (Thr172) (CST, Cat#2535, 1:1,000); AMPK (CST, Cat#5831, 1:1,000); LC3-II (CST, Cat#2775, 1:1,000); Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (HRP) (Abcam, Cat#AB205719, 1:20,000); Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L (HRP) (Abcam, Cat#AB205718, 1:20,000).

Immumohistochemical staining

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) bone marrow biopsy tissues sections of newly diagnosed MM patients and relapsed/refractory MM patients were purchased from Shanghai YEPCOME Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to the collection and use of their tissue samples. Sections were cut at around 4 µm thickness and placed on glass slides, and stained with CaMKII-γ antibody (Abcam, Cat#AB201966, 1:200). Adding 2 drops of freshly prepared DAB solution to each sheet, and then re-dyeing and dehydration seal. Sections were scanned by a digital pathology scanner.

H&E and Ki67 staining

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% formaldehyde solution and processed routinely for paraffin embedding. Sections were cut at around 4 µm thickness and placed on glass slides, and counterstained with hematoxylin and eosin and anti-Ki-67(CST, Cat#9449, 1:400).

MDC staining

To visualize autophagic activity in BRMM cells, monodansylcadaverine (MDC) staining was performed using the MDC Autophagy Detection Kit (Beyotime, Cat#C3018S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For suspension cell staining, 1 × 106 cells per group were collected by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min at room temperature and washed once with PBS. Cells were resuspended in 1 mL working MDC staining solution (prepared by diluting 1000× MDC stock 1:1000 with 1× Assay Buffer) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following incubation, cells were washed three times with 1× Assay Buffer (1 mL each time) and then resuspended in 1 mL Assay Buffer for imaging. Stained cells were smeared on glass slides and immediately examined under a fluorescence microscope using a UV excitation filter set (excitation wavelength 335 nm, emission wavelength 512 nm). MDC-labeled autophagic vacuoles appeared as distinct punctate green fluorescence in the cytoplasm. For all experiments, staining and imaging were performed under minimal light exposure to reduce photobleaching. Negative controls were included and processed in parallel.

RNA interference analysis

The specific CaMKII-γ small interfering RNA (siRNA) was synthesized by Guangzhou RiboBio Co. Ltd. Cells were plated into 6-well plates at 8 × 105 cells/well, and then transfected with each density of 100 nM CaMKII-γ siRNA or scrambled negative siRNA using Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent (Invitrogen, Cat#11668030), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The medium was replaced 24 h post-transfection, and the cells were cultured in fresh medium. Cells were harvested at 72 h post-transfection. The success of the siRNA transfection was confirmed by demonstrating the inhibition of CaMKII-γ protein expression.

High-throughput screening for CaMKII-γ inhibitors

To discover new inhibitors for CaMKII-γ, a high-throughput screening system was established based on the CaMKII-γ kinase activity using 384-well plates. Human recombinant CaMKII-γ was purchased from OriGene (Cat# TP317059L) to test the inhibitory effects of the FDA-approved drug library containing 2665 FDA-approved drugs (Obtained from MedChemExpress). CaMKII-γ kinase activity was monitored by ADP-Glo™ luminescence assay (Promega, Cat# V9102). When CaMKII-γ was activated, it phosphorylated substrates by converting ATP to ADP. In this assay, the phosphorylation of a peptide substrate (autocamtide-3, KKALHRQETVDAL, produced by BioAct Peptide Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) of CaMKII-γ was enzymatically coupled to the transition of ATP to ADP. The ADP-Glo reagent (Promega, Cat# V9102) was used to stop the kinase reaction and remove unreacted ATP. After adding kinase detection reagent (Promega, Cat# V9102) to stop ATP depletion reaction and convert ADP to ATP, ATP was further converted to light by luciferase reaction (Promega, Cat# V9102), with the final volume of 10 μL in CaMKII-γ (2 ng/μL diluted in 25 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 2 mM DTT), 25 mmol/L Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.2 mmol/L CaCl2, 10 mmol/L MgCl2, 0.035 mmol/L ATP (Promega, Cat# V9102), 200 μmol/L autocamtide-3 (BioAct Peptide Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), 30 nmol/L CaM (Sigma, catalog No. C4874) and 0.1 mg/mL bovine serum albumin. The reactions of the positive control group contained all the above components except compounds. The reactions of the experiment group contained all the above components, and different compounds with a final concentration of 10 μM were added into the different wells. The reactions of the negative control group contained all the above components except ATP. The kinase reactions were allowed to run for 30 min at room temperature (25–27 °C). The luminescence of different wells was then quantitatively measured using a microplate reader. The inhibitory effects of the tested compounds were calculated using the following formula: (Experimental group − Negative control group)/(Positive control group − Negative control group).

Kinase activity assay

To evaluate the inhibitory effect of the tested compounds on CaMKIIs activity, ADP-Glo™ Kinase Assay (Promega, Cat# V9102) was used following the manufacturer’s protocol. Human CaMKII recombinant proteins were purchased from OriGene (Cat# TP318186, Cat# TP305669, Cat# TP317059 and Cat# TP307454). CaMKII isoforms (2 ng/μL diluted in 25 mM Tris-HCl pH7.5, 2 mM DTT) were assayed against autocamtide-3 (KKALHRQETVDAL, produced by BioAct Peptide Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) in a final volume of 10 μL 25 mmol/L Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 0.2 mmol/L CaCl2, 10 mmol/L MgCl2, 0.035 mmol/L ATP (Promega, Cat# V9102), 200 μmol/L autocamtide-3 (BioAct Peptide Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), 30 nmol/L CaM (Sigma, catalog No. C4874) and 0.1 mg/mL bovine serum albumin. The reactions of the positive control group contained all the above components except compounds. The reactions of the experiment group contained all the above components, and different concentration gradients of compounds were added into the different wells. The reactions of the negative control group contained all the above components except ATP. The kinase reaction was allowed to run for 30 min at room temperature (25–27 °C). Then, ADP-Glo reagent (Promega, Cat# V9102) was used to stop the kinase reaction and remove unreacted ATP. After adding kinase detection reagent (Promega, Cat# V9102) to stop the ATP depletion reaction and convert ADP to ATP, ATP was further converted to light by luciferase reaction (Promega, Cat# V9102). The luminescence was then quantitatively measured using a microplate reader. The inhibitory effects of the tested compounds were calculated using the following formula: (Experimental group − Negative control group)/(Positive control group − Negative control group).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability assay was conducted using CellTiter-Glo™ luminescence assay (Promega, Cat#G9243), which indirectly reflected the number of living cells by measuring the ATP content within living cells. Its core principle is to use luciferase to catalyze the reaction between luciferin and ATP to produce a luminescent signal. The intensity of the luminescence is proportional to the ATP concentration.

To verify the resistance of BRMM cell lines to bortezomib, U266, U266.BR, KMS11 and KMS11.BR cells were seeded at 8000 cells/well in 96-well plates and treated with bortezomib at different concentrations for 48 h. Then, adding the same volume of CellTiter-Glo™ reagent (Promega, Cat#G9243) to each well and incubating the plates in a Microporous quick shaker for 10 min. Then the incubated 96-well plate was placed on the microplate reader to quantitatively measure the luminescence.

To verify whether knock-down of CaMKII-γ effects the proliferation of BRMM cells, 2000 BRMM si-NC cells/well and 2000 BRMM si-CaMKII-γ cells/well were seeded in 5 different 96-well plates, and one of the 96-well plates was detected every day. From day 0 to day 4, adding the same volume of CellTiter-Glo™ reagent (Promega, Cat#G9243) to each well in one of 96-well plates every day and incubating the plates in a Microporous quick shaker for 10 min. Then the incubated 96-well plate was placed on the microplate reader to quantitatively measure the luminescence. To get the relative proliferative rate, the luminescent values of day 0 were normalized to 1.0, and the resulting normalized values were plotted.

To verify the inhibitory activity of ruxolitinib on the proliferation of MM cells and BRMM cells, MM cells and BRMM cells were seeded at 8000 cells/well in 96-well plates and treated with ruxolitinib at different concentrations for 48 h. Then, adding the same volume of CellTiter-Glo™ reagent (Promega, Cat#G9243) to each well and incubating the plates in a Microporous quick shaker for 10 min. Then the incubated 96-well plate was placed on the microplate reader to quantitatively measure the luminescence.

Xenograft mouse model

Four-week-old to six-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Huachuang Sino Laboratory Animals Co., Ltd. The experimental procedures and animal protocols were approved by the Biological and Medical Ethics Committee of Taiyuan University of Technology. The temperature and humidity of the animal room are maintained at 20–26 °C and 40%–70%, respectively. All mice were given 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness in turn each day. All experiments involving mice were performed in compliance with all relevant ethical regulations. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and all procedures were carried out by trained personnel in compliance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC).

To investigate the effect of CaMKII-γ on BRMM in vivo, U266.BR cells transfected with negative control siRNA were resuspended in PBS:Matrigel (1:1) and subcutaneously injected into the right groin of each mouse (1.5 × 10⁷ cells in 0.1 mL PBS mixed with 0.1 mL Matrigel). When the subcutaneous tumors reached 80 ~ 100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into 2 groups (n = 5 per group): si-NC group and si-NC + bortezomib group. The mice in si-NC + bortezomib group received intravenous injection of bortezomib (0.1 mg/kg in 0.2 mL saline) twice per week. At the same time, U266.BR cells transfected with CaMKII-γ-targeting siRNA were resuspended in PBS:Matrigel (1:1) and subcutaneously injected into the right groin of each mouse (1.5 × 10⁷cells in 0.1 mL PBS mixed with 0.1 mL Matrigel). When the subcutaneous tumors reached 80 ~ 100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into the other 2 groups (n = 5 per group): si-CaMKII-γ group and si-CaMKII-γ + bortezomib group. The mice in si-CaMKII-γ + bortezomib group received intravenous injection of bortezomib (0.1 mg/kg in 0.2 mL saline) twice per week. Tumor volumes and body weight were measured every three days. Tumor volume was calculated as follows: volume (mm3) = (length × width2)/2. At the study endpoint, mice were sacrificed, and tumors were excised, weighed, photographed, and processed for H&E staining and Ki-67 immunohistochemistry. Then, the histological features and proliferation indices were compared between the above 4 groups.

To investigate the effect of ruxolitinib on BRMM in vivo, U266.BR cells in the logarithmic growth phase were resuspended in PBS:Matrigel (1:1) and subcutaneously injected into the right groin of each mouse (1.5 × 10⁷cells in 0.1 mL PBS mixed with 0.1 mL Matrigel). When the subcutaneous tumors reached 80 ~ 100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 5 per group): vehicle control group, bortezomib treatment group, ruxolitinib treatment group and ruxolitinib + bortezomib treatment group. The bortezomib treatment group received intravenous injection of bortezomib (0.1 mg/kg in 0.2 mL saline) twice a week, ruxolitinib treatment group received oral administration of ruxolitinib (60 mg/kg in 0.2 mL saline) every other day, ruxolitinib + bortezomib treatment group received intravenous injection of bortezomib (0.1 mg/kg in 0.2 mL saline) twice a week and oral administration of ruxolitinib (60 mg/kg in 0.2 mL saline) every three day, while the vehicle control group received an equal volume of 0.9% saline following the same schedule. Tumor volumes and body weight were measured once every 3 days. Tumor volume was calculated as follows: volume (mm3) = (length × width2)/2. At the study endpoint, tumors were harvested and processed for H&E staining, Ki-67 immunohistochemistry and western blotting analysis.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data were expressed as mean ± SD. Unpaired numerical data were compared using an unpaired t test (two groups) or analysis of variance (ANOVA) for more than two groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. As indicated in the figure legends, all in vitro experiments were performed in three biological replicates unless stated otherwise. Representative micrographs and western blotting shown in figures were repeated three times independently with similar results.
