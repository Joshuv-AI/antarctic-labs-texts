Return to play time for the 30 most common injuries in the UEFA Women’s Elite Club Injury Study
Authors: Jan Ekstrand, Vanna Roos, Anna Hallén, Katrine Okholm Kryger, Håkan Bengtsson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesOur aim was to describe the absence days caused by the 30 most frequent injury diagnoses in elite European women’s club football.MethodsMembers of the medical staff in football clubs participating in the Union of European Football Associations (UEFA) Women’s Elite Club Injury Study (WECIS) reported injury occurrences from a total of 72 team-seasons. Injury absence was defined as the number of days between injury occurrence and when the injured player was allowed to return to full participation by the medical team.ResultsIn total, 2 390 injuries were registered and included in the study. Sixty-six per cent of these injuries were included among the 30 most common injury diagnoses. Most of the injuries included among the 30 most common were mild leading to a median absence of 7 days or less in 397 cases (23%) or moderate leading to a median absence of 7–28 days in 1177 cases (69%). Severe injuries accounted for a median absence of ≥28 days in 140 cases (8%) were less common and included only four diagnoses, all related to knee injuries. These four diagnoses, however, caused 35% of all absence during the study period.ConclusionMost of the time lost due to injuries in elite European women’s football teams results from moderate and severe injuries. This article provides guidelines for expected time-loss for the most common injury types in women’s professional football and will aid clinicians to make more accurate estimations of when players can be expected to return to participation following injuries.Keywords: Female, Anterior cruciate ligament, Epidemiology, Hamstring, Soccer

Introduction

When a football player sustains an injury, the primary question for medical staff is often, “When can they return to play?"1 2 Prognostic information is essential for the medical staff to answer such inquiries from players, coaches, managers, media and agents.1 2

There are economic and competitive repercussions tied to the absence of elite players, making the timeline for returning to training or match-play crucial for both the players and the team.3 A safe return to play (RTP) relies on effective injury management and reinjury prevention strategies.1,3 The art of balancing risks and rewards in determining the optimal timing for a player’s return is crucial. While it is recommended that RTP decisions be guided by specific criteria,4,7 the lack of reliable benchmarks often complicates this process, increasing the risk of reinjury due to premature returns.5,8 Therefore, evidence-based epidemiological data for estimating recovery times can be beneficial for medical personnel when addressing RTP inquiries.9

Data from men’s elite football injury studies, notably the Union of European Football Associations (UEFA) Elite Club Injury Study (ECIS), offer valuable insights for RTP decisions.9 10 Similarly, the UEFA Women’s Elite Club Injury Study (WECIS), initiated in July 2018 using the same framework as the ECIS, has generated a database containing 2390 time-loss injuries from women’s elite football clubs across Europe.11 This extensive dataset provides valuable information regarding the incidence and recovery duration applied for various injuries.11

The objective of this study was to provide details about absence days and return to play for the 30 injury types with the highest incidence rate in elite European women’s club football.

Materials and methods

Study design

This analysis is a post hoc examination of data collected prospectively from the highest level of women’s elite club football in Europe, specifically the UEFA Women’s Elite Club Injury Study (WECIS). The study tracked a total of 16 teams across eight countries over six seasons, from 2018 to 2024, resulting in 72 team-seasons being included. During this period, 2390 injuries were reported, comprising 2162 index injuries and 228 reinjuries.

Study participants

Eight of the 16 teams were among the top 10 of the UEFA Women’s Club ranking list for seasons 2018/19 to 2023/24. Another six teams had a ranking between 11 and 60. Two teams were not included in the top 100 of the UEFA ranking but were included as the club’s men’s team were already included in ECIS. At the beginning of pre-season, or on joining mid-season, all first-team squad players were informed about the study by their team physician and gave their written informed consent to participate. Players who transferred to or from a team outside of WECIS during the season were included for the entire time at a participating team.11

Study procedure

The methodology followed the guidelines set forth in the original consensus statement for football12 and largely adhered to the International Olympic Committee (IOC) consensus statement,13 along with its recent extension specifically addressing football.14

Data collection

The definitions and methods of data collection for WECIS have been detailed in prior publications.11 In summary, on inclusion in the study, each team designated contact personnel from their medical staff responsible for collection and reporting all relevant data throughout their participation. Information regarding football exposure and injury occurrences was collected at an individual player level using standardised forms uniformly across all participating clubs. The collected data were submitted to the research group for monthly evaluation, ensuring compliance with the study methodology.

Injuries were defined using a time-loss criterion as “any physical complaint incurred by a player during a football match or training that prevented full participation in subsequent football activities.”12,14 RTP was defined as when an injured player returned to full participation in team training and was available for match selection. The duration of a player’s absence due to an injury was measured as the total number of days from the date when the injury occurred to the date of RTP. The injury cards also contained information on whether the injury was considered to be a reinjury, defined as “an injury of the same type and location as a prior injury.”

Injury diagnosis

The standardised injury forms included details regarding the nature of reported injuries. Initially, the location and type of injury were specified in accordance with the consensus statement,13 utilising closed questions where contact persons selected from a range of options to identify the injury’s characteristics. Additionally, contact persons were instructed to provide a free text diagnosis for each reported injury, which was then used by members of the study group to assign a standardised diagnosis to the injury.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive data about injury absence was described for the 30 injury diagnoses that were most frequently reported during the six-season study period. Injury absence was described as days absent and presented with medians including 95% CI, means including 95% CI, the 25th and 75th percentiles, and the range for the 30 most common injury diagnoses separately.

The CI was calculated using the standard deviation (σ), along with the sample size (n) and the appropriate z-value (z*) from the standard normal distribution for the desired confidence level (for 95% CI the z*-value 1.96 was used). The formula was 95% CI = ± z*×(σ∕√n). The 95% CI for the median was calculated using the formulas j=n× m˗1.96×sqrt (n×m (1 m)) for the observation of the lower limit and k=n× m˗1.96×sqrt (n×m (1 m)) for the observation of the upper limit, where n is the number of observations and m is the median. The parameters j and k are rounded up to the next integer and the 95% CI is found between the jth and the kth observations in the ordered data. The reinjury rate was presented as the proportion of injuries with the specific injury diagnosis that were considered a reinjury according to the reporting team.

SPSS Statistics (version 29.0, IBM, Armonk, NY) was used for all analyses.

Mild injuries with median absence no more than seven days included contusions, functional muscle injuries (muscle disorder without macroscopic evidence of muscle tear)3 5 and overuse pain syndromes such as lower back pain. All structural muscle injuries (muscle injury with macroscopic evidence of muscle tear)3 5 were moderate with a median absence ranging from 13 days, for structural hamstring injuries, to 18.5 days, for structural calf muscle injuries. The moderate injury category, with median absence between 8 and 28 days, also included groin-related pain syndromes and both lateral and medial ankle ligament injuries, while all severe injury diagnoses (median absence ≥29 days) affected the knee (table 1).

The frequency of lateral meniscus injury was similar to medial meniscus injury, but the lateral injuries had a longer median absence (83.5 vs 52.5 days). On the other hand, the reinjury rate for medial meniscus injuries was 21.4% compared with 14.3% for lateral injuries.

Discussion

The most important finding of this study was that the 30 most prevalent injury diagnoses account for over three-quarters of all injury-related time lost in women’s professional football. As such, medical professionals will be able to collect data from this study about expected absence days and valuable prognostic insights considering most injuries that they treat in their clinical practice at women’s football clubs.

Mild injuries – The possibility of staying and playing

Ten of the 30 most common injury diagnoses were mild conditions, associated with a median absence of no more than 7 days. These injuries are not likely to have much impact on the long-term health of players or team performance, with only 5% of all reported absence during the study period caused by these 10 diagnoses, despite their frequency. In fact, most of these injuries will probably not cause players to miss any matches and can as such be considered “stay-and-play” injuries.14 Notable among these mild conditions were contusions to five different locations but also functional injuries to both the quadriceps and hamstring muscles. It is also worth noting that all mild diagnoses in this study were considered mild injuries in men’s football as well, highlighting the potential for quick recuperation following these types of injuries.2

Moderate injuries – The leading cause of absence in professional football

Moderate injuries, resulting in a median absence of 8 to 28 days, appear to be the most common in women’s football. This is true in terms of the number of diagnoses included in the category, with 16 of the 30 most common diagnoses, in terms of the total number of absence days caused, being responsible for 37% of the total absence caused by injuries during the study period, and in terms of the total number of injuries. In fact, the seven most common injury diagnoses in our study were all moderate injuries. Six of these (hamstring muscle injury (structural), groin adductor pain, ankle lateral ligament injury, quadriceps muscle injury (structural), calf muscle injury (structural) and knee medial collateral ligament injury) were also the six most common in men’s football. The only exception to this similarity, between men’s and women’s football, were concussions which were less common and not among the seven most common injury types in men’s football and also caused shorter absence in men’s football and was thus considered a mild injury.

The relative frequency of concussions in the women’s game has been described and discussed previously11 and is concerning since concussions may have long-lasting effects,15 16 especially if players suffer a second blow to the head before a prior concussion has been totally resolved, known as the second impact syndrome.17 A structured rehabilitation protocol has been developed to reduce the risk of second impact syndromes.18 This protocol states, in accordance with international consensus,16 that athletes suffering from a concussion should stepwise increase their activity level over a period of a minimum of 6 days, without recurrent symptoms, before they are allowed to RTP. It is thus encouraging that the median period of absence following a concussion in this cohort was 9.0 days, indicating that at women’s elite level clubs the minimum recommended period of 6 days of rehabilitation is respected in most cases.

Severe injuries – Possible long-term implications for the players

Similar to men’s football,2 there were only a few injury diagnoses that were included in the severe injury category with a median absence of 29 days or longer. These were knee cartilage injury, medial meniscus injury, lateral meniscus injury, and ACL (anterior cruciate ligament) injury. It is worth noting that all four of these diagnoses are located to the knee, highlighting the need for effective preventive measures targeting knee injuries but perhaps also indicating that preventive measures that have been proven effective in reducing knee injuries among youth footballers, such as knee control and FIFA11+, may not be as effective in the highest level of women’s football.19 20

Together, the four severe injury diagnoses accounted for 6% of all reported injuries but 35% of all injury absence during the study period. Severe knee injuries can thus substantially affect teams’ player availability and team performance, despite their infrequency, due to the lengthy absences they entail. It is thus obvious that just looking at the frequency of injuries is not enough to quantify their impact on professional football, their expected absence will also be of utmost importance. As such, we recommend injury burden, a combined measure of injuries frequency and severity, as the most clinically viable method to establish the impact of different injuries on player availability in professional football.21

The median RTP to full team training following ACL injuries was approximately 10 months

Specifically, ACL ruptures are notable for a median absence of 292 days (95% CI: 285 to 314 days), which falls within the recommended recovery period of 9 to 12 months for the general population, as outlined in a recent systematic review and multidisciplinary consensus.22 The reinjury rate is relatively low at 9.8%. In a prior study examining RTP in elite men’s athletes (utilising the same definitions), the median RTP to full team training was 205 days (about 7 months), and fewer than 15% of players (25 out of 183 ACL injuries) in the male cohort experienced an absence period exceeding 9 months.2

Furthermore, the reinjury rate in this male cohort was even lower, at 6.6%.2

Lateral meniscus injuries as common as medial ones but with longer RTP

Traditionally, orthopaedic surgeons have thought that medial meniscal tears occur about three times more often than lateral tears in the general population.23 However, there are few studies confirming this belief, and to our knowledge, no research has investigated whether there are gender differences in the frequency of these injuries. In a retrospective study conducted by Ridley et al,24 which examined patients who had surgery for isolated meniscal injuries based on age, it was found that lateral meniscal tears were more common in individuals under 20 and decreased with age, whereas medial meniscal tears became more frequent as people got older. Interestingly, in the 20–30 age group, the rates of both types of injuries were the same. This aligns with the findings in professional football players within that age range. The observation of a longer recovery period for lateral meniscus injuries suggests that more research is needed in this area.

Methodological considerations

This study is strengthened by the cooperation of 16 professional football teams, all competing at high level of European football, allowing for a large dataset including a relatively homogeneous group of women’s professional footballers. The study methodology adheres closely to international guidelines for conducting epidemiological research in football11,13 which increases the potential to reliably use the results to assess injury risk and patterns in women’s elite football.

While the homogeneous dataset is considered a strength, it should also be mentioned that the cohort consists of professional footballers who have access to optimal care, including prompt diagnoses and, in many instances, personalised rehabilitation overseen by dedicated physiotherapists. Consequently, the absence days reported in this study should be viewed as best-case scenarios. It is thus plausible that most diagnoses would cause longer absences in other populations, such as lower-tier football, in which optimal care and rehabilitation may not always be available. It should also be noted that, even though the large dataset is considered a strength, there were only a few observations of some of the more uncommon diagnoses. Absence days for these injuries are thus based on data from few cases and the precision of these estimates may thus be lower as indicated by relatively wide CIs and interquartile ranges. Furthermore, striving for the shortest possible absence after an injury may not always be in the players' best interest, as it could elevate the risk of reinjury.8 Lastly, the absence data presented in this study should not be used as time-based return to play criteria but rather considered prognostic information about expected absence days.

This study prioritises median absence over mean absence, as there can be significant discrepancies between the two for certain diagnoses. Several injury diagnoses in the present study had a mean absence more than twice as high as the median indicating a skewed dataset. The mean is typically applicable to data that adhere to a normal distribution and can be heavily influenced by outliers, while the median is more suitable for skewed distributions, providing a more robust representation of central tendency. Given the skewness of the data analysed, the median serves as the primary measure of expected absence in this study.

However, we have also included means alongside the 95% CIs in table 1. The 95% CI indicates that if we were to obtain 100 different data samples and compute a 95% CI for each, approximately 95 of those intervals would include the true mean value. The standard deviation quantifies how much individual observations within a sample deviate, on average, from the sample mean.

A possible limitation is that the study does not account for clustering by team or player. This might be a methodological limitation, as players within the same team are likely to be exposed to similar medical support, rehabilitation pathways, and team policies.

Conclusion

This study will aid clinicians with valuable information about expected absences for the most common injury diagnoses in women’s professional football. Our data shows that almost three out of four injuries in women’s professional football will be diagnosed as one of the 30 diagnoses included in this study and these data will thus be helpful in the management of the majority of all injury occurrences in this setting providing clinicians with estimated absence times. Our data also suggest that even fewer injury diagnoses contribute with the majority of all time-loss in this cohort with the four severe injury diagnoses providing 35% of all injury absence. This suggests that these four injuries might have the largest clinical impact on professional football players and teams, and it will therefore be important for clinicians in the field to excel in the management of these injuries to be able to provide the best medical service possible.
