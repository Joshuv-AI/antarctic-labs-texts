Cytokine markers in intracerebral hemorrhage are linked to ventricular imaging and outcomes
Authors: Gracey Sorensen, Lauren Grychowski, Jennifer Yan, Will Remillard, Alice Lidman, Tori Edwards, Alison L. Herman, Conor W. Johnson, Kevin N. Sheth, Charles Matouk, Guido J. Falcone, Emily J. Gilmore, Seyedmehdi Payabvash, Lauren H. Sansing, Jessica Magid-Bernstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Central nervous system (CNS) inflammation after intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is substantial, but its relationship to radiographic severity and outcome remains unclear. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) obtained via external ventricular drains (EVDs) offers a direct window into CNS inflammation. We hypothesized that a stronger proinflammatory CSF cytokine response would be associated with ventricular enlargement and worse functional outcome.Methods:In this study, 56 ICH patients and 25 controls with normal pressure hydrocephalus were analyzed. CSF (days 0-16) and plasma (days 0-7) samples were. Concentrations of C—C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), and vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A) were measured. Ventricular volumes were derived from two computed tomography (CT) scans per patient (admission and EVD-clamp time point). Associations with ventricular volume change and 3-month modified Rankin Scale (mRS) were assessed using regression models.Results:Detectable CSF cytokines were elevated in ICH patients compared to control plasma (all p < 0.001), supporting compartmentalized CNS inflammation. Higher late CSF log[CCL2] was independently associated with greater ventricular enlargement (β = 33.41, 95% CI 7.22–59.60, p = 0.019; q = 0.034). Higher late CSF log[IL-6] (β=0.78, p = 0.011) and log[IL-8] (β=0.77, p = 0.009) remained independently associated with worse 3-month mRS.Conclusions:CSF cytokines reflect the post-ICH central inflammatory response and are associated with ventricular enlargement and functional outcome, highlighting their potential as biomarkers of disease progression and therapeutic response post-ICH.

Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a leading cause of global disability and mortality, with studies showing that 33% of patients die within one month and 50% within one year.1 Despite encompassing only 10-20% of strokes, ICH is associated with a high global burden of disability due to years of healthy life lost from disability and premature death.1, 2 Studies typically focus on hematoma size, bleed location, and blood pressure control as predictors of functional outcomes.3 Long-term outcomes for ICH patients are multifaceted, encompassing not only survival but also health-related quality of life.

An inflammatory response is known to be activated after ICH, yet specific relationships between outcome and levels of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation remains poorly understood. Animal models have shown various phases of CNS inflammation after ICH leading to neuronal death and contributing to recovery.4 Transcriptomic analysis of hematoma evacuates from patients also revealed distinct, time-dependent phases of myeloid cell activation.5 Given the complex nature of ICH and its long-term impact, further research into the inflammatory response after ICH is essential to improving outcomes, reducing the burden on patients and caregivers, and for future development of targeted therapeutics.

In this study, we aimed to characterize CNS inflammation in ICH patients and determine whether cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytokine levels correlate with radiographic markers of inflammation and morbidity. Mouse models suggest that blood breakdown products can trigger a choroid plexus–mediated, innate immune “cytokine storm” that drives CSF dysregulation and post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus, highlighting a plausible mechanistic link between hemorrhage and ventricular pathology.5, 6 However, longitudinal human CSF data linking inflammatory signals to ventricular remodeling and outcomes after ICH remain limited.6, 7 Pro-inflammatory cytokines are released immediately after ICH, followed by a shift toward an anti-inflammatory response within three days.4, 5, 8 This evolving immune response may significantly influence both acute injury and long-term recovery. To investigate this, we analyzed a panel of cytokines in CSF and plasma samples collected from ICH patients. We compared cytokine levels between ICH patients and controls and examined their relationship with radiographic ventricular size and neurological outcome. Given the variable severity of ICH and its lasting impact, identifying specific inflammatory biomarkers could guide the development of targeted therapies and inform personalized treatment strategies aimed at improving both short- and long-term outcomes.

Methods

This prospective study with a control group included 220 patients with ICH admitted to the Yale Neurosciences Intensive Care Unit between 2014 and 2025. All participants had plasma and/or CSF samples collected as part of their enrollment in the Yale Longitudinal Study of Acute Brain Injury Biorepository. CSF samples were obtained from patients (n = 56) who required EVD placement as clinical care for management of obstructive hydrocephalus related to IVH and/or mass effect from the hemorrhage. 44 of the 56 patients had a primary ICH (etiology adjudicated to hypertension or cerebral amyloid angiopathy) and 12 had a secondary ICH (etiology adjudicated to vascular abnormality or cavernoma).

The dataset represents a convenience sample of CSF specimens collected from eligible patients between the time of presentation and up to 16 days following symptom onset. Plasma samples were collected at specified time points between days 0 and 7 per IRB protocol at the time of study initiation. ICH patients from whom CSF was obtained between the day of the bleed and 3 days post-bleed were categorized into the early sample cohort (n = 46). Those with samples collected between day 4 and day 16 post-bleed were classified into the late sample cohort (n = 34). 27 patients had both early and late CSF samples collected, but early and late cytokines were analyzed separately, as patients with more than one sample represents a minority of this convenience cohort of patients. One patient with culture-confirmed ventriculitis was excluded from the analyses.

Additionally, ventricular CSF was collected from 25 individuals undergoing ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement for the treatment of normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH), with no prior history of brain hemorrhage, which served as control CSF for our analyses. The use of NPH controls reflects the practical and ethical challenges of obtaining CSF from neurologically healthy individuals, as well as the importance of obtaining ventricular CSF for direct comparison to our EVD CSF samples.9,10

The study protocol was approved by the Yale School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (HIC#1405014045 and HIC#1506016023) and informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives.

Immunologic assays

CSF was collected in non-additive polyethylene tubes, and blood collected for plasma samples was collected in heparinized polyethylene tubes (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). Within one hour of collection, blood and CSF samples were centrifuged, the supernatant was divided into aliquots, and samples were frozen at −80°C. A panel of cytokines and chemokines including interleukin (IL) −6, granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF), IL-8, vascular endothelial growth factor-A (VEGF-A), C—C motif chemokine ligand-2 (CCL2), IL-1β, IL-4, IL-10, and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) were analyzed using Cytometric Bead Array (BD Biosciences), a multiplex bead-based immunoassay run on an LSR Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). The lower limit of detection (LLD) for each immune molecule was the concentration of the lowest standard on the linear curve for that assay, and any values below the LLD were normalized to the value of half of the LLD for all analyses (Supplemental Table 1). The panel of cytokines includes a representative sample of cytokines, chemokines, and other immune proteins involved in innate and adaptive immunity. As described below, analyses were completed only for those cytokines detectable within the CSF and plasma of patients at the measured timepoints.

Demographic and clinical data collection

Demographic, hospitalization, and outcome data were prospectively obtained through chart review. Trained study personnel (GS, LG, JY, WR, AL, TE, JMB) extracted relevant clinical and radiographic variables, including admission Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, ICH score, hemorrhage etiology, intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), and permanent shunt placement (indicating shunt dependent hydrocephalus [SDHC]). All clinical variables were adjudicated by at least two team members. Modified Rankin scale (mRS) scores were collected at discharge, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year by trained research assistants as part through planned follow up through the Yale Longitudinal Study of Acute Brain Injury; no subjects lost to follow up. The modified Graeb score (mGS), a validated CT-based measure of IVH burden that correlates with IVH volume and predicts poor outcomes, was assessed by two independent reviewers and was included in the analysis.11,12 Reviewers were blinded to cytokine data.

Subjects with CSF samples were categorized as primary (n = 44) and secondary (n = 12) ICH. Primary etiologies include hypertension and cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), while secondary etiologies include structural vascular abnormalities (arteriovenous malformation (AVM), dural arteriovenous fistula (dAVF), or moyamoya disease), and cavernoma. Diagnosis of CAA was determined by a board certified neuro-intensivist based on the Boston Criteria 2.0, 13 and hypertensive hemorrhage was defined as a deep, brainstem, or posterior fossa hemorrhage that was not due to a vascular abnormality. Secondary hemorrhages related to structural vascular abnormalities were characterized based on vessel imaging (computed tomography angiography, magnetic resonance angiography, or digital subtraction angiography) performed as part of the patient’s clinical workup, and hemorrhages due to cavernoma were characterized based on magnetic resonance imaging performed as part of the patient’s clinical workup for hemorrhage etiology. Hemorrhagic transformation of an ischemic stroke and hemorrhage related to brain tumors were excluded.

Volumetric measurements of ventricular size

Semi-automated segmentation of ventricular volumes was performed on two CT scans per patient. Imaging data were downloaded in DICOM format and analyzed using 3D Slicer software (slicer.org).14 Ventricular volumes were segmented and measured at two time points: (1) initial scan at presentation and (2) CT of the head (CTH) at the time of EVD clamping (“clamped ventricular volume”), which was used clinically to determine EVD removal or need for permanent shunt placement. During the training period, scans were reviewed by an experienced neuroradiologist (SP) to ensure accurate ventricular measurement and confirm protocol accuracy. The average of two independent measurements was used to determine initial and clamped ventricular volumes. The change in ventricular volume was calculated by subtracting the initial volume from the clamped volume, assessing changes in ventricular size over time. Ventricular imaging analyses were restricted to patients with primary ICH. Patients who died prior to EVD removal and those whose EVDs were removed in the setting of transition to comfort only were excluded from imaging analyses to avoid confounding by indication for EVD removal (n = 14). EVD management and weaning was at the discretion of the treating team, but our institutional practice is typically to maintain EVDs open to drain, gradually wean over the course of 2-4 days, and then clamp for 24-48 hours followed by a clamped CTH prior to EVD removal.

Statistical analysis

Due to non-normal distributions, all cytokine concentrations were log-transformed prior to analysis. For CSF samples, two sample T-tests were used to compare log-transformed cytokine levels between ICH patients and control subjects. Additional t-tests were performed to compare log CSF and log plasma cytokine concentrations and to assess differences between primary and secondary ICH groups. Linear regression models were used to evaluate associations between log-transformed CSF cytokine concentrations, clamped ventricular volume and change in ventricular volume. Logistic regression was used to assess the relationship between log CSF cytokine concentrations and SDHC. Ordinal logistic regression was used to evaluate the relationship between log CSF cytokine concentrations and categorical 3-month functional outcome (mRS). Multivariable regression models were used to assess associations between cytokine concentrations, ventricular volume metrics, and mRS, adjusting for relevant covariates. False discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamin Hochberg procedure was applied within predefined families of hypotheses including the cytokine–ventricular volume associations and cytokine–functional outcome associations to balance type I error control with preservation of statistical power.

In cases of missing cytokine data, samples were excluded from analyses involving only the missing analyte. As this study analyzed previously collected biospecimens, a priori sample size calculation was not conducted. All statistical analyses and data management were performed using R software (version 4.3.1).

Results

Plasma and/or CSF samples were collected from 220 ICH patients (n = 190 primary ICH, n = 30 secondary ICH) and 25 control patients. Patients had a median age of 66.5 years (interquartile range [IQR] 19-95), 43.5% were male, 71.4% were Caucasian (Table 1). For ICH patients with CSF collected (n = 56), 78.6% were primary ICH (n = 44), 82.1% of subjects had early CSF samples collected (n = 46; median [IQR] day of sample collection = post bleed day 1 [day 0 - day 3]), and 60.7% had late samples collected (n = 34; median [IQR] day of sample collection = post bleed day 5-7 [day 4 – day 16]; Table 2).

Cytokines are elevated in ICH CSF compared to control CSF and ICH plasma samples

CCL2, IL-6, IL-8, VEGF-A, and G-CSF were detectable in CSF of ICH patients (Supplemental Table 1). Compared to control CSF samples taken from NPH patients at the time of permanent shunt placement, early log concentrations of all 5 cytokines were higher in ICH patients’ CSF (p < 0.001, Fig. 1A). CSF log[CCL2], log[IL-6], log[IL-8], and log[G-CSF] were also higher than plasma concentrations of these cytokines in ICH patients (p < 0.001 for all cytokines, Fig. 1A/B). Based on prior experiments in the lab which found that VEGF-A was not present in plasma samples from ICH patients, VEGF-A was not measured in plasma samples in this study.

CSF cytokines differ between patients with primary and secondary etiology of ICH

ICH etiology was characterized as either primary (CAA, HTN) or secondary (vascular malformation, cavernoma; Table 2). When comparing log-transformed cytokine concentrations between patients based on etiology, log[VEGF-A] and log[IL-8] were found to be higher in the CSF of patients with primary ICH relative to those with secondary ICH. No differences were observed for the remaining cytokines across etiology groups (Supplemental Figure 2).

Ventricular volume is associated with CSF cytokines in primary ICH patients

The mean ± standard deviation number of days between the baseline CTH and CTH at the time of EVD clamping was 11.68 ± 5.01. Supplemental Figure 3 displays the distribution of clamped and change in ventricular volumes among patients who survived to shunt decision. Higher early CSF log[G-CSF] was associated with a decrease in ventricular volume (Fig. 2A); however, this association did not remain significant after FDR correction. Higher late CSF log[CCL2] and log [VEGF-A] samples were associated with a larger increase in ventricular volume between the presentation and the time of EVD clamping (Fig. 2B). After FDR correction, the association between late CSF log [CCL2] and increase ventricular volume change remained significant (q = 0.034), whereas the association with late CSF log[VEGF-A] was no longer significant. Early and late CSF cytokines were not associated with clamped ventricular volume (Supplemental Figure 4).

Among these cytokines, log[CCL2] and log[G-CSF] exhibited a difference between early and late CSF samples, showing a higher concentration at the earlier timepoint (p = 0.002 and p = 0.012, respectively; Supplemental Table 1).

Univariable analyses of age and sex showed no association with cytokine concentration (p > 0.05 for all analyses). Higher mGS was inversely associated with late CSF log[IL-6] and log[G-CSF] (IL-6, β = −2.98, 95% CI: −5.20 to −0.76, p = 0.013, q = 0.0496; G-CSF, β = −4.52, 95% CI: −8.17 to −0.87, p = 0.02, q = 0.0496). Given prior known associations between age, sex, intraventricular hemorrhage burden (mGS), and ventricular volume, we performed multivariable linear regression using three models.15 In Model 1 (adjusted for age and sex), late log[CCL2] remained associated with change in ventricular volume (β = 35.72, 95% CI 11.89–59.55, p = 0.008). In Model 2 (adjusted for mGS), late log[CCL2] remained associated with change in ventricular volume (β = 30.10, 95% CI 6.91–53.29, p = 0.016). In Model 3 (adjusted for age, sex, and mGS), late log[CCL2] remained associated with change in ventricular volume and late log[IL-6] showed a trend (CCL2, β =33.41, 95% CI 7.22–59.60, p = 0.019; IL-6, β = 14.16, 95% CI −0.02–28.34, p = 0.050).

Although ventricular volume at the time of EVD clamping can influence the decision to place a permanent shunt in patients with post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus,16 in this cohort, neither clamped ventricular volume nor CSF cytokine concentrations were associated with the likelihood of permanent shunt placement. This analysis is limited by the fact that only 2 patients (3.6%) in this cohort developed SDHC.

Outcome

There were no associations between early CSF concentrations of detectable cytokines and 3-month mRS. Univariate analysis showed higher late CSF log[CCL2], log[IL-8], log[IL-6], and log[G-CSF] were all independently associated with worse functional outcome at 3-month (Fig. 3). Additionally, all univariate analysis for outcome remained significant after FDR correction (q < 0.05).

As age, sex, and ICH score have been linked to functional outcome following ICH, multivariate analyses were performed to assess these potential confounders.15 In Model 1 (adjusted for age and sex), higher late log[CCL2] (β = 1.60, 95% CI 0.08–3.12, p = 0.041), log[G-CSF] (β = 0.70, 95% CI 0.09–1.31, p = 0.027), log[IL-6] (β = 0.79, 95% CI 0.41–1.18, p = 0.001), and log[IL-8] (β = 0.64, 95% CI 0.18–1.10, p = 0.010) were each associated with higher 3-month mRS. In Model 2 (adjusted for ICH score), log[IL-6] (β = 0.65, 95% CI 0.10–1.20, p = 0.024) and log[IL-8] (β = 0.75, 95% CI 0.16–1.34, p = 0.017) remained associated with higher 3-month mRS, while log[G-CSF] showed a trend towards an association (β = 0.67, 95% CI −0.04–1.38, p = 0.062). In Model 3 (adjusted for age, sex, and ICH score), log[IL-6] (β = 0.78, 95% CI 0.22–1.34, p = 0.011) and log[IL-8] (β = 0.77, 95% CI 0.23–1.30, p = 0.009) remained associated with higher 3-month mRS, with log[G-CSF] again showed a trend (β = 0.66, 95% CI −0.03–1.34, p = 0.058).

Discussion

Cytokine profiling of CSF after ICH provides insight into the evolving neuroinflammatory response. In this study, we found that cytokines including CCL2, IL-6, IL-8, and G-CSF were higher in CSF than in plasma during the first 16 days post-hemorrhage, independent of ICH etiology, reinforcing the concept of compartmentalized CNS inflammation. This observation aligns with prior findings in patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, where elevated CSF cytokine levels relative to plasma have been reported17–19 and transcriptional analyses of myeloid cells in ICH, where activation was much higher in the hematoma than peripheral blood.4 Cytokine levels in CSF from ICH patients were markedly elevated compared to controls, suggesting that this inflammation is related to the hemorrhage itself.

CCL2 is a multifunctional chemokine that mediates both monocyte migration and influences myeloid cell function.20 IL-6 is a proinflammatory cytokine extensively investigated as a marker of systemic inflammation following acute brain injury.21 Given their complex and context-dependent roles in immune modulation and disease progression, especially in inflammatory conditions, targeting CCL2 and IL-6 remains a challenging yet promising therapeutic strategy. CCL2 and IL-6 have previously been found to be associated with hematoma expansion and early neurological decline,21,22 and our data support this, as late CSF log [CCL2] and log[IL-6] were associated with worse mRS at 3 months. Additionally, late CCL2 levels correlated with greater change in ventricular volume from admission to EVD clamping, suggesting a link between delayed monocyte-driven inflammation and ventricular remodeling. These findings echo experimental studies demonstrating that monocyte-driven inflammation contributes to edema, secondary injury, and long-term dysfunction following ICH.8 Late log[IL-6] also showed an inverse correlation with mGS which argues against the idea that more cytokines are present due to a larger volume of IVH. Together with the multivariable finding that late log[CCL2] was independently associated with greater ventricular volume change, these results suggest that post-ICH ventricular remodeling is more closely linked to a subacute inflammatory signal than to IVH burden. Notably, associations between CSF cytokines and functional outcome remained robust after FDR correction, while only late log[CCL2] retained a significant relationship with ventricular volume change, suggesting a more specific link between CCL2-mediated inflammation and ventricular remodeling.

IL-8 is a neutrophil chemoattractant and pro-inflammatory and angiogenic chemokine implicated in neuroinflammatory processes.23 Prior studies in animal models have suggested a correlation between elevated IL-8 levels and increased cerebral edema in ICH.23 In our cohort, IL-8 levels differed by ICH subtype (primary vs. secondary) and higher late log[IL-8] was associated with worse mRS at 3-months, suggesting IL-8 may serve as a marker of injury severity or a mediator of delayed injury severity.

Although there is limited literature on VEGF-A and G-CSF in the CSF of ICH patients, both have demonstrated neuroprotective and angiogenic roles in experimental models.24,25 VEGF-A contributes to vascular remodeling and oxygen delivery in hypoxic environments and has been shown to support neuronal and glial cell survival.24 In animal models, G-CSF has been found to upregulate VEGF-A expression in brain and endothelial tissues, promoting post-ICH angiogenesis.25 Clinically, elevated serum levels of VEGF-A and G-CSF have been associated with improved outcomes in ischemic stroke.26 In our study, higher late log [VEGF-A] was associated with increased ventricular volume and higher early log[G-CSF] was associated with a decrease in ventricular volume between presentation and EVD clamping. Our research utilized protein VEGF-A, a major modulator of angiogenesis, and we plan to investigate VEGF-C in the future, given its role in lymphangiogenesis.27

This study has several limitations and should be considered exploratory in nature and hypothesis-generating for future studies of inflammation in ICH. CSF sampling was limited to patients requiring EVDs as part of their clinical care, thereby enriching the cohort for individuals with more severe ICH, although these severely affected patients would most benefit from novel therapeutic interventions. We did not systematically account for fever at the time of CSF sampling, which may influence inflammatory cytokine levels. Additionally, the CSF and plasma samples analyzed were part of a convenience cohort patients enrolled in our institutional biorepository, and management including EVD weaning was not standardized. The categorization of samples into early (0–3 days) and late (4–16 days) timepoints may introduce heterogeneity within the late sampling window, as cytokine profiles likely evolve over time. These timepoints were selected based on prior work demonstrating a transition in the neuroinflammatory response around day 3; however, more granular temporal analysis was limited by sample size.4 Another limitation is that our control CSF samples which were obtained from patients with NPH rather than from individuals without intracranial pathology or hemorrhage. While this provided pragmatic access to ventricular CSF, NPH may carry its own baseline neuroinflammatory signal, making it an imperfect comparator and potentially reducing specificity. Importantly, any residual inflammation in the control group would be expected to bias results toward the null, suggesting the observed elevations in hemorrhage groups likely represent conservative, rather than exaggerated, estimates of effects. While CSF from patients with NPH is not an ideal comparator, its use reflects the practical and ethical challenges of obtaining CSF from neurologically healthy individuals. Additionally, prior studies have demonstrated that ventricular and lumbar CSF differs in composition, reflecting rostrocaudal gradients in CSF constituents. This suggests that ventricular CSF may more directly reflect intracranial processes.9,10 The relatively small sample size reduced our power to detect smaller effect sizes but still allowed for identification of significant trends warranting further investigation. Notably, prior studies of CSF inflammatory markers in ICH/IVH have similar been limited by small cohort sizes, reflecting the challenges of CSF sampling in this population.8,9,10,17 Our analysis was restricted to a panel of 9 cytokines, and the sensitivity limitations of the assay may have precluded the detection of lower-abundance mediators, potentially underestimating the complexity of the inflammatory response.

Despite these limitations, our findings support the potential utility of CSF cytokine profiling in the acute and subacute phases after hemorrhagic stroke. Inflammatory mediators may contribute to post-hemorrhagic ventricular remodeling and serve as biomarkers of injury progression, informing future strategies to modulate neuroinflammation and improve recovery after ICH.
