Short-term sacral neuromodulation sustained long-term efficacy in the management of postoperative urinary retention after pelvic surgery: a case report
Authors: Wu Tang, Yuanzhi Li, Tao Yang, Tongxin Yang, Kewei Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSacral neuromodulation (SNM) serves as an effective therapeutic option for lower urinary tract dysfunction, and long-term intervention is generally required to sustain clinical benefits. Reports regarding durable therapeutic outcomes achieved with short-term SNM remain uncommon. This case indicates that short-term SNM can bring about sustained improvement of refractory non-obstructive voiding dysfunction after resection of large pelvic teratoma.Case presentationA 19-year-old female developed refractory urinary retention following excision of a huge mature cystic teratoma of the pelvis measuring 12.3 × 12.3 × 9.6 cm. Conventional conservative treatment for three months failed to relieve her symptoms. Urodynamics demonstrated reduced bladder capacity, hypersensitivity, late-filling uninhibited detrusor contractions, and a flattened voiding flow curve, with a post-void residual urine volume of 1020 mL. The patient underwent one-stage sacral neuromodulation implantation at the left S3 nerve root, with stimulation parameters set at 14 Hz, 0.6 mV and 200 μs. Spontaneous micturition returned on the first postoperative day with a single voided volume over 100 mL. During the initial six postoperative days, the average voided volume was 245 ± 76 mL, and the average daily voiding frequency was 5.5 ± 1.5 episodes. No recurrence of voiding dysfunction was observed after deactivating the stimulator. One-month urodynamic studies showed improvements in bladder capacity, though maximum flow rate remained low under study conditions. However, following stimulator deactivation, objective real-life data including a 6-day voiding diary confirmed excellent spontaneous voiding (253 mL/void, 4.5 times/day) with a post-void residual of 0 mL, showing no recurrence during the six-month follow-up.ConclusionsThis case suggests that short-term sacral neuromodulation can exert rapid and long-lasting effects on bladder dysfunction secondary to pelvic nerve injury, which may help reconstruct the micturition reflex and improve bladder function. The therapeutic effect could be well maintained after stimulation discontinuation and electrode removal, and no recurrence of dysuria was noted within the six-month follow-up period. This observation provides a novel reference for the treatment strategy and duration of SNM in managing urinary retention. Nevertheless, its definitive efficacy still requires verification through more clinical cases and prolonged follow-up.

Background

Sacral neuromodulation (SNM) represents an established therapeutic modality for lower urinary tract dysfunction. Current clinical application predominantly relies on long-term continuous or intermittent stimulation to achieve symptom control. As research progresses, SNM is being increasingly utilized in the management of both urological and gastrointestinal disorders, with novel indications under active investigation [1–4]. In the present case, short-term sacral neuromodulation effectively ameliorated urinary retention arising after pelvic surgery—a sequela likely attributable to partial pelvic nerve injury—and durable therapeutic benefit was observed; symptoms did not recur following electrode removal. Pelvic nerve injury constitutes a potential complication of pelvic surgical procedures in urology and obstetrics/gynecology. Although the incidence remains low, such injury may engender a spectrum of postoperative complications, including lower urinary tract dysfunction and lower limb impairment. Intraoperative identification and timely repair of neural injury are associated with favorable outcomes; conversely, when nerve injury is not recognized until clinical sequelae manifest postoperatively, the prognosis is considerably compromised [5, 6].

Case presentation

A 19-year-old female patient was admitted for evaluation of urinary retention that had persisted for three months following surgical resection of a massive pelvic teratoma. Three months earlier, the patient had presented to an outside hospital for a planned termination of pregnancy at 4 weeks of gestation. Ultrasonography demonstrated a small intrauterine hypoechoic focus consistent with early pregnancy, together with a mixed-consistency pelvic mass measuring approximately 12.3 × 12.3 × 9.6 cm and exhibiting predominantly cystic and solid components. After exclusion of surgical contraindications, vacuum aspiration was performed at the outside facility. A pelvic mass was palpable intraoperatively, and no voiding difficulty was noted in the immediate postoperative period. Two weeks later, the patient returned to the outside hospital. Abdominal imaging revealed a fat-containing space-occupying lesion within the extraperitoneal/subperitoneal pelvic compartment, with anterior displacement of the rectum, uterus, and bladder secondary to mass effect (Fig. 1). Laparoscopic resection of the large pelvic tumor was subsequently undertaken. Postoperative histopathology confirmed a mature cystic teratoma containing mature neural tissue elements. On the first postoperative day, the patient developed dysuria and constipation. Imaging demonstrated moderate left hydronephrosis and mild right hydronephrosis; serum creatinine remained within normal limits. Ultrasonography documented a post-void residual urine volume of 1020 mL, and an indwelling urinary catheter was placed. Following catheter removal, dysuria recurred. Oral administration of alpha-adrenergic antagonists and cholinergic agonists failed to ameliorate symptoms. Intermittent catheterization was initiated, and the patient was monitored expectantly; however, voiding dysfunction persisted. On admission, cystourethrography (Fig. 2) revealed incomplete bladder emptying with substantial residual contrast medium. Imaging studies confirmed bilateral hydronephrosis and ureteral dilatation with distention of the urinary bladder (Fig. 3). Urodynamic evaluation (Fig. 4A) showed diminished bladder capacity, and hypersensitivity; uninhibited detrusor contractions were observed at the conclusion of the filling phase, accompanied by urge incontinence. At an infused volume of 176 mL, intense urinary urgency with spontaneous leakage occurred; maximum flow rate (Qmax) was 3 mL/seconds, detrusor pressure at Qmax (PdetQmax) was 17 cmH₂O, and detrusor leak point pressure was 41 cmH₂O, indicating elevated bladder outlet resistance with a flattened voiding curve. Sacral neuromodulation was performed. Preoperative cystoscopy excluded urethral obstruction. Under fluoroscopic guidance, a six-contact sacral neuromodulation electrode was implanted at the left S3 level. Postoperative temporary stimulator settings were as follows: contacts 1 (negative) and 5 (positive); stimulation parameters: pulse width 200 μs, frequency 14 Hz, amplitude 0.6 mV. Spontaneous voiding resumed on the second postoperative day, with single voided volumes exceeding 100 mL. Over the initial six postoperative days, the mean total voided volume was 245 ± 76 mL, and the mean daily voiding frequency was 5.5 ± 1.5 times. The stimulator was deactivated for one week, during which sustained therapeutic benefit was confirmed. Following the deactivation of the stimulator, a subsequent six-day voiding diary demonstrated sustained and satisfactory spontaneous voiding, with an average voided volume of 253 ± 87 mL and a daily voiding frequency of 4.5 ± 1.1episodes. No intermittent catheterization was required. At one-month follow-up, urodynamic reassessment (Fig. 4B) revealed a pre-void residual volume of 0 mL and a maximum cystometric capacity of 305 mL; Qmax was 4 mL/seconds and PdetQmax was 28 cmH₂O. Bladder capacity and sensation were within normal limits, detrusor contraction was classified as weak. Both dysuria and constipation had improved. The patient declined second-stage implantation of a permanent sacral nerve stimulator, and the electrodes were subsequently explanted. At six-month follow-up, there had been no recurrence of voiding dysfunction.

Discussion and conclusions

Three principal etiologies currently account for postoperative urinary retention following pelvic surgery: bladder outlet or urethral obstruction; injury to or inhibition of the afferent and efferent neural pathways subserving bladder control; and impaired contractile function of the detrusor muscle. Among these, extensive pelvic procedures—such as resection of large pelvic masses and radical hysterectomy—are particularly implicated in disturbances of the micturition reflex secondary to autonomic plexus injury and diminished bladder compliance [7]. In the present case, the patient developed acute dysuria and urinary retention following resection of a pelvic teratoma. The precise underlying mechanism remains uncertain. Nevertheless, it is plausible that chronic tumor compression of pelvic structures, including the urinary bladder, together with postoperative alterations in bladder anatomical positioning and regional inflammation and edema, may contribute to urethral compression and consequent voiding difficulty. During surgery, the confined operative space and compression of adjacent organs, tissues, and nerves markedly increase the likelihood of intraoperative injury to these structures. Urinary retention attributed to urethral valves has previously been documented following teratoma surgery [8]. However, the patient's urinary retention had persisted for more than three months, and preoperative cystoscopy revealed no evidence of overt urethral stricture or angulation. Moreover, pelvic surgery—particularly resection of massive tumors—may involve the autonomic nerve fibers and pelvic plexus responsible for bladder innervation, resulting in partial denervation, diminished detrusor contractility, and impaired micturition reflexes [7, 9–11]. During resection of teratomas, partial injury to the sacral nerves may occur, thereby impairing neural transmission to the detrusor muscle. Studies have documented that following resection of sacrococcygeal teratomas in children, bladder dysfunction—including straining, urinary incontinence, enuresis, and neurogenic bladder—has been reported in 7–55% of patients [12]. Damage to the pelvic autonomic nervous system (acquired pelvic autonomic neuropathy) has been implicated in the pathogenesis of bowel and bladder dysfunction following resection of sacrococcygeal teratomas [13].

Currently, standard management of postoperative non-obstructive urinary retention includes catheterization and bladder training. Pharmacological interventions primarily consist of alpha-adrenergic antagonists and cholinergic agonists. For refractory urinary retention, modalities such as sacral neuromodulation are increasingly employed, although these typically require long-term electrode implantation. In the present case, the patient derived no symptomatic relief from intermittent catheterization and oral administration of alpha-blockers and cholinergic agonists. Given the refractory nature of the symptoms and the history of failed conservative therapy, the patient declined a trial of tibial nerve stimulation and requested sacral neuromodulation. Failure of tibial nerve stimulation in the treatment of postoperative urinary retention following resection of an anterior sacral teratoma in adults has been reported previously [14]. Sacral neuromodulation rapidly alleviated the inhibitory state of bladder voiding function, restoring autonomous bladder emptying within a short period. The therapeutic effect persisted even after stimulation was discontinued and the electrodes were explanted, thereby obviating the need for second-stage implantation and prolonged therapy. The precise mechanism of action remains unclear but may involve short-term sacral neuromodulation activating inhibited micturition and defecation reflexes and modulating neural pathways that mediate urethral and bladder sensation. It influences bladder sensory function while concurrently reducing urethral sphincter tone and facilitating bladder emptying [15–17].

Evidence indicates that sacral neuromodulation can lower the sensory threshold of the bladder without reducing that of the urethra, suggesting that its therapeutic effects may be mediated via afferent fibers within the pelvic plexus, thereby regulating detrusor and urethral sphincter function and ameliorating urinary retention [18]. The mechanism underlying the sustained efficacy of SNM following electrode removal in our case remains speculative but may involve multiple pathways. Previous studies have proposed that SNM alleviates voiding dysfunction by modulating urethral sphincter activity, regulating the balance between excitatory and inhibitory signals in the central nervous system, and influencing autonomic nervous system equilibrium [19–21]. In animal models, excessive pudendal afferent firing has been shown to trigger central inhibition of bladder activity, a process that can be reversed by activating sacral afferent pathways via SNM [22]. Furthermore, the long-term therapeutic effect observed even after device removal suggests that SNM may induce more durable biological changes beyond simple stimulation-dependent modulation, such as the restoration of detrusor contractility potentially mediated by changes in M3 receptor expression or neurotrophic facilitation of nerve regeneration [23]. In the management of postoperative urinary retention following pelvic surgery, if short-term sacral neuromodulation can restore normal micturition reflexes, improve urinary and bowel dysfunction, and sustain these benefits without necessitating long-term continuous stimulation, this approach holds potential to enhance patient experience and reduce treatment-related costs.

This case suggests that short-term sacral neuromodulation may confer rapid and substantial therapeutic benefit for bladder dysfunction attributable to pelvic nerve injury. It can promptly ameliorate symptoms of non-obstructive urinary retention resulting from neural injury and other contributing factors, restore the micturition reflex, enhance bladder compliance, and facilitate voiding. Moreover, these improvements may be maintained over the long-term following cessation of stimulation and removal of the electrodes, with no recurrence of voiding dysfunction. However, extended follow-up data are required to corroborate long-term outcomes. This case provides new insights into treatment strategies and treatment durations for sacral neuromodulation in the management of urinary retention; however, its actual efficacy requires validation through additional case studies. These observations warrant further investigation to refine treatment approaches for urinary dysfunction following pelvic surgery.
