Endoscopic Ultrasound-Guided Gallbladder Drainage for Acute Cholecystitis in Billroth II Anatomy via Afferent Limb
Authors: Neha Narayan, Orrin Wilson, Sherifatu Abu, Zachary Wilmer Reichenbach, David Lichtenstein, Malorie Simons, Nicholas McDonald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEndoscopic ultrasound-guided gallbladder drainage (EUS-GBD) is an alternative treatment of acute cholecystitis, though there is limited literature on its use in surgically altered anatomy. A 64-year-old woman with gastric adenocarcinoma and Billroth II reconstruction presented with clinical signs and symptoms of acute cholecystitis, cholestatic liver function tests, and an hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid scan supporting the diagnosis of acute calculous cholecystitis. She was deemed a nonsurgical candidate due to medical comorbidities; therefore, EUS-GBD was pursued. Our case highlights technical modifications to EUS-GBD to navigate challenges in Billroth II anatomy and supports the addition of this technique for high-risk surgical patients.

INTRODUCTION

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the standard treatment of acute cholecystitis; however, there is a subset of patients who are not candidates for surgery due to comorbidities, frailty, or altered anatomy. For nonsurgical candidates, percutaneous cholecystostomy, endoscopic ultrasound-guided gallbladder drainage (EUS-GBD), or transpapillary gallbladder drainage are options.1 Recent literature has shown that endoscopic approaches have similar efficacy to the percutaneous route with significantly fewer adverse events, lower rates of unplanned reinterventions/hospital readmissions, and improved patient comfort without the need for an external drain.2–4

EUS-GBD has emerged as a minimally invasive management option for acute cholecystitis in nonsurgical candidates, providing infection control and symptom relief.2 EUS-GBD has been shown to have a lower risk of adverse events such as bleeding, bile leak, infection, and recurrent cholecystitis in comparison with percutaneous cholecystostomy.3 Given all of this, EUS-GBD is becoming more favorable for treatment of acute cholecystitis in nonsurgical candidates.1,4–6 However, there are limited data on EUS-GBD for patients with altered foregut anatomy, including Billroth II gastrojejunostomy.7,8

In this study, we report a case of successful EUS-GBD in a patient with a prior diagnosis of gastric adenocarcinoma who underwent partial gastrectomy and Billroth II reconstruction. Our case demonstrates an endoscopic approach for managing acute cholecystitis in high-risk patients with altered foregut anatomy by using a pediatric colonoscope instead of an adult colonoscope for improved maneuverability.

CASE REPORT

A 64-year-old woman with a history of poorly differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma complicated by gastric outlet obstruction underwent subtotal gastrectomy with Billroth II reconstruction 9 months prior. Her postoperative course was complicated by a duodenal stump leak, which was managed nonoperatively. She presented with 2 weeks of poor oral intake and 3 days of progressive right upper quadrant abdominal pain associated with nausea and inability to tolerate any oral intake.

Laboratory evaluation revealed elevated direct bilirubin 3.0 mg/dL (ref range 0.0–0.2 mg/dL). Hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid scan, magnetic resonance imaging, and computed tomography showed pericholecystic stranding and gallbladder wall thickening concerning for acute cholecystitis (Figure 1).

Coronal computed tomography with contrast of the abdomen demonstrating a distended gallbladder (red arrow) with wall thickening and surrounding pericholecystic fat stranding consistent with acute cholecystitis.

Given her history of gastric adenocarcinoma, Billroth II anatomy, frailty, and concern for severe adhesive disease or “frozen abdomen,” she was deemed a nonsurgical candidate. EUS-GBD was pursued after a discussion with the patient of the treatment alternatives including percutaneous drainage.

A pediatric colonoscope was advanced to the afferent limb under fluoroscopic guidance, and a wire was left in the afferent limb. A linear echoendoscope was advanced alongside the wire into the afferent limb using fluoroscopy and wire guidance. From the afferent limb, the gallbladder was noted to have thickened walls and intraluminal debris consistent with acute cholecystitis. A 10 mm × 10 mm lumen-apposing metal stent (LAMS) was placed from the jejunum into the gallbladder, resulting in immediate return of purulent material (Figure 2). A 7 Fr × 3 cm double pigtail plastic stent was placed across the LAMS to maintain patency.

Endoscopic view from the afferent limb showing the deployed 10 mm × 10 mm lumen-apposing metal stent extending from the jejunum into the gallbladder with immediate return of purulent material confirming successful transluminal gallbladder drainage.

There was rapid clinical improvement following the procedure, and within 24 hours, the patient reported pain relief and was able to tolerate a regular diet. She was discharged home the following day without complications.

DISCUSSION

EUS-GBD has increasingly become the accepted alternative to cholecystectomy for the management of acute cholecystitis in nonsurgical candidates. Many studies demonstrate comparable to superior clinical outcomes, including patient comfort and decreased rates of reintervention for EUS-GBD in comparison to percutaneous cholecystostomy.1,3,5 Other risks of EUS-GBD include stent dislodgement, stent blockage, and peritonitis which are mitigated by placing a pigtail plastic stent.6,9

Most studies of EUS-GBD include patients with native anatomy, with only a few case reports and no clinical trials of EUS-GBD in surgically altered anatomy. Billroth II anatomy presents unique challenges in endoscopy, including accessing the afferent limb.10 In the few published reports of EUS-GBD in Billroth II anatomy, an adult colonoscope was used to access the afferent limb.7,8 We describe technical modifications to this procedure to navigate these challenges in surgically altered anatomy by using a pediatric colonoscope. The smaller diameter of the pediatric colonoscope allowed for improved maneuverability to overcome the sharp angulation into the afferent limb in the Billroth II anatomy.11 Subsequent passage of the linear echoendoscope under fluoroscopic guidance over a long stiff guidewire into the afferent limb helped achieve successful transluminal gallbladder drainage in a frail and otherwise high-risk surgical patient. There is no consensus on when to remove the coaxial pigtail plastic stent though generally this can be removed along with LAMS in 4–6 weeks.12

This case adds to the growing body of literature of EUS-GBD in patients with altered foregut anatomy and supports the addition of this technique to the armamentarium of endoscopists for high-risk surgical patients.
