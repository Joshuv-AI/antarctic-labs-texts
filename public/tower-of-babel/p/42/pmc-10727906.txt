A Case of Breast Carcinoma en Cuirasse in Male
Authors: Jin Hyeok Hyeong, Soo Hyun Jeong, Eun Joo Park, Kwang Joong Kim, Kwang Ho Kim
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 63-year-old male presented with a painful skin lesion on the left side of the neck and upper chest approximately two months prior to presentation. Diffuse erythematous to purplish-colored sclerotic patches were observed. He had been treated with intravenous antibiotics for two weeks for cellulitis, but the lesion did not improve. Punch biopsy, and neck computed tomography (CT) with contrast enhancement were performed to differentiate between cellulitis and scleroderma. Histopathological examination revealed infiltration of pleomorphic and poorly differentiated tumor cells extending into thickened collagen bundles, and mitotic activity. Based on histologic and radiologic findings, the patient was suspected to have poorly differentiated carcinoma, and further evaluation of the origin of the carcinoma was performed. A subareolar mass on the left breast was observed on chest CT, and a needle biopsy was performed; results were consistent with findings from the skin biopsy. Finally, the patient was diagnosed with carcinoma en cuirasse, a subtype of cutaneous metastasis of breast cancer, was transferred to oncology, and underwent palliative chemotherapy.Keywords: Breast cancer, Carcinoma en cuirasse

INTRODUCTION

Cutaneous metastasis is an uncommon phenomenon among malignant tumors. Breast cancer is the most common type of malignant tumor that metastases to skin, and 33% of all cases of cutaneous metastasis are breast cancer metastasis1,2. 2.5% of all breast cancer patients experience cutaneous metastasis2.

There are various types of cutaneous metastases of breast cancer. Carcinoma en cuirasse (CeC), a rare form with an incidence of approximately 6% of breast cancer cutaneous metastases, presents as nodules that coalesce and form diffuse sclerodermoid indurations involving the chest wall and abdomen3. It shows poor prognosis and its treatment is focused on palliative therapy3.

Less than 1% of all patients with breast cancer are male4. Because of its low incidence, diagnosis is often delayed and it leads to poor prognosis. Diagnosis is even more challenging if the initial sign is a skin lesion, not a breast palpable mass. Herein, we report a case of breast CeC in a male patient whose initial complaint was a skin lesion.

CASE REPORT

A 63-year-old male presented with a painful skin lesion on the left side of the neck and upper chest approximately two months prior to consultation. Diffuse erythematous to purplish-colored sclerotic patches were observed (Fig. 1A). The patient did not have any underlying diseases. He was initially diagnosed with an insect bite and treated with intravenous antibiotics for two weeks, but the lesion did not improve. There were no specific laboratory findings suggestive of cellulitis. He was suspected to have actinomycosis and bacterial cellulitis based on neck computed tomography (CT) findings (Fig. 1B). Therefore, he was started on intravenous ceftriaxone and oral doxycycline therapy, and skin punch biopsy was performed thereafter.

(A) Clinical presentation. Diffuse erythematous to purplish-colored sclerotic patch was observed on the left neck. Punch biopsy was taken in purplish sclerotic patch (arrow). (B) Computed tomography image. Diffuse extensive soft tissue thickening in entire left lateral neck, supraclavicular area, and chest wall was observed.

Histopathological examination revealed infiltration of hyperchromatic and pleomorphic atypical cells extending into thickened collagen bundles, and the cells were observed in single rows following a linear pattern (Fig. 2). Acid-fast bacteria staining and gram staining for detecting infection were both negative. Immunohistochemistry was positive for cytokeratin (CK) and negative for leukocyte common antigen, CD30, and S100. Based on histological findings and positive result for CK, the patient was suspected to have poorly differentiated carcinoma, and further evaluation of the origin of the carcinoma was performed.

Histopathologic findings of neck skin. Infiltration of hyperchromatic and pleomorphic atypical cells extending into thickened collagen bundles were observed, and the cells were seen as single rows in a linear pattern (A: H&E, ×100; B: H&E, ×400). Immunohistochemistry was positive for cytokeratin (C: ×100).

There were no specific findings for primary cancer on gastroscopy, colonoscopy, or laryngoscopy. Chest and abdominal CT scans revealed a subareolar mass on the left breast, which was suspected to be bone metastasis. Needle biopsy was performed on this subareolar mass. Histopathology showed atypical cells with a linear pattern and tumor emboli in the lymphatic vessels (Fig. 3), consistent with findings from the skin biopsy. Immunohistochemistry results were positive for human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and negative for estrogen and progesterone receptors. However, the fluorescence in situ hybridization result for HER2 gene was negative, indicating the carcinoma as a triple negative breast cancer. Immunohistochemistry of HER2 and hormone receptors was also performed on the skin biopsy specimen, which was found to be the same as that of the breast specimen (Fig. 3). Therefore, we diagnosed the breast lesion as the primary tumor source.

Histopathologic findings of subareolar mass (A, B) and neck skin (C). Tumor emboli in lymphatic vessel and atypical cells in a linear pattern were observed (A: H&E, ×400). Immunohistochemistry results were positive for human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) in subareolar mass and neck skin, both (B, C: HER2, ×100).

Finally, the patient was diagnosed with CeC, a subtype of cutaneous metastasis of breast cancer. He was transferred to oncology and underwent palliative chemotherapy with paclitaxel and cisplatin. We received the patient’s consent form about publishing all photographic materials.

DISCUSSION

Breast cancer is a common cancer that metastasizes to the skin, and accounts for 33% of all cutaneous metastases2. It is commonly detected after the diagnosis of primary cancer; furthermore, skin lesions are rarely the initial presenting sign of cancer. This is the reason for delayed diagnosis and treatment that results in a poorer prognosis when the skin lesion is the initial sign of cancer. As less than 1% of all breast cancer patients are men, it is easily misdiagnosed as other skin diseases when it occurs in their sex4.

Cutaneous metastases of breast cancer are divided into lymphatic and hematogenous types. CeC, as in our case, is a rare subtype of lymphatic dissemination with an incidence of approximately 6% of breast cancer cutaneous metastases named by anatomist Alfred Velpeau3. In most cases, it develops during the post-mastectomy state or while on radiotherapy after diagnosis5. Clinically, CeC shows diffuse morphea-like induration in the chest wall and abdomen. In histopathological findings, tumor cells are often seen in an “Indian-filing” pattern, with cells lined up in single rows within collagen bundles of the fibrotic dermis3. In immunohistochemistry findings, most of male breast cancer are hormone receptor positive and HER2 negative, which makes tamoxifen (selective estrogen receptor modulator) as a treatment option4. Its pathogenesis is still unclear, but it is speculated that pleiotrophin, an extracellular signaling molecule, acts as a multifunctional tumor promoter by inducing tumor angiogenesis and activating stromal fibroblasts6.

In our case, the patient was initially misdiagnosed with cellulitis, received antibiotic therapy, and was correctly diagnosed a few weeks after skin biopsy. This reveals that if CeC appears as the initial sign of primary cancer, it may easily be misdiagnosed as cellulitis or other skin diseases. In particular, it was difficult to make a correct diagnosis because the patient was male. Only a few cases of cutaneous metastasis of male breast cancer have been reported to date (Table 1)6,7,8,9,10. In immunohistochemistry findings, the patient was diagnosed as a triple negative breast cancer, which is different from most of male breast cancer. This is why tamoxifen or trastuzumab (monoclonal antibody binding HER2 receptor) were not used as a treatment option for the patient.

In conclusion, clinicians should consider skin biopsy if morphea-like induration in the chest wall and abdomen does not improve after antibiotic therapy. In addition, if a malignancy is detected in the biopsy, it would be better to identify the primary malignancy suspicious for breast cancer to save the patient’s time and expenses.
