Optimized spatial planning offers a dual solution for managing urban heat and air pollution in humid subtropical climates
Authors: Liuhua Zhu, Fan Wang, Chris P. Nielsen, Gregory R. Carmichael, Meng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As cities expand, urban heat islands and air pollution have merged, especially in humid subtropical cities. Yet a dual solution remains lacking to simultaneously mitigate these two issues due to complex meteorology-chemistry interactions. Based on numerical simulations and a multi-objective optimization algorithm, we develop an optimized spatial planning that considers the intricate interplay between urban heat and pollution. Optimized development prioritizes urban locations upwind of the summer monsoon, and suggests developing medium-density urban (where the built-up fraction ranges from 0.5 to 0.8), as well as increasing urban green space fractions from 0.15 to 0.27. Such an urban layout yields the greatest reductions in urban warming and pollution. Both regression analysis and numerical simulations confirm the effectiveness of the framework. Enhanced ventilation improves air quality, while strong southerly winds mitigate urban heat. This study underscores the necessity of addressing urban heat and air pollution simultaneously.

Introduction

Urbanization is an inevitable part of societal progress in China. During this process, natural vegetation is replaced with impervious surfaces, which decreases surface albedo and increases solar energy absorption1. These changes lead to higher temperatures in urban areas that can exacerbate heat-related illness and affect local ecosystems2–5. In parallel, human activities in urban areas contribute over 70% of greenhouse gas emissions6 and generate the majority of air pollutants7,8, resulting in deteriorated air quality.

Urbanization intensifies temperature differences between urban and rural areas, resulting in the urban heat island (UHI) and degrading the urban thermal environment9,10. Although clean air actions in China since 2013 have effectively reduced emissions11, haze and ozone pollution continue to pose major challenges for urban air quality12–14. As primary emissions have declined, secondary organic aerosol (SOA) has become increasingly important in particulate matter formation13. Many urban areas are Volatile Organic Compounds (VOC)-limited for ozone formation during warmer months, meaning that changes in VOCs concentrations strongly impact ozone levels15. Elevated temperatures over urban areas enhance biogenic and anthropogenic emissions of VOCs and accelerate chemical reaction rates, often leading to higher ozone and SOA16–18. Consequently, compound heat and air pollution events are increasingly observed in cities19–22, presenting substantial health risks and economic burdens23,24.

To enhance urban resilience, cities have implemented various green (vegetation-based), blue (water-based), and grey (engineered) infrastructures (GBGI)25–29. These measures have proven effective in mitigating urban warming30–33 or improving air quality34–36 to varying degrees. However, they are typically applied to existing urban areas and often require substantial financial investment. An alternative strategy is to optimize future urban expansion to achieve long-term balanced development37,38. By refining urban land use locations39–41, building density42,43, location of green spaces44–47, and other factors, urban planners can minimize adverse effects while maximizing social and environmental benefits.

Most studies on optimizing urban layout for sustainable development consider either urban heat48–50 or air pollution43,51 separately, neglecting their coupled processes. Recent work demonstrates two-way feedback between urban heat and air quality52,53. Urban heat affects air quality through changes in atmospheric dynamics and chemistry. Warming strengthens atmospheric turbulence and reshapes urban–rural circulations, altering pollutant transport, mixing, and deposition, whereas cooler conditions stabilize the boundary layer and limit dispersion54–58. Elevated temperatures also increase emissions of gaseous precursors and accelerate photochemical reactions, enhancing ozone formation and degrading air quality17,59,60. Air pollutants in the boundary layer, especially aerosols, alter the surface energy balance through their radiative effects61,62. By scattering and absorbing incoming solar radiation, particulate matter reduces shortwave radiation reaching the surface, lowering surface temperatures63–66, yet absorption warms the lower atmospheres67–69. Therefore, efforts to mitigate urban heat and air quality issues simultaneously can lead to synergies or trade-offs, and actions focused on one issue may unintentionally worsen the other.

In this study, we develop a multi-objective optimization framework for urban development in the Great Bay Area (GBA), one of the most densely populated and developed urban clusters in China. This framework integrates urban thermal comfort and improved air quality. Recognizing that urban green spaces can enhance thermal comfort while degrading air quality17,18,70, we also incorporate green space allocation within the proposed framework. We employ geographically weighted regression (GWR) to link land use decisions to objective functions71–74. Multiple objectives are solved using the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm III (NSGA-III)75. We further advance the framework by evaluating candidate plans using a climate and chemistry coupled model (WRF–GC)76,77, which enables two-way meteorology–chemistry feedbacks and provides physically grounded mechanistic interpretation. By applying the proposed optimization framework during a compound heatwave and ozone episode, we demonstrate that co-mitigation of urban heat and air pollution is achievable, despite conflicting geophysical mechanisms. The framework has the potential to improve living conditions, decrease energy consumption, and reduce health risks in global urban areas.

Results

Robustness of estimated relationships between land use types and environmental variables

Robust relationships between environmental variables and land use types are crucial for constructing objective functions in optimization. The environmental variables considered include 2-meter air temperature (T2), fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentrations, and ground-level ozone (O3) concentrations. We first grouped the detailed land use types used in the urban climate-chemistry model into eight categories, namely tree, shrub, grass, wetland, crop, urban, barren, and water (Supplementary Table 1). The particulars for developing estimated relationships can be found in the Methods section. Evaluation metrics, including the coefficient of determination (R2), mean absolute error (MAE), and root square mean error (RMSE) were used. As shown in Fig. 1, GWR models demonstrate reliable predictability due to their consideration of spatial variability, with all models exhibiting mean biases smaller than 0.5%. The T2 and land use relationship exhibits good fitting, with an R2 value of 0.80, comparable to the findings of Duan et al.74. (R2 = 0.73). Although there is an overestimation of more than 1.5 K (Kelvin) for T2 values smaller than 298 K, these deviations are rare (~ 1.63%) and exert negligible impacts on subsequent analyses. PM2.5 and ozone predictions display superior performances, with R2 = 0.82 and R2 = 0.84, respectively, outperforming the models proposed by Huang et al.78. (R2 = 0.72 for PM2.5 and R2 = 0.65 for ozone). Major features of optimized urban plans

We carried out spatial optimization with multi-objectives based on an assumption of 10% urban development (details in the Methods section). We then generated competitive urban plans through optimization (details are listed in Supplementary Note 1). These urban plans are all considered optimal on the Pareto front, meaning here that no single urban plan outperforms the others across all objective functions (Supplementary Fig. 1). This flexibility empowers decision-makers to customize their selections to specific requirements. Figure 2 delineates the spatial characteristics of optimized versus unplanned urban development. Figure 2A and B show the frequency among all urban plans that a given grid cell is developed as urban. Unplanned urban expansion exhibits a dispersed pattern across the area, with grid cells sharing similar probabilities for development. Conversely, optimal urban plans demonstrate an uneven likelihood of urbanization, with higher development potential in the western of the GBA urban clusters and more centrally along the coastline (as shown in red rectangles in Fig.2B), which corresponds to the upwind areas of the summer monsoon. Urban density, representing the average built-up (impervious) area fraction in an “urban” land use type grid cell, was categorized into low (<0.5), medium (0.5–0.8), and high (>0.8) density following the WRF-Noah urban canopy model. The average urban density for unplanned and optimized urban development is 0.77 and 0.64, respectively. Optimized urban land use plans favor medium-density urban areas, making up 75.68% of newly developed urban grid cells. In contrast, unplanned urban development tends to produce medium and high-density areas equally, accounting for 52.74% and 46.04%, respectively. High-density urban cells are predominantly covered by roofs and paved areas. A shift from high-density urban to medium-density urban indicates a decrease of impervious spaces, which can lead to better ventilation and reduced heat retention within the urban environment. We averaged green space fractions across all newly developed urban grid cells. Figure 2E displays that unplanned urban development exhibits low vegetation cover with a 0.15 areal share, whereas optimized urban development plans exhibit more green spaces with a share of 0.27 (Fig.2F). The optimized plans promote a shift from impervious surfaces to more contiguous green spaces, thereby enhancing shading and evapotranspiration, improving thermal comfort, and increasing stormwater retention. Urban heat and air quality benefits of the optimized urban plans

We used regression to evaluate the effectiveness of the urban development optimization framework, which was established by analyzing the relationships between environmental factors and land use patterns. As shown in Fig. 3, unplanned urban development leads to a 0.04 K rise in T2 over the whole domain, whereas optimized urban development yields a 0.01 K increase. Although the 0.03 ± 0.001 K reduction from optimization is small, it is statistically significant at the 95% significant level based on a t-test analysis. Because only 1.10% of grid cells were changed in the 10% urban development scenario, the domain-mean signal is diluted by the large share of unchanged areas. Over the changed urban cells, average mitigated warming is 0.20 K, with maximum local cooling of 1.42 K. Kang et al.79 indicated that each 1 K rise in urban temperature is linked to a 6% increase in cardiovascular risk and a 7% increase in coronary heart disease risk. Given GBA’s dense and aging population, the 0.2 K cooling could lead to measurable health benefits. Unplanned urban development is associated with increased PM2.5 levels and ozone concentrations, reflecting that higher anthropogenic emissions is conducive to pollutant formation over urban areas. In the optimized cases, both regional means and changed urban area means show less degraded or even better air quality, indicating that strategic siting can reduce exposure in the areas where development occurs. Simple regressions between land use and environmental variables seldom incorporate realistic feedback between meteorology and pollutants. Therefore, we performed the simulations based on the WRF-GC model to further assess the effectiveness of optimization strategies. Given that simulations for all generated urban land use plans are resource-intensive, we selected ten representative urban plans using the ranking of weighted scores from normalized objective function values (see methods). Five of these urban plans were derived from unplanned urban development scenarios, while the other five were taken from optimized scenarios. The objective function values distribution of these ten urban plans is summarized in Supplementary Fig. 2. The distribution of newly developed urban grid cells and urban green spaces fraction in each simulated urban plan is shown in Supplementary Fig.3 and Supplementary Fig. 4. The selected urban plans can present the general characteristics of optimal urban plans.

Model simulations indicate that optimized land use plans consistently outperform scenarios of unplanned urban expansion across all environmental objectives (Fig. 4). Unplanned development leads to increases in ozone concentrations across the entire domain and within newly developed urban areas by more than 2ppb, whereas optimized plans suppress O3 at both the regional scale (−1.60 ppb) and within urban areas (−2.67 ppb) (Fig.4A). For PM2.5, both scenarios result in substantial reductions; however, the optimized plans deliver greater benefits, reducing PM2.5 concentrations by 20.80% in the changed urban cells. To investigate the drivers behind these improvements, we focused on two principal urban development areas identified in the optimized plans (red rectangles in Fig. 2B). In Location 2, optimization strengthens coastal near-surface winds and increases planetary boundary layer height (PBLH), while in Location 1, both wind speed and PBLH are enhanced (Supplementary Fig. 5). These changes collectively improve urban ventilation through more effective horizontal dispersion and enhanced vertical mixing. Consistent with this mechanism, vertical profiles reveal pronounced decreases in PM2.5 and O3 throughout the 0–5 km layer after optimization (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating that enhanced mixing and stronger dispersion clean the entire column, rather than merely redistributing pollutants near the surface. Near-surface air temperature increases under both scenarios; however, the optimized plans consistently moderate the warming relative to unplanned expansion (Fig.4B). The spatial pattern of T2 under the optimized plans indicates that cooling is concentrated along the coastline (Supplementary Fig. 7), coinciding with the areas where urban development is preferentially allocated. Discussion

The expansion of urban areas has led to significant environmental challenges that require urgent mitigation efforts. In this study, we introduced a viable method for optimizing urban development with a focus on environmentally conscious planning. By acknowledging the conflicts between urban warming and the deterioration of air quality, our proposed framework integrates four trade-off objectives within the optimization process. We utilized the NSGA-III algorithm to produce a diverse array of urban plans for stakeholders to select. When applied to a case study in the GBA, this approach revealed several important insights. Both regression analyses and numerical modeling simulations highlight the effectiveness of optimized urban development, with benefits extending beyond urban areas into the surrounding areas. The simulations attribute air quality improvements primarily to enhanced ventilation, and heat mitigation to southward summer monsoon flows. By incorporating environmental considerations into urban development optimization, it is possible to co-mitigate urban heat and air pollution, both of which are associated with dense population and rising energy consumption in urban areas.

Our optimization framework determined the locations and the fractions of built-up and green spaces in modified urban grid cells on a regional scale. Although the spatial resolution is coarser than that used in typical land use optimization studies at the street or block level38,45,48,80, regional scale optimization more effectively captures atmospheric circulation, pollutant transportation, boundary layer evolution, and meteorology-chemistry interactions. In China, urbanization is characterized by clusters of cities81, and its impacts extend beyond local urban cores into the surrounding regions. It is therefore crucial to understand the regional impacts of urbanization and to find effective ways to mitigate its negative effects. Performing urban land use optimization at this larger scale not only highlights the regional impacts of urbanization but also enhances our mechanistic understanding of how optimized land use configurations can achieve co-mitigation benefits.

Green roof and cool roof are widely used strategies in megacities to mitigate UHI, particularly during the summer time. We implemented WRF-GC-based simulations to evaluate these two retrofits against the proposed optimized planning strategy, assuming a 10% urban expansion relative to the current quo (see Methods). Although green roofs and cool roofs result in a greater decrease in near-surface temperatures compared to the optimization strategy, they also degrade air quality, leading to increased concentrations of ozone and PM2.5 (Supplementary Fig. 9 and Supplementary Table 2). Green roofs emit additional Biogenic Volatile Organic Compounds (BVOC) emissions, which promote the formation of SOA18. Cool roofs lower the surface temperature and boundary layer height, thereby inhibiting the dispersion of PM2.5 and ozone56. This comparison highlights that optimized urban development offers a different perspective to achieve co-benefits of thermal comfort and improved air quality without incurring substantial retrofit costs.

Despite these advances, several limitations remain and should be acknowledged. First, in the WRF urban canopy model, the fraction of urban green spaces is prescribed uniformly across all urban grid cells. This configuration does not account for the spatial heterogeneity introduced by the optimized plans, nor does it capture the enhanced evapotranspiration associated with increased greenery, which may underestimate the reduction in T2 under the optimized urban development scenarios18. Future work should incorporate spatially varying urban vegetation parameters, as well as explicit representations of urban trees and irrigated surfaces, to improve the realism of thermal response. Second, the WRF-GC modeling system involves uncertainties from multiple sources, including initial and boundary conditions, emission inventories, chemical mechanisms, physical parameterizations, and numerical schemes. Due to computational constraints, it is not feasible to conduct a systematic uncertainty analysis, such as large ensembles or comprehensive sensitivity experiments, which limits our ability to robustly quantify the uncertainty of our results. Future research should aim to further explore numerical modeling techniques to improve the accuracy of models and emissions estimates based on altered land use patterns. The optimization results are influenced by several factors, including local meteorological characteristics, air pollutant transport pathways, and the regional atmospheric circulation background. Consequently, when implementing the proposed method in other cities, it is essential to carefully evaluate and account for these site-specific variables in order to ensure the effectiveness and applicability of the approach.

Methods

Coupled urban climate-chemistry modelling

We used WRF-GC v2.076 to perform the urban climate and air quality simulations. WRF-GC is an online coupling of the Weather and Research and Forecasting meteorological (WRF) model and the GEOS-Chem atmospheric chemistry model. Previous studies have demonstrated its satisfactory performance against observations regarding the magnitudes and spatial patterns of air pollutants and meteorology variables76,77,82–84. Supplementary Fig. 10 shows the characteristics of meteorological and air pollution variables during the selected event. We configured 3 domains with grid resolutions of 27 km, 9 km, and 3 km, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 11). The 3 km inner domain is convection-permitting, allowing for explicit resolution of key urban and coastal circulations in the GBA, while maintaining computational feasibility for simulations across all ten scenarios. The primary parameterization options used for physical schemes and domain settings are listed in Supplementary Table 3. The meteorological initial and boundary conditions were obtained from the National Center for Environmental Prediction Final Operational Global Analysis (NCEP FNL, 1° × 1°) with 6-hour intervals. The chemical boundary and initial conditions were taken from a standard full-chemistry simulation from GEOS-Chem v12.8.1. The monthly mean anthropogenic emissions covering China with 0.25° × 0.25° grid resolution in 2020 were obtained from the Multi-resolution Emission Inventory for China (MEIC)85. Anthropogenic emissions for the areas outside China were obtained from a mosaic Asian anthropogenic emission inventory86,87. Validation against observations indicates that, although biases exist, the accuracy is comparable to that reported in other studies59,76,88. Further details regarding the validation are provided in Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Fig. 12, and Supplementary Tables 4 and 5.

Multi-objective optimization framework for urban land use development

Supplementary Fig. 13 outlines the structure of multi-objective optimization for urban development. Initially, we identified the key objectives and represented them with mathematical functions. Subsequently, optimization was conducted based on NSGA-III to yield anticipated results. The effectiveness of optimization was evaluated through regression analysis and numerical simulations.

Our primary motivation is to promote sustainable urban development by simultaneously mitigating urban warming and enhancing air quality. Urban heat is represented by T2, which is an important indicator related to human health89 and a widely used variable for evaluating urban heat adaptation measures32. Urban air quality is characterized by hourly concentrations of PM2.5 and the maximum daily 8-hour average ozone (MDA8 O3). We adopted MDA8 O3 rather than the daily mean to capture sustained periods of high exposure, as O3 exhibits a strong diurnal cycle driven by photochemical processes, with pronounced peaks in the afternoon. The 2019 Outline Development Plan for the GBA advances an “ecological civilization” and calls for strict protection of key ecological spaces90. To promote resilience, we incorporated the maximization of ecosystem services value (ESV) as one of the objective functions38. According to the China Statistical Yearbook for 2012 to 2023, the built-up urban area in Guangdong province increased by about 7% from 15984.1 to 17126.6 square kilometers92,93. We therefore selected a 10% urban expansion scenario to reflect a plausible and policy-relevant decadal change. We also evaluated the urban expansion ratios of 5% and 15%, and obtained results similar to those of the 10% scenario (Supplementary Fig. 14). For consistency across experiments, all data, including land use, land use fractions, meteorological data, and air quality variables, were extracted from the simulations of the innermost domain (Supplementary Fig. 8). This domain comprises 1105 urban-type grid cells, each with a horizontal spatial resolution of 3 km x 3 km. Notably, a cell is classified as urban if at least one-third of its area has urban land use, while the remainder can have other land uses such as water bodies, forests, shrubs, and crops. Several constraints were applied to ensure that the selection of newly developed urban cells is consistent with regulatory and geographical realities in the region: (1) Urban development should not encroach upon croplands or water bodies, in order to protect these vital resources; (2) The elevation for new urban cells must not exceed 95 m, which corresponds to the 95th percentile of current urban elevations; (3) Selected urban cells should have water on no more than 50% of their borders to exclude isolated islands.

Application of these constraints yielded 1164 grid cells being identified as candidate developed urban sites. Then the optimization issue becomes determining which grid cells should be developed, as well as how to allocate the urban fraction and green spaces fraction within each selected cell to achieve optimal co-mitigation of urban warming and degraded air quality. The distribution of potential urban grid cells and urban land use grid cells is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 15.

Considering that highly urbanized areas in the GBA are densely concentrated around the Pearl River estuary, we imposed a compactness45 constraint on each candidate urban plan. Compactness quantifies the similarity among neighboring land uses. We calculated local compactness by counting the number of urban cells within the 3×3 neighborhood surrounding each focal urban cell. Based on the landcover dataset from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS)94, we identified a positive trend in compactness of 0.016 per year in the GBA from 2001 to 2024, indicating progressively more compact urban development (Supplementary Fig. 16). Accordingly, we set threshold for the mean urban compactness at 5.4 for each urban plan, corresponding to the average value observed over the most recent five years. This constraint ensures alignment with the contemporary urban growth pattern in this region.

Given the potential trade-offs among different objectives, no singular optimal solution is attainable. We therefore employed the widely used Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm (NSGA-III)95–97 to generate a diverse set of Pareto-optimal urban plans for decision-makers to consider. NSGA-III is a state-of-the-art multi-objective evolutionary algorithm specifically designed to address problems involving more than three objectives. A reference point-based scheme98 is introduced in this algorithm to maintain solution diversity in high-dimensional objective spaces by guiding the search and preserving a well-distributed set of solutions along the Pareto front. Detailed technical particulars for NSGA-III are provided in Supplementary Note 1.

To ensure precision in the regression relationships, we also employed the Least Squares method and Random Forest regression for comparison with the GWR method. The results indicated that GWR provided superior accuracy (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). Although T2 and PM2.5 variations show diurnal patterns37, our analysis of GWR regression coefficients for different land use types during the day and night revealed consistent characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 19). Therefore, we chose not to include diurnal variations in the T2 and PM2.5 regression models to simplify the number of objective functions.

Assessment of spatially-optimized planning strategies

Spatially optimized urban planning strategies were evaluated by integrating the estimated relationships with the WRF-GC model. To assess the estimates, environmental variables were calculated for both optimized and unplanned urban development scenarios.

Given the computational constraints of running the WRF-GC model for all urban plans, we simulated ten representative cases: five from the unplanned set and five from the optimized set. To identify these cases, we normalized each objective function value to a scale from 0 to 1 within each urban plan set. We then calculated a composite score for each urban plan as the weighted sum of the normalized values for the four objective functions. Weights for T2, PM2.5, O3, and ESV-related objective functions were assigned as 0.3,0.3,0.3 and 0.1, respectively. We prioritized T2, PM2.5, and O3 to emphasize co-benefits for urban heat mitigation and urban air quality improvement. For each set, plans were selected at the minimum score, the 25th percentile, the median, the 75th percentile, and the maximum score. These five plans cover both the extremes and bulk of the normalized objective function value distribution, yielding a set of scenarios that are representative across the full performance spectrum defined by the weights. This approach ensures coverage of diverse performance regimes, while accurately reflecting the range of outcomes prioritized by the weights. Supplementary Fig. 2 presents the distribution of objective function values from selected plans.

Updated land use, land use fraction, leaf area index, and vegetation type fraction data were obtained to align with expanded urban land use scenarios before being integrated into the model. Adjustments to anthropogenic emissions were also necessary in response to changes in land use, given the distinctive characteristics of various land types. Emission reallocations were implemented following the procedure detailed in Supplementary Note 3. The total emissions after adjustment were consistent with those during the case study. Supplementary Fig. 20 illustrates the differences in PM2.5 emissions for one of the optimized urban plan sets. Increased PM2.5 emissions from power, industry, residential, and transport can be seen in the newly developed urban areas.

All model physics, chemistry, and boundary conditions were kept identical across the simulations of the selected urban plans, with only land use inputs and the corresponding anthropogenic emissions varied. We specifically focused on differences between the unplanned and optimized scenarios; thus, systematic biases are minimized under this experiment design, allowing us to attribute scenario differences primarily to optimization. Therefore, model uncertainties may not significantly affect the results of this study83.

We employed the WRF–GC modeling system to quantify the impacts of an optimized urban expansion strategy relative to two widely implemented retrofit measures, namely green roof and cool roof, on urban heat and air quality. For comparability, the urban development plan for the retrofit scenarios was from the initial plan set as the member at the 25th percentile of the objective function ranking, and the optimized strategy was analogously selected as the 25th percentile within the ensemble of optimized plans. For the green roof scenario, we enabled the green roof option and set the green roof fraction to 0.5 in urban grid cells. In addition, BVOC emissions from green roofs were calculated using the Model of Emissions of Gases and Aerosols from Nature (MEGAN). In this way, both the evapotranspiration effect and BVOC emissions from green roofs were taken into account. For the cool roof scenario, 50% of urban roofs were assigned a high-albedo material (albedo = 0.8), while the remaining 50% retained the baseline albedo of 0.2, resulting in an effective roof albedo of 0.5 at the urban grid-cell level. All other model configurations (including domain, period, physics, chemistry, boundary conditions, land use, and anthropogenic emissions) were held constant across all scenarios to ensure that inter-scenario differences reflect only the specified modifications.
