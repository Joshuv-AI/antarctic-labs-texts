Virtual reality to characterize anticipation skills of top‐level 4 x 100 m relay athletes
Authors: Loïc Chomienne, Martin Egiziano, Laurine Stefanuto, Martin Bossard, Eulalie Verhulst, Richard Kulpa, Nicolas Mascret, Gilles Montagne
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne marker of expertise in sport is athletes' ability to anticipate future events. In the 4 × 100 m relay, these anticipation skills are an essential asset for initiating their run at the right time. However, no study has focused on describing the underlying perceptual‐motor processes involved. Virtual reality provides powerful tools to describe and understand these processes, overcoming the drastic constraints encountered in the real world. Nineteen athletes from the French national teams were immersed in a digital replica of the Stade de France and confronted with digital twins of potential partners based on motion capture. Their task was to initiate their run exactly when their virtual partner passed over a go‐mark placed on the ground. The timing of different body motor events and visual behavior were measured and analyzed. Results showed that the execution of this highly constrained task is the result of a significant reduction in the variability of motor events preceding the start. These findings reveal the implementation of a perceptual‐motor dialog until the initiation of the run. This study is a first step toward understanding the mechanisms underlying anticipation skills in the 4 × 100 m relay; it constitutes a preliminary step to the deployment of VR training protocols.

INTRODUCTION

Performance in the 4 × 100 m athletics relay depends on athletes' ability to maximize baton speed over the entire lap of the track. The running speed of each athlete is physiologically determined, but their ability to optimize the transmission of the baton by minimizing speed loss is just as important. Highly developed anticipation skills are required to accurately assess the partner's approach and produce a start that enables the baton to be passed under ideal conditions. Before going any further, a precise description of the task constraints involved in the passing of the baton is necessary. For the athlete who receives the baton, the transmission phase consists of initiating his/her run when the giver passes vertically above a white mark placed on the ground, in order to be able to receive the baton at full speed before the end of the transmission zone. The location of the mark depends on the acceleration capabilities of the athlete receiving the baton and the assumed speed of the athlete transmitting the baton to optimize the exchange of speed (Boyadjian & Bootsma, 1999).

Numerous studies have focused on analyzing coordination between athletes through mathematical modeling (Hsu, 2014; Masedu & Angelozzi, 2008; Ward‐Smith & Radford, 2002; Zarębska et al., 2021). To the best of our knowledge, only one study has investigated the perceptual‐motor processes involved in the 4 × 100 m relay (Boyadjian & Bootsma, 1999). In this study, the authors showed that athletes practicing at a regional level were able to adjust the timing of run initiation and their acceleration according to the partner's approach speed. However, despite the important information it provided, not only did this study not focus on an elite athlete population, but also the level of analysis chosen did not make it possible to explore underlying processes. Conversely, the present study made this possible via a precise chronological analysis of the different steps which precede the initiation of the race.

To our knowledge, no study has focused on the characterization of the initiation of the run among top‐level athletes during the transmission of the baton. Preliminary studies carried out with athletes from the French 4 × 100 m relay teams made it possible to identify a certain number of events occurring systematically before rear foot lift (Stefanuto et al., 2023). As an example, the initiation of the run (which is identified by the coach when the rear foot is lifted) is systematically preceded by a preparatory movement called the wave by coaches (lowering of the center of mass in a back‐and‐forth movement), followed by a gaze release corresponding to the last moment when the athlete can collect information about his partner. A precise analysis of the chronology (mean and variability) of these three events (i.e., wave initiation, gaze release, and foot lift) with reference to the athlete's passing over the mark would be worthwhile.

This study was carried out as part of a project intended to use virtual reality tools to characterize and optimize the anticipation skills of athletes of the French 4 × 100 m relay teams with a view to the 2024 Olympic Games (Montagne et al., 2024). The use of virtual reality technologies to carry out this study proved necessary for various reasons. First of all, virtual reality makes it possible to overcome several limitations linked to the inability of athletes to repeat runs at maximum speed but also to the difficulties of precisely recording athletes' behavior in the field. More importantly, the use of virtual reality allows precise control of the context (approach speed of the athlete, position of the mark, presence or absence of competitors, etc.) in which the task is carried out, which facilitates study of the underlying anticipation processes. For this purpose, 19 athletes from the French national teams (both men and women) were placed in a digital replica of the Stade de France through a Head‐Mounted Display (HMD) and were asked to initiate their run as precisely as possible, while digital twins of their partner were approaching. Using motion capture sensors, the time of occurrence of the three events (wave initiation, gaze release, and foot lift) was extracted together with gaze behavior with a view to characterizing the anticipation behavior implemented as precisely as possible. To gain an understanding of this task, which, to the best of our knowledge, has never been studied at this level of detail, a global analysis was first carried out. Moreover, because individualization is essential among experts (Morris‐Binelli et al., 2021; Müller et al., 2023), individual analysis was also carried out through linear regressions.

We may wonder to what extent the timing of these three events is part of an extremely rigid pre‐established routine (cf., the operational timing concept proposed by Tyldesley & Whiting, 1975). In this case, the timing of wave initiation should condition the timing of foot lift and the signature of the expertise would lie in the minimization of the variability of wave initiation. However, as suggested by more recent work, we can hypothesize that the timing of these three events is adjusted over time based on a close information‐movement coupling (e.g., Warren, 2006). Thus, visually guided regulations should make it possible to adjust the timing step by step in order to optimize the timing of foot lift. If that is the case, the expertise would lie in the ability to reduce variability over the three events so that it is minimal at foot lift. Whatever the case, we believe that precise knowledge of the processes implemented is essential with a view to optimizing performance among high‐level athletes in order to be able to offer the most effective training programs.

METHODS

Participants

Nineteen athletes (12 males, mean age = 20.58 ± 1.56 years and 7 females, mean age = 24.8 ± 3.43) participated in the experiment. All were part of the French athletics team and competed at national or international levels. They had no prior experience with this VR setup, had normal or corrected‐to‐normal vision, and reported no injuries. Before the study, participants gave their written informed consent. The experimental protocol was in accordance with the Helsinki Convention and approved by a national ethical committee (CERSTAPS IRB00012476‐2023‐01‐03‐237).

Virtual reality setup

Participants were immersed in the athletic track of the Stade de France. The virtual scene was built with Unity software (version: 3.2.0) and projected via an HMD device (model: HTC Vive Eye Pro, field of view: 110°, image resolution: 2160 × 1200 pixels). Foot and pelvis positions were recorded with HTC Vive trackers (<1 mm spatial resolution, 90 Hz temporal resolution). Using these markers, the participants' feet were reconstructed in the virtual world, enabling precise placement on the virtual starting line. Eye movements were recorded through the eye‐tracking system integrated in the HMD (1° spatial resolution, 60 Hz temporal resolution).

Task

The experiment was carried out on a 30‐m indoor athletics track, providing sufficient space for athletes to move around without risk of collision with the physical world (Figure 1). In the fourth lane of the virtual track, participants stood on the starting line of the baton transmission zone, waiting for their virtual partner positioned 50 m behind them. The motion capture of the virtual partner was collected on an athletics track at the outset of the project (on young athletes—i.e., less than 20 years old—from the French national team) using a markerless system (Xsens MVN; Xsens Technologies B.V., Enschede, NL). The subjects were male and female athletes and motion captures were taken in different lanes and positions (straights and turns); this made it possible to have an animation library. Although the French team athletes tested in this study were able to recognize the kinematics of some of the animations, as they were not in the same age category, they did not compete together. At the start of each trial, the partner's avatar (Figure 2A) runs at a constant speed which, in agreement with the coaches and to reflect the reality of the terrain as closely as possible, were 10.8 ms in a straight line and 10.5 ms in a bend for men (8.4 and 7.6 for women, respectively). Given that no significant differences were observed between the two conditions, data were merged. A go‐mark was placed on the ground in the running lane between the avatar and him/her in a range of 7.6–8.4 m, which corresponds to the theoretical distance required to allow an exchange of the baton at the end of the exchange box. These speed and distance values are individualized and were provided by the trainers based on data collected during training sessions. Participants were instructed to start their run (objectified by lifting the rear foot) at the precise moment their partner (virtual pelvis position) passed over the go‐mark. Immediately after starting their run, participants had to decelerate in 3/4 strides. Participants could then return to the starting line and wait for a new trial to begin.

Procedure

The experiment was divided into two blocks:

In the first block, the athletes completed 10 calibration trials in the presence of qualitative and quantitative feedback. The value of error was displayed on the virtual ground and the color of the feedback was red or green when the performance was out‐of‐range or in‐range (±50 ms with reference to the passage of the avatar's pelvis vertically to the mark), respectively. This range represents a tolerance zone defined by the coach inside which the start can be made without compromising the smooth passing of the baton. In the second block, the athletes completed 40 experimental trials without feedback, split into four sessions of 10 trials each.

Between each session, participants could take a 3‐min break during which they could remove the HMD and sit down. Finally, before and after the experiment, they completed an acceptability questionnaire, which will be dealt with in another study.

Data processing

To characterize expert behavior under conditions that are as close as possible to reality, data analysis was carried out only on the second block without feedback. This is a preparatory movement before run initiation. Using the Vive tracker on the pelvis, we calculated when the center of mass started to move. Raw positional data were low‐pass filtered with a dual‐pass Butterworth (cut‐off frequency: 2 Hz; order: 3). To detect the wave initiation, the algorithm initially identifies the minimum value of the y position and subsequently identifies the stability phase that precedes the movement. The timing of wave initiation was computed for each participant as the mean difference in milliseconds between the moment of the wave initiation and the moment the partner passes over the mark. To analyze the within‐athlete between‐trials variability of these three key events, in addition to the variable error of foot lift, we computed for each participant the standard deviations of the time of occurrence of both wave initiation and gaze release. We also computed a linear correlation between the variable error and absolute error of foot lift to evaluate the link between the variability of the final motor event and performance.

The analysis of visual strategies could provide another marker of expertise (Hüttermann et al., 2018). Using the HMD‐integrated eye‐tracking system, we calculated the percentage of time spent in pre‐determined areas of interest (AOIs)—partner, path, and go‐mark (Figure 2A). The analysis was performed on a 2‐s time window before gaze release.

Statistical analyses

Repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on both the time of occurrence of the three key events (i.e., wave initiation, gaze release, and foot lift) and its variability (within‐athlete between‐trials). Between‐athlete variability was also assessed for each event through the standard deviation of the mean time of occurrence of the event.

Finally, we described visual strategies by comparing the two AOIs predominantly observed using linear correlation. Statistical analyses were performed using JASP (Version 0.18.3). The normal distribution of data for each variable was confirmed by Shapiro–Wilk tests and a Greenhouse–Geissner sphericity correction was applied to the ANOVA results when necessary. Bonferroni tests were used for post hoc analyses and the significance threshold was set at p < 0.05. It should be noted that no data are missing.

RESULTS

Timing error

The analysis of the foot lift errors showed a mean absolute error of 82 ± 32 ms. Moreover, the analysis of the mean constant error highlights different athlete behaviors. Some athletes start early (−89 ms on average for the earliest) and others start late (+85 ms on average for the latest; Figure 3A). These first results are interesting for two reasons: firstly, they show that the athletes' level of performance is reasonably good given the high spatio‐temporal constraints of the tasks (79% of athletes are below 80 ms of the mean absolute error). Secondly, they show that performance can still be optimized and homogenized by a training program to enable all athletes to reach the range defined by the coach, and this is what this project is all about.

Events chronology

As shown in Figure 3B, wave initiation occurs on average at 2078 ms before the partner crosses the mark (−2078 ms), followed by gaze release (−457 ms) and foot lift (10 ms). The ANOVA confirmed that these 3 events occur at different times (F(2, 18) = 68.08; p < 0.001; ηp 2 = 0.791). A posteriori comparison revealed that wave initiation occurs earlier than gaze release (−1621 ms; p < 0.001) and foot lift (−2088 ms; p < 0.001). Despite the proximity in time between gaze release and foot lift, gaze release occurred earlier than foot lift (−0.467 ms; p < 0.05; Figure 4A).

Detailed representation of between‐ (A) and within‐ (B) athlete variability of the three motor events. (C) Linear correlation between absolute and variable errors. The timing error is smaller in the least variable athletes. **p < 0.001; *p < 0.05.

Within‐athlete variability

The analysis of within‐athlete between‐trials variability reveals a significant main effect of the event (F(2, 18) = 53.08; p < 0.001; ηp 2 = 0.747; Figure 4B). The variability of wave initiation is higher (452 ± 222 ms) than the variability of both gaze release (86 ± 36 ms; p < 0.001) and foot lift (81 ± 29 ms; p < 0.001). Conversely, no significant difference was found between the variability of gaze release and the foot lift (p = 0.908). Positive correlation was found between absolute error and variable error (R = 0.935; p < 0.001; Figure 4C), which suggests that the athletes with the least variation in foot lift timing would be the best performers.

Between‐athlete variability

The results also revealed between‐athletes differences in the timing of the appearance of each event, with an attenuation of these differences over time (Figure 3B and 4A). In other words, the moment of appearance of the initiation of the wave is very variable from one athlete to another (±976 ms); this variability is reduced for gaze release (±107 ms) and becomes minimal when the foot is lifted (±46 ms).

Visual strategies

The visual behavior of athletes is characterized by a focus on two areas of interest: the partner and the path. The visual anchoring in these two areas of interest represents 98% of the total visual behavior observed. While the athletes spent 42 ± 27% of the time focusing on the partner, they spent 56 ± 26% of the time focusing on the path. The great diversity of visual behaviors observed in athletes can be summarized by the negative correlation between these two AOIs (R = −0.99; p < 0.001; Figure 5). While some athletes are focused on the path (up to 98.9%) or on the partner (up to 87.7%), others show balanced visual behavior between the two AOIs. Despite this high variability between athletes, the visual anchoring in the mark is marginal (1.4 ± 1.8%).

DISCUSSION

The ambition of this study was to characterize the anticipation behavior of top‐level 4 × 100 m relay athletes. Thanks to an immersive VR environment, the athletes' behavior was objectified by analyzing the timing of different motor events and the visual strategies implemented. Taken together, the results revealed the athletes' ability to produce a good level of precision despite the constraints imposed. They also indicate that this level of precision is possible thanks to continuous adjustments of the movement which precedes the initiation of the run, despite large inter‐individual differences in the behaviors produced and the information‐gathering strategies implemented.

Performance

Given the spatio‐temporal constraints of the task, the athletes demonstrated a high level of coordination with their virtual partner. Most of them were able to trigger their run as their partner passed over the go‐mark in a range of 80 ms. This finding is thought to be the result of functional perceptual‐motor regulations (Boyadjian & Bootsma, 1999).

Within‐athlete (between‐trials) variability of key events

The inspection of within‐athlete (between‐trials) variability revealed that while wave initiation was highly variable from one trial to another, both gaze release and foot lift were much less variable. This reduction in variability reflects the implementation of a close perceptual‐motor dialog, allowing athletes to adjust their preparatory movement to allow them to initiate their run at the right time. This ability of high‐level athletes to use the information at their disposal to make functional movement adjustments in highly constrained tasks has already been demonstrated in gymnasts (Bardy & Laurent, 1998; Lee et al., 1992), table tennis players (Bootsma & van Wieringen, 1990), or in long jumpers (Lee et al., 1992). Obviously, anticipation in relay is not a matter of initiating the wave at the right time so as to execute a standardized motor sequence (Tyldesley & Whiting, 1975) but rather of adjusting the preparatory movement so as to initiate their run at the right time, using information relating to the partner's approach with reference to the mark (Warren, 2006). In this context, the fact that the last two events (i.e., gaze release and foot lift) were equally variable is not so surprising given the fact that gaze release corresponds to the last moment at which the athlete can collect information about his/her partner.

While all athletes show a reduction in variability from movement initiation to foot lift, the linear correlation between absolute and variable errors reveals that the best athletes are those with the least variability in foot lift timing. This result supports the importance of regulation processes which, for the best athletes, lead to maximum final stability. This accuracy and precision observed in the best athletes is in line with numerous studies highlighting the ability of experts to start from highly variable movement to finish with a very stable performance (Seifert et al., 2013).

Between‐athletes variability

The second major result of this study concerns the great variability in behaviors observed from one athlete to another, whether in the chronology of events or in visual search patterns. The timing of wave initiation is a perfect illustration of such a large variability with average initiations of the wave ranging from −4 s to −1 s with reference to the partner's passing the mark. Interestingly, between‐athletes variability decreases significantly at gaze release and even more at foot lift, reflecting the fact that different strategies are used to achieve the same goal (i.e., initiate the run at the appropriate time). The same observation applies to the results which concern visual prospecting patterns. Besides the fact that our results reveal that athletes prefer to focus on the lane and/or on the partner to the detriment of the mark, above all they reveal a great diversity of preferential visual prospecting patterns ranging from athletes focusing on one of the two areas of interest to athletes splitting their time between the two. While the fixation of the partner can be likened to a foveal point strategy, which provides a precise vision of the approaching partner's movement (Vater et al., 2020), the fixation of the lane and marginally of the mark can encourage the acquisition of visual information on the periphery (Limballe et al., 2022). Overall, this heterogeneity of the behaviors described must be linked to the specificities (biomechanical, physiological, and psychological) of the athletes. Identifying the most effective strategies is beyond the scope of this work but deserves to be addressed in future studies.

Conclusion

Our study represents a first step in characterizing the anticipation behavior of elite athletes in the 4 × 100 m relay using an immersive and realistic VR setup. This work allowed us to identify the contours of the processes implemented to reveal not only the significant diversity of the behaviors implemented but also to identify avenues for optimizing performance among high‐level athletes. Nevertheless, recent studies have demonstrated that a global approach can result in the misinterpretation of behavior (Morris‐Binelli et al., 2021; Müller et al., 2023). Observations made at the group level can mask individual specificities. Although this initial work at the global level was necessary to understand the task, it is therefore essential that this work on understanding the perceptual‐motor processes involved in 4 × 100 m relay athletes evolves through an individual approach. To achieve this, it will be interesting to evaluate a larger cohort of athletes and identify typical profiles. Taking into account inter‐individual differences is essential at the very highest level of sport performance, where certain recommendations need to be quasi‐personalized.

However, to accurately characterize behavior, the question of ensuring that VR faithfully reproduces the characteristics of the real world is central. The simulator has been developed in collaboration with those most familiar with its constraints (athletes and coaches) through a process of iterative co‐construction. Although the first stage of the project involving measuring the acceptance indicates that the tool was perceived very positively by athletes and coaches, it is possible that wearing a helmet influences athletes' behavior. The speed perceived in VR, the field of view of the HMD, or the mobility constraint inherent to VR setup are all elements that can create a discrepancy with reality. Therefore, the validation of the VR tool and the transfer of skills to the real world are crucial steps in the next stage of the project. Further investigations using wireless HMD will enable us to achieve a better understanding of the underlying processes with the aim of proposing VR training protocols intended to optimize these processes.
