Beyond detection: the unmet need for somatic BRCA variant interpretation guidelines in HRD assays
Authors: Kok-Siong Poon, Kevin Mong Sheng Sim, Kah Weng Lau
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent article by Papadopoulou et al., "Evaluation of the 1021-HRD assay compared to established HRD testing platforms in ovarian cancer" published in Translational Oncology [1] . The study provides a valuable contribution by validating a decentralized comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) assay against the established Myriad myChoice CDx reference test. The demonstration of high concordance for homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) status and the added value of broader genomic profiling is particularly noteworthy for the advancement of personalized oncology.

However, we wish to draw attention to a point in the Discussion [1] that we believe warrants clarification as it touches upon a critical and ongoing challenge in the field which is the accurate interpretation of somatic BRCA1 and BRCA2 variants. The authors discuss a discrepant case where a BRCA2 variant (NM_000059.4:c.10095delinsGAATTATATCT, NP_000050.3:p.(Ser3366Asnfs*4)) was detected by the 1021-HRD and AmoyDx assays but not by the reference test (CGP tab of the supplementary material) [1]. They suggest that this variant is "likely a true positive rather than a false positive" and raise "the possibility that the reference assay may have failed to detect it." With respect, we believe this interpretation may not fully account for the current body of evidence regarding this specific variant. A review of the literature and clinical databases suggests that the variant is not pathogenic and therefore the Myriad myChoice CDx result which did not report it is more likely to be correct. If this is the case, the detection of this variant by the 1021-HRD and AmoyDx assays would represent a false positive finding, rather than a failure of the reference test.

We acknowledge, however, a key nuance raised by the reference test's design. Myriad myChoice CDx is known to report only clinically significant BRCA1 and BRCA2 variants. It remains unclear whether the assay detects all variants but selectively reports only actionable ones or whether detection itself is limited. If the former is true, that is if Myriad did detect this variant but omitted it from the clinical report due to its likely benign nature then the 1021-HRD and AmoyDx assays would indeed be generating a false positive clinical call, not a detection failure by the reference test. Conversely, if the reference test failed to detect the variant entirely, the authors' interpretation would be reasonable. Resolving this distinction would ideally require access to raw Myriad data rather than the final clinical report alone, though we recognize such data are rarely available in validation studies. In the absence of that information, the most prudent approach is to rely on the best available guidelines-based biological and clinical evidence for variant pathogenicity.

This distinction is not merely semantic but has direct implications for the study's reported performance metrics. The authors' current interpretation treats this case as a missed detection by the reference test. However, if the variant is accounted as benign in the analysis, it would instead be counted as a false positive for the 1021-HRD assay. Such a reclassification could marginally alter the concordance characteristics presented in the study [1].

This case underscores a broader issue which is the critical need for robust, guideline-driven variant interpretation in the somatic context. The AMP/CAP/ACCC HRD guideline explicitly states that "classification of the pathogenicity of detected variants in HRR genes" remains a key challenge and that laboratories must apply rigorous criteria to distinguish driver from passenger alterations [2]. While much of the literature has historically focused on germline BRCA testing, the accurate identification of actionable somatic BRCA1 and BRCA2 variants is equally crucial. Unlike germline testing which levarages on the extensive population and segregation data, somatic testing often confronts a wider spectrum of variants including rare or novel alterations. Critically, there is currently a lack of standardized guidelines specifically tailored for somatic BRCA variant interpretation. In most diagnostic laboratories, functional studies are not feasible and clinical decisions must therefore rely on a combination of in-silico predictors, population databases, literature and by necessity, adapted principles from well-established germline guidelines [3,4].

For the BRCA genes, the interpretation process must be deeply informed by gene structure and protein biology. The landmark work by the ClinGen ENIGMA BRCA1 and BRCA2 Variant Curation Expert Panel (VCEP) has provided extensive, evidence-based specifications for germline variant classification [5]. A key principle is that variant pathogenicity cannot be inferred from mutation type alone; factors such as exon location, escape from nonsense-mediated decay, and functional domain integrity are paramount [5].

Applying these adapted germline principles to the variant in question which is a frameshift located in the extreme C-terminal region of the BRCA2 gene (exon 27), downstream of the critical DNA-binding domain provides substantial evidence that it is unlikely to be a driver of HRD. A comprehensive study by Butz et al. evaluating this specific variant found no evidence of loss-of-heterozygosity in tumor tissue and no association with hereditary cancer risk, leading to a consensus re-evaluation as "Likely Benign" in databases such as ClinVar [6]. According to the current AMP/ASCO/CAP guidelines for somatic variant interpretation, this evidence places the variant in Tier IV (Benign or Likely Benign), as it lacks the essential criteria required for Tier I or Tier II designation [3]. This classification readily explains its absence from the Myriad myChoice CDx report, regardless of whether the assay detected the variant technically. The variant is therefore not clinically actionable and should not have been categorized as a "true positive" in the context of HRD determination.

This case serves as an important reminder that for clinical testing, mere detection of a variant is insufficient. As emphasized in the new AMP/CAP/ACCC guideline, adherence to stringent interpretation principles including the variant pathogenicity and the use of gene-specific variant curation rules is paramount to prevent the misclassification of benign variants as pathogenic [2]. Such missteps can have direct consequences for patient care, if happened will potentially leading to incorrect predictions of PARP inhibitor benefit. As CGP assays like the 1021-HRD panel become more widespread, ensuring that interpretation pipelines are harmonized with community standards for variant classification is as vital as demonstrating analytical concordance with reference methods.

We commend the authors for their work in validating this important assay. Our intent is to offer this perspective in the spirit of scientific dialogue, to ensure that the reference standard is given due credit in clinical assay validation and to highlight a broader and critically important aspect of precision oncology which is the need for rigorous, biology-informed variant interpretation to ensure that patients are stratified correctly for targeted therapies.
