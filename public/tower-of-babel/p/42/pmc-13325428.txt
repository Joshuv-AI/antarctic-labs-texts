Cerium-modified niobium oxide/reduced graphene oxide (Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO) hybrid: advances in charge transport dynamics for photocatalytic applications
Authors: Tahani A. Alrebdi, Shamroza Mubarik, Abdul Rauf, Tasneem I. Hussein, Imran Shakir, Sultan Albarakati, Mazen R. Alrahili, Muhammad Aadil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The low quantum efficiency of photocatalysts, constrained by their limited solar spectral response and substantial recombination of photo-generated charge carriers, is the primary bottleneck to the scalability of solar-light-mediated photocatalytic mineralization of organic compounds. In the present report, a hybrid material (Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO) has been rationally designed by doping niobium oxide (Nb2O5) with cerium and embedding the resulting material (Ce-Nb2O5) on reduced graphene oxide (r-GO). The intrinsic Nb2O5 and modified Ce-Nb2O5 materials were synthesized by the facile surfactant-free hydrothermal route, and the hybrid Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO was prepared by applying the ultrasonication method. The structural and morphological characteristics, optical response, and charge-carrier (h+–e−) dynamics in the as-designed materials were studied using various characterization techniques. The Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO exhibits the dispersion of Ce-Nb2O5 particles over the r-GO sheets with substantial broadening of optical response, and facilitated charge transport dynamics, owing to the rare-earth metal doping and combining with the conductive framework. The Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO showcased notable photocatalytic performance, investigated by degrading methyl orange (MO), as it exhibited 91.9% MO degradation and 82.7% mineralization (total organic content analysis) under optimized conditions, pH = 6, [MO] = 5 mg L−1, and Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO = 50 mg/100 mL dose, following first-order kinetics (0.033 min−1) with excellent reusability and photostability. The radical-trapping experiment, combined with electron spin resonance (ESR) analysis, validated the generation of HO˙ and ˙O2−. The substantial photocatalytic performance, significant reusability, and photostability of the designed photocatalyst highlight its promising potential for solar-light-mediated photocatalytic mineralization of organic pollutants.

Introduction

Photocatalysis, the cornerstone of sustainable chemistry, provides a green solution to the critical problem of surging environmental pollution.1,2 It utilizes solar light, a clean and inexhaustible source, to generate highly reactive radical species that drive the degradation of resistant organic pollutants, which account for more than 70% of contaminants in industrial effluents and are often resistant to conventional chemical and biological treatments.3–6 However, the transition of photocatalysis from laboratory to scalable industrial applicability has not yet been realized due to the low quantum efficiency of traditional semiconductor metal oxide photocatalysts.7,8 These photocatalysts exhibit wide bandgaps (∼3–3.5 eV) and absorb in the ultraviolet region, which comprises only ∼3–4% of the solar spectrum.9 The other major shortcoming is the high rate of photo-generated h+–e− pairs recombination, which subsequently undermines the production of active radicals.10,11 These intrinsic limitations must be addressed by strategic modifications (such as doping) to raise the quantum efficiency, which would be promising for the practical scalability of photocatalysis.12–14 Very recently, hybrid materials have attracted attention as highly efficient photocatalysts due to their multicomponent engineering, which overcomes the limitations of single-component photocatalysts.15–17

Niobium oxide (Nb2O5), a wide-bandgap n-type semiconductor, has physicochemical stability and finds applications in multiple domains, including waveguides, sensors, photodetectors, optical filters, and solar cells.18,19 But its application in photocatalysis systems is limited by its wide bandgap, which prevents visible-light absorption and the generation of h+–e−.20 But several investigations have shown that the unique structural framework of Nb2O5 allows the incorporation of foreign metal/non-metal ions into its lattice (doping), tailoring its bandgap for visible-light absorption.21,22 Various methodologies, for instance, laser pulse ablation,23 Sol–gel,24 and reverse microemulsion,23 have been applied to fabricate a modified Nb2O5-based catalyst to facilitate interfacial charge transfer. The modified Nb2O5 could harvest solar light, which contains a substantial portion of visible light (∼43–45%), generating an h+–e− pair. The rapid recombination of these photo-generated h+–e− results in sluggish redox performance, hampering the production of radical species.25,26 Multicomponent engineering-based photocatalysts leverage the synergistic effect of two different materials to facilitate spatial charge transfer and inhibit rapid recombination.27 The heterostructure materials, owing to the strategic design of the charge-transport framework, shift the carriers' dynamics from a single-electron band structure to a multicomponent system, enabling suppressed h+–e− recombination.28,29 Graphene, the 2D honeycomb-like network of carbon, enables electronic mobility without scattering and could serve as a support in the rational design of photocatalysts to enhance charge separation, promising redox performance and the production of highly reactive radical species.30 Multiple studies report promising charge-separation kinetics and a substantial increase in photocatalytic performance in r-GO-based engineering.31–35

In the present report, a series of materials (Nb2O5, Ce-Nb2O5, and Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO) was fabricated to rationally design a photocatalyst with an extended solar spectral response and facilitated h+–e− separation and transportation. Initially, pure Nb2O5 and cerium-doped variants with dopant concentrations of 1%, 2%, 3%, and 4% were synthesized by the surfactant-free, cost-effective hydrothermal route, and optimized for the catalytic performance. The 1% Ce-doped variant with the highest performance was further processed to fabricate Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO. The r-GO mass loading was optimized through the photocatalytic performance of the Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO with 10%, 20%, and 30% of r-GO loading, and the Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO hybrid with 10% of the r-GO exhibited the maximum photocatalytic performance and was selected for the detailed investigation. The fabricated series was characterized for the structural development, Ce-doping, thermal stability, morphology, and elemental composition. The spectral response and dynamics of h+–e− separation and transport were studied using optical, electrochemical, photoelectrochemical, and electrical analysis. The photocatalytic (PC) activity test was conducted by degrading MO over the synthesized series to detail the influence of Ce-doping and hybrid design comprehensively. The process parameters, zero-point charge pH of the photocatalyst, pH effect, MO concentration, catalyst dose, kinetics, total organic content analysis, radical-trapping experiment, electron spin resonance (ESR) detection of radicals, reusability, and photostability of the catalysts have been studied in detail.

Experimental

Chemicals

Analytical-grade chemicals (Sigma-Aldrich), including cerium nitrate (Ce(NO3)3·6H2O, 99.99%), niobium(v) chloride (NbCl5, 99%), ammonium hydroxide (NH4OH, 28% NH3 in H2O, 99.9%), and ethanol (C2H5OH, 99.5%), were used for the synthesis of intrinsic Nb2O5 and modified Ce-Nb2O5 materials. In PC activity studies, methyl orange (C14H14N3NaO3S) was used as a model organic pollutant. In the radical trapping experiment, silver nitrate (AgNO3), benzoquinone (BQ), isopropyl alcohol (IPA), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) were used as scavengers.

Synthesis of Nb2O5 and Ce-Nb2O5 materials

A facile surfactant-free hydrothermal route was used to synthesize intrinsic Nb2O5 and modified Ce-Nb2O5 compositions with dopant concentrations of 1%, 2%, 3%, and 4%. Typically, NbCl5 was dissolved in ethanol, and Ce(NO3)3·6H2O in DI water were subsequently mixed in an ice bath. NH4OH was added dropwise to maintain a pH of ∼9, and the mixture was poured into a Teflon cup (filling ≤ 80%) enclosed in a stainless autoclave and heated at 180 °C for 12 hours. The suspension containing the precipitates of the synthesized material was collected after the heated period by allowing the autoclave to cool to room temperature. The precipitates were thoroughly washed with water and ethanol and then calcined at 600 °C for 6 hours at a rate of 2 °C min−1. The intrinsic Nb2O5 was fabricated without the addition of cerium precursor.

Fabrication of Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO hybrid

The hybrid Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO was fabricated by ultrasonically embedding the modified material onto r-GO. Initially, the r-GO mass loading was optimized based on the photocatalytic performance of Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO with 10%, 20%, and 30% r-GO loading. For instance, for the Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO with 10% of r-GO loading, the Ce-Nb2O5 powder was added to DI water (90 mg/100 mL) and dispersed by sonicating for 10 minutes. The suspension was mixed with the freshly (chemically) reduced r-GO (10 mg/100 mL), and the resultant blend was sonicated for 1 hour to embed Ce-Nb2O5 over the r-GO uniformly. The subsequent hybrid mixture was dried (60 °C/2 hours), ground, and characterized. The Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO with 10% r-GO loading was optimized for maximum photocatalytic performance in MO degradation.

Characterization

The as-designed materials were studied through comprehensive characterization for structural confirmation: X-ray diffraction (XRD, Shimadzu 6100AS X-ray diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiations) and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, IRAffinity-1S Shimadzu spectrophotometer), thermal stability: thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Thermo Plus Evo/Rigaku TG8120), morphology and elemental composition: scanning electron microscopy SEM and energy dispersive X-ray (SEM-EDX, scanning electron microscope FEI S50), optical response: UV-visible spectroscopy (UV-vis, Jenway-6850 double beam spectrophotometer) and Photoluminescence spectroscopy (PL, FLS 1000 spectrophotometer), h+–e− separation and transport: current–voltage analysis (I–V, KEITHLEY 2450 sourcemeter), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS, IVIUM-n-Stat ZRA workstation, three-electrode configuration), and transient photocurrent generation (I–t).

Electrochemical and electrical measurements

Electrochemical (EIS) and photoelectrochemical (I–t) measurements were conducted on an electrochemical workstation using a three-electrode configuration, with Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) as the reference electrode and a platinum wire as the counter electrode. The working electrode was prepared by drop-casting the sample material onto indium tin oxide (ITO) glass in a 0.1 M Na2SO4 electrolyte over a frequency range of 1–106 Hz. For photoelectrochemical measurements, 1 Sun intensity (λ > 420 nm) was applied to the working electrode. The I–V measurements were performed using the 2-probe method on 1-mm-thick, 10-mm-diameter sample pellets. Silver paste was applied to connect the pellets to the copper wire.

PC activity studies

The PC studies were conducted by degrading MO in aqueous solutions under a 300 W Xe lamp (λ > 420 nm) with an intensity of ∼90–95 mW cm−2 at the reaction vessel, positioned 15 cm below the light source. Typically, 40 mg of the photocatalyst was dispersed in 100 mL of solution ([MO] = 5 mg L−1) and stirred in the dark for 1 hour to reach adsorption/desorption equilibrium of MO on the catalyst surface. Subsequently, the suspension was exposed to light, and samples were withdrawn from the aliquot at specific intervals to monitor changes in MO concentration by measuring the absorbance of the collected samples. Results and discussion

Structural studies and thermal stability

The structural confirmation and phase identification of the synthesized materials were determined by XRD analysis. The diffraction patterns of Nb2O5 and Ce-Nb2O5 at different concentrations of dopant are presented in Fig. 1(a), while that of Nb2O5 and (1% of dopant) optimized Ce-Nb2O5 before annealing, in Fig. 1(b). The well-defined diffraction peaks-unified with standard XRD data in Fig. 1(c)-at 2θ = 22.6°, 28.3°, 28.9°, 36.6°, 37.1°, 42.6°, 45.1°, 46.2°, 49.9°, 50.9°, 55.1°, 55.3°, 56.4°, and 58.6° correspond to (001), (180), (200), (201), (211), (1130), (330), (002), (0160), (380), (1170), (202), (381), and (2160) diffractions which confirm the structural development of orthorhombic Nb2O5 (Pbam), as the collected XRD data well aligns with the standard, 00-027-1003 (ref. 38). Notably, a slight variation in 2θ position and a decline in intensity of the diffraction peaks were observed on Ce-doping ((Fig. 1(d)), which corresponds to the insertion of Ce ions in the Nb2O5 lattice.39 The significant drop in diffraction intensity for the Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO hybrid corresponds to the shielding effect of the r-GO, which inhibits the penetration of X-rays and their subsequent diffraction from the Ce-Nb2O5 lattice planes.40 The low-intensity broadband at 2θ ∼24.5° corresponds to the typical (002) reflection of r-GO.41,42 The calculated crystallite sizes of Nb2O5 and Ce-Nb2O5 were 19.08 and 21.17 nm, respectively.

To investigate thermal stability, TGA analysis was performed, and the curves for Ce-Nb2O5 and Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO showing mass loss as a function of temperature are displayed in Fig. 1(f). Optical analysis

The spectral response, specifically the light absorption characteristics of the designed series, was analyzed by UV-vis spectroscopy, and the (absorption) spectra of these materials are presented in Fig. 4(a). The strong absorption edge in the spectra of these materials corresponds to the absorption of the light in the visible region. The shift in the absorption band by modifying the intrinsic material and fabricating a hybrid increases visible-light absorption, clearly indicating the potential for solar light harvesting to generate h+–e−.50,51 The optical bandgap (Eg) of Nb2O5, Ce-Nb2O5, and Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO, estimated from the Tauc plot model, were Eg = 2.56, 2.39, and 2.33 eV, respectively (Fig. 4(b)). The Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO with reduced Eg could be a potential photocatalyst for performing under solar light.

Charge separation and transport dynamics

The efficient transportation of the photo-generated h+–e−, which notably controls the PC activity, was studied by the EIS, I–t, PL, and I–V analysis. The Nyquist plots, photocurrent on–off curves, PL spectra, and I–V profiles of the designed series, Nb2O5, Ce-Nb2O5, and Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO are presented in Fig. 5. The prominent semicircles featured in the Nyquist plots describe their electrochemical response with a decrease in radius of the apparent semicircle after Ce-doping and fabricating a hybrid with the r-GO (Fig. 5(a)).52 The synergistic modification accelerates charge-carrier transport, reducing recombination and improving PC performance. The transient I–t response was recorded, and all photocatalysts exhibited on–off cycles with varying photocurrent intensities. The hybrid Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO exhibited the maximum photocurrent density compared to the Nb2O5, Ce-Nb2O5, and r-GO (Fig. 5(b)). The response directly demonstrates efficient h+–e− separation with a minimal recombination rate.53,54

(a) EIS Nyquist plots, (b) photocurrent, (c) PL spectra, and (d) I–V analysis of Nb2O5, Ce-Nb2O5, Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO, and r-GO.

In the PL spectra of Nb2O5, Ce-Nb2O5, and Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO, the intrinsic Nb2O5 demonstrated the maximum PL intensity (Fig. 5(c)), while for the hybrid Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO, a notable quenching in PL intensity was detected. This change in PL of the hybrid, decrease in intensity, and red shift correspond to the generation of defect states by the dopant Ce-ions in the Nb2O5 lattice, substantially facilitating h+–e− separation compared to intrinsic materials. For the Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO, the charge-transport facilitation by the r-GO counterpart further suppresses h+–e− recombination, as evidenced by its lowest PL.55 The I–V characteristics (showing the conductivity of material under applied voltage window from −10 V to +10 V) for Nb2O5, Ce-Nb2O5, rGO, and Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO are recorded in Fig. 5(d). The pristine Nb2O5 has a highly non-linear I–V response (like an inverse S) that is the characteristic property of a high-resistive wide band-gap semiconductor.56 With doping of Ce and a combination of r-GO, Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO hybrid shows a more quasi-linear I–V response with a steeper slope than intrinsic Nb2O5, indicating better conductivity with good carrier mobility. The measured current is still within the µA range, not mA or A, so the hybrid still retains its semiconducting nature rather than behaving as a true electrical conductor. The improved conductivity of the Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO hybrid can be attributed to the synergistic contribution of intrinsic and extrinsic effects arising from Ce doping and r-GO reinforcement, respectively.57 The EIS, I–t, PL, and I–V findings reveal decreased impedance, increased photocurrent density, quenched PL, and higher conductive response for the hybrid Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO, which demonstrate improved photo-generated h+–e− dynamics in the rationally designed photocatalyst.

Process parameters optimization

The pHPZC value for the Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO was computed to be 6.77 (Fig. 7(a)). The influence of pH on MO degradation was investigated by degrading MO (5 mg L−1) in the presence of Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO (10 mg) at pH 4–9 (Fig. 7(b)). The maximum degradation was observed at pH = 6 < pHPZC (6.77), where the catalyst surface was positively charged, facilitating electrostatic interactions between the anionic MO and cationic sites on the catalyst.58 The degradation rate decreased rapidly when pH > pHPZC, due to reduced MO adsorption as the catalyst surface became negatively charged.59 The MO concentration was varied at the optimized pH to examine its effect. At the lowest concentration of 5 mg L−1, the maximum MO degradation was achieved, and beyond this concentration (>5 mg L−1), degradation decreased (Fig. 7(c)). This decrease corresponds to hindered light penetration to the catalyst surface and to the saturation of surface catalyst sites, which compromise degradation.60,61

(a) pH drift plots pHPZC determination and effect of (b) pH, (c) MO concentration, and (d) catalyst dose on MO degradation.

The catalyst dose was studied at the optimized MO concentration and pH by varying the catalyst loading from 10 to 60 mg/100 mL in a 5 mg L−1 MO solution (Fig. 7(d)). The increase in loading surges MO degradation, reaching a maximum at 50 mg of loading; beyond this, no further significant increase was observed, but a minimal shift toward lower degradation. The adverse effect of the catalyst at doses exceeding 50 mg is driven by multiple factors, including light scattering, which limits photon penetration, and catalyst particle aggregation, which leads to sedimentation.62,63

Kinetics study

The MO degradation kinetics profile was studied by applying the first-order kinetics equation (Fig. 8(a and b)). The linear fit, characterized by high regression coefficients,64 describes first-order kinetics for MO degradation. The rate constants (k) were calculated from the slope of the linear fitting curves and determined to be 0.009, 0.015, and 0.033 min−1, which aligns with the MO degradation (%) trend 45.3%, 62.2%, and 91.9% for the catalysts Nb2O5, Ce-Nb2O5, and Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO, respectively, solidifying the position of Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO as the most efficient catalyst in the synthesized series (Fig. 8(d)). The TOC measurements indicate 82.7%, 49.2%, and 34.2% MO mineralization by the Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO, Ce-Nb2O5, and Nb2O5 catalysts, respectively (Fig. 8(c)).

Scavenging studies, ESR analysis, and reusability

The contribution of different species during the MO degradation process was studied by applying the radical trapping experiment in the presence of Ce-Nb2O5 (Fig. 9(a)) and Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO (Fig. 9(b)) using multiple scavengers, specifically, IPA, BQ, EDTA, and AgNO3 to quench correspondingly HO˙, ˙O2−, h+, and e−.65 The IPA caused a significant reduction in MO degradation, whereas AgNO3 had a minimal effect compared with the scavenger-free system. The presence of BQ declines the degradation process, highlighting the substantial contribution of the ˙O2− radicals, while the EDTA presence shows the moderate role of h+. The findings demonstrate the involvement of the different active species in MO degradation following hierarchical order HO˙ > ˙O2− > h+ > e− (Fig. 9(c)). For the catalyst Ce-Nb2O5, the scavenging studies revealed no substantial decline by the BQ or AgNO3, which indicates the MO degradation was dominated by the HO˙ followed by the h+, attributing to the no generation of ˙O2− radicals. The ESR profile of the active radical species validates their efficient generation during the process (Fig. 9(d)), consistent with the radicals-trapping experiment, in which scavenging these radicals notably decreases MO degradation.

(a–c) Radicals trapping experiment, (d) ESR analysis, (e) reusability, and (f) before-and-after XRD profile of Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO.

The Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO was reused for five cycles of MO degradation to explore its photostability and reusability (Fig. 9(e)). The catalyst performed well, with a nominal decrease in its PC activity after five cycles, attributed to incomplete washing, which left residual pollutant molecules adsorbed on the catalyst surface, or to recovery loss. The XRD profile of the used catalyst aligns with that obtained previously, indicating the structural integrity (Fig. 9(f)). The reusability test and XRD analysis of the recovered catalyst demonstrate the hybrid material's reusability and stability.

Mechanism of degradation

To propose the detailed mechanism of MO degradation (Fig. 10), the electronic band potential values were calculated by applying the relations, EVB = X − Ee + 1/2(Eg) and ECB = EVB − Eg, where EVB = valence band potential, ECB = conduction band potential, Eg = optical bandgap, Ee = free-electron energy (hydrogen scale), and X = Electronegativity.66,67 In Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO, the Ce-Nb2O5 exhibits ECB = 0.21 eV and EVB = 2.60 eV, where EVB is sufficient enough to drive the HO˙ generation as HO−/HO˙ (1.99 eV) and H2O/HO˙ (2.23 eV). But the ECB is not negative enough for ˙O2− production as O2/˙O2− (−0.33 eV).68–70 The scavenging studies, and ESR analysis validate the ˙O2− generation and active involvement in MO degradation. The electrons from the conduction band of Ce-Nb2O5 are spatially migrated to the r-GO counterpart, where they reduce O2 and generate ˙O2−, which are involved in MO degradation.

Conclusion

The study demonstrates the successful fabrication of the designed hybrid Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO via the hydrothermal-ultrasonication approach. The structural characteristics were investigated through a series of characterizations that describe the structural phase (orthorhombic) development with elongated Ce-Nb2O5 particles embedded in r-GO, demonstrating notable thermal stability and uniform elemental distribution. The UV-vis spectroscopic analysis shows visible-light absorption extending the solar spectral response for potential PC performance. The EIS, I–t, PL, and I–V findings highlight substantial separation and efficient transport, as evidenced by decreased impedance, increased photocurrent density, quenched PL, and enhanced conductive response in the hybrid Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO. The PC test, in which MO was degraded, demonstrates the notable PC performance of Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO compared to Nb2O5 and Ce-Nb2O5, achieving 91.9% MO degradation and 82.7% mineralization (TOC) within 60 minutes of visible-light exposure. The process parameters were optimized to pH = 6, [MO] = 5 mg L−1, and Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO = 50 mg/100 mL of MO, and first-order kinetics (0.033 min−1) were followed during the degradation process. The radicals-trapping experiment findings align with the ESR analysis, which validates the ˙O2− and HO˙ generation with hierarchical order HO˙ > ˙O2− > h+ > e− contribution to MO degradation. With notable PC activity and excellent reusability and photostability, Ce-Nb2O5/r-GO emerges as a promising photocatalyst for solar-light-mediated treatment of organic pollutants.
