Reduced multiplicity of crystallographic sites for superior thermoelectric performance of cubic Cu6GeTeS4 via chemical tailoring
Authors: Ya Yang, Ying Zhang, Zixuan Chen, Pengju Han, Shuailiang Chen, Chong Hua, Yunpeng Zheng, Hongwei Ming, Naifeng Zhuang, Zhong-Zhen Luo, Zhigang Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Argyrodites have garnered substantial interest as ultralow lattice thermal conductivity (κlat) materials. However, their development is constrained by poor thermal stability and inferior thermoelectric performance. Here, a cubic argyrodite compound, Cu6GeTeS4, was successfully synthesized via a chemical-tailoring strategy. Moreover, a strong correlation between the multiplicity of crystallographic sites and ion mobility was found. The reduced multiplicity of Cu crystallographic sites leads to superior thermal stability compared to Cu8GeS6, which undergoes a phase transition from orthorhombic to cubic. The weak Cu─S/Te chemical bonds and cage-like [TeCu18]16+ vibrations at low frequencies lead to intrinsically ultralow κlat ~ 0.47 to 0.35 W m−1 K−1 and realize the maximum ZT (ZTmax) of ~0.62 for Cu6GeTeS4. By introducing S deficiencies, the Cu6GeTeS3.82 obtained an excellent peak ZTmax of 1.23 at 925 K with an ultralow κlat of ~0.25 W m−1 K−1. This study presents high-performance thermoelectrics and provides insights to the design of liquid-like systems.

INTRODUCTION

Thermoelectric materials can enable a direct reversible conversion between heat and electricity using the Seebeck and Peltier effects (1–3). Generally, the dimensionless figure of merit ZT (ZT = S2σT/κtot) is evaluated as the energy conversion efficiency, where S, σ, and T are the Seebeck coefficient, electrical conductivity, and absolute temperature, respectively, and κtot (κtot = κele + κlat) is the total thermal conductivity contributed by electronic thermal conductivity (κele) and lattice thermal conductivity (κlat). All these parameters are interdependently coupled with carrier concentration, with κlat being the only independent parameter, creating a fundamental constraint that limits ZT enhancement (4, 5). Therefore, suppressing κlat can uniquely enhance the thermoelectric performance of a material. The κlat can be suppressed by effective extrinsic approaches by introducing grain boundaries (6), dislocations (7, 8), nanostructures (9, 10), and off-center atoms (11–13). In contrast, strategies such as lattice anharmonicity (14), ferroelectric instability (15), rattling of atoms (16), bonding anisotropy (17), influence of antibonding interactions (18, 19), and liquid-like cationic motion (20–22) in a solid framework have been used as intrinsic approaches to reduce κlat. As thermoelectrics with intrinsically low κlat contributing to liquid-like cationic motion, argyrodites demonstrate characteristic “phonon liquid-electronic crystal (PLEC)” behavior, receiving increasing interest.

Argyrodites represent a promising thermoelectric material with substantial potential for further development. Most argyrodites are Ag-based compounds and exhibit n-type conductivity, such as Ag8GeSe6 (23), Ag9GaSe6 (24), Ag8SnS6 (25), and Ag9AlSe6 (26). In particular, the ZT value of Ag9GaSe6 was enhanced from 0.67 to 1.51 with Te alloying (27). The high thermoelectric performance can be attributed to the covalent framework, which ensures efficient electronic charge transport, similar to the behavior in conventional thermoelectric semiconductors (28). Meanwhile, the intrinsically low κlat in argyrodites arises from their hierarchical chemical bonding, which enables high concentrations of mobile Ag/Cu ions within a rigid covalent framework, together with weakly bonded ions, strong lattice anharmonicity, and low sound velocity (20). These dual structural characteristics allow electrical and thermal transport to achieve decoupling between them.

In comparison, the limited research and low performance of p-type Cu-based argyrodites have hindered the development of p-type counterparts, making further studies imperative. In addition, argyrodites also experience a series of structural transitions with hexagonal, orthorhombic, and monoclinic crystal structures at low temperatures and cubic crystal structures at high temperatures (20). The structural phase transitions pose a substantial challenge for thermoelectric applications, caused by thermal expansion, which can compromise device integrity. Therefore, inhibiting these transitions is essential in p-type argyrodite thermoelectrics.

Moreover, the long-range migration of the Ag/Cu ions is also a substantial challenge, which hinders their widespread application due to challenges in maintaining stability under electro-thermal-chemical conditions (29–32). Previous strategies are used through cation-anion co-doping (30), composite nanophase (33), or programmed thermal annealing (24) to improve the stability. However, the aforementioned strategies involve complex synthesis processes (34) and extrinsic doping, which are unsuitable for large-scale production. The strategies are required to address the long-range migration of ions. In the literature, Qiu et al. demonstrated that the atom displacement parameters (ADPs) of ions exhibit a proportional relationship with ion mobility, as described by the Lindemann parameter δ = ADP1/2/RNN (RNN is the nearest-neighbor distance) (35). When the δ reaches 0.07 or above, the ions in the material are considered to have reached a liquid-like state. Furthermore, Ren et al. used in situ synchrotron x-ray diffraction and single-crystal neutron diffraction to demonstrate that high multiplicity crystallographic sites in single-crystal Ag8SnSe6 exhibit increased ADPs upon heating (36). The above results indicate a strong correlation between the multiplicity of crystallographic sites and ion mobility.

Furthermore, we summarized the correlation between ion mobility and crystal data, including Wyckoff position and site occupancy fraction of different Ag/Cu crystallographic sites in Ag/Cu-based argyrodite compounds with F4¯3m space group, as shown in Fig. 1, fig. S1, and table S1. The chart format and corresponding three-dimensional (3D) bar chart are shown in table S1 and fig. S1. The sites with high multiplicity and complex Wyckoff letters (such as 96i, 48h, and 24g) of partially occupied Ag/Cu ions indicate large structural disorder (36–38). Meanwhile, the abundance of equivalent sites (atoms) after symmetry operations suggests enhanced ion mobility (36, 39, 40). In addition, special positions with low multiplicity (such as 4c and 4b) have fixed fractional coordinates. Hence, the reduction of disorder positions effectively decreases long-range migration of Ag/Cu ions, serving as pinning sites to enhance structure stability. Therefore, in complex crystal structure systems, it is essential to design and synthesize new compounds containing crystallographic sites with low multiplicity. An effective strategy is required to effectively manipulate crystallographic sites and structure, thus improving the electro-thermal-chemical stability.

We propose a crystal structure with a straw checkerboard, where fully occupied Cu ions serve as pinning sites located on the cubic framework, while the remaining partially occupied Cu ions function to scatter phonons within the framework. Moreover, the strategies used in other fields could provide inspiration. In nonlinear optical (NLO) crystal research, researchers address structural symmetry and enhance NLO effects through chemical composition design strategies, including grafting (41), cutting through (42), and motif optimization (43). They achieve a noncentrosymmetric structure and enhance second-harmonic generation responses. These strategies can inspire the targeted regulation of crystallographic sites in argyrodite compounds, enabling the design of new stable high-performance argyrodite compounds.

Hence, we propose the chemical-tailoring strategies to modulate crystal structure and probabilistically reduce the multiplicity of Cu ions. By removing one Cu2S from the unit cell of classical Cu8GeS6 (F4¯3m space group), the reduced multiplicity of Cu crystallographic sites in Cu6GeTeS4 leads to superior thermal stability. In this work, the p-type argyrodite compounds Cu6GeTeS4 with F4¯3m space group was synthesized. Single-crystal diffraction data reveal that the 3D network structure is constructed by cage-like [TeCu18]16+ and tetrakaidecahedron [Cu13]13+ via shared surface. The intrinsically ultralow κlat ~ 0.47 to 0.35 W m−1 K−1 is caused by the weak Cu─S/Te chemical bonds and cage-like [TeCu18]16+ vibrations at low frequencies. Cu6GeTeS4 exhibits high thermoelectric performance with a maximum power factor (PF) of 4.10 μW cm−1 K−2 and a ZTmax of 0.65 at 865 K for intrinsic samples. In addition, Cu6GeTeS3.82 shows an ultralow κlat ~ 0.25 W m−1 K−1 from 300 to 925 K due to S deficiencies, achieving a ZTmax value of 1.23 at 925 K. The Cu ion mobility was successfully partially suppressed through coordinated optimization of crystallographic sites, structural stability, and thermal cycling performances. These results provide valuable guidance for the rational design of high-performance chalcogenide thermoelectric materials.

RESULTS

Crystal structure

The high-quality Cu6GeTeS4 crystal was well refined based on the single-crystal x-ray diffraction data at 298 and 170 K, as listed in table S2. They all crystallized in space group F4¯3m with the cell parameters of a = 10.14 and 10.07 Å at 298 and 170 K, respectively. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS) mapping studies confirmed that the elements are evenly distributed in the crystal, as shown in fig. S2. The photographs of polycrystals with micrometer-sized Cu6GeTeS4 are shown in fig. S2A. Using the strategy of chemical tailoring to subtract two Cu atoms, one S atom, and add a Te atom per unit cell, a Cu6GeTeS4 crystal was obtained by taking Cu8GeS6 (F4¯3m space group) as the template. The transition of the structure is shown in Fig. 2 (B, C, F, and G). The isolated tetrahedra [GeS4]4− units with Ge atoms on the 4d position are in a rigid framework for both of them in Fig. 2H and fig. S3C. As shown in fig. S3A, both Cu(1) and Cu(2) atoms are coordinated with four S atoms to form tetrahedra [CuS4]7− units, while Cu(3) is coordinated with the nearest three S atoms to form trigonal [CuS3]5− units in Cu8GeS6. In comparison with Cu8GeS6, as shown in figs. S3B and S4E, the tetrahedron [CuS4]7− units are absent, both Cu(2) and Cu(3) atoms form the trigonal [CuS3]5− units, and Cu(1) atoms with 12 Cu(2) atoms form a tetrakaidecahedron.

Like other Cu-based argyrodites, a rigid framework is formed by close-packed [MX4]4− and/or X2− anions (23, 27, 36). The Te2− forms a body-centered cubic lattice for Cu6GeTeS4 in Fig. 2 (D and F). The partial occupation of Cu(2) and Cu(3) atoms, with an occupation ratio of around 0.38 and 0.07, is highly diffused in the rigid framework in Fig. 2G. Meanwhile, the larger refined anisotropic displacement parameters of Cu(2) atoms indicate a weak Cu─S/Te bond, as shown in table S6. This feature is expected to promote the production of a greater proportion of low-frequency phonons. The cation site labeled Cu(1) is fully occupied and located in the 4c site with low multiplicity as the pinning sites, which reside in a high-symmetry crystallographic site, suggesting low mobility. Compared to the known Ag/Cu-based argyrodites, the Cu(1) site in Cu6GeTeS4 exhibits a lower multiplicity of the crystallographic site in Fig. 1. The cage-like [TeCu18]16+ cluster is formed by four ternary rings and four six-rings and is located at the center of 12 edges and the body center along the a axis in Fig. 2E and figs. S3D and S4D. The cage-like [TeCu18]16+ and tetrakaidecahedron [Cu13]13+ via shared surface formed a 3D framework, as shown in fig. S4A.

All anion positions are fully occupied. For the Te atoms, the 4b and 4c sites are the favored occupied positions due to their size and polarizability (44) in fig. S4 (B and C). It is consistent with our previous work with Ag4M0.5S2Te (M = Sn and Ge) (45). In addition, Cu8GeSe4Te2 single-crystal refinement at 323 K with Te only on 4d positions (46). Moreover, this can also be explained by coordination spheres around Te and favorable dTe─Cu(2) = 2.68 Å of bond lengths, as shown in figs. S3D and S4D, which also follows typical Te─Cu bond lengths in binary Cu2Te (dTe─Cu = 2.67 Å) (47). The bond lengths of Cu(1)─Cu(2) for the tetrakaidecahedron are 2.66 Å compared with the KCu7S4 compound with 2.60 to 2.99 Å in fig. S3B (48). The bond lengths and angles for Cu6GeTeS4 are shown in tables S7 and S8.

Powder x-ray diffraction

The Rietveld refinement of powder x-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns for the polycrystalline powder Cu6GeTeS4 is shown in Fig. 3B. The results of Rp = 4.91% and Rw = 6.15% indicate the correctness of the structure and purity of the powder sample. However, a second phase invariably emerges during large-scale synthesis before and after Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS). The presence of second phases indicates that the studied argyrodites crystallize with a deviation from the stoichiometry. The investigated samples were annealed for 100 hours at 823 K, while the second phase did not disappear. Most of the observed PXRD diffraction peaks for Cu6GeTeS4−x (x = 0, 0.05, 0.15, 0.18, 0.20, and 0.30) SPSed samples can be indexed in cubic F4¯3m space group. The visible second phase was identified as corresponding to Cu2GeS3 (Inorganic Crystal Structure Database: 146583) with the Cc space group in Fig. 3C. This is further confirmed by the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) characterization in the following discussion. In addition, the secondary phase was quantitative at ~9.7 wt % for x = 0.18, lower than that of the intrinsic sample, ~18.4 wt %, by Rietveld refinements of the SPSed powder in fig. S5. The PXRD patterns for Cu6GeTeS4−x and the Bragg peaks for a shift to a higher diffraction angle agree with the linearly decreased lattice parameters, as shown in Fig. 3D. The apparent saturation of the lattice parameter at x = 0.15 in the Cu6GeTeS4−x series can be rationalized by the combined effects of the solubility limit of S deficiencies within the lattice.

Microstructure analysis

The microstructure of the Cu6GeTeS4 sample was analyzed using TEM. Figure 4B indicates that large amounts of Salmon-type micrometer-sized precipitates Cu2GeS3 with lengths of 1 to 5 μm and widths of 100 to 200 nm embedded in the Cu6GeTeS4 matrix. EDS elemental maps are shown in Fig. 4A. These micrometer-sized precipitates create extensive interfaces with the matrix, leading to a substantial increase in phonon scattering in fig. S13. The element distribution along the red line corresponds with the selected area in the red square of Fig. 4B, which reveals that these microprecipitates are Cu2GeS3. In Fig. 4C, the elemental intensity profiles show the changes in the content of Cu, Ge, Te, and S along the selected line. The content of Cu significantly decreases in Cu2GeS3 compared to Cu6GeTeS4, resulting in the most notable reduction in intensity. This is because the relative content of Ge and S is higher in Cu2GeS3 than in Cu6GeTeS4, leading to an increase in the intensities of these elements. Furthermore, the Te-free Cu2GeS3 exhibits a near-zero intensity of Te. Figure 4 (D and E) shows high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images along the [1¯1¯0] zone axis, along with the fast Fourier transform (FFT) result (inserted on the right side), confirming that the phase is Cu6GeTeS4. The red square zooming in on an area in Fig. 4D gives a clearer view of the lattice fringes. The interplanar distances of 5.9 Å correspond to the (111) planes, which are consistent with the value obtained from the lattice parameter 5.86 Å (dhkl = ah2+k2+l2) (49). Moreover, the selective-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern along the zone axis [01¯1] is shown in Fig. 4F and matches with the F4¯3m space group. The secondary phase was further analyzed by SAED along the [02¯1] zone axis, which provides conclusive evidence for its identification as Cu2GeS3 in Fig. 4G. Moreover, through the two interplanar crystal spacings (3.04 and 3.04 Å) and the angle of 110° between them, it is inferred that the two spots represent (112) lattice planes of Cu2GeS3 (Cc space group). The results show complete concordance with the PXRD analysis. Meanwhile, we also analyzed the Cu6GeTeS3.82 sample by TEM. The results show that it maintains the F4¯3m cubic phase in fig. S6.

Electro-thermal-chemical analysis for Cu8GeS6 and Cu6GeTeS4

The optical reflectance spectrum indicates that the electronic bandgap of polycrystalline Cu6GeTeS4 is 1.45 eV in fig. S7A. Cu6GeTeS4 has a smaller bandgap than that of Cu8GeS6, ~1.645 eV (50). The thermogravimetry (TG) analysis curves show excellent thermal stability and the decomposition temperature of 990 K under N2 conditions for Cu6GeTeS4 in fig. S7B. The differential thermal analysis (DTA) curves indicate that there is no melting or phase transition process from 300 to 873 K in Cu6GeTeS4, in accordance with the PXRD patterns on recrystallization at different temperatures in Fig. 5 (A and B). The Cu8GeS6 exhibits a phase transition at 333 K from Pmn21 to F4¯3m space group in Fig. 5A, same with other works (50–53). The Cu6GeTeS3.82 sample exhibits good thermal stability, as shown in Fig. 5 (C, D, and E), which confirms the effectiveness of the chemical-tailoring strategy. Meanwhile, as shown in fig. S11, the alternating current (ac) impedance and direct current (dc) polarization measurements confirmed that reducing the contribution of Cu ion conduction is the desired outcome in Cu6GeTeS3.82, which is corroborated by the good thermal stability observed for Cu6GeTeS3.82.

The Cu8GeS6 was prepared using the same synthesis and densification process as Cu6GeTeS4. However, as shown in Fig. 5H, the sample cracked and could not be measured by testing the Voltage–Current curve (U-I) curve under a dc of 200 mA after 8 s (left) and 24 s (right) at ZEM (ZEM-3 system, ULVAC-RIKO), indicating the poor stability under the current of Cu8GeS6. In addition, samples from different batches consistently exhibited cracking at 200 mA after 8 and 24 s, as shown in fig. S8 (C and E). Meanwhile, the sample did not crack under an applied current of 20, 40, or 80 mA until the current reached 100 mA, as shown in fig. S10. In contrast, Cu6GeTeS4 maintains its integrity and can still be measured in Fig. 5 (I and J). The SEM micrographs show that the microstructure of Cu8GeS6 after SPS was dense and without macroscopic voids. However, macroscopic cracks and triggered pore formation in specific zones are created under 200 mA and a reduced density of Cu8GeS6 in Fig. 5 (K and L) and fig. S9. Density measurements of the cracked fragments, obtained via the Archimedes drainage method, also indicated a reduction in density from 5.177 g cm−1 of the SPSed sample to 5.012 g cm−1 of the cracked sample in fig. S8A. This also shows that removing one Cu2S from the unit cell through a chemical-tailoring strategy can effectively enhance the stability of Cu6GeTeS3.82.

Thermoelectric properties of Cu6GeTeS4

The temperature-dependent (300 to 866 K) σ, S, PF, κtot, κlat, and ZT values are shown in fig. S12. The σ of intrinsic Cu6GeTeS4 monotonously decreases from 362 to 137 S cm−1 with the temperature in fig. S12A, exhibiting typical conducting behaviors of degenerate semiconductors. The S is positive from 55 to 175 μV K−1, indicating that holes are the majority carriers for transport in fig. S12B, agreeing with the Hall measurements. The increased S also testified to the large optical bandgap of ~1.45 eV for Cu6GeTeS4. It has a moderate PF of ~4.2 μW cm−1 K−2 at 866 K in fig. S12C. As shown in fig. S12E, the κtot decreases from 0.67 to 0.54 W m−1 K−1 from 310 to 866 K.

According to the Wiedemann-Franz law (κele = LσT, where L represents the Lorenz number), the κlat was determined by κtot subtracting the κele from 0.47 to 0.34 W m−1 K−1 in fig. S12F (54, 55). Moreover, based on the diffusion-dominated model developed by Allen and Feldman, the calculated theoretical minimum of κDiff is ~0.40 W m−1 K−1, which describes the thermal conductivity in disordered crystalline materials (56). The κlat is lower than that of κDiff at 600 to 866 K. The reason is that the Cu2GeS3 second phase enhances the phonon scattering at high temperatures. Compared to other state-of-the-art Cu-based classical materials, the Cu6GeTeS4 has an ultralow κlat in fig. S12G. Last, the temperature-dependent ZT for Cu6GeTeS4 is shown in fig. S12D. The ZTmax is 0.62 at 866 K, which is far superior to that of other members in the Cu-based argyrodite materials (38, 50, 57).

Analysis of thermal transport properties

To explore the underlying mechanisms of low κlat, the low-temperature heat capacity Cp of Cu6GeTeS4 was measured from 2 to 30 K. As shown in Fig. 6 (A and B), except for the single Debye model, which contributes to the κlat, we further analyze the experimental data by incorporating three additional reliable Einstein modes. The temperature-dependent Cp/T3 fitting curves with three Einstein modes exhibit a Boson peak at 8 K (58, 59), originating from excitations of low-energy vibrational phonons or interactions between them (60). Based on the fitting data, the low-energy optical phonon modes are characterized as ΘE1 = 16.51 K, ΘE2 = 39.52 K, and ΘE3 = 75.54 K, corresponding to the phonon vibration modes as 8.89, 21.28, and 40.68 cm−1. They can be obtained by the equation: ω = Θ(ℏ/2π)k, where ω is the phonon vibration, Θ is the Einstein mode, ℏ is the Planck constant, and k is the Boltzmann constant. The parameters obtained by fitting the experimental low-temperature heat capacity data to the Debye-Einstein model are presented in table S4.

These phonon energies of Einstein modes are reasonably consistent with the density functional theory (DFT)–calculated low-energy overlapping optical phonon modes and coupling between low-frequency optical and acoustic phonons regime from 34 to 75 cm−1 in Fig. 6C. Meanwhile, they can strongly scatter long-wavelength acoustic phonons. Based on the kinetic theory equation κlat = ⅓Cvval, where Cv is specific heat per volume, l is the phonon mean free path, and va is the average sound velocity. We calculated the l ~ 1.7 Å based on the measured Cp (0.595 J g−1 K−1), which is below the average interatomic spacing in table S7. The lower limit of the actual phonon mean free path arises because all modes in the first Brillouin zone contribute to Cv, whereas only those with dispersions contribute to κlat (25). Moreover, Raman spectroscopy was carried out to experimentally examine the presence of low-energy optical phonon modes in Cu6GeTeS4 single crystal, Cu6GeTeS3.82 powder, Cu8GeS6 powder, and Cu2GeS3 powder. The vibration modes in the range of 393 to 408 cm−1 are attributed to Ge─S bond vibration. The low-energy excitations of Cu6GeTeS4, Cu6GeTeS3.82, and Cu8GeS6 near 39 to 68 cm−1 are characteristic of acoustic-optical phonon coupling, often observed in complex or disordered systems, as shown in Fig. 6D.

In total, the origins of the intrinsically low κlat of Cu6GeTeS4 are discussed below. The low κlat in intermetallic clathrates arises from bonding inhomogeneity and the coexistence of multiple atomic interactions that increase structure asymmetry. The similarly low κlat observed in other argyrodites suggests a shared origin rooted in their complex chemical bonding from multiple Cu─S/Te coordination environments. The weakly bonded Cu ions with high diffusivity lead to an ultralow acoustic branch with a low cutoff frequency of ~34 cm−3. Moreover, the strong lattice vibrational anharmonicity results in a low sound velocity (36). The elastic properties were measured by ultrasonic pulse-echo measurements for the intrinsic sample by SPSed pellets at room temperature in table S3. The soft elastic moduli (22 GPa) and low average speed of sound (2311.5 m s−1) indicate that low-energy optical phonons are strongly coupled with acoustic phonons, which reduces κlat. Furthermore, the incoherent interface between the Cu6GeTeS4 and Cu2GeS3 is another reason for the low κlat value.

Phonon dispersion

To confirm the existence of low-energy optical phonon vibrations, Fig. 6C shows phonon dispersion patterns at 0 K and corresponding to the partial density of states (PDOS) for Cu6GeTeS4 to investigate the intrinsic κlat. The presence of a large imaginary frequency may be due to the phase transition in Cu6GeTeS4 at low temperatures. However, the single-crystal measurement indicates that it retains the F4¯3m cubic phase at 170 K. The PDOS shows the prominent contribution of Cu atoms, with a minor contribution from Te. The low-energy optical phonon mode at the Γ point starts from about 34 cm−1, which is lower than that in the cage-like compounds (such as skutterudites and clathrates) (61). In addition, the low-energy overlapping optical phonons are located within the range of 34 to 75 cm−1, which is primarily dominated by Cu and Te associated vibrations. In general, the thermal conductivity of semiconductor materials is predominantly governed by the acoustic phonon branch. However, low-frequency optical phonons can couple with acoustic phonons in specific systems, thereby transporting heat. Moreover, in Fig. 6C, the phonon dispersion shows avoided crossing points between longitudinal acoustic (LA) and low-energy optical phonons at about 41 cm−1, which indicates the emergence of strong phonon-phonon coupling in Cu6GeTeS4. Such an avoided crossing character shows the softening of the LA mode, resulting in a decrease in group velocity and an increase in scattering rate, which enables the ultralow κlat (62). The experimentally determined low-temperature Cp and Raman spectrum, as well as phonon dispersion spectra of Cu6GeTeS4, both provide evidence of coupling between low-frequency optical and acoustic phonons at ~40 cm−1. The convergence of these datasets provides mutually corroborative validation of this phenomenon.

Moreover, the PDOS at low frequency is mainly contributed by Cu and Te atoms, corresponding to the cage-like [TeCu18]16+ vibrations in Fig. 6C. The cage-like [TeCu18]16+ motifs show that the Te atom is the main lattice vibration with Cu atoms loosely connected. These modes of vibration in lattice acoustical phonon and the local low-frequency optical phonon can be coupled, resulting in resonant scattering and leading to intrinsically low κlat. In contrast, the high-frequency optical phonon modes above 100 cm−1 mainly originate from Ge and Te atoms. Moreover, previous studies have demonstrated that low-frequency flat phonon bands, as in this work, can substantially enhance phonon-phonon scattering rates by creating additional scattering channels, primarily due to their unique dispersion characteristics (21, 63).

Thermoelectric properties of S deficiencies–doped Cu6GeTeS4

Figure 7 illustrates the temperature-dependent σ, S, PF, carrier concentration/mobility nH/μH, and weighted mobility μW for Cu6GeTeS4−x (x = 0, 0.05, 0.15, 0.18, 0.20, and 0.30). The σ decreased from 323 S cm−1 for x = 0 to 92 S cm−1 for x = 0.30 at 300 K in Fig. 7A. Conversely, the S increased from 61 μV K−1 for x = 0 to 131 μV K−1 for x = 0.30 in Fig. 7B. To elucidate carrier transport mechanisms, Hall effect measurements were conducted at 300 K in Fig. 7C. It reveals consistent trends: Increasing S deficiencies reduce nH (~1019 cm−3) through electron-hole recombination (S deficiencies acting as n-type dopant), explaining the observed σ reduction and S enhancement. Although σ decreases with S deficiencies, the substantial S increase maintains PF values, as shown in Fig. 7D.

As shown in Fig. 7E, the S as a function of nH curves indicate that the band effective mass (m*) is 1.74 me at 300 K, which is consistent with Cu7PSe6 (~2.0 me, 300 K) (64) and (Cu8GeSe6)1−x(In2Te3)x (~1.64me, 373 K) (65). To further assess the impact of S deficiencies, we calculated the μW, as shown in Fig. 7F, which is a key indicator of the optimal electrical performance in thermoelectric materials. In the high-temperature region, we observed the decline followed by an increase of the μW in the series with decreasing S deficiencies due to the low nH for x = 0.30 (~1019 cm−3). The temperature trend also roughly follows ≈T−1.5, suggesting that charge carriers are mostly scattered at acoustic or optical phonons (31).

The κtot and κlat as a function of temperature for Cu6GeTeS4−x (x = 0, 0.05, 0.15, 0.18, 0.20, and 0.30) are shown in Fig. 8 (A and B). The κtot initially decreases and then increases with increasing S deficiencies at the tested temperature. Moreover, the κlat is a constant independent of elevated temperature, exhibiting a liquid-like behavior of Cu ions. This is contrary to the crystalline behavior decreasing with temperature caused by Umklapp phonon scattering (22, 26, 66–68). Meanwhile, the estimated Grüneisen parameters γ yield values from 1.89 to 2.09 for x = 0 and 0.18, which are superior compared to previous reports for Cu-based argyrodites (38), indicating substantial lattice vibration anharmonicity in Cu6GeTeS3.82 in table S3. The Cu6GeTeS3.82 sample has the lowest κlat in Fig. 8B. This is because alloying with foreign elements, which have different mass/radius compared to classical materials, strengthens point defect scattering, suppresses high-frequency phonons, and lowers κlat (69–73). However, in the present Cu6GeTeS4−x samples, the influence of point defect scattering on κlat suppression is weak due to the unique crystal structure. The crystal structure of Cu6GeTeS4−x consists of two distinct sublattices: a rigid framework formed by Te2− and a disordered sublattice with highly mobile Cu(2) and Cu(3) that exhibits liquid-like behavior. These Cu ions with liquid-like behavior act as the point defect centers, which have already greatly disturbed the normal transport of the high-frequency phonons. Thus, introducing S deficiencies has a weak influence on κlat.

To better reveal the decoupling between electrical and thermal transports of S deficiencies in Cu6GeTeS4−x, μW/κlat and quality factor B were calculated in Fig. 8C and fig. S17. It indicates that the x = 0.18 sample realizes synergistic optimization between thermal and electrical transport. With the low κlat, the x = 0.18 sample achieves the highest ZT of 1.23 at 925 K and ZTavg of 0.65 at 400 to 925 K in Fig. 8 (D and E). Compared to other p-type Cu-based argyrodites, Cu6GeTeS3.82 shows one of the highest performances among this system in Fig. 8F. Furthermore, we designed a segmented single-leg architecture to assess the actual power generation efficiency (ηmax). Under a temperature difference of 280 K, the segmented single-leg device exhibits a maximum conversion efficiency ηmax of ~2.45% in fig. S20D.

DISCUSSION

In summary, the cubic p-type argyrodite compound Cu6GeTeS4 was synthesized with F4¯3m space group by a chemical-tailoring strategy using the classical Cu8GeS6 as a template. The reduced multiplicity of Cu crystallographic sites in Cu6GeTeS4 results in excellent thermal stability without phase transition and enhanced thermoelectric performance, which achieves a ZT value of ~0.62 for the intrinsic sample. The weak Cu─S/Te chemical bonds cause strong coupling between acoustic and local low-frequency optical phonons. Meanwhile, the cage-like [TeCu18]16+ clusters mainly contributed to vibrations at low frequencies, realizing low κlat. Last, introducing S deficiencies, the Cu6GeTeS3.82 achieves a ZTmax value of 1.23 at 925 K with moderate electrical transport of ~4.50 μW cm−1 K−2 and an ultralow κlat of ~0.25 W m−1 K−1. The conversion efficiency ηmax reaches 2.45% at ∆T = 280 K based on a single-leg device for Cu6GeTeS3.82. For the challenges in electro-thermal-chemical stability of Cu2X and Cu-based argyrodites, we innovatively applied the chemical-tailoring strategy, reducing the multiplicity of Cu crystallographic sites to decrease the long-range migration of Cu ions. This high-performance Cu6GeTeS4 fills a gap in p-type Cu-based liquid-like argyrodites with outstanding stability. The chemical-tailoring strategy holds substantial implications for the design of thermoelectric materials.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Synthesis

The starting materials Cu (shots, 99.99%), Ge (pieces, 99.99%), Te (pieces, 99.99%), and S (blocks, 99.999%) were stoichiometrically weighted and mixed according to the nominal ratio Cu6GeTeS4. The spontaneous crystallization processes were completed in the vacuum-sealed silica tubes in a tube furnace. The silica tubes were slowly heated to 1273 K over 20 hours, soaked at this temperature for 5 days, and slowly cooled to room temperature over 30 hours. Last, the high-quality crystals were obtained. Large-scale synthesized samples with nominal compositions were melted at 1373 K for 24 hours and then slowly cooled to room temperature over 10 hours.

Structural refinement and crystal data

The black high-quality single crystal was used for data collection on a Rigaku XtaLAB PRO II diffractometer and Rigaku XtaLAB Synergy R diffractometer (Mo Kα radiation, λ = 0.71073 Å) at 298 and 170 K. The crystal structures were determined by the intrinsic phasing methods using SHELXT and refined with anisotropic thermal parameters by full-matrix least squares fitting on F2 using SHELXL on the Olex2 program. The program PLATON was used to verify the possible symmetry structures without the higher symmetry structure suggested.

Powder x-ray diffraction

The phase purities were evaluated by a Rigaku MiniFlex600 powder x-ray diffractometer with Ni-filtered Cu Kα radiation operating at 40 kV and 15 mA at room temperature, with the 2θ ranging from 10° to 70°. The results show that the experimental patterns agree well with the theoretical ones simulated and derived from Crystallographic Information File (CIF) data. Rietveld analysis of the powder diffraction data was performed using FullProf software.

Densification

The fine powder of compounds was filled into a 12.7-mm diameter graphite die and densified using the SPS technique (SPS-211LX, Fuji Electronic Industrial Co., Ltd.) at 823 to 873 K for 5 min and under a uniaxial pressure of 40 to 45 MPa. The fully densified cylinder-shaped samples with a thickness of 7 to 9 mm were obtained.

Bandgap measurement

The optical bandgaps were evaluated at room temperature using fine powder. The spectra were collected in the wave number range of 4000 to 400 cm−1 with an IRAffinity-1S spectrometer. The optical bandgap was obtained from reflectance data using Kubelka-Munk equations: α/S′ = (1 − R)2/(2R), where R, α, and S′ are the reflectance, absorption, and scattering coefficients, respectively.

Differential thermal analysis

A STA449-F5 thermal analyzer was used to perform DTA and TG measurements on Cu8GeS6 and Cu6GeTeS4 samples (10 mg). The temperature was varied between 300 and 1173 K at a constant rate of 10 K/min under N2 conditions for heating.

SEM and TEM

A single-crystal Cu6GeTeS4 was characterized by SEM-EDS. The surface of Cu8GeS6 SPSed/cracked bulk samples was characterized using a Carl Zeiss Supra55 microscope at 10 kV. The TEM specimens were prepared via mechanical thinning (cutting and grinding) followed by low-temperature Ar-ion milling (4 kV, 0.5 hours until perforation and then 0.3 kV, 1 hour for final polishing). The high-angle annular dark field (HAADF), EDS, SAED, and HRTEM were performed using an FEI Talos F200i microscope operating at 200 kV.

Hall measurements

The cylinder-shaped samples of 12 mm with a thickness of 1 mm were tested in a homemade system. The carrier concentration nH was calculated from the Hall coefficient obtained by this equation, assuming a single carrier, n = 1/(e|RH|), where e is the electron charge, and RH is the Hall coefficient.

Electrical transport

The S and σ were tested in the 300 to 925 K range by a CTA instrument (CRYOALL) under a He atmosphere. The samples with a size of ~2.5 mm by 2.5 mm by 10 mm were used for measurement.

Thermal conductivity measurements

The thermal diffusivity D was measured from 300 to 925 K using the laser flash diffusivity technique under a N2 atmosphere on an LFA-467 instrument (Netzsch, Germany). The rectangular samples (~6 mm by 6 mm by 1 mm) were coated with a thin graphite layer to prevent the emissivity of the materials during the measurement. The κtot was obtained by the formula κtot = DCpρ, where Cp is calculated according to the Dulong-Petit law, Cp = 3R/M¯ (where R is the ideal gas constant, M¯ is the average molar mass of each element, and ρ is the density), and measured by Archimedes’ principle.

Speed of sound measurements

Longitudinal (νl) and transverse (νt) sound velocity measurements were tested for samples at room temperature on a wafer with dimensions of ~6.0 mm by 6.0 mm by 1.0 mm using an ultrasonic material characterization (UMS-200) detector with a transducer frequency of 5 MHz.

Raman measurements

Cu6GeTeS4 single crystal, Cu6GeTeS3.82 powder, Cu8GeS6 powder, and Cu2GeS3 powder were placed on an object slide, which was tested through a Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution spectrometer equipped with a charge-coupled device detector by a 532-nm laser, which was used to record the Raman spectra.

Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical impedance spectra and dc polarization of Cu6GeTeS3.82 and Cu8GeS6 were measured on a CHI 660E workstation (CH Instruments Inc., China). The frequency of ac impedance measurement ranges from 1,000,000 Hz to 0.1 Hz. The dc polarization was performed by applying a voltage of ~0.1 V with a constant current for 200 s. The samples have a thickness of ~1 to 1.4 mm and a diameter of ~5 to 5.5 mm.

Single-leg conversion efficiency test

The single-leg power generation efficiency is estimated using a Mini-PEM instrument (Advance Riko, Japan) under vacuum, with a hot-side temperature (Th) ranging from 303 to 553 K. The hot and cold sides of the single-leg devices were galvanized to form a Ni-based barrier layer using a nickel plating method, and copper wires were soldered with the Ag-based solder.

Theoretical calculations

The first-principles calculations based on the DFT were performed using the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package for electronic band structure and phonon dispersion. The generalized gradient approximation (74) with Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) was chosen for the determination of exchange correlation energy. Plane-wave basis sets with an energetic cutoff of 500 eV were used, achieving a total energy convergence of well below 10−5 eV. The special quasirandom structure (SQS) was used to simulate the crystal structure. The Cu(2) and Cu(3) sites were assumed to be fully occupied. The 35 different configurations of Cu6GeTeS4 without structural relaxation are shown in fig. S21 (1 to 35 configurations). Last, configuration-19 shows the lowest energy in fig. S22, which was chosen for the energy band structure calculation and phonon dispersion.
