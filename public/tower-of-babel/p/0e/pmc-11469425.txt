Relationship between time to clinical remission and relapse in adults with steroid-sensitive minimal change disease: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xiuyue Ye, Shenglan Gao, Kangyuan Shen, Zengzhi Xiao, Huafeng Liu, Qingjun Pan, Yongzhi Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMinimal change disease (MCD) is a common nephrotic syndrome that is usually steroid-sensitive and has high relapse rate. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between time to clinical remission and recurrence after the initial steroid therapy.MethodsAmong 305 adult patients diagnosed with MCD via light and electron microscopy, sensitive to steroids, and hospitalized for nephrotic syndrome in the Department of Nephrology of the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University in China, 88 were included in this retrospective cohort study. Cox regression analysis was performed with time to clinical remission and 24-hour urine protein quantification (24 hUTP), absolute basophil (BA) and basophil percentage (BA%) as independent variables. Independent variables with significant differences and the time to remission were used to construct a Cox regression model to exclude the influence of confounding factors. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was plotted according to the independent variable of time to clinical remission.ResultsNo significant differences were found between the relapse and non-relapse groups in terms of sex, age at onset, or prevalent hypertension. There were significant differences in time to clinical remission, 24 hUTP, BA and BA% between the relapse and non-relapse groups. The risk of recurrence was significantly higher in patients with clinical remission of 15–21, 22–28 and 29–56 days than in those who had clinical remission of 1–7 days. In addition, patients with clinical remission of >26.5 days had a significantly higher risk of recurrence than those in the other groups.ConclusionsOverall, the time of clinical remission is a potential factor for predicting the recurrence of steroid-sensitive MCD in adults.Keywords: Minimal change disease, steroid sensitive, urine protein, relapse

Introduction

Minimal change disease (MCD) is a common cause of idiopathic nephrotic syndrome. It is both a common primary nephrotic syndrome in children and one of the most common glomerular diseases in adults with primary nephrotic syndrome [1]. In Asian populations, the incidence of MCD patients is age-dependent, accounting for 10–15% of primary nephrotic syndrome in adults [2]. The principal drug treatment for MCD is steroids. The remission rate of these treated MCD patients is 80% [3]; however, patients are prone to relapse after remission [4]. The majority of adult patients with MCD exhibit clinical remission within a few weeks of steroid therapy. However, approximately 40–60% of these patients experience at least one relapse of proteinuria [5,6], and approximately 10–35% require complete dependence on steroids and other immunosuppressive agents [7,8]. Moreover, frequent relapses of MCD may result in disease evolution to focal and segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) over years, with a small number (5.3%) of MCD patients eventually progressing to the end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [9–14]. Thus, it is clinically important to identify predictive factors for MCD relapse.

Relapse in adult MCD patients is associated with various clinical features and pathological factors, including age [5,15–17], respiratory tract infections [18], estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [19], mesangial hyperplasia [15] and histological changes [20]. A retrospective cohort study in Japan showed that intravenous infusion of methylprednisolone during initial treatment in adult MCD patients can achieve earlier clinical remission and reduce the incidence of relapse, suggesting that time to clinical remission at initial treatment may be associated with relapse [21]. Another 5-year cohort study in Japan showed that 45.1% of patients with steroid-sensitive MCD and FSGS experienced recurrence with an inverted U-shaped relationship between time to clinical remission and relapse observed after adjusting for age and other clinically relevant factors [22]. However, a retrospective cohort study from the UK [8] reported that 76.5% steroid-sensitive MCD patients experienced relapse, with time to clinical remission positively correlated with age and negatively correlated with number of relapses (which may been confounded by age). In another retrospective cohort study in the UK, 98.1% patients with new-onset MCD achieved remission, of which 60.8% experienced relapsed [5]. However, in an unadjusted Cox proportional risk model, time to clinical remission as a continuous variable was not associated with the relapse rate. Based on these studies, the relationship between time to clinical remission and relapse in steroid-sensitive MCD adults remains controversial owing to differences in treatment regimens, ethnicity and environmental factors in different regions. Therefore, when evaluating the relationship between time to clinical remission and relapse, the influence of multiple factors should be comprehensively considered to more accurately evaluate patients’ prognosis.

This study is aimed to retrospectively analyse the relationship between baseline clinical data, time to clinical remission, treatment regimen and relapse in adult steroid-sensitive MCD patients by using the Mann–Whitney’s U-test, t-test, Chi-square test and Cox regression analysis. The results of the current study provide useful clinical information for early assessment of MCD relapse, timely intervention, and reduction of clinical relapse.

Methods

Study subjects

From January 2015 to December 2019, 305 patients hospitalized for nephrotic syndrome in the Department of Nephrology of the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University in China were diagnosed with MCD and underwent a renal biopsy. Among them, 121 patients were lost to follow-up, had insufficient baseline data, or were not followed up regularly; 88 of the remaining 184 patients met the inclusion criteria. Steroid treatment time served as a baseline and observation was continued for 3 years, or until patient relapse. The study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University (ethical approval number: PJ2017-082). Patients/participants provided written informed consent to participate in this study.

Inclusion criteria: Patients of ≥18 years who were hospitalized for nephrotic syndrome and underwent renal puncture biopsy for a definite MCD diagnosis from January 2015 to December 2019 in the Department of Nephrology of the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University. Sex and ethnicity were not restricted. Follow-up time ≥3 years. Availability of complete clinical data and necessary laboratory test results. Clinical remission within 8 weeks of administration of a standardized dose of steroid therapy.

Exclusion criteria: Patients with chronic or acute renal failure who required dialysis. Patients with severely combined cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, cerebral and other important organ diseases. Patients with secondary factors including malignancy (lymphoma, leukaemia, multiple myeloma and thymoma), active tuberculosis, hepatitis, syphilis and HIV infection. Combined rheumatic immune system diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, uveitis and connective tissue diseases, and other types of glomerular diseases including membranous nephropathy (MN), lupus nephritis (LN), IgA nephropathy (IgAN), membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN), mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis, endocapillary proliferative glomerulonephritis (EnPGN) and crescentic glomerulonephritis (GN).

Sample collection

General information of adult patients with steroid-sensitive MCD were collected, including sex, age, blood pressure and steroid treatment time.

Laboratory data at the initial consultation or before administration of steroid therapy including 24-hour urine protein quantification (24 hUTP), haemoglobin (HGB), white blood cell (WBC) count, absolute neutrophil (NE), absolute basophil (BA), basophil percentage (BA%), absolute eosinophil (EO), eosinophil percentage (EO%), serum albumin (ALB), blood creatinine (Scr), eGFR, serum uric acid (SUA), triglycerides (TGs), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), blood total cholesterol (CHOL), urea, complement 3 (C3), complement 4 (C4) and urine routine were collected.

Treatment conditions included clinical remission, relapse, time to clinical remission, and time to first relapse after clinical remission.

Outcome evaluation

Clinical remission: Complete remission was determined by 24 hUTP <0.3 g/day or a urinary protein to creatinine ratio of <300 mg/g (or <30 mg/mmol) after steroid therapy.

Relapse: 24 hUTP of ≥1.0 g/day or urine protein to creatinine ratio of ≥1.0 g/gCr, or urine protein level of ≥2 for two consecutive days or more days following clinical remission.

Non-relapse: No relapse was observed after clinical remission with steroid therapy until the end of the observation period.

Steroid sensitivity: Clinical remission achieved within 8 weeks of treatment with a standardized dose of steroids (prednisone 1 mg/kg/d, maximum dose 80 mg/d).

Study grouping

The included 88 patients were divided into relapse and non-relapse groups according to their status of relapse. All patients were further divided into 1–7, 8–14, 15–21, 22–28 and 29–56 days groups according to time to clinical remission.

Statistical methods

The statistical analysis of all data in this study was performed using SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Continuous variables belonged to normally distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and the t-test for independent samples was used for comparisons between two groups. Continuous variables that did not belong to normal distribution were expressed as the median and interquartile ranges, and the Mann–Whitney U-test was used for comparisons between two groups. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and proportions, and comparisons between the groups were made using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The analysis of differences between groups was performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by least significant difference (LSD) post hoc comparison tests.

The statistically significant variables were first screened out after single factor analysis and then the parameters (p < .05) were included in the Cox regression survival analysis model, and the variables that could be used to the Cox survival analysis model were drawn as receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Thereafter, the point corresponding to the maximum Youden index (YI = sensitivity − specificity − 1) was selected as the cutoff value, which was transformed into a dichotomous variable for Kaplan–Meier’s analysis to generate the cumulative recurrence risk map. p < .05 was considered statistically significant.

The results revealed a 1.06-fold rise in relapse risk with each additional day in time to clinical remission (p < .01).

Independent variables with significant differences and time to clinical remission were used to construct a Cox regression model to exclude the influence of confounding factors. Model 1 did not adjust for any covariates. Model 2 only adjusted for the effects of BA%. In model 3, independent variables including age, BA, BA%, EO, EO% and 24 hUTP, which had significant differences (p < .1) between relapse and the non-relapse groups (Table 1), were also included to avoid missing covariates that needed to be adjusted. During the observation period, 42 patients (47.70%) relapsed, of which 1 (5.26%), 3 (33.33%), 8 (53.33%), 12 (54.55%) and 18 (78.26%) achieved clinical remission on days 1–7, 8–14, 15–21, 22–28 and 29–56, respectively (Table 4). Even after adjusting for relevant laboratory influences (models 2 and 3), the risk of relapse remained significantly higher in the 15–21, 22–18 and 29–56 days groups than in the 1–7 days group.

Longer time to clinical remission increases relapse in MCD patients

The ROC curve was plotted according to the independent variable of time to clinical remission (Figure 1), and the analysis yielded an area under the curve for time to clinical remission of 0.812, standard error of 0.044, 95% confidence interval of 0.73–70.90, maximum Yordon index of 0.491 (and corresponding cutoff value of 26.5), sensitivity of 64.3%, specificity of 84.8%, and p < .01.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for time to clinical remission predicting relapse.

The time to remission was converted into a dichotomous variable according to the cutoff value and divided into a time to clinical remission <26.5 days group and a time to clinical remission >26.5 days group (Figure 2). Kaplan–Meier’s survival analysis was performed, and the cumulative relapse function was plotted. During the observation period, there were 15 (27.78%) relapses in the <26.5 days group and 27 (79.41%) relapses in the >26.5 days group. The relapse rate was significantly higher in the patients with time to clinical remission of >26.5 days (p < .05). The cumulative relapse rate was significantly higher for time to clinical remission >26.5 days group than in the other two groups.

Discussion

MCD is primarily treated with steroids, with approximately 70% of patients sensitive to steroids therapy. However, approximately 73% of patients with MCD relapse at least once, and approximately 28% experience multiple relapses [23]. Therefore, it is clinically important to find the potential risk factors for the prevention or reduction of MCD relapse.

In this study, the median time to clinical remission was 28.00 days (range: 20.00, 45.00 days) in the relapse group and 14.00 days (range: 1.00, 23.25) in the non-relapse group, with a significant difference between the two groups. Clinical remission time was an independent risk factor for relapse in adults with steroid-sensitive MCD. Kaplan–Meier’s survival analysis curves indicated a notable increase in relapse rate as time to clinical remission increased. The relapse rate was significantly higher in patients who did not meet the remission criteria for proteinuria after 26.5 days. As a result of prolonged clinical remission, persistently high levels of proteinuria lead to immunoglobulin or complement deposition, which activates inflammatory pathways leading to progressive glomerular and tubular interstitial injury caused by damaged podocytes. Podocyte injury also leaves exposed spaces in the glomerular basement membrane, which, if not repaired properly, can eventually lead to glomerular scarring [24]. This may account for the increased risk of relapse with prolonged remission. However, results from a UK retrospective cohort study [8] showed that time to clinical remission was negatively associated with the number of MCD relapses during the mean observation period, whereas age was positively associated with time to clinical remission. Another retrospective cohort study conducted in the UK [19] found that time to clinical remission as a continuous variable was not associated with the rate of MCD recurrence in an unadjusted Cox proportional risk model. A report from China showed that time to clinical remission in adults with steroid-sensitive MCD was not associated with recurrence [18]. However, confounding factors, such as age, sex and other laboratory indicators, were not excluded from these studies. In the three Cox proportional risk models constructed in this study, time to clinical remission was not associated with relapse in the 8–15 days group, which is consistent with the UK and China studies [12,13].

We also found that the risk of relapse gradually increased with time to clinical remission prolonged and showed a positive linear correlation with the relapse rate. The results of a multicentre cohort study in Japan [22] showed that the relapse rate was significantly higher for a clinical remission of 15–28 days than in the 3–7 days group, which is consistent with the results of the present study. In contrast, in this Japanese cohort study, there was a decreasing trend in the relapse rate in the 30–56 days group, with an inverted U-shaped relationship between time to clinical remission and relapse rate, which was associated with an increased proportion of cyclosporine (42.8%) and cyclophosphamide (35.70%) use in patients in the 30–56 days group in this study. Calcium-regulated neuro phosphatase inhibitors include cyclosporine and tacrolimus, which are the main agents used to treat patients with frequently recurrent or steroid-resistant MCD [25]. However, in this study, the vast majority of patients were predominantly treated with steroids therapy within 8 weeks of treatment initiation. The use of cytotoxic drugs in the 29–56 days group (39.13%) was significantly lower than that in the Japanese cohort study. Therefore, the opposite recurrence rate of these two studies may be related to the above reasons.

Basophils (BA) are implicated in the initiation of Th2 response by producing Th2 cytokines [26]. Pirotzky et al. [27] and Mack and Rosenkranz [28] reported that BA might participate in pathological events in MCD. Moreover, we recently showed that the number and activation of peripheral BA were increased in adult-onset MCD, indicating that BA may play an important role in the pathogenesis of adult-onset MCD, and that elevated number and activation of peripheral BA could predict adult MCD recurrence [29]. In this study, we observed higher BA, and BA% in the relapse group compared with the non-relapse group, suggesting that the number and activation of BA in MCD patients is closely associated with disease relapse.

This study had several limitations. First, it did not include information on the specific steroid increases or decreases in patients during the observation period. The adjustment of steroid dosage for each patient after clinical remission varied based on individual clinical symptoms, potentially introducing confounding factors to the relationship between time to clinical remission and relapse rate due to varying steroid regimens. In addition to individual differences in steroid regimens, 32 patients (36.36%) in this study used renin–angiotensin system (RAS) blockers, which may have biased the results because RAS blockers reduce proteinuria [30]. Second, there was no strict standardization of patient follow-up interval, method or interval of urine retention for analysis, which may have led to bias in the analysis. Finally, inter-individual differences in steroid pharmacokinetics may have influenced the relationship between time to clinical remission and relapse rate.

In conclusion, our results show that adult patients with MCD and clinical remission of >26.5 days had a higher risk of recurrence.
