Examining the influence of tumor-infiltrating macrophages on breast cancer outcomes and identifying relevant genes for diagnostic purposes
Authors: Xiaoyun Zhang, Cheng Peng, Xuesong Xiong, Jianchun Lian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe purpose of this research was to investigate how different types of immune cells impact the outlook of individuals with breast cancer, as well as identify the essential genes associated with immune cell subtype enrichment.MethodsThe Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database were used to obtain global transcriptome sequencing data sets of breast tissue. The study utilized the CIBERSORT algorithm to determine the presence of 22 different types of immune cells in both breast cancer tissue and normal breast tissue.Immune cell infiltration content was utilized to conduct univariate COX analysis in order to identify risk factors linked to breast cancer prognosis.ResultsUnivariate COX analysis indicates that Macrophages M1 and B cells naive are beneficial factors for the outlook of individuals with breast cancer (P < 0.05), while Macrophages M2 and Monocytes are detrimental factors for the prognosis of breast cancer patients (P < 0.05). The high infiltration group of macrophage M2 had a poorer prognosis compared to the low infiltration group (P < 0.001); Conversely, the high infiltration group of macrophage M1 had a better prognosis than the low infiltration group (P = 0.002).ConclusionThe study provided an overview of immune cell infiltration in breast cancer tissues, identifying macrophage M1 and macrophage M2 as potential factors in breast cancer development and progression. Additionally, genes associated with macrophage phenotype were analyzed, offering insights into macrophage polarization mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01397-z.

Introduction

Breast cancer is a highly heterogeneous tumor in terms of morphology, molecular expression profiles, and clinical progression [1]. Despite most breast cancers being invasive ductal carcinoma, there is significant variability in their molecular expression profiles [2, 3]. Breast cancer is commonly categorized molecularly in clinical settings using estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER-2), and Ki-67 proliferation index [4, 5]. It is essential to customize additional treatment plans according to the molecular subtypes of breast cancer in order to provide personalized precision therapy [4, 5]. For example, the majority of patients with ER-positive and PR-positive tumors experience positive outcomes with established hormonal treatments, whereas HER2-positive patients have seen significant improvements in survival rates thanks to targeted therapies like trastuzumab [6]. Nevertheless, certain individuals with breast cancer do not respond to these treatments, referred to as treatment-resistant breast cancer, encompassing triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), endocrine therapy-resistant breast cancer, and trastuzumab-resistant breast cancer [7]. Solid tumors have seen significant advancements in immunotherapy in the past few years [8]. It is crucial to discover specific targets for immunotherapy in treating breast cancer and improving patient outcomes, especially in TNBC [8]. This highlights the importance of investigating the immune microenvironment and key challenges in immunotherapy for TNBC.

Within breast cancer tissue, not only do cancer cells themselves exhibit high heterogeneity, but there also exists a complex tumor immune microenvironment [9, 10]. The “seed and soil” theory proposed by MATHOT et al. [11] regarding the relationship between tumor cells and their surrounding environment has broadened the focus of tumor treatment from just the tumor to include innovative therapeutic approaches that involve modifying and disrupting the tumor microenvironment [12]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) make up more than half of the solid tumor composition, surpassing other immune cells in number [13]. TAMs are involved in all stages of breast cancer progression and spread, showing promise in forecasting disease-free survival and overall survival rates among individuals with breast cancer [14]. Macrophages exhibit different phenotypes, which may play distinct roles in breast cancer. M1 macrophages are believed to inhibit tumor progression, while M2 macrophages may promote tumor progression [15–17]. The process by which macrophages become polarized is still not well understood. Understanding how macrophages become polarized can lead to the identification of new targets for immunotherapy and provide fresh perspectives on categorizing breast cancer. This study aims to showcase the various types of immune cell infiltration in breast cancer tissue, investigate how different types of macrophages affect patients' outcomes, and pinpoint the crucial genes and markers that influence macrophage polarization.

Research materials and methods

Data acquisition

The study acquired an extensive transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) dataset of breast tissue by retrieving information from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. The dataset contained 1091 breast cancer tissue samples and 113 normal breast tissue samples. The data is presented in FPKM format. We have converted the gene names in the gft3 file. The clinical data files for breast cancer tissue were obtained, and important clinical details like age, gender, tumor stage, grade, and survival duration were collected and arranged. The data that was downloaded was freely accessible and open to the public, therefore it did not need to be approved by the local ethics committee. We downloaded transcriptome sequencing data of breast cancer samples from the GEO database as validation cohort (GSE103091), corresponding to the platform file GPL570. The GSE103091 cohort contained 238 breast cancer samples. We used R software and R package (limma) for data normalization. Relative expression = log2 (FPKM + 1).

Calculation of immune cell infiltration

In this study, the CIBERSORT algorithm was utilized to assess the levels of infiltration of 22 different types of immune cells in breast cancer tissue and normal breast tissue. The CIBERSORT algorithm incorporates a feature matrix constructed from microarray data, encompassing various immune cell types and their functional states. It models the gene expressions of tissue samples as weighted summations of cell expression profiles within the population mixture.

Building of protein–protein interaction (PPI) network

The PPI network in this study was built using data from the STRING database. STRING is a database that offers information on both confirmed and anticipated interactions between proteins, encompassing both direct and indirect connections. The interactions stem from computational forecasts, exchange of knowledge among organisms, and compilation of interactions from various databases.

Selection of genes with correlation analysis

In this study, the correlation between genes and immune cell subtypes was demonstrated using Pearson’s test. Genes showing a correlation coefficient |Cor|> 0.3 and a significance level of P < 0.05 were chosen as linked to M2 macrophages. Likewise, genes that had a correlation coefficient |Cor| greater than 0.5 and a significance level of P less than 0.05 were chosen as linked to M1 macrophages. We use the R language and the R package (psych) for correlation calculations.

Statistical analysis

The data in this study was analyzed statistically with the R software and appropriate R packages. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the levels of immune cell infiltration in normal breast tissue samples with those in breast cancer tissue samples. The study utilized the Kaplan–Meier technique for survival analysis, along with the Log-rank test. Prognostic factors linked to breast cancer were identified by using Cox regression analysis, focusing on levels of immune cell infiltration. The correlation analysis between the 22 immune cell subtypes was conducted using Pearson’s test. A statistical significance was considered when the P-value was less than 0.05.

Survival analysis based on immune cell content

Based on the ideal threshold values identified for the four immune cell infiltrates mentioned earlier, the samples were categorized into two groups: those with high levels of immune cell infiltration (exceeding the median value) and those with low levels of immune cell infiltration (below the median value). Kaplan–Meier survival plots were generated and log-rank tests were conducted as appropriate.

The outlook for the high Monocytes infiltration group was worse compared to the low infiltration group (P = 0.017). Similarly, the high infiltration group of Macrophages M2 had a poorer prognosis compared to the low infiltration group (P < 0.001). Conversely, the high infiltration group of Macrophages M1 exhibited a better prognosis than the low infiltration group (P = 0.002). Furthermore, the high infiltration group of B cells naive showed a better prognosis compared to the low infiltration group (P < 0.010), as shown in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Analysis of immune cell infiltration and survival

Discussion

In our study, we presented the proportions of immune cell subtypes within the immune microenvironment in all patients. Prior research has shown that tumor-infiltrating macrophages (TIMs) make up more than half of the cellular makeup of solid tumors [12]. Our study revealed that the presence of Macrophages M0 and Macrophages M1 was elevated in breast cancer tissues in contrast to normal breast tissues, whereas Macrophages M2 were less prevalent in breast cancer tissues. Furthermore, it was found that a greater presence of Macrophages M1 is linked to a positive outlook for individuals with breast cancer, while a higher presence of Macrophages M2 is seen as a negative factor for prognosis. Macrophages M0, Macrophages M1, and Macrophages M2 are unique stages of macrophages [18], with Macrophages M1 and Macrophages M2 arising from Macrophages M0 [19, 20]. Macrophages, a diverse group of cells, display distinct characteristics and roles within the intricate environment of the body [21, 22]. Macrophages exist in a continuum of functional states, with M1 and M2 macrophages representing two extremes of this continuum. M1 macrophages release inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, excel in displaying antigens, contribute to beneficial immune reactions, and are involved in monitoring the immune system. Conversely, M2 macrophages exhibit a diminished ability to present antigens and play a role in immune regulation through the release of inhibitory cytokines like IL-10 or TGF-B, which reduce immune reactions [23–26]. Our research findings are consistent with the idea that M1 macrophages contribute to a positive immune response and lead to a better prognosis, whereas M2 macrophages are linked to negative immune reactions and worse outcomes.

Within the tumor microenvironment, M1 and M2 macrophages undergo a continuous shift in their characteristics. TAMs transition from M1, which possesses tumor-killing functions, to M2, which exhibits tissue repair functions, greatly undermining the immune system's ability to eliminate tumors [27]. The shift from M1 to M2 not only impairs the natural detection and engulfing abilities of macrophages but also suppresses the tumor-fighting functions of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells that work together with macrophages. Moreover, it has the ability to stimulate Treg cells and helper T cells, resulting in the suppression of tumor immunity. M2 macrophages exhibit intricate characteristics and roles that could aid in tissue healing, the formation of new blood vessels in tumors, the phagocytosis of dying cells, and the inhibition of immune reactions, along with various other biological functions [28–31]. Studying the roles of macrophages in various physiological and pathological situations is highly important. However, there is currently no widely recognized phenotypic marker to identify and differentiate different types of macrophages, and the phenotypic characteristics of M1 and M2 macrophages remain inconclusive. After conducting correlation screening, we identified 15 genes that exhibit a positive correlation with M1 macrophages and a negative correlation with M2 macrophages. This may help in distinguishing macrophage characteristics and understanding the processes that drive macrophage activation.

Breast cancer heterogeneity is the determining factor that causes patients to have different clinical phenotypes. Macrophage polarization is one aspect of breast cancer heterogeneity. Some previous studies have explained the influencing factors of breast cancer heterogeneity from different perspectives. It has been confirmed that gel proteins may participate in the enrichment of immune cell subtypes and affect the shaping of immune microenvironment [32]. Filamentous proteins can promote migration, invasion and attachment of breast cancer cells [33]. TUBA1B affects some cancers by increasing tumor mutation burden, microsatellite instability, neoantigen formation, immune cell infiltration, and immune checkpoint regulation [34]. In addition, different breast cancer phenotypes have different drug resistance. Research on breast cancer needs to be carried out from multiple angles and in all aspects. While attaching importance to the characteristics of the immune microenvironment, it can combine other factors to describe the phenotype of breast cancer more comprehensively [35, 36].

This study still has some limitations. Firstly, we did not explore the roles of the identified key genes in the two macrophage phenotypes. Secondly, CIBERSORT algorithm is a classical algorithm that can quantify immune cell infiltration. Other algorithms, such as ssGSEA algorithm and xCell algorithm, can also quantify immune cells. But CIBERSORT is an algorithm that can distinguish between different subtypes of macrophages. The results of data analysis must be validated from multiple perspectives. The best way to verify this is probably through experiments. Our study lacks validation using clinical samples, cellular-level studies, and animal models. We still need to carry out continuous experimental studies to confirm our conclusions. Thirdly, the immune microenvironment is one of the essential attributes of tumor and is the entry point to describe tumor heterogeneity. Different histological subtypes of breast cancer may have different immune microenvironment characteristics. This is a direction worth exploring in depth. Characterization of the immune microenvironment in breast cancer samples with different histological subtypes may lead to more specific conclusions.

In conclusion, our study offered a broad look at the patterns of immune cell infiltration in breast cancer tissues, pinpointing the significance of Macrophages M1 and Macrophages M2 in the development and advancement of breast cancer. We examined genes linked to the two different macrophage characteristics, offering insights into recognizing macrophage traits and understanding macrophage polarization processes.
