Comprehensive assessment of fungal peroxygenases for the selective synthesis of 25-hydroxyvitamins D2 and D3

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe metabolite 25-hydroxyvitamin D is significantly better for clinical use in several diseases and food supplementations than vitamin D (calciferol) itself. The present study reports an efficient biotechnological method for the synthesis of 25-hydroxyvitamin D2 and 25-hydroxyvitamin D3, in one step, starting from the readily available and comparatively inexpensive vitamin D2 (from fungi and yeast) and vitamin D3 (from animals).ResultsA widespread investigation of a series of up to 71 unspecific peroxygenases (UPOs), including UPO samples from 41 fungal species (together with several isozymes and variants) expressed in Komagataella phaffii (formerly Pichia pastoris), has been performed for the regioselective C25-hydroxylation of vitamin D (D2 and D3). First experiments revealed that 42 UPOs were able to transform vitamin D2 into its 25-hydroxy derivative within a wide range of conversion rates (from 5% to > 99%), with strict regioselectivity in most cases, determined using GC–MS. Background

Vitamin D is a secosteroid compound derived from a broken B-ring steroid skeleton. It plays important roles in health, and its deficiency or insufficiency is accompanied with an increased risk of a plethora of disorders and illnesses such as rickets, osteomalacia, preeclampsia, dental pathologies, cancers, cardiovascular, and autoimmune diseases [1, 2]. In nature it is found in two forms predominantly, vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol) and vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol), formed from ergosterol (in fungi and yeast) and 7-dehydrocholesterol (in animals), respectively (Fig. 1) [3].

Conversion of ergosterol to vitamin D2 (top) and 7-dehydrocholesterol to vitamin D3 (bottom)

Nevertheless, vitamin D is a prodrug that needs to be transformed in vivo into active forms. These biologically active forms are hydroxylated metabolites at C-25 and C-1 produced by several cytochrome P450s. In vertebrates, vitamin D is metabolized in the liver producing 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OH-D), the most abundant metabolite circulating in blood, and then it is subsequently hydroxylated in the kidneys to form 1α,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (1,25-di-OH-D) which is less abundant but much more active [4]. Besides, 25-OH-D is easier absorbed than vitamin D and also its half-life is significantly longer (including 1,25-di-OH-D) [5] becoming it in a convenient candidate for food supplementations [6, 7] and clinical uses in patients with malabsorption of vitamin D or liver diseases [4]. In addition, the use of 25-OH-D as feed additive for poultry [8, 9] and swine [10] nutrition is more and more frequent. Although in most cases the 25-OH-D used for these purposes is produced from vitamin D3, the development of new procedures for the synthesis of the D2 form could be interesting to obtain the corresponding metabolite from non-animal (vegan) sources since ergosterol is industrially produced from yeasts [11].

Therefore, there is a growing interest in developing sustainable and efficient methods to synthesize 25-OH-D. Several approaches of chemical synthesis from vitamins D2 and D3 are currently used, which involve tedious and large routes to construct the side-chain and negatively impact the overall yield [12–16]. Biocatalytic synthesis based on whole-cell systems (fungal and bacterial strains) is also extensively studied, in particular for producing 25-OH-D3 in a greener and more efficient approach. However, although this strategy improves the conversion of the process, the substrate load is limited and linked to the production of the microbial strain [17–20]. Most recently, enzymatic synthesis using hydroxylases from microbial sources appears as an alternative to chemical and whole-cell syntheses. P450 monooxygenases stand out as one of the most used enzymes but the need of expensive cofactors and a relatively low stability derived from their intracellular nature limit their applicability [21–23]. Remarkably, steroid C25 dehydrogenases, a subfamily of molybdenum enzymes, efficiently produce 25-OH-D3 in high yields with the added-value of using K3[Fe(CN6)] instead of NAD(P)H as electron source. Regrettably, the anaerobic conditions necessary for the catalytic activity of these enzymes and the associated costs hinder their industrial exploitation [24, 25]. Despite the progress achieved in biocatalytic synthesis, to date most vitamins with the exception of vitamins C, B2, and B12 are still manufactured by chemical methods [26].

In this context, unspecific peroxygenases (UPOs, EC. 1.11.2.1), the so-called “dream biocatalysts,” have become promising candidates for the oxyfunctionalization of added-value compounds of industrial interest due to their advantages such as the use of hydrogen peroxide or organic hydroperoxides as the only cosubstrate, a high stability relative to their extracellular nature, and a great tolerance to certain organic solvents [27]. However, up to now only a few studies on enzymatic hydroxylation of vitamin D have been reported, which were mostly focused on the wild-type UPO from Agrocybe aegerita (AaeUPO, isolated from cultures of the native fungus), or its laboratory evolved variant PaDa-I [28–32] since the widespread use of UPOs in the field of biocatalysis was hindered by their arduous heterologous production, considerably limiting the panel of enzymes available for investigation and enzyme engineering. Fortunately, very recently, the heterologous expression of UPOs has been successfully established and improved in different recombinant hosts improving their production and enabling the access to engineered variants [33–37]. Particularly, Komagataella phaffii (formerly Pichia pastoris), a widely used yeast platform for recombinant protein production ‒ including high-yield secretory expression of industrial enzymes ‒ has been firmly established as a favorable host for recombinant UPO (rUPO) production in recent years. So far, more than 60 different UPO sequences (short- and long-type enzymes) have been recombinantly produced using K. phaffii [33, 37–40] with secretory protein amounts up to 742 mg/L published for short-type UPOs [41].

Taking advantage of these advances in enzyme expression, in the present work we evaluate for the first time a panel of up to 71 UPO samples produced in K. phaffii, for the selective hydroxylation of vitamin D2 and vitamin D3. Next, the hydroxylation of both vitamin D forms is upscaled with a representative UPO using two different procedures. Several reaction parameters such as cosolvent, solubilizing additive, enzyme dose, and reaction time were optimized to achieve high conversions and regioselectivity. All the UPO reactions with vitamins D2 and D3 were analyzed by GC–MS.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Vitamins D2 and D3 as well as their C-25 hydroxylated metabolites (25-OH-D2 and 25-OH-D3) were acquired from Merck. Their corresponding C-1 hydroxylated metabolites (1-OH-D2 and 1-OH-D3) were acquired from TCI and the calcitriol derivatives (1,25-di-OH-D2 and 1,25-di-OH-D3) were purchased from Alfa. The partially methyl and 2-hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin (MCD and HPCD) were purchased from Merck and the sulfobutylether-β-cyclodextrin (SBECD) from Targetmol. Hydrogen peroxide solution (35%) was acquired from Panreac. The reducing agents, namely, ascorbic acid (AscA), gallic acid (GA), and pyrogallol (PG), were obtained from Merck. The derivatizing agent N,O-bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (BSTFA) was also purchased from Merck. The organic solvents (GC or HPLC grade) were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without further purification.

Enzymes

Expression of enzyme sequences was done as described previously, and information on all tested UPOs is given in Table 1. Bioreactor scale UPO production was adapted from Rotilio et al. [37, 42] employing a fed-batch bioprocess and lyophilization of the resulting enzyme-containing supernatant. The bioprocess was downscaled employing a small-scale parallel bioreactor system (DASBox, Eppendorf) with a working volume of 250 mL and using the batch and fed-batch medium described by Maurer et al. [43]. Enzyme concentrations were estimated by UV–Vis spectroscopy from the reduced-UPO adducts with CO [44].

Enzymatic reactions

Screening of 71 UPOs for vitamin D hydroxylation

The reactions were conducted with 0.05 mM of vitamin D, 4 µM of UPO and 2.5 mM of H2O2 in 1 mL of phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7) containing acetone as cosolvent (40% v/v). For this purpose, stock solutions of vitamins D (5 mM) were freshly prepared in acetone. An aliquot of the corresponding vitamin D solution (10 µl, 50 nmol) was added to the buffer/acetone mixture followed by the UPO solutions (4 nmol). The resulting solutions were stirred at 30 ºC and the H2O2 solution (25 µl, 100 mM) was gently added with a syringe pump over 50 min, and then for a further 10 min. The reactions were extracted with methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) and derivatized with BSTFA/pyridine for GC–MS analyses. Additionally, reactions with HspUPO using calcidiols as substrates to investigate the formation of calcitriols were conducted under the same conditions. Control experiments were performed without enzyme. The reactions were mostly single-point determinations, although some replicates were performed in some selected cases, and a deviation lower than 10% was produced in all cases.

Scale-up of vitamin D hydroxylation with HspUPO

For the scale-up reactions two different cosubstrates were evaluated, namely, H2O2 and O2/reductant. Three reductants, namely, ascorbic acid, gallic acid, and pyrogallol, were tested. Stock solutions of vitamins D (100 mM) and cyclodextrins (50% p/v) were freshly prepared in acetone and sodium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7), respectively. In both procedures, the appropriate aliquot of the corresponding vitamin D solution was dried under nitrogen stream and redissolved in sodium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7) containing the cyclodextrins (MCD, HPCD or SBECD) and the cosolvent (Tables S3, 2 and 3). The UPO solution was added and the mixture was stirred and heated at 30 ºC for 10 min to ensure the solubilization of the substrate. When H2O2 was used, the UPO solution was added in a split dose and the second one was added after 2 h. Besides, the H2O2 solution was added using a syringe pump over 4 h and then, the reaction mixtures were stirred under the same conditions for a further 20 h. When O2/reductant was used, the UPO solution was added in a single dose. In all cases, the total volume was 250 µL. The reactions were extracted with MTBE and derivatized with BSTFA/pyridine for GC–MS analyses. Control experiments were performed without enzyme. Reactions at substrate concentrations higher than 10 mM were carried out in duplicates and a deviation lower than 5% was observed in all cases.

GC–MS analyses

GC–MS analyses were performed using a Shimadzu GC–MS QP2010 Ultra equipment, with a DB-5HT (15 m × 0.25 mm i.d. and 0.1 μm film thickness) capillary column. Helium was used as carrier gas at a flow of 0.70 mL min−1. The injector was set at 300 °C, and the oven was programmed starting from 150 °C (1 min) to 200 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1 and then to 290 ºC (7 min) at a rate of 20 °C min−1. The transfer line was set at 300 °C and the ion source at 280 ºC. Compounds were identified as a pair of pyroisomers by mass spectrometry and/or with authentic standards, and the quantification was carried out from total-ion peak summed areas.

Results and discussion

Assessment of UPOs in the hydroxylation of vitamins D2 and D3

Up to 71 UPO samples, which included enzymes from 41 different fungal species, and several isozymes and variants from some of these species, produced with K. phaffii (Table 1), were tested with vitamin D2 (D2) and the results were evaluated using GC–MS (Fig. S1, Table S2). First, to assess the high number of UPO samples we assayed short-term reactions (1 h) with low substrate loading (0.05 mM), 4 µM UPO and 2.5 mM of H2O2. The GC–MS results (Fig. 2) revealed that 42 UPOs out of 71 transformed D2 into its hydroxylated derivative at C25 position with strict regioselectivity in most cases.

Vitamin D2 (0.05 mM) conversion (%) by several UPOs (4 µM) and 2.5 mM H2O2 within 1 h, and relative percentage of products

Five UPOs, namely, AaeUPO, CabUPO-III, HpuUPO, HspUPO, and HspUPO_18G3, achieved a very high substrate conversion (≥ 90%) with strict regioselectivity toward C25 position, with the exception of AaeUPO and CabUPO-III. Seven UPOs, namely, CmaUPO-I, CmaUPO-II, DspUPO-I, HpuUPO-I, HspUPO_V1, HspUPO_V2, and HspUPO_20H11, reached high conversions (82%–88%), also with strict regioselectivity toward C25. Five UPOs, namely, AsoUPO, DbiUPO-IV, HspUPO_8G3, HspUPO_23E12, and HspUPO_20H3, attained moderate to good conversions (69%–77%) and strict regioselectivity. The rest of UPOs exhibited low or very low conversions of D2.

We first tested all UPO samples with D2 assuming that the enzymes showing good conversions with this vitamin will achieve at least similar performance with D3, due to the less bulky side-chain structure in the latter (absence of methyl group C28), as previously observed [28, 29] using rCciUPO and AaeUPO. Thereafter, in view of the results, we selected 16 UPO samples, which showed ≥ 80% vitamin D2 conversion or > 70% conversion together with very high regioselectivity, and tested them with vitamin D3 (D3). The reactions were analyzed by GC–MS (Fig. S2, Table S3). Overall, these UPOs also transformed D3 with high conversion in most cases (> 80%) and yielded different amounts of the desired product, 25-OH-D3 (Fig. 3).

Vitamin D3 (0.05 mM) conversion (%) by several UPOs (4 µM) and 2.5 mM H2O2 within 1 h, and relative percentage of products

Unlike with D2, these enzymes demonstrated higher promiscuity with D3, since they promoted its hydroxylation at different positions, and delivered di- and tri-hydroxylated compounds and other products, presumably derived from overoxidation processes. This different behavior may be related to the presence in D2 of a methyl group in C-24 and a double bond in C-22–C-23 that causes a steric hindrance that limits the formation of possible conformers.

Although in several reactions, the position of the additional hydroxy groups is unclear, calcitriol (C-1,25 hydroxylation) has been identified among the regioisomers in half of the reactions. Nevertheless, HspUPO and CabUPO-III were the two best UPOs, in terms of regioselectivity toward 25-OH-D3, which was completely strict in the case of HspUPO, and the two UPOs that showed more similar performance with D3 and D2 (Fig. 4). Despite the existence of many more oxygenated derivatives of D3, only a few are reported to have a clear biological functionality, in addition to the well-known 1,25-di-OH-D3 derivative. Therefore, 21,25-di-OH-D3 has been reported to have a preferential activity in mobilizing calcium from the bone, while 25,26-di-OH-D3 appears to have its greatest effect in stimulating intestinal calcium transport [47]. On the other hand, 24,25-di-OH-D3 is commonly considered as biomarker of vitamin D degradation. Additionally, some evidences of its role in bone formation and lower bone fracture risk are reported [48]. According to this, the unidentified byproducts obtained in our reactions might have similar or resembling activities, although a complete characterization of their structures together with the biological evaluation would be required.

GC–MS analyses of UPO reactions (as TMS derivatives) with vitamin D2 (top) and vitamin D3 (bottom) catalyzed by HspUPO (left, A and C) and CabUPO-III (right, B and D) producing 25-hydroxyvitamins D (25-OH-D2 and D3) as the main product. The isopyro (left) and pyro (right) isomers from secosteroid thermal rearrangement are obtained

Although calcitriol (1,25-di-OH-D) was not identified in high amount in most of the reactions with either vitamin D2 or D3, these bioactive compounds are easily accessible starting from the corresponding calcidiols (1-OH-D), as previously described with AaeUPO [49]. In this case, both calcitriols were successfully prepared in near quantitative conversion using HspUPO and calcidiols as substrates (Fig. 5 and S3).

GC–MS analyses of UPO reactions (as TMS derivatives) with 1-hydroxyvitamin D2 (left) and 1-hydroxyvitamin D3 (right) and HspUPO that yielded the corresponding calcitriols. The isopyro (left) and pyro (right) isomers from secosteroid thermal rearrangement are obtained

Scale-up of 25-hydroxyvitamin D2 and D3 synthesis with HspUPO

One of the main drawbacks for the industrial upscaling of the bio-catalyzed hydroxylation of vitamin D (enzymatic as well as whole-cell transformations) is related to the substrate loading. In fact, to the best of our knowledge there are only two approaches of upscaling the hydroxylation of both forms of vitamin D with UPOs. Using rAaeUPO (or variants), Huang et al. [31] reported excellent conversion and regioselectivity in the hydroxylation of D3 and moderate regioselectivity in the case of D2 but with low substrate loading (5 and 2 mM, respectively) despite scaling-up the reaction volume. Moreover, Xu and coworkers [30] tried to circumvent the moderate regioselectivity of rAaeUPO with the regioselective hydroxylation of 7-dehydrocholesterol followed by a photocatalyzed conversion (to pre-vitamin D3) and a subsequent thermal isomerization to 25-OH-D3. However, this creative approach depleted the overall yield.

On the other hand, to accomplish efficient and economically feasible application of UPOs as industrial biocatalysts, sufficient amounts of high-quality recombinant enzyme need to be available, making production host as well as specific enzyme sequence a critical choice. In this sense, K. phaffii has not only proven to be an excellent host for rUPO production [38], but is also a widespread “industrial workhorse” for the production of recombinant proteins and industrial enzymes, making the technology and expertise for scaling-up protein production readily available. Regarding enzyme choice, especially in combination with producibility, the application of short UPOs is more favorable, as recombinant production yields are typically higher [37, 40].

In this context, aimed at improving the previous results for scaling-up the hydroxylation of vitamin D, several reaction parameters such as cosolvent, solubilizing additive, enzyme dose, and reaction time were optimized to achieve high conversions and regioselectivity. The short UPO HspUPO was selected for both, good conversion and regioselectivity in the hydroxylation of D2 and D3, and the previously detailed general robustness and straightforward recombinant production (HspUPO was produced at around 0.2 g/L in a standard not optimized bioprocess [42]) ‒ especially compared to previously applied UPOs like rAaeUPO. Two approaches using different UPO cosubstrates were investigated as described below.

Scale-up using peroxide

In preliminary experiments with D2, we started to increase the substrate loading from 0.05 mM to 1 mM, using higher concentration of acetone (50% v/v, Table S4), since acetone had been proved to be one of the best organic solvents in terms of UPO stability, and besides, it is not substrate for UPOs [50]. However, the solubility of D2 decreased at concentration above 1 mM so we opted to investigate β-cyclodextrins, namely, methyl-(MCD), 2-hydroxypropyl (HPCD) and sulfobutyl-β-cyclodextrin (SBECD), as solubilizing agents to improve the D2 solubilization. First, we tested MCD whose addition allow us to dissolve 1 mM of D2 using only 20% acetone, and better conversion degrees were observed (up to 70% conversion, Table S4, entry 5) within 4 h, as the UPO dose increased. Besides, longer reaction times, up to 24 h and the split dosage of the UPO had a positive effect showing similar conversions to those of the screening and using the same enzyme/substrate ratio (89% conversion, Table S4, entry 7). We also explored the use of HPCD as an alternative to MCD but no conversion of vitamin D was produced.

Being aware of the high acetone concentration used and the potential risk of explosion of certain combinations of acetone and hydrogen peroxide[51], we explored the use of iso-propanol (iPrOH) as cosolvent (Table 2). In this sense, using only 1% iPrOH in the presence of 10% MCD, the reactions at 5 mM of D2 worked with high conversions (86%, Table 2, entry 2). It should be noted that this conversion was achieved even using the same UPO dose than 1 mM reactions (80 µM UPO, 8 mol% UPO) representing a fivefold reduction of enzyme (1.6 mol% UPO). Then, we investigated the use of HPCD and SBECD as solubilizing agents, but the D2 conversions depleted dramatically (to 13% and 10%, respectively, entries 3 and 4).

Next, using again MCD (10%) under different conditions (entries 5–8) the reaction was successfully upscaled to 10 mM D2 loading after increasing the UPO and peroxide concentrations to 120 µM and 100 mM, respectively, achieving 85% of both D2 conversion and 25-OH-D2 formation.

In reactions at 15 mM D2, the conversions decreased in the presence of 10% MCD and 1% iPrOH even when using higher UPO doses (50%–70% conversion, entries 9–12). These results were improved using 20% MCD, 2% iPrOH and 160 µM UPO, and 86% D2 conversion (5.32 g/L of 25-OH-D2, entry 14) was attained. These results evidenced the importance of D2 solubilization for a successful reaction. Moreover, we also tested D3 hydroxylation under the same conditions, and 75% conversion (4.51 g/L of 25-OH-D3, entry 17) was achieved.

Finally, we tried to optimize the scaling-up of both reactions increasing the substrate (D2 and D3) loading up to 20 mM, and different results between D2 and D3 were obtained. On one hand, D2 was converted with high yield (88%), while maintaining the same MCD, UPO, and peroxide concentrations as in 15 mM reactions (7.26 g/L of 25-OH-D2, entry 15). This represents a tenfold reduction of UPO dose (0.8 mol% per mol of D2) compared with the screening conditions. Besides, the increasing of MCD and iPrOH did not improve the conversion, producing a slight reduction to 79% (entry 16). On the other hand, a significant decline in substrate conversion was observed in D3 reactions (entries 18–21) that only was partially mitigated by the use of higher concentrations of UPO and solubilizing agents (200 µM UPO, 30% MCD, and 3% iPrOH, entry 22) obtaining 63% 25-OH-D3 (5.04 g/L).

Scale-up using O2/reductants

Aimed to improve or provide an alternative for scaling-up the synthesis of 25-OH-D, we decided to use a different UPO-activation procedure, using oxygen and certain reductants-vicinal diols such as ascorbic acid (AscA), gallic acid (GA), and pyrogallol (PG) (Fig. 6), based on the very recent work by Deng et al. [52]. These compounds can be easily obtained from plant biomass sources [53–55]. This is the first time that this procedure is used in a UPO reaction with vitamins D (Table 3).

Reaction conditions: HspUPO 160 µM (single dose), MCD 20% (w/v) and 2% iPrOH

The experiments were conducted with 160 µM of HspUPO (added in a single dose) in the presence of 20% MCD and 2% iPrOH (Table 3). The first reactions with D2 were carried out at 15 mM substrate loading using AscA as reductant, for 24 h. Different concentrations (5–60 mM) of AscA were tested (data not shown), and the highest conversion (52%) was achieved with 15 mM AscA, that is the same concentration as the substrate (entry 1). In fact, as proposed by Deng et al., since the reductant helps in the UPO-activation, the lower concentration of the reductant than the substrate is compatible with a lower substrate transformation, although the negative effect of the higher concentration of reductant is unclear. Next, the reaction time was increased up to 48 h, and a significant increase in substrate conversion (up to 72%) was produced (entry 2). When 20 mM D2 with 20 mM AscA was tested, up to 81% conversion was attained within 72 h (entry 3).

At this point, the reductants GA and PG were also investigated with 20 mM of D2. Under the optimized conditions, a moderate conversion of 68% was produced with GA after 72 h (entry 4). Interestingly, remarkable 86% and 97% D2 conversions were achieved with PG after 24 h and 48 h, respectively (entries 5–6).

On the other hand, the reaction of 15 mM D3 with AscA delivered 75% conversion under the optimized conditions (entry 8), matching the result reached with H2O2 (Table 2, entry 17). In 20 mM D3 reactions using AscA and GA, the conversions (65% and 67%, respectively) decreased (entries 9–10), in contrast with the one using PG, with 75% D3 conversion, which represents the highest production of 25-OH-D3 (5.37 g/L) achieved with UPOs.

In contrast to D2, there is a remarkable decrease of D3 productivity in a dose dependent manner with both procedures (peroxides and reductants). This fact could be explained by the apparent decrease in D3 solubility as the substrate loading increased.

Despite that the overall results in terms of yield and potential isolated product amount are in very good range, especially with the D2 form (19.4 mM, 8 g/L, 25-OH-D2), the total turnover number (TTN) obtained (TTN = 121) is relatively low due to the high charge of enzyme required. Further process engineering and improved enzyme stability may substantially increase the TTN of this process in the future.

Conclusions

A comprehensive study on the regioselective C25-hydroxylation of vitamins D2 and D3 has been performed using a series of 71 UPOs, recombinantly produced with K. phaffii. Overall, the reactions with D2 resulted in the formation of the 25-hydroxy derivative with higher regioselectivity than with D3. Particularly, 42 UPOs out of 71 were able to convert D2 with strict/high regioselectivity and high degrees of conversion (up to 99%) to 25-OH-D2. Among these, the 16 best UPOs were tested in D3 reactions where they showed less regioselectivity than with D2, since in addition to the desired 25-hydroxy derivative they produced other di- and tri-hydroxylated products, the only exception being HspUPO. Finally, the scale-up of both reactions was conducted using HspUPO ‒ because of its favorable regioselectivity with both vitamins and the previously reported ease of recombinant production and general robustness – and MCD plus minor amounts of iPrOH as solubilizing agents. Four different cosubstrates, namely, H2O2 and O2 with AscA, GA, or PG were investigated in the upscaling, and very good product yields and strict regioselectivity were attained in all cases (up to 19 mM for vitamin D2 and 15 mM for D3) especially with the latter cosubstrate. The new procedure using O2/reductants takes advantage of avoiding the UPO-inactivation by H2O2, and the need to produce engineered variants with high tolerance to them. Finally, the relatively low TTN attained still has room for improvement and investigations in process engineering may improve catalytic efficiency in the near future.
