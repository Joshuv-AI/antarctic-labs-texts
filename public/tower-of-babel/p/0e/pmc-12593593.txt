Peroxiredoxins in central nervous system: implications for chronic pain and anaesthetic-induced neurotoxicity management
Authors: Meng-meng Ge, Xiao-min Zhang, Chao Xu, Chang-hong Miao, Qing-wu Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxidative stress, an imbalance between the prooxidant-antioxidant system, is regarded as one of the crucial causative pathomechanisms of chronic pain. However, direct scavenging of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) by antioxidants may disrupt normal physiological homeostasis. Peroxiredoxins (Prdxs), a family of ROS-regulating enzymes, have recently aroused growing interest in the broad field of antioxidant research. Six distinct groups of Prdxs (Prdx1-6) have been identified to date. Prdxs are essential for regulating diverse biological processes, including oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, apoptosis, mitochondrial dynamics, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and synaptic plasticity. Interestingly, the differential expression patterns of Prdxs in central nervous system (CNS) tissues, cell types, and subcellular locations imply specialized functions. This review systematically summarizes the expression patterns of specific Prdxs in tissues and cells of murine and human, as well as the regulatory factors of their enzymatic activities. It also discusses the underlying cellular signaling pathways through which Prdxs participate in several biological processes as well as chronic pain and anaesthetic-induced neurotoxicity. In addition, we summarize the therapeutic potential of Prdxs inducers in CNS disorders based on preclinical studies. A better understanding of these processes can promote the development of novel therapeutic targets for treating chronic pain and anaesthetic-induced neurotoxicity.Keywords: Oxidative stress, Peroxiredoxins, Chronic pain, Anaesthetic-induced neurotoxicity

A general overview of Prdxs biology

Peroxiredoxins (Prdxs), a conserved family of thiol-dependent peroxidases, have recently emerged as critical regulators of redox homeostasis within biological systems [1]. Mammalian Prdxs encompass six different isoforms, which are primarily divided into three subtypes based on the number of cysteine residues: typical 2-Cys (Prdx1-4), atypical 2-Cys (Prdx5), and 1-Cys (Prdx6) [2]. The primary function of Prdxs is to eliminate various peroxides, such as hydrogen peroxide, organic hydroperoxides, and peroxynitrite. Prdx1-5 are known to utilize thioredoxin (Trx) as an electron donor in their redox cycle [1]. Uniquely, Prdx6 differs from its counterparts by using glutathione as the reductant to neutralize peroxides [3]. Of note, Prdx6, as a bifunctional enzyme, also exhibits calcium-independent phospholipase A2 (iPLA2) activity, which can promote the activity of NADPH oxidase (NOX), thereby modulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [4,5].

The expression patterns of specific Prdxs in tissues and cells, as well as the regulatory factors of their enzymatic activities

Each Prdx isoform shows a distinct tissue localization and cellular distribution [6]. Previous studies have revealed changes in Prdxs expression patterns in brains from patients with degenerative disorders [7]. Moreover, their enzymatic activities and subcellular localization can be modulated by a variety of post-translational modifications (PTMs), including phosphorylation, acetylation, hyperoxidation, and S-nitrosylation. To date, studies on PTMs of Prdxs mainly focus on the typical 2-Cys Prdxs (Prdx1-4). The potential differences in enzymatic activities and subcellular localization of Prdxs may be associated with the transition of states or functions in diverse pathological conditions, deserving further investigation [8]. In this review, we briefly summarize the regional and cellular expression patterns of Prdxs in the central nervous system (CNS) of murine and human (Table .1). Additionally, we discuss how different PTMs impact the enzymatic activities or subcellular localization of Prdxs.

Prdx1

In human brain tissue, Prdx1 is widely expressed in astrocytes, with strong immunoreactivity also detected in ependymal cells and the subependymal matrix of the substantia nigra and basal ganglia [9]. Murine Prdx1 exhibits broad expression in the nuclei of glial cells in the brain, suggesting its potential role in regulating signal transduction rather than exerting antioxidant effect [10]. Prdx1's positive cells show a linear distribution pattern in the white matter, particularly in oligodendrocytes. However, some neuronal populations, including hypothalamic nuclei, pontine nuclei, motor nuclei, the granular layer of the olfactory bulb or cerebellum, cortical neurons, and motor neurons, show slightly positive immunoreactivity [11]. In the murine spinal cord, Prdx1 is primarily located in glial cells in the white matter [12]. Notably, strong Prdx1 expression is detected in the nucleus of the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and sensory nerve root [13]. In the brain, Prdx1 is predominantly induced in reactive astrocytes and microglia. Upregulated Prdx1 plays a crucial role in promoting the proliferation of astrocytes and microglia [12]. Beyond CNS glial cells, Prdx1 expression has been verified in immune-stimulated macrophages [14].

The enzymatic activity of Prdx1 is regulated by multiple PTMs, such as acetylation, S-nitrosylation, and Ubiquitination. Specifically, increased acetylation at Lys197 enhances its enzymatic activity [15]. Histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) has been identified as the specific deacetylase that modulates Prdx1 acetylation, and pharmacological inhibition of HDAC6 improves the antioxidant activity of Prdx1 in CNS disorders [16]. In contrast, S-nitrosylation can temporarily sequester Prdx1 from the peroxidase catalytic cycle, thereby inhibiting its activity. It has been previously reported that S-nitrosylation primarily occurs at Cys52 and Cys83 of Prdx1 [17]. Moreover, the peroxidase activity of Prdx1 is regulated by polyubiquitination [18]. The E6-associated protein (E6AP)-mediated ubiquitin-proteasome pathway irreversibly impairs the activity of Prdx1 [19].

Prdx2

Human Prdx2 is extensively expressed in neurons within specific brain regions, including the cerebral cortex, cerebellum, basal ganglia, and substantia nigra [20]. Glial and ependymal cells are generally negative for immunoreactivity, although weak positivity is observed in some ependymal cells of the basal ganglia [9]. Axonal immunoreactivity for Prdx2 is detected in the basal ganglia and spinal cord, whereas the hippocampus, cerebellum, and neocortical regions are essentially negative [9]. Consistently, murine Prdx2 staining is mainly detected in neurons of the grey matter in some brain regions, including the hippocampus, cerebral cortex, and thalamus. Additionally, murine spinal cord and DRG neurons exhibit strong Prdx2 immunoreactivity [13]. Prdx2 is expressed not only in the cytosol of some neurons but also in the nuclei of medial habenular neurons. This suggests the crucial role of Prdx2 in the nuclei of specific neurons in addition to its antioxidant activity. Moreover, recent studies have further revealed Prdx2's immunoreactivity in erythrocytes [21]. Substantial amounts of Prdx2 are released by fractured erythrocytes and damaged neurons after intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) [22]. This released Prdx2 can act as a potent damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) to regulate neuroinflammatory responses in microglia and macrophages [23].

The activity of Prdx2 is also modulated by various PTMs, including acetylation, S-nitrosylation, and peroxidation. Similar to Prdx1, the peroxidase activity of Prdx2 is enhanced via increased acetylation at Lys196, which is regulated by HDAC6 [17]. Interestingly, S-nitrosylation reduces the enzymatic activity of neuronal Prdx2, whereas its activity in erythrocytes is upregulated by this modification [24,25]. The catalytic cysteine of Prdx2 can also undergo hyperoxidation, typically resulting in transient inactivation of its peroxidase activity in both neuronal cells and erythrocytes [26,27]. Moreover, oxidation resistance 1 (Oxr1), a protein with neuroprotective effects against oxidative stress, can regulate Prdx2 hyperoxidation and S-nitrosylation [26]. Hyperoxidation of Prdx2 is increasingly recognized as a pathological marker for detecting oxidative stress-related diseases [28].

Prdx3

Prdx3 immunoreactivity is restricted to mitochondria and appears as a punctate or granular pattern within the cytoplasm of neurons [2]. Prdx3 staining is mainly observed in the hippocampus and cerebellum, with relatively weaker expression observed in the murine CA1-2 regions compared to the CA3 region [29,30]. Moreover, Prdx3 staining has been identified in the white matter of the spinal cord, sensory and motor root nerves, and DRG [13]. However, the location of Prdx3 in the human brain remains to be further investigated. Mechanistically, previous studies have suggested that Prdx3 participates in antioxidant defense and redox signaling. Overactivation of Prdx3 could protect against traumatic or excitotoxic neuronal injury by regulating mitochondrial function and mitochondrial biogenesis [29,31].

Previous studies have found the accumulation of oxidized Prdx3 under ischemic conditions and during the early stages of apoptosis [32,33]. However, the redox status of cytoplasmic Prdx3 remains unaltered, suggesting that Prdx3 oxidation is a mitochondria-specific event [34]. Prdx3 oxidation increases mitochondrial H2O2 levels and promotes the progression of apoptosis [33]. In addition, acetylation of Prdx3 inhibits its enzymatic activity and results in mitochondrial ROS accumulation, thereby contributing to oxidative stress-related disorders [35]. Recently, Li et al. demonstrated that spinal Prdx3 hyperacetylation (but not altered expression) is a pivotal mechanism involved in the pathogenesis of remifentanil-induced hyperalgesia (RIH) [36].

Prdx4

Prdx4 expression is detected in neurons, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes within the murine brain [11]. Immunoreactivity analysis reveals that Prdx4 is localized in the neuronal cytosol, whereas it is found in the nuclei of astrocytes and oligodendrocytes [30]. More importantly, Prdx4 is the only member among the six Prdx family proteins localized in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), owing to its conserved hydrophobic residues at the N-terminus. In addition, mild staining of Prdx4 has been found in the vascular system, white matter of the spinal cord, and DRG [13,37]. Functionally, Prdx4 exerts antioxidant activity in neurons and plays a crucial role in the nuclei of neuroglial cells in the CNS [38]. Studies have shown that Prdx4 downregulation can perturb oxidative protein folding in the ER, leading to abnormal ER homeostasis and consequently contributing to glutamate or amyloid-beta oligomer (AβO)-mediated HT-22 neuronal cell damage [39,40]. Interestingly, recent studies indicate that Prdx4 may directly participate in protecting endothelial cells from ROS damage. Consequently, its overexpression mitigates ischemic/reperfusion injury by suppressing endothelial dysfunction and blood-brain barrier (BBB) damage [41,42].

Research on PTMs of Prdx4 in the CNS remains limited. Notably, a recent study has detected hyperoxidation of Prdx4 in lung epithelial cells treated with tertbutyl hydroperoxide. This oxidation event induces a conformational shift from monomer/dimer to high molecular weight species and enhances interactions with proteins involved in protein folding and ER stress [43]. Future research is encouraged to explore PTMs of Prdx4 in CNS disorders.

Prdx5

Prdx5 is the most extensively distributed member of the Prdx family, with detectable expression in the nucleus, cytosol, mitochondria, and peroxisomes [2]. Prdx5 immunolabeling is primarily observed in neurons. Unlike Prdx3, Prdx5 immunoreactivity is evenly dispersed in the CA3 and CA2 regions of the murine hippocampus [30]. In addition, Prdx5 staining has been observed in the spinal cord and sensory nerve root [13]. In the human peripheral nervous system, Prdx5 is primarily localized to axons, with lesser expression in Schwann cells [44]. The subcellular localization of Prdx5 in human tissues is consistent with that observed in murine tissues [44]. This extensive cellular and subcellular distribution of Prdx5 suggests its crucial roles in the CNS. Mechanistically, Prdx5 is involved in redox-sensitive signaling. Overexpression of Prdx5 has been shown to protect against neuronal cell death induced by glutamate, iron overload or AβO via suppressing mitochondrial fission and ER stress in vitro [[45], [46], [47]]. Moreover, recent studies demonstrate that Prdx5 is highly expressed in activated macrophages [48]. It can act as an inducible inflammatory regulator by modulating redox signaling in macrophages and microglia [49]. Given Prdx5's important roles in various cell types in vitro, further studies are needed to verify its precise role in murine models of various neurodegenerative diseases in vivo.

Prdx6

Prdx6 immunoreactivity is highly detected in astrocytes and oligodendrocytes, with extensive distribution throughout the murine brain, spinal cord and DRG [8,13,50]. While primarily cytosolic, Prdx6 is also found in the nuclei of astrocytes and oligodendrocytes [10]. Uniquely among Prdxs, Prdx6 represents multiple enzymatic activities, including iPLA2, glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase [51]. The enzymatic activities of Prdx6 have been previously shown to be modulated by its subcellular localization, substrate binding, and PTMs [51]. For instance, Prdx6 exhibits maximal iPLA2 activity under acidic conditions and peroxidase activity under neutral conditions [52]. Cytosolic Prdx6 mainly displays peroxidase activity under basal conditions. Moreover, several PTMs, such as phosphorylation, hyperoxidation, glutathionylation, and sumoylation modulate the enzymatic activities of Prdx6 [51]. Specifically, phosphorylation of Prdx6 at Thr177 via mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) promotes its translocation to the plasma membrane and upregulates iPLA2 activity [53]. Hyperoxidation of Prdx6 at Cys47 inhibits Gpx activity while enhancing iPLA2 activity [54]. Prdx6 has also been demonstrated to undergo sumoylation by SUMO1 at Lys122 and Lys142. Aberrant sumoylation of Prdx6 can suppress its gene expression [55,56]. Notably, the differential regulation of Prdx6's activities exerts opposing biological roles. Specifically, the GPx activity of Prdx6 contributes to antioxidant defense in reducing ROS [4]. Prdx6 deficiency has been reported to promote cell death and contribute to various pathological conditions [57,58]. In contrast, the iPLA2 activity of Prdx6 participates in pro-inflammatory response, thereby exerting detrimental effects in animal models of Parkinson's disease and cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury [4,5]. Further studies are encouraged to investigate the expression dynamics of Prdx6 and clarify its functions in specific disease pathogenesis.

Prdxs and oxidative stress

Oxidative stress is recognized as closely associated with various CNS disorders. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), a key transcription factor regulating antioxidant enzymes and phase Ⅱ detoxification enzymes, has been demonstrated to be involved in various CNS disorders [60]. Prdxs, as members of the antioxidant enzymes family, have been shown to interact with several ROS-dependent effectors [61]. Interestingly, recent studies suggest that the promoters of Prdx1 and Prdx6 contain putative Nrf2 binding sites [18,62]. Supporting this, Shi et al. identified a direct interaction between Prdx1 and Nrf2 via co-immunoprecipitation in a rat model of osteoarthritis (OA)-related pain [63]. Furthermore, the basal promoter activity of Prdx5 primarily depends on nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 1 (Nrf1) and Nrf2, which modulate mitochondrial biogenesis [64]. Additionally, Prdx1-5 are known to utilize the Trx system as a reductant [62]. Recently, Zhao et al. verified that Prdx1-4 levels decreased following Trx-1 siRNA treatment in a rat model of transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO). They also found that Trx-1 expression is regulated by the Nrf2-anti-oxidative response element (ARE) pathway [65]. Collectively, these findings suggest that Prdxs play a crucial role in regulating oxidative stress-related signaling pathways.

Prdxs and neuroinflammation

While the crucial antioxidative function of Prdxs is well-established, recent studies suggest that Prdxs may also regulate the progression of neuroinflammation in various inflammatory-associated disorders [66]. Due to the different cellular locations of Prdxs, they can exert opposite effects in regulating neuroinflammation. Recent studies suggest that induced intracellular Prdxs may exert a protective effect against inflammatory-associated disorders. Among the Prdx isoforms, Prdx1 and Prdx5 have been identified as inducible inflammatory antioxidants in the CNS [67]. Moderate Prdx1 staining was previously demonstrated in a small population of murine brain microglia [30]. Prdx1 expression is found to increase in response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation, and this upregulation is associated with activation of ROS/p38MAPK signaling [67]. Prdx1 deficiency accelerated the nuclear translocation of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), thereby promoting inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) induction and nitric oxide (NO) secretion [67]. Similarly, Prdx5 is induced by LPS and recombinant interferon-γ (IFN-γ) treatment, which is closely associated with the activation of p38 and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) MAPK cascade in immune-stimulated macrophages [68]. Additionally, the ROS/JNK signaling pathway plays a crucial role in LPS-mediated Prdx5 induction in BV-2 microglial cells in vitro. Deficiency of Prdx5 promotes microglial activation and NO production [48]. However, extracellular Prdxs (including Prdx1, 2, 5, and 6) released by dying cells are increasingly recognized as potent DAMPs that contribute to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in microglia and macrophages [69]. Notably, Du et al. demonstrated that Prdx1 released by dying cells activates toll receptor 4 (TLR4)/NF-κB signaling and induces inflammatory cytokines in macrophages, contributing to neuroinflammatory injury following ICH [70]. Similar to Prdx1, extracellular Prdx2 released by erythrocyte lysis also promotes microglial activation via the TLR4/myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88)/NF-κB signaling pathway in a murine model of subarachnoid hemorrhage [21]. Lipocalin-2 (LCN-2) is a crucial protein secreted by astrocytes following inflammatory response. Intriguingly, recent research indicates that intracerebral administration of Prdx2 upregulates LCN-2 levels, promotes neutrophil infiltration and microglial activation, and ultimately exacerbates brain damage in mice. Moreover, LCN-2 deficiency significantly attenuates these Prdx2 mediated effects [22].

Prdxs and apoptosis

Emerging evidence indicates that 2-Cys Prdxs not only regulate intracellular peroxide levels but also directly participate in apoptosis signaling pathways [71]. For instance, overexpression of Prdx1 in dopaminergic neuronal (MN9D) cells conferred neuroprotection against 6-hydroxydopamine-induced apoptosis by inhibiting activation of p38MAPK and caspase-3 [72]. Similarly, Sun et al. showed that the expression of Prdx5 was upregulated in HT-22 cells following glutamate stimulation. Prdx5 depletion remarkably enhanced glutamate-induced apoptosis by promoting nuclear translocation of apoptosis-inducing factor in HT-22 cells in vitro. Moreover, this effect was attenuated through ROS inhibition or blockade of the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signaling pathway [73]. Additionally, Prdx2 overexpression was also revealed to exhibit an anti-apoptotic effect in various types of neurons by inhibiting apoptosis signal-regulating kinase 1 (ASK1) activity and the activation of JNK and p38MAPK-dependent cell death pathways [71,74].

Prdxs and mitochondrial dynamics

Mitochondria are highly dynamic organelles that frequently fuse and divide, performing several cellular functions [75]. Previous studies have shown that excessive ROS accumulation induces abnormalities in mitochondrial dynamics, eventually leading to mitochondrial dysfunction in various CNS disorders [76]. Among the Prdx family, Prdx3 and Prdx5 are specifically localized to mitochondria, rendering them critical for modulating mitochondrial redox homeostasis [77]. As a predominant mitochondrial antioxidant protein, Prdx3 overexpression has been shown to preserve mitochondrial membrane potential and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels. It also upregulates the levels of mtDNA content and proteins associated with mitochondrial biogenesis. All these results suggest that Prdx3 overexpression promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and suppresses mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby alleviating traumatic neuronal injury in primary cultured cortical neurons in vitro [31]. Moreover, Prdx5 also exerts protective effects via inhibiting mitochondrial fission both in neurons and in activated microglia [45,48]. Prdx5 overexpression can regulate Ca2+/calcineurin-dependent de-phosphorylation of dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) (Ser637) [46,49]. Additionally, Lee et al. reported that AβO treatment upregulated Prdx5 levels and that Prdx5 suppressed ERK-Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fragmentation in HT-22 neuronal cells [47].

Mitophagy is increasingly recognized as associated with oxidative stress. It acts as a double-edged sword in various CNS disorders [78]. Moderate mitophagy is critical for maintaining cellular homeostasis. Conversely, excessive or dysregulated mitophagy may trigger the production of ROS and impair nervous system function [79]. Prdx6, primarily localized in the cytosol, has been recently implicated in mitophagy [80,81]. Ji et al. demonstrated that treatment with carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone, a well-known ROS generator, could promote Prdx6 accumulation and its recruitment to the damaged mitochondria in HeLa cells in vitro. Prdx6 depletion caused PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) stabilization and promoted excessive mitophagy by disrupting ROS homeostasis [82]. Based on these findings, Zhao et al. further explored the relationship between Prdx6 and mitophagy in the brain in a rat model of MCAO in vivo. They reported that Prdx6 knockdown aggravated cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury via promoting PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, ultimately augmenting neuronal apoptosis [83]. Collectively, these findings suggest that Prdx6 accumulation in the mitochondria plays a critical role in protecting cells from death by inhibiting excessive mitophagy.

Prdxs and ER stress

The ER is another crucial subcellular organelle involved in regulating calcium homeostasis, as well as the synthesis of lipids and proteins [84]. Oxidative stress and dysregulated calcium homeostasis can induce ER stress, which in turn triggers cell death [85,86]. Among the six members of the Prdxs family, Prdx4 is uniquely localized to the ER lumen [87]. Previous studies have demonstrated that Prdx4 plays a pivotal role in maintaining ER homeostasis by regulating oxidative protein folding of ascent proteins and peroxidase activity [88]. Recent studies suggest that Prdx4 overexpression plays a protective role against glutamate or AβO-mediated oxidative stress, ER stress, and neuronal apoptosis in vitro [39,40]. Takujiro et al. further verified the important role of Prdx4 in ER homeostasis in vivo. They reported that Prdx4 knockout mice exhibit impaired oxidative protein folding and aberrant ER homeostasis in neurons, ultimately resulting in deficits in spatial memory formation [89].

Prdxs and synaptic plasticity

Numerous studies have reported that ROS play a crucial role in synaptic plasticity [90]. Nevertheless, the role of Prdxs in the regulation of synaptic plasticity remains understudied. Lee et al. suggested that Prdx2 protected hippocampal synaptic plasticity from age-related oxidative damage. They found that Prdx2 deficiency impaired the induction of long-term potentiation (LTP) and disrupted key synaptic plasticity-related signaling pathways in the hippocampus, including the protein kinase A (PKA)/cyclic adenosine monophosphate response element binding protein (CREB) pathway, calcium/calmodulin kinase II (CaMKII), ERK1/2, and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). This ultimately led to an abrupt decline in learning and memory function [91]. Prdx4, another widely distributed antioxidant enzyme in the brain, is also suggested to participate in neuronal synaptogenesis. Moreover, Takujiro et al. revealed that Prdx4 knockout mice showed lower spatial memory ability and increased expression of ER-associated degradation proteins [89]. In addition to neurons, astrocytes are also involved in the regulation of synaptic plasticity and memory performance, as the activation of astrocytes is essential for maintaining sufficient energy supply to neurons [92,93]. Prdx6, predominantly expressed in astrocytes in the brain, has recently been revealed to be necessary for regulating synaptic plasticity. Liu et al. showed that Prdx6 knockout mice displayed elevated hippocampal LTP and decreased reactive astrocyte markers after the probe test. Moreover, Prdx6 deficiency could affect synaptic plasticity and impair spatial memory via upregulating ERK1/2 signaling in the hippocampus [94]. All these results suggest that Prdxs play a crucial role in synaptic plasticity.

The role of Prdxs in chronic pain and anaesthetic drugs-induced neurotoxicity

Prdxs and chronic pain

Chronic pain is currently one of the most common global health problems, affecting over 30 % of the world's population [95]. Based on pathogenesis characteristics, it is broadly classified into three categories: inflammatory pain, neuropathic pain (NP), and cancer pain. Notably, NP can be further subclassified into peripheral and central NP, depending on the lesion or disease of the peripheral or central somatosensory nervous system [96]. Different types of chronic pain may possess unique pathophysiological mechanisms. The pathophysiology of chronic pain is highly complex, encompassing peripheral and central nervous system alterations alongside interactions with psychosocial factors [97,98]. The DRG, spinal cord and some CNS regions, including the thalamus, periaqueductal grey, amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, medial prefrontal cortex, and hippocampus are crucial tissues for pain transmission and have been extensively explored in previous studies. The incomplete elucidation of chronic pain contributes to suboptimal treatment outcomes [99]. Oxidative stress, defined as an imbalance between the prooxidant-antioxidant system, is regarded as a crucial causative pathomechanism of chronic pain. Furthermore, our previous study has demonstrated that excessive accumulation of ROS contributes to the development of chronic pain [100]. In this review, we summarize the role and underlying mechanisms of Prdxs in different types of chronic pain. It may provide novel therapeutic targets for the treatment of chronic pain in the future.

Prdxs in diabetic neuropathy

Diabetic neuropathy (DN) is the most debilitating outcome of diabetes mellitus [101]. Although multiple metabolic pathway dysregulations contribute to DN pathogenesis, enhanced oxidative stress is recognized as a common initiating factor [102]. However, the role of Prdxs, a superfamily of antioxidant enzymes, in DN remains incompletely elucidated. Novoselova et al. revealed that intravenous injection of Prdx6 (20 mg/kg) on days 1 and 8 alleviated hyperglycemia and reduced the severity of pancreatic cell loss in a mouse model of streptozotocin-induced type 1 diabetes. Moreover, Prdx6 administration prevented excessive activation of NF-κB and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), as well as the increased heat shock protein 90α (Hsp90α) levels [103]. In addition, Cao et al. verified a critical role for Prdx6 S-palmitoylation in protection against DN. They found that S-palmitoylation at Cys47 enhanced Prdx6's interaction with the C-terminal domain of anion exchanger 3 (AE3), thereby activating the influx of Cl−/HCO3−, which is associated with increased excitability and pain susceptibility in DRG in diabetic mice [104]. Thus, further studies are encouraged to explore the role and related mechanisms of other Prdx isoforms in DN.

Prdxs in OA-induced neuropathic pain

OA, the most prevalent chronic articular disorder, manifests primarily as pain and affects millions globally [105]. Emerging evidence highlights the imbalance in the oxidant-antioxidant system as a critical factor in the initiation and progression of OA [106]. Multiple enzymes and molecular factors have been implicated in mediating the antioxidant processes. Notably, Shi et al. recently reported that Nrf2 and Prdx1, two key antioxidant proteins, show decreased expression with advancing OA severity. Co-immunoprecipitation assays further confirmed a direct interaction between Nrf2 and Prdx1. Strikingly, methylene blue treatment significantly upregulated the expression of both Nrf2 and Prdx1, thereby suppressing calcitonin gene-related peptide (a neural inflammatory mediator) and ultimately relieving OA-related pain [63].

Prdxs in spinal cord injury-induced central neuropathic pain

Spinal cord injury (SCI), one of the most devastating traumatic injuries to the CNS, often leads to motor and sensory dysfunction below the injury site [107]. Pathophysiologically, SCI is categorized into primary and secondary stages [108]. The primary stage involves mechanical impaction causing direct hypoxia-ischemia at the spinal cord lesion site, while the secondary stage is a complex, multi-factorial process that can last for weeks or even months [109]. The pathophysiological mechanisms of SCI are intricate, involving several molecular pathways [110]. Recent studies have suggested crucial roles of Prdx1 and Prdx6 in these stages. Agrawal et al. reported that Prdx6 levels were remarkably upregulated in the white matter of the spinal cord after hypoxia, whereas reperfusion caused a subsequent downregulation of Prdx6 levels in a rat model of SCI. Notably, the reduction of Prdx6 contributed to inflammatory responses and apoptosis following reperfusion injury, suggesting a protective role of Prdx6 in SCI [111]. However, recent studies have verified different effects of Prdx1 in a rat model of SCI. Gong et al. observed that upregulation of Prdx1 by AAV-fibroblast growth factor 1 enhanced autophagy and exerted anti-ROS activity in PC12 cells treated with H2O2 in vitro [112]. However, Cui et al. found increased expression of Prdx1 in the astrocytes and microglia in the spinal cord after SCI. They demonstrated that Prdx1 may promote the proliferation of astrocytes and microglia during the secondary phase of SCI. Notably, knockdown of Prdx1 causes downregulation of proliferating cell nuclear antigen in primary rat spinal cord astrocytes and microglia after LPS treatment in vitro [12]. Prdx1 exhibits distinct functional roles in these two studies, likely due to differences in cell types. This observation underscores the functional diversity of Prdx1, highlighting the need for further investigation into its role in animal models of SCI.

Prdxs in cancer-induced bone pain

Cancer-induced bone pain (CIBP) is a refractory pain condition that arises from primary bone malignancies or when advanced tumors, including breast, lung, and prostate cancers, metastasize to bone [113]. This persistent pain remains inadequately managed due to unclear pathogenesis. However, emerging evidence, including findings from our own research, has implicated excessive ROS accumulation in CIBP pathogenesis [114]. Prdxs, a superfamily of multifunctional antioxidant enzymes, mitigate oxidative stress by scavenging ROS [20]. Recently, Liu et al. found a decreased expression of Prdx4 in the spinal cord in CIBP rats. Moreover, treatment with recombinant PTD Cu/Zn SOD was shown to upregulate the expression of Prdx4, restore the balance between excitatory (NMDA) and inhibitory (GABA) receptors in the spinal cord, and consequently attenuate bone cancer-induced hyperalgesia [115].

Prdxs and anaesthetic drugs-induced neurotoxicity

During clinical administration of anaesthetic drugs, adverse effects on the CNS may occur, including neuronal damage, functional impairment, or neurodegeneration. A variety of anaesthetic drugs, such as bupivacaine, sevoflurane, isoflurane, propofol, and ketamine have been shown to exhibit neurotoxicity [116,117]. Despite extensive research into the potential neurotoxicity of anaesthetic drugs, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. Emerging studies indicate that oxidative stress is a crucial factor contributing to the pathogenesis of anaesthetic drugs-induced neurotoxicity [118,119]. Prdxs, a family of ROS-regulating enzymes, have garnered increasing attention in the broader field of antioxidant research. However, the role and underlying mechanisms of Prdxs in anaesthetic drugs-induced neurotoxicity warrant further investigation.

Prdxs and local anaesthetics-induced neurotoxicity

Local anaesthetics (LAs)-induced neurotoxicity is a life-threatening risk, especially for patients with comorbidities like diabetes [120]. Excessive accumulation of ROS has been recognized as a key driver in the initiation of LAs-induced neurotoxicity [121]. Li et al. investigated the role of the antioxidant enzyme Prdx6 in high-glucose (HG)-aggravated bupivacaine neurotoxicity. They found that Prdx6 levels decreased in cultured SH-SY5Y cell line following bupivacaine and hyperglycemia treatment, and this effect was reversed by Krüppel-like factor 9 (KLF9) deficiency. Importantly, the protective effects of KLF9 deficiency on cell survival and mitochondrial function were abolished by Prdx6 knockdown [122]. Collectively, these results indicate that KLF9 may promote oxidative stress by downregulating Prdx6 levels and consequently disrupting mitochondrial function, thereby exacerbating HG-aggravated bupivacaine neurotoxicity in neurons.

Prdxs and inhalation anaesthetic-induced neurotoxicity

Sevoflurane, a commonly used inhalation anaesthetic, has been previously implicated in exerting neurotoxic effects on the developing brain [123]. Long-term sevoflurane exposure leads to neuronal degeneration by causing neural cell death [124]. Additionally, emerging evidence confirms that oxidative stress critically contributes to this cell death process [125]. Prdx1, a ROS-regulating enzyme, has been revealed to exert a protective effect against oxidative damage [126]. Zhang et al. showed that sevoflurane treatment downregulated various antioxidant molecules, including Prdx1, hemeoxygenase-1 (HO-1), NADPH quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), and glutamate cysteine ligase (GCL) in primary rat hippocampal neurons in vitro. Importantly, theaflavin (TF), a natural antioxidant compound, significantly reversed this effect, thereby mitigating sevoflurane-induced neuronal apoptosis [127].

The therapeutic potential of Prdx inducers in CNS disorders

The CNS is particularly vulnerable to oxidative damage owing to its high metabolic demand, elevated oxygen consumption, and abundance of polyunsaturated fatty acids [115]. ROS-mediated oxidative stress is a key contributor to the pathophysiology of various neurological disorders [128,129]. Direct scavenging of intracellular ROS by antioxidants may disrupt normal physiological homeostasis. Previous studies have clarified an important role of Prdxs, a family of ROS-regulating enzymes, in various CNS diseases. Therefore, targeting Prdxs may provide new insights for the treatment of various neurological disorders. In this review, we summarize the therapeutic potential of Prdx inducers, including some natural compounds and traditional Chinese medicine in CNS disorders based on preclinical studies.

Ferulic acid is a phenolic phytochemical compound naturally occurring in various grains, fruits, and vegetables [130]. It has been reported to exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in various neurodegenerative disorders [131]. Koh et al. demonstrated that intravenous administration of ferulic acid (100 mg/kg) reversed MCAO-induced reductions in Prdx2 and Trx levels, and inhibited the interaction between Trx and ASK1, thereby exerting a neuroprotective effect against oxidative stress [132]. Curcumin, a phenolic compound extracted from Curcuma longa, possesses antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic properties [133]. In a rat model of MCAO, Li et al. suggested that intraperitoneal administration of curcumin (300 mg/kg) attenuated ischemic oxidative stress via upregulating Prdx6 and specific protein 1 (SP1). Importantly, the beneficial effect of curcumin was reversed by pre-injection of Prdx6-siRNA or the SP1 antagonist Mithramycin [134]. Astaxanthin (ATX), a xanthophyll carotenoid found in marine seafood, exhibits various biological functions, including antioxidative and neuroprotective properties [135]. Previous studies have shown that ATX can easily cross the BBB, positioning it as a potential therapeutic drug for CNS disorders [136]. Consistently, Li et al. demonstrated that oral gavage of ATX (75, 150 mg/kg) significantly improved neurological outcomes following traumatic brain injury. Mechanistically, ATX treatment upregulated levels of Prdx2, Nrf2, and sirtuin 1, while downregulating phosphorylation of ASK1 and p38MAPK. These beneficial effects were remarkably reversed by intervention with Prdx2-siRNA or Nrf2 knockout, underscoring the critical role of Prdx2 in ATX-mediated neuroprotection [74]. TF, a polyphenolic compound derived from black tea, displays antioxidant activity by scavenging superoxide anions [137]. Recently, Zhang et al. have demonstrated that TF protects primary rat hippocampal neurons from sevoflurane-induced cytotoxicity by activating the Nrf2 pathway and suppressing apoptotic markers. Following TF treatment, various Nrf2-related antioxidant molecules, such as HO-1, NQO1, GCL, and Prdx1, were significantly upregulated [127]. These results suggest that the antioxidant property of TF underpins its neuroprotective effect.

Artesunate, a key derivative of artemisinin, is commonly used as an anti-malaria drug with low toxicity [138]. Artemisinin and its derivatives possess multiple pharmacological properties, including antioxidative, anti-cancer, and antiviral [139,140]. Additionally, previous studies have demonstrated the analgesic effect of artesunate against complete Freund's adjuvant-induced inflammatory pain [141]. Recently, Li et al. have shown that intrathecal injection of artesunate (10 μg and 100 μg, but not 1 μg) attenuates RIH by downregulating the expression of metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5) and mitigating oxidative stress mediated by hyperacetylation of Prdx3 [36]. 4-hydroxybenzyl alcohol (4-HBA) is one of the active phenolic components isolated from Gastrodia elata Blume (Tianma in Chinese). This traditional Chinese medicine has been used as a neuroprotective drug for millennia [142]. Zhao et al. further explored the underlying mechanisms of 4-HBA's neuroprotective effect. They found that 4-HBA treatment upregulated levels of Nrf2, Prdx6, and protein disulfide isomerase via the phosphatidyqinositol-3 kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) pathway both in vitro and in vivo [143]. Gastrodin, another major bioactive constituent from Tianma in Chinese, has been shown to exert neuroprotective effects by activating the Nrf2 pathway and downregulating the mitochondria-dependent caspase-3 apoptosis pathway [144]. However, its low BBB permeability limits CNS efficacy. Du et al. synthesized a novel Gastrodin derivative (Gas-D) with enhanced BBB permeability. Furthermore, they found that pretreatment with Gas-D (10 μM) inhibited intracellular ROS and expression of 4-HNE in H2O2-induced oxidative injury in SH-SY5Y cells in vitro. More importantly, intraperitoneal administration of Gas-D (100 mg/kg) inhibited expressions of Prdx1/2/4, TLR4 signaling activation, and production of inflammatory factors 10 h after reperfusion following 2 h MCAO [145]. Danhong injection (DHI), a traditional Chinese medicine widely used clinically for cardiovascular and cerebral occlusive disorders, exhibits known antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [146]. In a rat model of ICH, DHI (1.0 ml/kg/d) administration significantly upregulated the level of Prdx1 in astrocytes, and suppressed the release of inflammatory factors, including TNF-α and interleukin-1β. Moreover, these neuroprotective effects were reversed by the Prdx1 inhibitor Chelerythrine [147]. Ligustilide (LIG), a characteristic phthalide component of several medicinal Umbelliferae plants, was verified to exert neuroprotective effects against various cerebral ischemic disorders [148]. Previous studies showed that LIG could inhibit inflammatory response induced by LPS in primary rat cerebral microglia in vitro [149]. Recently, Du et al. demonstrated that intraperitoneal administration of LIG (20, 40 mg/kg/day) significantly suppressed MCAO-induced extracellular release of Prdx6 and TLR4 signaling activation, reflected by decreased expressions of TLR4, p-ERK1/2, as well as transcriptional activity of NF-κB and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) in the ischemic brain [150]. Obovatol, isolated from the medicinal herb Magnolia obovate, exhibits multiple biological activities, including anti-tumor, anti-bacterial, and anti-platelet properties [151]. Of note, previous studies have revealed its anti-inflammatory activity in LPS-induced mouse macrophage RAW264.7 cells in vitro [152]. Suk et al. recently demonstrated that obovatol inhibited the proinflammatory factors and iNOS in LPS-stimulated microglial BV-2 cells in vitro. Moreover, in a mouse model of LPS-induced neuroinflammation in vivo, intraperitoneal treatment of obovatol (10 mg/kg) remarkably increased the ROS-scavenging activity of Prdx2, thereby inhibiting microglia-mediated neuroinflammation-a process in which ROS plays a crucial role [153].

However, none of these compounds are specific Prdx inducers. One potential reason is that oxidative stress is a complex biological process. Targeting a specific antioxidant enzyme alone may not completely reverse CNS damage caused by oxidative stress. Second, preparations targeting specific antioxidant enzymes may face limitations in low bioavailability and instability. Therefore, Prdx inducers discussed herein are potential therapeutic drugs in the treatment of CNS disorders. Further studies are warranted to develop specific Prdx modulators and explore their roles in CNS disorders.

Conclusions

Studies from our laboratory and others have revealed that excessive ROS is associated with various CNS disorders. Notably, Prdxs, a family of ROS-regulating enzymes, have recently gained recognition for their critical roles in antioxidant defense in biological systems [1]. The differential expression patterns of Prdxs in CNS tissues, cell types, and subcellular locations suggest specialized functions that are currently being elucidated [20]. This review summarizes the cellular localization and underlying mechanisms of Prdxs and describes in detail the role of Prdxs in chronic pain and anaesthetic-induced neurotoxicity. We also outline the therapeutic potential of Prdx inducers in CNS disorders in preclinical studies. However, these findings raise further questions. First, different types of chronic pain may possess unique pathophysiological mechanisms. Therefore, further studies are warranted to explore the roles of Prdxs in multiple types of chronic pain. Second, none of the Prdx inducers summarized here are specific Prdx activators. Their effects are pleiotropic. The main disadvantages of specific preparations targeting Prdxs are their limitations in bioavailability and instability. These shortcomings can perhaps be exploited by biochemical technology and available genetic engineering [154]. For example, encapsulating Prdxs within particles, hydrogels, and liposomes can enhance their pharmacokinetic properties and protect them from degradation. Alternatively, adsorbing Prdxs onto nanoparticle surfaces can serve as therapeutic drug carriers. Additionally, conjugation with polyethylene glycol or sugars can enhance antioxidant enzyme stability and prolong bioactivity [155]. Genetic engineering approaches have also been utilized to achieve targeted delivery of specific antioxidant enzymes, such as the generation of enzymes with protein transduction domains, which facilitate the penetration of enzymes across the plasma membrane [156]. Therefore, developing novel preparations targeting Prdxs using these approaches and evaluating their efficacy in chronic pain treatment are critical areas in future exploration. Moreover, PTMs of Prdxs have been shown to modulate their activities and expression [15,17]. For example, the enzymatic activity of Prdx2 is enhanced by various PTMs, such as the increase of nitration and acetylation, as well as the reduction of phosphorylation, peroxidation, methylation, and S-nitrosylation [157]. Therefore, targeting PTMs of Prdxs might offer a promising strategy for developing novel therapies against CNS disorders. Moreover, current understanding of Prdxs' roles in CNS diseases has primarily relied on rodent models, which may exhibit discrepancies compared to humans in disease induction, drug metabolism, and nervous system organization. Future research thus urgently needs to validate these findings in other animal models, such as non-human primates, to better clarify Prdxs' functions in human health and disease. Furthermore, since the function of Prdxs is regulated by both expression and enzyme activity, future studies should focus on characterizing Prdxs activity states in chronic pain and anaesthetic-induced neurotoxicity.
