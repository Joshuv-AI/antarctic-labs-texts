Afforestation of severely desertified land in semi-arid areas promotes soil carbon and nitrogen accumulation through microbial necromass
Authors: Yun Chen, Wenjie Cao, Xiaoming Mou, Xuyang Wang, Yuqiang Li, Jinliang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Afforestation is an important measure used to expand carbon (C) sequestration and mitigate climate change. However, the impact of soil microbial necromass on soil C and nitrogen (N) sequestration still remain unclear following afforestation of severely desertified land. We investigated 10-, 20-, and 40-year-old Caragana microphylla and Pinus sylvestris var. mongolica plantations (±2 years) established on mobile dunes in northern China, taking mobile dunes and natural grassland and forest as references. We then collected topsoil (0 − 20 cm) samples and measured amino sugar. Afforestation would ideally increase soil organic carbon (SOC) and total nitrogen (TN) storage by 26.3 and 2.5 Tg, respectively, after 40 years based on the baseline of the year 2000. It would take more than 110 years to realize SOC and TN restoration, taking natural grassland as a reference. Microbial necromass were highest in natural ecosystems, followed by plantations and mobile dunes. Afforestation promoted the accumulation of microbial necromass, leading to an increase in SOC and TN. Microbial necromass contributed more to TN (24.4 to 49.5%) than to SOC (22.3 to 41.0%). Fungal necromass were approximately four times greater than bacterial necromass. Improving soil physical properties and mitigating N limitation are potential effective methods for increasing microbial necromass.

Introduction

Afforestation is widely considered an important natural solution in promoting carbon (C) capture and mitigating climate change1, having been implemented worldwide2–4. Transforming degraded ecosystems into forests can reduce soil erosion, improve soil fertility, and enhance ecosystem services, such as C sequestration and biodiversity conservation5,6, thereby enhancing the contribution of these areas to climate change mitigation7. Thus, it is crucial to assess the impact of afforestation on soil C dynamics to support the sustainable restoration of degraded ecosystems. Microbial necromass is an important constituent of soil organic carbon (SOC)8,9, contributing greater than 40% of SOC in topsoil10. Decomposition- resistant substances composed of bacterial and fungal cell walls are important contributors to SOC stability11,12. Intermediate products of microbial necromass decomposition promote the formation of soil aggregates, thereby providing physical and chemical protection for both SOC and nutrients13. Therefore, the quantification of microbial necromass will profoundly aid in a deeper understanding of SOC sequestration8,14.

Generally, afforestation on degraded grassland and cultivated land promotes the accumulation of microbial necromass C15–17. This result may be related to increased plant diversity, productivity, and microbial biomass. Firstly, greater biodiversity and productivity provide more plant-derived inputs, which in turn increase microbial abundance and promote microbial assimilation18. Secondly, high-quality plant inputs can enhance microbial C use efficiency, thereby promoting microbial necromass accumulation15. Thirdly, afforestation reduces or even eliminates environmental disturbances, leading to a more stable substrate and physicochemical protection for soil C sequestration19.

Microbial necromass dynamics are strongly influenced by the ecosystem type20, as litter and root residue properties will vary greatly within different ecosystems11,21. For example, compared to grasslands, afforestation with trees brings a large amount of slowly decomposing plant residues (e.g., lignin), which are mainly decomposed by fungi, leading to the accumulation of fungal necromass22,23. In contrast, shrubification in grassland impedes the microbial transformation of plant-derived substrates, reducing microbial necromass C but increasing the proportion of fungal necromass C in SOC24. Furthermore, the organic layer formed by litter lacks mineral particle protection, and microbial necromass is easily decomposed and utilized25. These may lead to changes in the distribution pattern of microbial necromass after afforestation. Climate and soil properties (e.g., texture and pH) also affect microbial necromass accumulation by influencing primary productivity and nutrient availability11. Therefore, microbial necromass may vary greatly under strong influences from specific climate types, land-use types prior to afforestation, the species used for afforestation, and the available soil properties26,27. Afforestation age is another important factor. Vegetation restoration after both farmland afforestation and forest reforestation leads to the accumulation of microbial necromass. But the proportion of microbial necromass C to SOC increases first and then decreases with the increasing restoration years16,28–30. In other words, an increase in microbial necromass does not necessarily enhance the proportion of microbial-derived SOC. One of the reasons is the dilution effect caused by the increase in plant-derived inputs24.

Similar to SOC, microbial necromass nitrogen (N) is also a key component of the soil N pool, account for more than 40.5% of soil N12. Due to the well-constrained C:N ratio in microorganisms, the N pattern in microorganisms and soil can be used to infer the contribution of microbial necromass to SOC based on the stoichiometry. Thus, microbial necromass N enhances our ability to predict C dynamics. Previous study showed that microbial necromass N accounted for 56–59% of soil total nitrogen (TN) in an Caragana korshinskii plantation established on abandoned farmland, but 25–39% at plantation without leguminous plants in arid desert ecosystems28. This is because symbiotic N fixation increases microbial biomass and reduces the decomposition and reuse of microbial necromass N28. N availability within substrates is an important factor affecting the N turnover rate of microbial necromass, and bacterial necromass appears to be more sensitive to N availability than fungal necromass31.

Northern China has experienced the most severe desertification. It is also a representative region for desertification control in the world. Unfortunately, previous studies have mainly focused on the ecological impacts of afforestation on grasslands and cultivated land. We still know little about soil C and N dynamics and their microbial mechanisms under different afforestation types on severely desertified land. The changes in the contribution of fungal and bacterial necromass to soil C and N with afforestation ages remain unclear26,30,32,33. These gaps collectively limit our in-depth understanding of soil C and N sequestration after afforestation of severely desertified land. Here, we conducted an extensive investigation in the semi-arid region of Northern China to fill these knowledge gaps. We aimed to reveal the capability of SOC and TN storage, the contribution of microbial necromass to SOC and TN, and factors affect the accumulation of microbial necromass following afforestation by quantifying microbial necromass dynamics based on amino sugar biomarkers and measuring biotic and abiotic factors. We hypothesized that if afforestation is carried out on mobile dunes, then microbial necromass C and N would increase and mediate by vegetation-related factors, while their contributions to corresponding SOC and TN would decrease, because more plant-derived inputs promote microbial assimilation and produce dilution effects. Given that the study area is representative of desertification control in semi-arid regions, results from this investigation can provide new insights into soil C sequestration following afforestation of desertified land in semi-arid regions and support desertification control.

Compared with mobile dunes (wherein the means of SOCD and TND were 0.2 and 0.03 kg m−2, respectively), afforestation significantly (p < 0.05) increased SOCD and TND, which increased with increasing afforestation age (Fig. 1). For the C. microphylla plantation, SOCD increased by a magnitude of 3.0, 5.6, and 6.0 times following 10, 20, and 40 years of afforestation, respectively. TND increased by a magnitude of 2.4, 4.2, and 4.2 times, respectively. For the P. sylvestris plantation, SOCD increased by a magnitude of 1.5, 5.4, and 7.9 times following 10, 20, and 40 years of afforestation, respectively. TND increased by a magnitude of 0.7, 3.4, and 4.9 times, respectively. Overall, SOCD and TND recovery occurred faster in the early stage of C. microphylla afforestation, while long-term afforestation of the P. sylvestris plantation was more advantageous to SOCD and TND recovery. Taking natural grassland (2.5 and 0.3 kg m−2 for SOCD and TND, respectively) as a reference, it would take approximately 133 and 116 years for SOCD to fully recover following C. microphylla and P. sylvestris afforestation, respectively, and approximately 156 and 115 years for TND recovery, respectively (Fig. 2). Additionally, it would take approximately 248 and 146 years for SOCD and TND recovery following P. sylvestris afforestation, respectively, taking natural P. sylvestris forest as a reference (3.9 and 0.3 kg m−2 for SOCD and TND, respectively) (Fig. 2). Changes in microbial necromass C and N following afforestation

Both afforestation type and age individually and significantly (p < 0.05) effected fungal and total microbial necromass C and N. The afforestation age significantly (p < 0.05) effected bacterial necromass C and N. Afforestation type and age showed significant (p < 0.05) interactions on fungal necromass C and N (Table 1).

Compared with mobile dunes, afforestation significantly (p < 0.05) increased fungal and bacterial necromass C and N (Fig. 3a–d). Following 10 years of C. microphylla afforestation, fungal necromass C and N increased by a magnitude of 7.4 times, which were significantly (p < 0.05) higher compared to the 10-year-old P. sylvestris plantation (Fig. 3a, b). However, fungal necromass C and N in the C. microphylla plantation did not change significantly (p > 0.05) with increasing afforestation age. Following 10 years of P. sylvestris afforestation, fungal necromass C and N increased by a magnitude of 2.2 times, and increased significantly (p < 0.05) as afforestation age increased. No matter the plant species used for afforestation, fungal necromass C and N following 40 years of afforestation were much lower than those of the natural grassland (2.1 and 0.3 kg m−2 on average, respectively) and the natural P. sylvestris forest (3.1 and 0.5 kg m−2 on average, respectively). Additionally, bacterial necromass C and N did not differ significantly (p > 0.05) between the C. microphylla and P. sylvestris plantations. Bacterial necromass C and N significantly (p < 0.05) increased as afforestation age increased (Fig. 3c, d). After 40 years of afforestation, bacterial necromass C and N did not differ significantly (p > 0.05) compared to those of the natural P. sylvestris forest and the natural grassland. Given that we derived total microbial necromass from the sum total of fungal and bacterial necromass, and that fungal necromass was much higher than bacterial necromass, microbial necromass dynamics were basically consistent with fungal necromass dynamics (Fig. 3e, f). In summary, microbial necromass C and N were highest in natural grassland and forest, followed by plantations and mobile dunes. Regarding the different afforestation types, microbial necromass C accounted for 22.3–41.0% of SOC (Fig. 4c). The percentage of microbial necromass C in SOC increased following afforestation, which did not differ from that of the natural grassland (30.9%). Additionally, the percentage of microbial necromass C in SOC in mobile dunes (23.9%) and the natural P. sylvestris forest (22.3%) was significantly (p < 0.05) lower compared to the other types. Compared to microbial necromass C, the percentage of microbial necromass N in TN was higher, namely, from 24.4 to 49.5% (Fig. 4d). Compared with mobile dunes (24.4%), afforestation significantly (p < 0.05) increased the percentage of microbial necromass N in TN, which did not differ significantly (p > 0.05) from the natural grassland (41.5%) and the natural P. sylvestris forest.

Effects of environmental factors on SOCD, TND, and microbial necromass

The RDA results showed that the geographical factors explained 93% of the total variation of SOCD and TND (Fig. 5 and Table 2). Variations explained by fungal necromass C were the highest, account for 85.7%. For different types of factors, soil properties explained the most of variations of SOCD and TND (90.5%), with a contribution of 97.3% (Table 2). The result of pSEM also showed that SOCD and TND were significantly and directly affected by corresponding microbial necromass C and N, with a standardized path coefficient of 0.51 and 0.65 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. S2). For microbial necromass C, variables used in the pSEM explained 72%, 42%, and 73% of fungal, bacterial, and total microbial necromass C variance, respectively (Fig. 6). Soil properties were the main predictor variables for microbial necromass. Soil physical properties had significant direct and indirect effects (via the soil N:P ratio) on fungal and total microbial necromass C (Fig. 6a, c). However, bacterial necromass C was mainly directly affected by the soil N:P ratio (Fig. 6b). Besides the direct effects of climate variables on fungal necromass C, vegetation, topographic, and climate variables mainly had indirect effects on microbial necromass C via soil properties. Additionally, pH effected fungal, bacterial, and microbial necromass C differently. For instance, pH had a direct positive effect on fungal necromass C, an indirect negative effect on bacterial necromass C, and no effect on total microbial necromass C. Discussion

Although afforestation has led to considerable SOCD and TND growth at a depth of 20 cm in this study, growth rates following afforestation were still much lower compared to that of northwestern Patagonia in Argentina (at a depth of 200 cm)34, Geitasandur sandy desert in Southern Iceland (at a depth of 10 cm)35, the alpine sandy land of Western China36, and China’s Horqin Sandy Land (at a depth of 20 and 100 cm)37. In contrast, growth rates following afforestation in this study were higher compared to shrub afforestation in China’s Tengger Desert (at a depth of 10 and 100 cm)38,39. These results may be attributed to local land use practices and degradation history, climate type, and afforestation age26. For example, the Tengger Desert is characterized by its high temperature and dryness levels, with an annual average temperature of 10 °C, an annual precipitation of 186 mm, and a potential evapotranspiration of 2300–2500 mm during the growing season38. However, the precipitation in this study were higher, and temperatures were lower (Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4) compared to the Tengger Desert. Additionally, these results may be attributed to the deeper soil layer depth (1 m) reported in a previous study because deeper soil layers encourage additional SOC and TN growth17. For severely desertified areas, afforestation mainly affects soil properties in the topsoil (e.g., 0–20 cm) and does not significantly affect soil layers deeper than 20 cm38,40.

According to severely desertified land area in the year 2000 (21,175 km2) (Supplementary Fig. S5 and Supplementary Table S1) and the average growth rate of SOCD (31.0 g C m−2 yr−1) and TND (3.0 g N m−2 yr−1) following 40 years afforestation, SOCD and TND are expected ideally to increase to 26.3 Tg C and 2.5 Tg N in topsoil (0−20 cm) by 2040 after converting those severe desertification land to plantations. The predictive SOC increase is equivalent to approximately 11% of the SOC storage increase by afforestation in Northern China over the past 30 years4. Present study indicate that SOC and TN will recover slowly after afforestation. Referring to natural grasslands, for example, it will take over 110 years for SOCD and TND to recover, which is basically consistent with the time required for afforestation restoration in the Tengger Desert38 and the Horqin Sandy Land37.

The results showed that SOCD, TND, and microbial necromass were highest in natural grassland and forest, followed by plantations and mobile dunes. Compared to mobile dunes, microbial necromass C and N concentrations and their proportions to corresponding SOC and TN increased following afforestation (especially in the early stages of afforestation). The results support our hypothesis that afforestation increases microbial necromass. It indicates that the relative accumulation of microbial synthetic substances is an important reason for the increase in SOC and TN sequestration32, which was confirmed in the influencing factor analysis (Figs. 5, 6, and S2). On the one hand, those results are related to the large input of plant biomass in natural ecosystems and plantations11, which can stimulate microbial growth and residue accumulation. On the other hand, organic matter and nutrients decrease with the loss of fine particulate matter during the desertification41–43, while establishing plantations eliminates some interference, which is beneficial for SOC and nutrients accumulation. The mitigation of soil C and N limitations following afforestation promotes microbial synthesis and metabolism and reduces the recycle and reuse of microbial necromass28. During the P. sylvestris plantation’s initial 10 years, increases in SOC and TN were slow compared to the C. microphylla plantation. This is because leguminous C. microphylla promotes the capture of N. We also found that fungal necromass decreased relative to bacterial necromass during this period. This may be related to the near absence of litter and herbaceous biomass, as fungi are the main decomposers of recalcitrant substances (e.g., lignin and tannins)22.

Following afforestation, except for the slightly higher microbial necromass C proportion to SOC (41%) in the 10-year-old C. microphylla plantation, the contribution of microbial necromass mainly ranged from 30.5 to 35.8%, which is close to that of global temperate forests (32.6%)12. However, the contribution of microbial necromass C to SOC in the natural grassland and the natural P. sylvestris forest was lower compared to that of the corresponding temperate grassland (61.8%) and forest (32.6%)12. Firstly, the SOC concentration in our study was less than 25% of that measured in the previous studies, which limits microbial synthesis and exacerbates residue decomposition (i.e., reuse). Secondly, compared to microorganisms, natural grasslands and forests experience dilution effects under long-term plant-derived inputs12,24. This may also explain why microbial necromass C is the highest in natural P. sylvestris forest, yet its proportion in SOC is the lowest. A large amount of slow-decomposing persistent plant necromass (such as lignin and cellulose) accumulate in natural forests, leading to a relative accumulation compared to microbial necromass. These results collectively support our hypothesis. The observation that natural P. sylvestris forest have higher fungal necromass and lower bacterial necromass than natural grasslands further support this conclusion. Additionally, the contribution of microbial necromass N to TN (44.7–49.5%) following afforestation in this study is consistent with global measurements (>40.5%)12. But it is lower compared to leguminous plantations on abandoned farmland in arid sandy areas (56–59%), which may be attributed to fertilization practices prior to afforestation on abandoned farmland28.

Results also showed that the contribution of fungal necromass to SOC and TN was higher compared to bacterial necromass (fungal/bacterial necromass > 1). The ratio of fungal to bacterial necromass significantly increased following afforestation, especially in the P. sylvestris plantations (Fig. 4). In general, fungi have a strong ability to decompose recalcitrant organic compounds, such as lignin, lipids, and tannins, which are more abundant in woody plants22,24. A previous study also reported that living fungal biomass is approximately three times that of living bacterial biomass44, which may be another important reason. Secondly, chitin in fungal cell walls is more resistant to decomposition than peptidoglycan in bacterial cell walls, resulting in its longer retention in soil9,16. This may also explain why the contribution of bacterial necromass to SOC and TN is decreasing.

The main factors affecting microbial necromass in this study were soil physical properties and the N:P ratio. Among these factors, microbial necromass positively correlated with the field water capacity and the silt + clay content, whereas negatively correlated with bulk density, which is consistent with previous study32. Firstly, in semi-arid areas, an increase in soil moisture availability has a positive impact on microbial activity, soil nutrient diffusion and supply, and plant-derived inputs45, resulting in the accumulation of microbial necromass. Secondly, an increase in mineral particles following afforestation is beneficial for the formation of microaggregates, thereby providing physicochemical protection for microbial necromass46,47. Additionally, studies have reported that microbial necromass is the main contributor of mineral-associated organic C14,46. Thirdly, the soil N:P ratio is an important indicator reflecting the balance and limitation of soil nutrients and has a highly significant positive effect on the accumulation of microbial necromass C, indicating that microbial (especially bacterial) necromass is significantly affected by N limitation48. The global pattern of microbial nutrient limitation supports that N limitation is stronger in the north than in the south49. Our previous studies have further proven that there is mainly N limitation in the study area50,51. Especially, both plant and microbial species diversity are strongly limited by N following desertification41. Nitrogen is an important component of amino acids and ribonucleic acid. Insufficient N supply will limit microbial growth and negatively affect its biomass52. Moreover, microorganisms will reduce the accumulation of synthetic substances31 and increase the decomposition of persistent microbial necromass to promote the reuse of nutrient by plants under N limitation11,28. These results indicate that N limitation mitigation is an important way to promote microbial necromass accumulation and ultimately increase SOC storage. Interestingly, apart from the N:P ratio, environmental factors had almost no direct impact on bacterial necromass (Fig. 6), indicating that bacterial necromass is more dependent on an increase in N availability22,53. In contrast, a previous study has reported a decrease in SOC in N-rich soil54. Thus, more evidence is needed on the relationship between C and N during afforestation. These findings emphasize the importance of incorporating N into microbial dynamics for SOC sequestration.

Except for the direct effect that climate factors had on microbial necromass, the effect of climatic, topographic, and vegetation factors on microbial necromass was mainly indirect via soil properties. The climate pattern and the redistribution of local hydrothermal conditions caused by topography determine microbial and plant patterns48,55,56. For semi-arid regions, the growth of plants and microbes is mainly influenced by water availability45. For example, drought caused by warming will inhibit microbial activity and lead to N limitation, resulting in reducing microbial necromass accumulation and promoting decomposition57–59. The increase in net primary productivity and litter quantity promotes C and nutrient release into soil, while an increase in species richness can accelerate microbial growth and turnover rates18,29,60. Particularly, an increase in herbaceous diversity is crucial for microbial necromass and SOC accumulation in topsoil, due to the shallow roots and easy decomposition of herbs61. Additionally, vegetation restoration and microtopographic differences may affect the erosion and deposition of fine soil particulate materials related to mineral-associated organic C62.

Although we estimated the time required for restoration (over 110 years) and the potential SOC (26.3 Tg C) and TN (2.5 Tg N) sequestration of afforestation in severely desertified areas, this estimation is based on the assumption that afforestation is successful and plantations follow the same SOC/TN trajectory we studied. However, different natural resource endowments (e.g., soil properties, climate, and water availability), management measures, and afforestation success rates in each region will affect soil C and N accumulation to varying degrees. These estimates may be relatively optimistic. In addition, soil C and N restoration may be slower in the later stage of afforestation (>40 years) due to the aging and slow renewal of plantations and the resulting plant input saturation63.

Previous studies have shown that afforestation on mobile dunes mainly affects soil C and N sequestration at a depth of 20 cm38,40, which is why we chose topsoil as the main research object. However, over 90% of the root biomass of C. microphylla and P. sylvestris are distributed within a depth of 100 cm63–65. The input of underground biomass will improve the fertility of deeper soil layers. In addition, the dynamics of the soil C pool are depth-dependent. For example, research on the Qinghai Tibet Plateau has shown that changes in microbial necromass of topsoil are dominated by both plant inputs and minerals. However, in subsoil, microbial necromass is mainly driven by minerals66. Thus, it is necessary to strengthen the dynamic monitoring of C and N in deeper soil layers in future research.

Water availability is the main limiting factor for vegetation productivity in arid and semi-arid regions. The water-carrying capacity of sand fixing vegetation is crucial during land restoration. Even in Horqin Sandy Land with relatively good hydrothermal conditions (Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4), previous studies suggested that the density of C. microphylla and P. sylvestris plantations should not exceed 2500 trees hm−2 and 515 trees hm−2, respectively64,67. The suitable density for 45-year-old P. sylvestris plantation is 177–214 trees hm−268. Those results indicated that the density of plantations investigated in this study generally exceeded the range of soil moisture carrying capacity (Supplementary Table S2). Therefore, in the future, further research on the water carrying capacity of artificial forest ecosystems at different stages is needed to achieve sustainable development of ecological restoration.

In summary, in this study, we revealed how afforestation on severely desertified land in the semi-arid region of Northern China promoted SOC and TN sequestration from a microbial perspective based on amino sugar biomarkers. Although the potential of afforestation for SOC and TN recovery is great, the recovery time is slow and persistent. Regarding the specific afforestation species used, long-term P. sylvestris afforestation resulted in better SOC and TN recovery compared to C. microphylla afforestation. The accumulation of microbial necromass is an important means for increasing SOC and TN following afforestation, and the contribution of microbial necromass to TN was higher than that to SOC. Fungal necromass was the main contributor. Soil physical properties and N:P ratio were the main influencing factors for microbial necromass accumulation following afforestation. Improving soil physical properties and mitigating N limitation are potential effective methods for increasing microbial necromass. Results highlight that afforestation is an effective way to address desertification and promote soil C sequestration in grassland. Finally, studying soil microbial dynamics will help improve our ability to predict soil C dynamics and mitigate regional climate change.

Methods

Study area

The selected study areas (the Horqin Sandy Land, the Hulun Buir Sandy Land, the Otindag Sandy Land, and the Mu Us Sandy Land) were primarily located in Northern China (Fig. 7), all under the influence of a temperate continental semi-arid climate. Mean annual temperature in these areas ranges from −1.4 to 10.4 °C, and mean annual precipitation ranges from 156 to 658 mm (Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4). The native vegetation is comprised of temperate grassland with sparsely distributed woody plants, which has since changed into sand dunes and inter-dune lowlands dominated by annual psammophytes due to desertification. As of 2020, the area of desertified land is approximately 101,601 km2, accounting for approximately 61% of the total area of these four major sandy lands. Field investigation

Afforestation using C. microphylla and P. sylvestris are representative measures for preventing desertification, representing different afforestation strategies (i.e., shrub afforestation and tree afforestation, respectively). Thus, 10-, 20-, and 40-year-old C. microphylla and P. sylvestris plantations (±2 years) were selected as the main targets of this investigation. We selected plantations and determined the afforestation ages based on previous research37,40,69 and information provided by the local forestry department. All of the plantations investigated had been established on mobile dunes (i.e., severe desertification). Mobile dunes were mainly transformed from grasslands. Thus, mobile dunes and natural grasslands were selected as references for severe desertification (equivalent to 0 years of afforestation) and climatic climax ecosystems, respectively. Because the P. sylvestris plantation was established as a representative restoration strategy, a natural P. sylvestris forest was also selected as a reference for P. sylvestris afforestation. Natural grasslands and forests investigated in this study are almost non degraded natural ecosystems. Finally, nine vegetation types were investigated in total (Fig. 7). The soil types in the natural grassland and natural P. sylvestris forest are mainly Kastanozems and Chernozems, respectively, and Arenosols in plantations and mobile dunes.

At least three 20 m × 20 m plots that have only experienced minimal anthropogenic disturbances were randomly established for each vegetation type in the study area from August to September 2022 (Supplementary Table S2). Three 1 m × 1 m quadrats were then established along a diagonal in each plot at 10 m intervals (Supplementary Fig. S6). We recorded the height, coverage, and density of each herbaceous species, separately collecting aboveground herbaceous and litter biomass within each 1 m × 1 m quadrat, to describe species composition, diversity, and biomass. We then collected soil samples using a soil auger (2.5 cm in diameter) to a depth of 20 cm. For mobile dunes and natural grassland, nine soil samples from the same quadrat were combined to produce a single composite sample (Supplementary Fig. S6a). For natural and artificial forest, to avoid the “fertility island effect”, three sampling locations (corresponding to the quadrats) between woody plants were selected along a diagonal at a 10 m interval in each plot (Supplementary Fig. S6b). We then collected soil samples at 20 cm intervals and combined samples from the same sampling locations to produce a single composite sample. To reduce the sample size and measurement costs, three samples from the same plot were well-mixed to produce a composite sample. We obtained 180 samples in total, including 84 samples from plantations. Among them, 63 samples were located in the Hunlun Buir Sandy Land, 52 samples were located in the Horqin Sandy Land, 35 samples were located in the Otindag Sandy Land, and 30 samples were located in the Mu Us Sandy Land. Soil cores (100 cm3 in volume) were collected from each quadrat and location to measure the soil bulk density and saturated and field water capacities. Additionally, temperature and precipitation data during the growing season were obtained at each plot from the National Tibetan Plateau Data Center (https://data.tpdc.ac.cn/).

Analysis of amino sugars and soil properties

Amino sugars of all 180 soil samples were measured using the gas chromatography method70. Briefly, air-dried soil samples were hydrolyzed with 10 mL of 6 M HCl solution at 105 °C for 8 h, and 100 μg of myo-inositol was used as the recovery standard for each sample. After sequentially filtering, drying, and redissolving the hydrolyzed samples, the resultant samples pH was adjusted to 6.6–6.8 using a 0.4 M KOH solution. After centrifugation, the supernatant was freeze-dried and then dissolved in methanol to remove any salts. The procured solution was then dried using N gas at 45 °C. Subsequently, the residual materials were heated in a derivative reagent at 75 °C for 30 min applying intermittent oscillation. These residual materials were once again heated after adding 1 ml acetic anhydride. After cooling, dichloromethane was added to the resultant residual materials. Excess derivative reagents were then removed with 1 M HCl and ultrapure water, and the resultant organic matter was dried with N gas. The purified materials were then redissolved to measure amino sugars using the TRACE 1300 Series Gas Chromatograph (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA). Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 20.0 (https://www.ibm.com/). Kruskal–Wallis one-way ANOVA was used to analyze the impact of afforestation on SOC and TN accumulation. Two-way ANOVA was conducted to reveal interactions between afforestation age and type using the Scheirer–Ray–Hare test based on the rank transformation. Regression and visualization were performed using Origin 2018 (https://www.originlab.com/). We examined several common regression methods and ultimately chose the power function as the optimal model (Supplementary Table S3). Based on our prior knowledge of SOC, biodiversity driving mechanisms, and ecosystem nutrient limitations in the study area41,50,51, we selected climatic (i.e., average temperature and precipitation during the growing season), topographical (i.e., elevation, slope, and slope aspect), soil physicochemical properties (i.e., soil bulk density, electrical conductivity, saturated and field water capacity, and silt and clay content, pH, N:P, and microbial necromass), and vegetation (i.e., diversity and biomass) factors as drivers, after conducting collinearity diagnosis (variance inflation factor > 10) (Supplementary Table S4). We conducted redundancy analysis (RDA) using Canoco 5 to analyze the impact of these geographical factors on SOCD and TND. Considering the close relationship between geographical factors of the same type and a more concise analysis of the impact pathways of geographic factors on microbial necromass, the piecewise structural equation model (pSEM) with mixed effects was performed to reveal the effects of environmental factors on microbial necromass using the “piecewiseSEM” package71 in R 4.4.1 (https://www.r-project.org/). Briefly, we carried out multiple linear regression on factors of the same type (independent variables) with SOCD or TND (dependent variables), and formed a composite variable using the standardized regression coefficients obtained. It resulted in a total of four composite variables (i.e., soil physical properties, climate variables, terrain variables, and vegetation variables). Subsequently, we established a prior model containing all assumed path (Supplementary Fig. S8). We then conducted model iterations using statistics, such as Akaike information criterion (AIC), chi squared (χ2), and Fisher’s C to obtain the optimal path model72,73. Additionally, plot locations were used as a random effect in the model.
