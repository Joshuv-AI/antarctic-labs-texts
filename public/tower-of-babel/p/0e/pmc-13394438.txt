Repression of ROBO1 Enhances Cisplatin Sensitivity in Hypermethylated Subtype of HPV‐Positive Head and Neck Carcinoma
Authors: Kenta Saeda, Takuya Nakagawa, Atsushi Okabe, Kazuko Kita, Tomoya Kurokawa, Masaki Fukuyo, Hiroki Ueda, Motoaki Seki, Bahityar Rahmutulla, Syuji Yonekura, Toyoyuki Hanazawa, Atsushi Kaneda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlthough human papillomavirus‐positive (HPV(+)) head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), particularly oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC), is generally associated with favorable outcomes, a subset of HPV(+) OPSCC remains resistant to chemoradiotherapy. We previously stratified clinical OPSCC cases using DNA methylome profiling and identified a significant correlation between elevated DNA methylation levels and favorable treatment response. To elucidate the molecular basis of this correlation, we performed integrated methylome analyses of 64 HPV(+) HNSCC cases in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and 29 HPV(+) OPSCC cases in our Chiba cohort. We identified 595 genes showing significantly higher DNA methylation in good responders in each cohort. Additionally, using HNSCC cell lines, we extracted genes that exhibited both high DNA methylation and decreased expression in cisplatin (CDDP) sensitive cells. Twelve genes were consistently hypermethylated across both clinical cohorts, among which two genes, ROBO1 and SULT4A1, showed statistical significance and effect size. Functional validation demonstrated that ROBO1 knockdown in unmethylated HNSCC cells significantly suppressed cellular growth (p < 0.05), and additional exposure to CDDP further synergistically reduced cellular growth (p < 0.05). Conversely, ROBO1 overexpression conferred resistance to CDDP. CDDP resistance in unmethylated HNSCC cells led to upregulation of ROBO1 expression, suggesting an inverse relationship between ROBO1 silencing and drug resistance. Mechanistically, ROBO1 suppression increased DNA damage and apoptosis following CDDP treatment. These indicate that DNA hypermethylation of ROBO1 causally correlates with better treatment response of HPV(+) HNSCC, and its chemosensitivity might be enhanced by silencing of methylation target genes.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is the sixth most common cancer worldwide, with almost 890,000 new cases and 450,000 deaths worldwide annually. The overall HNSCC incidence is increasing, with a predicted increase of 30% by 2030 [1, 2]. Major etiological factors include tobacco use, alcohol consumption [1, 3, 4] and infection with human papillomavirus (HPV) [1, 5, 6, 7]. Among these, the incidence of HPV‐positive (HPV(+)) oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC) has steadily increased [6, 7, 8, 9]. HPV(+) OPSCC typically exhibits a more favorable prognosis than HPV‐negative (HPV(−)) [10]. However, approximately 20%–30% of HPV(+) OPSCC cases demonstrate poor outcomes or chemoradiotherapy [11, 12]. The molecular mechanisms underlying treatment resistance in this high‐risk subset remain unclear.

Recent large‐scale genomic studies, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), have revealed that head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) harbors a broad spectrum of genetic and epigenetic alterations that contribute to tumorigenesis and therapeutic response [13, 14]. Among these, DNA methylation—a key epigenetic mechanism regulating gene expression—has emerged as a critical player in HNSCC biology [15, 16]. Somatic mutations in key tumor suppressors, such as TP53, CDKN2A, and FAT1, are frequently observed in HNSCC. According to TCGA data, TP53 mutations occur in approximately 84% of HPV(−) tumors, compared to only 3% in HPV(+) tumors. Similarly, CDKN2A is mutated or deleted in over 50% of HPV(−) cases, whereas alterations are rare in HPV(+) cases [13, 17]. These distinct mutational landscapes reflect divergent tumorigenic pathways between subtypes: in HPV(−) HNSCC, genetic inactivation of TP53 and CDKN2A is common, whereas in HPV(+) tumors, viral oncoproteins such as E6 and E7 functionally inactivate p53 and Rb, thereby reducing the selective pressure for these mutations [18, 19]. Hence, epigenetic aberrations such as DNA hypermethylation may play a more prominent role in the pathogenesis and treatment response of HPV(+) HNSCC. Our previous studies analyzing clinical 170 OPSCC samples have revealed that HPV(+) tumors exhibit more extensive aberrant DNA methylation than HPV(−) tumors. Moreover, promoter hypermethylation improves prognosis in HNSCC [20, 21], suggesting that DNA methylation profiles could serve as biomarkers for prognostic stratification and therapeutic decision‐making [22].

Although we and other groups have reported DNA methylation‐based biomarkers associated with prognosis and recurrence risk across various cancer types, the functional relevance of the methylated genes themselves remains insufficiently characterized [23, 24, 25]. In this study, we performed integrative epigenomic and transcriptomic analyses using data from 64 HPV(+) HNSCC cases from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), 29 HPV(+) OPSCC clinical cases from our institution, and six HPV(+) HNSCC cell lines. ROBO1 emerged as a candidate gene epigenetically associated with treatment response. Functional assays revealed that ROBO1 promotes cell proliferation and cisplatin resistance, with increased expression observed in resistant cells. DNA hypermethylation is thus found to causally correlate with better treatment response of HPV(+) HNSCC via enhancing chemosensitivity by silencing of methylation target genes, such as ROBO1.

Materials and Methods

Detailed information can be found in Doc S1.

Cell Lines and Culture

UMSCC104 was purchased from Merck Millipore (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). HMS001, UDSCC2, and NOK‐SI were gifts from Joseph Califano. VUSCC147 was provided by Tomokazu Yoshizaki. UMSCC47 was obtained from the University of Michigan. UPCISCC090 was a gift from Dr. Robert Ferris. FaDu was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATTC, Manassas, VA, USA). HPV status of each cell line was determined based on previously published reports [26], and detailed information on the cell lines including HPV status is provided in Table S1.

Clinical Samples

Clinical samples were collected from 29 patients with OPSCC who were diagnosed at Chiba University Hospital. HPV infection status in the Chiba cohort was determined by PCR amplification of the L1 region of high‐risk HPV using GP5+ and GP6+ primers.

TCGA Data of HNSCC

Infinium methylation datasets of 72 HPV(+) HNSCC samples provided by TCGA (https://gdac.broadinstitute.org/) were used for combined and additional analyses of DNA methylation status. HPV status was obtained from cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/), based on the Pan‐Cancer Atlas annotations of The Cancer Genome Atlas. In this dataset, HPV positivity was defined using integrated molecular data, primarily based on RNA sequencing–derived viral transcript detection.

RNA Extraction and Analysis

RNA‐seq was conducted following standard procedures [21]. Total RNA was isolated with TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and further purified using the Direct‐zol RNA MiniPrep Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA) in accordance with the manufacturers' instructions. Library construction for RNA sequencing was carried out using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Deep sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq6000, sequenced reads were aligned using HISAT2 (version 2.1.0), and transcript assembly was performed with Cufflinks (version 2.2.1).

Gene Ontology (GO) Analysis

GO enrichment was evaluated through functional annotation analysis using Metascape's Gene Annotation and Analysis Resource (http://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1) [27].

Statistical Analysis

All sequencing experiments were performed in biological duplicate. Gene expression levels were compared using the t‐test. Unsupervised two‐way hierarchical clustering was performed using the R software (www.r‐project.org/). Progression‐free survival (PFS) distributions were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Differences between groups were determined by the log‐rank test using the R software (www.r‐project.org/). Significance was set at p < 0.05.

DNA Extraction

Genomic DNA was isolated with the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Anticancer Agents

Cisplatin (cis‐diamminedichloroplatinum(II), CDDP; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) immediately before use and directly diluted with culture medium to the desired concentrations.

siRNA Transfection

Silencer Select Pre‐designed or Validated siRNAs targeting ROBO1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Silencer Select Negative Control siRNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for transfection. siRNA sequence is described in Table S2.

Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) With Reverse Transcription

cDNA was prepared from 500 ng of total RNA using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green and CFX96 Touch Real‐Time PCR (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Target gene expression levels were normalized to that of GAPDH. The primer information was shown in Table S3.

Establishment of CDDP‐Resistant Cell Lines by Stepwise Exposure

CDDP‐resistant cell lines were established from parental cells by a stepwise exposure method [28, 29, 30, 31]. Resistance was confirmed by determining IC50 values using the WST‐8‐based cell viability assay.

Evaluation of Chemosensitivity

Viability of cells treated with CDDP was estimated using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan). All experiments were independently repeated at least three times, and results are expressed as the mean ± standard error (SE).

Western Blotting

Rabbit anti‐ROBO1 polyclonal antibody (1:2000; 20219‐1‐AP; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), rabbit anti‐cleaved caspase‐3 monoclonal antibody (1:600, 5A1E, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and mouse anti‐β‐actin monoclonal antibody (1:4000; M177‐3; MBL, Nagoya, Japan) were used. The immune complexes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized by chemiluminescence using the ECL Prime System (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA).

DNA Methylation Analysis

DNA methylation profiling was performed using either the Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip (Illumina) or the Infinium MethylationEPIC v2.0 Kit (Illumina).

ROBO1 Overexpression Assay

A lentiviral expression vector encoding full‐length human ROBO1 (NM_133631.4) under the control of the CMV promoter (Vector ID: VB900125‐1861khs, pLV[Exp]‐Puro‐CMV>hROBO1[NM_133631.4], VectorBuilder, Chicago, IL, USA) was used for overexpression studies. ROBO1 overexpression was confirmed by RT‐qPCR and western blotting.

Results

DNA Methylation Profiling Identifies a Hypermethylated Subgroup Associated With Favorable Prognosis in HPV(+) HNSCC

In addition to 29 cases from our Chiba cohort, we utilized data of 72 HPV(+) HNSCC cases from TCGA. After excluding censored cases with PFS < 12 months in TCGA, 64 TCGA cases remained for analysis (Figure S1), resulting in a total of 93 cases for the subsequent integrated analysis. Patients were classified based on progression‐free survival (PFS): those with PFS ≥ 12 months were defined as responders (R, n = 78), whereas those with disease progression within 12 months were defined as non‐responders (NR, n = 15) (Figure 1A).

Among 485,577 probes available on the Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadArray, we extracted one probe upstream and nearest to the TSS of each gene (excluding sex chromosome genes), resulting in a total of 15,212 promoter‐associated probes. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of β‐values of these probes revealed two distinct methylation clusters. Non‐responder cases were significantly enriched within the Low‐methylation group (p = 0.004) (Figure 1B,C).

To achieve a robust and reproducible classification, we applied an iterative resampling‐based clustering approach to the integrated cohort of 93 HPV‐positive HNSCC cases. Across 500 iterations of random partitioning into training (70%) and test (30%) sets, clustering analysis of the training data of 65 randomly chosen cases consistently identified two distinct methylation groups (Figure 1D). Test samples of the remaining 28 cases were assigned to the nearest cluster using the L1‐norm, and the stability of classification for each sample was evaluated across all iterations. Final group assignment was determined by a two‐sided binomial test (p < 0.05), enabling the identification of samples with statistically consistent cluster membership, where 21 and 62 cases were significantly assigned into High‐ and Low‐methylation groups (Figure 1E, Table S4). This approach allowed the high‐ and low‐methylation groups to be defined robustly and reproducibly, and NR cases were significantly enriched within the Low‐methylation group (p = 0.03) (Figure 1F).

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated significantly worse PFS in the Low‐methylation group compared to the High‐methylation group (p = 0.009, log‐rank test) (Figure 1G).

Differential methylation analysis using the Infinium 450k BeadArray platform between the non‐responder and responder groups was performed using the same set of 15,212 promoter‐associated probes, identifying 595 genes with significantly different β‐values between the NR and R groups (standard deviation [SD] > 0.1 and p < 0.05; Figure 2A). Most genes, including ROBO1, LINC00605, and ZNF793, exhibited higher methylation in the R group (Figure 2B).

(B) Scatter plot comparing β‐values of the 595 genes between the NR and R groups. (C) Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the 595 differentially methylated genes.

GO enrichment analysis of the 595 hypermethylated genes revealed significant enrichment of neural development‐related processes, with ROBO1 implicated in multiple top‐ranked categories (Figure 2C).

Identification of Candidate Genes to Regulate CDDP Resistance in HNSCC Cell Lines

Given that aberrant DNA methylation might contribute to treatment resistance, we explored the functional relevance of DNA methylation and treatment response. We evaluated the CDDP sensitivity of six HPV(+) HNSCC cell lines after 72 h of CDDP exposure (Figure 3A). IC50 values ranged from 0.87 μM (HMS001) to 24.7 μM (UDSCC2), with UDSCC2 exhibiting the highest resistance (Figure 3B). IC50 values differed markedly across the six cell lines. Notably, the most resistant cell line (UDSCC2) exhibited significantly higher IC50 values compared with the sensitive cell lines (HMS001 and UMSCC104), and these differences remained significant after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (Table S5).

We then compared gene expression profiles between resistant (UDSCC2) and sensitive (HMS001 or UMSCC104) cell lines. RNA‐seq experiments were performed using independent replicates (n = 2 per condition), where RNA was extracted from separately cultured cells in different dishes to ensure independence. Differential expression analysis was initially performed using an unpaired t‐test based on FPKM values, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

RNA‐seq analysis revealed 1001 genes downregulated in HMS001 compared to UDSCC2, and 2106 genes downregulated in UMSCC104 compared to UDSCC2 (log2FC < −1 and p < 0.05) (Table S6). GO analysis of these gene sets highlighted pathways related to neurodevelopment, including “neuron projection morphogenesis,” and “brain development” (Figure 3C,D). Intersecting the downregulated gene sets identified 586 commonly downregulated genes, and GO analysis revealed significant enrichment in “neuron projection development,” “brain development,” and “tissue morphogenesis” (Figure 3E). ROBO1 was included in these downregulated gene sets. Among the analyzed genes, those meeting the significance threshold (q‐value < 0.05) are presented in red in Table S6.

DNA methylation analysis identified 628 genes showing high methylation in the sensitive cell lines (UMSCC104 and HMS001) and low methylation in UDSCC2 (Figure 3F). GO analysis of these genes similarly emphasized neurodevelopmental pathways.

Integration of Clinical and Cell Line Data Identifies ROBO1 as a Convergent Candidate Gene

To further refine candidate genes that may be involved in treatment resistance via DNA methylation‐mediated regulation, 595 genes hypermethylated in clinical responder cases were overlapped with 628 genes hypermethylated in CDDP‐sensitive cell lines. We identified 98 overlapped genes (Figure 4A), with GO enrichment again pointing to neurodevelopmental processes. To prioritize candidate genes associated with both DNA methylation and gene expression changes, we intersected the 586 commonly downregulated genes with the 98 commonly hypermethylated genes. Hence, 12 overlapping genes were identified (Figure 4B,C), with ROBO1, CDH2, and NEFL highlighted by GO analysis as involved in “cerebral cortex development” and “pallium development.” Genes with substantial missing data were excluded from the analysis to ensure reliability of the overlap. We further calculated effect sizes (Δβ) and q‐values for these genes (Figure 4D), and applied additional selection criteria (|Δβ| ≥ 0.10 and q‐value < 0.05) to identify biologically and statistically significant candidates, resulting in two candidate genes, ROBO1 and SULT4A1 (Figure 4D).

A heatmap of β‐values for these genes confirmed higher methylation levels in sensitive lines (Figure 4E), with ROBO1 showing the most pronounced difference. Violin plots of β‐values for ROBO1 and SULT4A1 revealed significantly higher methylation in the responder group, especially for ROBO1 (Figure 4F).

To further validate the RNA‐seq differential expression analysis, we additionally performed a count‐based analysis using edgeR based on HTSeq raw count data. The results were consistent with the original FPKM‐based analysis, confirming that all 12 overlapping genes identified by the integrative analysis were significantly downregulated in CDDP‐sensitive cell lines in both comparisons (UDSCC2 vs. HMS001 and UDSCC2 vs. UMSCC104) (Figure S2).

Kaplan–Meier analysis stratified by ROBO1 methylation level (β ≥ 0.2 vs. β < 0.2) demonstrated significantly longer PFS in the high‐methylation group (log‐rank p = 2 × 10−5) (Figure 4G). We performed both univariate and multivariable Cox regression analyses to evaluate the association between ROBO1 methylation status and PFS, along with clinical covariates including age, sex, stage, and treatment (Figure 4H).

In the univariate analysis, ROBO1 methylation status was significantly associated with PFS, with high methylation showing a favorable prognosis (HR = 0.22, p < 0.001). None of the clinical covariates demonstrated significant associations. Given the limited number of events (n = 30), we performed variable selection using stepwise regression based on Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) as an exploratory approach. This procedure retained three variables (ROBO1, sex, and stage), which were subsequently included in the final multivariable model. In the multivariable analysis, high ROBO1 methylation remained significantly associated with improved PFS (HR = 0.24, p < 0.001), whereas sex and stage were not significant predictors. No violations of the proportional hazard assumption were observed (global p = 0.25; Table S7).

ROBO1 Functionally Contributes to CDDP Resistance in HNSCC Cells

To investigate whether ROBO1 expression is regulated by DNA methylation, we analyzed the correlation between ROBO1 methylation levels and gene expression across eight cell lines, including additional cell lines, HPV‐negative FaDu cells and normal head and neck epithelial NOK‐SI cells. High ROBO1 expression could be observed in ROBO1‐unmethylated or partially methylated cell lines, while ROBO1 expression was silenced in ROBO1‐hypermethylated cell lines, such as HMS001 and FaDu (Figure S3A,B). Furthermore, treatment of HMS001 cells with 5‐aza‐2′‐deoxycytidine (5‐aza‐dC), alone or in combination with trichostatin A (TSA), resulted in a decrease of methylation levels accompanied by an increase of ROBO1 expression, supporting epigenetic regulation of ROBO1 (Figure S3C,D).

To assess the functional role of ROBO1, we performed siRNA‐mediated knockdown in UDSCC2 cells by two independent siRNAs. RT‐qPCR and western blot analyses confirmed robust downregulation of ROBO1 at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figure 5A). WST‐8 assays revealed that ROBO1 knockdown significantly reduced cellular growth under basal conditions and enhanced sensitivity to 1 μM CDDP (Figure 5B).

Conversely, we overexpressed ROBO1 in UMSCC104 and HMS001 cells, confirming marked increases of ROBO1 in both mRNA and protein levels (Figure 5C,D). WST‐8 assays demonstrated that ROBO1 overexpression significantly increased cell viability following CDDP treatment, with higher IC50 values than those of empty vector controls (HMS001: 1.2 vs. 0.9 μM, p = 0.0005; UMSCC104: 3.6 vs. 2.8 μM, p = 0.004) (Figure 5E,F). Violin plots of IC50 values confirmed significant increases in CDDP resistance upon ROBO1 overexpression (Figure 5G).

Furthermore, time‐course WST‐8 assays demonstrated that ROBO1‐overexpressing cells retained proliferative capacity even in the presence of CDDP (0.5 μM), which markedly suppressed cellular growth of empty vector controls (Figure 5H).

We next assessed DNA damage and apoptosis following CDDP treatment. Under CDDP treatment, ROBO1 knockdown significantly increased γH2AX levels in UDSCC2 cells, indicating enhanced DNA damage (Figure S4). Cleaved caspase‐3 levels were elevated in ROBO1‐silenced cells, suggesting increased apoptosis (Figure S5). Conversely, in ROBO1‐overexpressing cell lines, γH2AX levels decreased in association with increased ROBO1 expression, indicating reduced DNA damage (Figure S6). These results from both loss‐ and gain‐of‐function experiments demonstrate that ROBO1 modulates CDDP sensitivity by regulating DNA damage and apoptosis.

ROBO1 Expression Is Upregulated in Cisplatin‐Resistant HNSCC Cells

To further validate the functional relevance of ROBO1 in acquisition of CDDP resistance, we established a CDDP‐resistant derivative of UMSCC47 cells by continuous exposure to gradually increasing concentrations of CDDP over seven months (Figure 6A). Parental WT cells were maintained as controls, and cell viability was assessed after 72‐h treatment with various CDDP concentrations. The CDDP‐resistant derivative exhibited a markedly right‐shifted dose–response curve (Figure 6B), with significantly elevated IC50 values compared to the WT (14.3 μM vs. 2.4 μM, p = 0.0002) (Figure 6C), confirming successful CDDP resistance.

Transcriptomic analysis by RNA sequencing revealed that ROBO1 expression was significantly upregulated in CDDP‐resistant derivative (log2FC > 1 and p < 0.05), placing ROBO1 among the most differentially expressed genes (Figure 6D). To further validate this finding, we additionally performed count‐based differential expression analysis using edgeR based on HTSeq raw count data, which confirmed significant upregulation of ROBO1 in the CDDP‐resistant derivative (Figure S7). Western blot analysis further confirmed increased ROBO1 protein expression in CDDP‐resistant derivative relative to WT, supporting that both transcriptional and translational upregulation of ROBO1 occurred during the acquisition of CDDP resistance (Figure 6E). GO analysis of the 1228 genes significantly upregulated in CDDP‐resistant derivative revealed enrichment of biological processes related to neurodevelopment and cell migration (Figure 6F).

ROBO1 expression was consistently elevated in HNSCC cells that acquired CDDP resistance, further substantiating its role as a mediator of treatment resistance and a potential therapeutic target.

Discussion

Despite its favorable prognosis, HPV(+) OPSCC shows treatment resistance in 20%–30% of cases [11, 12], requiring molecular insight for personalized therapy. In this study, we found that global DNA hypermethylation significantly associates with favorable treatment response in HPV(+) HNSCC. By integrating transcriptomic and methylation data, we identified 12 methylation‐regulated genes, including ROBO1, as potential mediators of CDDP sensitivity. Our findings suggest that ROBO1 silencing enhances and its overexpression reduces chemosensitivity, indicating its role in acquired treatment resistance.

To evaluate treatment efficacy, we stratified patients into the responder and non‐responder groups considering PFS at 1 year after the therapy. This cutoff was selected because the majority of recurrences of HNSCC after the therapy occur within the first year [32], and early recurrence is strongly associated with poor prognosis [33]. Therefore, 1‐year PFS provides a practical and clinically relevant endpoint for distinguishing biologically aggressive tumors.

The association between global DNA promoter hypermethylation and good treatment response in HPV(+) HNSCC is consistent with previous studies [20]. In other cancer types, such as gastric [34], colorectal [35], and breast cancer [36], DNA methylation‐based classification has been shown to correlate with distinct clinical outcomes and prognoses, indicating that epigenetic profiles can serve as robust prognostic stratification tools [37, 38]. Similarly in HNSCC, previous studies have reported that DNA methylation‐based subclassification in HPV(+) OPSCC is associated with better clinical outcomes and reduced tumor aggressiveness [22], in line with our findings. The current results therefore support the notion that DNA methylation profiling may serve as a valuable biomarker for prognostic stratification and treatment selection in HPV(+) OPSCC. In these tumors, where p53 is often functionally inactivated by the HPV E6 oncoprotein rather than by somatic mutations [13, 17], epigenetic regulation may play a particularly prominent role in modulating tumor behavior. Therefore, the observed correlation between high methylation and improved prognosis suggests a possible protective epigenetic phenotype within this subgroup.

Although FAM135B methylation has been reported as an independent favorable prognostic biomarker for overall survival in HNSCC patients [39], prognostication in current clinical practice still primarily relies on anatomical staging based on the TNM classification [40], and there are no widely accepted molecular biomarkers that can reliably predict therapeutic outcomes. Therefore, the development of gene‐based biomarkers capable of stratifying prognosis holds significant value in advancing personalized treatment strategies. The 12 DMEGs, especially ROBO1, identified through integrative epigenomic and transcriptomic analyses using TCGA and Chiba cohorts in this study, may serve as novel predictive biomarkers for clinical stratification in HPV(+) OPSCC. Notably, ROBO1 was selected based on both statistical significance (q‐values) and effect size (Δβ), supporting its robustness as a candidate gene.

Among the DMEGs, ROBO1 emerged as a particularly compelling candidate based on both in vitro functional assays and resistance modeling. ROBO1 encodes a single‐pass transmembrane receptor originally characterized in neural development, functioning as a guidance receptor for axonal migration via the Slit–Robo signaling pathway [41, 42]. Its function in cancer may be context‐dependent. ROBO1 has been reported as a tumor suppressor in breast and prostate cancers [43, 44], yet shows oncogenic roles in small cell lung cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma [45, 46, 47]. To date, however, no study has investigated the role of ROBO1 in HPV(+) OPSCC. Furthermore, functional analyses demonstrated that ROBO1 modulates CDDP sensitivity by regulating DNA damage and apoptosis. Our findings provide novel insight into how epigenetic repression and overexpression of ROBO1 might contribute to therapy resistance in this biologically defined subset of HNSCC.

The establishment of CDDP‐resistant cell lines is typically defined by a 3‐ to 5‐fold or greater increase in IC50 compared to the parental cells, along with a marked rightward shift in the dose response curve [28]. Our CDDP‐resistant derivative satisfied these criteria, supporting its characterization as a successfully established CDDP‐resistant cell line. By using the resistant cells, we found that acquired resistance is accompanied by transcriptional and translational upregulation of ROBO1. Similar findings have been reported in esophageal carcinoma and osteosarcoma, where ROBO1 activation enhances radioresistance or pro‐survival signaling [48, 49]. This highlights the potential of targeting ROBO1 as a strategy to overcome acquired chemoresistance.

This study, however, has some limitations. As this study analyzed only six HPV(+) HNSCC cell lines, analysis of more HPV(+) cells and additional HPV(−) cells will further clarify the role of high DNA methylation in genesis and treatment response of HNSCC. In addition, this study lacked in vivo validation. Future investigations should focus on delineating downstream signaling networks of ROBO1 and testing the efficacy of ROBO1 inhibition in preclinical models. Furthermore, the number of progression events available for multivariable Cox regression analysis was relatively limited (n = 30), which may affect the stability and generalizability of the prognostic model. Although stepwise selection based on AIC was used as an exploratory variable‐selection procedure to reduce model complexity, the prognostic significance of ROBO1 methylation should be further validated in larger independent cohorts. The original FPKM‐based RNA‐seq analysis was performed using a limited number of biological replicates (n = 2 per group), while the main findings were independently confirmed by an additional count‐based differential expression analysis using edgeR, supporting the robustness of the transcriptomic results.

In conclusion, DNA hypermethylation causally correlates with better treatment response of HPV(+) HNSCC, and its chemosensitivity might be enhanced by silencing of methylation target genes. These findings raise the possibility that epigenetic or ROBO1‐targeted interventions may improve therapeutic outcomes in resistant HPV(+) HNSCC.
