Clinical perspective on management of key adverse events with sacituzumab govitecan
Authors: Sara M Tolaney, Guru P Sonpavde, Paolo Tarantino, Maryam B Lustberg, Hope S Rugo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSacituzumab govitecan (SG) is a first-in-class trophoblast cell surface antigen-2-directed antibody-drug conjugate that selectively delivers a well-characterized and potent payload of SN-38 to cancer cells and the surrounding environment. In the United States, SG is indicated for unresectable, locally advanced or metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and hormone receptor-positive (HR+)/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (immunohistochemistry 0, 1+, or 2+/in situ hybridization-negative) breast cancer (HR+/HER2− BC). Real-world management of SG-related adverse events can vary widely from clinical studies. Therefore, it is vital to provide clinicians with first-hand experience to aid in the management and support of patients before treatment initiation. In clinical studies (IMMU-132-01, ASCENT, TROPiCS-02, and TROPHY-U-01), the pooled incidence of key adverse events was 61% for neutropenia, 64% for diarrhea, 51% for fatigue, and 45% for alopecia. Here, we discuss the incidence of neutropenia, diarrhea, fatigue, and alopecia associated with SG treatment and provide recommendations on their management based on established guidelines and personal clinical experience. We also discuss important risk factors such as UGT1A1 polymorphism and advanced age (eg, ≥65 years). From our practical experience, we present a case of a patient with TNBC experiencing neutropenia and diarrhea, and another with HR+/HER2− BC experiencing diarrhea. Our review provides practicing oncologists considering SG therapy important information regarding its safe and appropriate use in real-world settings to maximize clinical benefit for patients with locally advanced or metastatic TNBC and HR+/HER2− BC.

With the expanding use of sacituzumab govitecan for cancer treatment, practicing oncologists need to be informed of the prevalence and real-world management of common adverse events to provide prompt, appropriate, and effective patient support and ensure maximum clinical benefit. In this review article, we discuss and provide recommendations based on our clinical experience managing sacituzumab govitecan-related adverse events of neutropenia, diarrhea, fatigue, and alopecia in patients with unresectable, locally advanced, or metastatic triple-negative breast cancer and hormone receptor-positive/human epithelial growth factor receptor 2-negative breast cancer.

Introduction

Sacituzumab govitecan (SG) is a first-in-class monoclonal antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) targeting trophoblast cell surface antigen (Trop)-2, a transmembrane calcium signal transducer that is overexpressed on many types of epithelial tumors, including breast.1–3 This Trop-2-directed ADC is composed of a humanized antibody conjugated to an SN-38 toxin payload (high drug: antibody ratio of ≈8:1) by a hydrolyzable linker.1 Upon antibody binding and hydrolysis, SN-38, an active metabolite of the topoisomerase-I inhibitor irinotecan,4 is delivered to cancer cells through internalization and to the surrounding microenvironment via the bystander effect.1,5 In particular, results from the phase I/II IMMU-132-01 basket trial (NCT01631552) of SG in various advanced epithelial cancers demonstrated encouraging outcomes.3,6–8

SG has been approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for patients with unresectable, locally advanced, or metastatic breast cancer (BC).9 SG is approved for treatment of patients with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) after receiving 2 or more prior systemic therapies, at least one for metastatic disease, based on statistically significant and clinically meaningful longer progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) than single-agent chemotherapy in the phase 3 ASCENT (NCT02574455) study.10 Consensus treatment guidelines include SG as a recommended option in this setting.11 SG is also approved for hormone receptor-positive (HR+), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2−; immunohistochemistry 0, 1+, or 2+/in situ hybridization-negative) BC after receipt of endocrine-based therapy and at least 2 additional systemic therapies in the metastatic setting based on statistically significant and clinically meaningful longer PFS and OS than single-agent chemotherapy in the phase 3 TROPiCS-02 (NCT03901339) study.12 Consensus treatment guidelines include SG as a recommended option for HR+/HER2− BC after 1 or 2 prior therapies (depending on the specific guideline) in the metastatic setting.11,13,14 Both ASCENT and TROPiCS-02 studies also showed that SG was associated with a statistically significant improvement in health-related quality of life versus chemotherapy control.15,16 Primary efficacy outcomes for these trials are summarized in Table 1.

Considering the expanding use of SG in real-world settings and its potential therapeutic role in earlier lines of BC therapy (including in combination with immunotherapy) and other types of Trop-2 overexpressing solid tumors, it is important for oncologists who are considering the use of SG for patient treatment to understand how to safely and appropriately use this therapy to ensure maximum clinical benefit for patients. We discuss 4 key SG-associated adverse events (neutropenia, diarrhea, fatigue, and alopecia), 3 of which can affect SG dosage and schedule, and provide recommendations on their management based on established treatment guidelines and our clinical experience. Example cases are provided for context.

Safety of SG

SG delivers cytotoxic chemotherapy (SN-38) to tumors and adjacent cancer cells (bystander effect) in concentrations 100- to 1000-times higher than those with standard irinotecan chemotherapy.4,17 Because SN-38 is an active metabolite of irinotecan, the toxic effects of SG are generally similar in type to irinotecan chemotherapy and manageable.4 In patients with metastatic TNBC (ASCENT; median 3 prior anticancer regimens in the metastatic setting for both arms10) and HR+/HER2− BC (TROPiCS-02; median 4 prior chemotherapy regimens in the metastatic setting for both arms16), there was a higher incidence of grade ≥3 treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) with SG versus chemotherapy, but the rates of discontinuation and dose reduction due to TEAEs were low in the SG group (≤6%), with 1 SG treatment-related fatal event (TROPiCS-02) (Table 1).

In metastatic HR+/HER2− BC (TROPiCS-02), patients who were able to maintain the recommended 10 mg/kg dose of SG on Days 1 and 8 of a 21-day cycle had a numerically higher benefit in median PFS, median OS, objective response rate, and clinical benefit rate compared with those who received a lower relative dose intensity18 (Table 2). However, patients across all relative dose intensities were able to benefit from SG therapy.18 Overall, these results support administration of the recommended SG dose of 10 mg/kg on Days 1 and 8 of a 21-day cycle, with subsequent dose modification as needed to manage adverse events.9

Pooled safety outcomes for key adverse events of neutropenia, diarrhea, fatigue, and alopecia from IMMU-132-01, ASCENT, TROPiCS-02, and TROPHY-U-01 are summarized in Table 1. Neutropenia and diarrhea are common grade ≥3 TEAEs associated with SG, with a pooled incidence of 46% and 11%, respectively19 (Table 1). The median time to onset for first any grade/grade ≥3 neutropenia and diarrhea were 2.3/2.3 and 1.9/2.1 weeks, respectively, in the pooled population.19

Fatigue and alopecia are distressing TEAEs associated with most cancer therapies regardless of patient age, with a pooled incidence of 51% and 45%, respectively, with SG treatment19 (Table 1). Generally, anticancer treatment-related fatigue can occur to varying degrees at any time during and after treatment,20 while alopecia is more gradual, usually becoming evident within 2-3 weeks after treatment starts, if not already present.21 The proportion of patients who experienced any-grade/grade ≥3 SG-related diarrhea, fatigue, and alopecia was relatively similar across metastatic TNBC (ASCENT)10 and HR+/HER2− BC (TROPiCS-02)16 (Table 1).

Safety populations of interest in SG studies include those with a UGT1A1 mutation and comorbidities. The *28 polymorphism in UGT1A1 is present in a minority of patients and has been associated with irinotecan-based therapeutic toxicity.22 UGT1A1*28 heterozygosity reduces SN-38 metabolism, and UGT1A1*28/*28 homozygosity increases the risk for certain adverse events, most commonly neutropenia and diarrhea.22,23 The pooled incidence of UGT1A1*28/*28, UGT1A1*28, and wild-type across SG trials was 11%, 40%, and 39%, respectively (Table 1); however, it should be noted that genetic testing to determine UGT1A1 status was not mandated.19 In patients with metastatic TNBC (ASCENT) and HR+/HER2− BC (TROPiCS-02), UGT1A1 mutation was associated with numerically increased rates of SG-related grade ≥3 TEAEs and TEAEs leading to dose reductions, as well as shortened the time to onset of certain TEAEs (eg, neutropenia24), compared with patients with wild-type UGT1A1.16,24–26 Specifically, UGT1A1*28/*28 homozygosity was associated with numerically higher rates of SG-related grade ≥3 neutropenia (ASCENT, 59%; TROPiCS-02, 64%) versus UGT1A1*28 heterozygosity (ASCENT, 47%; TROPiCS-02, 57%) or wild-type (ASCENT, 53%; TROPiCS-02, 45%).16,24–26 The same trend was also observed for grade ≥3 diarrhea, with UGT1A1*28/*28 being associated with a higher incidence relative to UGT1A1*28 and wild-type UGT1A1 in both ASCENT (15%, 9%, and 10%, respectively) and TROPiCS-02 (24%, 13%, and 6%, respectively) studies.16,24–26 The toxicity profile of SG was manageable irrespective of UGT1A1 mutation status in all key registrational studies and following existing adverse event management guidance per United States prescribing information (USPI).9,16,24–26 Therefore, routine UGT1A1 testing is not required at the start of treatment. Nonetheless, UGT1A1 testing may be considered in select patients at risk for UGT1A1 alterations, including those with Gilbert’s disease or those who may poorly tolerate severe toxicities, to take pre–emptive measures to mitigate toxicities (eg, premedication, patient education, over-the-counter antidiarrheals) if necessary.

Other important factors to consider before administering any drug include comorbidities, especially those associated with age. Generally, older patients (≥65 years) have a higher Comorbidity Index score and are less fit for cancer therapies, resulting in the receipt of less aggressive treatment, which can lower efficacy.27 Subgroup analysis in patients with BC treated with SG who were <65 and ≥65 years in ASCENT26 and TROPiCS-0218 revealed similar rates of TEAEs or treatment-related adverse events, respectively, between the age groups within each study. Although dose reductions tended to be more frequent in patients receiving SG who were ≥65 years (vs. those <65 years) in both metastatic TNBC (ASCENT, dose reductions due to TEAEs, 35% vs. 19%) and HR+/HER2− BC (TROPiCS-02, dose reductions due to TEAEs, 38% vs. 32%), there was no considerable impact on efficacy outcomes in either study; however, the sample sizes for patients ≥65 years was relatively small in both studies (efficacy and safety analysis sets: ASCENT, n = 49 for both sets; TROPiCS-02, n = 73 and 72, respectively).18,26 The favorable benefit/risk profile of SG supports its use in both younger and older populations with metastatic TNBC (ASCENT) and HR+/HER2− BC (TROPiCS-02), with the same TEAE management guidelines irrespective of age.1,18,26

From clinical trials to the real world: How we manage adverse events associated with SG

TEAEs related to SG are manageable with premedication, concomitant medication, lifestyle modifications, and dose reduction and/or interruption, in accordance with standard medical practice and treatment guidelines (Figure 1). The current USPI9 and published study protocols10,12,28 provide specific management recommendations for neutropenia and diarrhea, but not fatigue and alopecia. Here, we discuss management strategies that we have employed at our institutions for neutropenia, diarrhea, fatigue, and alopecia based on our real-world experience and in the context of, but not necessarily in full alignment with, current clinical trial protocol and USPI adverse event management recommendations, where applicable. Case 1: Neutropenia and diarrhea management in a patient with metastatic TNBC receiving SG.

History: A 57-year-old woman presented with metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) that progressed after first-line paclitaxel and pembrolizumab. She had new liver metastases and growth of her lung metastases. Alopecia occurred with first-line therapy, but there was no history of significant neuropathy. Comorbidities included hypothyroidism from pembrolizumab, but thyroid function was controlled on levothyroxine.

Chief initial concern: Programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) testing with 22C3 antibody on the breast biopsy was a Combined Positive Score of 11. Germline BRCA testing was negative, and genomic testing on the tumor revealed a TP53 mutation. The patient was started on SG 10 mg/kg IV on cycle 1, Day 1, without primary prophylactic G-CSF as she had not required G-CSF during first-line therapy. Her baseline ANC was >1500/mm3. Prophylaxis for nausea included dexamethasone (8 mg) and ondansetron (8 mg) on the day of SG infusion, and ondansetron was recommended on Days 2 and 3; loperamide was provided as needed for diarrhea. Her first dose of SG was well tolerated, without significant nausea, and loperamide 4 mg helped resolve loose bowel movements that started approximately 4 days after SG infusion. When the patient returned for cycle 1, Day 8 of SG infusion, she had an ANC of 800/mm3.

Treatment plan: SG infusion was withheld and one dose of filgrastim (5 μg/kg SQ) was given. The patient returned 3 days later with an ANC of 1300/mm3. A full dose of SG was given, and an on-body injector prefilled with pegfilgrastim (6 mg/0.6 mL) was used for prophylaxis.

Follow-up: The patient returned 14 days later for cycle 2, Day 1 of SG with an ANC of 1200/mm3 and was treated with a full dose of SG. Filgrastim was given for administration on Days 2, 3, and 4. She returned for cycle 2, Day 8 of SG therapy with an ANC of 1400/mm3 and received a full dose of SG and continued with the on-body injector prefilled with pegfilgrastim.

Concern at follow-up: Approximately 1 hour after SG infusion on cycle 2, Day 8, while in the infusion center, the patient developed acute diarrhea with abdominal cramping.

Treatment plan: Atropine (0.5-3 mg SQ) was administered, and gastrointestinal symptoms resolved. Atropine was then administered prophylactically with future infusions.

Follow-up and outcome: The patient returned for cycle 3, Day 1, with an ANC of 1600/mm3 and continued with SG therapy. Secondary prophylaxis with G-CSF was administered with the remaining SG cycles.

Case 2: Diarrhea management in a patient with metastatic HR+/HER2− BC receiving SG.

History: A 68-year-old woman presented with advanced metastatic hormone receptor-positive (HR+)/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2−) (IHC 0) BC who progressed following prior hormonal targeted therapies, followed by paclitaxel, and most recently, capecitabine chemotherapy. She had a history of hypertension and hyperlipidemia.

Chief initial concern: The patient recently progressed on capecitabine chemotherapy with increased symptomatic osseous and liver metastases. She was experiencing increased back pain and right upper quadrant discomfort at the site of an enlarging liver metastasis.

Treatment plan: The patient began SG 10 mg/kg IV infusions on Days 1 and 8 with prophylactic pegfilgrastim (6 mg/0.6 mL) given her advanced age and multiple comorbidities, and infusion reaction and emesis prophylaxis.

Follow-up: The patient was evaluated and underwent laboratory assessment before each SG dose in the infusion center.

Concern at follow-up: The patient noted that her pain improved after cycle 1. She was feeling well except for grade 1 diarrhea with loose watery stools 3-4 times daily that would start 3-4 days after each infusion. She had problems with constipation in the past and had been hesitant to use loperamide.

Treatment plan: C. diff assessment was negative. Blood laboratory assessments were within normal limits. Loperamide was started at 4 mg initially, followed by 2 mg with every episode of diarrhea for a maximum of 16 mg daily. The patient was also given a prescription for 5 mg diphenoxylate-0.05 mg atropine to use up to 4 times a day if loperamide was not effective. She was also encouraged to orally hydrate and notify her oncology team if diarrhea symptoms were not responding to these management strategies.

Follow-up and outcome: After using loperamide as needed for diarrhea, symptoms were controlled. Cycle 2, Day 1 of SG was administered at full dose without delay. A regimen for early loperamide therapy for diarrhea was again reinforced to the patient (as outlined in the Diarrhea subsection) as part of chemotherapy education during cycle 2, Day 1. The patient again experienced diarrhea that was treated immediately with loperamide at home, leading to early control and absence of progression to grade 2 diarrhea. The patient had renewed confidence that early and regular use of loperamide, as needed, was a proactive approach for managing her symptoms.

Neutropenia

Complete blood counts must be obtained before each SG infusion as neutropenia is very common, as early as the first cycle of SG treatment. We manage neutropenia based on individual patient circumstances, and as such, this adverse event has the greatest variation in management, especially when asymptomatic. Clinical study protocols10,12 and the USPI9 recommend SG administration when the absolute neutrophil count (ANC) is >1500/mm3 on Day 1 and without neutropenic fever and ANC >1000/mm3 on Day 8 of the 21-day administration cycle. However, depending on differences in institutional guidelines, some providers are choosing to use an ANC of <1000/mm3 as the cutoff for withholding SG infusion on all SG administration days, regardless of treatment day. If the patient’s ANC is <1000/mm3 on Day 1, we withhold SG and either wait and recheck in ≈1 week (or again in 2-3 days, depending on the case) to see if the ANC has spontaneously increased to >1000/mm3 or consider treatment with short-acting granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF; eg, filgrastim) daily for 3-5 days, depending on how low the ANC level is and the patient’s history with neutropenia and G-CSF use. Once the SG Day 1 dose can be administered, Day 8 dosing will be adjusted. If the patient’s ANC is <1000/mm3 on Day 8, we follow the same management strategy for neutropenia as used for Day 1. However, if Day 8 dosing is delayed by less than 7 days, we will restart the next SG cycle at the originally planned time. If Day 8 dosing is delayed more than 7 days, cycle 2, Day 1 is adjusted to allow for a minimum of 14 days between the Day 8 dose and Day 1 of the next cycle. SG dose modification per the USPI can also be considered (ie, reduce SG dose to 7.5 mg/kg for the first occurrence, then reduce to 5 mg/kg subsequently, as needed9), depending on the severity of neutropenia.

Primary prophylaxis with G-CSF is recommended per USPI starting in the first cycle of treatment in all patients with an increased risk of febrile neutropenia, including patients aged ≥65 years and/or with previous neutropenia, poor performance stats, organ dysfunction, or multiple comorbidities.9 In the phase 2 PRIMED study (NCT05520723) investigating SG in combination with primary prophylactic use of G-CSF in patients with metastatic TNBC and HR+/HER2− BC, G-CSF was administered to patients for 2 consecutive days, 48 hours after administration of SG (Days 3, 4, 10, and 11) during the first 2 cycles (with possible extension to the next cycle per physician discretion).29 Rates of neutropenia (28%)29 were lower compared with those reported in the ASCENT (64%)10 and TROPiCS-02 (71%)16,30 studies (Table 1). We generally rely on consensus guidelines (eg, American Society of Clinical Oncology [ASCO], European Society for Medical Oncology [ESMO], Advanced Breast Cancer) and patient risk factors when assessing which patients would be best candidates for primary or secondary prophylactic G-CSF from cycle 1. Although the febrile neutropenia rate in trials with SG is <20% (the threshold suggested by guidelines for primary G-CSF prophylaxis31,32; Table 1), patients with risk factors for life-threatening febrile neutropenia may still warrant primary prophylaxis from cycle 1, given that the highest risk of febrile neutropenia has been observed in the first 1-2 cycles of cytotoxic chemotherapy. Furthermore, the rate of febrile neutropenia in the real world may be higher than in a clinical trial population. G-CSF should be considered in patients at high risk of poor clinical outcomes, including those with prolonged neutropenia, ANC <1000/mm3, febrile neutropenia, and serious infections. If the patient was given G-CSF with their last therapy due to neutropenia and/or has a history of neutropenic infection/febrile neutropenia or poor bone marrow function and borderline ANC levels, we recommend considering G-CSF use as primary prophylaxis. Consideration of primary prophylaxis for elderly age, advanced tumor burden, poor performance status, frailty, and comorbidities is also recommended. If the patient has co-occurring diarrhea with grade ≥3 neutropenia, we recommend considering administration of G-CSF as secondary prophylaxis even after SG administration, even if no fever is present, due to the increased risk of sepsis.

In patients who experienced an SG dose delay due to treatment-emergent neutropenia in their previous cycle, we consider secondary prophylaxis in subsequent cycles with short-acting G-CSF daily on Days 2-4 after the SG Day 1 dose, then continue with long-acting G-CSF after the Day 8 dose. Long-acting G-CSF may be administered through an on-body injector, loaded on Day 8 or as an injection on Day 9. If, for some reason, long-acting G-GSF cannot be obtained for use, we continue using short-acting G-CSF daily for at least 5-7 days. However, we have found that there are more United States insurance coverage issues with short-acting G-CSF versus long-acting forms, and long-acting G-CSF works well when there is a 14-day gap in between dosing. In general, G-CSF tends to be preferred for initial management of neutropenia, followed by SG dose reduction if neutropenia persists. Currently, changes to the administration of SG outside of Days 1 and 8 of a 21-day cycle to manage neutropenia have not been evaluated.

SG USPI9 and study protocols10,12 recommend closely monitoring patients for TEAEs, regardless of UGT1A1 status, and do not recommend routine UGT1A1 testing. Although not required, UGT1A1 genotyping may be considered before initiation of SG to assist in understanding potential TEAEs that patients may experience while on therapy and help inform their optimal early management. If the patient is known to be UGT1A1*28/*28 homozygous, primary and secondary prophylaxis with G-CSF should be considered when appropriate. Additionally, consider SG dose modification to manage severe neutropenia according to the USPI, if needed. Bilirubin levels should be routinely monitored because UGT1A1 ­polymorphism has an additive effect with high pretreatment total bilirubin on SN-38 clearance (regardless of demographic risk factors such as age >65 years).33

Diarrhea

For acute, excessive cholinergic responses (eg, diarrhea, abdominal cramping, salivation) within 24 hours of SG infusion, we recommend atropine injection (0.5-3 mg, subcutaneous [SQ]), followed by premedication with atropine for subsequent SG infusions (consider building into treatment plan if not already present). For diarrhea, we typically manage with patient education, dietary modification, over-the-counter antidiarrheals (eg, loperamide), and additional supportive measures as indicated. It is also important to rule out possible infectious causes of diarrhea, such as Clostridioides difficile (C. diff); antibiotics can be administered as clinically indicated for infectious cases.

All patients should be advised on diarrhea management before starting SG. Patients should have over-the-counter antidiarrheals available while being treated with SG. Proper up-front education on hydration and use of antidiarrheals is important. This includes counseling on when to stop antidiarrheals if patients have concerns with constipation, which may have been experienced with prior anticancer regimens. Dietary modifications include BRAT diet (eg, bananas, rice, applesauce, and toast), small frequent meals, adequate fluid intake of clear liquids to maintain hydration and electrolytes, and discontinuation of lactose-containing foods and drinks containing alcohol. Use of probiotics can be discussed as an option to consider, though there is limited evidence to support the benefit of probiotics for diarrhea. However, it should be noted that some probiotics (eg, those containing Saccharomyces boulardii) are associated with the rare but potential risk of fungemia in individuals with a central venous catheter or central line.34

For SG-related any-grade diarrhea, we recommend initiating treatment with 4 mg of oral loperamide, followed by 2 mg thereafter with each episode, to a maximum dose of 16 mg/day until resolved. If diarrhea is not resolved after 24 hours or the patient is experiencing 3-4 stools per day when at a near maximum loperamide dose, oral 5 mg diphenoxylate-0.05 mg atropine sulfate should be considered 4 times daily, to a maximum dose of 20 mg diphenoxylate-0.2 mg atropine sulfate per day until control is achieved. Discontinue diphenoxylate-atropine use if clinical improvement is not seen within 10 days of administering the maximum dose. Because the severity of diarrhea associated with SG can vary widely by case, some patients may benefit from alternating loperamide with diphenoxylate-atropine administration. We have used loperamide prophylactically (not routine) and administered loperamide alternating with diphenoxylate-atropine. The PRIMED study included an investigation of the use of prophylactic loperamide with SG therapy, with loperamide given for 3 consecutive days after SG administration (ie, Days 2, 3, and 4 and Days 9, 10, and 11) for the first 2 cycles (with the option to extend to the next cycle based on the physician’s discretion).29,35 Rates of diarrhea (34%)29 were lower compared with those reported in the ASCENT (65%)10 and TROPiCS-02 (62%)16 studies (Table 1). Constipation was observed in 46% of patients in PRIMED; 29 whereas rates of 37% and 19% were observed in ASCENT and TROPiCS-02, respectively.10,16 Octreotide (100-150 μg, may increase to 500-2000 μg intravenous [IV] or SQ 3 times daily; discontinue within 24 hours of resolution to reduce risk of ileus) can also be effective with unusual cases of persisting diarrhea. However, the octreotide injection is painful, and ice on the injection site may be helpful. Additionally, patients should hydrate with oral or IV fluids as needed.

Diarrhea can also be responsive to SG dose modification, which should be considered in severe cases per USPI (ie, reduce SG dose to 7.5 mg/kg for the first occurrence, then reduce to 5 mg/kg subsequently, as needed9) depending on the severity. SG dose modification may also be considered in patients with persistent grade 2 diarrhea who have UGT1A1 polymorphisms. Keep in mind, patients may experience more than 1 SG-associated toxicity at a time, and dose modification could be a way to manage multiple toxicities. Similar to neutropenia, each case of diarrhea is different and requires individualized care.

It is also important to keep a broad differential and consider other possible causes of diarrhea, such as infections causing refractoriness to the antidiarrheal (eg, C. diff) or poor metabolism. If the diarrhea does not respond to medication, consider the timing. If the patient is refractory to antidiarrheals during cycle 1, it is possible that the symptoms may be SG-related and/or the patient has reduced metabolic capacity. Some of us routinely test for C. diff and consider testing for metabolic issues during cycle 1, regardless. If the patient becomes refractory to antidiarrheals at a later cycle (eg, cycle 7), alternative diagnoses should be investigated to determine the primary cause.

Fatigue

For all cases of fatigue, we recommend that healthcare providers discuss with patients that daily physical activity (ie, increased movement), not necessarily exercise, is usually helpful. However, increasing physical activity has a limited effect on fatigue and may not be possible in all cases. In terms of supportive medications, some of us consider the use of stimulants (eg, low-dose methylphenidate) per ASCO and/or ESMO guidelines on a case-by-case basis, but results have been varied and not significant. For patients experiencing fatigue, it is also important to assess possible alternative causes, such as anemia, hypothyroidism, or dehydration from diarrhea. Once nonpharmacological and pharmacological options are exhausted and symptoms are confirmed to be SG-related, we broadly recommend SG dose modification as described in the USPI for adverse event management.9 SG dose modification is recommended if fatigue symptoms are affecting the patient’s quality of life, and assuming other potential causes have been ruled out. Again, patients may experience more than 1 SG-associated toxicity at a time, and this should be factored in when considering SG dose modification.

Alopecia

While not life-threatening, alopecia is one of the more significant adverse events affecting the quality of life (especially psychological and social aspects) of patients with any cancer, yet its impact on patients is often underestimated.21 Although there are no evidence-based recommendations on the management of alopecia, we provide some consultation points for healthcare providers and patients considering SG use. Physicians should discuss the high potential of drug-induced alopecia with every patient about to start SG therapy. Alopecia occurs early after starting treatment with SG, and we recommend discussing the use of wigs within 1 week of treatment if the patient does not already have alopecia as a result of prior regimens. We also recommend directing the patient to any known support groups (online or in-person). For the prevention of hair loss, there is limited data on the use of cold caps with SG therapy. Additionally, scalp cooling is not generally recommended in patients with scalp metastases or central nervous system malignancies.36,37 The Dana-Farber Cancer Institute is currently sponsoring a clinical study with support from Paxman Coolers Ltd (NCT04986579) on the use of scalp cooling for alopecia prevention in patients with metastatic BC after receipt of standard-of-care treatment, including SG.

Conclusions

Although adverse event management guidelines are generally followed, there are often clinical requirements specific to individual patient cases, and sometimes a modified approach is considered. Patient characteristics often deviate from the strict inclusion criteria of clinical study protocols, and each case requires individual care throughout the course of treatment. Key adverse events associated with SG include neutropenia, diarrhea, fatigue, and alopecia, which are typically reversible and manageable with patient education, monitoring, proper and timely treatment, and support. Successful adverse event management practices in real-world settings will help to ensure maximum safety and clinical benefit for patients receiving SG.
