Texture and Color Enhanced Imaging in the Diagnosis of Colonic Neoplasia: A Systematic Review and Analysis
Authors: Ciaran Judge, David Law, Glynis Jones, Sherman Picardo, Krish Ragunath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionImage enhanced endoscopy (IEE) can augment the detection of colorectal cancer (CRC) and its precursor lesions, potentially reducing incidence and mortality. Early data on texture and color enhancement imaging (TXI) support its use for neoplasia screening. This study aimed to systematically review and perform a meta‐analysis to assess the impact of TXI on colonic adenoma detection.MethodsThe systematic review and meta‐analysis followed PRISMA guidelines. Studies comparing TXI with white light imaging (WLI) were eligible for inclusion. Searches were conducted through relevant databases and major conferences up to July 1, 2024. The primary outcome was ADR, with secondary outcomes including polyp detection rate (PDR), mean number of adenomas per patient (MAP), and size, location, morphology, and withdrawal time.ResultsFrom 1270 results, 4 studies were included in the meta‐analysis (n = 5481). TXI significantly increased ADR compared to WLI (55.8% vs. 47.8%, RR 1.24, p < 0.001), PDR (63.7% vs. 54.7%, RR 1.23, p < 0.001), and MAP (mean difference 0.41, p = 0.005). and improved detection of proximal lesions (MD 0.21, 95% CI [0.15–0.27], p < 0.001, I 2 = 0%). Pooled withdrawal times were not different between groups, with no differences between endoscopist experience or quality of bowel preparation, where reported. Sub‐analysis further demonstrated superiority of TXI, with an absolute difference in ADR of 14.2%.ConclusionTXI significantly improves ADR, PDR, and MAP compared to WLI, highlighting its potential to enhance CRC screening efficacy without impact from user experience, bowel prep, or withdrawal times. TXI is a useful and practical adjunct to enhance the detection of colonic neoplasia.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide. Strategies such as population‐based bowel cancer screening programmes have been successful in reducing the incidence and mortality of CRC [1]. These programmes rely on the detection and resection of early‐stage colonic neoplasia to prevent its progression to CRC [2]. Advances in quality control, technology, and publication of societal guidelines have contributed to an improvement in the detection of early lesions, most commonly defined by the adenoma detection rate (ADR) [3]. Adenomas per colonoscopy (APC, otherwise termed mean adenomas per patient [MAP]) and adenoma miss rate (AMR) have also been shown to correlate with ADR [4]. Such metrics are designed to provide benchmarks for quality control with an aim to limit the incidence of post‐colonoscopy colorectal cancer (PCCRC), which has been reported to occur in 3%–9% of all cases of CRC [5, 6, 7]. Pivotal data from California demonstrated that every 1% increase in ADR resulted in a corresponding 3% decrease in PCCRC and a 5% reduction in PCCRC mortality [8]. This effect has been shown to persist even as ADR has risen toward 40%, suggesting that all practitioners should be aspiring toward maximal ADR to reduce the potential for PCCRC [9].

Efforts to improve ADR have progressively recruited modern technologies which are now no longer exclusive to referral centers. While artificial intelligence and dye‐based chromoendoscopy may not be readily available in all units, high‐definition white light imaging (WLI) is the norm in many regions. However, high‐definition WLI has only modestly improved rates of neoplasia detection [10]. Several modalities of virtual chromoendoscopy (henceforth referred to as image enhanced endoscopy [IEE]) are now readily available and have been variably associated with improved ADR, polyp detection rate (PDR), MAP, and sessile serrated lesions (SSL) detection rate when compared with WLI [10, 11, 12, 13]. Controversy remains on the efficacy of specific types of IEE for enhancing lesion detection, so further exploration of this space is warranted.

The chosen modality of IEE often depends on local availability of endoscopic equipment, for example, narrow band imaging (NBI, Olympus, Tokyo Japan), linked color imaging (LCI) and blue light imaging (BLI, Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan), and i‐SCAN (Pentax, Tokyo, Japan). Texture and Color Enhancement Imaging (TXI) is the most recent option in the IEE toolkit and is an embedded function on the EVIS‐X1 CV‐1500 endoscopy system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) which supports 4 K image resolution with the 1500‐series colonoscopes, but also supports previous generation endoscopes. TXI has been designed to enhance lesion detection through post‐processing brightness correction of the dark part of the image; color difference expansion processing; and texture component emphasis to highlight differences in mucosal patterns—thereby accentuating the contrast between bright and dark areas to enhance visibility and detectability of lesions [14]. Established modalities of IEE, such as NBI, have conflicting data over their comparative utility in detection of colonic neoplasia. Dark images, bowel preparation, and endoscopist experience have been shown to limit potential beneficial effect [15]. Contrast enhancement using LCI has shown promise through increasing detectability and visibility of sessile and flat lesions [16, 17, 18]. Therefore, it is important to review the effect of TXI as a newcomer to this class to determine its effect on detection of colonic neoplasia.

To date, studies of TXI suggest improved outcomes when compared to WLI [19]. Given the potential for meaningful clinical impact, we aimed to conduct a comprehensive synthesis of available data through a systematic review, coupled with a meta‐analysis, to clarify the true impact of TXI on colonic adenoma detection.

Method

This systematic review and meta‐analysis adhered to the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) statement. The PRISMA checklist can be viewed in Table 1, Data S1. The project was registered with PROSPERO, the international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD42024575920).

Study Outcomes

The primary arm of this study was to determine the ADR of TXI when compared to WLI. Other outcomes included PDR, MAP, and further subdivisions where available including detection based on lesion size, location, and morphology.

ADR was defined by the proportion of colonoscopies where one or more adenomas were detected, with adenomatous polyp confirmed on histopathological analysis. MAP was defined as the mean number of adenomas detected per patient; PDR was the mean number of all recorded documented polyps. SSLs were confirmed on histopathological analysis.

Study Eligibility

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, and propensity score matching studies comparing the outcomes of TXI versus WLI were eligible for inclusion in the primary arm of this study. By necessity, the Olympus EVIS X1 system was used in all cases. This system supports newer 1500 series colonoscopes but also retrofits previous series, for example, HQ190 series. Studies involving adult patients undergoing colonoscopy for colorectal cancer (CRC) screening, polyp surveillance, or post‐resection follow‐up were included. Patients undergoing colonoscopy for suspected or confirmed inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or polyposis syndromes were excluded.

Search Strategy

The search strategy was devised and executed in association with a professional librarian with expertise in the field. The databases searched included PubMed, Embase, Medline, Web of Science Core Collection, the Cochrane Library, in addition to ClinicalTrials.gov and International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP), with searches conducted from inception up to July 1, 2024. The search strategies prioritized sensitivity to maximize retrieval of relevant records. Abstracts from international conferences including Digestive Disease Week (USA), United European Gastroenterology Week, and Asian Pacific Digestive Week were also screened. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and keywords related to TXI, WLI, colonoscopy, polyp, and adenoma detection were used in combination with Boolean operators to construct search queries. Details of the search strategy are provided in Appendix A–C. Duplicate results were removed prior to review.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Two authors (CJ and DL) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all articles using Covidence (Covidence, Melbourne, Australia). Articles identified as duplicates on further review were removed, as were conference posters or abstracts related to a published article. Full‐text articles of potentially eligible studies were assessed for final inclusion. In the event of disagreement, consensus was reached through discussion among the first and second authors, with involvement of the supervising author if needed. Baseline data including first author, publication year, country of origin, number of centres, study type, colonoscopy indication, and endoscopic system were abstracted into a standardized template. Clinical data collected included ADR, MAP, PDR, and withdrawal times. When values were not specified, they were calculated manually from available data.

Risk of Bias

If sufficient studies were identified, then the risk of bias in included studies was to be assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB2) for RCTs [20], and The Risk Of Bias In Non‐randomized Studies—of Interventions (ROBINS‐I) assessment tool [21]. Studies were to be rated as low, moderate, or high risk of bias based on predefined criteria. The risk of publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and further analyzed using Egger's test.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata Statistical Software: Release 18 (Statacorp, College Station, Texas, USA). The risk ratio (RR) was calculated for between‐group effect sizes for dichotomous variables, whereas the mean difference (MD) was used for continuous variables. Analyses were performed using a restricted maximal likelihood (REML) random effects model. Outcomes were reported with a 95% confidence interval with corresponding p‐values. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated using the chi‐squared test at the I 2 statistic (0%–25% no homogeneity, 25%–50% mild heterogeneity, 50%–75% moderate heterogeneity, > 75% high heterogeneity).

Results

Study Selection and Design

A search of databases, clinical trial registries, conferences, and abstracts returned 1270 results, of which 837 were eligible for screening (Figure 1). 31 results underwent full text review, with 4 ultimately included in the meta‐analysis (n = 5481; 2 RCTs, 1 propensity score matched cohort study and 1 retrospective cohort study) [19, 22, 23, 24]. Flat Lesions, Small Lesions and SSLs

Where reported, TXI was numerically but not significantly associated with improved detection of flat lesions and proximal colonic lesions (MD 0.21, 95% CI [0.15–0.27], p = 0.57, I 2 = 0%) (Figure 5). SSL detection rate was significantly superior in the TXI arm (RR 1.44; 95% CI [1.02–2.02], p ≤ 0.01). Lesion size was reported in a variety of ranges, making meta‐analysis difficult; however, as expected, detection of smaller lesions (< 10 mm) was significantly greater in the TXI arm where it could be analysed (Antonelli et al.; Young et al.) with this effect lost when evaluating larger lesions > 10 mm. Toyoshima et al. found the MAP for polyps < 6 mm to be significantly higher in the TXI arm, but not for polyps ≥ 6 mm.

Pooled proximal adenoma detection rate. Meta‐analysis of proximal adenoma detection rate in studies where outcome was available.

Withdrawal Times and Other Notable Outcomes

There was no significant difference in withdrawal times for the initial analysis between groups (9.48 min (SD 2.96) vs. 9.58 min (SD 3.29), MD −0.06, 95% CI (−0.57–0.46), p = 0.8338) (Figure 6). Given the overall withdrawal times were likely to be prolonged by the routine use of dye spray in the Toyoshima study, sub‐analysis expectedly demonstrated pooled withdrawal times were shorter overall, with the effect most notable in the TXI arm (TXI = 7.73 min (SD 2.58) vs. WLI = 8.07 min (SD 3.02), MD −0.31, 95% CI (−0.39—0.23), p < 0.01, I 2 = 0%).

There were no reported differences in bowel preparation between groups; however, formal analysis of this was lacking. When assessing user experience, Young et al. reported persistence of increased MAP for TXI across both high‐detectors (ADR > 55%, 2.82 vs. 1.13, p = 0.006) and standard‐detectors (ADR < 55%, 1.47 vs. 0.88, p = 0.03) in their cohort.

Sub‐Analysis

We decided to perform a sub‐analysis excluding Toyoshima et al. due to its relative size and routine use of dye‐based chromoendoscopy, which we postulated may have influenced the effect of TXI and may have raised the baseline neoplasia detection rate to a point where further improvements with TXI would have been less evident. On sub‐analysis (n = 1541 patients [TXI: n = 775, WLI: n = 766]) there remained a significantly higher ADR in the TXI group (Table 4, Figure 7) with improved heterogeneity (57.8% vs. 43.6%, RR 1.32 95% CI (1.2–1.46), p < 0.0001, I 2 = 0). The absolute difference in ADR was 14.2%. PDR was higher (76.3% vs. 58.6%, RR 1.30 95% CI (1.21–1.39), I 2 = 0%), as was MAP (0.49, 95% CI [0.31–0.68], p < 0.01, I 2 = 0%).

Discussion

This study is the first early systematic review and meta‐analysis comparing the effect of TXI versus WLI on the detection of colonic neoplasia in average‐risk patients. This study adds to the increasing evidence supporting the clinical utility of virtual chromoendoscopy in detecting gastrointestinal lesions.

TXI leverages post‐processing contrast enhancement for better visibility and detectability of polyps, whilst retaining a brighter image more akin to WLI. It is a push‐button embedded function on the Olympus EVIS X1 endoscopy system. It offers two separate modes, however mode 1 is generally preferred as if offers superior contrast compared with mode 2, and was the mode used in all of the studies in this review [25]. Although a relatively novel form of IEE, published series appear consistent in demonstrating superiority in ADR, PDR and MAP when compared with WLI. This review revealed TXI to have an ADR of 55.8% vs. 47.8% (RR 1.24 95% CI [1.1–1.39]), with an absolute difference in ADR of 8%. On sub‐analysis, this absolute difference increased to 14.2%. This is consistent with a meta‐analysis by Wang et al. comparing LCI (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) which demonstrated that LCI significantly improved ADR (51.3% vs. 43.8, p = 0.0001), PDR, MAP and MPP in comparison with WLI, with an absolute difference in ADR of 7.5% [26]. LCI operates on a similar principal to TXI, by enhancing color differences in the mucosa to highlight lesions and optimize visibility. Both techniques appear to perform better when compared to more “established” forms of IEE such as NBI or BLI. These tools remain controversial for neoplasia detection and may be more suitable for lesion characterization, as series have reported excessive image darkness, interference from residual stool or poorer outcomes with non‐experts [15, 27].

TXI does not appear to suffer from these inconsistencies. Indeed, TXI appears to be generalizable and practical to implement, which is key to adoption. A large proportion of colonoscopies are performed outside of specialist centers, so efforts to optimize ADR must be reasonable to implement without excessive cost, and considerate of endoscopist experience and factors affecting provision of endoscopy lists. Regarding cost, TXI comes as standard on the EVIS X1 system and does not require adjunctive devices or dyes. The effect of baseline ADR does not appear to influence outcomes, as found in the RCT by Young et al. Here, TXI yielded a significant increase in MAP between high and low‐detectors (mean difference 1.69, p = 0.006, mean difference 0.59, p = 0.03, respectively) as defined by an ADR > 55% or < 55%, respectively [22]. This may encourage non‐tertiary centres to incorporate TXI into their practice. There were no differences in outcomes reported with bowel preparation, which is in contrast to prior studies of NBI showing loss of effect on ADR with preparation classed as “adequate” or below [15]. In addition, pooled withdrawal times were numerically shorter in the TXI group in both analyses. In summary, this meta‐analysis suggests that TXI optimized lesion detection regardless of baseline operator ADR, while not adding to withdrawal times or being influenced by bowel preparation, supporting its inclusion in both the specialist and non‐specialist settings.

This study highlights that the detection of small lesions, flat lesions, and those in the proximal colon appeared to be more likely in the TXI arm. These lesions are often easy to overlook during colonoscopy. Their clinical relevance may be debatable on an individual basis—or even more collectively when relating to diminutive adenomas. However, the contribution of such lesions to MAP, risk stratification, and their phenotypic association with SSLs warrants acknowledgment. Firstly, MAP has been shown to be a useful correlate with ADR, and with PCCRC and CRC‐related mortality [4, 28]. MAP benefits from encouraging complete clearance of lesions and minimizing any negative bias for multiple polyp detection, the so‐called one‐and‐done effect. This study demonstrates that TXI outperforms WLI in MAP in both the primary and sub‐analysis. Secondly, this phenotype of small, flat lesions is associated with SSLs, which are known to account for up to 30% of PCCRC. All three studies reporting SSLs showed trends for increased detection favoring TXI, which on meta‐analysis proved significant. The lack of significance in each study was potentially explained by underpowering, particularly as series evaluating LCI such as that by Suzuki et al. have reported a significant increase in the detection of serrated lesions [11]. Finally, while these lesions may not be individually sinister, Antonelli et al. found TXI yielded more “high risk” patients (as per ESGE criteria) versus the WLI group (26.7% and 19.9%, respectively (RR 1.34; 95% CI 1.03–1.74)), primarily driven through multiplicity (> 4 adenomas). Such an increase would have knock‐on effects on demand for endoscopic surveillance and requires further evaluation to determine its clinical relevance.

We performed a sub‐analysis to consider the effect of excluding the study by Toyoshima et al. This was conducted for several reasons; the size of the contribution to the final cohort; considerable calculated heterogeneity; the routine use of dye‐based chromoendoscopy; and the notably smaller absolute difference in ADR between groups when compared to the other studies. We postulate this may have been due to either the impedance of the effect of the TXI due to indigo‐carmine chromoendoscopy, or a higher “baseline” level of lesion detection limiting further improvement by TXI. The authors gave three reasons for the use of indigo‐carmine. First, that this technique increases polyp detection when compared to traditional endoscopy and is widely adopted in clinical practice, which we would argue may not be the case for routine endoscopy in Western practice. Second, that indigo‐carmine dye may complementarily highlight the blue end of the spectrum to the reddish contrast of TXI. Third, that no data exist on the utility of chromoendoscopy and TXI in lesion detection. Our exclusion of this study in sub‐analysis exclusion led to a significantly larger ADR in the TXI group, with a more pronounced absolute difference in ADR of 14.2%, and shorter but not significantly different withdrawal times between groups. There was also a considerable improvement in heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%). This supports our impression and gives additional strength to both the validity of this review and to the preferential use of TXI over dye‐based chromoendoscopy.

Recent publications have highlighted that longer withdrawal times are associated with higher rates of lesion detection and reduced rates of missed lesions. A multicenter tandem RCT by Zhao et al. showed improvements in ADR, AMR, and advanced AMR with a 9‐min withdrawal versus a 6‐min withdrawal [29]. In that study, ADR was 42.3% versus 33.5% (p = 0.02) in the 9‐min versus 6‐min groups, respectively. In our meta‐analysis, mean pooled withdrawal times were 7.73 min (SD 2.58) versus 8.07 (SD 3.02) in the TXI and WLI groups, respectively (after exclusion of Toyoshima et al. as mentioned above); yet despite the shortened withdrawal times, pooled ADR for these three studies was higher (57.8% vs. 43.6% in favor of TXI).

The reason for the difference is unclear, as all cases were performed in tertiary hospitals, but may in part reflect use of newer equipment which is not discussed in the former study. The impact on neoplasia detection of newer generation, 4 K compatible processors and endoscopes remains to be clarified. The EVIS X1 system is compatible with 4 K image resolution; however, this is only possible with the latest generation, 1500‐series endoscopes. The system supports the use of previous generation colonoscopes, for example, HQ290; however, the image generated is HD rather than 4 K. Whether this makes a difference is controversial and uncertain. Sakamoto et al. conducted multivariate analysis on the effect of newer endoscopes on ADR and MAP and found it to be significant (ADR: OR 1.5, 95% CI [1.2–1.9], p < 0.001; MAP: OR 1.7 95% CI [1.1–2.7], p = 0.025). Of note, no study exclusively used newer generation colonoscopes. There are very limited data on the effect of these newer systems; however, previous studies did find improvements in ADR with newer generations of HD colonoscopy and newer generations of NBI [10, 15]. It is therefore conceivable that as 4 K systems embedded with adjuncts such as TXI are increasingly adopted, ADR and MAP may continue to be driven upwards. The clinical implications of improving the ADR to > 50% require further clarification, but previous data reported continued correlation between ADR approaching 40% with PCCRC—a key consideration regarding clinical utility [9]. If ADR > 50% can be consistently achieved with embedded functions like TXI, then perhaps the need for longer withdrawal times may be negated, with its associated positive effects on endoscopy lists. Further research is required to determine the effect of various withdrawal times with and without IEE.

There are limitations to our study. First, there are variations in study design as mentioned previously. The largest cohort (Toyoshima et al.) was a propensity score matched (PSM) observational cohort. Although this leaves the study more open to bias, PSM is effective at matching groups to eliminate confounding. It must be noted that a common theme between endoscopic interventional studies is that of operator bias; however, the design of such studies necessitates that endoscopists cannot be blinded and therefore will be open to bias. Funnel plots and Egger's tests performed for ADR did suggest a moderate likelihood of publication bias where small studies with larger effect sizes may be over‐represented, which was not seen on assessment for PDR; however, interpretation of these results is limited by the small number of studies. The effect of using indigo‐carmine chromoendoscopy is discussed previously and was assessed in sub‐analysis, an approach which also resolved the heterogeneity seen on initial review. As mentioned, we could not perform sub‐analysis based on lesion size. However, the available data is reported above and consistently trends toward superior detection of smaller lesions. In the study by Sakamoto et al., they report a higher rate of pre‐treatment workup with TXI versus WLI (18% vs. 9%); however, there were more screening and FIT positive cases in the WLI group (36.1% vs. 24.5%), likely offsetting any impact from this. Although all studies necessitated the use of the EVIS X1 system to access TXI, there were differences in the generation of colonoscope used within and between studies. As newer generation colonoscopes become more widespread, further studies evaluating potential differences between generations will be illuminating. Positives of this study include its novelty, size of the cohort, and timeliness in the comparison of IEE‐modalities, particularly in an environment where evaluation of all the components of the toolkit to increase ADR is becoming increasingly important.

This study is the first systematic review and meta‐analysis evaluating the effect of TXI versus WLI in the detection of colonic neoplasia. It demonstrates a consistent trend toward higher ADR, PDR, and MAP and may represent a readily available method for making a significant impact on reducing CRC‐related morbidity and mortality.
