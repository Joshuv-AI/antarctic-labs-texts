Downregulation of ANKRD22 promotes ovarian cancer cell proliferation by enhancing the immunosuppressive capacity of M-MDSCs
Authors: Huanhuan Chen, Tianhui Pan, Qionghua He, Shu yu Li, Jing Fei, Jianwei Zhou, Dandan Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ANKRD22 is a protein involved in tumor immune regulation. To elucidate its role in ovarian cancer, we analyzed its expression pattern and association with the tumor immune microenvironment. Bioinformatics analyses revealed upregulation of ANKRD22 in ovarian cancer tissues compared with normal controls. High ANKRD22 expression correlated with favorable patient prognosis, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker. In vitro experiments using the ID8 murine ovarian cancer cell line, selected for its relevance to immunocompetent mouse models in tumor microenvironment studies, revealed that ANKRD22 expression is regulated by the tumor microenvironment. Monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) with Ankrd22 knockout exhibited enhanced immunosuppressive function, inhibited T lymphocyte proliferation and reduced IL-2 secretion. These M-MDSCs promoted ovarian cancer cell proliferation primarily through direct contact. RNA sequencing suggested that Ankrd22 might regulate the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs by modulating fatty acid metabolism. In vivo rescue experiments in a subcutaneous tumor model confirmed that Ankrd22 mediates the protumor effect of M-MDSCs. In summary, this study demonstrated that downregulation of ANKRD22 promotes ovarian cancer progression by enhancing the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs, providing a theoretical basis for targeting ANKRD22 in ovarian cancer treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04405-z.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is among the most aggressive malignancies of the female reproductive system in the world, ranking third in incidence among gynecological tumors [1–3]. However, owing to the lack of specific early symptoms and effective screening methods, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, resulting in a low survival rate [4]. Although surgery combined with chemotherapy and targeted therapy has improved the treatment of ovarian cancer, the therapeutic outcomes are still unsatisfactory because of the presence of immune escape mechanisms in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [5]. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), important components of the TME, have gradually become a research focus in antitumor treatment because of their potent immunosuppressive functions [6, 7].

As one of the two major subtypes of MDSCs, monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs), have attracted significant attention because of their ability to suppress T-cell activity and secrete a variety of immunosuppressive factors. M-MDSCs considerably attenuate T-cell-mediated antitumor immune responses by secreting immunosuppressive factors such as interleukin-10 (IL-10), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and expressing arginase 1 (ARG1) and indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), thus promoting tumor growth and metastasis [8, 9]. However, the upstream molecular mechanisms underlying the immunosuppressive functions of M-MDSCs have not yet been fully elucidated, which limits the development of immunotherapies targeting their functional pathways.

ANKRD22 (Ankyrin repeat domain 22), a cell regulatory protein characterized by its ankyrin repeat domains, has been shown to have potential functions related to cell proliferation and immune regulation in various diseases [10–13]. In recent years, ANKRD22 has been found to be abnormally expressed in several types of cancer and may play a role in regulating the TME and immune evasion [13–16]. We previously reported that ANKRD22 mediates the immunosuppressive properties of polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs) [16]. However, the role of ANKRD22 in the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs and its impact on ovarian cancer progression remain unclear.

Therefore, the expression characteristics of ANKRD22 in M-MDSCs and its influence on the immune microenvironment of ovarian cancer were investigated in this study. We aimed to elucidate the mechanisms through which ANKRD22 regulates the immunosuppressive capacity of M-MDSCs to influence ovarian cancer cell proliferation. These results provide a new theoretical basis for the development of immunotherapy targets for ovarian cancer and increase the understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of the tumor immune microenvironment.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

The expression profile of ANKRD22 was analyzed using the Xiantao Academic platform (https://www.xiantaozi.com/). Differential expression was assessed between ovarian cancer samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and normal ovarian tissue samples from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project. Furthermore, its association with the prognosis of ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (the predominant histological subtype) was evaluated. The correlation between ANKRD22 expression and immune cell infiltration in ovarian cancer was also explored using both the Xiantao Academic platform and TIMER databases[17].

Clinical blood sample collection

With approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of the Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Reference number: 2022-0059) and written informed consent from all participants, peripheral blood samples were collected for this study. In total, 8 participants were included, comprising 4 newly diagnosed ovarian cancer patients and 4 control individuals without any malignancy. The inclusion criteria for the ovarian cancer group were females aged ≥ 18 years with stable vital signs, a confirmed histopathological diagnosis of ovarian cancer, and provision of informed consent. The control group consisted of age-matched females with stable vital signs and no history of cancer who were either healthy volunteers or patients undergoing routine health checks.

Exclusion criteria for all participants included unstable vital signs, any history of other primary malignancies, or active systemic diseases (e.g., acute infections, uncontrolled autoimmune disorders), age < 18 years, or unwillingness to provide consent. Single-cell suspensions from peripheral blood mononuclear cells were subsequently prepared using standard density gradient centrifugation methods as described in our previous publication[16].

Isolation of M-MDSCs and PMN-MDSCs

Human M-MDSCs and PMN-MDSCs were sorted by FACS from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using the surface markers CD11b+HLA-DR–CD14+CD15– and CD11b+HLA-DR–CD14–CD15+, respectively. Mouse M-MDSCs were identified and sorted from bone marrow (BM) as CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells. The sorting procedures were performed as previously described in our publications[16].

RT‒qPCR

All experiments were conducted in accordance with the MIQE guidelines. Total RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol reagent (Macherey–Nagel, Germany) and reverse transcribed into cDNA with a PrimeScript™ RT reagent kit. Real-time quantitative PCR was carried out with a Premix Ex Taq™ kit (Takara). The sequences of primers and probes are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Primer specificity was confirmed by melting curve analysis. Amplification efficiencies were determined using standard curves (see Supplementary Figure S1 for details). The efficiencies of mouse-specific primers for Tubb and Ankrd22 were 102.8% (R2 = 0.998) and 103.9% (R2 = 0.996), respectively, the efficiencies of human-specific primers for TUBB and ANKRD22 were 95.6% (R2 = 0.999) and 93.3% (R2 = 0.9996), respectively. All runs included no-template and no-RT controls.

Cell culture

ID8 mouse ovarian cancer cells were purchased from the Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and authenticated by STR profiling (Supplementary Material). Cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Corning) supplemented with 10% FBS (Corning) and 1% penicillin‒streptomycin (Haoyang Biological Manufacture) and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

The expression of ANKRD22 in immune cells

Mouse T cells and B cells were obtained by FACS sorting of CD45+CD3+ and CD45+CD19+ cells from splenic mononuclear cells, respectively. Mouse macrophages were generated by inducing bone marrow single-cell suspensions with 20 ng/mL mouse macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) for 7 days. Human T cells and B cells were isolated by sorting CD45+CD3+ and CD45+CD19+ cells from peripheral blood mononuclear cells, while human macrophages were obtained by culturing peripheral blood monocytes with 20 ng/mL human M-CSF for 7 days. Both mouse and human neutrophils were isolated from peripheral blood by density gradient centrifugation. The expression level of ANKRD22 in each cell type with or without 5 ng/mL IFN-γ stimulation for 24 h was detected and compared by RT-qPCR. Each group was set with three biological replicates.

Ovarian cancer xenograft model in mice

All animal experiments were approved by the Second Affiliated Hospital of the Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Reference number: 2020-96) and the Medical Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Chinese Medicine University (Reference number: IACUC-20210628-07).

Female C57BL/6 mice (aged 4–5 weeks) were used as recipients in this study. After weaning, 8 mice were uniquely earmarked and randomly allocated into two groups (n = 4 per group) on the basis of a computer-generated sequence: one group received cells from Ankrd22 knockout (KO) mice, and the other received cells from wild-type (WT) mice. Global Ankrd22 KO donor mice on a C57BL/6 background were generated via CRISPR/Cas9 technology, and the knockout was verified in BM-derived CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells by PCR and agarose gel electrophoresis. Matched WT C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Shanghai Experimental Animal Resource Center of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells were isolated from the BM of these Ankrd22 KO or WT donor mice. Each recipient mouse was then inoculated with a mixture containing 1 × 107 CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells and 5 × 106 ID8 cells.

The mixture was resuspended in 200 µL of growth factor-reduced Matrigel matrix (Corning). Tumor formation and growth were monitored weekly using manual calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. After tumors formed, a growth curve was generated. All mice were euthanized at the 8th week post-inoculation, and tumors were harvested and weighed.

For the rescue experiments, we employed the same tumorigenesis model as described previously and inoculated mice with a mixture of 1 × 107 BM-derived CD11b+Ly6G⁻Ly6Chi cells and 5 × 106 ID8 cells. Multiple treatment groups were established with each consisting of 5 mice. Specific interventions included (1) in vitro pretreatment of CD11b+Ly6G⁻Ly6Chi cells with 1 μM ANKRD22 agonist for 3 days before mixing with ID8 cells; (2) in vitro pretreatment of CD11b+Ly6G⁻Ly6Chi cells with 5 ng/mL IFN-γ (Novoprotein, C746) for 2 days before mixing with ID8 cells. All mice were euthanized 4 weeks after inoculation for tumor volume comparison across groups.

Effects of M-MDSCs on T-cell proliferation and IL-2 secretion

The methods for obtaining mouse splenic lymphocytes, activating T cells, and labeling with CFSE are detailed in our previously published literature [16]. For the T-cell suppression assay, BM-derived CD11b+Ly6G⁻Ly6Cʰⁱ cells were treated with 100 ng/mL GM-CSF and 100 ng/mL IL-6 and then cocultured for 96 h with CFSE-labeled T lymphocytes that had been stimulated with anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies (BioLegend). T-cell proliferation was evaluated by flow cytometry (FCM) on the basis of the dilution of CFSE fluorescence. The proliferation index was calculated to reflect the proliferative capacity of T cells. The level of IL-2 in the supernatant was detected using an IL-2 ELISA kit (Proteintech).

Tumor microenvironment simulation

The sorted CD11b+Ly6G⁻Ly6Chi cells were seeded at 2 × 106 cells per well in 24-well plates containing complete DMEM. These cells were then cultured under various conditions to simulate different aspects of the TME, including hypoxia, oxidative stress (H2O2), lactic acid (LA) accumulation, glucose deprivation and TGF-β. Ankrd22 expression was subsequently detected by RT‒qPCR.

Coculture experiments

In preliminary experiments to assess the effect of cell contact, 5 × 103 GFP-labeled ID8 cells were seeded in the lower chamber of a Transwell plate, while 1 × 105 BM-derived CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells from Ankrd22 KO or WT mice were placed in the upper chamber to establish an indirect coculture system. For direct contact conditions, the same numbers of GFP-labeled ID8 cells and BM-derived CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells were cocultured in the same well.

To further investigate the dose–response relationship of the contact-dependent effects, the coculture system was refined using varying M-MDSC-to-ID8 cell ratios (1:1, 1:5, and 1:10). For indirect coculture, 4 × 103 GFP-labeled ID8 cells were placed in the lower chamber, with 4 × 103, 2 × 104, or 4 × 104 BM-derived CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells (from Ankrd22 KO or WT mice) seeded in the upper chamber, corresponding to the respective ratios. Direct coculture was performed in parallel using the same cell numbers and ratios. After 48 h of coculture, GFP-labeled ID8 cells were harvested and subjected to staining with Zombie viability dye and anti-Ki67 antibody, followed by flow cytometric analysis to determine the percentage of Ki67‑positive cells.

RNA sequencing

Three samples of BM-derived CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells were obtained from each of the Ankrd22 KO and WT mice via FACS, with each sample containing more than 1 × 107 cells. The extracted RNA was sent to Lianchuan Biotechnology (Hangzhou, China) for transcriptome sequencing. The sequencing results were analyzed using R software. On the basis of the obtained list of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using relevant R packages.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0) was used to generate statistical plots. SPSS software (version 23.0) was used to conduct statistical analyses. The differences between groups were assessed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 in all analyses, with *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001; NS means not significant. Partial correlation and Spearman correlation analyses were used to assess the correlations. An absolute value of partial correlation coefficient or Spearman’s rho greater than 0.3 was considered to indicate a correlation. FCM and FACS data were processed using FlowJo software (version 10.0).

Results

Correlation analysis between ANKRD22 expression and prognosis in Ovarian cancer patients

To investigate the correlation between ANKRD22 expression and the prognosis of patients with ovarian cancer, as well as the infiltration of immune cells in the TME, we conducted bioinformatics analyses focusing on ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma, the most common histologic subtype. Analysis via the Xiantao Academic platform revealed that ANKRD22 expression levels were significantly higher in ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma tissues than in normal ovarian tissues (Fig. 1a). Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that high ANKRD22 expression was significantly associated with improved overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) in patients with ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (Fig. 1b–d). Furthermore, high ANKRD22 expression was significantly correlated with increased infiltration of most immune cell types within the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 1e). Analysis using the TIMER database revealed that in the immune microenvironment of ovarian cancer, ANKRD22 expression levels were positively correlated with the infiltration extent of CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, neutrophils, and dendritic cells (Fig. 1f), while negatively correlated with the infiltration extent of MDSCs (Fig. 1g). These results suggest that ANKRD22 may influence the prognosis of ovarian cancer patients by regulating immune cell infiltration. Although ANKRD22 is highly expressed in neutrophils, neutrophils have a short lifespan, which makes in vitro research difficult. In addition, ANKRD22 expression is very low in T cells, and its expression level does not change significantly after IFN-γ stimulation. Meanwhile, ANKRD22 expression in macrophages is significantly upregulated after IFN-γ stimulation, and we have already investigated the mechanism of ANKRD22 in tumor-associated macrophages (Fig. 1h, i) [13]. We therefore focused on the regulatory role and mechanisms of ANKRD22 in MDSCs. Several studies have reported the driving effect of MDSCs on ovarian cancer [9, 18]. Both PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs were detected in the peripheral blood of ovarian cancer patients, with PMN-MDSCs being more abundant (Fig. 1j, k). Moreover, the abundance of M-MDSCs in the peripheral blood of ovarian cancer patients was greater than that in the peripheral blood of healthy controls (Fig. 1l). Ankrd22 expression in M-MDSCs was markedly upregulated after IFN-γ activation (Fig. 1i). In our previous work, we demonstrated that Ankrd22 is expressed in both PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs in the bone marrow and that it regulates the immunosuppressive function of PMN-MDSCs [16]. Tumor microenvironment downregulates the expression level of Ankrd22 in M-MDSCs.

To investigate whether the TME regulates ANKRD22 expression in M-MDSCs, we first established an in vitro TME simulation system (Fig. 2a, b). BM-derived CD11b+Ly6G⁻Ly6Cʰⁱ cells from wild-type C57BL/6 mice were exposed to various TME-related stimuli, including hypoxia, oxidative stress, lactic acid, glucose deprivation and TGF-β. We found that these diverse TME stimuli significantly downregulated Ankrd22 expression (Fig. 2c–g). To validate these findings in vivo, we subcutaneously inoculated ID8 cells into C57BL/6 mice. Six weeks later, tumors were harvested, and Ankrd22 expression in CD11b+Ly6G⁻Ly6Cʰⁱ cells was compared among three compartments: the bone marrow of tumor-bearing (TB) mice, the tumor tissue itself, and the bone marrow of wild-type mice. Notably, Ankrd22 expression was lowest in CD11b+Ly6G⁻Ly6Cʰⁱ cells isolated from tumor tissues (Fig. 2h). Downregulation of ANKRD22 enhances the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs and promotes tumor cell proliferation

To investigate the immunosuppressive potential of BM-derived CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells and the role of ANKRD22 in regulating this function, we performed both in vitro and in vivo studies utilizing an Ankrd22 KO C57BL/6 mouse model previously generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. For the in vitro functional assay, CD11b+Ly6G⁻Ly6Cʰⁱ cells were isolated by FACS from the bone marrow of Ankrd22 KO and WT mice, and successful knockout was confirmed (Fig. 3a). These sorted M-MDSCs were then cocultured for 96 h with CFSE-labeled splenic T lymphocytes from mice. T-cell proliferation was evaluated by FCM on the basis of CFSE fluorescence dilution, and the proliferation index was calculated (Fig. 3b, c). Concurrently, IL-2 secretion levels in the culture supernatant were measured by ELISA (Fig. 3d). The results demonstrated that while M-MDSCs from both Ankrd22 KO and WT mice possessed immunosuppressive effects, those derived from Ankrd22 KO mice exhibited a significantly enhanced ability to suppress both the proliferation and IL-2 secretion of T lymphocytes. In vivo, subcutaneous tumor formation experiments in mice were performed by mixing BM-derived CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells from Ankrd22 KO or WT C57BL/6 mice with ID8 cells, followed by subcutaneous injection into C57BL/6 mice to observe tumor growth (Fig. 3e-g). Ankrd22 KO M-MDSCs enhance ovarian cancer cell proliferation through direct contact-dependent effects

To investigate how M-MDSCs promote the proliferation of ID8 cells, we sorted BM-derived CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells from Ankrd22 KO and WT mice. These cells were cocultured directly with GFP-labeled ID8 mouse ovarian cancer cells or indirectly using the Transwell system. Flow cytometric quantification revealed that ID8 cell counts were significantly greater in direct coculture than in indirect coculture, suggesting that the pro-proliferative effect is predominantly mediated by direct cell contact. Moreover, in the same coculture system, compared with those from WT mice, M-MDSCs from Ankrd22 KO mice promoted ID8 cell proliferation more potently (Fig. 4a–b). To further investigate the contact-dependent mechanism, we performed coculture experiments with varying M-MDSC-to-ID8 cell ratios (1:1, 1:5, and 1:10). Ki67 staining revealed that direct contact consistently induced stronger ID8 cell proliferation than indirect contact did across all ratios, while both conditions showed a clear dose-dependent relationship (strongest effect at 1:10, although some pairwise comparisons did not reach statistical significance). Notably, compared with WT cells, M-MDSCs from Ankrd22 KO mice exhibited enhanced pro-proliferative capacity under all conditions, with the genotypic difference being most pronounced at the highest ratio under direct contact (Fig. 4c, d). Ankrd22 KO may affect lipid metabolism in M-MDSCs

To clarify the mechanism underlying the enhanced immunosuppressive function of Ankrd22 KO M-MDSCs, CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells were sorted from the BM of Ankrd22 KO and WT mice and subjected to RNA-seq. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the DEGs revealed a prominent association between Ankrd22 and lipid metabolism (Fig. 5a–c, Supplementary Figure S2a). Notably, some lipid metabolism-related DEGs, including Ptgs2, Il1b, and Cd36, are well-characterized for their involvement in mediating the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs [19–21]. On this basis, we hypothesized that the augmented immunosuppressive activity of Ankrd22 KO M-MDSCs might be linked to altered lipid metabolism. To further validate the regulatory role of Ankrd22 in M-MDSC immunosuppression in vivo, we established a tumor co-injection model, where IFN-γ, a known inducer of Ankrd22 expression in M-MDSCs (Fig. 1i), or a specific ANKRD22 agonist (validated in our previous study) [13], was pre-mixed with ID8 cells and M-MDSCs prior to tumor cell inoculation. Tumor volumes were measured and quantified four weeks post-inoculation. The results revealed that IFN-γ treatment, and Ankrd22 upregulation reversed the enhanced immunosuppressive phenotype of M-MDSCs (Fig. 5d-f). Discussion

ANKRD22 is a molecule that has gradually gained attention in the context of tumor immune regulation in recent years [13, 16]. This study is the first to reveal the role of ANKRD22 in regulating M-MDSCs in the immune microenvironment of ovarian cancer and to explore its relationship with tumor immune escape and progression. We found that the expression of ANKRD22 is significantly greater in ovarian cancer tissues than in normal ovarian tissues and that its high expression is closely related to better OS, DSS and PFI in patients, indicating that ANKRD22 may serve as a prognostic marker for ovarian cancer. Furthermore, while high ANKRD22 expression was significantly associated with increased infiltration of most immune cell types in ovarian cancer, it was negatively correlated with the infiltration of MDSCs. We previously described the immunomodulatory role of ANKRD22 in PMN-MDSCs [16]. In addition, ANKRD22 regulates the phenotype of macrophages in the colon cancer microenvironment [13]. M-MDSCs are enriched in the ovarian cancer microenvironment. Interestingly, high tissue levels of ANKRD22 are associated with better patient prognosis, but its loss of function in M-MDSCs promotes tumor growth. These findings suggest that ANKRD22 plays different roles in different cell compartments of the TME. The favorable prognosis associated with the overall expression of ANKRD22 may indicate a more active antitumor immune background at the macroenvironmental level, while its specific downregulation in M-MDSCs constitutes a microlevel adaptation of local immunosuppression. Our study on the immunosuppressive effects of ANKRD22-regulated M-MDSCs in ovarian cancer has expanded our understanding of the role of ANKRD22 in tumor immunity.

During the development of ovarian cancer, the TME induces changes in immune cell phenotypes, such as enhancing the immunosuppressive effects of M-MDSCs to promote tumor immune escape. In this process, alterations in ANKRD22 expression within immune cells may be associated with their immunosuppressive functions. Our study further revealed that the expression of Ankrd22 in CD11b+Ly6G−Ly6Chi cells dynamically changes in response to different TME stimuli. A hypoxic tumor microenvironment has been shown to induce immunosuppression in a variety of immune cells. Under hypoxic conditions, MDSCs upregulate PD-L1 expression, which promotes T-cell exhaustion and induces immune tolerance. Additionally, HIF-1 drives the differentiation of MDSCs into immunosuppressive tumor-associated macrophages, further dampening the antitumor immune response [22]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are essential for maintaining MDSCs in an undifferentiated state. In turn, MDSCs release ROS to inhibit T-cell immune function [22]. A low-glucose microenvironment promotes metabolic reprogramming in MDSCs, leading to a dependence on aerobic glycolysis and enhanced glucose uptake, thus sustaining their immunosuppressive function [23]. Lactic acid and TGF-β have also been demonstrated to be closely associated with the infiltration of MDSCs into the TME and their immunosuppressive phenotype [24–26]. Our further in vivo experiments confirmed that the expression of Ankrd22 in M-MDSCs in tumor tissues is greater than that in bone marrow tissue. Therefore, we speculate that under the stimulation of TME factors, downregulation of Ankrd22 expression in M-MDSCs may regulate their immune function. Our experiments confirmed that BM-derived M-MDSCs from Ankrd22 KO mice strongly inhibited T-cell proliferation and IL-2 secretion, thus enhancing the tumorigenic potential of ovarian cancer cells. These results indicate that downregulation of ANKRD22 promotes the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs.

To further validate the specific tumor-promoting effect of Ankrd22, we performed rescue experiments in the subcutaneous tumorigenesis model, in which BM-derived M-MDSCs from Ankrd22 KO and WT mice were pretreated with IFN-γ or an ANKRD22 agonist. Both treatments effectively reduced the accelerated tumor growth mediated by Ankrd22 KO M-MDSCs. These data provide direct evidence for the protective, tumor-restraining role of Ankrd22 in M-MDSCs.

MDSCs can regulate tumors not only through direct contact with tumor cells but also through the secretion of a variety of factors. These factors include IL-6, IL-10, VEGF, TNF-α, MMP9, and PGE2, which can perform multiple functions, such as immunosuppression, promoting tumor angiogenesis, inducing epithelial‒mesenchymal transition metabolic reprogramming, and facilitating immune escape and tumor metastasis [6]. We found that Ankrd22 KO M-MDSCs promoted the proliferation of ID8 ovarian cancer cells through direct contact. Unfortunately, our experiments have not yet elucidated the mechanism by which MDSCs directly contact tumor cells to regulate their proliferation.

RNA sequencing analysis revealed that ANKRD22 may be involved in regulating lipid metabolism reprogramming of M-MDSCs. Interestingly, our previous study demonstrated that ANKRD22 also promotes lipid metabolism reprogramming in colorectal cancer cells [27]. We therefore hypothesize that the mechanism by which ANKRD22 regulates the immunosuppressive phenotype of M-MDSCs is likely associated with altered lipid metabolism. Accumulating evidence has shown that fatty acid metabolism plays a crucial role in maintaining the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs [23, 28]. Under TME stimulation, such as hypoxia and oxidative stress, MDSCs enhance their immunosuppressive function by upregulating lipid metabolism, such as by increasing fatty acid uptake and promoting fatty acid oxidation [23]. Among these lipid metabolism-related DEGs with altered expression, several genes, including Ptgs2, Il1b, and Cd36, are well-characterized for their involvement in mediating the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs [19–21]. For instance, COX2 (encoded by Ptgs2), a key rate-limiting enzyme in arachidonic acid lipid metabolism, regulates the synthesis of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) and its downstream signaling pathways. It not only specifically upregulates the activity of ARG1 and expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) in MDSCs to directly inhibit T cell activation and function, but also promotes MDSC proliferation, thereby significantly increasing the number of MDSCs infiltrating the tumor site [29].

To build on these findings, future investigations will focus on identifying the key lipid metabolism molecules targeted by ANKRD22 in M-MDSCs and elucidating the precise mechanisms by which these molecules modulate immune escape in ovarian cancer. Additionally, the biological basis underlying the high expression of ANKRD22 in ovarian cancer cells remains to be explored, and we postulate this may represent a reactive response within the TME. Further studies will therefore clarify how ANKRD22-regulated M-MDSCs interact with other immune cells and ovarian cancer cells, as well as the modulation of these interactions by specific TME factors—an objective that can be achieved through enhanced in vitro and in vivo assays, particularly single-cell level analyses.

In the current tumorigenesis assays, ovarian cancer cells were co-injected with BM-derived M-MDSCs isolated from CRISPR/Cas9-mediated Ankrd22 KO or WT mice into WT C57BL/6 recipients. To further refine the specificity of these observations, subsequent work will employ a myeloid lineage-specific conditional Ankrd22 knockout mouse model, which will enable more precise characterization of the specific effects of Ankrd22 deficiency in M-MDSCs on ovarian cancer progression by eliminating potential confounding effects from host-derived M-MDSCs.

In summary, ANKRD22 is involved in ovarian cancer immune escape and tumor progression. The downregulation of ANKRD22 may promote the proliferation and metastasis of ovarian cancer cells by regulating the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs. Therefore, ANKRD22 is not only a potential prognostic marker for ovarian cancer but also a potential target for future immunotherapy.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that TME enhances the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs and promotes the proliferation of M-MDSCs by downregulating the expression of Ankrd22. As a novel immune regulator, ANKRD22 may be involved in tumor immune escape and tumor progression through the regulation of fatty acid metabolism. Therefore, ANKRD22 is not only a potential prognostic marker for ovarian cancer but also a new target for immunotherapy.
