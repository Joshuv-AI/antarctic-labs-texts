Walking ability as a prognostic indicator in elderly patients with community-acquired pneumonia: a retrospective multicenter study
Authors: Jeeyong Lim, Kyungman Cha, Hyo Joon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommunity-acquired pneumonia is a leading cause of mortality among the elderly, and traditional severity scores may not fully apprehend functional vulnerability. This study evaluated whether pre-morbid walking ability predict 28-day mortality in elderly pneumonia patients.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter observational study included patients aged ≥ 65 years diagnosed with community-acquired pneumonia at two emergency departments between 2018 and 2019. Patients with comorbidities affecting walking ability, as stroke, cognitive disorders, malignancy, were excluded. To evaluate the predictive performance of walking ability and clinical variables for 28-day mortality, multivariable logistic and Cox regression analyses were performed. For calculation of area under the curve of variables and severity scores, receiver operating characteristic analysis was used.ResultsOf 428 patients, the median age was 77 years, 377 patients (88.1%) could walk independently, and 33 (7.7%) expired in 28 days. In logistic regression, female gender, albumin, and lactate-to-albumin ratio were independent predictors. The prediction model combining inability to walk and lactate-to-albumin ratio showed an area under the curve of 0.793, superior to CURB-65 (0.658, p = 0.026). Background

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) has an estimated global incidence ranging from 1.4 to 15 cases per 1,000 population, varying by season and region [1]. It remains one of the most frequent causes of death among infectious diseases, and mortality increases with age, with 30-day mortality rates of 6.3% to 15.5% reported among elderly patients aged 65 years or older—approximately three to five times higher than in younger adults [2–5]. Intrinsic risk factors contributing to increased mortality in CAP include preexisting chronic pulmonary diseases, other comorbidities, impaired physical function, immunosuppression, and malnutrition, all of which are common in elderly patients [6–8].

Several clinical studies have evaluated physical performance in the elderly, using quantitative parameters such as walking speed on flat ground, ability to climb slopes or stairs, and the proportion of time spent standing or engaging in physical activity during daily life [9–12].

The primary objective of this study is to determine whether walking ability, as an indicator of physical function, can serve as a prognostic factor for 28-day mortality in elderly patients with CAP.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study utilized a multicenter registry of elderly patients and standardized electronic medical records from two emergency departments (ED) located in urban areas, each with an annual patient volume exceeding 60,000. Clinical data were retrospectively collected over a 24-month period, from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2019.

Eligible patients were aged 65 years or older and had a primary diagnosis of pneumonia or bronchopneumonia confirmed by chest radiography or computed tomography. Patients were excluded if they had active pulmonary tuberculosis, newly diagnosed or preexisting lung cancer, aspiration pneumonia, abscess without pneumonia or comorbid conditions that could confound walking ability, such as senile cognitive disorders (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease), Parkinson’s disease, stroke, epilepsy, musculoskeletal disorders, or other malignancies.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, vital signs, and mental status as assessed by the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS). Information on comorbidities—such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary artery disease, valvular heart disease, heart failure, chronic liver disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic lung disease, peptic ulcer disease, and arthritis—was recorded. Data on pre-morbid walking ability, residence type prior to ED presentation (including nursing facility residence), laboratory test results, and radiographic findings were also obtained. Procedural and outcome variables, including endotracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation, central venous catheterization, vasopressor use, length of hospital stay, admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), in-hospital mortality, 28-day mortality, and interfacility transfer were recorded.

The CURB-65, Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI), and SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) scores—widely recognized predictors of mortality—were calculated using data obtained from ED medical records and laboratory results.

Walking ability was defined as the capacity to ambulate independently without assistance from another person, immediately before the onset of pneumonia. Gait speed and the presence of lower limb amputation were not considered in this assessment. Based on initial and follow-up emergency medical records, nursing documentation, neurology or neurosurgery consultation notes, and transfer records, patients who were bedridden or required a wheelchair for mobility were classified as unable to walk, whereas those who could walk using a device such as walker, rollator or cane were considered able to walk. In each emergency center, one board-certified emergency physician reviewed the records, and any discordant cases were re-evaluated and categorized by a third senior emergency physician.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as the means ± standard deviations if they had a normal distribution or as medians if not, and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. χ2 and Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare the proportion and distribution of variables between the survivor and non-survivor groups. To evaluate the predictive value of the variables, those with significance on univariable logistic regression analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression, and the odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated.

To address missing data in lactate (n = 36), we employed multiple imputation with 10 iterations. The imputation model included all variables used in the multivariable models as well as the outcome of 28-day mortality. Furthermore, we performed a sensitivity analysis comparing the results from the multiply imputed datasets with those from the complete case analysis to evaluate the robustness of our findings. In addition to discrimination, the calibration of the predictive model was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

In addition to the primary multivariable model, we constructed a simplified prognostic model restricted to variables representing pre-morbid physical status and pathophysiologic response, to facilitate bedside use and comparison with existing severity scores. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity and specificity were calculated. Cox regression analysis was performed to estimate the 28-day survival rate according to walking ability. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R version 4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

During the study period, a total of 1,020 patients aged 65 years or older with a primary admission diagnosis of CAP presented to the ED. A total of 146 patients with a history of stroke, 132 patients with cognitive impairment including Alzheimer’s disease, 49 with Parkinson’s disease, 8 with epilepsy, 2 with myopathic disorders, and 184 patients with a prior malignancy or a new diagnosis of malignancy during the hospitalization were excluded from the study cohort. In addition, 263 patients without radiographic evidence of pneumonia on either plain chest radiography or computed tomography were excluded, and a total of 428 patients were included in the primary analysis (Fig. 1).

The process of ruling the research cohort; patients ≥ 65 years with community-acquired pneumonia

Of the 428 patients, 256 (59.8%) were male, and the median age was 77 years (interquartile range [IQR], 71–82). 24 patients (5.6%) were residents of nursing hospitals or long-term care facilities at the time of ED presentation. A total of 377 patients (88.1%) were able to walk independently before their ED visit.

Pathogenic organisms were isolated from sputum cultures in 161 patients (37.6%). A total of 164 patients (38.3%) were hospitalized, of whom 36 (8.4%) were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). Mechanical ventilation was required in 19 patients (4.4%), 32 patients (7.4%) expired during the hospitalization, and 33 patients (7.7%) expired within 28 days (Table 1).

Comparison of 28-day mortality prediction between a novel prognostic model and established clinical severity scores

In the primary multivariable logistic regression model, female gender, albumin, and the LAR were identified as independent predictors of 28-day mortality. However, for clinical risk estimation and to enable a simple bedside calculation, we developed a reduced prediction model that included only LAR and pre-morbid walking ability, which are conceptually linked to pathophysiologic response, physiologic reserve, and functional status.

Using multivariable Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis, the area under the curve (AUC) of the prediction model combining inability to walk and LAR was compared with those of established prognostic scores. The primary ROC curve analysis was performed using the complete case cohort (n = 392). To evaluate the impact of missing data, we additionally calculated the AUC across all multiply imputed datasets. The AUC of the LAR–walking ability model for predicting 28-day mortality (0.793, pooled average 0.7938) did not differ significantly from that of SOFA or PSI scores (0.694, p = 0.152; 0.749, p = 0.503), but was superior to that of CURB-65 (0.658, p = 0.026) (Table 4) (Fig. 2).

The LAR-walking ability model demonstrated excellent calibration, indicating a high degree of agreement between the predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (Hosmer-Lemeshow test, χ2 = 7.895, p = 0.444).

To address the potential for optimism in model performance due to the relatively low number of events (n = 33), we performed rigorous internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples. The optimism-corrected Somers’ Dxy rank correlation was calculated. The analysis yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.789. This value demonstrates a negligible decrease from the apparent AUC of 0.793 (calculated optimism = 0.004), confirming the excellent internal validity and robust discriminative performance of the LAR-walking ability model.

In decision curve analysis, the LAR–walking ability model showed higher net benefit than both the PSI and SOFA scores across 8 to 12% threshold probabilities for 28-day mortality. Within this risk range, the median LAR was 0.46 (IQR, 0.28–0.67), the median PSI was 125 (119–128; risk class IV), and the median SOFA score was 3 (3–4), and on the threshold probability of 8%, the net benefit values were 0.04 for LAR–walking ability model, 0.02 for PSI and 0.021 for SOFA score, showing a maximum difference of 2%. The PSI and SOFA scores are powerful, traditional tools that encompass almost all of a patient’s physiological responses and can guide admission decisions in elderly patients with CAP. Although the LAR–walking ability model did not demonstrate superior predictive performance compared with PSI or SOFA, it could provide a modest but potentially adjunctive clinical utility to PSI and SOFA scores in deciding on admission to facilities capable of providing a higher level of monitoring and treatment for the elderly patients with CAP in the low-to-intermediate risk of 28-day mortality (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Park and colleagues conducted a prospective study of 201 hospitalized patients aged 65 years or older with pneumonia, in which they used a deficit-accumulation frailty index including activities of daily living (ADL), such as walking and bed mobility and physical tasks, as walking half a mile and climbing stairs. They found that patients who followed a poor post-discharge functional trajectory had significantly higher frailty index scores at baseline (p < 0.001) and presented with more severe pneumonia, reflected by higher CURB-65 and PSI scores (p < 0.001), than those with excellent or good recovery trajectories [13]. Mody and colleagues conducted a prospective study of 112 patients aged 60 years or older who were admitted with pneumonia through the ED, in which they assessed preadmission and in-hospital functional status using measures including walking and bed mobility. They reported that patients who were not independently active before admission more frequently presented with atypical pneumonia manifestations such as confusion (p < 0.001), experienced pronounced declines in functional status during hospitalization than those who were independent at baseline [14]. These findings are concordant with our research results, in which older patients who were unable to walk independently before admission were outnumbered in the 28-day mortality group (p < 0.001) and exhibited higher CURB-65, PSI, and SOFA scores than survivors (Mann-Whitney test, p < 0.001), and suggest that pre-morbid walking ability in elderly influences not only pneumonia severity and short-term mortality, but also the subsequent trajectory of physical functional status from admission through the post-discharge.

Elevated arterial lactate levels are an important indicator of tissue hypoxia and have been shown to predict adverse outcomes in pneumonia and other infectious diseases, including the need for vasopressor or mechanical ventilation, ICU admission, and in-hospital mortality [15, 16]. Serum albumin not only reflects a patient’s nutritional status but also contributes to the maintenance of immune competence. Numerous studies have demonstrated that low albumin levels are associated with adverse outcomes in pneumonia and sepsis, including greater disease severity and increased mortality [17, 18]. In a cohort study of 244 adults ≥ 18 years, hospitalized with CAP, Hanci et al. reported that serum albumin, lactate, and LAR were all significantly higher in patients requiring ICU admission compared with those managed on general wards (p < 0.001). Among these markers, LAR was also significantly elevated in non-survivors during the admission (p < 0.001) and demonstrated a superior discriminative ability for in-hospital mortality, with an AUC of 0.761 (95% CI 0.702–0.814, p < 0.001), exceeding those of CURB-65, PSI, and qSOFA [19]. Taken together with these results, our research supports the understanding that LAR is a clinically useful and potentially superior prognostic indicator in hospitalized patients with CAP, capable of guiding ICU admission decisions and improving prediction of in-hospital mortality beyond traditional severity scores.

Despite using of standardized criteria and carefully reviewing as many medical records as possible to classify walking functional status, the inherent limitations of retrospective assessments could not be completely eliminated. Unlike prior studies that quantified gait over a minimum distance required for basic ADL, the reviewed data did not allow verification of such standardized walking distances. Furthermore, walking ability was not measured under controlled conditions with respect to gait posture, surface characteristics, slope, or the degree of assistance from caregivers or devices [11, 20, 21].

This study was a retrospective observational study, it is susceptible to selection and information bias related to missing patient data, incomplete or inaccurate records, and potential data entry errors. The relatively small sample size (n = 428) restricts the generalizability of the findings and may have contributed to wide confidence intervals for some estimates, and the statistical assumptions underlying DeLong’s test for comparison of AUCs may not have been satisfied.

Another limitation of this study is that our cohort was derived from two urban ED, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to rural or non-metropolitan population. Ibrahim et al. reported a higher mortality rate of 9.1% in a 5-year study of CAP patients presenting to a rural emergency center in southwestern Nigeria, identifying advanced age over 65 years as the most critical predictor of mortality. Similarly, a 22-year retrospective study by Barua et al. using the Centers of Disease Control and Prevention-Wide-ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC-WONDER) database demonstrated that pneumonia and influenza mortality rates were significantly higher in non-metropolitan areas, a disparity that was particularly pronounced among patients aged 85 years and older [22, 23]. Given that the 28-day mortality rate in our study was 7.7%, it is likely that our data underestimate the true case fatality rate of elderly patients with CAP in rural regions or the broader general population, where access to advanced healthcare resources and early interventions may be relatively limited.

Conclusion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort of elderly patients aged 65 years or older with CAP, a prognostic model combining the pre-morbid inability to walk and LAR demonstrated an AUC of approximately 0.79 for 28-day mortality, exceeding that of CURB-65 and showing broadly comparable discriminative performance to PSI and SOFA. Although the LAR-walking ability model did not clearly outperform PSI or SOFA, it provided modest additional net benefit over these scores within a low-to-intermediate mortality risk range in decision curve analysis. Furthermore, inability to walk remained independently associated with 28-day mortality after adjustment. Because both walking ability and biological markers such as lactate and albumin are readily obtainable at ED presentation, this simple assessment can serve as a practical adjunct to existing severity scores when triaging elderly patients with CAP and considering the need for closer monitoring or higher-level care.
