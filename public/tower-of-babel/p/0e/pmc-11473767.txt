Diversity in perceptual, social, and executive functions in preschoolers from Germany and Iran
Authors: Vahid Nejati, Jennifer Lehmann, Petra Jansen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The cultural background and age shape the cognitive and social development of children. This study aimed to compare perceptual, executive, and social functioning in two age groups of preschoolers from Germany and Iran. One hundred sixty-five children (83 Iranian and 82 German) participated in the study, with 87 children aged three years and 78 children aged four years. The participants completed a series of tasks to assess different cognitive functions, including the theory of mind as a measure of social cognition, picture mental rotation test to evaluate spatial ability, and several tests including digit span, Corsi block tapping, day-night, grass-snow, dimensional change card sort, and active and passive vocabulary tests to assess executive functions. The findings revealed that German children performed better than their Iranian peers regarding spatial ability and theory of mind. Additionally, German children outperformed Iranian children in working memory, cognitive flexibility, and verbal fluency, while Iranian children demonstrated better inhibitory control. Furthermore, the correlational analysis indicated that in German children, executive function correlated with the theory of mind, whereas in Iranian children, executive function was associated with perceptual functions. Age impacted the results. These results highlight the role of cultural factors in shaping cognitive functioning and emphasize the need to consider cultural influences when examining cognitive development in different populations.

Introduction

Our cultural background shapes our values, beliefs, and habits, shaping our information processing systems and neural wirings1,2. The information processing system underlying culture encompasses a variety of cognitive functions, beginning with perception and continuing sequentially through attention, memory, executive functions, and social cognition. The influence of culture on these stages has been discussed extensively in prior research2.

Culture shapes how individuals perceive, understand, and interact with others in their social environment1. Cultural distinctions shape various aspects of daily life, including communication, social interactions, family dynamics, and educational systems, which influence underlying cognitive functions2. Despite the vast literature on cultural differences in cognitive functions, some studies have reported a null effect of culture on cognitive functions3. Notably, culture can have a domain-specific impact on cognition. A cross-cultural study with samples from China and the United States indicates that, while working memory performance was comparable between both cultures, Chinese children demonstrated superior performance compared to American children on tasks related to inhibition and attentional control4. However, better executive functions and a worse theory of mind in British children than their Hong Kong peers have been described earlier. Similarly, Japanese children showed worse theory of mind but better executive function performance than British peers5.

When comparing the influence of culture on behavior and brain development, two crucial factors that should be considered are age and the information processing stage. Most cultural studies focus on youth as the primary demographic and acknowledge that acculturation significantly influences cultural values6. Additionally, many studies narrow their focus to a single domain of cognitive functions when assessing cultural differences. In contrast, the configuration of information processing is more influenced by culture than any single cognitive function7. The cultural learning process starts from childhood and continues through adulthood and older adulthood, with the environment playing a critical role in shaping behavior and cognition. The question arises whether age plays a significant role in this process. Children have more plastic patterns of behavior and brain8, while older adults have a longer exposure to the surrounding culture9. Several methodological limitations hinder the exploration of the impact of culture on cognitive functions across different age groups. Firstly, normal development of cognitive functions occurs independent of culture, making it challenging to establish comparable tools for assessing cognitive functions in individuals from different age groups. A cross-cultural study conducted with samples from Hong Kong and the United Kingdom underscores the significance of incorporating developmental and cultural perspectives. The study reveals both similarities and differences across the two locations. School-age children and young adolescents in Hong Kong outperformed their United Kingdom counterparts on executive function tasks10. Secondly, longitudinal studies that span the entire lifespan are challenging to conduct due to the time, effort, and cost required to collect and analyze data over an extended period. Therefore, it is not easy to fully understand the influence of culture on cognitive functions across different age groups.

The stage of information processing is another crucial factor to consider when studying the impact of culture on cognitive functions. On the one hand, information processing follows a bottom-up approach, from perception to higher cognitive functions, and the dependency on environmental stimuli decreases as the information progresses11. On the other hand, taking a top-down standpoint, the higher cognitive functions influence lower perceptual processing, and more abstract higher cognitive functions tend to be more related to our value attribution system12. In addition to the impact of culture on individual cognitive functions, there is evidence to suggest that cultural factors may also influence the overall cognitive architecture. This includes the relationship between higher cognitive functions and lower perceptual processes, which can be influenced by a bottom-up or top-down approach. In detail, irrespective of cultural influence, the performance of lower perceptual functions is crucial for the higher cognitive functions in healthy individuals13–15 and those with various conditions such as blindness16, attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder17, specific learning disability18, and autism spectrum disorder14,19. Some studies found a culture-specific role for this association. For instance, Jansen et al. (2018) a study was conducted investigating motor and visual-spatial development in primary school-aged children in Cameroon and Germany. The study found a positive correlation between overall motor ability and mental rotation performance, but this correlation was only observed in the German sample20.

Cultural differences between Western cultures, such as in Germany, and Eastern cultures, for example, Iran, have been widely studied in the literature21,22. These two cultures have fundamentally different values, beliefs, and norms that shape their lifestyle, social interactions, and communication. For instance, Germans value individualism, self-reliance, and independence, while Iranians value collectivism, interdependence, and cooperation22. Several studies have compared cognitive performance in Germany with other Eastern countries. For instance, German children outperform Omani children in spatial perception23 and working memory24. An earlier study of schoolchildren in Germany and Iran found different emotional management in the two cultures25. Iranian children reported higher levels of internalizing and externalizing symptoms compared to German children. This study attributed these differences to cultural factors and norms of emotional expression. Young children in Iran may face challenges expressing themselves due to cultural expectations of respecting and maintaining harmony within the family. Besides this, German children exhibit superior mental rotation performance compared to children from Brazil26 and Cameroon20.

In the study presented here, we aimed to explore the potential impact of culture on cognitive functions in preschool-aged children at a critical age for cognitive development. Specifically, we compared the performance of German and Iranian preschoolers on tasks that measured perceptual, executive, and social cognition. We selected tasks that assessed mental rotation, executive functions, and theory of mind. Mental rotation is the ability to rotate two- or three-dimensional objects quickly and accurately mentally27, and this skill develops continuously between the ages of 3 and 5 years, with individual differences28. Executive functions include working memory, inhibition, and shifting, which involve storing, monitoring, and updating mental representations, suppressing dominant actions in favor of less dominant ones, and switching between different mental sets or tasks29. Theory of Mind (ToM) involves attributing feelings, wishes, or beliefs to oneself or others and develops in the first year of life30. Previous research has suggested that these three cognitive functions are interrelated in young children. For example, mental rotation ability and ToM positively correlate in a group of young German children who can solve mental rotation tasks31. Inhibition has also been found to be associated with ToM in American 3- and 4-year-old children, even when controlling for age and verbal intelligence32 Additionally, positive correlations have been found between mental rotation and tasks that assess the visual-spatial sketchpad and phonological loop, which are part of executive functions, in German children aged 3 and 4 years old31.

Along with culture and age, gender is crucial in determining cognitive functions. Earlier meta-analyses indicated gender differences in specific domains of cognitive functioning among children, such as verbal fluency. However, no significant gender differences have been observed in mathematical abilities33. An earlier cross-cultural study in children from Germany and Iran revealed a significant main effect of sex and nation in emotion management, but not the interaction25.

Children were recruited from several kindergartens in one city in each country. Due to missing data regarding their age, eight children from the Iranian sample had to be excluded. Materials

Demographic questionnaire

A questionnaire was administered to collect demographic and socioeconomic data, including information about the parents’ nationality, educational background, and occupational characteristics. The participants’ parents completed the questionnaire to provide the necessary information.

Theory of mind (ToM)

ToM was assessed using the Theory of Mind scale. The assessment consisted of six tasks in a child-friendly format, utilizing Playmobil figures, which progressively increased in difficulty. The tasks were followed by a control and memory task for conclusion and evaluation. This test has been employed in both German and Iranian cultural contexts35,36. For the analysis, we use the total score.

Picture mental rotation test (PMRT)

The PMRT has been developed to assess mental rotation ability within the selected age range. This paper-and-pencil test consists of 18 items, including two examples, and does not have a time limit. Each item presents a standardized picture on the left and three repeated photos on the right, rotated at 45°, 90°, 135°, or 180° relative to the standardized object. One of the three rotated pictures is identical to the standard item, while the other two are mirror-reversed images. The test demonstrates a split-half reliability of 0.74, with a maximum achievable score of 16. This test has been previously used in German and Iranian cultures14,37.

Digit span task (DST)

The DST assessed working and short-term memory capacity in both forward and backward versions. In the forward version, participants were presented with a series of digits and were required to repeat them in the same order as they were given. In the backward version, participants were presented with a series of digits and were asked to repeat them in reverse order, with the length of the given numbers increasing as the task progressed. Prior studies have demonstrated strong reliability for this assessment, with consistency ranging from 0.76 to 0.78 across various ethnic backgrounds38,39.

Corsi block tapping test (CBT)

The Corsi block tapping test assessed the visuospatial sketchpad and central executive components of working memory, utilizing both forward and backward versions40. In this test, participants were asked to repeat sequences of tapped blocks presented on a wooden panel in either the same or reverse order. The maximum score was determined by the length of the block sequences that could be accurately repeated twice. Previous research has indicated high reliability for this test, ranging between 0.81 and 0.89, across diverse ethnic populations, highlighting its consistent performance as a measure of working memory function across different cultural groups24,41,42.

Day-night task (DNT)

The DNT was used as a child-friendly version of the Stroop task as a measure of inhibitory control to measure the ability to suppress a predominant response in favor of a less dominant one43. During the DNT, children are presented with pictures or cards depicting a sun or a moon. The task is to respond based on the concept of “day” or “night” rather than the actual visual stimulus. For example, when presented with a picture of a moon, the child is instructed to say “day,” when presented with a picture of a sun, they are taught to say “night.” The maximum score for this task was 16, including the two practice trials. This assessment has previously been employed within both German and Iranian cultural contexts44,45.

Grass-snow task (GST)

In the GST, as a measure of inhibitory control, children are presented with two cards or pictures—one depicting grass and the other depicting snow. The task requires the child to respond based on the card’s color rather than the content displayed. For example, when the grass card is presented, the child is instructed to say “snow,” and when the snow card is given, they are trained to say “grass.” The GST serves as the motoric analog to the day-night task32,46. The utilization of this test has been documented in both German and Iranian cultural settings44,45.

Dimensional change card sort task (DCCS)

During the DCCS, participants are presented with cards that vary along two dimensions: color and shape47. The task involves sorting the cards based on a specific dimension or rule. For example, the child may be instructed to sort the cards by color and place all the blue cards in one pile and all the red cards in another. After several trials sorting the cards based on one dimension, the rules are switched without warning. Now, the child is asked to sort the cards based on a different dimension, such as shape, while ignoring the previously relevant dimension (color). This requires the child to flexibly adjust their sorting strategy and inhibit the previously learned rule. In this study, participants were instructed to sort two practice cards and 12 test cards according to varying dimensions, such as “color” or “form.” Six cards were used in each phase, giving a maximum score of 12. This examination has been previously administered within the cultural contexts of both Germany and Iran39,44.

Active and passive vocabulary tasks

To evaluate both expressive and receptive verbal developmental stages, the active and passive vocabulary tasks from the Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence were utilized48. The Active Vocabulary Task assesses the child’s expressive language abilities by measuring their ability to provide verbal responses. The child is presented with pictures or objects and asked to name or describe them. This task evaluates their ability to retrieve and express vocabulary. The Passive Vocabulary Task, on the other hand, evaluates the child’s receptive language abilities. The child is presented with spoken words or sentences and is asked to select the corresponding picture or object from a set of options. This task assesses their understanding and comprehension of vocabulary. Each vocabulary task had a maximum score of 26 and a total of 52 for both together. This test has been utilized in both German and Iranian cultural contexts14,44.

Procedure

Two examiners collected data, one for the German children and one for the Iranian children, in quiet rooms for two-morning sessions. Each session lasted approximately 30 min and included four or five measurements. To minimize the influence of task order, one of the two main tasks (Theory of Mind and Mental Rotation) was administered at the beginning of either the first or second session, with the order counterbalanced across all children. The Digit Span and Corsi Block Tapping tasks and the Day-Night and Grass-Snow tasks were split between the two sessions to prevent redundancy and maintain engagement. The order of these tasks was counterbalanced, and the testing order for each participant was pseudo-randomized. The tests were introduced to the children as new games, and after each session, small presents were given to them as a token of appreciation.

Data analysis

Nationality (German or Iranian) and gender (boy or girl) were the independent factors. Age in months was considered a covariate. The following analyses were conducted: A Co-variate Analysis of the Theory of Mind (ToM), a Co-variate Analysis of the Picture Mental Rotation Test (PMRT), and a MANOVA with the following tests: Digit Span Task (DST), Corsi Block Tapping Task (CBT), Day-Night Task (DNT), Grass-Snow Task (GST), Dimensional Change Card Sort Task (DCCS), and Active and Passive Vocabulary Test (APVT) scores and age in months as a co-variate. A correlation analysis was also performed to explore the relationships between the scores obtained in all the tasks. We applied a Bonferroni correction to address multiple comparisons, considering only correlations with p < 0.001 as statistically significant.

Correlational analysis

Discussion

The study results indicated that German children outperformed Iranian children in theory of mind, spatial ability, working memory, cognitive flexibility, and verbal fluency. Conversely, Iranian children performed better in inhibitory control than their German counterparts. Additionally, higher age was related to better performance regardless of the nation. Furthermore, the study found that gender did not significantly impact any of the cognitive functions examined. In the following section, we will discuss the influence of culture on the various cognitive constructs that were evaluated.

Social cognition

This study utilized the theory of mind to measure social cognition. The results unveiled higher theory of mind test scores in German children compared to their Iranian counterparts. Earlier studies have also highlighted cross-cultural differences in the sequential development of the theory of mind among children, explicitly comparing Iranian and Australian children. Despite the similar performance, subtle variations have been observed, indicating cultural influences on the specific progression of the theory of mind milestones49.

Perceptual ability

In this study, we utilized a mental rotation test to evaluate spatial ability and assess perceptual skills. The results revealed a substantial disparity in perceptual ability between German and Iranian preschoolers, as representatives of Western and Eastern cultures in order, with the former demonstrating higher performance. Previous studies have consistently shown a notable disparity in mental rotation ability, with, for example, German children exhibiting a higher level of proficiency than their counterparts from Cameroon, a country with a blend of Western and Eastern cultural influences20. This preference for mental rotation skills among German participants has also been observed when comparing them to adolescents from Brazil, a Western country20, and adults from Cambodia, an Eastern culture50, and Oman, also considered an Eastern culture23. However, a comprehensive study with a large sample size of 3363 adults revealed no differences in mental rotation abilities among students from Germany and Canada, both Western and Japan, an Eastern culture51.

In the present study, there was an age effect regardless of nationality. This relates to a previous study examining the development of mental rotation in children aged 3 to 5 years, which consistently demonstrated an improvement across these three age groups. Furthermore, gender did not significantly impact perceptual ability. This is partly in contrast to a study of Frick et al. (2013)28. They identified a noteworthy gender difference in mental rotation performance among 3-year-old children, but it was not observed among 4- and 5-year-olds. Specifically, the study found that boys exhibited a 28% lower accuracy than girls for a 135° angular disparity, whereas girls displayed a 30% lower accuracy than boys for a 180° angular disparity. A cross-cultural study conducted among adults from China and the United States has indicated that the male preference for mental rotation is not contingent on culture52. This finding might be explained based on the Masculinity-Femininity dimension of Hofstede’s cultural dimensions, which describes differences in masculine and feminine preferences across Eastern and Western societies22,53.

Executive functions

In the current study, we assessed three primary domains of executive functions, encompassing working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility, aside from verbal fluency. The study results indicated that German preschoolers exhibited higher working memory and cognitive flexibility performance, while Iranian children demonstrated superior performance in inhibitory control. An earlier study has documented superior working memory performance in German children compared to Omani children (Jansen, et al. 2019). A cross-cultural research conducted among Hong Kong and German preschoolers found no significant differences between the two nations’ inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, and working memory54. A comparative study examining preschoolers from the United States and China, representing Western and Eastern countries, found that working memory performance was comparable between the two groups. However, Chinese children demonstrated superior performance in inhibitory control compared to their American counterparts55. These findings may be attributed to the cultural emphasis on self-control during preschool in Asian countries. Observational studies conducted in Chinese classrooms have revealed intensive training focused on following directions and maintaining concentration on subject matter56. Similarly, Kwon (2004) it was reported that discipline is enforced in Korean nurseries and kindergartens. In contrast, American classrooms often prioritize concepts like free choice and self-expression, as noted by Chen et al. (1998). Indeed, Western kindergarten focuses on fostering social skills, emotional development, and self-regulation and prioritizes collaborative play, communication, and promoting individuality. In contrast, Eastern kindergarten often prioritizes discipline, respect for authority, and group harmony. In Eastern kindergarten, there is an emphasis on following rules and instructions, with less focus on individual expression. Discipline may involve strict rules, routine adherence, and consequences for non-compliance57. The superior performance of Iranian children in inhibitory control compared to their German counterparts could be attributed to the educational discipline prevalent in their culture. This discipline may emphasize the importance of avoiding guessing or providing uncertain responses. As a result, Iranian children may be more cautious and selective in their answers, responding only when they are confident. This approach to inhibitory control might explain their higher performance in this specific executive function. However, it is essential to note that this cautious approach may also contribute to their comparatively lower performance in other executive functions, as they prioritize accuracy over speed or guessing.

Configuration of cognitive constructs

The configuration of cognitive constructs plays a vital role in the systemic functioning of cognitive processes. Within this framework, cognitive functions work collaboratively to execute skills and adapt to the environment. The components’ roles, weights, and interactions may vary depending on the specific demands of the environment or skill. Perceptual processing serves as the gateway for information to enter the processing system. Executive functions, encompassing various domains, manipulate this information, while social cognition aims to understand and apply these processes in social interactions. The present study’s findings demonstrate correlations between perceptual processing and several cognitive functions, including theory of mind, working memory, cognitive flexibility, verbal fluency, and inhibitory control.

Moreover, the theory of mind shows associations with cognitive flexibility and working memory, although no significant correlation is observed with inhibitory control. Adopting a cross-cultural approach, our study revealed distinct patterns in the correlation between executive function and cognitive domains among German and Iranian children. German children exhibited a significant correlation between executive functions and social cognition, while Iranian children displayed a correlation between executive function and spatial ability. These findings emphasize the influence of cultural contexts on the relationships between executive function and other cognitive domains. It underscores the importance of considering cultural factors in understanding and interpreting cognitive processes across diverse populations. The distinct parental and educational styles can explain this finding in the two nations. In Eastern cultures, where discipline, respect for authority, and group harmony are emphasized, executive function may be more influenced by environmental factors and the perceived environment.

On the other hand, Western cultures tend to prioritize the development of social skills, emotional growth, and self-regulation, which can result in executive functions being more intertwined with social cognition. These cultural differences in parenting and education approach likely contribute to our study’s observed variations in the relationship between executive function and cognitive domains. Previous research has provided valuable insights into the diverse configurations of cognitive underpinnings across different skills. For instance, a cross-cultural study comparing preschoolers from China and the United States revealed a significant distinction in the relationship between working memory and reading and mathematical abilities58. Working memory was equally important in predicting reading and mathematics skills in China.

In contrast, working memory in the United States exhibited a stronger association with counting and calculation tasks than reading58. These findings emphasize the profound impact of cultural contexts on the interplay between cognitive processes and specific skill domains. Recognizing these cultural influences, we understand how cognitive functions contribute to skill development in different societies. Another study investigated the cultural differences in the relationship between self-control ability and free will beliefs in childhood across the United States, China, Singapore, and Peru. The findings revealed a significant influence of cultural context on the association between self-control abilities and beliefs in free will among children. Specifically, in the United States, individual differences in self-control behaviors were strongly correlated with individual differences in free will beliefs, indicating a robust relationship between these factors. However, this correlation was not evident in children from China, Singapore, or Peru, suggesting that cultural factors moderated this link. Furthermore, the timing of emerging free will beliefs differed across cultures, with Singaporean and Peruvian children developing these beliefs later than Chinese and U.S. children. Additionally, while a causal influence of self-control performance on free will beliefs was observed in the U.S. sample, it was not apparent in the other cultural groups, underscoring cultural variations in the factors shaping the development of beliefs about free will. These findings highlight the importance of considering cultural contexts when examining the interplay between self-control ability and beliefs in free will during childhood59.

In conclusion, this study highlights the profound impact of cultural contexts on the configuration and interplay of cognitive constructs. The distinct patterns observed between German and Iranian children underscore the need to consider cultural factors in understanding cognitive processes and their development, emphasizing that cultural environments deeply influence cognitive functions and their interrelationships.

This study compared parents’ educational levels as an alternative to socioeconomic status60. The results show a higher education level for Iranian mothers than German mothers education Fathers’ education did not differ. This hints that one reason for the better performance of the Iranian children in the inhibitory task might be the better education of the mother.

Limitations and future directions

It is important to acknowledge certain limitations when interpreting this study’s results. Firstly, it should be noted that the two samples included in the study represented specific local areas within two large and diverse countries (Teheran in Iran and Regensburg in Germany). By including samples from different regions within these countries, future studies would provide a more representative understanding of the target societies and enhance the generalizability of the findings.

One implication of the present study underscores the importance of considering cultural variations in cognitive assessment and the subsequent interpretation of results. Additionally, the diverse configurations of correlates suggest that a tailored approach to cognitive training is warranted, especially when addressing fundamental and culturally relevant functions across different societies.

Secondly, it is worth noting that we employed a diverse range of tests to assess different cognitive domains in our study. While these tests provided a comprehensive evaluation of cognitive function, it is essential to consider the distinction between dysfunctions and diversity when interpreting results in cultural studies. Dysfunctions are deviations from typical or expected cognitive functioning, such as cognitive impairments or delays. On the other hand, diversity refers to variations in cognitive styles or information processing across different cultural contexts. Several cultural studies have provided insights into distinct cognitive styles and information-processing patterns across various cultures. These findings underscore the significant influence of cultural factors on cognitive functioning. For example, research has revealed differences in eye movement patterns during tasks involving global and local attention between students from the Czech Republic and Taiwan61. Additionally, studies comparing the developmental sequence of the theory of mind in preschoolers from Iran and Australia have documented variations in the order and timing of the theory of mind stages49. These studies demonstrate the rich diversity of cognitive processes and highlight the role of cultural contexts in shaping the cognitive processing style. Therefore, it is crucial to differentiate between potential cognitive dysfunctions and the diverse cognitive styles present within a cultural group in future studies. This distinction allows for a more nuanced understanding of cultural cognitive differences and helps avoid misinterpretation or pathologizing normal cognitive variations. Furthermore, the study lacks detailed information on the two groups’ socioeconomic status, lifestyle, and educational backgrounds. This limits our ability to fully account for these factors as subdomains of cultural differences.

Moreover, longitudinal studies can offer valuable insights into the developmental trajectories of cognitive functions within different cultural contexts. Examining how cognitive abilities evolve and exploring potential cultural-specific developmental patterns would enhance our understanding of the complex interaction between culture and cognition. Additionally, it would be beneficial to investigate the underlying cultural mechanisms that contribute to the observed differences. Exploring cultural values, parenting practices, educational systems, or societal norms can provide a deeper understanding of how these factors shape cognitive processes and outcomes.
