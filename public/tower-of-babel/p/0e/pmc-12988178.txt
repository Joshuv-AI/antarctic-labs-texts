Psychedelic therapy and cultural humility

Introduction

The clinical promise of psychedelic-assisted therapy revives longstanding debates about how medicine can—and should—attend to the existential dimensions of suffering. However, amid renewed interest in psilocybin and other psychedelics for conditions such as depression and anxiety for those facing life-limiting illness, there is an evidence gap on the cultural realities of global care. Nearly all clinical protocols have been developed in Euro-American contexts, leaving aside important questions about how other cultural contexts may shape therapeutic experiences and processes.

Palliative care aims to relieve suffering and improve quality of life for individuals living with serious or life-limiting illness, including—but not limited to—those approaching the final stages of life. Palliative care services often provide a symptom-relief medication tool kit, whether it be at home, in a hospice, or hospital. This includes subcutaneous or intravenous drugs – given that most will eventually lose the ability to swallow pills – such as morphine for pain, midazolam for agitation, lorazepam for anxiety, haloperidol for delirium, and olanzapine for nausea. These kits are designed primarily to address physical and neuropsychiatric symptoms. Missing from palliative medicine, however, are treatments that effectively treat existential distress—the negative emotions that people experience when confronted with a life-limiting illness, including in anticipation of dying and ceasing to exist.

While spiritual care and counseling are often deployed to address this domain, some individuals continue to experience persistent distress despite such interventions. In the search for new interventions, psychedelic-assisted therapy has shown promise. Importantly, such therapies are not administered as routine bedside medications but as structured, time-limited treatment episodes in which one or more psychedelic sessions are carefully prepared for, facilitated, and integrated with psychological support. This commentary explores the evidence behind the use of psychedelics in palliative care, a brief history and evolution of the use of psychedelics, and the cultural dynamics that may impact psychedelic therapy in palliative care – with Chinese populations as an example. We consider how social and cultural expectations of a “good death” might inform psychedelic-assisted care, whether in research or service delivery, and explore the importance of cultural humility in developing such therapies.

Cultural humility is an important framework for the clinical translation of psychedelic therapy. Originally introduced as a critique of cultural competence, the emphasis on humility foregrounds clinician reflexivity, patient beliefs and worldview, and power-awareness in healthcare encounters [1]. These qualities are especially relevant for psychedelic therapy, we suggest, due to how the altered states of consciousness they occasion can amplify or augment spiritual beliefs, cultural expectations, and the social and relational dynamics of care. An orientation towards cultural humility supports wider calls for cultural responsiveness within psychedelic medicine research and practice [2–5].

What are psychedelics?

Psychedelics are a group of hallucinogenic compounds that alter a person’s mood, perception, and sense of self – causing a spectrum of visual, auditory, and other sensory changes or hallucinations [6]. They may on occasion create a dissociative (i.e., “out-of-body”) experience that is euphoric, pleasurable, and/or deeply distressing and emotionally challenging. In a landmark study on healthy volunteers receiving psilocybin at Johns Hopkins, 30% reported intense fear during the drug sessions [7]. Classic serotonergic psychedelics commonly used include psilocybin (i.e., “magic mushrooms”), mescaline (3,4,5-trimethoxyphenethylamine) and LSD (lysergic acid diethylamide), also known as “acid”. Psychedelics act as serotonin receptor agonists, with partial or full agonism of the 5-HT2A receptor thought to be responsible for their primary psychoactive effects [8, 9]. Other substances cause hallucinogenic effects similar to psychedelics including the entactogen MDMA (3,4-Methylenedioxymethamphetamine) also known as “ecstasy/molly”, and the dissociative ketamine. Psychedelics are substances that are not typically legal, with the exception of Australia, the first country to legalize their use as psychiatric medicines in 2023.

The previous twenty years have produced clinical evidence to suggest that psychedelic-assisted therapies—especially psilocybin and LSD—may help to reduce existential distress, depression, and anxiety in individuals facing life-limiting illness [10–14]. Concurrently, psychedelic-like substances, such as ketamine or MDMA, are also showing promise in alleviating distress, depression, and pain in palliative care patients [15–18]. While palliative care has evolved significantly over the last sixty years, clinicians continue to grapple with effectively handling existential distress in dying patients. Recent clinical studies exploring psychedelics in palliative care, commonly psilocybin, demonstrate promise – being administered to patients with life-limiting illness (predominantly cancer patients) to reduce their reactive anxiety and depression associated existential distress [19].

Past studies on psychedelics in palliative medicine

Most of the research on psychedelic-assisted therapy had been conducted in Euro-American settings, beginning in the late 1950s [20]. Studies of the analgesic potential of LSD discovered a reduction in death anxiety in patients with life-limiting cancer [21]. Several studies that provided similar results with cancer patients ensued [22–25] before research abated due to the US Controlled Substances Act of 1970, which reclassified LSD, psilocybin, MDMA and cannabis (among others) as Schedule 1 substances with no therapeutic value [26].

A review that identified six clinical studies on psychedelic-assisted therapy for treating anxiety, depression, or existential distress associated with life-limiting illness concluded the evidence for efficacy is still uncertain, suggesting more research is needed [27]. In 2004, Harbor-UCLA Medical Center was the first to recommence psychedelic-assisted therapy research (psilocybin) to treat anxiety in patients with advanced-stage cancer [28]. This was followed by two other clinical trials in cancer patients at Johns Hopkins and the New York University School of Medicine [10, 29]. These studies used up to two psilocybin treatment sessions ranging from moderate to high doses of psilocybin, and paired the sessions with psychotherapy protocols designed to help patients prepare for, experience, and integrate the drug sessions, resulting in a significant and sustained reduction in anxiety and depression about 6 months post-treatment. A further long-term follow-up of surviving patients [29] reported a reduction in anxiety, depression, hopelessness, and demoralization up to 4.5 years after the initial treatment, in addition to improved spiritual well-being [13]. A recent trial on psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy for patients with life-limiting illness at St Vincent hospital in Melbourne demonstrated durable relief from depression and anxiety [30]. Notably, these clinical trials demonstrated both the safety and efficacy of psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy under controlled conditions to treat psychological distress in patients with life-limiting disease such as cancer.

The origins of naturally occurring psychedelics, however, extend beyond Euro-American settings, prompting important questions about how these interventions align—or diverge—with diverse cultural perspectives on suffering, dignity, and existential significance. In East and Southeast Asia, for example, the notion of a “good death” may invoke different ancestral ties, beliefs, and family obligations. A recent scoping review of a good death in palliative care settings in China [31] underscores the potential tensions between these cultural philosophies and biomedical approaches—an issue we further explore in this article. Although interest in psychedelic-assisted therapy is growing, questions remain about how these interventions might help to alleviate the distress of patients facing life-limiting illness in diverse social and cultural contexts.

The rise of psychedelics – from nature to medicine

The development of psychedelic substances from natural products to their current status is important for understanding their potential roles in contemporary medicine. Historically, psychedelics were derived from plants, fungi, and other natural sources used in Indigenous societies of the Americas and elsewhere for healing, social welfare, identity formation, and other reasons [32]. Over the twentieth century, however, all classic psychedelic substances became classified as illicit substances, subject to strict regulation and criminalization, and dismissed as having no medical benefits [26, 33].

In recent years, there has been a dramatic shift: psychedelics have entered mainstream discourse and clinical research shaping what some diagnose as a period of potential hype or exaggerated hope in the field [34]. This flood of interest is occurring during a crisis in biological psychiatry innovation since the 2010s, following widespread disinvestment in neuro-psychopharmacological research and development [35]. In parallel, global psychedelic wellness tourism is increasing, particularly in South and Central American countries that permit such activities [36]. In some urban centers—such as major Canadian cities—psychedelics are increasingly available through unregulated channels, sometimes reminiscent of “corner store” products [37]. In 2022, Health Canada enabled a special access program for healthcare practitioners to employ psychedelics for serious or life-limiting conditions, with long-standing special access schemes in Switzerland and elsewhere offering precedents [38, 39]. In July 2023, the Therapeutic Goods Administration in Australia rescheduled psilocybin and MDMA from Prohibited Substances to Controlled Medicines for two indications: post-traumatic stress disorder (MDMA) and treatment-resistant major depressive disorder (psilocybin), inspiring a growing number of authorized prescribers currently administering psychedelics under clinical guidelines [40]. Regulatory momentum is increasing elsewhere, including in the United States, Czech Republic, Germany, New Zealand, and other jurisdictions drafting or implementing their own pathways.

Amid these developments, psychedelic-assisted interventions typically integrate drug administration with psychotherapy or counseling to guide and safeguard the experience and to enhance outcomes. However, the significance of the therapeutic support has been minimized in clinical trial research, focusing instead on isolating drug effects, a reflection of regulatory frameworks such as the FDA’s focus on drugs rather than psychotherapies [41, 42]. Given the profound relational ethics faced by clinicians providing palliative care [43, 44] and the profound effects of psychedelic substances on meaning, perception and emotion [7], it is difficult to envision this class of drugs in palliative care within a purely pharmacological model (i.e., without concomitant support). While the psychotherapy that is paired with psychedelic administration in clinical trials has varied considerably [42], approaches such as logotherapy that focuses on helping people find personal meaning in life, have proved popular in palliative care contexts [45]. In parallel, researchers have suggested the neuroplastic effects of the substances – when paired with the sense of meaningfulness – can inspire and contribute to therapeutic benefit [46]. Furthermore, given the evidence for cultural priors and set and setting variables to psychedelic drug responses [47], we suggest interventions should account for the drug’s interactions with the unique social and cultural expectations of patients facing life-limiting illness.

Psychedelics, cultural context, and a “good death”

We now explore anthropological and thanatalogical perspectives to reveal how psychedelics have long been intertwined with notions of mortality. Such evidence is helpful for understanding the therapeutic and cultural potential of administering psychedelics to dying patients. Psychedelics have been linked to themes of death, suffering, healing, ancestors, and salvation—or other soteriological pursuits—across Amerindian, African, and other societies [48, 49].

One emblematic example is the Amazonian psychedelic brew “ayahuasca,” which translates to “vine of souls” in the Quechuan language. Its diverse Indigenous origin narratives feature luminous beings emerging from mythical struggles of death and birth, embodying knowledge about the phenomenological effects of the tryptamine plant brew [48]. Today, ayahuasca continues to be consumed by Indigenous societies for cultural purposes [50] and by millions of users across the globe, often to promote well-being through the deeply existential experiences it occasions [36]. In fact, medical researchers suggest that high-dose psychedelic experiences mirror near-death experiences [51, 52], pointing to the possible relevance of these substances in end-of-life care. Yet, as Tramacchi reminds us, “The naive notion of psychedelics as ‘enlightenment in a pill’ rings hollow when faced with a viscous vegetable brew of indescribable acridity and bitterness that initiates sweating, nausea, chills, vomiting, and a visionary audience with the forces of death” [48]. Such experiences have been termed “ego death” and “ego dissolution” in psychedelic science [53], referring to spiritual and psychological experiences of dying, or encountering “a little death” [48] that provides a contained yet profound confrontation with mortality. Although these dynamics are neither simple nor universally uniform, they hint at why contemporary palliative care may benefit from the resonance between psychedelic phenomenology and the human quest for meaning when faced with life-limiting illness.

A good death

Research and practice in palliative and hospice care has suggested there are general attributes of a “good death,” including: “pain and symptom management, awareness of death, patient’s dignity, family presence, family support, and communication among patient, family, and health care providers” [54]. Yet, anthropologists and others have critiqued such universal views by pointing to variation and differences [31, 55, 56]. Anthropological perspectives consider how a good death unfolds within social, familial, and cosmological frameworks rather than through purely individual choices. Religious and spiritual beliefs can differ markedly across cultural contexts, shaping how people conceive of dying and bereavement. For instance, Mathews, Yang, and Kwong [57] show that while many Americans wrestle with Christian notions of heaven or a secular void, many Japanese incorporate Buddhist concepts of reincarnation alongside ancestral consciousness.

In Chinese contexts, the “religious question” is markedly pluralistic [58] where diverse traditional philosophies and Western medical perspectives both shape conceptions of dignity and decision-making in palliative care [31].1 Informed by Chinese philosophies of Confucianism, Daoism, Buddhism, and/or folklore traditions, along with biomedical knowledge, families might decide together whether a patient should pursue novel therapies, or shield that individual from distressing news if open discussion is deemed taboo [59–61]. Yet, Jing-Bao Nie [62] cautions against framing these differences purely in East–West terms—stressing that concealment of diagnosis or prognosis from patients, for example, emerges from relational ethics rather than simply paternalism. At the cultural level, Confucian ideals of filial duty can heighten fears of burdening relatives, making conversations about prognosis or care emotionally charged. A psychedelic session that evokes strong feelings of gratitude or guilt might therefore reinforce obligation in some patients, while in others it could open space to reframe duty as shared care. Buddhist and folk understandings of rebirth and karma can also shape how patients experience ego-dissolution or visions of the dead. For some, these moments might affirm continuity beyond death; for others, especially those who view illness as karmic retribution, they may intensify distress unless carefully supported. Such dynamics suggest that Chinese ethical and spiritual worlds may influence not only the content of psychedelic experiences but also their emotional trajectory and therapeutic challenges. When considering these practices and questions, psychedelic therapy becomes a deeply moral undertaking, ultimately illuminating the cultural dimensions within which psychedelic-assisted interventions might operate.

What is good about a “good death” is both a clinical and moral question. Psychedelic humanities research has tended to expand rather than resolve moral diversity, suggesting that these substances reflect and amplify the ethical and cultural worlds into which they are introduced [63]. Seen alongside research on moral enhancement and relational medical ethics, this perspective invites us to consider how these drugs, by intensifying emotion and deepening the sense of meaning, interact with patients’ own moral and cultural understandings of what a “good death” entails [64]. In this view, the ethical potential of palliative psychedelic medicine lies less in any pharmacological production of virtue than in the enhanced or augmented relational conditions through which diverse forms of meaning and “goodness” may emerge in the process of dying or confronting mortality.

Within psychedelic research, the concepts of “set” (a patient’s mindset, including expectations, mood, and beliefs) and “setting” (the physical and social environment) have long been recognized as crucial to shaping psychedelic experiences [65, 66]. The “set and setting” framework underscores that a purely pharmacological view cannot capture the full spectrum of factors influencing patient experiences and outcomes. Furthermore, a person’s cultural background can shape how they experience introspection, altered states, music, aesthetics, and the meaning derived from a psychedelic session [47, 67]. Therefore, psychedelic therapy protocols for end-of-life care can be designed with cultural dimensions in mind. This could start with clarifying, at the outset, whether families typically make decisions, whether spiritual advisors or elders have a customary role, or how death-related taboos might affect patient preparation. Clinicians might invite relatives or religious practitioners into preparatory and integration sessions, discuss how introspective content can be shared (or not shared) within a family, or align certain spiritual practices (such as chanting, prayer, or offerings) with the psychedelic session to support continuity between altered states and local beliefs.

Given the importance of “setting” to clinical outcomes [68], the physical environment can also be adapted. Simple measures—such as using culturally meaningful décor, playing supportive music, inviting patients to bring special objects, or displaying ancestral symbols—may help patients feel grounded in their heritage. Although, some research suggests that using music familiar to patients may generate idiosyncratic associations that conflict with therapeutic support approaches [69]. Studies are needed on the significance of the cultural appropriateness of music. The potential for engaging allied health workers, chaplains, or traditional healers offers additional support for integrating psychedelic insights into pre-existing belief systems during aftercare services. By emphasizing these dimensions, patients and families can more comfortably reconcile unusual or profound states of consciousness with existing cultural understandings.

These examples raise a challenging question: do psychedelics simply amplify existing belief systems, or can they meaningfully transform them? Clinical research suggests both possibilities [70]. Psychedelic states often recruit familiar symbols—ancestors, deities, cultural metaphors—but they can also inspire unfamiliar beliefs and reorder how these are felt in relation to self and others, sometimes softening rigid interpretations or enabling new forms of meaning-making. In Chinese palliative care, this means that psychedelic therapy may either reinforce, or gently reconfigure, pre-existing moral and cosmological commitments around filial duty, suffering, and the afterlife. Our argument is therefore not that psychedelics overwrite local worlds, but that they intensify and sometimes re-pattern them, making attention to those worlds ethically and clinically significant.

Yet, the cultural life of psychedelic medicine faces other challenges too. Despite growing scientific interest in psychedelics, their historical reputation as illicit substances can create uncertainty or fear within healthcare settings—especially those unfamiliar with their potential applications. Misinformation or stigma may impede buy-in from administrators, policy-makers, clinical staff, or patients themselves, underscoring the need for transparent dialogue and interdisciplinary education. Over time, providing reliable data on safety, efficacy, and cultural responsiveness can show a clearer picture for clinicians, patients, regulators, and other stakeholders.

From competency to cultural humility

Competency frameworks have helped establish psychedelic therapy as a credible field of practice. Early syntheses, such as Janice Phelps’ work, emphasized the importance of empathic presence, trust, and ethical awareness—qualities that echo across trial manuals and safety guidelines where “set and setting” remain significant for clinical practice [71–73]. In many ways, these frameworks aim to reassure clinicians and regulators that psychedelics could be brought into medicine with the same procedural clarity expected of any intervention. Yet these competencies, by their nature, also need to translate ineffable, sometimes spiritual, and often meaningful patient experiences into manageable interventions. A recent study on five English-language psychedelic therapy manuals and guidelines concluded that while the documents acknowledge the importance of cultural awareness, they “lack concrete skills and strategies to work within diverse cultural contexts effectively” [74]. The study highlights the importance of integrating cultural humility frameworks into psychedelic therapy training to enhance equitable and culturally-responsive care.

The notion of cultural humility in medicine was proposed by Tervalon and Murray-García in the late 1990s as a critique of cultural competence [1]. Whereas cultural competence stresses the ability of the clinician to ‘master’ the knowledge of their patients’ lived experiences from a professional lens, cultural humility emphasises the necessity for healthcare professionals to acknowledge the power imbalance within the patient-physician relationship, meaning that every clinical encounter becomes a learning process for the physician. As Lekas, Pahl and Lewis state, cultural humility implies “admitting that one does not know and is willing to learn from patients about their experiences” from the outset [75]. This shift reflects a growing awareness that psychedelic therapies may draw from diverse religious, spiritual and healing practices, and that bringing these substances into biomedical settings may raise complex questions about cultural appropriation, epistemic authority, and historical injustice [2, 3]. With this in mind, cultural humility is not simply about learning to be open-minded or inclusive. It involves sustained attention to diversity of belief and patient expectation, a willingness to sit with uncertainty, and a readiness to question the frameworks clinicians bring to the therapeutic encounter [75]. In this light, humility becomes a practice of listening and self-reflexivity—not just towards patients, but also towards the cultural, historical, and spiritual dimensions of care.

Nowhere does this need to prioritise the patients’ lay experience become clearer than in psychedelic-assisted palliative care. Psychedelic experiences can disrupt or augment ordinary ways of making sense of illness, identity, and mortality, and so they demand a clinical posture that is flexible and relational rather than directive or expert-driven. Death and dying constitute a highly intimate process. Beyond the complexity of addressing one’s existential distress, for which there is no ‘gold standard’ as in many other domains of clinical medicine, empowering patients to find therapeutic value in altered states during psychedelic sessions and embracing the uncertainties that are inevitable during treatment demand a high degree of humility [76]. This sense of humility, while not yet accepted as a norm in Western medicine, is a deeply valued virtue in the Chinese Confucian tradition [77]. While much of the clinical literature on humility has emerged from Euro-American contexts, it resonates with Chinese cultural perspectives.

In a recent qualitative study, palliative care providers affirmed the importance of cultural humility in the successful clinical implementation of psychedelic-assisted therapy [78]. Indeed, Mayer et al. highlight the peculiar nature of psychedelic use, citing an interviewed physician who observed that the physician is no longer in a dominant or controlling role, but must instead “rely on other members of the team rather than just using a medical approach”[78]. It is worth noting that palliative care is itself a recent invention. In this modern context, death and dying have become a medicalised process, and we have yet to reconcile the philosophical divide between the physical and the spiritual [79]. Societies around the world are arguably trending towards inclusiveness and multiculturalism, but we can only expect cultural humility to be incorporated into clinical practice if humility-based principles are integrated into medical education [80]. The road to establishing psychedelic-assisted therapy as a legitimate clinical option in palliative care will likely demand substantial capacity-building among future clinicians to facilitate this shift.

Conclusion

The promise of psychedelic-assisted therapies for end-of-life care is not solely a novel intervention in modern psychopharmacology but offers a translational lens on the cultural nature of dying. For decades, conventional medicine has struggled to address the profound existential distress that accompanies terminal illness, often leaving patients with a sense of isolation and loss of meaning. This distress has even been thought of as one of the driving forces in a patient’s desire to seek out medical assistance in dying (MAID) – currently legal in countries such as Canada and the Netherlands [81], prompting special ethical attention to the potential of psychedelic medicines for people facing life-limiting disease [82]. Although early trials report sizeable, durable reductions in anxiety and demoralization after a single psychedelic session, these data originate almost entirely from Euro-American contexts. Given the complexity of how cultural and relational dimensions of dying interact with psychedelic drug phenomenologies, frameworks need to be developed as to how culturally sensitive psychedelic interventions can be standardized. The Chinese case, with its pluralistic religious landscape and strong traditions of family-centred decision-making, illustrates how such a translational agenda might unfold and why psychedelic-assisted palliative care cannot be assumed to generalise straightforwardly from Euro-American trial settings.

This commentary invites clinicians to embrace a model of care that is both scientifically robust and culturally resonant. Ultimately, this interdisciplinary dialogue calls upon relevant stakeholders—clinicians, nurses, chaplains, scientists, policymakers, bioethicists, humanities scholars, and others, to continue to treat palliative care as a holistic endeavor, aiming to offer patients not merely an escape from distress but a more dignified, meaningful transition in the face of death.
