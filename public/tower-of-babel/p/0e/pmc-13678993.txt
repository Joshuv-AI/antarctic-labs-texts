The power of the pen: effectiveness of hand-drawn schematic diagrams In congenital heart disease ultrasound education for resident physicians : a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Liqing Lin, Xiaoqiong Chen, Qun Huang, Qiulin Zheng, Zhue Wu, Guorong Lyu, Ming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEducating resident physicians to master the complex. Educating resident physicians to master the complex echocardiographic diagnosis of congenital heart disease (CHD) represents a substantial pedagogical challenge. The central difficulty lies in translating dynamic two-dimensional ultrasound images into a robust three-dimensional spatial understanding. While high-technology teaching aids have emerged, their significant cost and limited accessibility impede widespread adoption. This study was designed to rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of a low-cost, high-cognitive-engagement instructional method—instructor-led, real-time hand-drawn schematic diagrams—in augmenting the CHD ultrasound diagnostic capabilities of resident physicians.MethodsA single-center, prospective randomized controlled trial was conducted. Forty residents rotating through the Department of Ultrasound at Zhangzhou Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University from September 2024 to August 2025 were enrolled. Participants were randomly assigned to either an intervention group (n=20) or a control group (n=20). The control group received conventional PowerPoint-based instruction. The intervention group received the same conventional instruction supplemented with instructor-led dynamic hand-drawn schematic diagrams. The primary outcomes were objective assessment scores in ultrasonic theoretical knowledge, practical skill operation, and clinical reasoning abilities. Secondary outcomes encompassed subjective learning satisfaction and self- perceived understanding.ResultsAll 40 participants successfully completed the study protocol. Theintervention group demonstrated significantly superior performance compared with the control group in theoretical knowledge (88.20 ± 3.96 vs. 82.95 ± 2.37, p < 0.001) and clinical reasoning abilities (90.50 ± 2.76 vs. 82.75 ± 3.60, p < 0.001). However, no statistically significant difference was observed in practical skills scores (89.40 ± 3.24 vs. 87.20 ± 4.99, p = 0.106). Subjective questionnaire surveys revealed that the intervention group provided significantly higher ratings across all assessed dimensions of learning and overall teaching satisfaction (all p < 0.05).ConclusionsIn the context of CHD ultrasound education for residents, theintegration of instructor-led hand-drawn schematic diagrams markedly enhances learners’, theoretical knowledge and clinical reasoning. The limited direct impact on practical skills highlights the distinct developmental pathways for cognitive versus psychomotor competencies. These findings validate hand-drawn diagrams as an effective, economical, and readily implementable pedagogical tool, particularly well- suited for constructing the complex conceptual and spatial frameworks essential for mastering echocardiography.Keywords: Medical education, Residency training, Echocardiography, Congenital heart disease, Hand-drawn diagrams, Cognitive Load Theory, Generative Learning

Introduction

Echocardiography stands as the cornerstone technology for the diagnosis of congenital heart disease (CHD), yet its learning curve is notoriously steep for trainees [1].Resident physicians universally confront the formidable challenge of mentally reconstructing complex, three-dimensional cardiac anatomy and understanding aberrant hemodynamics from a series of dynamic, two-dimensional sectional images presented on a screen. Traditional.

pedagogical methods, predominantly PowerPoint-based lectures, often deliver information through static diagrams or pre-recorded video loops. This passive mode of information delivery can hinder the development of deep, coherent spatial cognitive models, frequently resulting in fragmented knowledge and a superficial understanding of intricate pathophysiological processes [2].

In response to these challenges, the field of medical education has embraced a variety of technology-enhanced learning (TEL) tools. These include high-fidelity simulators, 3D-printed cardiac models, and immersive virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) platforms [3]. While such technologies offer highly realistic and interactive visual experiences, their practical implementation is often constrained by prohibitive costs, reliance on specialized technical support, and significant maintenance requirements. These factors limit their widespread application, particularly in resource-constrained healthcare environments. More fundamentally, a potential pedagogical pitfall of these advanced tools is that they may inadvertently cast learners as “cognitive bystanders”—passive observers of a technologically perfected representation, rather than active participants in the intellectual process of knowledge construction [4].

To overcome the above drawbacks, we adopt a stepwise sequential schematic drawing teaching strategy in CHD training. Instead of presenting finished illustrations directly, instructors sketch cardiac anatomical structures and ultrasonic scanning diagrams progressively following a logical sequence from normal cardiac anatomy to pathological lesions and from gross anatomy to localized sonographic features alongside classroom explanation. This step-by-step drawing conforms to residents’ cognitive habits and facilitates the formation of systematic knowledge architecture.

The theoretical underpinning of our study is rooted in established principles of cognitive psychology. Generative Learning Theory posits that meaningful learning occurs most effectively when learners are actively engaged in selecting, organizing, and integrating new information with their pre-existing knowledge schemas [5]. The act of drawing, or even closely observing the act of drawing, is a quintessential generative activity. It compels the learner to translate abstract textual and conceptual information into a concrete graphical representation, a process that necessitates deep cognitive processing. Furthermore, this approach aligns with Cognitive Load Theory (CLT), which distinguishes between intrinsic, extraneous, and germane cognitive loads [6]. By systematically deconstructing complex anatomical structures and hemodynamic pathways into simplified, sequentially drawn schematic diagrams, the instructor can effectively minimize extraneous cognitive load (the mental effort unrelated to learning). This allows learners to allocate their finite cognitive resources toward germane cognitive load—the deep processing required for schema construction and automation [7].

Although the utility of hand-drawing has been recognized in disciplines such as gross anatomy and surgery [8], its efficacy as a systematic teaching intervention within the highly dynamic and spatially demanding field of CHD ultrasound education has not been rigorously established. Specifically, the differential impact of this technique on cognitive abilities (knowledge and reasoning) versus psychomotor skills (hands-on scanning) lacks robust evidence from randomized controlled trials. Therefore, this study was designed to test the following hypotheses: (1) Compared with traditional PowerPoint-based teaching, an instructional method integrating instructor-led hand-drawn schematic diagrams would significantly enhance residents’ theoretical knowledge and clinical reasoning abilities in CHD echocardiography. (2) The cognitive benefits conferred by this intervention would not directly translate into a statistically significant improvement in hands-on practical scanning skills, highlighting the need for distinct pedagogical strategies for different learning domains.

Methods

Study design and participants

This investigation was conducted as a single-center, prospective, two-arm parallel randomized controlled trial at the Department of Ultrasound, Zhangzhou Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. The study period extended from September 2024 to August 2025. This study lasted for one academic year, including four independent resident rotation cycles, rather than a single rotation. Each rotation included 8–10 resident learners, and the number of participants remained stable in each batch.

The study population consisted of residents enrolled in the standardized residency training program at our institution who were undertaking their scheduled rotation in the Department of Ultrasound.The participants in this study were general clinical residents in the standardized residency training stage, not specialized radiology or cardiology residents. They had no systematic CHD ultrasound learning experience before this training, which ensures the homogeneity of the research objects. Inclusion criteria required participants to be current residents within the hospital’s training program. Exclusion criteria were defined as: (1) previous systematic training in cardiac ultrasound exceeding one month in duration, or (2) demonstrated ability to.

independently perform and interpret routine cardiac ultrasound examinations prior to the rotation.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group or the control group. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent statistician, not involved in participant recruitment or outcome assessment, using a computer-based random number generator. To ensure allocation concealment, the group assignments were sealed in sequentially numbered, opaque envelopes. Upon a resident’s enrollment, the research.

coordinator would open the next available envelope in the sequence to reveal the group assignment. We adopted a phased grouping teaching mode to avoid cross-interference. The control group and intervention group received training in different months, and no simultaneous training of two groups occurred during the whole study period, which effectively eliminated the information leakage and learning bias between groups.The examiners responsible for assessing the primary and secondary outcomes, as well as the statisticians performing the data analysis, remained blinded to the participants’ group allocations throughout the study.

Interventions

Both groups participated in a four-week specialized training module on CHD ultrasound.Sessions were conducted once weekly, each lasting 90 min. The curriculum covered the fundamental diagnostic principles for common CHDs, including atrial septal defect (ASD), ventricular septal defect (VSD), and tetralogy of Fallot (TOF). To ensure consistency and homogeneity in teaching quality, a total of three attending physicians with rich CHD ultrasound teaching experience participated in the lecture teaching. All instructors received unified standardized training of hand-drawing teaching paradigm before the study, and were fully proficient in the whole process of schematic drawing and synchronous explanation, ensuring consistent teaching quality.

Control group (traditional teaching method):

Participants in this group received a 60-minute lecture delivered via PowerPoint. All lectures were strictly controlled within 60 min. To match the time limit with hand-drawing links, we appropriately streamlined the repetitive and low-value PPT picture display content, retained the core pathological and ultrasonic key knowledge, and realized the organic balance between interactive drawing teaching and knowledge explanation, without affecting the integrity of key teaching content. The presentation materials included standard text, static anatomical images, and pre-recorded ultrasound video clips. This lecture was followed by a 30-minute hands-on practice session where residents practiced scanning on standardized patients or simulators under instructor guidance.

Intervention group (hand-drawn schematic diagram teaching method):

This group also received a 60-minute lecture and a 30-minute practice session. However, during the lecture, when explaining key complex concepts (e.g., anatomical classifications of defects, intricate blood flow pathways), the instructor would pause the PowerPoint presentation. Using a whiteboard and multiple colored markers, the instructor would dynamically and sequentially draw schematic diagrams of CHD anatomy and illustrations of the corresponding hemodynamics. This drawing process was accompanied by a “think-aloud” narration, externalizing the expert’s reasoning process. Students were actively encouraged to replicate the drawings on paper. This hand-drawing component constituted approximately one-third of the total lecture time. The subsequent 30-minute hands-on practice was identical to that of the control group.

In the intervention group, all participating residents completed on-site schematic drawing replication under the guidance of instructors during the 30-minute practical practice session. In addition, we encouraged residents to sort out and replicate the hand-drawn diagrams after class to consolidate their learning (Figs.1, 2).

Outcome measures and assessment

Upon completion of the four-week training module, all participants underwent a.

comprehensive final assessment structured as an Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE).

Primary outcomes:

Theoretical Knowledge Assessment (100 points): A closed-book written examination consisting of multiple-choice questions and short-answer questions. This component assessed the participants’ mastery of CHD anatomy, pathophysiology, key ultrasound diagnostic criteria, and differential diagnoses.

Theoretical Knowledge Assessment (100 points): A closed-book written examination consisting of multiple-choice questions and short-answer questions. This component assessed the participants’ mastery of CHD anatomy, pathophysiology, key ultrasound diagnostic criteria, and differential diagnoses.

Hands-on Practical Skills Assessment (100 points): Participants were required to perform standard sectional scanning and measurements for specific CHD cases on either standardized patients or a high-fidelity ultrasound simulator. A blinded, trained examiner performed real-time scoring using the validated Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS) scale.

Hands-on Practical Skills Assessment (100 points): Participants were required to perform standard sectional scanning and measurements for specific CHD cases on either standardized patients or a high-fidelity ultrasound simulator. A blinded, trained examiner performed real-time scoring using the validated Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS) scale.

Clinical Reasoning Ability Assessment (100 points): Participants were provided with standardized CHD case vignettes, including clinical history and a set of ultrasound images/clips. They were required to independently write a complete, structured ultrasound report and provide a detailed diagnostic analysis within a 30-minute timeframe. Two blinded examiners independently scored these reports using a standardized rubric, and the average of the two scores was used for analysis.

They were required to independently write a complete, structured ultrasound report and provide a detailed diagnostic analysis within a 30-minute timeframe. Two blinded examiners independently scored these reports using a standardized rubric, and the average of the two scores was used for analysis.

Secondary outcomes

Subjective Assessment Questionnaire: A post-training questionnaire was administered to gauge participants’ self-perceived learning experiences. A Visual Analog Scale (VAS), ranging from 0 (not at all helpful/satisfied) to 10 (extremely helpful/satisfied), was used to assess five dimensions: (1) understanding of cardiac anatomy, (2) understanding of hemodynamics, 3) mastery of theoretical knowledge, 4) improvement in clinical reasoning abilities, and 5) overall satisfaction with the teaching methodology.

Statistical analysis

All data were compiled and analyzed using SPSS software, version 27.0 (IBM Corp.,Armonk, NY). Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Data that followed a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation(Mean ± SD), and between-group comparisons were performed using independent samples t- tests. Data that were not normally distributed or were ordinal in nature (i.e., VAS scores) were presented as median and interquartile range [M (IQR)] and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages [n (%)], with between-group comparisons conducted using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

A total of 40 eligible residents were successfully enrolled and randomized into the study, with 20 allocated to the intervention group and 20 to the control group. No candidates were excluded per exclusion criteria during enrollment. All 40 participants completed the entire four-week training program and the final assessments. There was no loss to follow-up or withdrawal from the study, as depicted in the CONSORT flow diagram (Fig. 3). Primary and secondary outcomes

Regarding the primary outcomes, the intervention group achieved a mean theoretical knowledge assessment score of 88.20 ± 3.96, which was significantly higher than the control group’s mean score of 82.95 ± 2.37 (p < 0.001). A similar significant difference was observed in the clinical reasoning ability assessment, where the intervention group’s mean score (90.50 ± 2.76) was substantially higher than that of the control group (82.75 ± 3.60) (p < 0.001). In contrast, there was no statistically significant difference between the groups in the hands-on practical skills assessment scores (intervention group: 89.40 ± 3.24 vs. control group: 87.20 ± 4.99, p = 0.106). Subjective assessment

The results from the subjective assessment questionnaire indicated a strong preference for the intervention methodology. Participants in the intervention group provided significantly higher ratings across all five assessed dimensions compared with the control group. They reported that the hand-drawn schematic diagram teaching method was more effective in helping them understand cardiac anatomy, comprehend complex hemodynamics, master theoretical knowledge, and improve their clinical reasoning abilities. Consequently, their overall satisfaction with the teaching method was also significantly higher (all p < 0.05). A detailed comparison of these subjective scores is provided in Table 3; Fig. 5.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial provides robust evidence that integrating instructor-led, hand-drawn schematic diagrams into CHD ultrasound education significantly enhances residents’ theoretical knowledge and clinical reasoning. This primary finding aligns powerfully with the principles of generative learning theory, which contends that active engagement in creating mental and physical representations fosters deeper cognitive processing and more durable learning than passively receiving pre-packaged information [5].The dynamic, step-by-step construction of diagrams by the instructor acts as a potent cognitive scaffold, deconstructing complex three-dimensional spatial relationships and hemodynamic principles into manageable, sequential chunks. This process effectively manages the intrinsic cognitive load of the subject matter, allowing learners to dedicate their cognitive resources to the germane load of building and reinforcing robust conceptual schemas, rather than struggling to decipher dense, static visuals [7, 9].

The “think-aloud” narration that accompanies the drawing process is a critical component of the intervention’s success. It externalizes the expert’s reasoning, providing learners with a transparent model of how to approach complex diagnostic problems—from identifying key anatomical landmarks to tracing aberrant blood flow. This contrasts sharply with conventional lectures, where the final polished slide often obscures the intellectual journey required for its creation. The inherent simplicity and clarity of hand-drawn diagrams, as noted by learners in similar educational contexts [8], are key to their effectiveness. They filter out extraneous visual “noise” and compel the instructor to highlight only the most critical anatomical and hemodynamic features. This intentional simplification is crucial for reducing extraneous cognitive load, a critical factor for novices who can be easily overwhelmed by the visual density of medical imaging [6, 10].

This study confirms that hand-drawing teaching markedly improves residents’ cognitive mastery of CHD ultrasound knowledge, which represents the core and most valuable finding of the present low-cost educational intervention. Notably, the current study observed a more prominent improvement in learners’ cognitive knowledge compared with psychomotor skills after standardized training. While the intervention yielded significant improvements in cognitive outcomes (knowledge and reasoning), it had no statistically significant effect on hands-on scanning abilities. Notably, the relatively high baseline cognitive score of the control group (mean score = 87%) reduced the difficulty of cognitive assessment, which made it inappropriate to define the inter-group outcome difference as an obvious dissociation between cognitive and psychomotor skill acquisition.This “null finding” is not a failure of the intervention but rather a crucial clarification of pedagogical principles: cognitive understanding (“knowing what”) and procedural skill (“knowing how”) are distinct learning domains that necessitate different instructional approaches [11]. Psychomotor skills, such as the nuanced manipulation of an ultrasound probe to obtain specific cardiac views, are acquired primarily through deliberate, repetitive practice coupled with immediate, targeted feedback—a process that builds muscle memory and procedural automaticity [12]. Our intervention provided a superior “mental map” for navigating cardiac anatomy, but it did not, nor was it designed to, substitute for the physical practice of “driving” the probe. This underscores the imperative for a blended curriculum where cognitive tools like drawing are employed to build a strong conceptual foundation before learners engage in intensive hands-on simulation or clinical practice, thereby making that practice more efficient, targeted, and purposeful [13].

In an era increasingly dominated by high-technology educational solutions, our findings champion a “return to basics” approach that is not only remarkably cost-effective and universally accessible but also pedagogically potent. Hand-drawing should not be viewed as an antiquated or inferior alternative to VR or high-fidelity simulation. Instead, it should be recognized as a complementary tool with a unique and powerful strength: fostering deep, generative cognition. Its utility is particularly pronounced in resource-limited settings, where it can serve as a powerful instrument for promoting educational equity. By empowering educators with a simple yet effective tool, we can ensure that high-quality conceptual training in complex fields like CHD echocardiography is not limited by budget or infrastructure.

This study possesses several strengths, including its rigorous randomized controlled design, the use of validated assessment tools, and blinded outcome assessment, which collectively minimize bias.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged.Intervention members could distinguish hand-drawing teaching, possibly causing subjective satisfaction bias. High baseline cognitive scores weakened test discrimination for cognitive improvement. As a single-center study with a modest sample size, the generalizability of our findings to other institutions or training programs may be limited. Furthermore, we did not assess the long-term retention of knowledge and skills, which is a critical measure of educational effectiveness. Current assessments rely only on written and practical tests without long-term clinical follow-up, limiting evaluation of real-world clinical performance and translational value. Future research should aim to address these limitations through multi-center trials with larger cohorts and long-term follow-up assessments, as well as explore the integration of advanced educational technologies and hand-drawing pedagogy to utilize innovative teaching tools while preserving hand-drawing’s strengths in facilitating active learning, improving learner engagement and realizing expert think-aloud interpretation. Moreover, long-term clinical outcome tracking is warranted in future studies to further verify the practical clinical value of hand-drawing teaching strategies.Additionally, it would be valuable to compare instructor-led drawing with learner-generated drawing activities to determine if the physical act of drawing by the student confers additional cognitive benefits, as suggested by generative learning theory.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial demonstrates that integrating instructor-led hand-drawn schematic diagrams is a highly effective, low-cost strategy for teaching the foundational cognitive components of CHD echocardiography to resident physicians. By promoting active cognitive engagement and effectively managing cognitive load, this simple method significantly improves theoretical knowledge and clinical reasoning. The findings advocate for a balanced and thoughtful pedagogical approach, where the timeless power of the pen is leveraged to build a strong conceptual framework. This foundation, in turn, enhances the value and efficiency of subsequent high-tech simulation and hands-on clinical training.Medical educators should embrace such evidence-based, high-impact, low-cost tools to cultivate a more effective, equitable, and engaging learning ecosystem for the next generation of physicians.
