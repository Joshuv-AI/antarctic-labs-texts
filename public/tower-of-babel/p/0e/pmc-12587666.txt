Ustekinumab enhances intestinal stenosis resolution by modulating fibrotic pathways in Crohn’s disease: a retrospective single-center study with translational analysis
Authors: Rieko Nakata, Shuhei Hosomi, Yumie Kobayashi, Yu Nishida, Hirotsugu Maruyama, Masaki Ominami, Yuji Nadatani, Shusei Fukunaga, Koji Otani, Noriko Kamata, Fumio Tanaka, Tatsunari Fukuoka, Kiyoshi Maeda, Yasuhiro Fujiwara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimFew studies have evaluated the impact of biologic therapy on endoscopic findings and stenoses of small intestinal lesions in Crohn’s disease (CD). This study aimed to compare the endoscopic effectiveness and stenotic changes induced by anti-tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (anti-TNF) and ustekinumab (UST) in patients with CD, along with histological and molecular comparisons of stenosis between the groups.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled patients with CD who underwent balloon-assisted enteroscopy before and after initiating anti-TNF therapy or UST. For pathological and molecular evaluation of stenosis, we analyzed resected ileal specimens from patients with CD treated with anti-TNF, UST, or those who were biologic-naïve (bio-naïve).ResultsThe anti-TNF group (n = 18) showed a higher improvement rate in endoscopic activity scores compared to the UST group (n = 19). However, 27.8% of patients in the anti-TNF group experienced worsened stenosis, significantly higher than that in the UST group (0%). Histologically, the UST group showed a lower fibrosis score and reduced collagen III protein expression in the inactive ileum compared to both the anti-TNF and bio-naïve groups. Additionally, the UST group exhibited lower mRNA levels of IL22, TGFB1, and TNF in both active and inactive ilea. Immunostaining revealed fewer α-smooth muscle actin-positive cells in the UST group compared to the anti-TNF and bio-naïve groups in both active and inactive ileum.ConclusionThis study suggests that inhibition of the IL-22/TGF-β pathway by anti-IL-23 treatment with UST may reduce myofibroblasts and improve small intestinal fibrous stenosis in patients with CD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04386-w.Keywords: Small intestinal stenosis, Crohn's disease, Ustekinumab, IL-22/TGF-β pathway

Introduction

New biologics and Janus kinase inhibitors for Crohn’s disease (CD) have become available in recent years, leading to an increase in medical treatment options and a corresponding decrease in surgical resection rates [1]. However, there are no established standards for selecting among these biologics, and we currently choose biologics based on our clinical experience. Although several reports have compared the effectiveness of various biologics [2–4], few have examined their therapeutic effects on small intestinal lesions. Small intestinal lesions occur in approximately 65% of patients with CD [5, 6], and deep small intestinal lesions have been reported as a risk factor for poor outcomes [7]. Therefore, evaluating these lesions is important. Additionally, endoscopic healing has been proposed as a treatment goal in the Selecting Therapeutic Targets in Inflammatory Bowel Disease-II [8], and endoscopic evaluation is essential for assessing depth of remission [9]. However, there are limited reports comparing the effectiveness of biologics through endoscopic assessment of small intestinal lesions using balloon-assisted enteroscopy (BAE) [7].

We have occasionally encountered cases in which mucosal healing occurs with the introduction of biologics, yet stenosis worsens, necessitating endoscopic dilation or surgery. Reports indicate that fibrosis scores increase in surgical specimens following the administration of anti-tumor necrosis factor-α inhibitors (anti-TNF) [10], and that stenosis has developed in several patients after receiving infliximab (IFX) [11], suggesting that anti-TNF may elevate the risk of fibrosis. However, some studies suggest that surgery can be avoided by using anti-TNF [12], and that IFX is not a risk factor for intestinal stenosis [13]. In addition, experiments using mouse and human intestines have shown that anti-tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α stimulates myofibroblasts to produce excessive collagen and alters the expression of matrix metalloproteinases, thereby promoting fibrosis [14, 15]. While blocking TNF-α has been shown to prevent fibrosis in only animal models of CD [16], the effect of TNF-α on fibrosis remains controversial. Although a study using shear wave elastography reported that the stiffness of the intestinal wall at the stenosis site is reduced by the introduction of ustekinumab (UST) [17], there are no reports evaluating endoscopic or histological changes following the administration of UST. In a mouse model, the administration of IL-12/23 antibodies has been reported to reduce inflammation and fibrosis in dextran sulfate sodium-induced colitis; however, no comparisons have been made with the administration of anti-TNF [18].

This study aimed to compare the endoscopic effectiveness and changes in stenosis caused by anti-TNF and UST for small intestinal lesions in patients with CD who could be evaluated endoscopically using BAE before and after the introduction of these biologics. Regarding changes in stenosis, UST was adapted for CD than anti-TNF and was used in many cases where anti-TNF was ineffective; therefore, the inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to adjust for confounders in the evaluation. In addition, fibrosis was assessed pathologically and molecularly using resected specimens obtained during ileal surgery from patients receiving anti-TNF, UST, or those who were biologic-naïve (bio-naïve) CD. Comparing the effectiveness and changes in stenosis associated with each biologic in the small intestine may help in the selection of biologics.

Materials and methods

Patients who underwent endoscopic evaluation

This retrospective study was conducted at Osaka Metropolitan University Hospital and included patients with CD who underwent BAE to evaluate small intestinal lesions before and after the initiation of anti-TNF or UST treatment between September 2015 and January 2023. For patients who received multiple biologic agents for different within the period, the first biologic agent used was considered. The study protocol was approved by the Osaka Metropolitan University Graduate School of Medicine Ethics Committee (number 2021 − 227, approved on April 20, 2020) and was conducted in accordance with the 1975 Helsinki Declaration and later versions. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and information regarding this study was also publicly available online.

Endoscopic evaluation of small bowel lesions

The small intestine was divided into three sections based on previous reports [19]: jejunum, deep ileum, and terminal ileum. The terminal ileum was defined as within 10 cm of the Bauhin’s valve. Each section was evaluated using the modified Simple Endoscopic Score for CD (SES-CD) [19], which assesses ulcer size, proportion of ulcerated surface, proportion of affected surface, and stenosis, each rated on a scale of 0 to 3. The modified SES-CD for each section was summed, with a score of 0 assigned to sections where the scope could not be inserted. Comparisons were made for the same section before and after the introduction of biologics. Endoscopic activity was evaluated using the SES-CD active score (SES-CDa), which includes the sum of ulcer size, proportion of ulcerated surface, and proportion of affected surface [20]. Differences in modified SES-CDa before and after treatment >2 were considered an improvement, 0-1indicated no change, and < 0 indicated worsening. Endoscopic stenosis was assessed using the SES-CD item “presence of stenosis.” A decrease in stenosis scores before and after treatment was considered an improvement, no change was deemed unchanged, and an increase indicated worsening. Cases in which balloon dilatation was performed before and after the introduction of biologics were excluded from the analysis. Because this study focused on the evaluation of small bowel lesions, colonic and perianal disease activity was not assessed.

Tissue

For mRNA extraction and pathological evaluation, we used paraffin-embedded ileum specimens from patients with CD who underwent partial small intestine resection at our hospital’s Department of Surgery. These patients were either treated with anti-TNF or UST, or were bio-naïve, having never used biologics before, from June 2016 to January 2023. These biologic agents were not discontinued until before surgery. For protein extraction, we used ileum tissue from the cases mentioned above, which had been cryopreserved in our department.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using the AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE KIT (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. qPCR was performed in duplicate using SYBR Select Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR system. The primers used are listed in Additional file 1, and they were designed using Primer-BLAST on the NCBI Biotechnology Information genome browser. Gene expression was normalized to the expression level of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) mRNA.

Western blot analysis

Tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing a Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Protein content was measured using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were then resuspended in Sample Buffer Solution with 2-mercaptoethanol (2×) (Fujifilm, Osaka, Japan) and heated to 98℃ for 5 min. The proteins were resolved on polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4℃ with the following antibodies: mouse anti-collagen 1a1 (COL1A1) (1:1000; sc-293182; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA), rabbit anti-collagen 3a1 (COL3A1) (1:1000; 22734-1-AP; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL, USA), and rabbit anti-GAPDH (1:10,000; G9545; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). Proteins were detected using appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase and an enhanced chemiluminescence system (Amersham ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent; GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Buckinghamshire, UK). Chemiluminescence signals were captured and quantified using Fusion SOLO.7 S (Vilber, Collegien, France) and Evolution-Capt Edge software (version 18.02, Vilber).

Tissue staining

Section (μm) were cut, and Masson’s trichrome staining was performed. Sections were evaluated using a histological scoring system for fibrosis [21]. For immunostaining, deparaffinization and antigen retrieval were performed using Histofine antigen retrieval solution (pH 6, Nichirei Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan). The sections were incubated overnight at 4℃ with rabbit anti-α smooth muscle actin (SMA) (1:2000; ab5694; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Staining signal detection was performed using TaKaRa POD Conjugate for Tissue (MK205, Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) and a diaminobenzidine substrate (Nichirei Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Counterstaining was performed using hematoxylin. In three random fields for each case, α-SMA positive cells were counted, and the total area of the tissue was measured using WinROOF (Mitani Corporation, Fukui, Japan). Images were acquired using a 10× ocular lens and a 40× objective lens. The mean cell densities were calculated as cell numbers per 1 µm2. α-SMA in immunostaining has been used as a marker for myofibroblasts [22, 23].

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and as numbers for categorical variables. Categorical data were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when sample sizes were small. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Cumulative persistence rates of biologics were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test. Risk factors for intestinal stenosis were examined using univariate and multivariate ordinal logistic regression analyses, with anti-TNF use and SES-CD stenosis points included as variables in the multivariate analysis. To address the imbalance between the anti-TNF and UST groups, sensitivity analysis was performed using the IPTW method with 11 variables that could influence the main outcome. These variables were selected based on our clinical experience and knowledge, including disease activity prior to biologic induction (measured by the Harvey-Bradshaw index (HBI), C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin, white blood cells (WBC), and hemoglobin), age, sex, previous surgery, number of biologic failures, total SES-CDa score, and using immunomodulators. This approach enabled us to adjust for confounders using estimated propensity scores to construct weights for the data, allowing for evaluation of causal effects without reducing the sample size [24, 25].

Results

Endoscopic evaluation of effectiveness and intestinal stenosis following introduction of biologics

Additional file 2 shows the baseline patient characteristics. Compared to the anti-TNF group (n = 18), the UST group (n = 19) had a significantly higher number of surgeries and fewer bio-naïve cases, resulting in a higher number of refractory cases. WBC counts and SES-CDa scores were significantly lower in the UST group than in the anti-TNF group, resulting in more cases with low inflammation. The rate of improvement in the endoscopic activity score was higher in the anti-TNF group (83.3%) compared to the UST group (42.1%) (Fig. 1a). However, there were no significant differences in the SES-CDa score following the introduction of biologics (p = 0.17; anti-TNF: median 3.00, IQR 0.0–4.00; UST: median 4.00, IQR 2.00–6.00; Fig. 1b), nor in cumulative persistence rates between the two groups (p = 0.65, log-rank test; Fig. 1c), indicating that enterocolitis activity after biologic treatment may be similar. Regarding stenotic lesions, 27.8% of patients in the anti-TNF group experienced worsening after treatment, significantly higher than in the UST group (0%) (Fig. 1a).

Risk factors for intestinal stenosis analyzed after adjusting for background factors

Univariate analysis using ordinal logistic regression showed that anti-TNF use was a significant factor associated with worsening stenosis (odds ratio [OR], 11.7; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.84–230; p = 0.028; Additional file 3). Multivariate regression analysis confirmed that anti-TNF use remained a significant factor associated with worsening stenosis (OR, 11.3; 95% CI, 1.77–223; p = 0.030). Additionally, IPTW analysis, adjusted for background factors, yielded similar results regarding the risk of worsening stenosis after anti-TNF therapy (OR, 25.0; 95% CI, 2.55–3640; p = 0.035; Table 1).

Evaluation of histological fibrosis and comparison of collagen I and III protein expression

Because stenosis worsened endoscopically in the anti-TNF group, the degree of fibrosis was pathologically assessed using Masson’s trichrome staining of surgical resection specimens. Additional file 4 shows the baseline characteristics of patients from whom resected ileal specimens were collected. There were no significant differences in preoperative biomarkers or causes of surgery among the three groups. Masson’s trichrome staining showed no significant difference in the fibrosis score among the three groups in the active ileum; however, the UST group exhibited a lower fibrosis score compared to the anti-TNF group in the inactive ileum (Fig. 2ab). In the normal intestine, type I collagen is the most abundant, followed by types III and V [26]. It has been reported that in intestinal stenosis, the relative amounts of types III and V increase, with type III having particularly greater contractions [27]. Given that collagen types I and III are often used as indicators of fibrosis in CD [28, 29], COL1A1 and COL3A1 were also investigated in this study. In the inactive ileum, COL3A1 protein expression was significantly lower in the UST group compared to both the bio-naïve and anti-TNF groups (Fig. 2eg). Additionally, COL1A1 protein expression tended to be lower in the UST group than in the bio-naïve and anti-TNF groups (Fig. 2cg). In the active ileum, protein expression levels of both COL1A1 and COL3A1 were significantly lower in the UST and anti-TNF groups than in the bio-naïve group (Fig. 2dfg).

Comparison of T helper (Th) 1 and Th17 cytokine mRNA expression and myofibroblast expression numbers

T helper (Th) 1 and Th17 cytokine play a crucial in CD pathogenesis [30]. Th1 and Th17 cytokine levels decrease with the administration of anti-TNF and UST [31, 32], and these cytokines are known to affect fibrosis and myofibroblast activity [16, 33]. Given that differences in the expression of Th1 and Th17 cytokine resulting from the administration of anti-TNF and UST may contribute to variations in the severity of fibrosis, we measured the levels of specific Th1 and Th17 cytokine. In the inactive ileum, the mRNA levels of interleukins (IL) 22 and TNF were significantly lower in the UST group compared to the anti-TNF group. Additionally, the levels of transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGFB1) were significantly lower in both the UST and anti-TNF groups than in the bio-naïve group (Fig. 3a). In the active ileum, the mRNA levels of IL22, TNF, and TGFB1 were significantly lower in the UST group compared to both the anti-TNF and bio-naïve groups (Fig. 3b). Immunostaining for α-SMA revealed a significantly lower number of α-SMA-positive cells in the submucosa of the UST group compared to the anti-TNF and bio-naïve groups in both the active and inactive ileum (Fig. 3c).

Discussion

The study showed that anti-TNF was more effective in achieving endoscopic improvement than UST; however, it was also associated with a higher rate of worsening stenosis. In contrast, no cases of worsened stenosis were observed in the UST-treated group. Assessment of surgically resected specimens showed less histological fibrosis and lower collagen expression in patients treated with UST compared to those receiving anti-TNF and being bio-naïve. A potential molecular mechanism underlying these findings involve the suppression of IL-22, TGFB1, and TNF expression following UST administration may inhibit myofibroblast activity and subsequently reduce collagen production. To our knowledge, this study is the first to assess the molecular mechanisms of histological fibrosis along with changes in endoscopic stenosis in the small intestine following the induction of anti-TNF therapy or UST.

In a randomized trial comparing adalimumab (ADA) and UST, no significant difference in endoscopic remission was observed between the two groups; however, the evaluation of the small intestine was limited by the use of SES-CD [2], leading to an incomplete assessment. A network meta-analysis also compared clinical remission and response rates for IFX, ADA, and UST but did not specifically address endoscopic efficacy rates for small bowel lesions [34]. In contrast, studies evaluating the small intestine using BAE reported endoscopic response rates for small intestinal lesions ranging from 36 to 58% for anti-TNF and 36% for UST; however, these response rates were not directly compared [35, 36]. Takenaka et al. also lacked direct comparison of biologic efficacy in small intestinal lesions alone [37]. Our study employed BAE to directly observe the small intestine and evaluated the endoscopic response rate of small intestinal lesions to anti-TNF and UST using a modified SES-CD. The results showed that the response rate to anti-TNF therapy was significantly higher than that for UST. It is essential to consider that the UST group may have included many patients who had previously failed anti-TNF therapy, making these cases more challenging to treat. Therefore, further studies are necessary. Given the differences in background factors, we employed IPTW to minimize selection bias and confounding factors. This analysis revealed that that anti-TNF therapy is associated with an increased risk of stenosis compared to ustekinumab, highlighting the need for careful evaluation in clinical practice.

Based on the observed endoscopic changes in stenosis, we considered it necessary to evaluate the histological and molecular differences using surgical specimens. Because only tissues collected after the introduction of biologics could be evaluated, bio-naïve surgical specimens were compared in the same manner. Masson’s trichrome staining revealed that the pathological fibrosis score of the inactive ileum was lower in the UST group compared to the anti-TNF group, and collagen protein expression was decreased, consistent with the endoscopic findings. Further investigation into the expression of Th1 and Th17 cytokine, along with the number of myofibroblasts, suggested that UST administration suppressed the Th1 and Th17 pathways to determine the cause of fibrosis, leading to reduced levels of IL-22, TGFB1, and TNFα, subsequently reducing myofibroblast (Fig. 4). UST is an anti-IL-12/IL-23p40 antibody that blocks IL-12 and IL-23 to suppress the Th1 and Th17 pathways, respectively [38, 39]. In this study, it was presumed that the Th1 pathway was suppressed, which decreased TNF-α expression, and the Th17 pathway was suppressed, which decreased IL-22 expression. It has been reported that IL-22 and TNF-α promote the expression of TGF-β [40–42]. Therefore, in the UST group, TGFB1 expression might be suppressed by the decrease in the expression of IL22 and TNF levels observed in this study. IL-22 is known to facilitate the transformation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts [43], and TNF-α contributes to collagen accumulation and the migration of myofibroblasts [14, 15, 44]. TGF-β is known to be involved in fibrosis by acting on myofibroblasts [45, 46], and in cultured fibroblasts, IL-22 and TGF-β have been shown to act synergistically to induce fibrosis [43]. Based on these findings, we propose that the reduction in IL-22, TNF-α, and TGF-β leads to decreased myofibroblast activity and inhibited collagen production. However, although IL-22 has been reported to have protective effects in acute intestinal injury models [47], recent findings indicate that IL-22 may contribute to chronic colitis by inducing pathological epithelial ER stress and apoptosis [48]. The role of IL-22 also appears to vary by organ and disease phase—for example, it is protective in acute liver injury but promotes fibrosis during chronic inflammation [49]. While accumulating evidence suggests that IL-22 can exert either beneficial or harmful effects depending on the inflammatory environment and disease stage, its precise role in intestinal inflammation and fibrosis remains incompletely understood and warrants further investigation.

Schematic representation of the proposed pathway by which ustekinumab suppresses fibrosis The IL-12 and IL-23 levels were blocked by ustekinumab. Blocking IL-12 suppresses the Th1 pathway and downregulates TNFα expression. Blocking IL-23 suppresses the Th17 pathway and downregulates IL-22 expression. Decreased expression of TNF-α and IL-22 downregulate the expression. The effects of TNF-α, IL-22, and TGF-β on myofibroblasts are reduced, and fibrosis is suppressed

In the active ileum, the expression of collagen types I and III was significantly decreased in the anti-TNF and UST groups compared to the bio-naïve group, suggesting that anti-TNF and UST administration may improve fibrosis. However, Masson’s trichrome staining revealed no differences among the three groups, suggesting that severe inflammation may mask the accurate assessment of fibrosis. In the UST group, IL22, TGFB1, and TNF were suppressed compared to the bio-naïve group, raising the possibility that ongoing suppression of these cytokines during the healing process could inhibit collagen production by myofibroblasts, resulting in reduced stenosis in the future. Furthermore, TNF-α is also inhibited in the anti-TNF group [50, 51], implicating IL-22 and TGF-β as potentially the most important and specific factors in improving fibrosis in the UST group. However, because TGF-β has various effects, not only fibrosis but also suppression of T cell proliferation and activation through Treg differentiation [52], the present results may only show one aspect of the mechanism.

The present study had some limitations. First, it was a retrospective study with a very small sample size, and there were differences in background factors, which may have contributed to the wide confidence intervals in the risk analysis. To minimize the effects of selection bias and confounding factors, we used IPTW in the assessment of endoscopic stenosis; however, residual imbalance may still have influenced the results. Furthermore, nonrandomized treatment assignment may influence the results and requires further prospective study. Second, because the entire small intestine could not be observed with BAE, the endoscopic evaluation of effectiveness and intestinal stenosis for the introduction of biologics was analyzed within the range that could be observed. Patients with CD often experience stenosis of the small intestine and intestinal adhesions due to surgery or inflammation, complicating to examination of the entire small intestine [53]. Third, since the cases in this study required surgery, inflammation in the small intestine may be more severe than what is typically observed in clinical practice. As surgery was required for reasons despite the administration of anti-TNF and UST, there are cases in which these biologics could not completely eliminate activity or stenosis. Furthermore, as the causes of surgery varied, including stenosis, perforation, and inflammation, and anastomotic strictures were also included, the pathophysiology may differ among them. However, although anastomotic sites are known to be prone to stenosis [54], no increase was observed in the UST group on endoscopic evaluation, despite many postoperative cases. Finally, the effect of concomitant medications on fibrosis could not be investigated in this study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, inhibition of IL-22 and TGF-β by anti-IL-23 treatment could reduce myofibroblasts and contribute to the improvement of small intestinal fibrous stenosis in CD. These results may influence the selection of biologics for CD associated with small intestinal fibrosis and support the development of targeted therapeutic approaches for this condition.
