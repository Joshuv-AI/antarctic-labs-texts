Targeting the BMX in cancer: molecular mechanisms and emerging therapeutic strategies
Authors: Man Zhang, Jinchi Chen, Rong Liang, Wei Jiang, Min Lin, Siqi Wen, Dejun Liu, Bei Yi, Ruiqi Chen, Tianyu Chen, Zhao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBone Marrow Tyrosine Kinase on Chromosome X (BMX), also known as epithelial/endothelial cell kinase (ETK), is a core member of the Tec family of non-receptor tyrosine kinases. Structurally, BMX kinase contains an N-terminal PH domain, a BH domain, a SH2 domain, a SH3 domain and a C-terminal catalytic tyrosine kinase domain. This distinctive modular architecture enables BMX to engage in diverse cellular signalling cascades. It has been reported that BMX is highly expressed and plays an oncogenic role in various cancers. In this paper, we mainly explore the function and mechanism of BMX in tumour cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, metastasis, cancer stem cell properties, angiogenesis and drug resistance. Furthermore, the research focus on summarising the research progress of BMX inhibitors for cancer treatment. This review aims to provide a theoretical framework for cancer therapy by targeting BMX.Keywords: BMX, proliferation, apoptosis, stemness, drug resistance

Introduction

Bone Marrow Tyrosine Kinase on Chromosome X (BMX), also known as epithelial/endothelial cell kinase (ETK). It is Located in the Xp22.2 region of X chromosome between the genetic markers DXS197 and DXS207. The gene encodes a protein consisting of 675 amino acids1. The protein structure of BMX (Figure 1) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/; PDB ID: 6I99) and rendered using PyMOL. BMX belongs to the Tec family of non-receptor tyrosine kinases and shares the characteristic modular architecture conserved among Btk, Itk, Tec and Txk, which comprises an N-terminal pleckstrin homology (PH) domain, a Tec homology (TH) domain, Src homology 3 (SH3) and SH2 domains, and a C-terminal kinase domain2 (Figure 2). The N-terminal PH domain of approximately 100 residues mediates membrane localisation and protein–protein interactions by binding to phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3), which is essential for upstream activation3. The TH domain of around 80 residues contains a zinc-coordinating Btk-homology (BH) motif but lacks the proline-rich region (PRR) found in other Tec kinases; its structural integrity is critical for BMX activity4. Distinct from other family members, the SH3 domain of approximately 60 residues is intrinsically disordered in solution, as demonstrated by hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry and NMR spectroscopy, this disorder prevents canonical PXXP ligand interactions, and autophosphorylation at Tyr215 is required for full activation and substrate engagement5,6. The adjacent SH2 domain of roughly 100 residues recognises the phosphotyrosine-Xaa-asparagine (pYxN) motif, mediating interactions with activated receptor tyrosine kinases or adaptor proteins and recruiting BMX to signalling complexes to achieve substrate specificity7,8. The kinase domain of BMX exhibits the canonical bilobed architecture, with the ATP-binding pocket shaped by the G-loop, P-loop and key residues including Gly426, Lys445, Thr489 and Cys496. Lys445 (β3-lysine) forms electrostatic contacts with ATP phosphates and contributes to the catalytically essential Lys445–Glu460 salt bridge. Thr489 serves as the gatekeeper residue controlling inhibitor access to the back pocket, while Cys496 at the pocket entrance provides a thiol group for covalent binding to electrophilic moieties such as acrylamides, making it a key pharmacophore for designing covalent inhibitors such as BMX-IN-1 and JS24. The activation loop, containing Tyr566, undergoes conformational rearrangement to expose the substrate-binding site, enabling phosphorylation of multiple targets (e.g. FAK, IR, MET) and signal amplification. Unlike other Tec kinases, BMX lacks a C-terminal caveolin-binding motif, so its subcellular localisation depends primarily on PH domain-mediated membrane recruitment7,9,10. The synergistic action of these structural domains gives BMX kinase a key role in cell signalling.

However, beyond these structural disparities, BMX also diverges from other Tec family members in its activation mechanisms and tissue expression patterns. In terms of activation, BMX retains the conserved Tec family mechanism of PH domain–mediated membrane recruitment downstream of PI3K signalling but also displays distinctive regulatory modes, including Src-mediated Tyr566 phosphorylation and Phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-independent activation through focal adhesion kinase (FAK)–integrin signalling. Moreover, in contrast to the restricted haematopoietic expression profiles of other Tec family members, including Btk in B cells and erythroid-myeloid progenitors, Itk in T and NK lymphocytes, Tec in diverse haematopoietic lineages, and Txk in Th1 cell subsets, BMX exhibits an exceptionally broad tissue distribution extending beyond haematopoietic compartments. It is highly expressed in granulocytes and monocytes, as well as in arterial endothelial cells, diverse epithelial cells and numerous solid malignancies including prostate and breast cancers, where it regulates proliferation, apoptosis, differentiation, angiogenesis and inflammatory responses11–13.

BMX kinase activation (Figure 2) represents a fundamental mechanism in cellular signal transduction, occurring through a well-defined molecular cascade. The process initiates when extracellular ligands, including cytokines and growth factors, bind to their cognate receptors, leading to activation of the PH domain. This activated PH domain subsequently establishes interactions with three distinct molecular partners: phosphatidylinositol lipids, the β and γ subunits of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), and the FERM domain of FAK. These interactions disrupt the intramolecular association between the SH3 and BH domains of BMX kinase, thereby inducing a significant conformational rearrangement of the PH domain. The activation mechanism proceeds through a critical sequence of events: (1) The PH domain binds specifically to PIP3, facilitating BMX recruitment to the plasma membrane; (2) PI3K-mediated generation of PIP3 enhances this membrane localisation; (3) The resultant conformational changes promote BMX translocation to membrane microdomains containing SRC-family kinases. Within these specialised membrane compartments, phosphorylation occurs at the conserved tyrosine residue (Y566) in the kinase domain, initiating autophosphorylation events that culminate in full BMX kinase activation13–19.

Upon activation, BMX kinase mediates the phosphorylation and subsequent activation of both AKT and STAT family proteins. Within the PI3K/AKT signalling axis, BMX serves as a critical scaffolding component that facilitates the full activation of AKT at Ser473 following PI3K-mediated membrane recruitment, thereby potentiating downstream signalling through mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1), glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β), and forkhead box O (FOXO) transcription factors to coordinately regulate cellular proliferation, survival and metabolic reprogramming20. Concurrently, BMX establishes a non-canonical activation mechanism for signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) by directly phosphorylating Tyr705, thereby effectively bypassing the classical JAK kinase-dependent activation paradigm21. This direct phosphorylation capability is mediated through the cooperative engagement of PH domain-mediated membrane targeting and SH2 domain-dependent recognition of phosphotyrosine-containing docking sites on activated receptors or adaptor proteins. These interactions ensure substrate specificity and promoting efficient STAT3 dimerisation and nuclear translocation. Notably, BMX-mediated STAT3 activation exhibits marked resistance to suppressor of cytokine signalling 3 (SOCS3)-mediated negative feedback inhibition, which typically constrains JAK2-STAT3 signalling, thereby sustaining prolonged transcriptional programs governing cell growth, differentiation and inflammatory responses22. These two signalling axes are functionally interconnected through sophisticated feedback regulatory networks: activated STAT3 transcriptionally upregulates PI3K regulatory subunit p85α (encoded by PIK3R1), catalytic subunit p110α (PIK3CA), and AKT1 itself, establishing a positive feedback loop that amplifies PI3K/AKT signalling output; conversely, BMX-dependent AKT activation can indirectly modulate STAT3 transcriptional efficacy through mTORC1-mediated Ser727 phosphorylation, which context-dependently enhances or attenuates STAT3 target gene expression23,24. The capacity of BMX to mediate bidirectional crosstalk between PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT pathways, while evading canonical negative regulatory constraints, constitutes the fundamental molecular basis for its multifaceted roles in tumour progression, pathological angiogenesis and immune regulation. Furthermore, BMX interacts with the Toll-like receptor (TLR) signalling pathway, leading to the activation of NF-κB and MAPK signalling cascades. These cascades in turn regulate the expression and secretion of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and IL-8, thereby promoting inflammatory responses25–27. Additionally, BMX kinase directly activates the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), initiating angiogenic processes28–30. Collectively, BMX participates in diverse cellular biological and pathological functions through multiple signalling pathways (Figure 3).

BMX has gained increasing attention due to its involvement in multiple oncogenic signalling pathways. Elevated BMX expression has been reported in a range of malignancies, including prostate cancer, breast cancer and renal cell carcinoma31–33, where it contributes to tumour progression by promoting cell proliferation, survival and migration. In the era of precision oncology, targeted therapy has become a central strategy for cancer treatment34–36. However, its clinical success is often hindered by both primary and acquired resistance, driven by tumour heterogeneity, immunosuppressive tumour microenvironments and metabolic reprogramming37–39. Concurrently, emerging therapeutic paradigms, such as cancer immunotherapy, along with increasing recognition of psychosocial factors in patient management, have further expanded the landscape of cancer treatment40–44. Advances in molecular imaging technologies, particularly optical imaging, have also enabled real-time, non-invasive assessment of therapeutic responses and drug biodistribution, thereby facilitating the optimisation of targeted interventions45,46. Despite substantial progress in understanding the oncogenic functions of BMX, the development of effective BMX-targeted therapies remains in its infancy.

Currently available small-molecule inhibitors exhibit limitations including inadequate selectivity, suboptimal pharmacokinetic properties, and potential off-target effects, and no BMX-specific inhibitors have yet received clinical approval43,44,47. Therefore, elucidating the precise regulatory network of BMX in specific tumour microenvironments and developing novel, highly selective BMX inhibitors based on its protein structure are of significant importance for overcoming current therapeutic bottlenecks. This article elucidates the regulatory mechanisms of BMX in tumorigenesis and synthesises current research advancements in BMX inhibitors, with the objective of providing novel perspectives and therapeutic strategies for oncological interventions.

The relationship between BMX and tumours

The expression of BMX in tumours

A study found that BMX was expressed at higher levels in breast cancer cell lines than in the normal breast cell line MCF10A. Notably, triple-negative breast cancer cell lines displayed substantially higher BMX expression than cell lines expressing at least one hormone receptor. Furthermore, both mRNA and protein analyses revealed markedly increased BMX expression in breast cancer tissue specimens relative to normal tissue controls32,48.A similar pattern was observed in prostate cancer: malignant tissues demonstrated higher BMX expression than benign prostatic tissues49. Similarly, in hepatocellular carcinoma studies BMX expression was consistently upregulated in tumour cells compared to normal hepatocytes, with expression levels positively correlating with tumour differentiation status50. For intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, moderately and poorly differentiated tumours exhibited significantly greater BMX expression than well-differentiated tumors51. Cervical cancer studies showed elevated BMX expression in neoplastic tissues compared to normal cervical epithelium, with invasive cervical carcinoma (ICC) demonstrating higher expression levels than carcinoma in situ (CIS)52. Bladder cancer research revealed increased BMX expression in high-grade invasive cell lines (UM-UC-3 and T24) compared to normal urothelial cells, a finding corroborated by tissue analysis showing higher BMX levels in malignant bladder specimens53. In renal cell carcinoma, both established cell lines and tumour tissues exhibited significantly greater BMX expression than normal renal tubular epithelial cells (HK-2) and non-neoplastic renal tissues, respectively33. In neuro-oncology, glioblastoma stem cells were found to overexpress BMX relative to normal neural stem cells22. Moreover, BMX was preferentially expressed in undifferentiated neuroblastoma metastatic cells54. In studies of haematologic malignancies, BMX gene expression was detectable in patients with acute myeloid leukaemia and in patients with chronic myeloid leukaemia who presented higher levels of BMX expression. However, it was not detectable in cases of acute lymphoblastic leukemia55. Table 1 summarises the expression of BMX in various tumours. In summary, BMX may promote tumour development in a variety of solid tumours and malignant lesions, and its specific mechanism of action requires further investigation.

BMX in tumour proliferation

The essence of cancer lies in the transformation of normal cells into tumour cells, during which the cells acquire specific functions. Among them, a severe imbalance in cell cycle regulation allows tumour cells to proliferate without restriction. Therefore, an in-depth study of the mechanism of unrestricted proliferation of tumour cells is of crucial importance for the discovery of new therapeutic pathways and strategies. In breast cancer studies, BMX has been shown to enhance cellular proliferation by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway. Overexpression of BMX not only improves cell survival but also significantly accelerates proliferative activity32. In prostate cancer research, BMX has been found to phosphorylate and activate 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase-1 (3βHSD1), thereby promoting androgen biosynthesis and subsequent proliferation of LNCaP cells, an androgen-dependent cell line56. Furthermore, BMX contributes to neurotrophic factor-mediated activation of androgen receptor (AR), playing a crucial regulatory role in prostate cancer cell proliferation57. Studies in cervical cancer have revealed that elevated BMX expression enhances the proliferative capacity of C-33A cells. Mechanistic investigations demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of BMX activity induces cell cycle arrest at G0/G1 phase while reducing the proportion of cells in S and G2/M phases52. In renal cell carcinoma, experimental downregulation of BMX expression effectively suppressed the growth of clear cell renal carcinoma cell lines (786-O and 769-P)33. Furthermore, BMX knockdown disrupted the AKT/mTOR signalling pathway, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of clear cell renal cancer cells58. Studies showed that inhibiting the BMX/SOX9 pathway reduced pericyte coverage density and impaired blood-brain barrier integrity, leading to local microcirculation dysfunction, hypoxia and ischaemic necrosis in gliomas59. Moreover, BMX circumvented SOCS3-mediated JAK2 inhibition, sustained STAT3 activation in Glioblastoma Stem Cells (GSCs), and promoted glioma malignancy22. Additionally, knocking down BMX suppressed glioblastoma stem cell proliferation and tumour spheroid formation60. Summarily, BMX promotes tumour cell proliferation mainly through a number of different pathways, especially the Wnt/β-catenin, PI3K/AKT/mTOR and STAT3 signalling pathways.

BMX in tumour cell apoptosis

Apoptosis is an orderly, programmed process of cell death that plays an important role in organismal development, the maintenance of tissue homeostasis, and in disease processes. The ability of tumour cells to evade apoptosis is one of the key factors contributing to cancer treatment failure. One study revealed that downregulation of BMX resulted in enhanced sensitivity of small cell lung cancer (SCLC) H446 cells to adriamycin, which in turn elevated the level of adriamycin-induced apoptosis61. In breast cancer cells, BMX activated the aryl hydrocarbon receptor and contributes to the survival of MCF10AT1 breast cancer cells in an apoptosis-inducing environment62. A study of prostate cancer found that inhibition of BMX expression enhanced the transcriptional activity of p53, which promoted the expression of the cell cycle regulatory protein p21 and pro-apoptotic proteins and exacerbates the apoptotic response. Conversely, overexpression of BMX impaired the transcriptional activity of p53 and blocked its interaction with the mitochondrial protein BAK, leading to reduced apoptosis in prostate cancer cells63. In addition, high expression of BMX activated the PI3K/BMX signalling pathway, which helped prostate cancer cells evade apoptosis induced by photodynamic therapy or toxic carotenoids64. Another study revealed that reduced BMX gene expression induced apoptosis in renal cell carcinoma cells33, while BMX overexpression activated the AKT/mTOR signalling pathway, thereby inhibiting apoptosis in renal cell carcinoma cells58. Furthermore, high BMX expression enhanced radiation-induced G2/M cell cycle arrest in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and BMX interacted with apoptosis-associated proteins p53 and Bcl-2 to impair radiation-induced apoptosis in nasopharyngeal carcinoma65. In the human fibrosarcoma cell line HT1080, decreased expression of BMX resulted in increased activation of BAK proteins, which led to the release of apoptosis-promoting factors such as cytochrome c, thereby initiating the apoptotic program48. In conclusion, these studies indicated that BMX plays a crucial role in tumour apoptosis and survival.

BMX in tumour invasion and metastasis

Cancer progression and metastasis are key factors contributing to poor patient prognosis and death. Therefore, exploring the role of BMX in tumour invasions and metastasis is crucial for inhibiting tumour progression. Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a biological process that transforms epithelial cells to mesenchymal cells, played a central role in tumour invasions and metastasis66. BMX splice variant BMXΔN was able to enhance the migration ability of lung cancer cells. Wound healing assay showed that PC9 cells transfected with BMXΔN exhibited faster wound closure; Transwell assay also confirmed that the expression of BMXΔN significantly enhanced the migration ability of A549 cells67. In addition, miR-495 affected the EMT process in SCLC via regulation of BMX. Elevated BMX expression levels were associated with increased expression levels of mesenchymal markers (e.g. Zeb-2, Twist and Vimentin) that are associated with EMT. Conversely, decreased BMX expression levels were associated with decreased expression levels of EMT markers68. In breast cancer, BMX regulated the invasive ability of tumour cells by interacting with the C-terminus of PAR1. Mutations in PAR1 altered its ability to bind to BMX, thereby decreasing cell invasiveness. Wound healing and transwell assays showed that down-regulation of BMX expression inhibited the invasive ability of MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells, while overexpression of BMX reversed this inhibitory effect69. Furthermore, the complex formed by the combination of BMX and Fas promoted the metastasis of MC38 colon cancer cells to the lungs by the Tyr189 site of Fas and the SH2 structural domain of BMX. In addition, wound healing assay showed that inhibition of BMX reduced the promotion of Fas signalling on the migration ability of MC38 cells70. The fusion protein of BMX, BMX-ARHGAP, activated the JAK/STAT3 signalling pathway by enhancing the 5′UTR of ARHGAP, which increased the expression of BMX-SH2 protein and promoted the invasion of gastric cancer cells71. BMX regulated the migratory and invasive behaviours of renal cell carcinoma cells through activation of the AKT/mTOR signalling pathway, whereas knockdown of BMX significantly inhibited the migratory and invasive abilities of renal cancer cells58. Overall, BMX promotes tumour invasion and metastasis by regulating EMT and other signalling pathways.

BMX in tumour stemness

Cancer stem cells (CSCs) possess stem cell-like properties that have a decisive impact on tumour origin, progression, invasiveness, spread and resistance to therapy. Studies have revealed that BMX plays a key regulator role in the tumour stemness process. In gastric cancer, BMX-ARHGAP fusion protein elevated the level of BMX-SH2 protein by enhancing the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) of ARHGAP, a process that activated the phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3, which in turn promoted the expression of stemness markers such as CD133, CD44, SOX2 and Nanog. Overexpression of BMX-ARHGAP increased the expression of CD133, CD44, SOX2 and Nanog proteins and stimulated the proliferation and invasion of CD133-positive and CD44-positive cells, thereby enhancing the self-renewal ability of gastric cancer cells71,72. Moreover, STAT3 is a key signalling node for the maintenance of glioblastoma stem cells. BMX promoted the self-renewal of glioblastoma stem cells by activating the STAT3 signalling pathway in glioblastoma stem cells and regulating the expression of key transcription factors controlling the properties of the stem cells, such as SOX2, OLIG2, Nanog and OCT4. BMX knockdown effectively inhibited the STAT3 activation, expression of GSC transcription factors, and significantly reduced GSC activity, thereby inhibiting the malignant growth of glioblastoma (GBM)73–75. In summary, BMX plays a key role in tumour stemness. Therefore, therapeutic strategies against BMX may become a promising target for future anticancer therapy.

BMX in tumour angiogenesis

Tumour angiogenesis is the process by which neovascularization sprouts from an existing vascular network and extends into the interior of the tumour tissue during tumour development. Angiogenesis is a key marker of malignant progression of a tumor76. Tumour expansion is largely dependent on vascular proliferation and neovasculogenesis, which provide essential nutrients and oxygen to tumour cells and help eliminate metabolic wastes77. Accumulating evidence identifies BMX as a critical regulator of tumour angiogenesis through its integration of growth factor and inflammatory signalling pathways. In endothelial cells, BMX functions downstream of VEGFR2 and Tie2, where it promotes angiogenic signalling by enhancing transcriptional activation of VEGFR2 via Sp1 and facilitating downstream pathway activation, including ERK phosphorylation78. Loss of BMX markedly impairs VEGF-induced ERK activation, leading to reduced endothelial cell proliferation, migration and microvessel sprouting79.

Beyond VEGF-driven signalling, BMX also mediates inflammation-associated angiogenesis. TNF-α induces the formation of a BMX–VEGFR2 complex via TNFR2, resulting in reciprocal activation of both proteins. Notably, this process depends on BMX autophosphorylation at Tyr566 rather than canonical VEGFR2 phosphorylation sites, highlighting a non-canonical activation mechanism80. Through these pathways, BMX coordinates endothelial cell survival, adhesion and migration, thereby promoting vascular remodelling within the tumour microenvironment81.

In vivo studies further substantiate the pro-angiogenic role of BMX. Genetic ablation of BMX leads to significantly impaired angiogenesis in both ischaemic and tumour models, accompanied by reduced vascular density, diminished perfusion and increased tumour necrosis82. In models such as B16-F10 melanoma, Rip1-Tag2 pancreatic islet tumours and ApcMin intestinal adenomas, BMX deficiency delays tumour progression79. Conversely, endothelial or epidermal overexpression of BMX enhances neovascularization and accelerates tumour growth in chemically induced carcinogenesis models79,83.

Collectively, these findings establish BMX as a central mediator of tumour angiogenesis by coupling VEGF- and TNF-dependent signalling to endothelial activation and vascular development. Targeting BMX may therefore represent a promising strategy to disrupt tumour vascularisation and limit cancer progression, although its clinical translation in anti-angiogenic therapy remains to be fully explored.

BMX in tumour drug resistance

Chemoresistance refers to a decrease in the sensitivity of tumour cells to chemotherapeutic agents, which may make the treatment less effective or even ineffective. This phenomenon is one of the major challenges in cancer treatment which has a significant impact on patient outcomes84. BMX has been found as a key therapeutic target that could help address drug resistance in a variety of cancers. In small cell lung cancer (SCLC), BMX drives chemoresistance through four interconnected mechanisms that collectively enhance cell survival and therapeutic tolerance. Firstly, BMX activates the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-XL through phosphorylation modification at the Tyr-40 site, which regulates the cell survival signalling pathway and participates in the formation of the chemoresistance phenotype of SCLC85. Meanwhile, BMX promotes the nuclear translocation of β-catenin from the cell membrane, activating the transcription of drug-resistance-related genes and further enhancing SCLC tolerance to chemotherapeutic agents86. In addition, BMX synergizes with PFKFB4, a key glycolytic enzyme, to augment drug resistance by modulating cellular autophagy processes87. Critically, recent evidence reveals that BMX additionally drives SCLC chemoresistance through the BMX-E2F1 axis, wherein BMX phosphorylates E2F1 at Ser332/337 via the ERK1/2-Cyclin D1/CDK4/6 cascade, preventing E2F1 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. This stabilisation enhances transcription of DNA damage repair genes (BRCA1, CHEK1) and cell cycle regulators, thereby promoting DNA repair capacity and proliferation despite chemotherapy exposure88. In prostate cancer, BMX-mediated resistance exhibits distinct molecular features characterised by hormone-dependent regulatory switches and unique kinase substrate specificity. BMX directly binds to the androgen receptor (AR) through its SH2 structural domain and blocks the interaction of AR with the E3 ubiquitin ligase Mdm2, thereby stabilising AR protein levels under androgen-deficient conditions and driving prostate cancer resistance to hormone therapy. Following androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), AR-mediated transcriptional repression of BMX is relieved, resulting in BMX upregulation that promotes castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) progression. Mechanistically distinct from SCLC, BMX employs a unique substrate specificity by phosphorylating the phosphotyrosine-tyrosine (pYY) motif in the activation loop of receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), generating double-phosphorylated motifs that fully activate RTK signalling and sustain proliferation under androgen-deprived conditions89. Additionally, the SH3 structural domain of BMX directly binds to the 44 kDa Pim-1 protein kinase via the N-terminus to form a signalling complex. This interaction enhances the catalytic activity of BMX kinase by competitively inhibiting the activation of p53, making prostate cancer cells resistant to apoptotic signalling induced by chemotherapeutic agents90. Distinct from SCLC and prostate cancer, BMX in AML primarily functions to integrate microenvironmental hypoxic cues with kinase pathway activation. In acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), BMX contributes to therapeutic resistance through hypoxia-adaptive mechanisms and kinase network reprogramming. Hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) activates compensatory survival signalling pathways by upregulating BMX expression, which in turn enhances sorafenib resistance in AML cells. Under hypoxic conditions, BMX also induces resistance to gilteritinib in FLT3-mutant AML by enhancing STAT5 phosphorylation and modulating the bone marrow microenvironment, facilitating tumour cell escape from targeted therapy91.

Analysing the differences in the drug resistance mechanisms of these cancers is crucial for treatment strategies. BMX inhibitors might optimally complement cytotoxic chemotherapy in SCLC, synergize with AR-targeting agents in prostate cancer, and require concurrent targeting of hypoxia-HIF-BMX-STAT5 axes in AML to overcome microenvironment-protected resistance. Conclusively, BMX enhances chemotherapeutic resistance across diverse cancer types through context-specific molecular mechanisms that reflect the unique signalling dependencies and microenvironmental pressures of each malignancy, positioning BMX as a versatile but mechanistically adaptable target for precision oncology interventions.

The prognostic value of BMX in tumours

Several studies have revealed the prognostic value of BMX protein in a variety of cancers. The expression level of BMX was higher in extensive-stage SCLC than in limited-stage SCLC, and the expression of BMX was closely correlated with the pathological stage of SCLC. High expression of BMX was found to be significantly correlated with the overall survival (OS) of SCLC patients by Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, in which patients with high expression of BMX had significantly shorter overall survival68. The study found that the BMX-E2F1 (E2F transcription factor 1) axis was a key regulator of chemotherapy resistance in SCLC. BMX stabilised E2F1 through the ERK1/2-cyclin D1/CDK4/6 signalling axis, thereby reducing chemoresistance88. BMX expression is strongly associated with clinical stage, pathologic grade and metastatic progression of renal cell carcinoma. Also, it is associated with significantly shorter overall survival33. In bladder cancer, BMX expression levels were significantly higher in invasive tumours than in non-invasive tumours, and BMX expression increased with tumour grade. Further multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that BMX was a significant predictor of survival and an independent prognostic factor for overall survival after controlling for other clinicopathological factors such as age, tumour grade, stage and lymph node positivity status53. In neuroblastoma, integrated chromatin and transcriptome analysis using patient-derived xenograft models and sphere models revealed that BMX expression was positively correlated with advanced tumour staging and poor patient survival outcomes, and was critical for the self-renewal and tumorigenic potential of tumour stem cells. Further studies demonstrated that BMX expression was positively associated with mesenchymal phenotype, which was closely linked to chemotherapy resistance. Targeting BMX can reverse this cellular state and enhance chemotherapy sensitivity, suggesting that BMX represents an innovative therapeutic target for high-risk MYCN non-amplified neuroblastoma54. Furthermore, BMX expression was positively correlated with the phenotype of mesenchymal neuroblastoma cells, while BMX inhibitors enhanced the chemosensitivity of neuroblastoma54. In colorectal cancer (CRC), BMX promotes disease progression by activating the STAT3 signalling pathway and enhancing phosphorylation at the Y705 residue. BMX inhibitors can effectively inhibit CRC growth92. Bioinformatics analysis confirmed that BMX can serve as a diagnostic biomarker in breast cancer, colon cancer, lung adenocarcinoma, lung squamous cell carcinoma and colorectal cancer, and as a prognostic biomarker in renal clear cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, sarcoma and endometrial carcinoma. Additionally, BMX expression is positively correlated with immune infiltration of B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils and dendritic cells. Moreover, BMX promoter methylation levels are significantly elevated in lung cancer, suggesting that epigenetic factors participate in regulating BMX expression93. Furthermore, BMX silencing enhances the activation of the pro-apoptotic effector protein BAK, rendering tumour cells highly sensitive to sublethal doses of clinically relevant chemotherapeutic agents and thereby increasing the efficacy of chemotherapy drugs22. In light of these findings, BMX overexpression may serves not only as a novel biomarker for prognostic prediction in cancer patients but also to enable stratification-based optimisation of patient management strategies. Immunohistochemical detection of BMX expression in primary tumour tissues is recommended, using the median H-score as the cut-off value to divide patients into high-expression and low-expression groups. High-expression patients should be classified as high-risk populations, receiving more aggressive treatment regimens (e.g. intensive chemotherapy combined with immunotherapy) and prioritised enrolment in clinical trials of BMX-targeted inhibitors with increased follow-up frequency. Low-expression patients should receive standard treatment and routine follow-up. Additionally, combined detection of BMX with markers such as PD-L1 and Ki-67 can establish a multi-index risk scoring model. Dynamic monitoring of plasma BMX levels facilitates early identification of treatment resistance and disease progression, thereby enabling clinical translation of precision medicine and personalised prognostic management.

Collectively, these findings suggest that overexpression of BMX protein may be a new biomarker of value for cancer diagnosis and prediction of prognosis in tumour patients (Figure 4).

Inhibitors of BMX

BMX plays a central role in cancer development and is expected to be an important target for cancer treatment (Figure 4). Therefore, the development of BMX inhibitors may provide new strategies for cancer treatment. Recently, researchers have invested great efforts in the research and development of BMX inhibitors. Table 2 summarises the chemicals as inhibitors of BMX, highlighting their chemical names, types and inhibitory activities. The 2D structures of these inhibitors were illustrated (Figure 5). As previously described, The kinase domain of BMX possesses a conserved ATP-binding pocket with a reactive cysteine residue (Cys496) at the entrance, a gatekeeper threonine (Thr489), and the canonical Lys445–Glu460 salt bridge, all of which constitute critical pharmacophores for inhibitor engagement. Experimental studies indicate that covalent inhibitors, such as BMX-IN-1, Ibrutinib, Zanubrutinib, Poseltinib, Ritlecitinib and Canertinib, form irreversible bonds with Cys496 via electrophilic warheads (typically acrylamide or Michael acceptors), resulting in sustained kinase inhibition and enhanced cellular potency. Non-covalent inhibitors, including CTA095, CTN06, CHMFL-BMX-078 and Compound B6 series, rely on hydrogen bonding to hinge residues and hydrophobic interactions within the ATP-binding pocket to achieve high selectivity and activity. Structural modifications at the hinge-binding moiety, solvent-exposed regions and adjacent hydrophobic pockets have been shown to modulate both potency and selectivity, highlighting the importance of steric and electronic complementarity in ligand design. Notably, dual-targeted scaffolds that occupy both the ATP site and adjacent allosteric pockets have demonstrated improved inhibition of BMX autophosphorylation and downstream signalling in cancer cells. Collectively, these SAR studies reveal that covalent engagement of Cys496, optimal hinge interactions and strategic hydrophobic contacts are the primary determinants of inhibitory efficacy, providing a mechanistic rationale for the development of next-generation BMX inhibitors with enhanced potency, selectivity and pharmacokinetic profiles4,9. However, docking studies have not yet been conducted for most of these inhibitors. Docking studies are a useful computational tool that simulates the interactions between small molecules and their target proteins, offering insights into binding mechanisms, interaction sites and potential inhibitory efficacy 64–66. Herein, we provide detailed insights into their binding mechanisms within the BMX active site. As shown in Table 3, the docking scores (kcal/mol) and contact residues of inhibitors with BMX. In general, a lower (more negative) docking score tends to correlate with a lower experimental IC50, reflecting higher inhibitory potency, although the correlation is approximate because docking calculations do not account for factors such as protein flexibility, solvation effects and cellular pharmacokinetics. By analysing the amino-acid residues predicted to interact directly with the small molecule, the underlying ligand–protein interaction mechanism can be elucidated in detail94. BMX-IN-1

BMX-IN-1 is a synthetic, highly potent, specific and irreversible inhibitor of BMX kinase with an IC50 of 8 nM. In order to achieve an effective inhibitory effect, BMX-IN-1 must modify the Cys496 residue of the ATP-binding site in the BMX kinase via covalent bonding. The inhibitor was evaluated using intracellular target-binding kinase assays, where the inhibitory effect of the compound on BMX kinase activity was monitored in prostate cancer cells and HEK293 cells via the NanoBRET™ TE method (Promega), with ATP concentration as the Km parameter9,95. The study of prostate cancer demonstrated that BMX-IN-1 not only inhibited the growth of multiple prostate cancer cell lines, but also induced apoptosis in these cells. Moreover, BMX-IN-1 inhibited the viability of CWR22RV1 cells and blocked colony formation of these cells under three-dimensional matrix gel culture conditions. When BMX-IN-1 was combined with the AKT kinase inhibitor MK2206, its inhibitory effect on prostate cancer cells and its pro-apoptotic activity were significantly enhanced. In a xenograft model, BMX-IN-1 significantly reduced the in vitro recovery of VCaP cells after androgen deprivation. Meanwhile, BMX-IN-1 treatment significantly enhanced the sensitivity of the VCaP xenograft model to depot treatment. In the xenograft model of prostate cancer, either of the compounds in BMX-IN-1, when combined with conventional androgen deprivation therapy, can significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy in animal models of prostate cancer. This approach is currently under research96. Furthermore, flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that BMX-IN-1 was able to inhibit the proliferative activity of cervical cancer cells30. In addition, BMX-IN-1 increased the sensitivity of neuroblastoma spheroids to chemotherapy and partially reduced tumour growth in a preclinical neuroblastoma model33. The study found that BMX inhibitors, when combined with chemotherapeutic agents, increase cell death and overcome apoptosis resistance in cancer97. As a novel BMX kinase inhibitor, its unique inhibitory properties make it valuable for research and application in the field of cancer therapy. Despite its strong biochemical potency through irreversible engagement of Cys496, there are currently no registered clinical trials evaluating BMX-IN-1, reflecting limitations in pharmacokinetic properties and selectivity. Nevertheless, BMX-IN-1 has played a critical role in elucidating BMX-dependent oncogenic signalling, particularly in PI3K/AKT and STAT pathways, thereby informing the rational design of next-generation inhibitors with improved drug-like characteristics.

Compound B6a

Compound B6a, a novel selective inhibitor of BMX kinase based on the 1,3,5-triazin-2-amine core structure, irreversibly and selectively inhibits BMX kinase with an IC50 value of merely 12 nM. The inhibitor was evaluated using Ramos cells and multiple cancer cell lines, with the determination of IC50 and in vivo antitumor activity through methods such as MTT assay, flow cytometry and Calliper migration rate assay. The Calliper Mobility Shift Assay confirmed B6a’s inhibitory activity against BMX kinase, accurately reflecting the compound’s impact on kinase activity. B6a demonstrated significant antiproliferative effects across various cancer cell lines, including those of breast, gastric, cervical, nasopharyngeal and colon cancers. This broad-spectrum activity implied potential therapeutic applications in diverse cancer types. Mechanistic studies revealed that B6a could upregulate LC3B expression, downregulate p62 expression and enhance the expression of apoptosis-related proteins. It also could trigger protective autophagy and inhibit the BMX/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway. These mechanisms synergistically contribute to B6a’s potent inhibition of tumour cell proliferation and invasion. In the HGC-27 xenograft mouse model, B6a administered at 30 mg/kg/day significantly suppressed tumour growth, achieving a 49.93% antitumor efficacy without evident toxicity or side effects, underscoring its high safety profile98. Although no clinical trials have yet been reported, B6a demonstrates promising in vitro and cellular activity, suggesting potential for further preclinical development. Its reversible binding mode may offer advantages in reducing off-target toxicity compared with covalent inhibitors. These findings underscore B6a’s promising development potential as an antitumor agent and offer a novel direction for cancer therapy.

Compound B6

Compound B6 is a reversible potent inhibitor of BMX, exhibiting an IC50 value of 35.7 ± 0.1 nM. The inhibitor was evaluated using A549 and H1975 cells, purified recombinant Bmx and methods such as MTT assay and standard kinase assays to determine the kinase inhibitory activity of the compound. The ATP concentration was set at 10 μM ATP. Its molecular design was derived from the structural framework of Ibrutinib, with optimisation achieved through the incorporation of 7H-pyrrolo[2,3-d]pyrimidin-4-amine as a novel backbone. This modification was primarily aimed at improving both the calculated logarithm of the partition coefficient (ClogP) and aqueous solubility. Although B6 did not show stronger BMX inhibition than Ibrutinib in in vitro experiments, the significant antitumor activity it exhibited in vivo may be related to its suitable CLogP value (2.56) and good water solubility. Furthermore, B6 displayed notable antiproliferative activity against non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell lines harbouring EGFR mutations. In the H1975 xenograft tumour mouse model, B6 demonstrated superior tumour growth inhibition compared to ibrutinib, with tumour inhibition rates of 73.9% and 59.6%, respectively. At the late stage of treatment (day 14), all mice showed slight weight loss due to environmental changes, but no adverse effects or behavioural abnormalities were observed99. While still at the preclinical stage, B6 supports the development of reversible inhibitors that may achieve better safety profiles and tuneable pharmacodynamics. Therefore, B6 is considered to be expected to be an effective anticancer drug against drug-resistant NSCLC.

Chmfl-BMX-078

CHMFL-BMX-078 represents a synthetic irreversible inhibitor of BMX kinase, demonstrating potent activity and remarkable selectivity with an IC50 value of 11 nM. The inhibitor was evaluated using BaF3-TEL-BMX cells and parental BaF3 cells, with half-inhibitory concentration (IC50), GI50 and EC50 determined through assays of enzymatic inhibition capacity, cell viability and total tyrosine phosphorylation inhibition of BMX100,101. This compound exerts its inhibitory effect through covalent modification of Cys496 residue in the inactive conformation of BMX kinase. Molecular docking analyses have elucidated a type II irreversible binding mechanism underlying the interaction between BMX and CHMFL-BMX-078. The compound has been shown to effectively suppress tyrosine phosphorylation of BMX64 in cellular contexts100. In melanoma research, CHMFL-BMX-078 exhibited significant antiproliferative activity against drug-resistant A375R melanoma cells, concomitant with induction of cell cycle arrest. Notably, the inhibitor displayed selective growth suppression in vemurafenib-resistant cell populations. Mechanistic investigations revealed that BMX inhibition could reverse vemurafenib resistance in melanoma, with CHMFL-BMX-078 specifically potentiating vemurafenib’s therapeutic effect through suppression of AKT signalling pathway. Importantly, in vivo studies demonstrated that CHMFL-BMX-078 not only effectively overcomed vemurafenib resistance but also maintained a favourable safety profile without observable toxicity enhancement. However, it exhibits low toxicity to immortal keratinocytes, melanoma cells and other solid tumour cells such as glioma and breast cancer cells101. Currently, CHMFL-BMX-078 is in the preclinical research stage and is mainly used in tumour therapy. Although clinical translation has not yet been achieved, its improved selectivity profile compared to earlier inhibitors highlights its potential as a candidate for further drug development, particularly in cancers where BMX-driven signalling is dominant.

CTA095

CTA095 is a synthetic small-molecule inhibitor exhibiting dual activity against BMX and SRC kinases. Biochemical characterisation demonstrated potent BMX inhibition with an IC50 value of approximately 60 nM, while computational analyses through molecular docking and kinetic simulations suggested its mechanism of action involves structural perturbation of the BMX back pocket, thereby interfering with ATP binding. The inhibitor was evaluated using PC3, LNCaP, CWR22Rv1, RWPE1, purified recombinant Etk, Src, Btk, etc., through methods such as MTT assay and TLC kinase assay to determine cell anti-proliferation effects and kinase inhibition (IC50). In vitro validation confirmed concentration-dependent suppression of BMX autophosphorylation. Beyond its kinase-inhibitory properties, CTA095 displayed marked antitumor efficacy in prostate cancer models, manifesting both antiproliferative effects and induction of autophagic and apoptotic pathways. Notably, the compound effectively overcame SRC inhibitor resistance in prostate cancer cells, potentially through downregulation of Myc and Bcl-2 expression. thereby restoring apoptotic sensitivity. Additional pharmacological evaluation revealed antiangiogenic properties, as demonstrated by inhibition of both tubulogenesis and migration in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Combination therapy with either chloroquine (CQ) or paclitaxel (PTX) produced synergistic enhancement of antiproliferative activity against PC3 human prostate cancer cells. These therapeutic effects were further corroborated in vivo, with nude mouse xenograft models exhibiting significant tumour growth inhibition following CTA095 treatment102. As a potent dual inhibitor of BMX and SRC, CTA095 holds significant research and application value in cancer therapy. However, clinical evaluation remains limited, and further optimisation is required to enhance its pharmacokinetic and efficacy profiles before clinical advancement.

CTN06

CTN06, a dual inhibitor of BMX and Btk, has been shown to have significant inhibitory potency against BMX, with an IC50 value of approximately 200 nM. Using PC3, LNCaP, DU145 and RWPE1 cells along with purified recombinant Btk, Etk and Src proteins, cell anti-proliferation and kinase inhibition (IC50) were evaluated through TLC kinase assay and MTT cell viability assay. The ATP concentration was 5 μCi³³P-ATP. Molecular docking analyses revealed that CTN06 specifically engages with the ATP-binding pocket of BMX kinase. In PC3 prostate cancer cells, CTN06 effectively attenuated phosphorylation levels of both Btk and BMX, thereby suppressing the activity of downstream signalling molecules including PLCγ2, STAT3 and AKT. The dual inhibition mechanism of CTN06 produced a synergistic antiproliferative effect on prostate cancer cells. Notably, CTN06 exhibited potent cytotoxic activity against PC3 and other prostate cancer cell lines while effectively inhibiting Btk/BMX signalling pathways. Comparative analyses demonstrated that CTN06 has possessed superior cytotoxic efficacy relative to conventional inhibitors targeting SRC, Raf/VEGFR and EGFR. Furthermore, CTN06 induced both apoptotic and autophagic pathways in prostate cancer cells and enhanced cellular sensitivity to docetaxel (DTX). In vivo evaluation using a PC3 xenograft mouse model confirmed the compound’s antitumor efficacy through significant inhibition of tumour growth49. Overall, CTN06 showed significant antitumor activity in both in vitro and in vivo experiments, and its dual inhibition of Btk and BMX provides a new strategy for the treatment of prostate cancer and other tumours types. Similar to CTA095, CTN06 remains in the preclinical stage, and its development underscores ongoing efforts to identify non-covalent inhibitors with improved selectivity and reduced toxicity compared to covalent agents.

Ibrutinib

Ibrutinib, as a selective small-molecule inhibitor, demonstrates significant inhibitory effects on BMX kinase, with an IC50 value of 0.76 nM. Using CLL and B-ALL cell lines, purified recombinant kinases, and employing TLC methods and radioactive kinase assays to detect kinase inhibition and cell anti-proliferation effects, with ATP concentration as the Km parameter. The drug achieves sustained inhibition of BMX kinase activity through irreversible covalent binding to key cysteine residues within the kinase’s structural domain103,104. Ibrutinib not only inhibits the growth of prostate cancer cells but also induces apoptosis. Additionally, in xenograft models, ibrutinib reduces the in vitro recovery capacity of prostate cancer VCaP cells following androgen deprivation and suppresses tumour growth in the NSCLC H1975 cell line87,98. One study found that in prostate cancer, adding ibrutinib to androgen deprivation therapy to inhibit BMX significantly enhanced the efficacy of androgen deprivation therapy. A combined therapy targeting androgen receptors and BMX may be effective in some patients96.

In gliomas, Ibrutinib promotes cellular autophagy by targeting the AKT/mTOR signalling pathway105. By inhibiting BMX, Ibrutinib significantly impaired the activity of glioma stem cells, thereby inhibiting glioblastoma (GBM) progression. When combined with radiation therapy, Ibrutinib effectively inhibited GBM tumour growth and suppressed GSCs in vitro. Compared to temozolomide (TMZ), Ibrutinib demonstrated better results in inhibiting GSCs-derived tumour growth and prolonging survival in animal models. Preclinical data indicate that repurposing ibrutinib for targeted therapy of glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs), whether as monotherapy or adjunctive to conventional treatment, can effectively control the growth of glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) tumors22,106.Meanwhile, in mice with glioblastoma, primary central nervous system lymphoma, and lung cancer brain metastases, the small-molecule activator EG-011 pharmacologically activates Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome proteins, enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of ibrutinib107. Protophore array analysis of serum samples from B-ALL patients revealed that BMX plays a critical role in the BTK pathway, which is inhibited by ibrutinib. This finding may lead to the future designation of ibrutinib as an adjuvant therapy for young adult B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia108. In addition, Ibrutinib is also a preferred agent for combination therapy with PI3K/AKT/mTOR inhibitors or immune checkpoint inhibitors, as it inhibits immunosuppressive signalling in the tumour microenvironment and reverses resistance to existing anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies by modulating the CXCR4/CXCL12 pathway109. Furthermore, Ibrutinib can increase brain endothelial permeability through tight junction disruption and efflux inhibition, thereby increasing blood-tumour barrier drug entry78. Ibrutinib has been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of haematologic malignancies. Multiple clinical trials have evaluated ibrutinib in acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), including combination regimens aimed at overcoming drug resistance. Furthermore, over 20 clinical trials have explored ibrutinib in solid tumours, such as breast, prostate and lung cancers, driven by its dual tumour-intrinsic and immunomodulatory effects110. Preclinical studies demonstrate that ibrutinib inhibits BMX–STAT3 signalling in glioblastoma stem cells, suppressing tumour growth and radioresistance, supporting its repurposing potential in solid tumours where BMX is highly expressed. However, clinical efficacy in solid tumours remains modest, indicating the need for combination strategies and better patient stratification22. Additionally, due to its lack of specificity and non-targeted binding, it may lead to some side effects such as atrial fibrillation, hypertension and increased risk of bleeding.

Zanubrutinib

Zanubrutinib is an oral, highly selective second-generation Btk inhibitor that demonstrates potent inhibitory activity against BMX through covalent binding to cysteine residues within the BMX protein, exhibiting a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 0.62 nM. Using Ramos, Rec-1, HEK293, A431 (EGFR), Jurkat (ITK), and purified recombinant BTK, kinase selectivity, BTK biochemistry and cell viability were detected through methods such as TR-FRET assay, HTRF cell assay (pBTK, pEGFR), CellTiter-Glo cell viability assay and ³³P-ATP membrane binding assay, with ATP concentration set at 1 μM 111. Pharmacokinetic studies in rat models revealed favourable oral absorption characteristics and substantial bioavailability of Zanubrutinib. In OCI-LY10 xenograft models, Zanubrutinib exhibited superior antitumor efficacy compared to ibrutinib, demonstrating tumour growth inhibition (TGI) rates of 76% and 88% at doses of 2.5 mg/kg and 7.5 mg/kg, respectively112. As Zanubrutinib has higher target specificity and its selective inhibition of Btk is stronger113, it produces fewer side effects during the treatment process and at the same time can exert better anti-tumour effects. In addition, Zanubrutinib is approved for several B-cell malignancies and is under investigation in additional indications, including early-phase trials in AML114. Its improved pharmacokinetic profile and reduced toxicity may enable more effective targeting of BMX-driven signalling in solid tumours, although clinical evidence in this context is still emerging.

Poseltinib

Poseltinib is a Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (Btk) inhibitor that exhibits potent inhibitory activity against BMX kinase, demonstrating a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 0.64 nM. The compound was evaluated for kinase inhibition and BTK occupancy using Ramos, PBMC and purified recombinant BTK, employing Z′-LYTE FRET assays, biotinylated probe competition assays and AlphaScreen methods, with ATP concentration as the Km parameter. The compound displays high binding affinity for the ATP-binding site of BMX, and fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assays have confirmed its capacity to suppress BMX kinase activity at a concentration of 10 nM. In the field of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) research, Poseltinib has been explored for the treatment of this disease115. In a rat collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model, Poseltinib administration resulted in dose-dependent anti-inflammatory effects, including suppression of cytokine production, attenuation of arthritis severity, and improvement in histopathological scores116. Furthermore, in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrated that Poseltinib dose-dependently inhibited B-cell and osteoclast function85. Additionally, the compound exhibited therapeutic efficacy in a murine lupus model, suggesting broader immunomodulatory potential. While clinical trials have focused on inflammatory indications, its kinase selectivity profile suggests potential for oncology applications, particularly in cancers with aberrant Tec kinase signalling. However, clinical data in cancer remain limited.

Ritlecitinib

Ritlecitinib (PF-06651600) is an orally administered small molecule inhibitor that irreversibly and covalently binds to a conserved cysteine residue (Cys-909) in the catalytic domain of JAK3. Since BMX kinase contains similar cysteine residues, Ritlecitinib inhibits BMX and is highly selective for BMX inhibition with an IC50 value of 666 nM 117. The inhibitor utilises purified recombinant kinase and human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), with biochemical kinase inhibition detected through standard kinase assays and flow cytometry. The ATP concentration was 1 mM ATP. This selective inhibition reduces the risk of potential side effects by decreasing non-specific effects on other kinases118. Preclinical studies have shown that the inhibitory effect of Ritlecitinib on CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells is primarily driven by TEC kinase inhibition. Ritlecitinib is primarily indicated for the treatment of severe alopecia areata (alopecia areata), demonstrating clinically significant and sustained efficacy with favourable safety and tolerability profiles119. Given the underlying pathophysiologic mechanisms of inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, pemphigus vulgaris and vitiligo, dual inhibition of the JAK3 and TEC kinase families by Ritlecitinib may provide a significant benefit in the treatment of these diseases. Ritlecitinib has advanced into clinical trials for autoimmune diseases and demonstrates the feasibility of targeting Tec kinases with improved selectivity. Although not yet clinically evaluated for BMX-driven cancers, its pharmacological profile suggests potential utility in modulating BMX-related signalling pathways, particularly in immune-related tumour microenvironments.

Canertinib

Canertinib (alias CI-1033) is a pan-ErbB tyrosine kinase inhibitor that covalently binds to Cys496 in the kinase domain of BMX kinase and significantly inhibits its activity with an IC50 of 0.062 μM. Canertinib was found to reverse triamcinolone acetonide resistance and enhance sensitivity to paclitaxel in breast cancer MCF-7/TamR cells120. The inhibitor was evaluated using MCF-7 cells (parental and tamoxifen-resistant strains) and T47D cells (parental and tamoxifen-resistant strains) through methods such as MTT assay for cell viability, flow cytometry and Western blot to assess cell proliferation inhibition, apoptosis. In cervical cancer, Canertinib was found to induce autophagy and apoptosis and accelerate cell death by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK signalling pathways81. Notably, the Pd(II) complex and 5-FU had a significant inhibitory effect on the growth of HeLa cells when used alone, while the cytotoxicity was significantly increased when combined with Canertinib. In addition, Canertinib effectively inhibited the proliferation of glioma Tu-2449 cells and triggered cell death by inhibiting the phosphorylation process of BMX kinase and STAT3121. However, in animal studies, Canertinib exhibited ototoxicity in three preclinical models122. Its clinical development has been limited by toxicity concerns, underscoring the need for more selective BMX inhibitors. While primarily developed for EGFR-driven cancers, its ability to covalently target BMX highlights opportunities for polypharmacology-based approaches.

Conclusions and future perspectives

This paper reviews the function and mechanism of action of BMX in various aspects of tumour biology, including proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, metastasis, stemness, angiogenesis and drug resistance. Studies have shown that BMX expression levels are significantly elevated in a variety of malignant tumours, and BMX plays an important role in tumour development by promoting the phosphorylation of target proteins. Multiple connections and interactions have been identified between phosphorylation and other protein modifications, which are extremely critical in the regulation of cellular functions and physiological processes. Further studies are still needed to elucidate the relationship between BMX-mediated dephosphorylation and protein transport or distribution. In the field of angiogenesis, several studies have demonstrated the importance of BMX in vascular growth and neointima formation. Nevertheless, the direct role of BMX in tumour angiogenesis has not been fully clarified, which may represent an important direction for future research on tumour angiogenesis therapy. Studies have shown that BMX can serve as a novel tumour prognostic marker and a potential therapeutic target. Therefore, in-depth investigation of the mechanism of BMX action in tumours is important for the development of BMX-targeted antitumor drugs. To advance the drug to the IND stage (Investigational New Drug Application), a predictive AI platform was employed to design selective inhibitors, considering their novelty, protective properties, and drug similarity. Based on experimental findings, selective BMX inhibitors were developed as therapeutic agents for this type of cancer123. Currently, although a variety of BMX inhibitors have been discovered and synthesised, most of them are still in the laboratory stage, have not been advanced to clinical trials, and have been derived mainly through synthesis. As anticancer drugs, these chemically synthesised inhibitors have many advantages, such as high purity, stable chemical structure, no constraints from natural resources, and clear mechanism of action. However, BMX inhibitors also face many difficulties in the process of application, such as high research and development costs, toxicity and large side effects, which limit their potential to become clinically useful drugs. Therefore, how to reduce the toxicity and side effects of drugs through rational structural optimisation has become a key issue to be solved. In addition, the potency of BMX inhibitors is usually assessed by molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, or in vitro assays, but these assessment methods lack validation in clinical disease models, and thus are still quite far from practical application. Overall, direct targeting of BMX is expected to be an effective anticancer strategy. In-depth study of the mechanism of action of its inhibitors and optimisation of drug efficacy will provide more effective options for tumour therapy. The development of effective inhibitors against BMX and their clinical application is undoubtedly an important direction for future tumour therapy.
