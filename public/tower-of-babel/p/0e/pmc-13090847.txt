Coadministered Influenza- and Pertussis-Containing Vaccines in Pregnant Women
Authors: Nicole Sonneveld, Joanne Reekie, Lucy Deng, Kerry-Ann O’Grady, Kristine Macartney, Bette Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study assesses the associations between pregnancy, birth, and neonatal outcomes and concomitant influenza and pertussis vaccination compared with pertussis-only vaccination during pregnancy.

Introduction

Vaccination during pregnancy effectively reduces morbidity and mortality in pregnant women and their infants.1,2,3,4,5,6,7 An increasing number of vaccines are recommended during pregnancy, including vaccines against influenza,8,9,10,11,12 pertussis,12,13,14 tetanus,12,15 COVID-19,11,12,16 and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV),17,18 with new vaccines (eg, against group B streptococcus) in development.7,19

With a limited optimal window for vaccination during pregnancy, concomitant (ie, same-day) administration of multiple vaccines is often permitted or recommended to maximize uptake.9,11,20 However, few studies have focused on the safety of concomitant vaccination during pregnancy. Studies of short-term adverse events suggest concomitant administration of influenza and tetanus-diphtheria-acellular pertussis (Tdap) vaccines during pregnancy is associated with modestly higher rates of local reactogenicity and headache compared with vaccination alone or sequentially.21,22,23 Available evidence does not suggest a higher rate of fever or medically attended adverse events in pregnant women within days after vaccination.21,22,23,24 Data on pregnancy, birth, and neonatal outcomes following concomitant vaccination are limited,23,24 despite safety concerns for the pregnancy and unborn child being a known barrier to vaccination during pregnancy.25,26,27

While the limited available evidence is reassuring, to aid health care practitioners and patients in evidence-based decision-making, we aimed to assess whether rates of pregnancy, birth, and neonatal outcomes in women receiving concomitant influenza- and pertussis-containing vaccines (delivered under the Australian National Immunisation Program as diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis vaccine [hereafter pertussis vaccine]) were significantly different from those in a matched control population who received the pertussis vaccine alone. The outcomes selected were based on those identified by the World Health Organization and Brighton Collaboration as most important for the assessment of the safety of vaccines in pregnancy.28

Methods

This research was approved by the New South Wales (NSW) Population and Health Service Research Ethics Committee, and a waiver of informed consent was granted because of the deidentified nature of the data and the size of the study population. We report this study according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.29

Study Design, Data Sources, and Population

This retrospective, population-based, matched cohort study was conducted in Australia’s most populous state, NSW (population of 8.15 million in June 202230; annual birth cohort of approximately 90 00031), using 4 data sources linked by the NSW Centre for Health Record Linkage independent of the study investigators.32

The NSW Perinatal Data Collection (PDC) is a surveillance system collating information on pregnancies in NSW resulting in birth at 20 weeks’ gestational age or later or in birth weight of 400 g or more. It captures demographic data and information regarding the mother’s health, pregnancy, labor, birth and perinatal outcomes, and influenza and/or pertussis vaccination during pregnancy. The Australian Immunisation Register (AIR) records vaccines provided to people in Australia. Reporting has been mandatory for all instances of influenza vaccination since March 1, 2021, and for pertussis vaccination during pregnancy since July 1, 2021.33 The NSW Admitted Patient Data Collection (APDC) records all episodes of care in NSW hospitals and includes demographic data, diagnoses (maximum of 50) coded using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Health Related Problems, Tenth Revision, Australian Modification,34 and admission and discharge dates. The NSW Registry of Births, Deaths and Marriages registers all deaths in NSW. Data from all datasets were available from at least January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2022.

The study population included all singleton pregnancies ending at 20 weeks’ gestation or later in women aged 12 to 54 years recorded in the PDC (Figure 1). We included pregnancies with an estimated last menstrual period (delivery date minus completed gestation at delivery) between January 1, 2021, and March 12, 2022 (ie, 42 weeks before December 31, 2022), allowing sufficient follow-up to capture all outcomes. If more than 1 pregnancy occurred in this period for any person, only the most recent pregnancy was included (Figure 1). Pregnancies were excluded if more than 1 influenza and/or pertussis vaccine was administered during the pregnancy or if there was evidence of incorrect linkage (ie, more than 1 person was linked to a unique person identifier).

Exposure

Two data sources contained information regarding influenza and pertussis vaccination during pregnancy.35 The PDC records vaccination status as yes, no, or unknown, sometimes based on self-report, but not vaccination date. The AIR records are entered by the health care practitioner and include vaccination date. Data available in both datasets were combined to categorize exposure status (Figure 1; eTable 1 in Supplement 1). To be eligible for analysis, vaccination status as determined using both data sources had to align.

In Australia, pertussis vaccination is recommended between 20 and 32 weeks’ gestation (commonly given at 28 weeks), whereas influenza vaccination is recommended and received at any time during pregnancy.11,35 Pertussis vaccine coverage during pregnancy is high (approximately 80%), but influenza vaccine coverage is substantially lower (approximately 50%).35,36,37 In NSW, concomitant vaccination occurs in approximately 9% of pregnancies35; reasons for offering and/or receiving concomitant vaccination are currently not well-known.

Our exposure of interest was concomitant administration of influenza and pertussis vaccines at 20 weeks’ gestation or later. The control group included pregnancies during which the pertussis vaccine was administered alone, and the influenza vaccine had not yet been received, ensuring similar vaccine eligibility at the recommended gestation across both groups.

Outcomes

Outcomes were selected using the World Health Organization and Brighton Collaboration prioritized outcomes for the assessment of vaccine safety in pregnancy.28 Only outcomes for which hospitalization is generally indicated or those occurring at birth were examined; this ensured a date of onset was available for all events. Primary outcomes were preterm birth, stillbirth, small for gestational age, and low birth weight (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

Incident events were identified using (1) the PDC if the outcome was captured therein and occurred on the day of birth, with the infant’s birth date as the event date, or (2) the hospitalization data (APDC) using International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Health Related Problems, Tenth Revision, Australian Modification coding (any diagnostic position) to identify events, with the admission date as the event date (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

Statistical Analyses

Using replacement, we matched exposed individuals to controls on vaccination date (±14 days), gestation at vaccination (±1 week), and maternal age (±3 years). Controls could be selected as matches up to 10 times. Absolute standardized mean differences in confounders between the groups were determined. Due to incomplete APDC hospitalization data from 1 public hospital (entire study period) and all private hospitals (July 1 to December 31, 2022)—in which approximately 25% of NSW births occur38—for the secondary outcomes ascertained using APDC data, we excluded pregnancies in which births in the PDC were recorded as occurring at these hospitals (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). We matched the remaining pregnancies using the same approach as described for vaccination date, gestation at vaccination, and maternal age.

Separate analyses were conducted for each outcome. Kaplan-Meier curves were estimated for all pregnancy and birth outcomes. Cox proportional hazards models with robust variance estimation to account for clustering at the individual level (multiplicity induced by matching with replacement) were used to compare rates of pregnancy and birth outcomes. Time origin was the day of vaccination, and the underlying time scale was days. Matched pairs with the outcome of interest in either individual occurring before pertussis vaccination were excluded from that analysis. Censoring occurred on delivery or maternal death. For preterm labor and preterm birth, follow-up was additionally censored at 37 weeks’ gestation, and matched pairs in which either individual was vaccinated at 37 weeks’ gestation or later were excluded.

For the neonatal outcomes, we included only pairs with term live births to examine associations independent of preterm birth.39 Neonatal outcomes were evaluated using logistic regression models; clustered sandwich estimators40 were used to calculate SEs.

Adjustment was made for the matching factors and a priori determined confounders: Index of Relative Socio-economic Disadvantage of the area of residence,41 smoking during pregnancy, COVID-19 vaccination during pregnancy, and prepregnancy history of the outcome being assessed. COVID-19 vaccination uptake during pregnancy was expected to be relatively high given that pregnant women in Australia were advised to receive a primary course and booster dose within the study period.42 To assess for any differential effect by COVID-19 vaccination status,43,44 for the primary outcomes, we tested for an interaction between COVID-19 vaccine receipt during pregnancy and exposure status. We elected not to examine COVID-19 concomitant vaccination with influenza and/or pertussis vaccination due to the time-limited nature of the program (COVID-19 vaccination is no longer routinely recommended during pregnancy in Australia11) and the rarity of COVID-19 concomitant vaccination in our data. Additional fully adjusted models are provided, in which adjustment was made for the previously mentioned covariates and any other potential confounder that was unbalanced between the groups, defined as standardized mean difference greater than 0.10. All potential confounders, their data sources, and classifications are listed in eTable 3 in Supplement 1.

We performed 2 sensitivity analyses for the primary outcomes. First, we used a cohort matched without replacement to ensure that controls matched several times did not disproportionately affect the estimates. Second, to investigate the robustness of the results, we repeated the main analyses, censoring pairs at the times of controls’ influenza vaccination, if this occurred (for the time-to-event model), or completely excluding these pairs from the analysis (for the logistic regression models), thus including only (person-time of) controls who only received the pertussis vaccine.

Analyses were performed using Stata version 18 (StataCorp LLC) and R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing),45 including the packages survival46 and sandwich.40 Data were analyzed between April and August 2025. P values were 2 sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of a total of 104 896 pregnancies during the study period, 13 918 singleton pregnancies (6959 with concomitant vaccination matched to 6959 with pertussis vaccination alone [control group]) in women with a mean (SD) maternal age at delivery of 31.7 (4.9) years were analyzed. In the group of 104 896 pregnancies, mean (SD) maternal age at delivery was 31.4 (5.2) years, 22.2% of women resided in the most and 20.8% in the least disadvantaged areas, 78.1% resided in an urban area, 5.3% had a preexisting high-risk medical condition, and 43.6% were nulliparous (Figure 1; eTable 4 in Supplement 1). Based on vaccine receipt as reported in the PDC alone, influenza vaccine coverage was 50.5%, and pertussis vaccine coverage was 78.0%. eTable 3 in Supplement 1 lists characteristics of the study population according to influenza and pertussis vaccination status, combining information from the PDC and AIR. Selecting only records with consistent vaccination status in the PDC and AIR, there were 7022 pregnancies in which the pertussis vaccine was concomitantly administered with the influenza vaccine at 20 weeks’ gestation or later (concomitant vaccination group) and 14 276 pregnancies with pertussis vaccination alone and no prior influenza vaccination (control group) (Figure 1).

Before matching, pertussis vaccination date differed between the groups (Figure 2A), and vaccination occurred on average 1 gestational week earlier in the concomitant vaccination group (mean [SD], 27.7 [3.9] weeks) compared with the control group (mean [SD], 28.6 [3.7] weeks) (Table 1). The concomitant vaccination group was less likely to live in a disadvantaged area, less likely to have received the COVID-19 vaccine during pregnancy by the concomitant or pertussis-only vaccination date, and more likely to be primiparous (Table 1). Concomitant COVID-19 vaccination at the time of concomitant influenza and pertussis vaccination or pertussis-only vaccination was rare, at a rate of 0.2% or lower in each group (21 across both groups). A total of 6959 (99%) pregnancies with concomitant vaccination were matched to controls, with most of the selected controls matched once (median [IQR], 1 [1-2] matches). Good balance was achieved on the matching factors and most of the potential confounders (Table 1; Figure 2B). For our secondary outcomes ascertained using APDC data, 3964 pregnancies with concomitant vaccination were matched to 3964 controls, with good balance achieved for most potential confounders (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). No maternal deaths occurred during pregnancy.

Primary Outcomes

In the matched cohort, there were 264 (3.8%) preterm births in the concomitant vaccination group and 302 (4.4%) among the controls. No significant difference in the rate of preterm birth was observed between the concomitant vaccination and control groups (adjusted hazard ratio, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.66-1.05; P = .12) (Table 2; eFigure in Supplement 1). Due to the small number of stillbirths (17 [0.1%] across both groups), additional analyses were not performed. Among 6391 term live birth pairs, 565 (8.8%) infants in the concomitant vaccination and 633 (9.9%) in the control group were small for gestational age, and 107 (1.7%) infants in the concomitant vaccination and 105 (1.6%) in the control group had low birth weight (Table 3). There was no significant difference in the odds of small for gestational age (adjusted odds ratio, 0.87; 95% CI, 0.74-1.04; P = .13) or low birth weight (adjusted odds ratio, 0.94; 95% CI, 0.66-1.35; P = .74) between the groups (Table 3). We did not find evidence of a differential effect by COVID-19 vaccination status during pregnancy.

Secondary Outcomes

In the 3964 concomitant vaccination and 3964 matched control pregnancies, antepartum hemorrhages occurred in 2.5% (97 of 3891) and 3.3% (129 of 3891), preeclampsia or eclampsia occurred in 2.9% (113 of 3963) and 2.2% (89 of 3963), prelabor rupture of membranes occurred in 7.1% (281 of 3958) and 6.7% (267 of 3958), preterm labor occurred in 2.1% (82 of 3913) and 2.8% (109 of 3913), and postpartum hemorrhages occurred in 16.4% (649 of 3964) and 15.0% (595 of 3964) (Table 2; eFigure in Supplement 1). There were 91 (1.1%) cases of chorioamnionitis in total (42 [1.1%] in the concomitant vaccination group and 49 [1.2%] in the control group), precluding further analysis. We observed 78 of 6391 (1.2%) low infant Apgar scores in each group (Table 3). We did not observe any significant differences in the rates or odds of any of the secondary outcomes between the concomitant vaccination and control groups (Tables 2 and 3).

Sensitivity Analyses

Results from sensitivity analyses were broadly consistent with the main analyses. Performing matching without replacement, there were 3758 pairs, and good balance was achieved for most potential confounders (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). The proportion of women experiencing each of the outcomes among the concomitant vaccination and control groups in this cohort was similar to that observed in the main matched cohort. Concomitant vaccination was associated with a significantly lower rate of preterm birth compared with pertussis vaccination alone (adjusted hazard ratio, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.60-0.96; P = .02) (eTable 7 in Supplement 1).

A total of 2093 (30.1%) controls were vaccinated against influenza a median (IQR) 20 (10-34) days after their pertussis vaccination. In a second sensitivity analysis, we censored follow-up for matched pairs at the controls’ influenza vaccination dates in the time-to-event analysis or excluded matched pairs from the logistic regression analyses if controls had received the influenza vaccine. Results were consistent with the main analyses (eTable 8 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this population-based cohort study including almost 14 000 pregnancies, we did not observe associations between concomitant influenza and pertussis vaccination and increases in the rates of 11 serious adverse pregnancy, birth, and neonatal outcomes compared with pertussis vaccination alone. We found few stillbirths and cases of chorioamnionitis, precluding further analysis.

Only 2 prior studies have assessed pregnancy, birth, and neonatal outcomes following concomitant vaccination in pregnancy. In a pilot study of 81 women in the US randomized to receive inactivated influenza and Tdap vaccines sequentially or concomitantly, there was no difference in proportions of pregnancies with preterm birth, small for gestational age, low birth weight, preeclampsia, or chorioamnionitis.23 Another US study including almost 9000 pregnant women found no difference in the rate of preterm birth, low birth weight, or small for gestational age between concomitant (4554 [51%]) or sequential administration of inactivated influenza and Tdap vaccines.24 Despite differences in study population, design, and methodology, our findings are broadly consistent with these earlier investigations. To our knowledge, this was the first study in which the outcomes of prelabor rupture of membranes, preterm labor, stillbirth, antepartum or postpartum hemorrhage, and low Apgar score have been assessed in this context.

Postmarket surveillance of influenza and pertussis vaccines administered during pregnancy both individually and together has not suggested any safety signals.22,47,48 Theoretical concerns regarding concomitant administration would include the potential for increased systemic reactogenicity generated in response to vaccination, including fever, leading to adverse outcomes. However, the findings from this study and others suggest this is not the case. We observed a potential decrease in the risk of some of the adverse outcomes among pregnancies with concomitant pertussis and influenza vaccination compared with pregnancies with pertussis vaccination alone. This may be due to residual confounding, for example, by health care–seeking behavior, for which we were unable to fully control, or could potentially be explained by the benefit of (earlier) influenza vaccination in pregnancy. Influenza disease in pregnancy is associated with adverse pregnancy and neonatal outcomes,49,50,51 and influenza vaccination has been shown in some studies to be associated with a reduced risk of preterm birth.47,49,52

Strengths and Limitations

Study strengths include the use of a population-based perinatal dataset, enabling a larger population sample compared with samples in earlier studies.23,24 Small undetectable but clinically meaningful differences between groups may still exist, highlighting the need for further assessment of concomitant vaccination in pregnancy in larger cohorts.

Use of linked data from registries ensures the consistency of the data collected and independence of outcome and exposure ascertainment. We were able to match our comparator group on key variables that could influence the likelihood of vaccine receipt, including maternal age, gestation, and calendar time, and use appropriate analytical methods.53

More than half of the recorded pregnancies had vaccination status for influenza and/or pertussis that was inconsistent between the PDC and the AIR. This is likely due to incomplete reporting to the AIR, with mandatory reporting only commencing in 2021. This limited our sample in size and generalizability. Agreement between the 2 data sources is known to be lower in pregnancies with public compared with private antenatal care practitioners.35 Finally, we did not have hospitalization data for 1 public hospital for the entire study period or for private hospitals during the period of July 1 to December 31, 2022, limiting the size and generalizability of the analyzed population for the secondary outcomes ascertained using APDC data.

We chose to compare pregnancies in women with concomitant pertussis and influenza vaccination with those with pertussis-only vaccination at the same gestation because in Australia, pertussis vaccination rates are relatively high, while influenza uptake is lower by more than 20 percentage points.35,36 Demonstrating the safety of concomitant administration with the influenza vaccine may improve its uptake. This also meant our comparator population was more similar on sociodemographics and prior obstetric history (ie, baseline risk of adverse outcomes) than if we had compared the concomitant vaccination group with the unvaccinated population (10.9% of our cohort), who were substantially younger, had more disadvantage, had more preexisting medical conditions, and had higher crude rates of most of the outcomes of interest.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort study of 13 918 pregnancies, we did not find an association between concomitant administration of influenza and pertussis vaccines during pregnancy and increased risk of adverse pregnancy, birth, or neonatal outcomes compared with pertussis vaccination alone. The evidence presented may help clinicians more confidently recommend pregnancy vaccination concomitantly and potentially provide more opportunities to optimize uptake. Our study is particularly relevant considering the recent availability of the maternal RSV vaccine, which in many countries is the third (or fourth) vaccine recommended at any time in pregnancy and the second vaccine specifically targeted to the latter half of pregnancy. Future research to assess a wider range of adverse outcomes, including rare events, those not requiring hospitalization, and less specific outcomes (eg, neonatal intensive care unit admission), and to evaluate the safety of different combinations of concomitantly administered vaccines, including RSV, is warranted.
