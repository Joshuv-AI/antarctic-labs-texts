Complex Management of Adults With Congenital Heart Disease During Pregnancy
Authors: Matthew C. Schwartz, Carrie Herbert, Jorge Alegria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

We applaud Hirschbaum and Aboulhosn1 for their excellent and informative paper in this issue of JACC: Case Reports. The authors describe the successful management of a woman with complex congenital heart disease during pregnancy that included a transcatheter intervention.

The interaction between congenital heart disease and pregnancy can be complex, especially when patients have significant residual hemodynamic abnormalities. Hirschbaum and Aboulhosn describe a pregnant woman with repaired tetralogy of Fallot who had moderate pulmonary valve regurgitation (PR) as well as right ventricular (RV) hypertension due to branch pulmonary artery and bioprosthetic pulmonary valve (BPV) stenosis. The patient was symptomatic with NYHA functional class III symptoms. The complex right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) physiology and the presence of heart failure symptoms put the patient at significant risk during pregnancy based on established scoring systems as well as prior published series.2, 3, 4 The patient had preserved RV systolic function, which helped her prognosis. The presence of significant heart failure symptoms in this patient at 12 weeks’ gestation is especially important. In the CARPREG II study of nearly 2,000 pregnancies in women with congenital heart disease, the presence of NYHA functional class III/IV symptoms was one of the strongest predictors of maternal cardiovascular events.2 The cardiac volume load during pregnancy combined with an already volume- and pressure-loaded RV caused the patient's significant exertional symptoms.

Given the patient's risk of a cardiac event, a multidisciplinary discussion was held, and the treating team chose to intervene percutaneously to improve RVOT function. This case highlights the importance of collaboration between adult congenital cardiologists, interventional cardiologists, maternal-fetal medicine experts, and anesthesiologists when caring for women with significant congenital heart disease during pregnancy. This point is critical and is also emphasized in the recently published ACC/AHA/HRS/ISACHD/SCAI Guideline for the Management of Adults with Congenital Heart Disease.5 The risks of general anesthesia with percutaneous RVOT intervention were weighed against the risk of future cardiac event without intervention.

Attempts were made to limit the risk of ionizing radiation to the developing fetus. Catheterization was performed during the early second trimester to avoid the impact of radiation on organ development during the first trimester. Few reported cases of transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement during pregnancy exist, and intervention in those cases was also done in the early second trimester.6,7 During catheterization, the authors very appropriately limited radiation exposure by using single-plane imaging with low dosing rates and by refraining from the use of cineangiography.

At the time of catheterization, the patient had severe RV hypertension due to BPV stenosis and right pulmonary artery (RPA) stent obstruction. An additional RPA stent was placed with modest improvement in RV systolic pressure. The BPV was balloon dilated in preparation for transcatheter pulmonary valve placement within the BPV. The frame of the BPV was intentionally fractured to facilitate placement of a sizable Sapien valve.8 However, during the initial dilation of the BPV, the balloon ruptured due to its interaction with the RPA stent. After frame fracture, the RV systolic pressure had significantly decreased from 90% to 65% systemic. A decision was made not to attempt to place the transcatheter pulmonary valve within the BPV due to the concern about interaction between the expanding Sapien valve and the RPA stent. The patient was left with moderate PR, but a malpositioned or embolized Sapien valve could have been catastrophic for this patient. This interventional decision was challenging, and we applaud the team's judgment in successfully navigating this complexity. The patient continued to have moderate PR, but the multilevel RVOT obstruction and RV hypertension were improved. Interestingly, a study of pregnancy outcomes in women with residual RVOT lesions showed that moderate/severe PR is associated with right heart failure during pregnancy only when accompanied by additional risk factors including branch pulmonary artery stenosis.3

The patient did well and eventually delivered a healthy infant at 37 4/7 weeks’ gestation. We congratulate the entire treating team on their impressive patient management. As the population of adults with congenital heart disease grows, more and more women with congenital heart disease will consider and possibly undergo pregnancy. This case highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary collaboration in the care of these patients. The report by Hirschbaum and Aboulhosn1 serves as an excellent reference to guide treating physicians in the management of pregnant patients with residual RVOT dysfunction.
