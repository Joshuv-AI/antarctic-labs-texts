Real-World Patient Outcomes on the Efficacy of Lanreotide Treatment for Neuroendocrine Tumors: The Influence of Body Mass Index, Body Surface Area, and Renal Function
Authors: Ilse de Weert, Mirjam Crul, Heinz-Josef Klümpen, Tim Schutte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLanreotide is an effective and safe first-line therapy to treat patients with a gastroenteropancreatic (GEP) neuroendocrine tumor (NET). Precision oncology may be used to provide each patient with the right dosage. Pharmacokinetic data suggest lower exposure in higher weight patients. However, no recommendations for dose individualization in specific patient groups exist. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association of body mass index (BMI), body surface area (BSA), and renal function on the efficacy and toxicity of lanreotide treatment for NETs.MethodsGEP-NET patients on lanreotide treatment at Amsterdam UMC from 2016 to 2022 were included. Outcome measures were primarily PFS and secondarily OS and toxicity. Kaplan-Meier analyses and Cox’s proportional hazard models were used to calculate median PFS/OS and compare subgroups based on BMI, BSA, and renal function.ResultsA total of 122 patients with NETs of diverse origin, functionality, and grade were included with a median PFS of 28 months (95% CI, 19.3–36.7). The median OS was not reached. No statistically significant difference in PFS among BMI subgroups was observed, although obese patients had the longest PFS (52 months, 95% CI, 27.4–76.7) compared to other BMI categories. No relation between BSA/renal function and PFS or toxicity was found.ConclusionThis large real-world data cohort of NET patients treated with lanreotide found no association of BMI, BSA, or renal function with PFS. Dose individualization of lanreotide treatment based on the patient characteristics BMI, BSA, or renal function does not seem rational based on these findings.

Introduction

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are a rare disease, with an average incidence of 1–5 in 100,000 people [1–3]. NETs are a heterogeneous group of malignancies originating from different types of neuroendocrine cells, mostly in gastrointestinal organs (e.g., pancreas or small intestine), altogether referred to as gastroenteropancreatic (GEP)-NETs. Furthermore, NETs can secrete peptides and affect hormone producing cells, which can cause hormone hypersecretion [3, 4]. NETs that produce excessive hormones are referred to as “functioning,” and can lead to symptoms such as (in the case of serotonin hypersecretion) diarrhea, flushing but also valve fibrosis and heart failure [5]. The prognosis for patients with GEP-NETs differs significantly between the different primary sites and tumor stages including possible local treatment options at diagnosis [6]. Other unfavorable prognostic features are the elevation of tumor marker chromogranin A (CgA) and a higher tumor grade based mitotic index and the proliferation marker Ki-67 [7–9]. According to the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification for GEP-NETs, three grades are distinguished: grade 1 (<2 mitoses/2 mm2 and/or Ki-67 <3%, grade 2 (2–20 mitoses/2 mm2 and/or Ki-67 3–20%), and grade 3 (>20 mitoses/2 mm2 and/or Ki-67 >20%) [10]. A higher grade is associated with increasingly worse prognosis [8, 11], while the low grade GEP-NETs behavior is more indolent.

In accordance with the European Neuroendocrine Tumor Society (ENETS) Consensus Guidelines, somatostatin analogs (SSAs), such as lanreotide, are the first-line therapy for patients with inoperable, locally advanced or metastatic GEP-NETs at grade 1 or 2 with a Ki-67 index up to 10% [11, 12], following the landmark CLARINET trial [4]. This trial demonstrated prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) compared to placebo and a favorable safety profile, with limited side effects such as diarrhea, abdominal pain, and pain on the injection site [4, 13–15]. Lanreotide inhibits the hypersecretion of specific hormones (e.g., growth hormone) and has anti-tumor effects by inhibiting growth hormone release through binding to somatostatin receptors 2 and 5 [16, 17], ultimately resulting in downstream cell cycle arrest and/or apoptosis. Moreover, antiproliferative effects are mediated indirectly by inhibiting tumor angiogenesis and the production of tumor growth-supporting substances (e.g., insulin growth factor-1) [15, 17].

Lanreotide is regularly administered subcutaneously at 120 mg every 28 days [11, 12, 18, 19]. As yet, no guidelines on dose individualizations exist. Interestingly, a subgroup analysis of the CLARINET study found a lower risk for progression or death in normal weight patients (HR 0.33 [95% CI 0.16, 0.67]) compared to overweight and obese patients (HR 0.71 [95% CI 0.32, 1.57] and 0.45 [95% CI 0.19, 1.05], respectively) [20, 21]. In addition, population pharmacokinetic analyses on pooled data from four clinical trials [4, 22–24] showed higher lanreotide concentrations and a larger area under the curve (AUC) in patients with lower weight classes versus higher weights [25]. Unfortunately, the used weight classes in this population pharmacokinetic study were weight percentiles of the studied population instead of validated cutoff values, and body mass index (BMI) or body surface area (BSA) were not evaluated as important pharmacokinetic parameters. Lanreotide is eliminated mainly by renal and biliary excretion, and (renal) clearance of lanreotide was found to be reduced in patients with severe chronic renal insufficiency. This would suggest higher lanreotide exposure in patients with a renal insufficiency [26], although Buil-Bruna found no impact of renal impairment on the pharmacokinetic profile of lanreotide in their pooled population pharmacokinetic analyses [25].

The unfavorable patient outcomes and lower lanreotide exposure in higher weight patients, and conflicting evidence of renal impairment, indicate that dosing based on body weight, BSA or BMI and renal function warrants further exploration. Moreover, lanreotide is also a costly drug (>EUR 1,000/injection of 120 mg) [27]. Thus, potential dose individualization, in particular dose reduction in under or normal weight patients may lead to lower doses, less side effects and reduced costs.

To avoid suboptimal lanreotide therapy, including hypothetically under dosing obese patients, or overdosing under/normal weight patients, it is pivotal to identify patient characteristics associated with patient outcomes that could be used for future dose individualization. The possibility of individualizing lanreotide treatment based on patient characteristics, such as BMI, BSA, or renal function, has not been explored yet. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate if BMI, BSA, and renal function are associated with the efficacy and toxicity of lanreotide treatment in real-world patients with GEP-NETs.

Methods

Study Population and Setting

The study population was patients with GEP-NETs treated with lanreotide at Amsterdam UMC, a tertiary referral center and a certified ENETS Center of Excellence [28]. All patients ≥18 years old that are or have been treated with lanreotide for a GEP-NET from January 1, 2016, until December 31, 2022, were included.

Study Design and Definitions

A retrospective cohort study was performed, assessing the efficacy of lanreotide treatment using PFS as the primary outcome measure and OS as the secondary outcome measure. The PFS was defined as the time from the first administration of lanreotide (120 mg every 28 days) to progression or stopping lanreotide treatment. Reasons for stopping lanreotide treatment could be death, toxicity, switching to second-line therapies, the disease becoming locally treatable or curable, or the patient’s choice for no further treatment. Furthermore, the OS was defined as the time from the first administration of lanreotide until either death or censoring. All patients alive or still on lanreotide treatment were censored at October 1st, 2023. For tumor grading, the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification for GEP-NETs was used [10].

Data Collection

This study used retrospective real-world data (RWD) extracted from electronic patient records (EPIC, Verona, Wisconsin USA). The Amsterdam UMC Medical Ethics Committee approved the study on October 23rd, 2023 (METC-2023-0360). Data were extractable from the local go-live of EPIC on January 1st, 2016, until December 31st, 2022. No patients were included who started therapy after December 2022, because the median treatment duration found in the literature is 24 months [14, 19]. Therefore, only a small part of patients who started lanreotide treatment after December 2022 would have had progression, limiting the maturity of the data. A flowchart of the inclusion process is given in online supplementary material A (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000548257). All patient data was handled according to medical-ethical regulations and Good Clinical Practice (GCP) [29]. Patient confidentiality and data privacy were ensured by anonymizing the collected data [30].

Both demographic and treatment data were retrieved. Demographic data included gender, age, weight, height, BMI, BSA, serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (measured with the CKD-EPI method), date of diagnosis, tumor localization, WHO tumor grade, Ki-67 index, presence of metastases, WHO performance score, resection of primary tumor, and functional status of the tumor. Treatment data included dose of lanreotide, duration of treatment, toxicity, baseline CgA, progression-free survival, overall survival, reasons for stopping lanreotide treatment, and second line of therapy. If data were missing at the time point of interest for a certain patient, e.g., the creatinine value after stopping lanreotide treatment, the closest data point for that patient was used instead. When these data point was >3 months from the time point of interest, it was reported as missing.

Data Analysis

To investigate the influence of BMI, BSA, and renal function on the efficacy of lanreotide treatment, the data were stratified. For BMI, the data were stratified based on WHO criteria: <18.5 kg/m2 (underweight), 18.5–<25.0 kg/m2 (healthy weight), 25.0–<30.0 kg/m2 (overweight) or ≥30.0 kg/m2 (obesity) [31]. Since no international stratification consensus for BSA exists, BSA strata were derived to resemble the BMI stratification as follows: <1.6 m2 (low), 1.6–<2.0 m2 (intermediate-low), 2.0–<2.2 m2 (intermediate-high), and ≥2.2 m2 (high). For renal function, the data were stratified based on the cutoff values used in clinical pharmacology with an estimated eGFR of <10, 10–<30, 30–<50, and ≥50 mL/min [32]. We subdivided the grade 2 (Ki-67 3–20%) NETs (according to WHO 2022 criteria [10]) between 3 and <10% and ≥10–20%, to distinguish between the grade 2 NETs eligible according to the CLARINET trial (3–<10%) [4] and those with a higher Ki-67 index.

To compare the occurrence of toxicity on lanreotide treatment between the different subgroups, included patient records were queried for adverse events, counted and graded (according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events [CTCAE] Version 5.0 criteria [33]), depending upon sufficient description in the patient records. Known adverse events of lanreotide treatment such as diarrhea, abdominal pain/discomfort, and pain of the injection site were examined by searching the electronic patient files using keywords commonly used to describe these side effects (online suppl. material B). All data were collected in Excel (Excel version 2016, Microsoft, Redmond, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was done in SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics version 28, IBM Corp., Chicago, USA). To determine the median overall PFS and OS time, and for each subgroup, Kaplan-Meier analyses were performed [34]. Patients who became locally treatable or curable were excluded in the PFS evaluable population in the PFS analysis. Subgroups were compared using the log-rank test. Toxicity data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Moreover, a post hoc Spearman’s rank correlation was performed to assess the correlation between renal function, PFS and OS [35]. For all statistical analyses, a p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The influence of covariates was investigated using a Cox’s proportional hazards model [34] reported in online supplementary material C.

Results

Study Population

A total of 122 patients who were treated with lanreotide for a NET were included (Table 1). The most common primary locations were the small intestine (n = 62, 50.8%) and pancreas (n = 37, 30.3%). All tumors were WHO grade 1 or 2 at/before start of lanreotide treatment. Within the WHO grade 2 classified tumors, a large majority had a Ki-67 index of 3–<10%, although a substantial group (n = 20; 33.9% of grade 2 tumors) had a Ki-67 index ≥10–20%. Patients typically had a WHO performance score of 0–1 and overall only 7 patients did not have known metastases at the start of treatment. The liver was the most prevalent site for (distant) metastasis and the majority of patients with metastases had them solely in the liver. Just over half of the patients had no clinical evidence for a functional tumor, and among the functional tumors, the serotonin producing NET (n = 47; 38.5%) was the most common type.

Stratifying the data on BMI, BSA, and renal function yielded uneven subgroups. Among the BMI subgroups, the large majority had a healthy weight (18.5–<25.0 kg/m2; n = 50, 41.0%) or overweight (25.0–<30.0 kg/m2; n = 46, 37.7%). The largest BSA subgroups was “intermediate-low” (1.6–<2.0 m2; n = 70, 57.4%), and renal function (median 80 mL/min/1.73 m2; IQR 62, 90) was skewed toward the maximum of 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 reported by our laboratory, with almost all (n = 111, 91%) patients within the ≥50 mL/min/1.73 m2 group.

Primary Outcome: PFS

Using Kaplan-Meier analyses, the overall median PFS times of patients were assessed as 28 months (95% CI, 19.3–36.7) (Table 2). In this analysis of the PFS evaluable population, 9 patients were excluded (per protocol) whose lanreotide treatment was not indicated anymore when the disease became locally treatable. Comparing the BMI subgroups revealed that the subgroup with obese patients survived without progression the longest: median 52 months (95% CI, 27.4–76.7), with optical diverging curves compared to the other BMI subgroups, although this difference was statistically nonsignificant (log-rank, p = 0.086) (Table 2; Fig. 1a). For the BSA subgroups, no differences between the subgroups were observed, and (eventual) differences were statistically nonsignificant by log-rank testing (log-rank, p = 0.915) (Table 2, Fig. 1c). The renal function subgroups were extremely unevenly distributed, limiting the validity of the Kaplan-Meier analyses between renal function categories and PFS. Instead, a post hoc Spearman’s rank correlation was used indicating no correlation between eGFR and PFS (Spearman’s rank rho: −0.035; p = 0.715).

Cox Proportional Hazard Model for PFS

A Cox proportional hazard model was used to assess covariates listed in Table 1. A forward selection procedure was followed, as no initial model could be fitted to use backward elimination (probably due to the sample size in combination with a relatively large number of categorical variables). The forward selection and univariate analysis of all (co)variates are described in online supplementary material C. In the final Cox proportional hazard model, the WHO performance (p < 0.001), CgA (elevation >2 ULN) at baseline (p = 0.015), and Ki-67 index (p = 0.021) (all categorized) were identified as factors with a significant association with the outcome PFS, and other variables were not significant. The hazard ratios for progression of the included variables in the Cox proportional hazard model are provided in Table 3 and the Kaplan-Meier analysis with median PFS is presented in online supplementary material D.

Secondary Outcome: Overall Survival

The estimated OS time could not be calculated using the Kaplan-Meier analyses, and the median was not reached in any of the subgroups. Among the BMI subgroups, we observed no clear trend nor a statistical significant difference in OS (log-rank, p = 0.329, Fig. 1b). For the BSA subgroups, the survival curves seem to diverge (high > intermediate-low > intermediate-high > low); however, this difference was statistically nonsignificant (log-rank p = 0.252, Fig. 1d). The renal function was also not correlated with OS (Spearman’s rank, rho −0.118; p = 0.196).

Secondary Outcome: Toxicity

Adverse effects were common (Table 4), with diarrhea and abdominal pain as the most prevalent reported for 62 (50.8%) and 54 (43.2%) patients respectively. A small minority reported pain at the injection site (n = 3, 2.4%). Adverse events could not be graded according to CTCAE criteria, due to a (too) limited description of side effects in the records. Occurrence of adverse events was assessed among BMI subgroups (Table 4), but no clear trends were observed.

Discussion

This real-world investigation of lanreotide treatment in patients with GEP-NETs from all origins and different stages found no statistically significant difference in PFS among BMI, BSA or renal function subgroups, although the obese patients had a median PFS twice as long as patients with lower BMIs. Upfront dose individualization of lanreotide treatment based on the patient characteristics BMI, BSA or renal function does not seem rational yet.

Overall, we observed a median PFS of 28 months. In the CLARINET study, the median PFS was not reached after 96 weeks (=22.1 months) [4], although the follow-up open-label extension study reported a median PFS of 32.8 months [14]. This longer median PFS may be attributed to the stricter CLARINET study inclusion criteria, as patients with a Ki-67 index ≥10% or a functional NET were excluded. A high Ki-67 index and functional status are negative prognostic predictors for survival and excluding these patients could yield better results. Evaluation of the specific subgroup of high Ki-67 index ≥10% in our study confirmed their unfavorable prognosis, although the difference in PFS between the WHO grade 2 tumors (<10% vs. ≥10%) was statistically nonsignificant (HR 1.307, 95% CI 0.648–2.635, p = 0.455). This signifies the potential value of lanreotide beyond the CLARINET study inclusion criteria (Ki-67 index <10%).

Compared to previous real-world reported outcomes, our study presents a similar median PFS. A smaller RWD study executed in Canada described 69 patients, with a median treatment duration of 21.6 months [36]. Another small RWD (n = 33, only small intestine NETs) study from Vienna reported a median PFS of 29.3 months [37]. Their exclusion of patients with pancreatic NETs (which have a worse prognosis) may explain the difference between the Viennese and our cohort [6, 13].

The numerical but statistically nonsignificant favorable PFS in our obese BMI subgroup was unexpected, as the CLARINET investigators reported a lower risk on a combined outcome of progression or death in normal weight patients compared to overweight and obese patients treated with lanreotide. Unfortunately, comparing our findings with the median PFS in the BMI subgroup analysis from the CLARINET study is not possible, as their median PFS was not calculated due to data immaturity among the patients treated with lanreotide [20]. However, for the placebo arm in the CLARINET study, a trend toward a longer PFS in the BMI subgroup with overweight patients (24.4 months, 95% CI, 18.0–not reached) was described, compared to obese and healthy patients, respectively (17.6 [95% CI, 9.0–24.5] and 13.0 months [95% CI, 12.0–18.2]). We hypothesize (besides the possibility of coincidence) that through a mechanism as yet unknown, adipocyte hormones could have a protective effect on NET biology.

We found no signs of reduced efficacy (by means of a shorter PFS) in higher weight/BMI or BSA classes in our study. Combined with the previous population pharmacokinetic analyses which showed lower lanreotide plasma concentrations in patients with higher weight classes, the (presumed) lower plasma concentrations in patients with higher weight classes yield no significant different patient outcomes in our study. This would suggest that the present dose of 120 mg each 28 days is adequate and may even be a relative overdose for healthy or underweight GEP-NET patients.

Given the limited number of patients with a reduced renal function, the absence of a correlation between renal function and PFS/OS cannot be extrapolated to patients with (severe) renal impairment. Furthermore, the estimated median OS time was not reached due to insufficient data maturity. Patients need a longer follow-up time to provide more mature results on OS. In addition, given the sometimes indolent NET biology, patients with a NET do not necessarily have disease-specific mortality and can also die with a NET rather than from a NET, limiting the usability of OS as outcome measure.

In future research, a larger sample size and longer follow-up is needed to boost study power and allow for further analysis of covariates. Another limitation is that our study was a single-center study, a multicenter approach, could provide a larger sample size and enhance generalizability. Moreover, since our study was retrospective we were reliant on documentation in electronic patient records. In our study, distinguishing and relating the cause of diarrhea as side effect of the treatment, or a direct effect of the tumor was difficult, as the documentation of the start of diarrhea and assessment of a causal effect with the treatment was mostly missing in the patient files. Also the exact reason/indication (symptom or tumor control) for starting or continuing the lanreotide treatment was not always documented. Lanreotide was sometimes continued upon disease progression, which might have biased and overestimated the PFS in some cases. This is of course a consequence of a retrospective real-world data cohort study. Future preferably prospective studies would optimally include pharmacokinetic analysis linked with effectiveness outcomes (favorably PFS), in a population with a complete dataset, especially with regards to the documentation and grading of adverse effects. In conclusion, this large RWD cohort shows that patients with a GEP-NET and undergoing lanreotide treatment in an expert ENETS center demonstrated an overall median PFS of 28 months and presented no differences in PFS among BMI, BSA, or renal function subgroups.
