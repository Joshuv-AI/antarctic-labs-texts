Towards trustworthy AI-driven cuffless blood pressure monitoring
Authors: Ana Cisnal, Itilekha Podder, Leoni Grossmann, Kanika Dheman, Mohamed Elgendi, Marco Valgimigli, Diego Paez-Granados
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCuffless blood pressure monitoring is becoming increasingly feasible with the rise of wearable technologies, offering significant promise for preventive cardiovascular care. Yet diverse sensing modalities and modeling strategies have produced fragmented evidence that is difficult to compare. In this systematic review, we introduce a unified multi-axis taxonomy that relates surrogate signals, modeling paradigms, and calibration strategies, providing a coherent structure for the field. Building on this foundation, we analysed current approaches across physiological and wearable sensing and evaluated performance using best machine-learning practices. We highlight key barriers to real-world deployment—including protocol realism, calibration drift, population diversity, and fairness relevant reporting—and translated these findings into an actionable model-card-style reporting framework for cuffless BP studies. Our framework establishes the methodological basis for reliable, equitable, and clinically valid cuffless BP monitoring, a vital step towards democratizing continuous BP monitoring for widespread clinical benefit.Subject terms: Cardiology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Accurate blood pressure (BP) measurements are vital in the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), which are the leading cause of death globally1. High BP is a modifiable risk factor for CVDs, and its effective management can delay or even prevent the onset of CVD-related morbidity and mortality. The initial step in managing hypertension (HTN) involves detecting pathologically elevated BP levels. Despite the widespread prevalence of HTN, most affected individuals remain unaware of their condition. Out of 1.5M adults screened in ref. 2, 32.0% had never had a BP measurement before and 34.0% had HTN, with a total of 41% unaware of their condition (see Fig. 1).

The traditional cuff-based sphygmomanometer remains the state-of-the-art device for BP measurement, primarily used in clinical settings. However, the white coat—a phenomenon where BP changes during a clinical visit3. Self-measured home BP and ambulatory BP monitoring are out-of-office BP, and they have been shown to exert more prognostic ability than conventional in-office BP4–6. Additionally, BP fluctuates throughout the day, influenced by numerous internal and external factors. BP rises sharply upon waking and falls during sleep, with variations occurring throughout the day. Consequently, major guidelines recommend the use of ambulatory or home BP measurement in the diagnosis of HTN7,8. Beyond its greater predictive power, home BP can substantially refine conventional in-office BP-based risk stratification9,10.

In this context, consumer-grade devices (smartwatches) aim at measuring BP using surrogate vital sensing, such as, photoplethysmography (PPG) and/or electrocardiography (ECG). Some devices even meet medical device grades as outlined in the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 81060-2:2018 for noninvasive sphygmomanometers and have been cleared by the United States Food and Drug Administration and the European Union (CE-label for Conformity Marketing).

However, the algorithms and validation studies for these devices are not openly disclosed, and most of them only utilize data collected from healthy individuals11. Due to the currently limited validation of the technology and the low reliability of smartwatch-based BP measurements12, their usage is not yet recommended for monitoring treatment response in hypertensive patients or those with cardiovascular comorbidities13. Moreover, current validation standards for cuff BP devices are not suitable for data-driven approaches14,15. Until specific standards, education, and clinical translation are proven for data-based cuffless BP devices, and their accuracy and clinical utility thoroughly examined, these devices should not be recommended for medical decision-making, diagnosis, or treatment16,17.

Despite more than 86 published methods evaluated in this work, no generalizable, clinically validated cuffless BP system currently exists. Existing reviews have provided valuable summaries of sensor technologies, model architectures, and signal-processing pipelines in cuffless BP estimation and hypertension monitoring. However, most prior reviews remain primarily descriptive or engineering-oriented: they summarize what sensors and algorithms have been used, but provide limited empirical evidence on whether reported models are evaluated in ways that support generalization, regulatory alignment, or clinical translation18–20. In particular, key machine learning (ML)-specific questions remain insufficiently addressed: whether validation is subject-independent, whether calibration-based and calibration-free approaches are evaluated under appropriate assumptions, whether reported errors are robust across study designs and realistic ambulatory conditions, whether subgroup-level reporting is sufficient for future fairness evaluation, and whether published results can be compared transparently across heterogeneous datasets and protocols.

To position our contribution relative to this literature, Table 1 summarizes existing reviews across key methodological, evaluative, and translational dimensions. Among recent works, Cheung et al.21 is the most closely related, combining systematic review with quantitative benchmark analysis and calibration-aware evaluation. Their work makes an important contribution by adopting a hardware-to-algorithm perspective, organizing the field by pipeline stage, and introducing explained deviation as a study-level normalization metric. However, it does not provide formal random-effects meta-analytic pooling, publication-bias diagnostics, study-level risk-of-bias assessment, or a reusable reporting instrument tailored to ML-based cuffless BP evaluation. Other recent reviews and statements provide important methodological or clinical guidance, but do not jointly integrate an ML-oriented taxonomy, quantitative synthesis of reported error, validation-design subgroup analysis, demographic and subgroup reporting-gap assessment, and a model-card-style reporting framework.

Therefore, the added value of the present review is that it links methodological classification, empirical evidence synthesis, and reporting guidance into a single evaluation-oriented framework. Specifically, we make four contributions. First, we introduce a three-axis orthogonal taxonomy that separates the signal-to-target relationship, modeling paradigm, and calibration strategy, thereby distinguishing fundamentally different algorithmic claims that are often conflated in prior work. Second, we perform a random-effects meta-analysis of reported SBP mean error with validation-strategy subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and publication-bias diagnostics, allowing heterogeneity and potential optimistic reporting to be examined empirically rather than discussed only qualitatively. Third, we assess demographic, clinical, and subgroup-level reporting practices relevant to future fairness evaluation, clarifying that the current literature supports a reporting-gap analysis. Fourth, we translate these findings into the BP-Card, a model-card-inspired reporting framework that separates calibration-free and calibration-based evaluation pathways and operationalizes minimum reporting requirements for cohort description, validation design, explicit baseline-model comparison to contextualize predictive performance, standardized metrics, and subgroup-relevant reporting.

In this work, we provide a systematic review and meta-analysis of cuffless BP estimation methods beyond PPG, evaluating the quality, robustness, and generalizability of reported results in ML-based approaches in relation to clinical guidelines and emerging validation expectations. By combining an ML-oriented taxonomy, quantitative evidence synthesis, validation-design analysis, fairness-relevant reporting-gap assessment, and the BP-Card framework, this review provides a structured basis for interpreting the current evidence and identifying the methodological and reporting factors that limit comparability across studies. The resulting synthesis clarifies how validation strategy, calibration paradigm, cohort reporting, and performance reporting shape the interpretation of reported accuracy, and proposes practical reporting guidance for future cuffless BP studies.

Additionally, we review validation practices and performance reporting across studies, followed by a quantitative meta-analysis assessing reproducibility, robustness, and methodological consistency.

Sensing modalities and data sources

Accurate BP estimation with non-invasive ambulatory sensors depends on the careful pre-processing of physiological signals, which typically involves baseline drift correction, proper signal alignment, and artifact removal. Signal quality can be significantly affected by both biological factors (e.g., tissue composition, respiration, vasomotor activity, or autonomic dysregulation) and external influences (e.g., light exposure or motion)22. To address this, signal processing techniques such as filtering, normalization, and segmentation are commonly applied. Although many advanced ML algorithms are capable of extracting meaningful features directly from raw physiological signals, in the reviewed literature, only 31% (n = 27 out of 86) of studies utilize raw signals, while the remaining works rely on features derived from preprocessed data.

Current common cuffless methods for measuring hemodynamic activity through waveform recording are imaging photoplethysmography (iPPG), ECG, PPG, impedance plethysmography (IPG) and pulse pressure waveform (PPW) (Fig. 2a). Unlike traditional arterial blood pressure (ABP) measurement, these methods allow for continuous BP monitoring. Although these methods show promising results, it is essential to understand and validate their estimations against traditional cuff-based BP monitors, as they offer indirect and proxy-data of the actual BP12,23.

PPG is a non-invasive optical technique used to quantify the arterial volume pulse by measuring dynamic variations in light absorption or reflection as blood circulates through peripheral vessels. A PPG sensor emits light into the underlying tissue, and the blood in nearby vessels absorbs part of this light while the rest is scattered back to the sensor24. PPG can be performed in reflectance or transmission modes22. Transmission-mode is limited to body sites where the optical light source and photodetector can enclose a pulsating blood vessel, such as the earlobe, fingertip and toes, while reflectance-mode offers more placement flexibility but is more susceptible to motion artifacts25. PPG signal amplitude is influenced by sensor positioning and contact pressure. Factors like ambient light, motion, skin color, keratinization, underlying adiposity and follicular density also impact signal quality.

iPPG, also known as remote photoplethysmography (rPPG), is a non-contact optical imaging method26. iPPG is based on capturing 2-D images of the uncovered skin using a high-definition digital camera27, detecting fluctuations in reflected light caused by heart volume pulse. It enables unobtrusive monitoring and flexible positioning, though its accuracy is strongly affected by motion and lighting artifacts.

ECG is a non-invasive technique that monitors heart activity through electrodes to capture electrical impulses produced during cardiac cycles. It is widely used for heart rate and rhythm monitoring. However, like PPG, it is prone to motion artifacts and depends on proper electrode placement. Its role in BP estimation is less direct, often requiring combination with other signals.

IPG measures bio-impedance to track volumetric changes in blood flow, typically using a tetrapolar electrode setup28. While promising, it is highly susceptible to motion artifacts, pressure, or temperature changes, and has a low signal-to-noise ratio, especially in individuals with poor circulation or health conditions, limiting its broader applicability.

Tonometry measures arterial wall pressure by applying controlled external force with a contact sensor to partially flatten the artery, thereby recording the resulting deformation and acquiring the PPW. Tonometric devices may incorporate optical, mechanical, laser-based or PZTs. Although more invasive than PPG or IPG due to the need for direct skin contact, tonometry offers relatively higher accuracy for BP estimation29,30.

Table 2 and Fig. 3a summarizes the signal modalities used in the reviewed studies, indicating their measurement location and sample size. We found the majority of studies (n = 70 out of 86) used PPG sensors, with 37 relying solely on PPG. PPG was most commonly combined with ECG, observed in 29 studies. In contrast, ECG was rarely used alone, appearing in only 4 studies. This pattern is consistent with the comparative analysis by Finnegan et al.31. They systematically evaluated ECG- and PPG-derived features for estimating changes in blood pressure (ΔBP) and found that PPG-based models consistently outperformed ECG-only models, while adding ECG features to PPG did not yield appreciable gains. Physiologically, the ECG–BP relationship is mediated by mechano-electric coupling, a cyclical interaction between cardiac electrical activity and mechanical contraction that is strongly influenced by autonomic, hormonal, and environmental factors and is therefore less specific to BP. In contrast, PPG directly captures peripheral blood volume changes and wave reflections, providing a more direct haemodynamic surrogate31.

A total of 35 publications employed two or more signals in combination, highlighting the dominance of either PPG-only approaches or multimodal sensing strategies. iPPG, one of the most recent methods, was used independently in 6 studies, while IPG was used alone in only one. No reviewed studies employed PPW or Peripheral Capillary Oxygen Saturation (SPO2) signals by themselves. The Piezoelectric Transducer (PZT) sensors were used in conjunction with ECG in a single study32, while SPO2 was included in only one study alongside ECG and PPG33. PPW was employed in two studies: one in combination with ECG and PPG34, and another with PPG and iPPG35. From all identified studies, the finger was the most common location for BP estimation using PPG sensors (n = 18), followed by the wrist (n = 11). In contrast, locations such as the face, ear, or chest were used less frequently, primarily in the context of recent sensor modalities of iPPG and ECG.

We also systematically reviewed all databases referenced across the included studies and summarized their main characteristics in Fig. 3b. To contextualize how these datasets are used in practice, Fig. 3a provides an overview of database usage and sample sizes across studies. Among these, the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) database series was the most commonly used, appearing in 31 studies in total, specifically, MIMIC-I in 3, MIMIC-II (Multi-parameter Intelligent Monitoring for Intensive Care) in 18, and MIMIC-III (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care) in 10. MIMIC-IV36, which was released in March 2021, had not yet been adopted in any of the reviewed studies, likely due to its recent release at the time.

On the other hand, the UC Irvine (UCI) database, a meticulously preprocessed subset of MIMIC, found its place in 8 articles. VitalDB appeared in 5 articles, while University of Queensland Vital Signs (UQVS), PPG-BP, and AuroraBP were each used in 2 studies. CAS-BP, PulseDB, and AuscWave appeared less frequently, with each being used in only one study. Noteworthy finding was a proportion of published works 17% (13 out of 78), where authors fail to specify the number of subjects used in their studies, particularly when utilizing public databases.

The original MIMIC (1992–1999)37 included recorded data from over 90 emergency care unit patients. Afterwards, MIMIC-II38 and MIMIC-III39 were released, which are many times the size of the original database. Additionally, the “Cuffless Blood Pressure Estimation Data Set”40, which is freely available on UCI Machine Learning Repository, contains preprocessed and validated ECG, PPG and ABP signals from MIMIC. The PPG-BP database41 contains 657 segments of PPG signals, each lasting 2.1 seconds, from 219 subjects, including individuals with conditions such as HTN and diabetes. Three good-quality PPG signal segments at rest position were saved from each subject, along with one measurement of reference BP. VitalDB 42 contains time-synchronized bio-signals (ABP, ECG, and PPG) from 6,388 patients (13,489 h recorded time). AusWare43 includes 350 upper arm BP records from 150 individuals, collected using a multi-parameter clinical monitoring unit, with reference BP values derived from automated processing of Korotkoff sounds. UQVS dataset44 comprises monitoring data and vital signs collected from 32 surgical cases involving anesthesia. This SHIMMER database45 consists of 50 recordings, each around 20 min, from 6 healthy subject at rest. ECG signals were captured using three electrodes for left arm - right arm derivation, while PPG signals were recorded with clip on the left index finger. Additionally, BP readings were taken every minute using the OMRON HeartGuide smartwatch worn on the right wrist.

PulseDB46 introduced in 2023 emerged as the largest cleaned dataset with 5,245,454 high-quality 10-s segments of ECG, PPG, and ABP waveforms from 5361 subjects. The data sourced from a subset of the MIMIC-III and the VitalDB databases, also includes demographic information and characteristic points of ECG and PPG signals, making it an excellent resource for benchmarking BP estimation models. Despite its scale, only one study has utilized PulseDB so far. In 2024, another curated subset of MIMIC-III was released as MIMIC-BP47, offering 380 hours of recordings from 1524 subjects. Each subject has 30 segments of 30-s recordings, including PPG, ECG and RESP signals. Unlike UCI dataset, MIMIC-BP retains subjects’ identifiers to prevent data leakage between training and testing sets. Demographic data is not included, but can be accessed in the original MIMIC dataset. MIMIC-BP provides a balanced representation of BP values, making it a promising benchmark dataset for BP estimation models, but it has not yet been used in any of the reviewed studies.

Aurora-BP48 was released in 2022 as the first large-scale study to assess the performance of wearable tonometric, ECG, and PPG devices on both healthy and hypertensive adults in an ambulatory settings. This marks a significant advancement for the development of ABP estimation models compared to other public databases, which are in-clinic. In 2023, the CAS-BP dataset49 was introduced through a large-scale validation study. It includes over 29,000 high-quality BP recordings collected from 3077 adults using a smartwatch equipped with ECG, multi-wavelength PPG, and PPW sensors. Reference BP was measured using mercury sphygmomanometers and dual-observer auscultation. The dataset captures a broad range of BP values, including 36% hypertensive individuals.

A critical aspect of BP estimation models is the inclusion of diverse BP ranges. Combining databases offers access to a larger and more varied pool of data, covering a wider BP spectrum. For instance, while the MIMIC database primarily features normotensive and hypertensive measurements, the UQVS dataset includes hypotensive data, thereby enhancing its suitability for comprehensive BP estimation research. Several studies adopted a strategy of using multiple databases. For example50, combined their own data with MIMIC-III51, integrated their data with MIMIC I, and52 combined MIMIC-II with UQVS data. Other studies validated their algorithms across different datasets; 53 undertook independent validation with PPG-BP and MIMIC-II, while54 used either UCI or UQVS.

Lastly, of the studies reviewed, 68% relied on openly accessible datasets, promoting research advancement and enhancing reproducibility. The remaining 25 studies (32%) employed their own collected data. Among these, only one study55 shared its data publicly (SHIMMER database45), promoting transparency and data sharing within the scientific community, while the rest had restricted access. Beyond our included studies, further publicly available resources include the ambulatory ECG-PPG dataset by Wong et al.56 and the 56-subject PPG database by Argüello-Prada and Castaño Mosquera57. These additional datasets, while not part of our meta-analysis, illustrate the growing but still limited availability of open data for cuffless BP research.

Estimation models and algorithmic approaches

Once the physiological signals are acquired, the next fundamental step is selecting an appropriate modeling strategy to infer blood pressure from these surrogate measurements. Successful cuffless BP estimation relies not only on the sensing technology but also on the methodological framework used to process, transform, and interpret the data to generate BP estimates. Previous works have typically classified BP methods according to the underlying algorithmic framework, distinguishing between hemodynamic, ML, and DL models20,23,58. However, we found this classification insufficient to capture the multidimensional landscape of current developments in the field.

To address this limitation, we introduce a taxonomy organized along three orthogonal axes (Fig. 4). The first axis describes the relationship between the surrogate input signal and the estimation target; the second axis defines the mathematical modeling paradigm; and the third axis distinguishes the information used for personalization or calibration. Along the first axis—linking surrogate signals to estimation targets—we propose three categories: hemodynamics-derived models, data-driven BP regression models, and ABP waveform reconstruction models. These categories differ fundamentally in their estimation targets: the first two focus on scalar BP values, whereas the third reconstructs the full ABP waveform. Importantly, the categories are agnostic to the sensing modality; instead, they reflect the expected output of the algorithm. Hemodynamics-derived models use physiology-grounded features such as Pulse Arrival Time (PAT), Pulse Transit Time (PTT), or Pulse Wave Velocity (PWV) to estimate scalar systolic BP (SBP), diastolic BP (DBP), or mean arterial pressure (MAP). Data-driven BP regression models learn a mapping from either raw biosignals or derived features—including morphological and statistical descriptors, time–frequency (TF) representations, pulse-wave analysis (PWA) indices, and demographic variables—to predict scalar BP. ABP waveform reconstruction models are typically end-to-end approaches that take raw or minimally processed PPG, ECG, or iPPG as input and reconstruct the ABP waveform, from which SBP, DBP, and MAP are subsequently derived.

Three-dimensional taxonomy of cuffless BP algorithms.

The horizontal axis groups methods according to the relationship between the surrogate signal and the estimation target; the vertical axis groups them by mathematical modeling paradigm; and the depth axis represents the calibration attribute, distinguishing calibration-based and calibration-free algorithms. Closed-form equations appear only within hemodynamics-derived models. Data-driven regression spans all three families, while deep learning models appear in both data-driven BP regression and ABP waveform reconstruction.

Furthermore, calibration constitutes the third orthogonal dimension that plays a critical role in both methodological design and performance evaluation. Two primary categories can be distinguished: calibration-based and calibration-free algorithms. Calibration-based methods are tailored to individual subjects and require periodic calibration using a conventional cuff-based device. In contrast, calibration-free aims to eliminate dependence on external calibration inputs—making accurate BP estimation more challenging. Calibration-free methods may or may not incorporate demographic information such as age or gender.

Applying this taxonomy to the reviewed literature showed a substantial 73% (n = 63 out of 86) focused on data-driven BP regression models, while hemodynamics-derived models were employed in 16% (n = 14 out of 86). The remaining 11% (n = 10 out of 86) focused on ABP waveform reconstruction models.

Hemodynamics-derived models rely on closed-form equations to fit their coefficients to an expected hemodynamics behavior59. Many of these formulations originate from classical physiological models such as the Bramwell-Hill or Moens-Kortweg models60, and appear in various forms, including logarithmic61, proportional62–65, inverse66,67, and inverse square68 equations. These models are typically calibration-based, requiring an initial calibration and periodic recalibration.

A key aspect of these methods is the use of timing biomarkers such as PAT, PTT, and PWV, all of which demonstrate a correlation with BP variations. PAT is calculated as the time difference between the R-peak of an ECG signal and a fiducial point on the PPG waveform, while PTT is measured between the same fiducial point extracted from two PPG signals recorded at different anatomical sites (Fig. 2). Although PPG is the most common modality, PTT can also be derived from other pulse-wave signals (e.g., IPG, iPPG, PPW), provided they also capture the arterial pulse-wave arrival48,69. PWV represents the speed of propagation of the arterial pressure wave and is computed as the arterial path length divided by PTT.

The optimal fiducial point for determining these biomarkers is not clearly defined70,71. Common options include the foot, systolic peak, and maximum first derivative72, while less frequently used fiducials include the diastolic minimum time, the intersecting target point, and the maximum second derivative on the systolic part. Several methods for fiducial points determiantion has been proposed73, with derivative-based pulse wave analysis (PWA) being the most common74. The first derivative, known as velocity plethysmogram (VPG), reflects the velocity of blood volume change, while the second derivative, the acceleration plethysmogram (APG), represents the acceleration of the change, which relates to BP gradient. However, this derivative-based approach is highly prone to noise and artifacts, making it essential to remove respiratory-induced artifacts and incorrect beat detection for accurate calculations75,76.

Two modeling dimensions coexist across the literature: the biomarker used as input and the physiological formulation used to map this biomarker to BP. Accordingly, diverse hemodynamic models have been reported, including PAT-based51,59,71,72,77,78, PTT&PIR-based79, PWV-based32,69,80–82, PWV&PWA-based81, PTT&HR based55, Moens-Korteweg equation32,83, Windkessel model26,50,84, PTT-BP calibration model85, Bramwell–Hill model69, TPS model50 as well as, other customized solutions.

Recent evidence highlights that SBP and DBP often require distinct hemodynamic formulations, as the optimal PAT-based model differs between targets. Likewise, the choice of PPG fiducial point affects SBP and DBP estimation differently, since each fiducial emphasizes different aspects of cardiovascular dynamics71. Together, these results underscore the importance of target-specific physiological modeling when using timing biomarkers for cuffless BP estimation.

Data-driven BP regression models utilize a broad set of features to capture complex, nonlinear patterns for BP estimation. These algorithms can extract BP-related variations from biosignals and biometrics, effectively analyzing multi-dimensional physiological data to handle intricate and continuous variations49,86–89. Physiological signals contain rich information about cardiovascular dynamics that is often difficult to characterize using traditional hemodynamics models. They exhibit patterns and fluctuations associated with arterial stiffness, vascular compliance, and blood circulation - properties that ML algorithms can exploit to improve BP estimation.

Data-driven BP regression models aim to learn complex, nonlinear relationships between physiological signals and BP. Unlike hemodynamics-derived approaches—which depend on explicit physiological equations—these models rely on statistical learning to extract BP-related information from biosignals and biometrics. Physiological waveforms contain rich information about arterial stiffness, vascular compliance and circulatory dynamics that is often difficult to model analytically but can be effectively captured by ML methods49,86–89.

A broad range of handcrafted features have been proposed to characterize the dynamics of the ECG, PPG and other pulsatile signals. Timing descriptors such as PAT, PPT, and PWM90 are derived from synchronized ECG and other pulsatile signal. PWA provides morphological features that include amplitude- and intensity-based features, temporal-based features such as pulse width indices and ratios derived from these values91–95. Derivative-based representations of PPG, such as VPG and APG, have also been used, to enrich feature extraction 57,96–98. These higher-order signals enhance sensitivity to morphology-related variations and can provide complementary information. Complementary frequency-domain features derived from Fourier or wavelet analyses—including semi-classical and Schrödinger-based formulations—capture additional hemodynamic information99,100.

Beyond traditional descriptors, growing interest has focused on image-based encodings of PPG segments, including spectrograms, scalograms, visibility graphs and other morphological maps, as well as ABP-derived waveform features, which enable synergy with computer-vision models101–104. Graph-based representations, including causal-graph descriptors and topology-aware features, have also been explored to model interactions within cardiovascular signals105,106. Many recent studies demonstrate that combining multiple feature families yields more accurate models than relying on a single descriptor set53,102.

Remote PPG (rPPG) brings additional methodological considerations, since raw observations consist of images or video frames rather than one-dimensional signals. These approaches typically require region-of-interest selection, pixel aggregation and illumination-artifact removal before feature extraction or direct learning27,35,107,108. Data scarcity has motivated the use of generative models, such as GAN-based PPG synthesis108, and the development of in silico simulators that generate physiologically plausible PPG sequences conditioned on BP trajectories84.

A wide spectrum of ML algorithms has been applied for cuffless BP regression. Classical approaches include support vector regression53,91,106, linear and penalized regression models86,109,110, Gaussian process regression57,111,112, random forests53,113,114, and boosting methods49,53,93,100,105. Hybrid classification–regression pipelines have also been explored, including k-nearest neighbors or decision-tree classifiers followed by regression refinements52,115. Additional approaches include multi-parameter fusion models for PPW-derived features116, confidence-aware filtering frameworks117 and scenario-adaptive formulations and cardiovascular coupling effects51.

DL has become increasingly prominent in recent years because it can automatically extract discriminative features from complex physiological signals, removing the need for manual feature engineering. These models are particularly valued for their ability to learn hierarchical representations and capture both spatial and temporal dependencies, substantially improving the modeling of BP-related dynamics. Reported architectures include artificial neural networks (ANNs)106,118–120, CNNs101,108,121–123, recurrent neural networks (RNNs)95,124, deep neural networks (DNNs)125–127, bayesian neural networks84,107, feed-forward neural network99, bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) with attention layer89 are extensively adopted. Additionally, hybrid DL models27,35,51,88,92,102–104,108,122–124,127–139. Optimization strategies such as AMSGrad and specialized neural topologies like dendritic neural regression have also been reported to refine BP predictions87. Transfer learning is widely adopted, with models pre-trained on large public datasets and fine-tuned for personalized BP estimation89,101,106,124.

Combining physiological signals with demographic and biometric information (e.g., as age, gender, weight, or BMI) further enhances BP estimation by accounting for inter-individual variability in cardiovascular health 27,89,118,119. Notably, recent studies indicate that SBP and DBP rely on distinct physiological descriptors, suggesting that target-specific modeling may be necessary for optimal performance57,93.

Overall, data-driven models offer significant advantages for continuous BP monitoring. They can integrate heterogeneous data sources and adapt over time by learning from new observations, allowing them to track evolving physiological conditions and maintain stable performance in long-term use. When embedded into wearable devices, these models enable real-time BP estimation, providing a practical approach for managing hypertension and other cardiovascular conditions outside traditional clinical settings. Despite these benefits, their performance remains highly dependent on data quality and diversity, and their generalizability is often restricted to populations similar to those represented in the training datasets. Moreover, issues of transparency and interpretability are frequently overlooked, limiting the clinical adoption of DL-based methods.

ABP waveform reconstruction models aim to estimate the full ABP waveform, which SBP and DBP can be derived. Recovering the complete waveform provides richer hemodynamic information—including pulse pressure, arterial stiffness, and beat-to-beat variability—which cannot be captured by scalar BP estimation alone. This makes waveform-based approaches particularly valuable for continuous, cuffless cardiovascular monitoring.

A variety of architectures have been proposed for this task. Reconstruction-based models include deep convolutional autoencoders140, GRNN-based models141, recurrent networks such as LSTMs90 and BiGRU-attention variants142, as well as temporal convolutional networks and Informer-style long-sequence forecasters143,144. A second family of methods adopts generative modeling, including GAN-based formulations54 and diffusion models145. A third line of research employs attention-based and Transformer architectures142,146. Unlike traditional feature-engineering pipelines, these models learn hierarchical representations directly from raw signals, eliminating the need for handcrafted features and enabling the extraction of morphology-rich patterns automatically.

Although these models demand large, high-quality datasets and are sensitive to motion and alignment artifacts, their ability to reconstruct full beat-to-beat waveforms offers richer hemodynamic information than point-wise regression methods, positioning waveform reconstruction as a rapidly advancing component of cuffless BP monitoring.

Evaluation of BP estimation models

Validation practices varied widely across the reviewed studies, as summarized in Table 2. A significant portion of the publications are subject-specific (n = 34 out of 86; 39.1%), highlighting the need for standardized validation methodologies to assess potential issues such as data leakage, bias, and generalization. In calibration-free methods (n = 44 out of 86; 50%), generalization is expected, however, subject-wise splits and subject-wise leave-out-cross validation results should be presented carefully. Moreover, hold-out methods should be clearly reported to contextualize the generalizability of the results.

Several validation techniques have been reported in BP estimation studies, with hold-out methods being the most frequently employed (n = 48 out of 86; 55.2%), followed by cross-validation (CV) approaches, including k-fold, leave-one-out CV (LOOCV), and nested CV (n = 38 out of 86; 44%). Prior to 2023, these methods were more evenly distributed. However, since 2023, CV-based strategies have become more prevalent (n = 22 out of 43; 42.3%), reflecting a shift toward more rigorous and generalizable evaluation practices. However, ensuring proper subject stratification remains crucial to prevent information leakage and reduce bias.

Notwithstanding, 34 publications conducted subject-wise validation. Of these, 7 LOOCV, 2 used leave-one-out, 13 applied hold-out validation, 9 used CV, and 4 performed subject-specific calibration. This indicates that 65.8% (48 out of 73) of studies performing CV or hold-out methods did not employ subject stratification. This might generate better-performing models, but in such scenarios, information leakage introduces bias, which is why the results cannot generalized. Furthermore, 12 publications did not report the ratio of their train-test split, 4 performed random splitting, and 13 applied a sample-wise split rather than a subject-wise approach. Additionally, 4 publications lacked sufficient detail about their data splitting strategy, introducing ambiguity regarding the validity of their evaluation methods.

Authors 34,50,51,79,81,85,91 performed subject-stratified calibration steps for the evaluation of their proposed method. Guo et al.83 validated their novel calibration-free, subject-wise approach by comparing its BP values with those acquired from a reference aneroid sphygmomanometer. Nonetheless, this method is not suitable for real-time or continuous monitoring. Authors 32 used correlation analysis and individual estimation to validate their methodology.

Furthermore, for calibration-based algorithms it is imperative to conduct static and dynamic tests. Static tests assess the baseline conditions, whereas dynamic tests 72 replicate real-world BP variation, guaranteeing the model’s flexibility. Publications such as, 51,79,81,85,89,91,109 conduct both the tests. Additionally, some works26,49,79,85,87,89,91,116,127,147,148 have started to conduct long-term studies, ranging from a few days to months, to evaluate the stability and reliability of their methods. Such comprehensive validation techniques ensure a detailed understanding of the model’s performance in real application. In calibration and recalibration scenarios, it is important to highlight that validated oscillometric devices are typically used, as manual auscultation is impractical. Given the variability in readings between oscillometric device brands and models, and the fact that calibration-based algorithms track BP changes relative to the calibration point, the choice of calibration device significantly impacts BP estimation accuracy 149. Moreover, for calibration-free algorithms validation among non-normotensive populations crucial to ensure effectiveness across diverse BP ranges. However, despite claims of superiority, several studies researches 77,91,99,116,141 fail to demonstrate applicability across all populations.

Beyond the choice of validation methodology, the standards and performance metrics used to assess BP estimation algorithms are equally critical. There are well-established standards for evaluating automatic cuff-based BP measurement devices, including the American National Standards Institute (ANSI)/American Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation (AAMI) SP 10-1987 standard150, IEEE Standard 1708a-2019151 and British Hypertension Society (BHS) protocol152, as shown in Fig. 2c. These standards clearly define the criteria for reporting and visualizing results. ANSI/AAMI recommends reporting mean deviation ± standard deviation (MD ± SD), whereas the BHS protocol emphasizes the cumulative error percentage (CP) at 5, 10 and 15 mmHg (CP5, CP10, and CP15). To provide a consistent and comparable evaluation, IEEE Standard 1708-2014 introduced a new grading system based on mean absolute difference (MAD), consistent with AAMI and BHS standards reporting. It recommends using multiple metrics, including MAD, mean absolute percentage difference (MAPD), MD ± SD, CP5, CP10, and CP15.

Additionally, IEEE Standard 1708a-2019151 recommends using graphical tools such as Bland-Altman plots, scatter plots, and BP histograms. Bland-Altman plots are vital for identifying systematic biases and defining limits of agreement, ensuring cuffless BP readings align with standard cuff-based measurements. Scatter plots of predicted versus true BP values visualize model accuracy and highlight potential inconsistencies or biases. Moreover, separate histograms of BP changes and population demographics provide crucial insights into model performance. Histograms of BP changes illustrate the distribution and stability of BP readings over time, showing how well the device tracks fluctuations in BP. Meanwhile, histograms of population demographics, such as age, gender, or health status, reveal whether the cuffless BP estimation models generalize well across different groups. This distinction ensures that the device performs accurately and consistently, not only within specific demographic groups but also across a broad patient population. Studies such as149 highlight the importance of these visualizations in validating both the accuracy and clinical applicability of cuffless BP devices. However, these protocols are designed specifically for validating automatic cuff devices and are unsuitable for cuffless BP devices. Cuffless devices present unique validation challenges, which have only recently been acknowledged in the field149,153.

This heterogeneity fundamentally biases error metrics and makes them non-comparable. To formally characterize the methodological sources of this heterogeneity, we developed a structured five-domain risk-of-bias (RoB) framework adapted from QUADAS-2155 and PROBAST156. The framework covered sample size adequacy, BP spectrum reporting, reference BP standard, calibration/evaluation clarity, and validation independence. Each study was rated as Low, Unclear, or High risk of bias in each domain, with overall RoB determined by the worst-domain rule. The distribution of domain-level and overall RoB ratings is shown in Fig. 8. Validation independence was the most problematic domain: no study achieved low risk in this domain, while 63% were rated as unclear risk and 37% as high risk. Consequently, no study achieved low overall RoB, reflecting the absence of fully independent external validation cohorts with complete methodological reporting. This converts the previously qualitative concerns regarding sample-wise splitting, unclear validation, and non-subject-wise evaluation into a structured study-level assessment. Stratification by overall RoB further supports this interpretation (Table 3): studies rated at high overall risk of bias yielded a pooled SBP ME of 1.68 mmHg [0.84, 2.51], compared with 0.39 mmHg [−0.16, 0.94] for studies rated at unclear risk. The point estimate was therefore more than four-fold higher in high-RoB studies, with only limited CI overlap, suggesting that a weaker methodological design is associated with larger signed bias.

The absence of strong evidence for classical publication bias suggests that the main limitation is not selective publication of positive findings, but rather the prevalence of small, underpowered, and methodologically heterogeneous studies. This directly motivates our proposal for standardized reporting. Overall, at a minimum, studies should clearly describe the dataset and population, specify the validation protocol (e.g., subject-wise vs. record-wise), and report standard performance metrics with uncertainty. Systematic reporting of these items would reduce optimistic interpretation, make cross-study comparisons more transparent, and provide a more reliable basis for judging whether a model’s performance is likely to generalize beyond the original cohort.

Discussion

Despite reported errors overlapping with cuff-based validation thresholds, current cuffless BP systems remain fragile in real-world settings. Many studies rely on non-ambulatory data, narrow BP distributions, or evaluation choices that risk data leakage and optimistic bias. Such data, which are often ICU, laboratory, or static seated-rest recordings, do not capture the motion, posture changes, behavioral factors, and autonomic fluctuations that dominate daily life. Weber-Boisvert et al.157 showed that PPG morphology and feature–BP relationships differ substantially between ICU and controlled environments, leading to sharp drops in cross-dataset performance when models are transported across settings. Similarly, Cisnal et al.93 quantified a marked performance gap between in-clinic and ambulatory recordings on the Aurora-BP cohort: despite robust Markov-blanket feature selection and nested CV, ambulatory models exhibited significantly higher errors and frequently failed to meet IEEE/AAMI criteria.

These findings suggest that the performance gap is not only driven by differences in protocol realism, but also by a breakdown of the physiological assumptions underlying cuffless BP estimation. Most approaches rely on surrogate signals such as PPG, which measure peripheral blood volume rather than arterial pressure, leading to an indirect and non-unique mapping to BP158–160. In ambulatory settings, this relationship becomes highly non-stationary due to motion artifacts161, sensor contact variability162, and hydrostatic effects163–165, which alter waveform morphology and therefore degrade the reliability of PWA-based features, independently of systemic BP. Simultaneously, autonomic regulation during daily activities induces rapid changes in vascular tone, altering peripheral resistance in ways that are only partially reflected in PPG, as it captures microvascular dynamics influenced by both local and systemic mechanisms166–168. In addition, timing-based biomarkers such as PAT are further confounded by variations in pre-ejection period (PEP), which varies with sympathetic activity and breaks the assumed monotonic relationship with BP169,170. These effects collectively violate the core assumptions of both hemodynamic models (stable physiological relationships) and some data-driven models (stationary signal–BP mappings), leading to domain shift and reduced generalizability, consistent with the degradation observed when models trained on controlled datasets (e.g., MIMIC) are applied to ambulatory cohorts171,172. In line with these findings, real-world reliability remains limited—particularly in ambulatory, hypertensive, elderly, and medicated cohorts—so current systems cannot yet be considered medical-grade without rigorous, standard-conformant validation across diverse populations and conditions.

In this context, inconsistent reporting of subject characteristics, lack of standardized metrics, and insufficient validation protocols severely limit the broader validity and comparability of current models. To address these gaps, we consolidate our findings into a structured BP-Card, a model-card-inspired framework that operationalises reporting and evaluation requirements for cuffless BP machine learning studies173. The BP-Card is designed to complement the proposed three-axis taxonomy: whereas the taxonomy classifies methods according to estimation family, modeling paradigm, and calibration strategy, the BP-Card translates these distinctions into structured reporting requirements. In particular, the estimation family and modeling paradigm define the methodological identity and intended use of the approach, while the calibration axis determines the appropriate validation and reporting pathway, including the distinction between calibration-free and calibration-based evaluation. The BP-Card is organized into modules covering clinical purpose and intended use, dataset and cohort characteristics, validation strategy, and standardized reporting for both calibration-free and calibration-based models, with explicit attention to bias, fairness-relevant cohort reporting, and equity. For cuffless BP systems, intended use strongly affects both the appropriate study design and the interpretation of reported performance. Studies should therefore clearly state the estimation family, modeling paradigm, input and output variables, signal modality, sensor placement, and intended deployment context, such as research use only, home or ambulatory monitoring, or adjunctive clinical use. This information is essential to distinguish fundamentally different modeling objectives and to assess whether the reported validation strategy and performance claims are appropriate for the proposed application. In this sense, Fig. 9 Module 0 operationalises the first two taxonomy axes by documenting what type of method is being studied and in what clinical context it is intended to be used.

Dataset characteristics are a primary determinant of the reliability and generalizability of cuffless BP estimation models. Across the reviewed literature, variability in sample size, BP distribution, and cohort composition substantially limits the comparability of reported results. Proper documentation of the dataset, including sample size and BP distribution, is critical for evaluating model performance. For calibration-based algorithms, documenting BP changes from the calibration point is equally important. Demographic information (age, sex, and medical history) should also be included to better understand the study cohort. Furthermore, post-data cleaning details, such as the amount of removed data or BP distribution before and after cleaning, are often omitted. Clear reporting of cleaning details is essential for reliable comparisons, especially in continuous ABP monitoring with highly variable signal quality. Standardized reporting is essential to advance the field. Fig. 9 Module 1 summarizes these dataset and cohort reporting requirements within the proposed BP-Card. The effect of anti-hypertensive drugs on BP measures validity with cuffless systems has never been investigated and requires dedicated studies and transparent reporting as the main beneficiary users of the technology. In particular, the ongoing medications which affect BP levels, such as diuretics, beta-blockers, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin II antagonists, mineralocorticoid drugs, alpha blockers and antagonists ideally should be reported inclusive of total daily dosage and regimens. Recent studies have highlighted that beyond dataset size and diversity, the choice and extraction of physiological features strongly influence performance. In the systematic analysis of PPG features in174, it was demonstrated that clear correlations with BP dynamics, underscoring the need to standardize feature-level reporting alongside dataset descriptors.

Validation is a central determinant of whether cuffless BP algorithms can be trusted. Across the reviewed literature, study sizes range from single-subject to cohorts with hundreds of participants, leading to substantial variability in robustness and generalizability. Both calibration-free and calibration-based models face reliability concerns: the former often lack validation across diverse BP distributions, and the latter frequently neglect significant BP variations from the calibration point. This heterogeneity is reflected in the wide dispersion of reported accuracy metrics (e.g., MAD: 0.018–11.3 mmHg, MD: −3.38 to 15.42 mmHg), underscoring the urgent need for standardized validation and transparent validation protocols. Calibration-based algorithms should not be evaluated solely under static conditions shortly after calibration. If models are calibrated and tested on similar resting measurements from the same individual, the evaluation does not reflect clinical use. Instead, validation must include dynamic conditions that induce BP changes from the calibration point, as well as assessments after a temporal delay to capture drift and physiological variability. IEEE Std 1708a-2019151 recommends inducing BP changes from the calibration point within predefined ranges: [−30, 30] mmHg for SBP and [-20, 20] mmHg for DBP. The total number of data points for accuracy analysis is calculated as: ntotal = 255 + (3 × nadd), where nadd = n − 85 represents the extra subjects required due to correlation effects in Phase 1. Each additional subject contributes 3 measurements, increasing the total sample size. Dynamic tests may involve altering device positioning, administering medications, sleep-wake monitoring, or following exercise175. In contrast, calibration-free algorithms are designed to generalize across subjects without calibration. Instead of dynamic or long-term evaluations, they require datasets covering a broad BP spectrum. According to IEEE Std 1708a-2019151, this includes at least 85 subjects, with a distribution of 21 subjects each for normotension, prehypertension, stage 1 HTN, and stage 2 HTN. Across both paradigms, evaluations should prioritize subject-level metrics to reduce bias and enhance generalizability, as sample-level metrics may disproportionately favor subjects with more data. LOOCV further ensures robust assessment by testing models on unseen individuals.

While CV is useful during development, blind testing on independent datasets is crucial for evaluating real-world generalizability, especially in continuous ABP monitoring, where dynamic conditions and noise may impact accuracy. The evaluation methodology (e.g., k-fold CV, train-test split) must be clearly defined. Finally, recent consensus recommendations for PPG-based BP estimation emphasize harmonized validation frameworks, including transparent reporting of preprocessing pipelines, fiducial point selection, and evaluation protocols176. Integrating such signal-level guidance into broader IEEE or ISO-aligned validation standards would substantially improve methodological consistency across the field.

Additionally, the evaluating of baseline models is highly recommended. For calibration-based approaches, baseline predictions are typically obtained using each subject’s calibration BP value, whereas calibration-free approaches uses the population-average BP values and/or demographic information when such variables are included in the model149. This comparison is particularly important because BP variability differs substantially across datasets. Models evaluated on datasets with narrow BP distributions may achieve apparently good error metrics despite limited generalization capability. Baseline comparisons therefore help determine whether the reported performance truly reflects the contribution of physiological signal processing. This issue has been emphasized in analyses of the AuroraBP project48,153, showing that the absence of baseline comparisons can lead to misleading interpretations of model performance. Therefore, reporting baseline model results provides a more transparent and rigorous assessment of BP estimation accuracy and clarifies the added value of the proposed methodology. To support consistent reporting, Fig. 9 Module 2 provides a table with the essential data elements needed to fully describe the validation strategy.

Adherence to established performance metrics advocated by the BHS, IEEE, and ANSI/AAMI is essential for evaluating BP estimation algorithm. The advantage of employing MD ± SD is that it incorporates all measurement results, but it can be influenced by outliers. Conversely, cumulative percentages are less influenced by outliers, but could yield misleading results in the presence of substantial systematic differences. To address this, the IEEE proposed using MAD alongside established metrics. However, the choice of the metric should be distribution-aware. RMSE is the maximum-likelihood and most informative summary when residuals are approximately Gaussian, whereas MAD is optimal under Laplacian (heavy-tailed) errors. If residuals depart markedly from both, robust alternatives or transformations are preferable177. Additionally, MAPD was also proposed to account for variability at higher BP levels, which inevitably leads to larger errors. Using relative differences helps reduce the impact of BP level on measurement accuracy. Although, many reviewed articles present MAD ± SD, this practice is redundant, as MAD already captures the average deviation between predicted and observed values. Hence, for a comprehensive presentation of results, it is advisable to include MD ± SD, CP5, CP10, CP15, MAD, MAPD and R. None of the reviewed studies provided all the aforementioned metrics simultaneously. Without diligent adherence to a standardized reporting protocol, comparative analysis and benchmarking of existing algorithms become markedly challenging.

In the proposed BP-Card, Fig. 9 Module 3A summarizes performance reporting for calibration-free models, including a standardized reporting table and recommended visualization formats for absolute BP agreement in (c) and (d). 9 Module 3B summarizes performance reporting for calibration-based models, including a standardized reporting table and recommended visualization formats for calibration-relative agreement in (e) and (f). For calibration-free algorithms, both overall performance and performance across BP groups should be reported. For calibration-based algorithms, performance should be reported under static and dynamic conditions and, where applicable, across time since calibration. Baseline results should also be included in both modules to support transparent comparison.

Appropriate visualization is essential for comprehensive and transparent BP estimation performance. For dataset reporting, histograms are particularly effective for illustrating the distribution of BP baseline in the study cohort as depicted in Fig. 9a. These histograms should include both the training and test set distributions, clearly highlighting the mean ± SD for each. Calibration-based studies should also include histograms, as shown in Fig. 9b, which depicts BP changes from calibration for both the training and testing sets151. To visualize results, we recommend employing Bland-Altman and scatter plots, as per IEEE standard151. The Bland-Altman plots illustrate the difference between cuffless and reference BP against their mean, and require explicit verification that these differences follow an approximately normal distribution. Additionally, the limits of agreement should be accompanied by their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) to properly quantify the uncertainty around the mean difference and the agreement boundaries. Scatter plot, shown in Fig. 9c illustrates the relationship between changes in cuffless BP and reference BP relative to calibration, emphasizing individual variability in response to calibration. Figure 9d examines BP predictions using population average BP, also excluding cuffless measurements, to assess the reliability of non-individualized baseline approaches. Additionally, for calibration-based algorithms, scatter plots showing cuffless BP minus calibration BP versus reference BP minus calibration BP are valuable. Figure 9e illustrates the correlation strength between cuffless BP and reference BP, showing the overall agreement between these two measurement approaches. Figure 9f highlights BP prediction based solely on calibration BP without using cuffless hemodynamic measurements, serving as a baseline comparison. Together, these visualizations offer insights into the performance and reliability of cuffless BP estimation compared to baseline models and population averages. Including scatter plots that integrate baseline models is also suggested, with all plots reporting the correlation coefficient for a more comprehensive analysis149.

While Fig. 9 defines the BP-Card as a generic reporting template, Fig. 10 illustrates how the framework can be operationalised in practice based on the work of Podder et al.178. Figure 10 illustrates the Model Card for calibrated algorithms: clinical purpose and use context Fig. 10(Module 0), dataset and cohort characteristics, including pre- and post-cleaning BP distributions Fig. 10(Module 1), and validation strategy with baseline model specification Fig. 10(Module 2), and condition-stratified calibration-based performance reporting (Module 3B). The BP-Card is designed to standardize the reporting of methods, metrics, cohort characteristics, and evaluation conditions; by doing so, it also enables structured comparison across studies and helps determine which approach is most appropriate for a given clinical context. The worked example makes this concrete. Figure 10 Module 1 requires reporting of cohort characteristics and BP distributions before and after data cleaning, showing that 20 participants were excluded during quality checks and that the post-cleaning cohort shifted toward lower mean SBP (122.7 vs. 128.1 mmHg) and a lower proportion of stage 2 hypertension (10.0% vs. 18.4%); without this transparency, performance comparisons across studies may reflect favorable data selection rather than genuine algorithmic differences. Module 2 mandates baseline-comparator reporting across input configurations, revealing a clear performance ranking: PPG alone (MAD 11.24 ± 10.53 mmHg), ECG alone (MAD 10.45 ± 10.70 mmHg), PPG + ECG (MAD 9.67 ± 9.52 mmHg), and PPG + ECG + Demographics (MAD 9.05 ± 9.02 mmHg). Without this systematic comparison, it would not be possible to quantify the incremental gain of each added modality—information that is essential when selecting among competing approaches for a given application. Module 3B requires results to be reported separately by testing condition, showing that accuracy is lower under dynamic ambulatory conditions (MAD 8.61 ± 8.28 mmHg, CP5 44.92%) than under static in-clinic settings (MAD 8.08 ± 8.43 mmHg, CP5 50.55%); this disaggregation ensures that a device’s suitability can be judged for the specific operating context in which it will be deployed, rather than based on a single pooled metric. Together, these observations illustrate that clinically relevant performance differences, modality-specific contributions, and dataset shifts are not apparent from headline summary metrics alone, and instead become visible only when structured, standardized disclosure is applied—enabling the community to compare methods on equal terms and identify which is best suited for a given clinical use case.

Beyond methodological heterogeneity, our supplementary model-card analysis further revealed substantial deficiencies in reporting transparency, even among studies that theoretically fulfilled current IEEE and/or AAMI validation criteria. Among the 86 reviewed studies, only 19 reported performance levels that theoretically satisfied at least one of the IEEE and/or AAMI validation standards. After excluding studies with fewer than 85 participants, only 12 publications remained (7 calibration-free94,103,129,144–146,179 and 5 calibration-based95,106,121,127,147). The corresponding model cards are provided in the Supplementary Material. Key dataset characteristics are frequently underreported particularly after data preprocessing, including participant count, total paired BP samples and BP distributions. As a result, the true predictive difficulty of the task and the real robustness of the proposed models often remain unclear. This issue is particularly critical because BP variability differs substantially across datasets, meaning that apparently favorable error metrics may simply reflect narrow BP distributions rather than strong predictive capability. Among the five calibration-based studies, only two reported subject-specific ΔBP distributions. Importantly, these studies showed markedly different variability ranges, with reported SBP/DBP ΔBP values of approximately 26/18 mmHg in one case versus 34/21 mmHg in another, illustrating how models evaluated under broader BP fluctuations likely face a substantially more challenging prediction task. However, such variability information is rarely disclosed, limiting meaningful cross-study comparison. Similarly, only one calibration-based study evaluated performance under dynamic conditions and only one additional study assessed model stability over time after calibration, despite both dynamic and long-term testing being essential for assessing robustness under realistic BP fluctuations and prolonged operation. Likewise, in calibration-free approaches, no study reported population-based BP distributions, and only one stratified performance across BP profiles. Notably, none of the 86 reviewed studies reported baseline model results. We only found one study71 that explicitly incorporated baseline model comparisons, demonstrating that several hemodynamic models, particularly PAT-based approaches, did not provide statistically significant improvements over calibration-only baseline predictions. These findings further reinforce the importance of baseline models for contextualizing performance and distinguishing genuine physiological signal contribution from population priors or demographic information. The corresponding model card is also provided in the Supplementary Material.

Taken together, these findings indicate that current limitations are not restricted to methodological heterogeneity, but also involve insufficient transparency in dataset characterization and evaluation reporting. Without standardized disclosure, it remains difficult to determine whether reported performances reflect clinically meaningful predictive capability or favorable dataset characteristics. This lack of transparency also hinders fair comparison across studies and slows progress toward robust cuffless BP systems suitable for real-world deployment. Finally, it is noteworthy that nearly all reviewed studies were conducted under in-clinic or controlled conditions, while truly ambulatory evaluations remain scarce. This represents a major translational gap, as the primary motivation for cuffless BP monitoring is precisely its potential for continuous real-world and ambulatory assessment, where conventional cuff-based devices remain impractical despite their superior accuracy in controlled settings. The importance of this limitation has already been demonstrated in previous work93, where statistically significant performance degradation was observed between in-clinic and ambulatory measurements, even when evaluated on the same subjects, further emphasizing the difficulty of robust real-world BP estimation. The model card for this study is also included in the Supplementary Material.

Our recommendations below focus on reducing this fragility through dataset breadth, subject-wise evaluation, baselines, and transparent reporting. Before discussing fairness-relevant findings, we distinguish between two related but distinct activities. A formal fairness audit evaluates whether model performance differs systematically across demographic or clinical subgroups and therefore requires subgroup-stratified error metrics, such as MAD or ME by skin tone, sex, age, BP stratum, comorbidity status, or medication use. A demographic and subgroup reporting-gap analysis, by contrast, evaluates whether the primary literature reports the cohort descriptors and subgroup-level performance information required to conduct such an audit. Because subgroup-stratified performance metrics were reported in only a small minority of the 86 reviewed studies, the prerequisites for a quantitative fairness audit are not met in the current literature. The analysis reported below should therefore be interpreted as a reporting-gap analysis: it characterizes the field’s readiness to support fairness evaluation rather than measuring algorithmic disparities directly. This distinction is important because the absence of subgroup-level evidence is itself a methodological limitation and directly motivates the fairness-relevant fields of the BP-Card.

Bias, fairness-relevant reporting, and equity are central to the clinical viability of cuffless BP estimation. Optical and wearable signals are affected by skin tone, contact pressure, adiposity, motion, and ambient light, which can alter signal-to-noise and downstream accuracy. Several systematic risk factors emerge from the reviewed literature: (i) Skewed datasets (ICU/healthy vs. ambulatory hypertensive) can bias models toward normotension. (ii) Skin-dependent light absorption and sensor contact force alter PPG morphology. (iii) Sex, age, and arterial stiffness modify PWV relationships. (iv) Antihypertensive medications (e.g., beta-blockers, diuretics) influence autonomic and vascular tone. From the 86 studies reviewed, only one reported skin tone without using the Fitzpatrick scale106 and two documented ethnicity106,120. Performance was stratified by sex in three papers103,106,120, by age in two49,103, and by hypertension stage in five49,93. Additionally, only one study vaguely reported antihypertensive medication status49. To mitigate these sources of bias and to enable future fairness audits, we recommend that future studies explicitly document cohort characteristics relevant to fairness and equity, including skin tone (e.g., Fitzpatrick I–VI), sex, age, BMI/arm circumference, ethnicity/region, contact pressure or strap tightness, sensor location, ambient light, activity state, and antihypertensive medication class, dose, and regimen. Model evaluation should move beyond aggregated performance and report subject-wise, stratified results across BP categories (following IEEE 1708a recommendations), using standardized metrics (MD ± SD, MAD, CP5, CP10, CP15, MAPD, and R). Priority should also be given to public benchmarks with broader diversity (e.g., PulseDB, MIMIC-BP, Aurora-BP), with transparent reporting of inclusion and exclusion criteria and their impact on fairness-relevant evaluation. In this context, multimodal sensing strategies have shown promise: combining multiple PPG modalities has been demonstrated to improve hypertension detection, particularly in populations where single-site PPG is unreliable, and may help mitigate bias arising from skin tone, contact pressure, or motion artifacts180.

Out-of-office BP assessment improves cardiovascular risk stratification beyond clinic readings, particularly among those whose in-office BP levels are assumed to carry no or tiny increased cardiovascular risk9,10. Reflecting this evidence, the 2017 ACC/AHA and the 2018 ESC/ESH guidelines recommend incorporating home or ambulatory BP monitoring to confirm HTN and to guide titration of therapy, including in conjunction with telehealth support7,8. Cuffless monitoring can extend these benefits by reducing discomfort, sleep disturbance, and adherence barriers associated with repeated cuff inflations. These advantages are especially relevant for longitudinal monitoring, nocturnal profiling, and pediatric settings where conventional devices are poorly tolerated. By enabling frequent, passive measurements, cuffless wearables offer a practical route to earlier detection, tighter control, and more personalized management of HTN.

Realizing this clinical potential, however, requires methodological rigor that ensures reproducible and clinically trustworthy results. Prior work indicates that features derived from PPG track BP dynamics, underscoring the need for systematic and transparent reporting of feature sets and preprocessing choices176. Consensus guidance further highlights consistent fiducial point selection and robust validation strategies, while complementary evidence shows that multimodal approaches can improve accuracy when single-site PPG is unreliable180 and ECG features add robustness and interpretability for HTN assessment181. Together, these findings motivate the need for a unified signal-domain consensus that can be embedded within broader evaluation frameworks.

At the same time, evidence standards must align with the intended clinical use: beyond subject-independent internal validation, studies should include external testing across diverse BP ranges and populations, explicitly sampling older adults, individuals with increased arterial stiffness, and patients with multimorbidity, groups that remain underrepresented in current validations. Protocols should induce dynamic BP changes (e.g., posture, activity, or medication timing) and report performance by BP stratum. Ultimately, randomized controlled trials powered for clinical outcomes are needed to determine whether cuffless monitoring improves awareness, increases the likelihood of achieving guideline-recommended BP targets, and reduces adverse cardiovascular events compared with current standard care. In summary, cuffless BP monitoring is well positioned to complement guideline-endorsed out-of-office assessment, but translation into routine clinical practice depends on the adoption of robust, standardized evaluation frameworks and population-aware validation strategies. Community benchmarks that adopt these practices can accelerate safe clinical integration while ensuring benefits are realized equitably across heterogeneous patient groups.

In this review, we identify limited generalizability across populations and real-world conditions as a central limitation of current cuffless BP estimation methods. Our analysis shows that inconsistent cohort characterization, heterogeneous performance reporting, and insufficient validation protocols remain major barriers to clinical translation. In particular, the lack of standardized reporting regarding BP distributions, calibration procedures, baseline comparisons, and validation under realistic ambulatory conditions makes it difficult to determine the true robustness and predictive capability of many published models. Based on current evidence, we distinguish the two dominant paradigms in BP estimation (calibration-free and calibration-based approaches) and clarify their distinct methodological requirements in terms of population diversity, validation strategy, and performance reporting. Beyond summarizing existing approaches, this review highlights critical methodological limitations that are frequently overlooked, including the absence of baseline-model evaluation, insufficient characterization of BP variability, and the limited use of ambulatory validation protocols. Building on this synthesis, we consolidate current knowledge into a structured set of practical recommendations for dataset characterization, evaluation design, and performance reporting through the proposed BP-card reporting framework. We believe that the adoption of harmonized validation standards and transparent reporting practices is essential to improve reproducibility, enable fair comparison across studies, and accelerate the development of clinically meaningful cuffless BP monitoring technologies suitable for continuous real-world and ambulatory use.

Methods

Systematic literature search and study selection

A systematic review on noninvasive, continuous cuffless BP estimation methods was conducted, following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Systematic searches were conducted in PubMed (MEDLINE) and IEEExplore. The inclusion criteria consisted of English, full-text articles published in peer-reviewed journals between January 2017 and March 2025, focusing on noninvasive, continuous cuffless BP estimation methods. Only original research articles were included, while review and perspective pieces were excluded. Studies were also excluded if they lacked methodological details on data collection or BP estimation algorithms, such as those focused primarily on electronic design or signal processing techniques. Two reviewers screened the records to determine whether a study met the inclusion/exclusion criteria based on its title and abstract. Subsequently, two independent reviewers screened the records based on the full text. The search terms used included: continuous, cuffless, photoplethysmogram, blood pressure, estimation, algorithm, and validation. Detailed database-specific search commands are provided in Supplementary Note 2.

A total of 284 records were identified through database searches, of which 74 were duplicates. After title and abstract screening, 69 records were excluded. Full-text assessment led to the removal of 55 additional articles, resulting in 86 studies being included in the final systematic review (Fig. 11).

Meta-analysis

We quantitatively pooled SBP mean error (ME; positive = overestimation) using random-effects meta-analysis. After applying the inclusion criteria (sample size reporting and non-subject-specific reporting) and excluding entries with missing mean or SE, the final pooled set comprised k = 46 effects. For each effect, we recorded the validation protocol (subject-wise, record-wise, LOOCV, etc.), sensor modality, and reference device.

Although the included studies vary in sensor modality, dataset, reference device, calibration protocol, and validation design, all studies estimate the same underlying construct: the signed bias (ME) of a cuffless BP estimator relative to a reference measurement. Pooling is therefore conceptually justified. We used a random-effects model, which does not assume a single common true effect but instead treats each study as estimating a draw from a distribution of true effects, with the variance of that distribution (τ2) estimated from the data. Under this framework, between-study heterogeneity is a quantity to be estimated and interpreted, not a violation of an assumption. The high I2 values observed throughout are accordingly reported as a substantive finding about the consistency of the literature, not as a pooling artifact. Pooled effects were estimated using DerSimonian–Laird (DL) random-effects (RE) models for the overall set and for a priori validation subgroups (subject-wise vs. record-wise). Sensitivity to extreme values was examined via (i) trimming (∣ME∣ ≤ 5 mmHg) and (ii) Winsorizing at 5 mmHg. To assess the impact of between-study variance, we also report pooled effects obtained using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) with Hartung–Knapp (HK) small-sample adjustment (REML+HK), which provides more conservative confidence intervals under high heterogeneity. Small-study and selective-reporting biases were examined using Egger’s regression test, the precision-effect test (PET), and the precision-effect estimate with standard error (PEESE), each estimated with heteroscedasticity-consistent HC3 covariance.

Risk-of-bias assessment

We developed a five-domain framework adapted from QUADAS-2155 and PROBAST156, using the Low/Unclear/High rating scale. The five domains are: (i) Sample size: number of independent participants; (ii) BP spectrum reporting: whether the study reports the BP distribution or range of the test cohort; (iii) Reference BP standard:invasive arterial or documented validated cuff measurement (Low); undocumented cuff measurement (Unclear); self-reported or unvalidated measurement (High); (iv) Calibration/evaluation clarity: whether the calibration procedure and evaluation protocol are described in sufficient detail to be reproducible; and (v) Validation independence: fully independent external test cohort (Low); subject-wise internal validation (Unclear); record-, window-, or beat-wise splits, or splits where participant-level separation cannot be confirmed (High). No study in our sample used a fully independent external test cohort, so no study was eligible for Low risk in this domain. The overall study-level rating used the worst-domain rule, following QUADAS-2 convention. Domains (i)–(iv) were rated from explicit metadata fields extracted during data abstraction; domain (v) was rated from the reported validation procedure. The resulting domain-level and overall RoB distributions are reported in Fig. 8.

Fairness-related extraction was limited to the availability of demographic descriptors, clinical subgroup variables, and subgroup-stratified performance reporting. Because subgroup-level performance data were sparse and inconsistently reported, this component was treated as a demographic and subgroup reporting-gap analysis rather than a formal fairness audit.
