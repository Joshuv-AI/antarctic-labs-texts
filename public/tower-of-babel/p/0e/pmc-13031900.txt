AT-101 inhibits the proliferation and invasion of tongue squamous carcinoma cells by targeting the miR-21-5p/FDX1 axis
Authors: Shuai Fu, Qing-ying Cui, Xiao-yu Tuo, Yong Wu, Ping Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) is an aggressive oral malignancy with an increasing incidence worldwide, however, the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying its progression still unclear. Existing studies demonstrate that microRNAs (miRNAs) play a critical role in TSCC progression. In particular, the regulatory relationship between the oncogenic miR-21-5p and the tumor suppressor gene FDX1, as well as its targeted effect on the natural compound AT-101, remains a clear research gap. In this study, CTD, dbMEMC, and TCGA databases were used to screen AT-101-related genes and the miR-21-5p/FDX1 regulatory axis, the expression patterns of which were validated in TSCC tissues and cell lines (HaCaT, HSC3, Cal27) using RT-qPCR and Western blot. Functional assays (cell proliferation, Transwell, wound healing) demonstrated that miR-21-5p promotes TSCC proliferation, migration, and invasion, and dual-luciferase reporter assays further confirmed FDX1 as its direct target. Additionally, AT-101 significantly down-regulated miR-21-5p, restored FDX1 expression, and suppressed TSCC cell growth. Mechanistic studies revealed that miR-21-5p drives TSCC progression by suppressing FDX1, while AT-101 reverses this effect by restoring FDX1 levels and modulating critical signaling pathways to inhibit tumor development. Collectively, the miR-21-5p/FDX1 axis plays a crucial regulatory role in TSCC, and AT-101 can inhibit the malignant phenotype of TSCC by interfering with this axis. Introduction

Oral cancer remains a significant global health burden, with more than 300,000 new cases annually and a 5-year survival rate ranging from 50% to 60%, resulting in approximately 145,400 deaths each year. Among these cases, tongue cancer accounts for approximately 40%, with tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) being the most common type1–3. Emerging epidemiological data indicate an increasing incidence of TSCC, highlighting its growing clinical significance4,5. TSCC is characterized by aggressive biological behaviour, with high rates of regional lymph node metastasis and local recurrence, which contribute to the poor prognosis of TSCC patients6. Current treatment strategies for TSCC primarily rely on surgical resection, often supplemented with adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy. However, the anatomical and functional complexity of the tongue poses significant challenges to surgical management. Excessive resection may lead to severe impairment of key functions such as speech, chewing, and swallowing, whereas insufficient resection increases the risk of residual disease and recurrence7. In addition, conventional chemotherapy regimens are limited by systemic toxicity, significant side effects, drug resistance, and limited therapeutic effectiveness, further emphasizing the need for novel targeted therapeutic strategies8. To address the limitations of existing treatments, research into novel pharmacological therapies has become a critical direction in TSCC management. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore safe and effective therapeutic agents to improve patient outcomes and reduce tumour progression.

Recent studies have found that microRNAs (miRNAs) are closely associated with the occurrence and development of TSCC. MiRNAs are evolutionarily conserved small RNAs (20–22 nucleotides in length) that lack protein-coding potential. miRNAs can negatively regulate gene expression after transcription by binding to complementary sequences on their target mRNAs9. Recent findings have shown that exosomal miRNAs derived from the serum of cancer patients are aberrantly expressed and, to some extent, reflect miRNA expression levels in solid tumours. Furthermore, exosomal miRNAs have been recognized as critical mediators of cancer-host crosstalk and are involved in cancer progression, such as the modulation of immune responses, tumour microenvironment remodelling, and premetastatic niche formation10,11. miR-21 functions as an oncogene in various human cancers, including ovarian cancer, non-small cell lung cancer, and colorectal cancer (CRC), where it promotes malignant biological behaviours by directly targeting multiple tumour suppressor genes. Additionally, studies have revealed that miR-21-5p is upregulated in gastric cancer tissues and SGC-7901 cells and that miR-21-5p knockdown inhibits cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and inflammatory responses12. These findings suggest that miR-21-5p may serve as a novel therapeutic target for TSCC. However, the role of miR-21-5p in TSCC and its underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

MiR-21-5p can regulate multiple target genes including Ferredoxin 1 (FDX1). FDX1 is a small iron-sulphur protein that transfers electrons from NADPH to mitochondrial cytochrome P450 via ferredoxin reductase (FDXR) and participates in the metabolism of steroids, cholesterol, and bile acid. FDX1 plays a crucial role in maintaining metabolic homeostasis13. A recent study demonstrated that FDX1 is involved in embryonic development in mice and that its deficiency can lead to altered lipid metabolism, steatohepatitis, and adenocarcinoma. In lung adenocarcinoma, FDX1 knockdown results in significant changes in metabolites involved in glucose metabolism, fatty acid oxidation, and amino acid metabolism14. Furthermore, we identified miR-21-5p as an upstream regulator that regulates FDX1 expression15. However, whether FDX1 participates in the miR-21-5p-mediated regulation of TSCC remains to be determined.

Gossypol is a natural polyphenolic aldehyde derived from pigments in cotton (Gossypium spp.) and is abundant in cottonseeds and their byproducts, such as cottonseed oil and cottonseed meal16. It has been reported to possess remarkable pharmacological properties, including antioxidant, antitumour, antiviral, and antibacterial activities17. Natural gossypol exists as a racemic mixture of two enantiomers, (+)-gossypol and (-)-gossypol (also known as AT-101), with varying proportions across different cotton populations16. AT-101 exhibits slower degradation kinetics, rendering it the more biologically active form18. Studies have demonstrated that AT-101 exhibits significant antitumour effects in multiple human cancer models, showing particularly promising anticancer activity in breast, colon, pancreatic, and prostate cancers. Furthermore, AT-101 is a natural Bcl-2 homeodomain 3 (BH3) mimic that acts as a small-molecule inhibitor to downregulate the expression of the antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2 and its associated proteins in human cancer cells. Additionally, AT-101 can upregulate the levels of pro-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bcl-XL/Bcl-Xs), thereby inhibiting the function of anti-apoptotic factors and promoting apoptosis19. Despite its therapeutic potential in various cancers and demonstrated antitumour efficacy in clinical studies, the therapeutic effects of AT-101 in TSCC and the molecular mechanisms underlying its relationship with miR-21-5p/FDX1remain underexplored.

In this study, a joint analysis based on the CTD, TCGA, and dbMEMC databases was performed to screen key miRNA/mRNA regulatory axes. Among these genes, the negative correlation between miR-21-5p (significantly upregulated) and FDX1 (significantly downregulated) was most significant in human tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) cell lines. Validation in clinical tissue samples revealed abnormally elevated miR-21-5p expression and significantly reduced FDX1 mRNA and protein levels in TSCC tumour tissues, suggesting a potential role for this axis in TSCC progression. In vitro functional assays confirmed that miR-21-5p promotes Cal27 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by targeting and inhibiting FDX1, whereas FDX1 overexpression reverses these malignant phenotypes. A dual-luciferase reporter assay further confirmed that FDX1 is a direct target of miR-21-5p. Notably, AT-101 exerts its antitumour effects by inhibiting miR-21-5p expression and upregulatingFDX1 expression. This study elucidates the molecular mechanism through which AT-101 suppresses TSCC progression through the miR-21-5p/FDX1 signalling axis, providing new theoretical insights and potential intervention strategies for the prognostic evaluation and targeted therapy of TSCC.

Materials and methods

Tissue samples and clinical data

In this study, 12 patients with primary tongue squamous cell carcinoma who underwent surgical treatment and were histopathologically diagnosed at the Stomatological Hospital of Kunming Medical University between May 2021 and September 2022 were included. None of the patients received radiotherapy or chemotherapy prior to surgery. Twelve pairs of TSCC tissue samples and their matched adjacent noncancer tissue samples were collected; all samples were harvested within 20 min after resection and stored at -80 °C. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Stomatological Hospital of Kunming Medical University(#KYKQ2021MEC063,Kunming, Yunnan, China) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient.

Data collection

Genes related to the activity of AT-101 were queried and downloaded using the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD; http://ctdbase.org/). The dbMEMC database (https://www.biosino.org/dbDEMC/index) and the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) were used to analyse significantly differentially expressed miRNAs between human tongue squamous cell carcinoma tissues and normal tissues and to predict the target genes of these differentially expressed miRNAs.

Cell culture and transfection

HaCaT epithelial cells, Cal27 cells (BioVector NTCC Inc., Beijing), and HSC-3 cells (purchased from Sonar Biological, Beijing, China) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All the miR-21-5p inhibitors, miR-21-5p mimics, and negative controls (NCs) were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, the NCs, miR-21-5p inhibitors, and miR-21-5p mimics were transfected into Cal27 and HSC-3 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (GenePharma, China), and the transfection efficiency was detected by RT‒qPCR.

Reagents

AT-101 (Cat No.HY-15464 A) was purchased from MCE (Shanghai, China). Anti-FDX1 antibody was obtained from Proteintech (Cat No. 12592-1-AP, Wuhan, China).

Cell proliferation assay (CCK-8)

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well (100 µL of medium) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Following 0, 24, 48, and 72 h of treatment with various concentrations of AT-101, 10 µL of CCK-8reagent (Biosharp Life Sciences) was added to each well. After the cells were incubated for 2 h, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

EdU assay

Cells (2 × 10^4) were seeded in a 24-well plate and treated with 20 µmol/L AT-101 for 48 h. In accordance with the standard protocol, the cells were incubated with EdU solution for 2 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized for 10–15 min, and then incubated with Click reaction solution for 30 min. Nuclear staining was performed using Hoechst, and images were captured using a Nikon 80i fluorescence microscope. EdU-positive cells were counted in 10 random fields.

Wound healing assay

During the logarithmic growth phase, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10^6 cells per well. When the cells reached 90% confluence, sterile pipette tips were used to create scratches, ensuring a uniform wound width across all samples. After being washed twice with PBS, the cells were incubated in serum-free medium supplemented with varying concentrations of AT-101. Wound healing was monitored, and images of cell migration were captured at different time points.

Transwell assay

In the invasion assay, a 4 × 10^4 cell suspension (200 µL) was prepared, and the upper chamber was filled with transwell inserts precoated with Matrigel matrix, while the lower chamber was filled with 500 µL of complete medium. After incubation for 24 h, the cells were fixed with methanol for 20 min, washed with PBS, and stained for 35 min. After air-drying, the cells in 9 randomly selected fields under a microscope were counted.

RT‒qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. The absorbance values at 260 nm and 280 nm were measured using a UV spectrophotometer to calculate the concentration and purity. cDNA was synthesized and diluted, and qRT‒PCR was performed using the SYBR Green method, with U6 and GAPDH serving as internal controls. Relative RNA expression levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are listed in SupplementaryInformation1.

Protein extraction and Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from tissues using RIPA lysis buffer. After protein quantification, 20 µg of protein was subjected to SDS‒PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was incubated with the primary antibody (anti-FDX1) and secondary antibodies according to the standard protocol and visualized using chemiluminescence. Quantitative analysis was performed using Quantity One software.

Double-luciferase reporter gene experiment

Cal27 cells were inoculated into 24-well plates and cotransfected with the miR-21-5p knockout plasmid or its control plasmid and the FDX1 3’UTR wild-type (pGL3-FDX1 3’UTR-wt) or mutant (pGL3-FDX1 3’UTR-mut) reporter gene construct. After incubation for 48 h, the cell lysate was collected, and the detected luciferase activity was compared with that in the control group.

Lentiviruspackaging and cell lines

The pLVX-IRES-ZsPuro lentiviral expression vector (Clontech) containing FDX1 cDNA or FDX1 shRNA was purchased from Hunan Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The target sequence was GTCCACTTTATAAACCGTGAT. Lentivirus was produced by cotransfection of lentiviral packaging plasmids with psPAX2 and pMD2.G using Jet PRIME (PolyPlus) in 293FT cells following the manufacturer’s instructions. The medium was changed 24 h after transfection, and medium containing the virus was harvested after 72 h, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was used to infect Cal27 cells in the presence of 5 µg/ml polybrene (Sigma‒Aldrich, cat. H9268). The selection of resistant colonies was initiated 48 h later using 5 µg/mL puromycin (Life Technology, A1113803).

Xenograft model

BALB/c nude mice (6-8-week-old males weighing 20–25 g) were provided by BEIJING HFK BIOSCIENCE Co., Ltd. Five × 106 Cal27-CTRL, shFDX1, and FDX1 cells were subcutaneously inoculated onto the dorsal left thigh of the nude mice, and the tumour size was measured every 2 days to establish a xenograft model. In the shFDX1 group, the tumour volume reached approximately 400 mm3 (length*width2*0.5, where length represents the largest tumour diameter in centimetres and width represents the shortest tumour diameter). The tumours were bluntly separated for the following experiments. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kunming Medical University.

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed and visualized using R (4.2.1) software. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For multiple group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used when data were normally distributed and had equal variances; Welch’s one-way ANOVA was used when data were normally distributed but had unequal variances; and the Kruskal‒Wallis test was used when data were not normally distributed. For paired-sample comparisons, the paired ttest or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Multidatabase screening of AT-101–related genes and analysis of miRNA‒mRNA regulatory networks

To explore the mechanism by which AT-101 inhibits the proliferation of tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) cells, we queried and downloaded 85 genes interacting with AT-101 from the CTD database (Supplementary Dataset 1). Based on the miRNA microarray datasets GSE28100 and GSE137865 from the dbMEMC database, as well as the miRNA microarray dataset from the TCGA database (Supplementary Datasets 2–4), we screened for significantly differentially expressed miRNA molecules present in human TSCC tissues and normal tissues (Fig. 1A). Three miRNAs whose expression significantly differed were selected, namely, miR-21-5p, miR-378a-3p and miR-155-5p, among which miR-21-5p was the only miRNA common to all three datasets. TargetScan and miRanda software were used to predict the target genes of the significantly different miRNAs. There were 7 overlapping genes between the predicted target genes of miR-21-5p and the drug target of AT-10 (Fig. 1B), among which the TargetScan score of FDX1 and SLC2A1 was greater than 90. There were 9 genes that intersected between miR-378a-3p and AT-101 as the drug target (Fig. 1C); among them, BNIP3 and ELAC2 showed TargetScan scores greater than 90. There were 6 genes that intersected between miR-155-5p and AT-101 as the drug target (Fig. 1D); among them, only GYG1 had a TargetScan score greater than 90. Therefore, we verified the effects of miR-21-5p/FDX1, miR-21-5p/SLC2A1, miR-155-5p/GYG1, miR-378a-3p/BNIP3 and miR-378a-3p/ELAC2.

Validation of the miRNA‒mRNA regulatory axis in TSCC cell lines

To elucidate the role of the miRNA‒mRNA regulatory network in TSCC cell lines, we first screened key miRNA‒mRNA axes and used RT‒qPCR to detect their expression levels in TSCC cell lines (Cal27and HSC-3 cells) and normal oral keratinocytes (HaCaT cells). Compared with that in HaCaT cells, the expression of miR-21-5p in Cal27 and HSC-3 cells was significantly upregulated, while that of its target gene FDX1 was significantly downregulated (Fig. 2A-C). The expression levels of miR-21-5p and FDX1 were negatively correlated in Cal27 and HSC-3 cells but not in HaCaTcells (Fig. 2D-F), which was consistent with the miRNA–mRNA regulatory network predicted by multiple databases. Notably, the expression level of another potential target gene, SLC2A1, was positively correlated with that of miR-21-5p in Cal27 cells and negatively correlated with that of miR-21-5p in HaCaT and HSC-3 cells (Fig. 2G-I). This trend of upregulation in cancer cells (Fig. 2A-C) was inconsistent with the negative regulatory relationship, suggesting that miR-21-5p may not directly regulate SLC2A1. In addition, we further analysed other candidate miRNA‒mRNA regulatory axes and found that the expression of miR-378a-3p tended to be upregulated in TSCC cells, but the expression trends of its predicted target genes, BMP3 and ELAC2, did not match the negative regulatory pattern (Fig. 2J-R). In contrast, the expression of miR-155-5p was significantly upregulated in TSCC cells, and its target gene GYG1 was significantly downregulated (Fig. 2S-T). However, the correlation between the expression of miR-155-5p and GYG1 was inconsistent in Cal27 and HSC-3 cells and was negatively correlated in HaCaT cells (Fig. 2U-W). Studies have shown that miR-21-5p can inhibit apoptosis in TSCC by regulating the PI3K/AKT/FOXO1 signalling pathway20, but its broader regulatory mechanisms in TSCC progression remain unclear. In summary, we identified miR-21-5p/FDX1 as a key miRNA‒mRNA axis with potential regulatory roles in TSCC, and we focused on the miR-21-5p/FDX1 axis to further explore its regulatory role in TSCC cells.

Validation of miR-21-5p and FDX1 expression in clinical TSCC tissues

To clarify the clinical relevance of the miR-21-5p/FDX1 regulatory axis in TSCC, we used RT‒qPCR to measure the mRNA expression levels of miR-21-5p and FDX1 in 12 pairs of TSCC tumour tissues and adjacent normal tongue tissues (Supplementary Datasets 5). Western blot analysis was also performed to assess FDX1 protein expression. The RT‒qPCR results revealed that, compared with that in normal tissues, the expression of miR-21-5p was significantly upregulated in TSCC tumour tissues, whereasFDX1 mRNA levels were significantly downregulated (Fig. 3A-B). Among 11 valid samples (excluding one abnormal sample with contradictory data caused by secondary surgery combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy), 6 pairs exhibited a strong negative regulatory relationship between miR-21-5p and FDX1, while 5 pairs showed coexpression. The expression levels of miR-21-5p and FDX1 were negatively correlated in TSCC tumour tissues but not in adjacent normal tongue tissues (Fig. 3C-D). Western blot analysis further confirmed that FDX1 protein expression levels in TSCC tumour tissues were reduced to 0.3 times those in normal tissues, which was consistent with the mRNA results (Fig. 3E-F). This finding was also consistent with data from public databases showing low FDX1 expression in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma tissues, confirming its potential function as a tumour suppressor. Furthermore, we analysed the correlation of miR-21-5p and FDX1 expression with patient survival using the OncoLnc database; however, there was no significant correlation between them (Fig. 3G-H).

AT-101 inhibits TSCC cell proliferation via miR-21-5p

To investigate whether AT-101 inhibits TSCC cell proliferation through miR-21-5p, we first determined the optimal concentration of AT-101 for Cal27 cells. The CCK-8 results (Fig. 4A) revealed that AT-101 treatment for 48 h decreased Cal27 cell viability in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC50 of 12 µM. Notably, 15 µM AT-101 had minimal effects on HaCaT cells but significantly reduced Cal27 cell viability (Fig. 4B); thus, we chose 15 µM as the treatment concentration for subsequent experiments. The RT‒qPCR results revealed that AT-101 treatment significantly decreased miR-21-5p expression (Fig. 4C). To further explore the role of miR-21-5p in AT-101-mediated inhibition, we transfected Cal27 cells with anmiR-21-5p inhibitor or mimic. The RT‒qPCR results verified the knockdown and overexpression of miR-21-5p (Fig. 4D). The CCK-8 results revealed that AT-101 treatment significantly inhibited Cal27 cell proliferation and that miR-21-5p overexpression reversed this inhibitory effect (Fig. 4E-F). EdU assays further confirmed that both AT-101 treatment and miR-21-5p inhibition reduced DNA synthesis, whereas miR-21-5p overexpression restored DNA synthesis (Fig. 4G-I), indicating that AT-101 mediates the inhibition of TSCC proliferation by downregulating the expression of miR-21-5p. Quantitative analysis of the proliferation rate (Fig. 4H-J) further supported these findings, showing that miR-21-5p overexpression significantly reversed AT-101–induced growth inhibition. In summary, these results demonstrate that AT-101 inhibits TSCC cell proliferation by suppressing miR-21-5p expression and that the restoration of miR-21-5p expression can antagonize the inhibitory effects of AT-101.

AT-101 affects the migration and invasion of Cal27 cells by regulating miR-21-5p

To investigate the effects of AT-101 regulation of miR-21-5p on the migration and invasion of Cal27 cells, we performed wound healing and Transwell invasion assays. Wound healing assays (Fig. 5A, C) revealed that the Cal27 inhibitor significantly reduced migration compared with the control and NC (Fig. 5B). Similarly, in AT-101–treated cells, the migration in the Cal27-AT-101-mimic group was significantly greater than that in the Cal27-AT-101-mimic-NC group (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, as shown by representative images (Fig. 5E, G) and statistical data (Fig. 5F, H) from the invasion assays, the number of invasive cells in the Cal27-inhibitor group was significantly lower than that in the corresponding control groups, whereas the number of invasive cells in the Cal27-AT-101-mimic group was significantly greater. These results indicate that AT-101 can significantly inhibit the migration and invasion of Cal27 cells by regulating miR-21-5p.

miR-21-5p targets and regulates FDX1

TargetScan and miRanda software predicted that FDX1 is the target gene of miR-21-5p (Fig. 6A). To verify whether miR-21-5p inhibits the migration and invasion of Cal27 cells by regulating FDX1, we performed RT‒qPCR analysis and found that the inhibition of miR-21-5p function (in the Cal27-inhibitor group) significantly upregulated FDX1 mRNA levels (Fig. 6B), whereas the activation of miR-21-5p (in the Cal27-AT-101-mimic group) resulted in a significant decrease in FDX1 expression (Fig. 6C). These results suggest that miR-21-5p negatively regulates FDX1. To determine whether miR-21-5p directly targets FDX1, we constructed luciferase reporter vectors containing wild-type (H30826) and mutant (H30827) FDX1 3’UTRs. The results showed that the overexpression of miR-21-5p (in the H30826 + hsa-miR-21-5p group) significantly inhibited the luciferase activity of wild-type FDX1 (Fig. 6D) but had no significant effect on the mutant vector (in the H30827 + NC group). These results confirm that miR-21-5p regulates FDX1 expression by directly binding to the FDX1 3’UTR. In summary, these bidirectional regulatory effects further support a direct targeting relationship between miR-21-5p and FDX1 and suggest their crucial roles in TSCC, providing experimental evidence for further investigation of the roles of the miR-21-5p/FDX1 axis in TSCC development and progression.

Effects of FDX1 on Cal27cell proliferation, migration, and invasion

In the abovementioned experiments, we verified the direct targeting relationship between miR-21-5p and FDX1. Next, we investigated the functional impact of FDX1 on Cal27 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. First, we analysed three different FDX1-silencing genes by RT‒qPCR (Fig. 7A). The results revealed that miR-480 significantly silenced FDX1expression in Cal27 cells. Therefore, miR-480 was selected as the gene for silencing FDX1 in subsequent studies. CCK-8 assays (Fig. 7B) demonstrated that compared with siRNA-NC, FDX1 silencing (in the siFDX1 group) significantly promoted the proliferation of Cal27 cells. Furthermore, the results of the EdU assay (Fig. 7C) visually confirmed the increase in the proportion of proliferating cells in the siFDX1 and AT101 + siFDX1 groups compared with that in the control group, which was consistent with the CCK-8 assay data. Additionally, wound healing assays (Fig. 7D-E) showed that silencing FDX1expression significantly increased the wound healing rate of Cal27 cell monolayers. Transwell migration assays (Fig. 7F, G and H) further revealed that both the migration rate and the number of migrated cells in the siFDX1 group were significantly increased. Notably, the combination of AT-101 treatment and FDX1 silencing (AT-101 + siFDX1 group) did not enhance the inhibition of TSCC cell proliferation, invasion, or migration and even weakened it to some extent. Together, these results highlight the crucial role of FDX1 in regulating the biological behaviour of Cal27 cells and the key tumour-suppressing role of FDX1 in TSCC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion and further suggest that FDX1loss may confer a more aggressive malignant phenotype to cancer cells.

FDX1 inhibits the growth of tongue squamous cell carcinoma

Next, we further verified whether FDX1could inhibit the proliferation of tongue squamous cell carcinoma cells in animal models. We generated two pools of Cal27cells in which theFDX1 gene was stably silenced or overexpressed by lentivirus, referred to asCal27-shFDX1 and Cal27-FDX1 cells, respectively. The RT‒qPCR results indicated successful construction of the stable cell lines (Fig. 8A). Then, these cells were injected subcutaneously into BALB/c nude mice. As shown in Fig. 8C and E, compared with the control treatment, FDX1overexpression significantly decreased the tumour volume and weight, whereas silencing FDX1 promoted tumour growth. There was no significant difference in the body weight of the mice among the different groups (Fig. 8B). Tumour samples were subjected to HE staining, immunofluorescence detection of Ki-67, and TUNEL staining to further establish the antitumour efficacy of FDX1. Compared with those in the control group, the tumour cells in the FDX1 group appeared necrotic and had a large number of vacuoles (Fig. 8F). Ki-67 is a reliable biomarker of malignant tissue proliferation, and Ki-67 expression increases substantially throughout tumour growth. The proportion of Ki-67-positive tumour cells in the shFDX1 group was considerably greater than that in the control group, and the proportion of Ki-67-positive cells in the FDX1 group was significantly lower. TUNEL staining is used to visualize a marker of apoptosis and showed the opposite trends as immunostaining for Ki-67 (Fig. 8F and G). These results demonstrate that FDX1 delays the proliferation of malignant cells and promotes apoptosis.

Disscussion

Tongue squamous cell carcinoma (TSCC) is highly invasive and is associated with a poor prognosis21, highlighting the urgent need to explore novel molecular targets and therapeutic strategies. In this study, we investigated the functional mechanism of the miR-21-5p/FDX1 axis in TSCC progression and found for the first time that AT-101can inhibit the malignant phenotype of TSCC by targeting this axis. By integrating multiomic analysis with functional experiments, we demonstrated that AT-101 can disrupt the metabolic homeostasis of TSCC cells and inhibit their growth by downregulating miR-21-5p expression and restoring FDX1 expression. These findings not only further support the oncogenic role of miR-21-5p but also reveal the crucial role of FDX1 as a potential tumour suppressor and therapeutic target (Fig. 8I).

As a recognized oncogenic miRNA, miR-21-5p promotes tumorigenesis in various cancers by targeting tumour suppressor genes such as PTEN and PDCD422,23. Our study further confirmed that the expression of miR-21-5p was significantly upregulated, while that of FDX1 was significantly downregulated in TSCC tissues and cell lines and that these two genes were significantly negatively correlated. Dual-luciferase reporter assays further validated FDX1 as a direct target of miR-21-5p, emphasizing the crucial role of miR-21-5p in TSCC. Although previous studies have clearly established the oncogenic role of miR-21-5p in TSCC, such as those by Liu et al.20, who revealed its ability to promote tumour cell survival and antiapoptotic activity by targeting PDCD4 and activating the PI3K/AKT/FOXO1 signalling axis, its broader molecular network and metabolic regulatory mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. Unlike previous studies, our study is the first to reveal that miR-21-5p promotes TSCC progression by directly targeting the mitochondrial ferredoxin FDX1 and confirm that AT-101 can restore FDX1 expression by inhibiting miR-21-5p, thereby suppressing the malignant phenotype of TSCC. These findings not only expand the functional scope of miR-21-5p but also reveal unrecognized metabolic vulnerability in TSCC. The conventional view is that miR-21-5p mainly promotes cancer cell survival by targeting classical tumour suppressor genes such as PTEN and PDCD424. However, our multiomic analysis revealed that FDX1 is a key downstream target of miR-21-5p in TSCC. As a mitochondrial iron‒sulphur protein, FDX1 plays a central role in iron‒sulphur cluster biogenesis, redox homeostasis, and fatty acid oxidation and exhibits tumour-suppressing properties in various cancer types25,26. We found that the expression level of FDX1 was significantly negatively correlated with that of miR-21-5p and that silencing FDX1 could mimic the pro-proliferative and pro-invasive effects of miR-21-5p overexpression, further supporting its role as an important metabolic regulator in TSCC. These data suggest that FDX1 is not only an important regulator of TSCC progression but also a potential key metabolic regulatory hub.

AT-101 is a BH3 analogue that induces apoptosis mainly by antagonizing the expression of Bcl-2 family antiapoptotic proteins27. In this study, we found that AT-101 not only induces apoptosis in tumour cells but also upregulates FDX1 expression by inhibiting miR-21-5p. The results of functional experiments demonstrated that AT-101 significantly inhibits the proliferation, invasion, and migration of TSCC cells and that these effects are dependent on the expression of miR-21-5p and FDX1. Notably, when AT-101 was combined with FDX1 silencing (siFDX1), the inhibitory effect on TSCC cell proliferation and migration was not enhanced and may have even been somewhat weakened. In contrast, miR-21-5p knockdown combined with AT-101 treatment resulted in a significant synergistic inhibitory effect, further suggesting that AT-101 exerts its antitumour effect mainly by targeting the miR-21-5p/FDX1 axis. These results indicate that FDX1 plays a crucial role in the AT-101-mediated anti-TSCC effect and suggest that AT-101 may inhibit the malignant progression of TSCC by restoring FDX1 function. These findings are consistent with the multitarget mechanism of BH3 mimetics, such as overcoming chemotherapy resistance in P53-inactive colorectal cancer through the miR-34a/LRPPRC/MDR1 axis28. These results further highlight the potential of combined multipathway therapeutic strategies for TSCC.

Clinical sample analysis revealed a significant correlation between high miR-21-5p expression and low FDX1 expression, with the expression level of FDX1 being negatively correlated with the degree of differentiation of TSCC. These findings are consistent with the trend of low FDX1 expression in public databases of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC)29, suggesting that FDX1 may serve as a potential prognostic marker for TSCC patients. Given that miR-21-5p can promote tumour growth by activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway20, we speculated that FDX1 may antagonize the oncogenic effects of this pathway by inhibiting AKT phosphorylation. Future studies can further elucidate the downstream metabolic regulatory network of the miR-21-5p/FDX1 axis through proteomic and metabolomic analyses.

Although this study revealed the potential mechanism of AT-101 in TSCC, certain limitations remain. First, the specific metabolic regulatory mechanisms of FDX1 in TSCC remain to be further elucidated. FDX1 is a key regulator of cuproptosis and can promote the accumulation of copper ions (Cu²⁺) in mitochondria, triggering the binding of lipidated proteins (such as DLAT) with copper ions to form toxic aggregates, which in turn leads to oxidative protein damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inhibition of cell metabolic reprogramming. Eventually, FDX1 induces copper-dependent programmed cell death in tumour cells, effectively inhibiting tumour proliferation and metastasis30. This process results in significant antitumour effects in various solid tumours, such as colorectal cancer and liver cancer, and there may be a similar mechanism in TSCC as well. Additionally, miR-21-5p may synergistically promote TSCC progression by targeting other tumour suppressor genes (such as PTEN)2,3, and future studies could combine CRISPR gene screening with single-cell sequencing to construct a more comprehensive regulatory network. Finally, differences in the tumour microenvironment and molecular subtypes may affect the therapeutic effect of AT-101, and multicentre clinical trials are needed to evaluate its responsiveness in different TSCC patients.

This study systematically elucidates for the first time the molecular mechanism through which AT-101 inhibits the malignant progression of TSCC by targeting the miR-21-5p/FDX1 axis and reveals the key role of FDX1 in the regulation of TSCC metabolism. Through multi-database integrated analysis and functional experimental validation, our study provides a novel theoretical foundation for prognosis evaluation and targeted therapy in TSCC.
