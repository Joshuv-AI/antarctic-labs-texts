Comprehensive wastewater genomic surveillance in the Netherlands: insights into country-wide SARS-CoV-2 lineage dynamics and implications for future surveillance
Authors: Auke Haver, Jaap T. van Dissel, Wouter A. Hetebrij, Dirk Eggink, Jeroen F. J. Laros, Willemijn J. Lodder
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDuring the COVID-19 pandemic, the spread and diversity of SARS-CoV-2 was monitored non-invasively in more than 99% of Dutch households by a comprehensive wastewater-based epidemiology (WBE) programme which analysed viral RNA in wastewater samples taken at every household wastewater-treatment plant (WWTP) in the Netherlands. In this study, we analyse next-generation sequencing data generated from these wastewater samples for tracking SARS-CoV-2 lineages. We aimed to determine how well patterns of lineage abundances correspond to findings from individual surveillance, including community testing and testing at hospitals, and how WBE can be employed efficiently for future surveillance of SARS-CoV-2.MethodsWhole-genome short-read sequencing was performed on 16,631 wastewater samples collected between Apr 9, 2021, and Dec 31, 2023, at 311 WWTPs. Observed lineages were grouped and week-aggregate estimates were compared to estimates from 138,374 individual surveillance sequences generated during the same period. Furthermore, we used resampling to examine the effect of sample size and catchment type on observed lineage dynamics.ResultsWBE enabled monitoring of SARS-CoV-2 lineages even during periods of reduced circulation in the Dutch population. Wastewater lineage proportions consistently mirrored those of individual surveillance. During periods of low turnover of dominant lineages, nationally representative lineage estimates can be obtained by sequencing only 24 samples per week, whereby population density or total population size of catchment areas would have a negligible effect on country-wide aggregate lineage estimates.ConclusionsWBE at household WWTPs is a valuable tool for SARS-CoV-2 surveillance, whereby comparatively little sequence data can be generated to identify lineage dynamics trends in large populations, and which can be scaled up and down in response to emerging lineages of concern. As such, it provides a useful complement to existing surveillance tools, to ensure optimal insight into pathogen diversity and spread.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24985-2.

Background

Wastewater-based epidemiology (WBE) enables surveillance of pathogens including SARS-CoV-2 by monitoring viral RNA excreted by infected individuals in wastewater [1]. Unlike individual or case-based surveillance in hospitals or in large-scale community testing facilities, it is not dependent on healthcare seeking behaviour or access to healthcare and thus can generate population-level estimates for a combination of symptomatic as well as pre- and asymptomatic individuals. Furthermore, WBE has been applied in various settings and for numerous pathogens [2–4]. When performed using household wastewater, the detected SARS-CoV-2 viral loads correlate with the number of clinical cases and hospitalizations, and therefore can serve as a measure of disease prevalence [5, 6]. Consequently, in regions where large parts of the population are connected to a household wastewater treatment plant (WWTP), WBE programmes have highlighted their valuable addition to public health surveillance [3, 7].

Although quantitative-PCR (qPCR) based methods are at the core of WBE, sequencing-based technologies enable in-depth wastewater genomic surveillance of SARS-CoV-2 variant diversity patterns and tracking of (novel) mutations of concern [4, 8, 9]. Because wastewater samples taken from WWTPs consist of mixtures of potentially many different infected individuals, various methods and bioinformatic tools have been developed and applied to detect and quantify relative abundances of SARS-CoV-2 lineages, including modelling approaches based on genome-wide single-nucleotide polymorphism frequencies and selective sequencing of large (+ 1,000bp) fragments to analyse mutation co-occurrence [10, 11]. Wastewater genomic surveillance studies have reported the ability to detect emerging SARS-CoV-2 lineages, in some cases ahead of individual surveillance [4, 12].

In the Netherlands, country-wide WBE for SARS-CoV-2 has been performed by the National Sewage Surveillance program of the Dutch National Institute for Public Health and the Environment (RIVM), as commissioned by the Dutch Ministry of Health, Welfare and Sport, and in collaboration with the local water authorities [13]. Throughout the pandemic, sampling was performed at every household WWTP in the Netherlands, of which the catchment areas encompass more than 99% of Dutch households. Quantification of SARS-CoV-2 viral loads was performed using Reverse Transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) and results were made publicly available as open data at www.data.rivm.nl [14]. A subset of these samples was analysed with whole-genome short-read sequencing to identify circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants.

In this study, we perform a comprehensive analysis of SARS-CoV-2 variant diversity patterns in the Netherlands between Apr 2021 and Dec 2023 through sequence analysis of household wastewater samples. We compare how well lineage estimates from wastewater surveillance reflect those of individual surveillance and how country-level lineage abundance estimates can vary as a result of sample size and WWTP catchment characteristics. Our findings further substantiate how wastewater surveillance can generate meaningful insights into SARS-CoV-2 diversity beyond the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Sample collection

During the COVID-19 pandemic, 24-h flow-proportional composite wastewater samples were taken at every WWTP in the Netherlands as described elsewhere [13]. These WWTPs varied largely in catchment population size; from 931 to 832,864 inhabitant equivalents (IE) (Fig. 1B). Total nucleic acids were extracted in duplicate directly from homogenized raw wastewater samples using a MagNA Pure 96 DNA and Viral NA Large Volume Kit (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Library preparation and sequencing

The methodology for short-read sequencing library preparation was optimised during the study period to improve data quality [16]. Initially, RNA purification was performed through treatment of nucleic acid extracts with TURBO DNase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and subsequent concentration with the RNeasy MinElute Cleanup Kit V3 (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). From Aug 2022 onward, purification was performed using the RNA Clean & Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research, Leiden, Netherlands) including the optional DNase treatment. cDNA was generated from purified RNA using random hexamer primers (pdN6) (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). Initially, cDNA amplification was performed using the modified Artic V3 primer set concentrations described by Han, et al. [17] for use in individual surveillance. During the shift to the updated Artic V4.1 primer set in Apr and May 2022, the supplemental V3 primers were added to the corresponding pools of the V4.1 set as well. However, as this did not improve sequencing coverage, further sequencing proceeded with the standard Artic V4.1 primer pool compositions. Sequencing libraries generated using the V4.1 primer set from samples before Apr 2022, were part of efforts to retroactively sequence biobanked RNA extracts. Sequencing library preparation was performed with the TruSeq Nano DNA kit (Illumina, CA, USA). Paired-end sequencing (2 × 300bp) of DNA libraries was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform.

Dataset selection

For this study, sequencing data was analysed of 16,631 wastewater samples, taken between Apr 9, 2021, and Dec 31, 2023, from 311 out of a total of 313 WWTPs in the Netherlands. Sequence data was included exclusively from wastewater samples reported in the open data [14]. Samples previously described as containing cryptic lineages or samples taken at WWTPs Woerden and Katwoude, for similar considerations, were excluded from this study [18].

Bioinformatic analysis of wastewater samples

Sequencing libraries were analysed using an in-house pipeline as described elsewhere [18]. Briefly, potential human reads were removed using the Human Read Removal Tool (v2.0.0). Next, paired end reads were merged using VSEARCH (v2.23.0) and aligned to the SARS-CoV-2 reference genome (NC_045512.2) using Minimap2 (v2.24). Residual adapter sequences were subsequently removed through removal of soft-clipped bases. Reads were filtered for a minimum length of 75 bases and minimum base Phred quality of 15 using fastp (v0.23.2) and subsequently trimmed using a minimum mean Phred quality of 20 over a sliding window of 5 bases. Primer sequences were removed using AmpliGone (v1.3.0), after which the cleaned reads were re-aligned to the reference genome. Wastewater samples sequenced in multiple experiments were individually processed and further analysis was performed on the merged alignment files. Samples with a coverage depth of fewer than ten reads for 50% of the genome were considered of insufficient quality for further analysis. The Freyja (v1.5.0; mutation barcodes version Jul 2, 2024) [4] software tool was used to estimate relative abundances of SARS-CoV-2 Pango [19] lineages in individual and merged datasets.

SARS-CoV-2 surveillance data

Individual surveillance data was retrieved from the Dutch national SARS-CoV-2 genomic surveillance, coordinated by the RIVM, which includes isolates from randomly selected SARS-CoV-2 positive PCR-tests collected from public testing facilities, hospitals, care centres and participatory community surveillance [13]. In this study, only sequences were considered with complete metadata information, which includes PC4 (numeric part of zip code), public sequence ID and sampling date. Multiple publicly available COVID-19 datasets were used in this study, including: Voluntary reported weekly positive tests results [20], National average viral load data, in # particles 100,000 per IE [14], and Daily COVID-19 hospital admissions per municipality [21, 22].

Data analysis

All analyses were performed in RStudio with R (v4.4.1) [23] using the tidyverse (v2.0.0) set of packages [24]. Visualizations were created using the ggplot2 (v3.5.0) package in combination with cowplot (v1.1.1), ggpubr (v0.6.0) and sf (v1.0).

To identify dominant lineages, observed lineages were grouped based on a minimum abundance of 25% in any three weeks, in either individual or wastewater surveillance data collected during the study period, using a custom algorithm which aimed to facilitate unbiased comparison between surveillance types (Supplement S1). Lineages not descendent from these lineage groups or recombinants thereof, were combined in a residual category (Ungrouped).

Aggregated lineage estimates were calculated both as week average frequencies (starting on Monday) and 15-day rolling average frequencies (7-days before, including and after a specific date). To determine variability in the 15-day rolling average frequency estimates, Confidence Intervals (CI) were calculated using a 1,000-fold bootstrapping approach. During each fold a fixed number of samples are randomly selected from each calendar week (starting on Monday) and used to calculate a 15-day rolling average for each day in a certain period. The range between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of these 1,000 estimates constitutes the 95% CI. In different downscaling scenarios, only samples are included from scenario-specific WWTPs, as opposed to all 311 WWTPs.

Results

The Netherlands experienced multiple waves of SARS-CoV-2 infections between April 2021 and December 2023, whereby dynamics of hospitalizations and positive tests were associated with similar patterns of viral loads in wastewater (Fig. 1A). Priority for whole-genome sequencing was assigned to wastewater RNA extracts with SARS-CoV-2 concentrations above 1,000 copies per ml (Fig. 1C). This was not possible during months of low circulation, marked by viral loads below 1013 genome copies per 100,000 IE per day, such as Jun and Sep 2021, and Jun and Jul 2023 (Fig. 1A, C). A higher variability in genome coverage was observed for libraries generated from these extracts (Fig. 1D). Consequently, a positive relation was observed between the viral load in wastewater and the proportion of libraries passing coverage-based quality control (Fig. 1D, E). Amplicon coverage varied largely between all libraries, with repeated dropout of amplicons corresponding to the Spike-gene (Supplementary Fig. 2). In total, libraries of sufficient quality were obtained for 14,617 (87.9%) wastewater samples (Fig. 1E), which corresponds to a median of 42 included samples (IQR = 25) per WWTP between Apr 2021 and Dec 2023. The majority of these samples (n = 9,398) were taken between Mar 2022 and Apr 2023 (Fig. 1E). Further sample selection criteria for sequencing included an even geographical coverage and consistent inclusion of samples from WWTPs of catchment areas with large inhabitant equivalents (> 100,000 IE) and WWTPs of catchment areas which contain areas with increased tourism, such as the North Sea coast and the Wadden Islands (Fig. 1F; Supplement S3). We included 138,374 sequences from individual genomic surveillance covering the entire study period. These primarily originated from community testing (n = 102,991), which was phased out in Mar 2023, after which a reduced number of samples originated from hospitals and healthcare studies (Fig. 1G, H).

By comparing observed week-aggregate proportions of selected lineages with individual surveillance, we assessed how well wastewater surveillance detected overall (sub)lineage patterns (Fig. 2). To facilitate this comparison, sublineages were phylogenetically grouped based on a minimum mean lineage group abundance of 25% in any three weeks during the study period (Supplement S1). This threshold was chosen to primarily focus on major lineage groups. As a result, 19 lineage groups were identified, which comprised lineages of the Alpha (B.1.1.7), Delta (B.1.617.2, AY.43, AY.9.2) and Omicron (BA.1, BA.1.1, BA.2, BA.5, BA.5.1, BA.5.2, BA.5.3, BA.5.2.1, BQ.1.1, XBB.1, XBB.1.5, XBB.1.9, XBB.1.16, EG.5.1 and JN.1) Variants of Concern (Fig. 2). Subsequently, we calculated the differences in lineage group proportions per week between surveillance types, to identify how they change over time and if certain lineage groups are over- or underrepresented in either wastewater (Fig. 2C) or individual surveillance (Fig. 2D). Based on these differences, three distinct periods could be discerned: 1) (Apr – Oct 2021) a period of large fluctuations of the total difference in proportion of all lineage groups during the emergence and dominance of Delta lineages; 2) (Nov 2021 – Apr 2022) a period containing a bimodal pattern during the successive emergence of the Omicron BA.1(0.1) and BA.2 lineages and 3) (May 2022 onward) a period of multiple co-circulating lineages whereby the total difference in lineage group proportions between surveillance type is stable, which includes the emergence of the Omicron BA.5 lineages up to the emergence of the Omicron JN.1 lineage. Each of these periods started and ended with the dominance of one or two lineage groups, and low differences (< 10%) in lineage abundance between surveillance types. Lineages which could not be assigned to the 19 lineage groups (Ungrouped), were near-consistently overrepresented in wastewater surveillance compared to individual surveillance from Dec 2021 onward. Finally, only the transition from Alpha to Delta between May and Aug 2021, and subsequently Delta to Omicron between Nov 2021 and Jan 2022, was associated with a lag of wastewater compared to individual surveillance (Fig. 2; Supplementary Fig. 3).

To determine the most efficient implementation of baseline surveillance during periods of low turnover of dominant lineages, we compared how downscaling of wastewater genomic surveillance by sequencing fewer samples affects variability in lineage group estimates on the national level. The period from Mar 2022 to Dec 2022 was selected for this analysis because of the high median number of wastewater datasets of sufficient quality available per week (n = 160, IQR = 36.5), distributed over multiple WWTPs and timepoints. Moreover, this period coincided with the emergence of both concurrent and successive BA.5 lineages (BA.5, BA.5.1, BA.5.2, BA.5.3, BA.5.2.1 and BQ.1.1) (Fig. 1E; 2 A). The downscaled surveillance was simulated by resampling wastewater samples from the full dataset using a fixed sample size per week, namely 96, 72, 48, or 24 samples taken randomly across all 311 WWTPs (Fig. 3A). The largest variability in 15-day rolling average proportions for a single day was observed for the BA.2 lineage for May 25, 2022, which displayed a bootstrap CI of 21.3% for a sample size of 24. The size of this CI decreased to 14.8%, 11.0% and 9.74% when the sample size was increased to 48, 72 and 96, respectively. Subsequently, we evaluated two scenarios in which only samples are included from a specific subset of WWTPs, as opposed to all 311 WWTPs, based on catchment population size (inhabitant equivalent/IE) or population density (IE/km2) (Supplementary Fig. 4–5). The first scenario compares catchment areas with high (> 1,000 IE/km2, n = 54) versus low (< 200 IE/km2, n = 84) population density (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Fig. 4–5); the second compares catchment areas with large (> 100,000 IE, n = 47) versus small (< 10,000 IE, n = 65) inhabitant equivalents (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Fig. 4–5). In both scenarios, a fixed sample size of 24 wastewater sampler per week was used. This analysis revealed no large differences when compared to random location sampling, although daily lineage abundances displayed fluctuations for each of the matched categories (Fig. 3A-C).

Lastly, we simulated a scenario in which sampling is performed exclusively at twelve sentinel sites, constituting the largest WWTP for each of the twelve Dutch provinces (Fig. 3D). Although the fixed subsampling size of 24 per week was maintained, the lower number of sampling sites reduced the actual available sample size to a median of 11 samples per week (IQR = 3) for this analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5). Again, these lineage abundance patterns were highly similar to those obtained from sampling with equal size randomly across all 311 WWTPs.

Discussion

In this study, we analysed the unique set of wastewater genomic surveillance data of SARS-CoV-2 collected in the Netherlands by the National Sewage Surveillance, covering 99% of the population between Apr 2021 and Dec 2023. Our findings substantiate that even over extended periods of time and during periods of decreased circulation, wastewater surveillance consistently detects lineage diversity and emergence patterns corresponding to lineages and proportions observed in genomic surveillance of samples from hospitals or from community testing facilities. Furthermore, our resampling analysis shows country-wide lineage trends can be efficiently monitored by sequencing as little as 24 wastewater samples per week.

Despite low SARS-CoV-2 RNA concentrations in wastewater samples (< 1,500 copies per ml) during periods of decreased circulation, we were able to accurately sequence circulating lineages from wastewater throughout the study period. This supports the robustness of wastewater genomic surveillance for future baseline surveillance and in low-prevalence settings. Difficulties in detecting and typing SARS-CoV-2 during periods of low transmission have been noted before [7]. Further optimisation of virus concentration methods could enhance recovery of viral genomes [4], and improve the feasibility of sequencing other respiratory pathogens from wastewater samples as well, such as influenza viruses, measles virus or respiratory syncytial virus, increasing the applicability of WBE accordingly [25].

Our study did not reveal consistent patterns of earlier detection of novel lineages in either wastewater or individual surveillance for the entire study period. Regarding the Alpha-Delta variant transition, we consider the scarcity of our wastewater genomic data during this period, in part due to the large number of sequence libraries of insufficient quality, to impede fair comparison between surveillance types (Fig. 1E; Supplementary Fig. 3). Izquierdo-Lara, et al. [9], however, observed earlier emergence of the Delta variant in individual surveillance compared to wastewater sequencing data for the Dutch city of Rotterdam, which was attributed to the intensity and potential biases of the strategy by which individual surveillance samples were sequenced [9]. Similarly, we observed a slight lead in country-wide lineage estimates from individual surveillance during the Delta to Omicron variant transition. Comparable leads for this transition and the Alpha-Delta transition were observed for the wastewater in Prague [26]. Whereas infection with the Omicron as opposed to the Delta variant is associated with reduced shedding [27], potentially leading to an underestimation of Omicron abundance frequencies, the most comprehensive study of the Delta-Omicron transition in England observed no overall lag, with only slight regional differences [28]. Accurate quantification of differential lineage detection between surveillance types would require in-depth regional comparisons for which whole-genome sequencing might be less suited than qPCR-based methods, and which is outside of the scope of this study [9]. Furthermore, the already high similarity in lineage emergence dynamics presented here underlines the reliability of wastewater genomic surveillance.

The high concordance between wastewater and individual surveillance, both during broad community testing and when relying primarily on hospital cases, shows that wastewater lineage estimates are robust at the country-level and beyond the COVID-19 pandemic [8, 9, 28, 29]. Consistent differences manifested primarily as a higher abundance of lineages not assigned to the 19 major lineage groups for wastewater surveillance, as well as a higher abundance of lineages such as BA.5, BA.5.3 and XBB.1 as opposed to their respective sub-lineages BA.5.1, BQ.1.1 and XBB.1.5/XBB.1.9/XBB.1.16, which were more abundant in individual surveillance (Fig. 2C, D). With regard to the ungrouped lineages, these comprised lineages which circulated at lower frequencies, such as pre-Alpha lineages (B.1*), Omicron BA.4 and Omicron XBB.2, as well as recombinants of major lineages that correspond to the period in which they were detected, such as XM, XZ, XAS and XCC, but which were rare or undetected in individual surveillance. For these observations, it is relevant to consider that the Freyja [4] software employed for wastewater lineage identification, tends towards false positive detection of lineages and struggles to discern highly similar lineages in cases of insufficient coverage of signature mutations [10, 12]. However, we consider the low false negative rate and higher accuracy compared to similar tools to outweigh these limitations [9, 26]. Despite these challenges, the broad toolkit available for WBE enables data considered of insufficient quality or inconclusive to be used as a stepping stone for further analysis, for example with digital droplet PCR or other sequencing-based techniques [10, 30, 31].

By evaluating different resampling strategies, we demonstrate that even a greatly reduced sequencing effort can yield accurate and relevant insights into country-level SARS-CoV-2 lineage dynamics. Such findings are of direct value to public health officials now that the pandemic has been declared ended and reduced funding necessitates a reconsideration of how SARS-CoV-2 surveillance should be structured. The underlying infrastructure for wastewater surveillance can be shared for monitoring multiple health-related biomarkers and by allocating a portion of sequencing capacity from individual surveillance to wastewater surveillance, minimal wastewater genomic surveillance can cost-effectively provide baseline estimates of population-level lineage abundances. This is especially useful when representativeness of individual surveillance cannot be guaranteed by sample availability, for instance due to healthcare-seeking behaviour or resource constraints. In our study, this was most evident for the period Apr until Oct 2023, when fewer than 500 samples were reported per week in individual surveillance. Furthermore, by downscaling and not suspending wastewater surveillance, expertise is preserved, and protocols are maintained and updated, enabling rapid up and down scaling when required and directly contributing to pandemic preparedness. For instance, when a novel lineage associated with increased transmissibility or disease severity is detected elsewhere in the world, wastewater genomic surveillance could be scaled up to include major urban centres, transport hubs or other regions with increased tourism, to assist in tracking this new lineage of concern. However, until such an upscale can be realised, the downscaled surveillance risks a delayed detection of novel lineages through insufficient geographic and temporal coverage.

We show how WWTP catchment characteristics such as attached population size or population density had negligible effect on country-level lineage dynamics. However, due to the distribution of analysed wastewater samples per WWTP, this analysis was limited to highly prevalent lineage groups in 2022, when multiple rounds of vaccination and prior infection had already influenced the immunity landscape [32]. Additionally, by this time control measures had been relaxed which enabled unrestricted travel and, consequently, transmission across a small and densely populated country. More research would be required to reconstruct geographical transmission patterns for emerging variants at different stages of the pandemic. Although such statistical models exist for individual genomic surveillance [17, 33] and community-based RT-qPCR assays [34], integration of wastewater surveillance data could further elucidate regional transmission patterns and motivate how surveillance should be scaled up in case of the emergence of novel lineages or variants of concern.

Conclusions

It is well established that wastewater genomic surveillance should not be considered a replacement of individual surveillance, as it cannot generate the high-quality individual reference data on which individual surveillance relies and findings cannot be directly used for outbreak investigation around individual cases or linked to clinical outcomes [7]. However, it is able to fill a specific niche whereby it enables continuous, cost-effective, population-level surveillance, of both symptomatic and asymptomatic cases, which in the form we present here cannot be traced to individuals, and can be used as a benchmark for individual surveillance coverage. Therefore, wastewater genomic surveillance remains a useful tool to monitor the spread and diversity of SARS-CoV-2.
