Hiding in Plain Sight: Radiologic and Pathologic Findings Can Identify Beckwith-Wiedemann Syndrome in Patients With Wilms Tumor
Authors: Laura M. Molina, Akhila Rao, Julia Meade, Judy H. Squires, Svetlana A. Yatsenko, Claudia M. Salgado, Miguel Reyes-Múgica
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Most pediatric specialists, including hematologists/oncologists, surgeons, radiologists, and pathologists, are familiar with the diagnosis and management of Wilms tumor (WT). However, it may be challenging to identify the underlying conditions causing cancer predisposition, which can change the management for the patient and potentially their entire family. In this paper, we present 3 cases of clinically suspected WT associated with Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (BWS). We review the radiologic and histologic findings to diagnose BWS. We also discuss the implications of a BWS diagnosis on the clinical management of WT and follow-up guidelines for BWS patients.

Wilms tumor (WT) is the most common kidney tumor in early childhood, with current survival rates nearing 90%.1,2 WT originates from oncogenic transformation of nephrogenic blastema and recapitulates stages of kidney development.3,4 Numerous genetic alterations have been associated with WT, often as part of syndromic constellations.1,4,5 However, syndromic abnormalities may be subtle; radiology or pathology testing done while assessing for WT may reveal the first evidence of an underlying cancer predisposition syndrome.6,7 Identifying genetic syndromes in patients with WT has lifelong consequences for cancer screening, fertility preservation, and detection of associated medical conditions.2,6,8,9

Here we focus on Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome (BWS), characterized by tissue overgrowth and asymmetric growth patterns.8,10,11 Several genetic alterations can lead to BWS through abnormal methylation or copy number alteration of the 11p15.5 genomic region.11 Each alteration carries a different risk profile for pediatric tumors including WT, hepatoblastoma, and adrenocortical carcinoma, among others. Gain of methylation at maternal imprinting center 1 (IC1) carries the highest risk (∼24%) of developing WT.10,11

The following cases demonstrate the critical role of histopathologic and radiologic evaluation in the diagnosis of BWS, especially when WT is the dominant finding. We describe the key histologic and radiologic features of kidney tissue adjacent to WT that should alert clinicians to the presence of underlying BWS and cue further genetic evaluation.

METHODS

This study was approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board (STUDY22010091). A retrospective review of cases from 2004 to 2024 was performed to identify patients with WT and BWS. For each case, the pathology results were reviewed by 2 pediatric pathologists (M.R.M. and C.S.) and a pathology resident (L.M.M.), and the imaging was reviewed by 1 pediatric radiologist (J.H.S.) and a medical student (A.R.). Clinical and genetic testing were performed, and collected as a part of standard patient care.

RESULTS

Patient A

Patient A is a 2-year and 10-month-old boy who presented after 1 month of worsening abdominal distension. He was born large for gestational age (birthweight of 4095 g) and he had a history of bilateral cryptorchidism and pyloric stenosis treated with laparoscopic pyloromyotomy. Ultrasound of the left kidney (Fig. 1A) showed a large mass in the upper pole, absent normal corticomedullary differentiation, and scattered linear and punctate hyperechoic foci. Coronal CT imaging with IV and oral contrast (Fig. 1B) highlighted a 15 cm heterogenous, exophytic tumor causing mass effect on the adjacent organs, concerning for WT. Nontumoral kidney parenchyma showed small ill-defined foci of hypoattenuation.

The patient underwent radical left nephroureterectomy with good postoperative course. On macroscopic examination, the left kidney appeared comparable to the CT image (Fig. 1C). Microscopic examination with hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed a blastema-predominant WT with favorable histology (Fig. 1D), presence of angioinvasion, and negative surgical margins. Based on its extension into the kidney fascia and kidney sinus, and the presence of tumor metastases at 2 hilar lymph nodes, the tumor was diagnosed as Stage III.

The surrounding nontumoral kidney parenchyma showed features of BWS,10,12 which correspond to the radiologic findings. Figure 1D shows kidney medullary dysplasia, in which a few scattered tubules are surrounded by mesenchyme (inset, Fig. 1E), as opposed to the “back-to-back” pattern of well-aligned tubules in a normal kidney medulla. There were medullary rays with abundant mesenchyme and focal calcification (Fig. 1F), and perilobar nephrogenic rests consisting of immature tubules and aborted glomerular tufts (Fig. 1G).

These pathology findings prompted a referral to medical genetics. Initial evaluation revealed features of BWS: macrosomia, microcephaly, left facial hyperplasia, left-sided hemihyperplasia, and a small umbilical hernia. Genetic testing revealed a mosaic gain of methylation at IC1. The tumor also revealed amplification of MYCN.

The patient underwent radiation therapy of the left flank concurrent with chemotherapy per the Children’s Oncology Group protocol AREN0532 with vincristine, dactinomycin, and doxorubicin. The patient completed all cancer-directed therapy, and the latest imaging showed no residual or recurrent tumor. The patient is overall doing very well and is on track developmentally. Surveillance is ongoing, including serum AFP levels every 3 months and alternating CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis or abdominal ultrasound every 3 months for 5 years after his initial diagnosis; later, imaging will be continued as needed.

Patient B

Patient B is a 6-month-old female who presented in utero with macrosomia and abdominal enlargement on prenatal ultrasounds. She was born by C-section with a birthweight of 4052 g, which was large for gestational age. In the first month of life, she had increasing abdominal distension, microscopic hematochezia, and large amounts of air in the intestines. Abdominal ultrasound found a right kidney larger than the left, bilateral duplicated kidney collecting systems, and a small umbilical hernia. Periodic surveillance imaging was recommended.

Abdominal ultrasound (Fig. 2A) and contrast-enhanced ultrasound (Video, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JPHO/A710) at 6 months of age showed a small heterogenous lesion in the upper pole of the right kidney, compatible with a nephrogenic rest, and multiple punctate hyperechoic foci scattered in the upper pole. Coronal contrast-enhanced CT imaging (Fig. 2B) showed a 2.2 cm solid enhancing lesion on the right kidney, and bilateral small hypoattenuating foci scattered throughout the kidney parenchyma. Of concern for possible WT, the patient underwent a radical right nephrectomy, which was well tolerated.

Pathology examination revealed that the nodule was a hyperplastic intralobar nephrogenic rest within a background of diffuse hyperplastic nephrogenic rests and medullary rays (Fig. 2C), matching the hypoattenuating foci seen on CT. Follow-up magnetic resonance (MR) imaging at 8 months old revealed poor corticomedullary differentiation in the left kidney, with suspected nephrogenic rests (Figs. 2D–F). Coronal Short Tau Inversion Recovery Magnetic Resonance (STIR MR), a T2-weighted sequence with fat suppression, Figure 2D showed subtle areas of abnormally high signal throughout the left kidney cortex. These areas had an abnormally low signal on coronal T1-weighted MR imaging (Fig. 2E) and persistently low signal without enhancement on coronal T1-weighted fat-saturated MR imaging following contrast administration (Fig. 2F).

Subsequent medical genetics evaluation revealed macrosomia, right hemihyperplasia, and a nevus flammeus at the glabella. Genetic testing identified a low-level gain of methylation at IC1, confirming the diagnosis of BWS. She continues to get abdominal ultrasounds every 3 to 4 months. Her kidney function has remained stable and she is developing normally.

Patient C

Patient C is a 2-year and 6-month-old male who presented with a family history of Waardenburg syndrome in several paternal relatives and BWS in a maternal relative. The pregnancy was complicated by polyhydramnios and premature rupture of membranes. He was born at 32 weeks and was large for gestational age, with hypoglycemia that resolved in the newborn period.

Medical genetics consultation at 3 months of age noted macroglossia, facial dysmorphic features, left hemihyperplasia, and an umbilical hernia, meeting the clinical criteria for BWS. He also had a white forelock, heterochromia in the left eye, and skin pigmentation defects, consistent with Waardenburg syndrome. Genetic testing identified a pathogenic nonsense variant in exon 2 of the PAX3 gene, consistent with a diagnosis of Waardenburg syndrome type 1. Chromosomal and methylation testing for BWS-associated alterations was negative at the time.

The patient was surveilled every 3 to 4 months with abdominal and retroperitoneal ultrasounds. At the age of 2 years and 6 months, surveillance ultrasound revealed 2 masses on his left kidney. Coronal contrast-enhanced CT scan (nephrographic phase, Fig. 2G) shows mid-left kidney mass concerning for WT and a smaller, lower pole lesion concerning for nephrogenic rest. The cortex and medulla are poorly differentiated and there are several small hypoattenuating foci and areas of contrast excretion puddling scattered throughout the kidney parenchyma bilaterally, consistent with medullary dysplasia.

The patient underwent left nephroureterectomy. Pathology examination (Figs. 2H–I) showed a Stage I WT with favorable histology within a background of diffuse perilobar nephrogenic rests. The kidney medulla was myxoid and expanded, consistent with BWS (Fig. 2I). No lymphadenopathy was identified. The patient underwent a short course of chemotherapy with vincristine and dactinomycin (EE-4A). After treatment, surveillance abdominal ultrasounds were continued every 3 to 4 months until age 8 years. After age 8 years, imaging decreased to an as-needed basis, and as of today, at the age 18, he remains tumor free.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we show that radiologic and pathologic findings can reveal an underlying disorder of kidney development and cancer predisposition syndrome, and there should be a low threshold to pursue a medical genetics evaluation. MRI has recently emerged as the primary recommended method for kidney tumor evaluation, it often provides better distinction between nephrogenic rests and malignant tumors, especially using contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging and diffusion-weighted imaging.6,13–15 This is highlighted in Figure 2, where the dysplastic areas in patient B’s left kidney are better resolved on MRI (Figs. 2D–F) than on CT (Fig. 2B). Notably, patient C had both a WT and a large nephrogenic rest, which are challenging to distinguish on CT. Nevertheless, signs of medullary dysplasia can be identified on ultrasound, CT, and MRI, and careful assessment for its presence should be performed at diagnostic and/or staging imaging of the nontumoral kidney in any patient who presents with a probable WT. Similarly, microscopic evaluation of nontumoral kidney parenchyma can establish the diagnosis of BWS, if features of medullary dysplasia are appropriately identified.10,12

Patients with BWS have an increased risk of developing bilateral WT among other tumor types, necessitating screening with abdominal ultrasounds every 3 to 4 months for the first 8 years of life.2,6,8 Furthermore, these patients have less functional kidney tissue at baseline, and may be disproportionately affected by a nephrectomy compared with patients with sporadic WT.6,16 A retrospective analysis of data from the SIOP (International Society of Pediatric Oncology) showed that BWS patients have a high response rate to preoperative chemotherapy with vincristine and dactinomycin, in contrast to other syndromic WTs, and this strategy could reduce tumor burden and preserve kidney function in patients diagnosed at later stages.6,17 They may also be candidates for nephron-sparing surgery (NSS), which in recent years has resulted in excellent outcomes with low recurrence rates when used either alone or combined with preoperative chemotherapy, despite the technical challenges and risks of NSS.2,6,18,19 While NSS was not available at the time of patient C’s procedure, patients A and B would have benefited from earlier detection of BWS or at least increased suspicion of a possible underlying genetic syndrome and subsequent consideration as candidates for NSS. Finally, a BWS diagnosis may have an impact on the patient’s future reproductive choices and may lead to genetic screening of other family members. Early diagnosis of BWS thus has significant impact on both tumor management and long-term health screening, and radiologic and pathologic examination should be appropriately evaluated for signs of BWS.
