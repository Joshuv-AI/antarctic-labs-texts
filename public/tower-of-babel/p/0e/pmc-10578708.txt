The effect of preexisting heart failure and coronary artery disease on motor function recovery in cerebral infarct patients: A retrospective observational study
Authors: Soyoung Kwak, Sang Gyu Kwak, Min Cheol Chang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Less is known about the impact of heart failure (HF) and coronary artery disease (CAD) on motor recovery after cerebral infarction although previous studies have reported that preexisting HF and CAD were associated with increased mortality in stroke patients as well as unfavorable functional outcomes. In this study, we aimed to accurately evaluate the impact of HF and CAD on motor recovery after cerebral infarction by including only patients with corona radiata using diffusion tensor tractography. A total of 110 patients were recruited, and diffusion tensor tractography was performed within 7 to 30 days of infarct onset. Motor function on the affected side was evaluated for each patient using the upper myocardial infarction (MI), lower MI, modified Brunnstrom classification, and the functional ambulation category at the onset of stroke and 6 months after the onset of stroke. The influence of preexisting HF and CAD on the recovery of motor function were analyzed, adjusting for critical factors for motor recovery after stroke, namely the corticospinal tract condition, lesion location, age, and upper and lower MIs at the onset of stroke. No significant difference was found in motor outcomes according to the presence of HF, while poorer outcomes in motor function of the lower extremities and gait ability were observed in patients with CAD compared to patients without CAD. Motor function of the lower extremities and gait ability in cerebral infarct patients with CAD is more impaired than those without CAD.

1. Introduction

Motor dysfunction is a major sequela of cerebral infarctions. More than half of the patients have significant motor dysfunction after cerebral infarction. This hinders stroke patients’ independent activities of daily living and deteriorates their quality of life. Several factors, including lesion location, condition of the corticospinal tract (CST), age, and initial motor function after cerebral infarction, affect motor outcomes after cerebral infarction.[1–4]

Heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome rather than a single pathological diagnosis, arising from structural and/or functional impairment of the heart which can lead to elevated intracardiac pressure and reduced cardiac output.[5] HF is known to be a risk factor for cerebral infarction.[6,7] In addition, previous studies have identified that preexisting HF is associated with increased mortality in stroke patients as well as unfavorable functional outcomes at discharge.[8–10] Similarly, coronary artery disease (CAD) is independently associated with increased mortality in patients with stroke,[11] and is the most common etiology of HF.[12] However, most studies have evaluated the functional status of patients using only modified Ranking Scale score, a seven-point scale ranging from 0 (no symptoms) to 6 (death), rather than directly evaluating motor function, and did not control for factors that can affect motor recovery after stroke other than age and stroke severity represented by National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores. Therefore, it remains elusive whether the presence of HF has an impact on motor recovery itself after stroke.

In this study, we aimed to accurately evaluate the impact of HF and CAD on motor recovery after cerebral infarction by including only patients with corona radiata infarct with severe motor impairment at the onset of stroke and controlling for CST condition using diffusion tensor tractography, age, and preexisting motor function.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Subjects

The study protocol was approved by the institutional research board of the university hospital. Written informed consent was waived by the institutional review board of a university hospital because this study was performed retrospectively using anonymous data. The Helsinki Declaration was adhered to in this study. A total of 110 patients (age: 64.8 ± 9.5, male:female = 52:58) admitted to the university hospital between January 2006 and October 2019 were enrolled in this retrospective study (Table 1). Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) first ever stroke; (2) age 20 to 79 years; (3) severe weakness of the affected extremities, to the extent of inability to move the affected limbs without gravity and complete weakness of the affected hand (finger flexors and extensors) within 24 hours of onset; (4) an infarct at the level of the corona radiata explaining motor weakness (including at least the posterior half of the middle third of the corona radiata and fibers adjacent to the lateral wall of the inferior horn of the lateral ventricle) determined by brain MRI with confirmation by a neuroradiologist; (5) diffusion tensor tractography performed within 7 to 30 days of onset for the evaluation of preservation of CST. Patients who had serious medical complications such as pneumonia or sepsis, which would hinder rehabilitation from onset to final evaluation, were excluded. All patients received movement therapy for 6 days/week (Monday through Friday, 2.5 h/day; Saturday, 1 h/day) to improve motor function, postural control, and movement patterns in the affected extremity.

2.2. Determining the presence of chronic heart failure and coronary artery disease

All patients underwent evaluation of risk factors for stroke, including history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, cigarette smoking, heart disease including atrial fibrillation, and CAD, and underwent repeated electrocardiogram and echocardiography. Atrial fibrillation was defined according to the criteria suggested by the European Society of Cardiology and was confirmed by repeated electrocardiogram; CAD, including myocardial infarction (MI), angina pectoris (AP), angioplasty, or coronary artery bypass surgery, was assessed by thorough history taking and medical confirmation. HF was defined according to the 2021 ESC guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic heart failure[5] and was confirmed by a cardiologist. HF severity was assessed using the New York Heart Association criteria. Among the 110 patients, 13 (11.8%) patients had HF, 4 (3.6%) patients had CAD (2 patients with previous MI and 2 patients with AP), and 1 patient had both HF and AP. 2.3. Diffusion tensor tractography

The mean duration from onset to diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) was 11.6 ± 5.9 days (range: 7–30 days). DTI was performed using a sensitivity-encoding head coil on a 1.5 T Philips Gyroscan Intera (Hoffman-LaRoche, Ltd, Best, the Netherlands) with single-shot echo-planar imaging and navigator echo. Sixty contiguous slices (matrix = 128 × 128; field of view = 221 × 221 mm2; time to echo = 76 ms; repetition time = 10,726 ms; sensitivity encoding factor = 2; echo planar imaging factor = 67; b = 1000 s/mm/mm; number of excitations = 1; and slice thickness = 2.3 mm) were acquired for each of the 32 noncollinear diffusion-sensitizing gradients. The scanning time for DTI acquisition was 7 minutes and 32 seconds. Head motion effects and image distortions due to eddy currents were corrected using affine multiscale two-dimensional (2D) registration. The integrity of the CST was evaluated via fiber assignment by continuous tracking using a three-dimensional fiber reconstruction algorithm in Philips PRIDE software (Philips Medical Systems, Best, the Netherlands). CST fiber tracking was performed with a fractional anisotropy (FA) threshold of 0.2 and a direction threshold of 60°. In each case, a seed region of interest (ROI) was drawn in the CST portion of the anterior mid-pons on a 2D FA color map, and another ROI was drawn in the CST portion of the anterior low pons on a 2D FA color map. Fiber tracts passing through both ROIs were designated as final tracts of interest (Fig. 1). In the 58 included patients, the CST was preserved around the infarct; in 52 patients, the CST was interrupted by the infarct.

2.4. Measurement of motor function

Motor function was measured twice in each patient: at infarct onset and 6 months after infarct onset. Motricity indices (MIs) were used to measure motor function (maximum score = 100). The function of the affected hand was categorized using the modified Brunnstrom classification (MBC) as follows: (1) unable to move fingers voluntarily; (2) able to move fingers voluntarily; (3) able to close the affected hand voluntarily but unable to open the hand; (4) able to grasp a card between the thumb and the medial side of the index finger and able to extend fingers slightly; (5) able to pick up and hold a glass and extend fingers; and (6) able to catch and throw a ball in a near-normal fashion and able to button and unbutton a shirt. Walking ability was quantified using the standardized functional ambulation category (FAC), which is based on the characterization of the level of assistance required during a 15-minute walk. The 6 FAC categories are as follows: (0) nonambulatory; (1) need for continuous support from 1 person; (2) need for intermittent support from 1 person; (3) need for verbal supervision only; (4) need for help on stairs and uneven surfaces; and (5) ability to walk independently anywhere. The reliability and validity of the MI, MBC, and FAC have been well established. We set MBC ≥ 5 and FAC ≥ 3 as the cutoff values for voluntary hand movement and gait achievement, respectively.

2.5. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0. Differences in the improvement of motor outcomes (upper limb MI, lower limb MI, MBC, and FAC) between patients with HF and those without HF and those between patients with CAD and patients without CAD were analyzed using one-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with the preservation of CST, age, and upper limb and lower limb MIs at infarct onset as covariates. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

3. Results

In the comparison between patients with HF and those without HF, no significant difference was observed in the measurements of motor function (upper MI, lower MI, MBC, and FAC) at 6 months after infarct onset (ANCOVA, P > .05) (Table 1). In contrast, in the comparison between patients with CAD and those without CAD, lower MI and FAC were significantly larger in patients without CAD than in those with CAD (ANCOVA, lower MI, P = .049; FAC, P < .001) (Table 3). However, upper MI and MBC at 6 months after infarct onset were not significantly different between patients with and without CAD (ANCOVA, P > .05) (Table 3).

4. Discussion

In this study, we investigated whether HF and CAD affect motor outcomes in patients with corona radiata infarct and found that there was no significant difference in motor outcomes according to the presence of HF, while poorer outcomes in motor function of the lower extremities and gait ability were observed in patients with CAD compared to patients without CAD.

HF is known to be associated with poor stroke outcomes, including higher short-term and long-term mortality rates, longer duration of hospital stay, more severe stroke symptoms, and higher numbers of patients discharged to long-term care facilities.[8–10] This can be explained by the physiological alterations seen in chronic HF, such as body fluid imbalances, worsening of cerebral tissue oxygenation, hemodynamic disturbances in arterial and venous systems, endothelial dysfunction, and proinflammatory and prothrombotic conditions.[7,13–15] However, although our study showed a tendency for poorer outcomes in patients with HF compared to those without HF, the differences did not reach statistical significance. The relatively small number of patients enrolled in this study might be responsible for this, aside from the fact that the prevalence of patients with HF in our study (11.8%) was similar to that in a previous study (9.1% in Pongmoragot et al, 2016 and 9.7% of Vemmos et al, 2012). In addition, the relatively mild symptoms of patients with HF in our study (the majority of patients with HF [76.9%] were categorized as New York Heart Association class 1 and had preserved ejection fraction) could be another explanation.

CAD shares a common mechanism of atherosclerosis with cerebral infarction, which is a systemic inflammatory vascular disorder.[16,17] preexisting CAD is associated with poor outcomes in stroke rehabilitation as well as an increased risk of short-term and long-term mortality.[11,18] Our findings are in accordance with those of previous studies in that patients with CAD showed poorer motor outcomes in the lower extremities and gait ability 6 months after the onset of stroke when compared to patients without CAD. Notably, these poorer outcomes in motor recovery were only observed in the lower extremities. Functional improvement of the lower extremities during rehabilitation is reported to be faster than that of the upper extremities in stroke patients.[19] In addition, the metabolic abnormalities of the lower extremities in patients with HF were reported to be more closely associated with systemic exercise intolerance when compared to those of the upper extremities.[20] In patients with sarcopenia, it is known that muscle strength of the lower extremities showed the strongest association with functional performance[21] and the longitudinal changes in the muscle mass of the lower extremities were significantly higher than those of the upper extremities.[22] Although no study has ever examined whether there is a discrepancy in the recovery of motor function between the upper and lower extremities in stroke patients with CAD, it could be hypothesized based on previous studies that (1) preexisting CAD might hinder the recovery of motor function in the lower extremities that precedes the recovery of motor function in the upper extremities and/or (2) the systemic effects of preexisting CAD would have a greater effect on the lower extremities than the upper extremities, as in the cases of patients with HF and sarcopenia.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to directly evaluate the influence of preexisting HF and CAD on the recovery of motor function, adjusting for critical factors for motor recovery after stroke, namely CST condition, lesion location, age, and upper and lower MIs at the onset of stroke. However, our study has some limitations. Frist, the small number of patients with HF and CAD in our study is a major limitation. Second, the retrospective design hindered further interpretation of the study results. Third, we did not calculate an appropriate sample size for our study. The lack of predefined sample size may have impacted the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. Lastly, the wide age range of participants might have introduced confounding factors in the analysis of motor recovery results. Further well-designed prospective studies with larger scales are necessary to better understand this issue. Also, future studies should employ appropriate statistical methods to determine the optimal sample size and increase the robustness of the study. In addition, we recommend narrowing the age range or conducting subgroup analyses to better understanding how age impacts the observed effects.
