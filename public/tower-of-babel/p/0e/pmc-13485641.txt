A zone zero physician-modified endograft for blunt thoracic aortic injury
Authors: Marie-Lyne Bourque, Stéphane Breault, Clément Willot, William Fortin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blunt thoracic aortic injury (BTAI) requires urgent treatment, often limiting options in patients with unconventional anatomies. We report the case of a 61-year-old man with grade III BTAI adjacent to a dominant left vertebral artery originating from the aorta, precluding standard endovascular repair. An emergent zone 0 physician-modified endograft with partially precannulated dual fenestrations was performed, combined with left vertebral artery transposition and carotid-subclavian bypass. The procedure achieved complete exclusion of the pseudoaneurysm without complications. Follow-up imaging confirmed durable repair and branch patency. Therefore, physician-modified endograft represents a feasible option for emergent BTAI in unconventional anatomies.

Blunt thoracic aortic injury (BTAI) is a life-threatening condition in patients with polytrauma, with an estimated incidence of 8000 cases annually in the North America.1, 2, 3 A significant proportion of patients may progress to free rupture within the first 48 hours.

Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) is the first-line treatment for most patients with favorable anatomy, offering lower mortality and fewer neurologic complications than open repair.4,5 However, complex arch anatomy limits the use of standard endovascular solutions.

Physician-modified endografts (PMEGs) have emerged as a potential solution for complex anatomies when off-the-shelf devices are unsuitable and time constraints preclude custom-made grafts. This report describes a case of grade III BTAI treated with an urgent PMEG with a zone 0 landing in a patient with an unconventional aortic arch configuration, highlighting the feasibility of PMEG in emergency settings.

Case report

A 61-year-old man with coronary artery disease, dyslipidemia, and active smoking was urgently transferred to our trauma center following a high-speed collision.

In the trauma bay, he presented with tachycardia and an otherwise unremarkable primary survey. Physical examination revealed pain on the left thoracic and pelvic areas. Initial imaging revealed no acute abnormalities on cerebral and cervical computed tomography. A computed tomography angiography (CTA) showed bilateral rib fractures, a left hemothorax, a nonsurgical pelvic fracture, and a grade III BTAI at the aortic isthmus departing next to a dominant left vertebral artery originating from the aorta.

Orthogonal CTA reconstruction demonstrated no adequate circumferential proximal sealing zone for conventional TEVAR devices, and no off-the-shelf devices were within the indication for use (Fig 1). Hemodynamic monitoring and pressure control with a perfusion of labetalol was initiated, and the patient prepared for urgent PMEG with a zone 0 landing, a left vertebral artery transposition, and a carotid-subclavian bypass. Technique

Once transferred to the angiographic suite and placed under general anesthesia, two surgical teams worked simultaneously. Following vascular exposures and access, systemic heparinization was administered. Given the limited nonaortic injuries and the need for aortic manipulation, an activated clotting time above 300 seconds was targeted throughout the procedure.

Percutaneous retrograde right femoral access was gained under ultrasound guidance and closure devices were predeployed. A Lunderquist double-curve guidewire (Cook Medical) was positioned through a 24F DrySeal introducer sheath (Gore Medical). A Medtronic Captivia thoracic stent graft (VAMF3434150; Medtronic) was unsheathed and modified based on preoperative CTA measurements. The radiopaque markers and the first row of stents were removed, and an 8-mm fenestration for the left common carotid artery (LCCA) was created using electrocautery. The fenestration was reinforced using the tip of a Lunderquist guidewire. A second 11-mm proximal fenestration was created to accommodate the innominate artery, with slight counterclockwise rotation to match the patient's anatomy (Fig 2). The LCCA fenestration was precannulated with a Terumo Glidewire (Terumo International) which was incorporated into the delivery system in a monorail configuration. Graft modification and resheathing took approximately 25 minutes without prolonging the overall procedure time.Fig 2Physician-modified endograft (PMEG) before deployment. Meanwhile, through a supraclavicular incision, the LCCA, vertebral artery, and subclavian artery were exposed. The left vertebral artery was transposed to the LCCA, followed by construction of a carotid-subclavian bypass using an 8-mm Dacron graft. The graft was punctured, and a 6F sheath was inserted at the level of the LCCA ostium to cannulate the descending thoracic aorta. A 300-cm Glidewire was advanced and snared through the right femoral access, establishing through-and-through wire access.

Using a modified technique described by Canaud et al,6,7 the PMEG was advanced and positioned with assistance from the precannulated Glidewire. Retrograde angiography via the carotid access confirmed accurate positioning of the endograft, and the device was deployed.

Via the femoral access, a 90-cm 7F Flexor sheath (COOK Medical) was advanced on the through-and-through wire, allowing deployment of an 8 × 37-mm BeGraft Plus stent (Bentley InnoMed) into the LCCA fenestration. Lastly, the left subclavian artery was embolized using a 16-mm Amplatzer Plug (Abbott Cardiovascular). The carotid-subclavian bypass was then completed. The patient was extubated, hemodynamically stable, and neurologically intact. He was transferred to the intensive care unit for 48 hours of monitoring. His postoperative course was uneventful. A CTA at 48 hours postoperative confirmed exclusion of the traumatic pseudoaneurysm, adequate perfusion of the innominate trunk, and LCCA via the fenestrations, with a patent bypass and left vertebral artery. The patient was discharged home on the fifth day on single antiplatelet therapy. At 21 months, he had no complaints. CTA showed complete exclusion of the lesion with favorable remodeling (Fig 4). Discussion

Blunt thoracic aortic injuries often require rapid intervention. Standard TEVAR with a proximal landing in zones 2 or 3 provides adequate sealing for most isthmic lesions. In this case, however, a standard endovascular repair was not feasible because of a dominant left vertebral artery originating from the aortic arch and the absence of an adequate circumferential sealing zone.

Open repair with a frozen elephant trunk and cardiopulmonary bypass was considered but was deemed less favorable in the traumatic setting, particularly given the need for both subclavian and vertebral artery revascularization. A single-fenestration device to accommodate the LCCA combined with a proximal scallop was also considered. In our experience, however, this configuration carries a risk of proximal fixation elements protruding into the innominate artery, potentially leading to graft malapposition and compromised sealing. The Gore TAG thoracic branched endoprosthesis could likewise have been used with complete supra-aortic debranching. However, this strategy would have required extensive arch-vessel exposure, adding operative time, and potentially increasing neurologic risk. Based on our institutional experience with the technique described by Canaud et al,6,7 we favored a precannulated zone 0 PMEG, which facilitated controlled delivery, accurate graft alignment, and minimal arch manipulation. The addition of an innominate artery scallop provided a circumferential proximal seal exceeding 15 mm. Furthermore, the teamwork approach allowed the graft modifications to be performed without adding time to the overall procedure.

Whether PMEGs should be used as a first-line option compared with thoracic branched endoprosthesis grafts in urgent or emergent scenarios should, in the authors’ opinion, be guided by device availability, institutional experience, and expertise. Ultimately, the team considers that arch PMEGs provide greater adaptability to a wide range of arch anatomies than other options in emergency settings. This case illustrates that PMEG techniques may provide a viable endovascular alternative for complex arch anatomy when conventional options are unsuitable and time constraints preclude custom-made devices. Nevertheless, the use of PMEGs remains off-label and should be restricted to centers with appropriate multidisciplinary expertise.

Conclusions

TEVAR is the treatment of choice for most patients with BTAI. For patients with unconventional anatomies and urgent presentations, PMEGs may offer an effective alternative when standard endovascular or open surgical options are unsuitable. This case demonstrates that a zone 0 PMEG can achieve durable exclusion while preserving supra-aortic branch perfusion in urgent situations.
