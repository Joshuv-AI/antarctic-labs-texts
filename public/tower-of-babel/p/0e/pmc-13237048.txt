Long-term prognostic value of ctDNA in early breast cancer: insights from the neoadjuvant ABCSG-34 Trial
Authors: Daniel Egle, Dominik Hlauschek, Simon Peter Gampenrieder, Gabriel Rinnerthaler, Christian Singer, Georg Pfeiler, Rupert Bartsch, Gregor Huber, Angelika Pichler, Edgar Petru, Zsuzsanna Bago-Horvath, Anna-Sophia Kermanidis, Christian Fesl, Qing Zhou, Ricarda Graf, Sabrina Hammer, Nadia Dandachi, Martin Filipits, Michael Gnant, Ellen Heitzer, Marija Balic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is a promising biomarker in early breast cancer for assessing treatment response, minimal residual disease (MRD), and recurrence risk. In the ABCSG-34 phase II trial, we previously reported that persistent ctDNA during neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) was associated with higher residual cancer burden and lower rates of pathological complete response. Here, we present long-term follow-up data evaluating the prognostic relevance of ctDNA dynamics. ABCSG-34 randomized 400 patients to chemotherapy or endocrine therapy, with or without the MUC1 vaccine tecemotide. Tumor-informed SiMSen-Seq assays were used to assess ctDNA at baseline, mid-therapy, and end-of-therapy. Of 145 patients with ctDNA data, 109 had long-term follow-up (median: 7.1 years). Baseline ctDNA positivity was significantly associated with inferior overall survival (HR 2.12, p = 0.043) and shorter survival durations across all endpoints. Trends toward improved outcomes were observed in patients who cleared ctDNA during NAT, though statistical significance was not reached—likely due to limited sample size. These findings support baseline ctDNA as a prognostic marker in early breast cancer. While ctDNA clearance may reflect treatment efficacy, further validation in larger trials is needed. Ongoing studies will determine the role of ctDNA in guiding therapy and monitoring MRD.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy among women worldwide1. Despite advancements in early detection and curative treatment, recurrence continues to pose significant clinical challenges. Neoadjuvant therapy (NAT), administered prior to surgical intervention is regarded as a potential standard in high-risk breast cancer subtypes, with research focusing on risk- and response-adapted treatment individualization2. Traditional imaging and pathological assessments, while valuable, may not capture dynamic molecular changes occurring during NAT. For risk assessment, clinico-pathologic factors are typically used, but there is a clear need for dynamic and biologically informative biomarkers to guide personalized treatment3. Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), consisting of DNA fragments shed from tumors into the bloodstream, provides a sensitive and timely indicator of tumor burden, therapeutic efficacy, and recurrence4. Several studies highlight the potential of ctDNA analysis for predicting relapse in breast cancer5–8. A recent study demonstrated that personalized cancer monitoring can detect molecular residual disease (MRD) via ctDNA with high specificity, identifying breast cancer patients at high risk of relapse up to a year before clinical recurrence, thus potentially enabling earlier intervention6. Furthermore, multiple studies have shown that ctDNA clearance during NAT or after surgery is associated with improved outcomes, while persistent ctDNA predicts poorer response and a higher recurrence risk9–12. A study focusing on triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) patients reported that ctDNA detection at mid-NAT was significantly linked to reduced overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival underscoring its prognostic potential in this aggressive subtype11. Magbanua and colleagues demonstrated that ctDNA clearance during neoadjuvant therapy in patients with early-stage breast cancer was associated with lower residual cancer burden (RCB) and higher rates of pathological complete response (pCR) in TNBC9,13. However, the study highlighted the need for more sensitive assays to effectively assess ctDNA dynamics in hormone receptor-positive and HER2-positive subtypes.

We recently published a study from the ABCSG-34 trial in which we employed patient-specific ctDNA assays to monitor early breast cancer patients undergoing NAT. Persistent ctDNA at mid-treatment (MT) and at the end of therapy (EOT) was significantly associated with higher RCB (RCB II/III) and lower rates of pCR, whereas early ctDNA clearance correlated with improved treatment response. However, at the time of publication, long-term outcome data - such as invasive disease-free survival (iDFS), distant recurrence-free survival (DRFS), and overall survival (OS) - were not yet available, as the trial was originally not designed for extended follow-up. We now present new long-term follow-up data from a subset of patients, enabling evaluation of the prognostic value of ctDNA dynamics for long-term outcomes.

Results

Study population and prior ctDNA findings

In our earlier publication10, we reported a correlation between ctDNA dynamics and response to NAT in 145 out of 400 patients (27.3%) from the prospective, randomized, multicenter phase II ABCSG-34 trial14. Briefly, ctDNA was detectable in 63 of 145 patients (43.4%). Among these, ctDNA persisted in 25 of 63 patients (39.7%) at MT and in 15 of 83 patients (23.8%) at EOT. Detection of ctDNA at MT was significantly associated with higher RCB, while no ctDNA was detected in patients with pCR, and only 6.7% of those with RCB I had detectable ctDNA, compared to 30.6% and 29.6% in patients with RCB II and III, respectively. The present study focuses on the subset (n = 109 patients) who consented to long-term follow-up (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of the follow-up cohort were comparable to those of the overall ABCSG-34 population (Table 1), indicating that the analyzed subset is representative. The greatest observed differences were seen for RCB (more RCB II) and subtype (more TNBC) indicating that the subset may have a slightly higher risk than the overall cohort. We evaluated the association between ctDNA status and long-term outcomes, including IDFS, DRFS, and OS. Association of baseline ctDNA with long-term outcomes and patterns of recurrence

Detection of ctDNA in plasma during NAT correlated with long-term outcomes and recurrence patterns (Supplementary Table S1). Among patients treated with chemotherapy, the majority of first iDFS events were distant recurrences, occurring in 19 out of 80 patients (23.8% of the total chemotherapy group), accounting for 63% of iDFS events in this subgroup. In contrast, for patients who received NET, most first iDFS events were deaths without a prior documented recurrence, observed in 6 out of 9 patients (20.7% of the endocrine group), representing 66.7% of IDFS events.

With respect to baseline ctDNA and long-term outcomes, survival analyses revealed that patients with detectable ctDNA had poorer outcomes across all endpoints. IDFS was 58.5% in the ctDNA-positive group vs. 70.8% in ctDNA-negative patients (HR 1.53, 95% CI 0.81–2.86, p = 0.187; Fig. 2A). DRFS was 60.9% vs. 77.9%, respectively (HR 1.98, 95% CI 0.99–3.98, p = 0.053; Fig. 2B), and OS was 62.7% in ctDNA-positive patients compared to 81.2% in ctDNA-negative patients (HR 2.12, 95% CI 1.02–4.41, p = 0.043; Fig. 2C). Because the proportional hazards assumption was violated, restricted mean survival time (RMST) analysis was also conducted. Within the first 8 years post-surgery, detectable baseline ctDNA was associated with significantly shorter time to an iDFS event (-13.6 months, 95% CI -26.7 to -0.6), shorter time to DRFS event (-16.0 months, 95% CI -28.5 to -3.4), and shorter OS (-12.3 months, 95% CI -22.8 to -1.9) (Table 2).Fig. 2Long-term outcome by ctDNA measurement at baseline.Kaplan–Meier curves of (A) invasive disease-free survival (iDFS), B distant recurrecen-free survival (DRFS), and C overall survival (OS) stratified by ctDNA detction at baseline. ctDNA dynamics during treatment and their association with long-term outcomes

In contrast to BL measurements, ctDNA status at MT and EOT showed a non-significant trend for long term parameters iDFS, DRFS, or OS (Table 2). Although hazard ratios at MT were comparable to those observed at baseline, the reduced number of ctDNA-positive samples limited the statistical power to detect significant associations.

It is important to note that analyses of post-baseline ctDNA samples are potentially subject to selection bias, as missing ctDNA data may not be random. Specifically, we found that ctDNA positivity at baseline was associated with a higher likelihood of missing EOT ctDNA data (Odds Ratio = 2.73; 95% CI 0.99–7.52), suggesting that previous ctDNA status may be associated with follow-up sample availability and must be considered when interpreting the results. See also Supplemental Table S4 for a comparison of patient characteristics between those with and without EOT sample.

ctDNA status and its association with long-term vaccine efficacy

Since the overall aim of the ABCSG-34 trial was to evaluate the efficacy of a therapeutic cancer vaccine in patients undergoing neoadjuvant therapy, we also sought to explore whether ctDNA status could serve as a biomarker for long-term vaccine efficacy. A stratified analysis by ctDNA status revealed no statistically significant differences between treatment arms (Supplementary Table 2). However, patients who were ctDNA-negative exhibited more favorable long-term outcomes following vaccination compared to those who were ctDNA-positive (Fig. 2D, E). In contrast, outcomes in the ctDNA-positive subgroup were less consistent and the small sample size limited interpretability.

Although these findings suggest a potential interaction between ctDNA status and vaccine response, the interaction p-values for distant DRFS (p = 0.15) and OS (p = 0.09) did not reach statistical significance. As such, these trends must be interpreted with caution and may be due to chance, underscoring the need for larger studies to validate these observations.

Discussion

The role of ctDNA as a dynamic biomarker in early breast cancer has gained increasing attention, with growing evidence supporting its clinical utility in monitoring treatment response, detecting MRD, and predicting long-term outcomes. Our study, conducted within the framework of the ABCSG-34 trial, contributes to this evolving field by evaluating the prognostic implications of ctDNA detection at multiple timepoints during and after NAT. Our earlier ABCSG-34 analysis was among the first to demonstrate the clinical relevance of ctDNA clearance in the neoadjuvant setting10. Since then, multiple independent investigations and randomized clinical trials have substantiated its prognostic significance5,6,9,11. In the present study, we build upon this initial observation by providing extended long-term follow-up, thereby further strengthening the growing body of high-quality evidence showing that ctDNA clearance is a robust and reproducible predictor of long-term clinical outcomes. We show that baseline ctDNA positivity is associated with inferior OS (HR 2.12) indicating a twofold increase in mortality rate. RMST analyses confirmed shorter time to recurrence and death in ctDNA-positive patients, reinforcing its prognostic value.

While ctDNA clearance during NAT trended toward improved outcomes, the associations did not reach statistical significance - likely due to limited sample size and missing post-treatment data. Importantly, we found evidence of selection bias, with patients who were ctDNA-positive at baseline more likely to lack follow-up samples, a known limitation in longitudinal ctDNA studies. It needs to be considered that this bias may have confounded (attenuated) observed associations of ctDNA positivity with long-term outcome. Nevertheless, our results align with those of Parson et al. and Magbanua et al., who showed that ctDNA clearance correlates with higher pCR rates and lower residual tumor burden, reinforcing its potential as a real-time marker of treatment response11,13,15. The predictive value of mid-therapy ctDNA for long-term survival, however, remains uncertain, both in our data and prior reports5. Larger, standardized trials with extended follow-up are essential to clarify this association.

Our results are also consistent with emerging data from other large trials. Lin et al. and Shaw et al. have shown that post-treatment ctDNA strongly predicts recurrence, particularly in ER-positive and TNBC populations, and that repeated longitudinal testing increases its prognostic value16,17. Data from large adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibitor trials are currently being evaluated and are expected to provide further insight into the frequency of ctDNA detection and its prognostic significance in ER-positive early breast cancer. Technical advancements, such as whole-genome sequencing (WGS), ultra-deep sequencing, and AI-supported variant detection, have further improved MRD detection sensitivity. While our tumor-informed SiMSen-Seq assay represents an early form of personalized ctDNA monitoring, newer platforms track dozens to thousands of patient-specific variants. WGS-based assays16,17 now offer greater sensitivity and are likely to become the future standard, particularly for low-shedding tumors like ER-positive early breast cancer.

Given the original aim of ABCSG-34 to assess the efficacy of a therapeutic cancer vaccine, we also explored whether ctDNA status predicted vaccine response. While no statistically significant interaction was observed, ctDNA-negative patients appeared to derive greater long-term benefit from tecemotide, suggesting a potential link between minimal residual disease burden and immunologic responsiveness. The immunologic concept underlying the MUC1 vaccine approach in ABCSG-34 is rooted in the altered glycosylation pattern of tumor-associated MUC1, which enhances antigen exposure and facilitates recognition by both humoral and cellular immune components, enabling effective vaccine-induced priming. Because the vaccination was administered neoadjuvantly, our exploratory analyses may suggest initial immune priming followed by immune-mediated eradication in the minimal residual disease (MRD) setting. These findings remain exploratory and should be viewed as hypothesis-generating for future clinical trials.

In summary, our long-term follow-up from the ABCSG-34 trial confirms that baseline ctDNA positivity is a strong predictor of poorer survival, consistent with previous literature. Although ctDNA clearance during NAT is promising as a response biomarker, its utility in predicting long-term survival requires further validation. Post-treatment ctDNA persistence remains one of the most robust indicators of recurrence risk, supporting its potential role in guiding adjuvant therapy escalation. To fully realize the clinical potential of ctDNA, future large-scale, multi-center studies should focus on improving assay sensitivity, defining optimal sampling schedules, and evaluating ctDNA-guided therapeutic strategies. Ongoing trials such as TRACERx-Breast (NCT03077776) and c-TRAK TN NCT03145961), are adding to clarification of the value of ctDNA-guided treatment decisions18.

Methods

Patient cohort

In the ABCSG-34 trial, patients (n = 400) were randomized based on hormone receptor (HR) expression, menopausal status, histopathological grade, and Ki-67 1:1 to receive either the therapeutic cancer vaccine tecemotide (formerly known as Stimuvax or L-BLP25) or no vaccine, in combination with either chemotherapy (NCT; n = 311) or endocrine therapy (NET; n = 89), as selected by the treating investigator14. Tumor response was assessed as RCB (rate of RCB0/1 as primary endpoint) and pCR14. Although neither the addition of the MUC1 vaccine nor chemotherapy sequence (standard versus reverse; anthracycline/ cyclophosphamide followed by taxane) significantly improved response rates to NAT14,19, long-term follow-up data suggest a potential clinical benefit from the vaccine20. Peripheral blood was collected at baseline (BL), after four chemotherapy cycles or 12 weeks of endocrine therapy (MT), and at EOT before surgery. ctDNA data were available for 145 patients (NCT, n = 102; NET, n = 43). A correlation of ctDNA dynamics and response has been published previously21. The availability of long-term follow-up allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the clinical relevance of previously generated ctDNA data. Thirty-six patients were excluded from the follow-up cohort due to withdrawal of consent or absence of follow-up consent, resulting in a final dataset of 109 patients with both ctDNA and long-term outcome data (80 patients from the chemotherapy cohort and 29 from the endocrine therapy cohort) (Fig. 1). This subset provides a valuable opportunity to assess the prognostic significance of ctDNA dynamics in relation to key long-term clinical endpoints, including invasive disease-free survival (iDFS), distant recurrence-free survival (DRFS), and overall survival (OS).

Ethics approval

All patients provided written informed consent prior to randomization in the ABCSG-34 trial. Additionally, all patients included in this follow-up analysis gave separate written consent for extended follow-up, allowing for the collection of long-term survival data or were already deceased at the time of data collection. The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and the ICH Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice (EudraCT#: 2011-004822-85) and was approved by the appropriate Ethics Committees of each of the participating centers before study initiation. All patients provided written informed consent. The translational part reported in this manuscript was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Graz (27-500 ex 14/15).

Tumor-informed ctDNA assessment

The methods for tissue sequencing and ctDNA analyses have been previously described in detail by Zhou et al. 10; a concise summary is provided below for context. DNA from FFPE diagnostic cores was extracted using the GeneRead™ DNA FFPE kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). cfDNA was isolated using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) as previously described21.

Tumor-informed assays were developed based on tissue sequencing using the QIAseq Human Breast Cancer Targeted Panel (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) targeting 93 genes. Libraries were prepared according to the manufacturer´s recommendation and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 550 instrument (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) in a 150 bp paired-end mode. Variant calling was performed using the smCounter V1 pipeline22. Following filtration for germline and subclonal variants, three to six pathogenic and likely pathogenic mutations were selected to design personalized SiMSen-seq assays for ctDNA analysis23. All SiMSen-seq assays were designed and validated according to Ståhlberg et al. 24 and described previously21. Samples were considered ctDNA-positive when any tracked mutation was detected at a variant allele frequency (VAF) ≥ 0.1%. ctDNA clearance was defined as a transition from a previously ctDNA-positive result (VAF ≥ 0.1%) to an undetectable level (no tracked mutation detected) at a subsequent timepoint, including MT and/or EOT.

Statistical analysis

IDFS, DRFS and OS were defined according to STEEP v2 criteria, with time-to-event analyses starting from primary surgery. Patients without events were censored at their last known follow-up. Associations between ctDNA status at BL, MT, and EOT and outcomes were assessed using Cox models and Kaplan–Meier analysis, with hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CI) reported. As proportional hazards assumptions may not hold, restricted mean survival time (RMST) over 8 years was additionally estimated using pseudovalue regression with an identity link25.
