Long-term outcomes and GVHD in allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation abroad: a 14-year UAE multi-center cohort
Authors: Naveed Syed, Mohamed Abuhaleeqa, Fatema Mohammed Al Kaabi, Naser Alzein, Zainul Aabideen, Aydah M. Al-Awadhi, Arif Alam, Azmat Ali Khan, Imrana Afrooz, Farooq Ahmed Mir, Riad Alhasan, Moussab Damlaj, Osama Taher Anaam, Manar Hassan Mohamed, Gehad ElGhazali, Kayane Mheidly, Shahrukh Hashmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHematopoietic cell transplant (HCT) data are well reported, but for cohorts transplanted abroad and returning to countries without transplant services, long-term outcomes such as chronic graft versus host disease (GVHD) and overall survival remain poorly captured, underscoring the need for longitudinal follow-up.MethodsWe conducted a 14-year retrospective multi-center study of allogeneic HCT recipients followed at three tertiary hospitals in the United Arab Emirates(UAE). Transplant characteristics, conditioning, GVHD prophylaxis, and outcomes were abstracted from medical records. Crude survival was reported for all indications, Kaplan–Meier (KM) overall survival for diseases with ≥20 cases.ResultsA total of 363 allogeneic HCTs were analyzed. Overall, cohort characteristics differed from those reported in the literature: adults undergoing HCT were relatively young, female predominance was observed in pediatric malignancies and immune deficiencies, and matched unrelated donor (MUD) utilization was limited, while peripheral blood stem cell use was higher than typically described. In contrast, haploidentical donor utilization, conditioning intensity, and GVHD prophylaxis patterns were broadly consistent with international practice. GVHD incidence was high:69.2% in adults; 45.7% in children. In adults with acute myeloid leukemia (AML), acute GVHD was associated with an inferior 5-year OS (p = 0.036). In pediatric AML, both acute-/chronic GVHD were linked to poor survival (<50%), while survival for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-/T-ALL) exceeded 85%. cGVHD worsened overall survival (OS) more in pediatrics than adults.ConclusionsAlthough most findings aligned with established benchmarks, the high GVHD incidence and its association with adverse outcomes highlight the need for improved prevention strategies and the development of local transplant services.

Introduction

Hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) is a curative therapy for numerous malignant and non-malignant hematologic disorders [1–3], but graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality [4–6]. Outcomes are primarily assessed by overall survival (OS) and, for allogeneic HCT, by the incidence and severity of acute and chronic GVHD and transplant-related mortality (TRM).

Global HCT activity is well documented by the Worldwide Network for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (WBMT) [7] and has expanded substantially with broader access and evolving donor strategies, including increased use of haploidentical donors. However, reporting gaps persist between regions. In the United Arab Emirates (UAE), most patients historically travelled abroad for HCT, and although local programs have recently emerged, outcome data for patients transplanted internationally but followed locally remain limited [8, 9].

Our pilot study highlighted a high prevalence of GVHD (62.2%) among patients transplanted abroad despite a young cohort and a predominance of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) identical related donors [10], suggesting that fragmented cross-border care may adversely affect outcomes. That study, however, was single-center with limited sample size and follow-up.

To address these gaps, we performed a 14-year multi-center analysis of all HCT recipients followed in Abu Dhabi. Objectives were to describe transplant activity, evaluate long-term OS across major disease groups, and assess acute and chronic GVHD incidence in a larger, representative cohort, providing essential benchmarks for this unique cross-border HCT population.

Methods

Patient identification and data collection

Following Institutional Review Board approval (SSMC REC-444; 11/30/2022), data were retrospectively extracted from the electronic medical records of three tertiary centers in Abu Dhabi: Sheikh Shakhbout Medical City, Tawam Hospital, and Sheikh Khalifa Medical City. A trained team of physicians and research staff used keyword-based searches (bone marrow transplant, stem cell transplant, graft-versus-host disease) and a standardized electronic case report form (eCRF).

Between 2009 and 2023, 501 patients were identified who had undergone transplantation abroad, primarily in the United States of America, United Kingdom, India, South Korea, Jordan, Egypt, Singapore, Lebanon, and Pakistan. Of these 363 met the inclusion criteria. Patients were excluded due to autologous HCT (n = 91), fewer than three follow-up visits (n = 20), or insufficient medical records (n = 27).

Insufficient medical records were defined as absence of key transplant or outcome data (e.g., donor type, graft source, or major post-transplant events). Because patients underwent transplantation abroad and presented locally for follow-up at variable intervals, inclusion required survival to establish care in the UAE; therefore, early mortality or complications managed exclusively at the original transplant centers may be under-represented.

Patient classification and definitions

Patients aged ≥ 18 years at transplantation were classified as adults. For malignant disorders, the diagnosis date was defined by the bone marrow biopsy; for non-malignant conditions (e.g., inherited marrow failure or immune deficiencies), the first clinical documentation was used. Second transplants were analyzed as independent cases in the main cohort and reported separately owing to their small number.

Transplant characteristics and GVHD Definitions

Donor classification was performed according to CIBMTR/EBMT registry definitions [11]:

Matched related donor (MRD): HLA-identical siblings or related family members (6/6, 8/8 or 10/10); Matched unrelated donors(MUD): High-resolution 10/10 or 8/8 unrelated donors; Mismatched related donor (MMRD): 1–2 allele mismatched related donors (9/10, 8/10); Haploidentical: half-matched related donors (typically ≤ 7/10); Mismatched unrelated donor (MMUD): ≥1 allele mismatched unrelated donors.

Conditioning regimens were assigned as myeloablative (MAC), reduced intensity conditioning (RIC), or non-myeloablative (NMA) based on explicit physician documentation when available; otherwise, EBMT/CIBMTR criteria and agent dosing were applied [12]. Selected severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) cases were categorized as “no conditioning” when appropriate.

GVHD classification followed National Institutes of Health (NIH) consensus criteria. When documentation was incomplete, onset ≤100 days post-transplant was categorized as acute GVHD and >100 days as chronic GVHD to maintain standardized assignment. Cases in which both acute and chronic GVHD were documented without clear temporal distinction were reported separately as ‘both reported,’ rather than assigned to an overlap subtype, as the cohort period (2009–2023) preceded consistent adoption of formal overlap definitions [13, 14]. Although contemporary recommendations advocate classification based on clinical manifestations and organ involvement rather than the historical 100-day cutoff, retrospective application of these criteria was not feasible. Many GVHD diagnoses occurred at external centers, and physician records frequently lacked precise onset dates or adequate phenotypic detail to reliably distinguish acute from chronic presentations. Given these documentation constraints and the extended study period spanning evolving diagnostic standards, we applied the traditional time-based approach to ensure uniform and reproducible classification across heterogeneous historical records [15]. GVHD prophylaxis categories followed EBMT/CIBMTR definitions and were analyzed only when regimen data were complete.

Study outcomes and survival Analysis

The final cohort included 363 patients (230 pediatrics, 133 adults), classified by age at transplant. OS was measured from transplant to death or last follow-up. Cause-specific mortality could not be reliably determined because patients entered follow-up in the UAE at variable time points after transplantation abroad. The analysis is subject to potential survivorship bias, with under-representation of early post-transplant mortality and complications managed at the primary transplant centers. Survival analyses were therefore based on observed follow-up without adjustment for delayed entry. For analyses evaluating the association between GVHD and OS, patients who died prior to the development or documentation of chronic GVHD were not modeled separately, and no competing-risk or time-dependent analyses were performed. Accordingly, these findings reflect associations rather than causal effects and may be influenced by early transplant-related mortality. Overall survival (OS) was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier (KM) method, and group comparisons were performed using the log-rank test. Survival probabilities were reported at predefined time points with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. These analyses represent univariate comparisons. Crude survival was calculated as the proportion of patients alive at last follow-up and expressed as percentages. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

KM OS with confidence intervals was generated for disease groups with ≥ 20 patients; for all other indications, crude survival was reported and detailed in the supplementary material. Comparative analyses were performed when sample sizes allowed.

Transplant characteristics and trends are summarized for all diseases, while survival, conditioning intensity, and GVHD prophylaxis are presented only for groups with ≥ 20 cases. Outcomes for rarer indications, including crude survival and GVHD data, are provided in the supplementary material.

Results

Transplant activity and patient characteristics

Transplant activity and indications

A total of 363 HCTs were analyzed. The median age of adults was 35.3 years (IQR 26.6–49.1). Non-malignant disorders accounted for 192 cases (52.9%), followed by lymphoid (79, 21.8%), and myeloid (92, 25.3%) malignancies. Within non-malignant indications, thalassemia (41.1%) and primary immune deficiencies, including SCID (17.2%), were the most common. In the malignant cohort, acute myeloid leukemia (AML, 43.8%) and acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL, 36.8%) were the most common myeloid and lymphoid diagnoses, respectively.

A total of nine patients underwent a second allogeneic transplant. Only one case occurred in adults (1/133, 0.8%), performed for relapsed AML. The remaining eight were pediatric patients (8/230, 3.4%), including β-thalassemia (n = 1), sickle cell disease (SCD, n = 1), myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS, n = 3), severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID, n = 2), and dyskeratosis congenita (DC, n = 1). Temporal trends in allogeneic transplant volume over the study period are shown in Suppl. Fig. 1.

A similar pattern was seen in lymphoid malignancies, with 52 adults (65.8%; median 33.4 years) and 27 pediatric cases (34.2%; median 6.6 years). Donor type distribution and temporal trends

As detailed in Suppl. Table 2, donor type varied by disease category and age group. In myeloid and lymphoid malignancies, MRD was the most common donor type, while alternative donors were more frequently used in non-malignant disorders, particularly thalassemia and SCID. Use of MUD was limited and largely confined to adult AML and lymphoid malignancies. Temporal trends (Fig. 1) show increasing use of haploidentical transplantation from 2013 onward, with low utilization of MUD, MMRD, and MMUD. Nationality and regional distribution

As illustrated in Suppl. Fig. 2, UAE nationals constituted nearly two-thirds of all transplant recipients, with smaller proportions from the rest of the Middle East, South Asia, Africa, and other regions.

Stem cell source by age group

Peripheral blood was the most common stem cell source in both adults (67.6%) and pediatric patients (63.4%), followed by bone marrow (12.8% and 18.9%, respectively). Umbilical cord blood was infrequently used (~3% in both groups), while the source was unknown in 16.5% of adults and 12.1% of pediatric transplants. More details are available in Suppl. Table 4.

GVHD incidence and its association with survival

Overall incidence of GVHD and association with survival

The cumulative incidence of GVHD and its association with overall survival are shown in Table 4. Among adults, any GVHD occurred in 69.2% (95% CI, 60.9–76.4), with acute and chronic GVHD in 36.8% (95% CI, 29.1–45.3) and 48.9% (95% CI, 40.5–57.3), respectively. No statistically significant differences in OS were observed across GVHD categories (p > 0.05). In pediatric patients, overall GVHD incidence was lower at 45.7% (95% CI, 39.3–52.1), with acute GVHD in 27.8% (95% CI, 22.4–33.9) and chronic GVHD in 30.4% (95% CI, 24.9–36.7). Discussion

Until 2022, all UAE patients requiring HCT traveled abroad, and many still do so based on prior experiences [9]. Despite this long-standing practice, systematic outcome data have been limited. Our earlier single-center analysis identified a high GVHD burden [10]. This multi-center study expands that work, providing the first comprehensive report of 363 patients transplanted abroad and followed at all three tertiary centers in Abu Dhabi. Major findings are summarized in the main text, with detailed methodology, crude survival estimates, conditioning regimens, GVHD prophylaxis, and disease-specific GVHD incidence included in the supplementary material.

In adults, AML and ALL were the leading malignant indications for allogeneic HCT in keeping with published reports [1, 4]. Unlike many pediatric series where malignancies predominate, non-malignant disorders, particularly thalassemia and primary immune deficiencies, formed the majority in our cohort, reflecting their higher prevalence in the Gulf region [16–18].

Adults with AML underwent HCT at a younger median age (45.5 years) than typically reported in Western series (55–65 years), consistent with referral patterns favoring younger candidates [19, 20]. A male predominance was observed in adult myeloid and lymphoid malignancies [21], whereas among children, females predominated across myeloid and lymphoid malignancies as well as SCID and SCD (Table 1, Suppl. Table 1), diverging from most reports noting male predominance [22]. Higher early mortality among male infants, a recognized pattern in SCID and SCD, may contribute to this difference [23].

As illustrated in Suppl. Fig. 1, HCT volume increased steadily over the study period, peaking in 2021 before declining thereafter. The predominance of UAE nationals in this cohort likely reflects higher return rates to local follow-up and registry capture after transplantation abroad, whereas non-UAE patients may remain overseas for ongoing care. MRD donors remained the predominant source, followed by haploidentical transplants [1, 24], reflecting the global rise due to improved GVHD prophylaxis [25]. MUD, MMRD and MMUD use was minimal in both cohorts, contrasting sharply with CIBMTR data and likely related to challenges of cross-border referral and the absence of national donor registries (Fig. 1). A transient decline in activity occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic [1]. Although second transplantation comprised a lower proportion of procedures in our cohort than reported in large registries, repeat transplants were observed predominantly in pediatric patients, consistent with published data demonstrating a higher need for re-transplantation in children due to graft failure or rejection in non-malignant disorders.

In adults, 5-year OS after allo-HCT for AML and ALL was 79.8% and 78.0% (Table 2). Crude survival was substantially lower (31.5%; Suppl. Table 5), reflecting loss to follow-up, which inflates KM estimates. While some studies report TRM or DFS, others using censored quantile regression show 5-year OS around 58% [26, 27]. Considering both KM and crude survival, our outcomes align broadly with OS-based reports; however, the relatively small sample size and low number of events limit interpretability and precision. The relatively higher observed OS is likely attributable to a combination of factors, including a younger cohort, predominance of MRD donors, survivorship beyond the early post-transplant period, and potential selection bias, as patients returning to the UAE may represent those who survived sufficiently long post-transplant and remained clinically stable without major complications. Additionally, censoring of patients lost to follow-up (often returning to their home countries) may further contribute to overestimation of survival. Missing documentation from overseas transplants and high follow-up attrition limited accurate reporting of transplant-related mortality (TRM), disease-free survival (DFS), and leukemia-specific mortality.

Among pediatric allogeneic HCT recipients, outcomes varied by diagnosis (Table 3). ALL demonstrated excellent survival (OS 87.7%), though crude survival fell to 41.1%, reflecting follow-up loss [28]. AML survival ranged from 65.8% to 84.7%, slightly above some benchmarks, but crude survival declined to 38% [29]. Non-malignant conditions, including β-thalassemia and SCID, showed consistently strong survival >90%, consistent with contemporary reports [30, 31]. Crude survival estimates for all indications are provided in Suppl. Table 6.

GVHD incidence patterns differed somewhat from CIBMTR reports. Any GVHD occurred in 69.2% of adults and 45.7% of children, both at the upper international range [1]. Pediatric acute (27.8%) and chronic GVHD (30.4%) were also toward the higher end [1, 32]. Only chronic GVHD in children was associated with inferior survival (p = 0.023), whereas registry data often highlight adverse outcomes for both acute and chronic GVHD [32]. The high use of peripheral blood stem cells in our cohort may have contributed to the observed GVHD incidence. Evidence from randomized trials and meta-analyses, largely in adult and unrelated-donor settings, demonstrates a higher risk of chronic GVHD with PBSC grafts, while the association with acute GVHD is less consistent [33, 34]. In contrast, data from pediatric sibling-donor cohorts suggest a higher incidence of grade II–IV acute GVHD with PBSC grafts, with less consistent differences in chronic GVHD [35]. This graft-source preference, early mortality among children who did not survive to establish follow-up at our centers, and predominance of non-malignant indications for transplantation may therefore have contributed to the overall GVHD burden observed in our population. Furthermore, late acute GVHD occurring beyond day 100, especially in children, could not be reliably distinguished from chronic GVHD due to incomplete documentation, potentially inflating the reported incidence of chronic GVHD in our cohort [36].

The high incidence of GVHD in adult AML and ALL recipients (68.5% and 71.1%) underscores the substantial burden of GVHD across both myeloid and lymphoid malignancies (Table 5). Acute GVHD occurred in ~one-third and chronic GVHD in > 40%, rates at the upper end of EBMT/CIBMTR reports [1, 37]. Despite standard conditioning and prophylaxis [38], elevated GVHD rates may reflect high peripheral stem cell grafts, MAC usage, and center-related variation [39]. Acute GVHD in AML was significantly associated with worse survival [40], whereas chronic GVHD had no significant effect(Table 6). In ALL, both forms were associated with numerically lower survival but lacked statistical significance [41]. In pediatric acute leukemias, GVHD incidence was similarly high [42, 43], despite guideline-concordant conditioning and prophylaxis [44]. GVHD incidence varied by disease in pediatric patients, being highest in ALL, intermediate in AML, and lower in non-malignant disorders, with a predominance of chronic or combined GVHD presentations. Non-malignant disorders showed lower GVHD rates, with chronic GVHD more common than acute [45, 46].

Survival patterns paralleled GVHD incidence: acute GVHD was associated with lower overall survival in pediatric AML (<50% vs. 100%), whereas β-thalassemia and SCID demonstrated > 90% survival irrespective of GVHD status (Table 8). Overall, acute GVHD was associated with poorer survival in both adult and pediatric AML, while chronic GVHD showed a variable association with outcomes and appeared less influential in non-malignant disorders.

Conclusions

This 14-year multi-center analysis of patients followed in the UAE after undergoing allogeneic HCT abroad highlights the unique features and challenges of cross-border transplantation. Adults undergoing allogeneic HCT were younger than in Western series, influenced by referral practices and regional demographics, while pediatric recipients showed an unexpected female predominance. MUD transplants were rare, emphasizing reliance on MRD donors and the absence of national donor registries. Survival outcomes varied across disease groups, with strong results in non-malignant disorders but reduced long-term survival in acute leukemias, particularly when GVHD was present. GVHD incidence in both adults and children was at the upper end of international ranges, potentially attributable to predominant peripheral blood stem cell use, myeloablative conditioning, variable center experience, and fragmented follow-up associated with overseas transplantation. These findings underscore the need to develop sustainable local transplant services, establish donor registries, and strengthen longitudinal follow-up to improve survival and long-term outcomes.
