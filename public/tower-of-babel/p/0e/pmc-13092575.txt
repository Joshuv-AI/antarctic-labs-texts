Clinician acceptability and use of smartphone digital photography in sexual assault forensic examination in an Australian regional district's sexual assault services
Authors: Anousha Victoire, Gemma Halliday, Shalu Singh, Parker Magin, Lyndal Bugeja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotography supports written and diagrammatic clinical documentation in forensic sexual assault examinations. In settings where specialist photographers are not available, clinician confidence in image capture affects the usage of photography as a clinical tool. A regional Australian health district with multiple sexual assault services across metropolitan, regional and rural sites had previously low usage of photography to support clinical documentation of injury. Smartphones were introduced as image capture devices and procedures were implemented to support their use and the secure storage of images. Training was provided to medical and nursing staff. In cases where injuries were present, the use of photography increased significantly from 13% to 27% (p = 0.0041). Surveys were conducted of clinicians to examine factors affecting use of and attitudes to photography in sexual assault examinations, both prior to and three years after introducing the new devices. Smartphones were generally well accepted by clinicians as an easy-to-use alternative to traditional single lens reflex (SLR) camera photography, particularly for documenting complex injuries that are hard to describe in words and diagrams alone. Smartphone photography may be particularly useful for rural and regional settings where clinicians have a lower caseload and therefore less regular experience with photography equipment, and no access to specialist forensic photographers.Keywords: Digital smartphone photography sexual assault forensic

Introduction

Role of photography in sexual assault examination

Photography is increasingly accepted by clinicians as an important aspect of clinical documentation supplementing written and diagrammatic records in forensic sexual assault (SA) examinations, though authors describe its role variously. While its longest history is in paediatrics where photo-colposcopy is standard to enable peer review of genital injury [1], photography is also considered by professional bodies and institutions as a standard method of documentation of injury in adult and adolescent SA examinations. The U.S. Department of Justice National Protocol for Intimate Partner Violence Medical Forensic Examinations states ‘photography is a useful tool for medical documentation of injury as well as memorializing damage to a patient's clothing or other belongings.’ [2] The UK Faculty of Forensic and Legal Medicine photography guidelines for healthcare professionals describe ‘the reluctance of practitioners to use digital cameras to document clinical injury findings,’ before noting the role of photographic images as ‘immensely valuable in supplementing written descriptions and hand-drawn body diagrams of injuries, and when properly taken, will assist in the justice process.’ [3] The Australian Faculty of Clinical Forensic Medicine describes additional benefits of photography, noting the primary purpose of forensic photography is to document clinical findings, with secondary purposes of aide-mémoire, peer review, use as evidence, teaching, research, and follow up of injury change over time [4].

Available evidence on patient acceptability in the forensic context is limited [5], though one Australian study noted photography to be acceptable to adult patients presenting for forensic examination after recent SA [6].

Clinician attitudes to photography in SA forensic examination have been mixed. The emphasis on photo-documentation as visual proof of injury has been critiqued as supporting a positivist overemphasis on presence of physical damage in SA, with potential implications for situations where lack of visual evidence may affect legal outcomes, with some clinicians in the same study reporting discomfort at ‘fragmenting and objectifying’ patients' bodies to photograph them [7]. A Sri Lankan study reported doctors agreeing that providing forensic photographs with a report will affect impartiality of legal professionals in court and give unnecessary information to defence lawyers, while also agreeing that photographs provide better understanding of the injuries than description alone [8].

Digital photography

Digital photography has been embraced as a practical tool to enable recording of clinical forensic evidence, peer review, teaching and electronic transfer of images, while also allowing the possibility of complainant review of the images at time of capture [9].

In a broader medical context, smartphone use has been shown to be generally acceptable to patients, with the caveat that the imaging device should be dedicated hospital equipment rather than a personal device, and the photographer should be the patient's own physician [10,11].

Digital photography in sexual assault forensic examination

A review of photography practices across 40 Sexual Assault Referral Centres (SARCs) in the UK reported that the photo-documentation process ‘need not be too technical or add cognitive dissonance that detracts the clinician from their primary focus’ [12]. In SARCs where photography was routinely included as part of the examination, it was performed using simple ‘point and shoot’ compact cameras. It was also noted that of the five SARCs that had provided training in the use of Digital Single Lens Reflex (DSLR) photography, none subsequently used their DSLR camera to document injuries [12].

Smartphone device use in clinical photography

Smartphone use has had significant uptake in clinical settings outside of SA. In a self-reported questionnaire-based cross-sectional study, 65% of 210 dermatologists surveyed reported using smartphones for clinical photography to gain advice from colleagues, for teaching purposes and for disease monitoring [13]. Artificial intelligence using smartphone collected clinical images have also been assessed to be highly accurate at diagnosing melanoma [14], and increasing numbers of smartphone applications have been developed to provide a novel approach to deliver dermatological care [15]. Smartphone use in general medical settings has been characterised by problems with privacy, lack of adherence to health policy, and issues around patient consent [16]. In the forensic medical setting, clinician storage of images in personal computers or phones has raised ethical and legal concerns around confidentiality and admissibility in court [8]. In this context, clinicians may be hesitant to adopt the technology unless these concerns are addressed.

Rationale

Despite investment in equipment and training and development of policy-compliant procedures, the use of photography was previously very limited in this New South Wales (NSW) regional health district's adult and adolescent SA services. Photo-documentation was underutilised as an aide-mémoire to assist in developing expert opinion, to supplement report writing and provide opportunity for peer review of injury documentation.

Having a specialist clinical photographer available 24/7 across a geographically spread regional district with several low caseload sites was not practicable. Easier to use options for clinician image capture were explored with advice from professional photographers. A smartphone device was selected anticipating that the ease of use and familiarity with the device may boost clinician confidence. It was hoped that this would lead to both increased use of photography along with greater clinician acceptability of photography generally in the SA forensic medical context across the health district.

Methods

Setting

The Hunter New England Local Health District is geographically spread over 131,785 km [2] and services a population of 962,390. It encompasses a busy regional metropolitan 24-h sexual assault service based in Newcastle, NSW, and also four smaller rural sites. Each rural site has its own local SA service with varying models of on-call availability but generally low caseload. A forensic examination is offered to all patients presenting after recent SA as part of a trauma-informed, holistic medical and psychosocial response.

Under NSW Health policy, photography (with patient consent) supplements written and diagrammatic injury documentation where injuries are difficult to describe in words or pictures, the clinician is unsure of a finding and requires senior clinician review that is not available contemporaneously, or to document complex, patterned, or pathognomonic findings. Photography is not used to document areas without injury. Photography may be used for general body or anogenital injury documentation, however for sensitive body areas a clear rationale and procedures to safeguard patient dignity and confidentiality must be in place. Images may be used as evidence in court where they enhance injury description in medical evidence.

Theoretical framework

A social ecological theoretical framework (drawing on Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory) is helpful in understanding factors influencing clinician acceptability of smartphones at the bedside in forensic examinations [17]. These factors operate at individual (familiarity, personal training and experience), community/institutional (access to devices, institutional structures to support safe and secure storage and transmission of images), and societal levels (legislative and courtroom requirements for photography).

Clinician population

Clinicians surveyed were a convenience sample of available SA nurse examiners and forensic medical examiners with specialised forensic and medical SA examination training working for the local health district at the time of survey distribution. All clinicians working for the district across regional metropolitan and rural services at the time of each survey were invited to participate.

Intervention

The iPhone 11 and a high quality ring light (YN608 with 4864 lumens of 5500K daylight balanced output) were initially chosen for the metropolitan service. Device selection followed blinded comparison of images taken using multiple models of smartphone, DSLR, and point and shoot camera by a professional photographer and a senior forensic examiner. Additional iPhone 11 and 12 models and ring lights were then progressively purchased for other regional sites once it was established that image quality was sufficient for use both for medical documentation and forensic purposes. To protect security and confidentiality, the iPhones were stored onsite in a locked cupboard at each service, did not have SIM cards, were not connected to any external cloud storage, and images were only stored on the local device at time of capture. They were then manually uploaded by daytime staff to a local secure server with purpose-built restricted database for sexual assault service medical forensic photography, for which access is regularly audited.

The Sexual Assault Services Medical Lead delivered online and face-to-face smartphone photography training to all forensic medical examiners. Video materials provided by the Education Centre Against Violence photographer assisted with training in use of the ring light. Photos were able to be uploaded directly to a secure, limited-access patient folder in Caradata's PACS Vue Image database maintained and securely stored by the health service imaging department.

Survey development

An initial pre-training anonymous survey of the forensic medical workforce was developed to better understand baseline level of experience and training in photography, as well as attitudes to and factors affecting clinician use of photography in SA examination. The survey comprised closed (categorical) and open (free text) questions (see Appendix, supplementary material). It was developed by two senior forensic examiners (one external to the service in a senior leadership position), and a medical photographer who trains forensic examiners in photography, with consultation from a health researcher with qualitative research expertise. It was pilot tested for face validity by a sexual assault nurse examiner and senior forensic medical examiner before circulation to the workforce.

Baseline survey of attitudes to photography and smartphone photography

Clinicians received the first survey by email link in the days prior to receiving initial face to face or online training in smartphone photography. The survey results of the baseline survey informed the development of the local training package and procedure for image capture, secure storage and transmission, to support the introduction of service-owned smartphone devices across the district.

Exploring clinician attitudes after implementing smartphone photography

In September 2023, three years after the staggered implementation of smartphone devices commenced, the original pre-training survey was circulated to all current forensic examiner staff, who received the second survey invitation via email link. The second survey was to explore current attitudes to SA photography and factors affecting use of photography in SA forensic examination three years post implementation of the smartphone and the associated training package. An additional question asked if they had completed the survey previously. Survey responses were anonymous and collated for analysis. This was completed as part of quality improvement processes for the purpose of updating the training package to suit current needs. As the survey was anonymised, targeted follow up reminders of clinicians who had not yet completed the survey were not possible.

Survey responses were analysed using a combination of descriptive statistical analysis of categorical data and inductive content analysis of free text responses to elucidate patterns among clinician workforce attitudes for both surveys prior to and after implementation of smartphone photography.

Use of smartphone photography

The proportion of cases where photography was used to supplement written and diagrammatic documentation was obtained from routinely collected SA presentation data maintained by the metropolitan service in an Excel spreadsheet, both in the period where the DSLR camera was available and in the period after purchase of the smartphone device. Chi-square tests were performed to assess the significance of the difference in the proportions of injury cases photographed.

Ethical considerations

Ethics committee waiver for negligible risk research was obtained from Hunter New England Local Health District Research Office to audit routinely collected data regarding the number of cases where photography was used. The surveys were undertaken as part of approved organisational quality improvement and training processes.

Results

Baseline characteristics of survey respondents

Survey 1 was emailed to 15 forensic examiners (100% female, 7 nurses and 8 doctors) in the days prior to them receiving initial training in smartphone use, and all 15 responded (Table 1). Most had prior photography experience outside the SA clinical setting using smartphones, though a small number had used SLR/DSLR cameras or compact digital cameras, and one had experience in colposcopic photography. While six respondents had received some prior training in photography in the SA setting, only four had prior experience with this (Table 1). One described a previous positive experience, one a neutral experience, and two described a negative experience of attempting to use photography in a SA setting.

Survey results: role of photography

Survey respondents were asked what they believe is the role of photography in a SA setting. Survey results were coded inductively according to factors of injury documentation, report writing, evidence in court, nature of injury, and peer review. Results for surveys 1 and 2 were similar, with the factors of ‘injury documentation’ and ‘nature of injury’ most frequently reported. 'Injury documentation’ responses presented photography as a way to ‘more accurately capture more complex injury’, ‘enhancing the written record’, with some respondents mentioning specifically ‘where injuries are difficult to draw or describe’. With ‘nature of the injury’, respondents mentioned number or complexity of injuries, severity of injury, and documentation of injuries that indicate the causative mechanism. In Survey 2 responses, peer review as a role of photography was more frequently reported than in Survey 1 responses: for example, ‘for a second opinion of difficult to describe injuries’.

Survey responses: factors affecting use of photography

Survey respondents were also asked about factors that make them more and less likely to use photography in a SA setting. Factors identified were coded as ‘device factors’, ‘patient factors’, ‘injury factors’, ‘clinician factors’, and ‘environment factors’ (Table 3).

Factors making clinicians more likely to take photos

In survey 1, seven respondents described device factors such as ease of use. They also reported clinician factors (seven responses) such as confidence/experience and training, and injury factors (five responses) such as ‘where I feel words wouldn't do it justice’, and ‘difficult to describe injuries’. Patient factors (two responses) such as patient preference and consent, and environment factors such as availability of instructions in the examination space (two responses), were also mentioned, with one respondent writing ‘if the environment supports good quality images’ without providing further specifying what a supportive environment might be.

In survey 2, respondents most frequently reported injury factors (12 responses) when asked about factors making them more likely to use photography in the SA examination setting. They mentioned injuries that were ‘difficult to describe or draw’, or ‘more clearly documented with a photo’, as well as injuries that were numerous, patterned or complex. Patient factors were mentioned in two responses, referring to patient comfort and consent. Only one respondent mentioned device factors, describing the ‘easy to use iPhone process.’

Factors making clinicians less likely to take photos

Survey 1 respondents reported that they would be less likely to use photography due to patient factors (six responses), such as ‘patients’ vulnerability, exhaustion, or irritability/impatience with our processes' or where they were ‘refusing photography’. They equally described device factors (six responses) such as difficulty using the complicated camera, and not having equipment accessible. This was closely followed by responses relating to clinician factors such as lack of confidence/experience (five responses), and lack of training (three responses), followed by four responses describing environment factors (time constraints, poor lighting). Only one response highlighted potential use in court and one noted lack of injury as a factor making them less likely to take photos.

In contrast, Survey 2 respondents overwhelmingly reported injury factors (10 responses) as the main reason they may not take photos, including minimal or no injury, injuries ‘very easily described and relatively minor’, and ‘when injury isn't clearly shown on the picture [photo]’. One respondent mentioned that they would not be comfortable photographing injury in the genital area. Patient factors were mentioned in eight responses, citing patient preference, distress or impatience. Only two respondents mentioned device factors (complicated process with camera use, and phone not locatable at time required), and one respondent described clinician experience factors (when unsure what to photograph).

Survey results: use of smartphone devices for image capture in SA forensic examination

Survey respondents were asked about their opinion on the use of smartphone devices to capture images in a SA setting. Survey 1 respondents' opinions were mixed, with many positive comments, describing smartphone devices as ‘simple and easy to use’ with ‘quicker set up’ and ‘good photo quality’ (11 positive responses). However, they also expressed concerns around ‘secure storage, use and disposal’, ‘quality of photos’, and how photos would be integrated to use in reports for court (eight responses expressing concerns). In contrast, Survey 2 respondents were overwhelmingly positive in their opinions of use of smartphone devices in the SA setting, with 11 positive responses, one neutral and no responses expressing concerns. Respondents commented on ease of use, ‘everyone knows how to use a smartphone’ and ‘minimal training requirements’, but also the experience for the patient in the clinical setting being ‘non-intrusive’, and technical aspects, ‘camera quality is generally excellent’ and ‘ring light really helps with the lighting’. One respondent commented ‘fine, no issues’ which was coded as a neutral response.

Clinician use of photography

Results of a chi-square analysis of the proportion of injury cases photographed, both when a smartphone was available and before the smartphone device was made available at the metropolitan health service site, are shown in Table 4. There were 16/120 (13%) cases with injury where photographs were taken during the period when the DSLR camera was available. After the introduction of the smartphone camera device, 64/240 (27%) cases where injury was seen were photographed. This was a statistically significant difference (p = 0.0041).

Discussion

Summary

This regional Australian health district with previously low use of photography to support clinical documentation of injury, was able to successfully introduce smartphones as image capture devices. Procedures were implemented to support the use of the service smartphones to capture and store images securely, and training provided to medical and nursing staff. Survey responses as well as data on cases with injury photographed indicate that photography generally has become a more routine and acceptable part of the SA forensic examination injury documentation. Smartphones were generally well accepted by clinicians as an easy-to-use, non-intrusive alternative to traditional single lens reflex (SLR) camera photography, being particularly useful for rural and regional settings where clinicians may have a low caseload and no access to specialist forensic photographers.

Clinician acceptability of smartphone photography

Key barriers to taking photos prior to the availability of smartphones were around clinician confidence and experience, and concerns about patient distress or consent. A notable difference in the factors affecting clinician use of photography between the two surveys was the greater importance of injury related factors over device and clinician factors in Survey 2. This may have been due to the availability of an easier to use image capture device, formal procedures and training having resolved any concerns around image capture procedures, clinician training and experience that existed at the time of Survey 1.

This was not a pure before and after study, in that the surveys were primarily intended as quality improvement activities, and they sampled a cross-sectional population of clinicians at each point in time rather than a cohort. However, these results do demonstrate a change in culture within the workforce where barriers to use of photography are no longer related to the technical aspects of photography or clinician perceptions of their own inexperience, but related to appropriate clinical considerations around the injury itself or patient factors such as distress or consent. Since the implementation of smartphone devices, the culture appears to have changed in such a way that the camera device itself and apprehension about clinicians' skill with the device are no longer the major factors affecting whether clinicians take photos. The key factors affecting use of photography relate to its clinical purpose and appropriateness as part of a trauma-informed response to a specific patient's needs.

Additionally, concerns about the use of photos in court, or security of storage and confidentiality, were not mentioned as a factor affecting use of photography in Survey 2. This may be due to the existence of clear procedures providing reassurance regarding all of these aspects, highlighting the importance of systemic supports for implementation of smartphone photography.

Clinician use of smartphone photography

There was a statistically significant increase in the use of photography for cases with injuries when comparing the period when smartphones were used to the baseline when a DSLR camera was used. This result can be interpreted as being due to the change to an easier to use smartphone device, but contributing factors could also be increased availability of training and resources, regular education on the use of photography for documentation and peer review of expert opinion (particularly for new staff), and greater normalisation of ring lights and smartphone photography in the broader community as general population access to digital media technology increases.

Limitations

We acknowledge methodological limitations associated with these surveys initially being developed as quality improvement tools to guide development of training and procedures, rather than as part of a prospectively designed research project. The first survey achieved an unusually high response rate, likely due to the timing just prior to photography education delivery for the small workforce, drawing on a convenience sample of highly motivated health professionals. The second survey, circulated to the larger workforce of that time, had a lower response rate, possibly due to reliance on email invitations with no concurrent education event to assist engagement. We note response rates were still higher than those typically cited for questionnaire-based studies of health professionals [18].

Additionally, the study population was the then-current team of forensic examiners working for the district at each point in time when the survey was administered, and not necessarily the same individual staff members. We are therefore not able to draw conclusions about changes in attitude in a particular cohort or at an individual clinician level, but we can comment on differences in attitudes generally in the contemporary workforce around photography before and after smartphone device implementation.

We did not seek to formally assess technical quality of digital photographs, as this is a separate project, but clinician attitudes were generally positive that the photographs were of suitable quality for their clinical purpose. Future research could include validation of standardised tools for technical quality assessment of sexual assault forensic examination photography by clinicians who are not professional photographers. We acknowledge that clinician smartphone photographs are unlikely to reach the same standards of photography as a specialised forensic photographer using a DSLR camera, but in regional areas without access to a specialist forensic photographer, when used appropriately they could be a crucial aide-mémoire supporting forensic examiner documentation.

Further research could also explore patient perceptions of smartphone digital photography. Survey responses from clinicians indicated that they saw smartphone digital photography as ‘non-intrusive’ and commented on ease of use and quicker set up. These responses suggest that the clinicians perceived the experience would be less time-consuming and less confronting for patients, but this should be explored directly with patients in future studies.

Conclusions

Smartphone digital photography can be safely implemented to improve use and acceptability of photography in sexual assault forensic examinations, enhancing service provision for patients with minimal cost in a regional service without availability of specialist clinical photographers. Our survey responses were generally positive and data demonstrated increased use of photography after smartphone devices became available compared to the previous period with a DSLR camera available. While not replacing a specialist forensic photographer's skills, smartphones are likely good enough for clinician use as an aide-mémoire supporting documentation where specialist photographers are not available.

Smartphone digital photography may be useful for services facing geographical barriers to access to specialist photographers, in resource poor settings, and in services relying on a workforce with a low case load who are not able to acquire specialist photography skills using SLR cameras.
