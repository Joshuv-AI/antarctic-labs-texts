National Trends in Healthcare Quality in Korea: A Multidimensional Assessment Using OECD Health Care Quality Indicators (2008–2023)
Authors: Hyejin Lee, Soo-Hee Hwang, Sang-A Cho, Hyemin Jung, Youngs Chang, Jieun Yun, Sanghyun Cho, Un-Na Kim, Hye Yeon Koo, Eun Byul Cho, Do Hee Kim, Jin Yong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesAssessing healthcare quality at the national level is essential for evaluating health system performance and identifying areas requiring improvement. This study examined long-term trends in healthcare quality in Korea from 2008 to 2023 using internationally comparable indicators.MethodsWe conducted a trend analysis of healthcare quality in Korea using the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) Health Care Quality and Outcomes framework and quality indicators. Indicators across multiple domains, including acute care, primary care, prescribing in primary care, mental healthcare, and patient experiences, were analyzed and compared with OECD averages.ResultsHealthcare quality in Korea improved across several domains. Thirty-day mortality for acute myocardial infarction decreased from 14.2% in 2008 to 10.2% in 2023, while mortality for ischemic stroke declined from 8.7% to 5.5%. Avoidable hospitalizations decreased substantially, with hospitalizations for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease declining by 59.7%. The proportion of patients with diabetes receiving cholesterol-lowering treatment increased from 44.1% to 82.5%. However, the proportion of broad-spectrum antibiotic prescriptions remained substantially higher than the OECD average (40.3 vs. 15.5%). In mental healthcare, excess mortality ratios increased from 4.3 in 2010 to 4.9 in 2023 for schizophrenia and from 3.5 to 4.3 for bipolar disorder, while post-discharge suicide rates showed little improvement. Patient experience indicators related to patient-physician communication improved and approached OECD averages.ConclusionsHealthcare quality in Korea improved substantially between 2008 and 2023, particularly in acute care outcomes and chronic disease management. However, persistent challenges remain in areas such as antibiotic use, polypharmacy, and mental healthcare. These findings provide internationally comparable evidence to inform future health policy and healthcare quality improvement efforts.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Improving healthcare quality has become a central policy priority for health systems worldwide. Beyond ensuring access and efficiency, the quality of care is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of patient outcomes and overall health system performance. In its landmark report, the Institute of Medicine defined healthcare quality across 6 domains—safety, effectiveness, patient-centeredness, timeliness, efficiency, and equity—providing a framework that has been widely adopted for evaluating healthcare systems [1].

To facilitate international comparisons of healthcare quality, the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) launched the Health Care Quality Indicators (HCQI) Project, now referred to as the Working Party on Health Care Quality and Outcomes (HCQO), which developed standardized indicators for assessing healthcare quality across countries [2,3]. These indicators encompass multiple dimensions of healthcare delivery, including preventive care, primary care, acute care, mental healthcare, and patient safety [4]. The HCQO framework and its associated indicators have been widely used to evaluate national healthcare performance and inform evidence-based health policy [5].

Korea has often been recognized for its strong healthcare system, which is characterized by universal health insurance coverage and high accessibility to medical services. The country has achieved substantial improvements in population health outcomes, including increased life expectancy and improved treatment outcomes for major diseases [6]. Nevertheless, several concerns regarding healthcare quality persist, including regional disparities in healthcare resources, hospital-centered patterns of healthcare utilization, and limited accessibility to emergency medical services [7].

Previous studies examining healthcare quality in Korea have largely focused on specific diseases or individual sectors of healthcare delivery. For example, some studies evaluated trends in outcomes for acute myocardial infarction (AMI) or assessed quality indicators in primary care settings [8,9]. However, comprehensive assessments of long-term national trends across multiple dimensions of healthcare quality remain limited.

Although such single-domain studies are valuable for understanding specific aspects of healthcare delivery, they are inherently limited in their ability to capture the broader performance profile of the healthcare system. A multidimensional approach that simultaneously examines multiple quality domains enables the identification of imbalanced patterns of development across the healthcare system—for example, whether improvements in acute care outcomes have been accompanied by comparable progress in primary care management or mental healthcare services [10,11]. Furthermore, long-term trend analyses over an extended period allow differentiation between sustained structural improvements and transient fluctuations attributable to external factors. By combining multidimensional assessment with systematic benchmarking against OECD member countries, it becomes possible to identify not only areas in which progress has been achieved but also areas in which critical gaps persist, thereby providing an evidence base for establishing policy priorities.

Therefore, this study aimed to comprehensively examine national trends in healthcare quality in Korea from 2008 to 2023 using the OECD HCQO framework and quality indicators. Specifically, we analyzed trends across multiple domains of healthcare quality, including acute care, primary care, prescribing in primary care, mental healthcare, and patient experiences. By providing a multidimensional assessment of healthcare quality trends, this study seeks to identify strengths and areas requiring improvement within Korea’s healthcare system and to provide evidence to inform future health policy.

METHODS

Study Design and Data Source

This study was a descriptive time-series comparative analysis using national-level healthcare quality indicators collected and compiled by the OECD. Secondary HCQO data from 38 OECD member countries for 2008–2023 were obtained from OECD Health Statistics (http://data-explorer.oecd.org/; last updated: December 4, 2025). The analysis period and countries included for each quality indicator were determined according to Korea’s indicator submission periods and the availability of data across countries.

Statistical Analysis

Among the OECD HCQO indicator domains, this study focused on domains for which Korea continuously submitted data during the study period and for which sufficient longitudinal data were available for time-series analysis. Domains with limited cross-country comparability, such as patient safety, which is subject to substantial under-reporting bias, and newly introduced domains with insufficient data continuity, such as end-of-life care and integrated care, were excluded. Ultimately, 5 domains were included: acute care, primary care, prescribing in primary care, mental healthcare, and patient experiences. For each indicator, OECD-calculated statistics based on age-stratified and sex-stratified numerator and denominator data submitted by each country were used in accordance with OECD definitions.

For the descriptive analysis, Korea’s annual indicator values by domain from 2008 to 2023, where available, were analyzed together with values from OECD member countries with data available in the corresponding years. The analysis included the OECD mean, including Korea, the number of countries reporting each indicator, and the maximum, minimum, and median values across OECD countries for each year. To visualize temporal trends and Korea’s relative position among OECD countries, composite graphs were used to overlay continuous trend lines representing the mean values for Korea and all OECD countries with high-low-median charts showing the distribution across countries. Trends over time were assessed using a generalized linear model with year as a continuous variable, and p-for-trend values were calculated to test statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide 7.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Ethics Statement

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No. X-2304-820-902). Informed consent was waived because we used anonymized data.

RESULTS

Trends in Quality of Acute Care in Korea

Figure 1 and Supplemental Material 1 present trends in 30-day in-hospital and out-of-hospital mortality among patients aged 45 years and older with major acute conditions—AMI, hemorrhagic stroke, and ischemic stroke—from 2008 to 2023. Overall, Korea demonstrated statistically significant reductions in mortality across all 3 conditions over the 16-year study period, whereas the OECD mean showed varying patterns by condition.

For AMI, Korea’s patient-based in-hospital and out-of-hospital mortality decreased from 14.2 per 100 patients in 2008 to 10.2 in 2023 (p-for-trend=0.008). The admission-based analysis similarly showed a decline from 11.2 per 100 admissions in 2008 to 8.4 in 2023 (p=0.028). The OECD mean also improved, with patient-based AMI mortality decreasing from 11.7 per 100 patients in 2008 to 8.6 in 2023 (p<0.001). However, Korea’s absolute mortality remained approximately 1.6 per 100 patients higher than the OECD mean throughout the study period.

For hemorrhagic stroke, Korea’s patient-based mortality was high in 2008, at 27.1 per 100 patients, but declined steadily to 21.5 in 2023 (p<0.001). Admission-based mortality also decreased substantially, from 20.4 per 100 admissions to 16.2 over the same period (p<0.001). In contrast, the OECD mean showed minimal change in the patient-based measure, from 33.0 per 100 patients in 2008 to 31.0 in 2023, with no statistically significant trend (p=0.610). Although OECD admission-based mortality declined modestly from 25.7 per 100 admissions to 23.5, reaching statistical significance (p=0.007), the stagnation in the patient-based measure contrasted markedly with Korea’s substantial improvement.

For ischemic stroke, Korea’s improvement was particularly notable. Korea’s patient-based mortality was already among the lowest in OECD countries in 2008 and declined sharply from 8.7 per 100 patients to 5.5 in 2023, reaching the lowest level observed during the study period (p<0.001). This trend was also observed consistently in the admission-based analysis, with mortality declining from 5.1 per 100 admissions in 2008 to 3.3 in 2023 (p<0.001). By comparison, the OECD mean patient-based mortality changed little, from 13.5 per 100 patients in 2008 to 11.9 in 2023, without reaching statistical significance (p=0.182).

Trends in Quality of Primary Care in Korea

Figure 2 and Supplemental Material 2 present changes in key chronic disease management indicators related to the quality of primary care in Korea among individuals aged 15 years and older from 2008 to 2023. Hospital admission rates per 100 000 population showed marked declines across most conditions.

For respiratory conditions, the admission rate for asthma decreased sharply from 107.4 per 100 000 population in 2008 to 41.1 in 2023, and the admission rate for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) declined from 247.5 to 99.8 over the same period; both conditions showed statistically significant improvement (p<0.001). For congestive heart failure (CHF), the admission rate decreased from 108.5 per 100 000 population in 2008 to 75.9 in 2023 (p<0.001). The admission rate for diabetes also fell by nearly half, from 318.6 in 2008 to 159.3 in 2023, showing a sustained downward trend (p<0.001).

Despite these improvements, performance varied across conditions compared with the OECD mean in 2023. Korea’s admission rates for COPD (99.8) and CHF (75.9) were substantially lower than the respective OECD means of 134.0 and 211.9 per 100 000 population, reflecting strong disease management in these areas. In contrast, admission rates for asthma (41.1) and diabetes (159.3) remained above the OECD means of 27.0 and 107.5, respectively, suggesting the need to strengthen primary care management for these conditions.

Indicators for diabetes-related lower-extremity amputation showed mixed results depending on the specific measure. The rate of major lower-extremity amputation decreased from 3.3 per 100 000 population in 2008 to 2.6 in 2023 (p=0.001), whereas the rate of minor lower-extremity amputation increased from 6.4 to 9.2 over the same period (p<0.001). Compared with the OECD mean in 2023, Korea’s rates of both major (2.6 vs. 7.0) and minor (9.2 vs. 14.9) amputations were lower than the OECD averages.

Trends in Quality of Prescribing in Korea

Figure 3 and Supplemental Material 3 present changes in key indicators reflecting the quality of prescribing in primary care in Korea from 2011 to 2023.

For chronic disease management, prescribing of cholesterol-lowering medications for patients with diabetes improved markedly. The prescribing rate of cholesterol-lowering agents among patients with diabetes nearly doubled, from 44.1% in 2011 to 82.5% in 2023 (p<0.001), and exceeded the 2023 OECD mean of 71.0%. The prescribing rate of first-choice antihypertensives among patients with diabetes also increased significantly, from 74.0% to 81.6% over the same period (p<0.001), substantially narrowing the gap with the OECD mean of 84.3% in 2023, which had remained largely stagnant.

Regarding psychotropic prescribing related to patient safety in older adults, notable achievements and persistent challenges coexisted. The rate of long-term benzodiazepine prescribing among patients aged 65 years and older increased from 9.9 in 2011 to 11.5 per 1000 patients in 2023 (p<0.001). However, the prescribing rate of long-acting benzodiazepines in the same age group declined by more than half, from 241.5 in 2011 to 98.3 per 1000 patients in 2023, representing a statistically significant improvement (p<0.001). Despite this reduction, the rate remained more than twice the 2023 OECD mean of 41.2 per 1000 patients. The antipsychotic prescribing rate among people aged 65 years and older increased from 29.7 in 2013 to 45.9 per 1000 population in 2023 (p<0.001), a trend also observed across the OECD, where the mean rose from 43.4 in 2013 to 53.4 per 1000 population in 2023 (p=0.002).

Polypharmacy management in an aging population was also identified as a key challenge. The proportion of patients aged 75 years and older who were chronically prescribed 5 or more medications decreased modestly from 67.2% in 2013 to 64.2% in 2021, although no statistically significant trend was observed (p=0.422). Nevertheless, this rate consistently exceeded the OECD mean each year and was markedly higher than the 2021 OECD average of 46.5%.

Antibiotic prescribing indicators revealed considerable room for improvement. The volume-based proportion of second-line antibiotics—specifically cephalosporins and quinolones—among all outpatient antibiotic prescriptions increased from 35.8% in 2011 to 40.3% in 2023 (p=0.002), a level more than 2.5 times the 2023 OECD mean of 15.5%. Similarly, the total volume of outpatient antibiotics for systemic use, measured as defined daily doses (DDDs) per 1000 population per day, remained high in Korea, increasing from 24.3 in 2011 to 27.5 DDDs per 1000 population per day in 2023; this did not reflect a statistically significant change (p=0.176). This pattern contrasted with the OECD mean, which decreased significantly from 20.7 in 2011 to 15.7 DDDs per 1000 population per day in 2023 (p<0.001). In contrast, total opioid prescribing volume in Korea was notably low at 0.9 in 2023, substantially below the OECD mean of 14.6 DDDs per 1000 population per day.

Trends in Quality of Mental Healthcare in Korea

Figure 4 and Supplemental Material 4 present changes in key indicators reflecting the quality of mental healthcare in Korea from 2010 to 2023, including the excess mortality ratio among patients with mental disorders aged 15–74 years and suicide rates after discharge among patients aged 15 years and older. Overall, mental healthcare indicators showed deterioration or stagnation over the 13-year study period.

The excess mortality ratio among patients with severe mental illness increased over time. The excess mortality ratio for patients with schizophrenia increased significantly from 4.3 in 2010 to 4.9 in 2023 (p<0.001), and the ratio for patients with bipolar disorder increased from 3.5 to 4.3 over the same period (p<0.001). Compared with the 2023 OECD means of 4.3 for schizophrenia and 2.8 for bipolar disorder, these findings suggest that patients with severe mental illness in Korea face a disproportionately higher mortality risk relative to the general population. Notably, while the OECD mean for bipolar disorder remained stable, with no significant change since 2010 (p=0.958), Korea exhibited a clear upward trend, resulting in a widening gap.

Indicators of suicide after discharge among patients diagnosed with a mental disorder also failed to demonstrate meaningful improvement. The suicide rate within 30 days of discharge from psychiatric hospitalization decreased marginally from 2.0 in 2010 to 1.9 per 1000 patients in 2022, and the suicide rate within 1 year of discharge declined slightly from 7.6 to 6.9 per 1000 patients. However, neither indicator showed a statistically significant trend (p=0.962 for suicide within 30 days after discharge; p=0.496 for suicide within 1 year after discharge). These rates remained substantially higher than the OECD means of 1.2 and 4.4 per 1000 patients, respectively, as of 2022, indicating a persistent gap in post-discharge community-based follow-up and crisis management systems.

Trends in Quality of Patient Experiences in Korea

Figure 5 and Supplemental Material 5 present changes in patient experience indicators in Korea among individuals aged 16 years and older from 2018 to 2024. The proportion of respondents who reported that their doctor spent enough time with them during consultations increased slightly from 80.8% in 2018 to 82.5% in 2024, although this change was not statistically significant (p=0.261) and remained below the 2024 OECD mean of 86.8%.

In contrast, indicators related to patient–physician communication and participatory decision-making showed positive trends. The proportion of respondents who reported being given the opportunity to ask questions or raise concerns with their doctor increased significantly from 81.4% in 2018 to 88.2% in 2024 (p=0.019), reaching the 2024 OECD mean of 88.2%. Similarly, the proportion who reported being involved in decisions about their care or treatment rose from 82.4% in 2018 to 88.3% in 2024, also demonstrating statistically significant improvement (p=0.010).

Regarding unmet healthcare needs due to financial reasons, results varied by type of care. The proportion of respondents who reported skipping a medical consultation due to cost remained low, changing from 2.8% in 2018 to 2.7% in 2024, as did the proportion who reported skipping prescribed medications, which decreased from 1.9% to 1.2%. Both figures compared favorably with the 2024 OECD means of 8.8% for skipped consultations and 3.6% for forgone medications. However, the proportion who reported skipping diagnostic tests, treatments, or follow-up care due to cost showed little change, from 5.5% in 2018 to 5.7% in 2024 (p=0.192), and was slightly above the 2024 OECD mean of 4.7%. These findings suggest that although financial barriers to basic consultations and medications remain low in Korea, cost burdens may be relatively greater for diagnostic testing and additional procedures.

DISCUSSION

This study comprehensively analyzed trends in healthcare quality in Korea between 2008 and 2023 using the HCQI framework developed by the OECD. By examining multiple domains—including acute care, primary care, prescribing in primary care, mental healthcare, and patient experiences—this study provides a multidimensional assessment of changes in healthcare quality at the national level. Overall, healthcare quality in Korea improved substantially over the study period, particularly in acute care outcomes and chronic disease management. However, several areas, including antibiotic prescribing, polypharmacy among older adults, and mental health outcomes, remain important challenges compared with the OECD average.

First, treatment outcomes for major acute conditions improved markedly over the study period. Thirty-day mortality rates for AMI, hemorrhagic stroke, and ischemic stroke declined significantly in both patient-based and admission-based analyses. In particular, mortality rates for ischemic stroke were already among the lowest in OECD countries at the beginning of the study period and continued to improve. Hemorrhagic stroke mortality also improved consistently, whereas the OECD average remained relatively stable. However, despite a declining trend, mortality following AMI remained higher than the OECD average. The decline in AMI mortality in Korea is presumably attributable to the high rate of percutaneous coronary intervention, which reaches approximately 99.1% [12]. However, reducing total ischemic time remains critical for improving prognosis and further lowering 30-day in-hospital and out-of-hospital mortality, which remains higher than the OECD average. Previous studies have suggested that delays occur at multiple stages of the care pathway, including patient delays related to symptom recognition, emergency medical service delays caused by shortages of medical personnel and hospital beds, and system delays related to the declining number of interventional cardiologists. Addressing these factors may be essential for further reducing mortality following AMI [13]. In addition, hospital characteristics, such as procedural volume and staffing levels, have been associated with patient outcomes, highlighting the importance of maintaining adequate capacity and expertise within the healthcare system [14]. The notable decline in AMI mortality observed between 2011 and 2015 may be associated with policy efforts implemented during this period. The Korean government conducted a national quality assessment program for AMI care beginning in 2007, systematically monitoring key quality indicators such as timely percutaneous coronary intervention and evidence-based medication prescribing [12]. Concurrently, the 2008 Comprehensive Plan for Cardiovascular and Cerebrovascular Disease and the expansion of regional cardiovascular centers strengthened emergency care infrastructure. However, discontinuation of the quality assessment program after 2017 may have contributed to the subsequent plateau in mortality reduction [13].

Second, indicators related to chronic disease management in primary care generally improved. Avoidable hospitalization rates for major chronic conditions, including asthma, COPD, CHF, and diabetes, declined over time. In particular, hospitalization rates for CHF and COPD fell below the OECD average by 2023, indicating strong performance in these areas. These improvements may reflect policy efforts in Korea to strengthen primary care management of chronic diseases, particularly diabetes and hypertension [9,15]. The Chronic Disease Management Program, introduced in 2012, reduced patient copayments from 30% to 20% at primary care clinics and provided financial incentives for physicians, contributing to improved medication adherence and continuity of care [16,17]. However, hospitalization rates for asthma and diabetes remained higher than the OECD average. Korea also has one of the highest numbers of hospital beds among OECD countries [5] and operates largely under a fee-for-service payment system, both of which may contribute to higher hospitalization rates compared with other countries [18]. The sharp decline in hospitalization rates for asthma and COPD observed between 2020 and 2021 should be interpreted with caution because this period coincided with the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Reduced healthcare utilization due to avoidance of medical facilities, widespread social distancing measures, decreased exposure to respiratory pathogens and air pollutants, and mandatory face mask use likely contributed to the observed decline independently of improvements in primary care quality [19].

Third, indicators of prescribing quality in primary care showed mixed patterns of improvement. Prescription rates for cholesterol-lowering agents and first-choice antihypertensive medications among patients with diabetes increased substantially during the study period and reached levels comparable to or higher than the OECD average. These findings suggest that chronic disease management in primary care has improved substantially in terms of evidence-based pharmacologic treatment. However, the proportion of second-line antibiotic prescriptions and the overall volume of outpatient antibiotic use remained considerably higher than the OECD average, and the share of broad-spectrum antibiotics increased during the study period. These findings indicate persistent challenges in antimicrobial stewardship in Korea. Furthermore, Korea has relatively high levels of antimicrobial resistance, and broad-spectrum antibiotic prescribing is often preferred by clinicians [20]. Although antimicrobial stewardship programs have been shown to reduce inappropriate antibiotic use [21], implementation remains limited because of shortages of infection control specialists [22] and the concentration of stewardship programs in large tertiary hospitals [23]. Expanding antimicrobial stewardship programs and strengthening national strategies to promote appropriate antibiotic use will therefore be critical. In addition, although prescribing of long-acting benzodiazepines among adults aged 65 years and older decreased, the prevalence of polypharmacy among individuals aged 75 years and older remains considerably higher than the OECD average. High levels of polypharmacy and antipsychotic use among older adults suggest the need for strengthened monitoring systems and education regarding potentially inappropriate medications [24].

Fourth, healthcare quality indicators in the mental healthcare domain showed limited improvement and, in some cases, worsening trends. Excess mortality among patients with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder increased significantly during the study period and remained higher than the OECD average. Suicide rates following discharge among patients with mental illness also showed no meaningful improvement and remained above the OECD average. These findings are concerning because they indicate persistent health disparities affecting individuals with severe mental illness. Higher mortality among individuals with severe mental illness may be explained by multiple factors, including inadequate management of physical comorbidities and increased risks of suicide and other preventable causes of death [25,26]. Effective mental healthcare systems typically rely on strong coordination between hospital-based treatment and community-based services after discharge. However, community-based mental healthcare services remain relatively underdeveloped in Korea [27]. The transition from hospital-centered care to community-based integrated care has been slow despite legislative efforts such as the 2016 revision of the Mental Health and Welfare Act. The persistent gap between Korea and the OECD average in post-discharge suicide rates further suggests that continuity of care after psychiatric hospitalization remains inadequate. Strengthening community mental health infrastructure, establishing systematic post-discharge follow-up programs, and expanding crisis intervention services will be essential for addressing these disparities.

Fifth, some positive changes were observed in patient experiences and healthcare accessibility. Indicators related to doctor–patient communication and shared decision-making improved significantly over the study period and reached levels comparable to the OECD average. In addition, the proportion of individuals who reported skipping medical care or prescribed medications due to cost remained substantially lower than the OECD average. These findings reflect the relatively strong financial protection provided by the National Health Insurance system. However, cost-related barriers to additional healthcare services, such as diagnostic tests or specialized treatments, were reported slightly more frequently than the OECD average, suggesting that financial barriers associated with uncovered services may still affect access to care [28,29].

Several limitations should be considered. First, this study relied on national administrative data, which limited the ability to account for individual-level clinical severity and socioeconomic factors. Second, although long-term trends were analyzed, this study did not explicitly account for exogenous factors, including country-level policy interventions and global health crises that may have influenced the observed trends. Third, the number of OECD countries reporting certain indicators varied by year, particularly for patient experience measures, which may have influenced the stability of OECD averages. These variations should be considered when interpreting cross-national comparisons. Fourth, this study did not encompass all emerging priority areas in healthcare quality because several domains of the OECD HCQO framework were excluded, including patient safety, end-of-life care, integrated care, mental health patient experience, and patient-reported outcomes. These exclusions were primarily due to limited cross-country comparability, under-reporting, instability in newly introduced indicators, and data unavailability resulting from the lack of national data sources.

However, this study has several strengths. It addresses the limitations of previous studies confined to specific clinical domains by conducting a multidimensional and comprehensive quality assessment spanning the national healthcare system, from acute care management to primary care-based chronic disease management, safe prescribing in primary care, mental healthcare, and patient experiences. This multidimensional approach enabled the identification of imbalanced patterns of development across the healthcare system. Furthermore, the 16-year study period allowed differentiation between sustained structural improvements and transient fluctuations, such as the temporary decline in respiratory disease hospitalizations during the COVID-19 pandemic. By systematically benchmarking Korea’s performance against the OECD average using a standardized indicator framework, this study provides a clear basis for identifying policy priorities, highlighting areas that have reached international standards and those requiring urgent attention, and establishes a baseline for continuous monitoring of healthcare quality at the national level [30].

In conclusion, healthcare quality in Korea has improved substantially over the past decade across multiple domains, particularly in acute care outcomes and aspects of chronic disease management in primary care. These improvements likely reflect sustained investments in emergency care systems, expanded access to advanced medical technologies, and policy efforts to strengthen chronic disease management. Nevertheless, important challenges remain. In particular, relatively high mortality following AMI, persistent issues related to antibiotic use and polypharmacy, and worsening outcomes among patients with severe mental illness highlight areas requiring urgent policy attention. Addressing these challenges will require coordinated strategies that strengthen emergency response systems, promote appropriate prescribing practices, and expand integrated community-based mental health services. In addition, high-priority quality domains, such as patient safety and patient-centeredness, that were excluded because of limitations in data availability and reliability should be incorporated into future analyses to enable systematic monitoring. Continuous monitoring using comprehensive and internationally comparable healthcare quality indicators will be essential for identifying emerging gaps and guiding evidence-based health policy. The findings of this study provide important evidence to inform future efforts to improve the quality, safety, patient-centeredness, and equity of healthcare in Korea.
