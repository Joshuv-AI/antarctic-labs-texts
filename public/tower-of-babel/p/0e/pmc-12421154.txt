Missed opportunities for vaccination among healthcare-seeking children: a cross-sectional study in Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan, 2023
Authors: Gulzada Dadanova, Roberta Horth, Aisuluu Kubatova, Gulbara Ishenapysova, Dinagul Otorbaeva, Dilyara Nabirova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHigh vaccination coverage is critical for preventing disease transmission. In Bishkek (population: 1.15 million), childhood vaccination coverage was <90% in 2022. Understanding missed opportunities for vaccination (MOV) at healthcare visits was needed to increase coverage.MethodsUsing the World Health Organization MOV assessment guide, we selected 33 primary care clinics in Bishkek. We conducted sequential exit interviews with caregivers of children aged <30 months who received healthcare for any reason. Caregiver’s attitude towards vaccines was measured by asking if it was good, neutral or negative. We extracted vaccine records from the facility’s medical records. MOV was calculated as the proportion of children who were not up to date on all their vaccines by the end of the visit, among those eligible for at least one vaccine at the start of the visit. We used multivariable Poisson regression to assess characteristics associated with MOV.ResultsOf 650 participating children, 448 (69%) were eligible for vaccination. Among whom, 86% had a MOV after their visit and 6% had never been vaccinated. MOV prevalence was 81% for children aged 0–11 months (n=240) and 92% for those aged 12–30 months (n=208), ranging from 79% to 93% across the four districts. Providers did not check the immunisation status of 53% of children. Among 133 children whose primary visit purpose was vaccination, 53% were still not up to date on all their vaccines by the end of the visit. MOV risk was higher among children whose vaccination status was not checked by the provider compared with those who were checked (adjusted prevalence ratio (aPR) 1.13, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.22) and children whose caregivers had negative (aPR 1.10, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.18) or neutral attitudes (aPR 1.11, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.19) vs positive attitudes.ConclusionNearly nine in 10 children missed a vaccination opportunity during their healthcare visit. Strategies for checking immunisation status at each visit and addressing caregiver and healthcare barriers may help increase immunisation coverage.Keywords: cross-sectional studies, primary prevention, vaccination

Introduction

Immunisation is a critical foundation of infectious disease prevention and primary healthcare.1 Access to vaccines is considered a fundamental component of the right to health. However, there are global inequities in the distribution of and access to vaccines.2 The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated these inequities as disruptions in primary healthcare services and preventive medicine affected different groups unequally.3 The pandemic contributed to the largest decline in childhood immunisation in over 30 years, and globally, an estimated 25 million children missed routine vaccinations in 2020 and 2021.4 Particularly alarming was the increase in the number of children with zero vaccinations, which increased from 12.9 million in 2019 to 18.1 million in 2021.5

In 2020, the World Health Assembly adopted a global strategy aimed at preventing vaccine-preventable infections and reducing mortality and morbidity from these infections.6 The strategy aims to achieve at least 90% coverage with three diphtheria-pertussis-tetanus-containing vaccine doses (DTP3) within the framework of routine immunisation programmes for children.

In the Kyrgyz Republic, an average of 150 000 children aged under 1 year require vaccination annually.7 According to the National Statistical Committee of the Kyrgyz Republic, vaccination coverage in the country with the DTP3 vaccine averaged 91% in 2022.7 However, some regions of the country report DTP3 coverage below 90%, including Bishkek city, the capital, with a population of 1.15 million. In 2022, Bishkek reported a DTP3 coverage of 79%.7

Reducing missed opportunities for vaccination (MOV) is an evidence-based strategy to increase immunisation coverage. A MOV occurs when a child is eligible for one or more vaccine doses and has no contraindications at the start of a healthcare facility visit and does not receive their missing vaccine dose(s) by the end of the visit.8 9 The prevalence of MOV ranges widely across countries, from 5% in Colombia to 76% in Burkina Faso.10 11 Children eligible for vaccination could have missed opportunities for various reasons, including healthcare workers’ failure to review child vaccination records, parental refusal, treatment for a different illness or health facility supply shortages.

MOV prevalence estimates were not available for Kyrgyzstan or Central Asia. We aimed to quantify the magnitude of this issue and identify the primary factors associated with MOV to develop actionable solutions to reduce missed opportunities at the facility level.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional study using the WHO methodology to assess the prevalence of and factors contributing to MOV.9 Specifically, we carried out the quantitative components of the WHO MOV methodology, which involved interviews with caregivers of children visiting a healthcare facility.

Study setting

Bishkek has 48 state-owned primary healthcare facilities in four districts and seven private healthcare facilities that provide vaccination services. We stratified facilities by district and facility type and randomly selected 30 (62%) public facilities (Leninsky District—12, Oktyabrsky District—3, Pervomaysky District—4, Sverdlovsky District—11) and three private facilities.

Participant selection

We sequentially recruited 20 participants from each public or private healthcare facility who were exiting the facility after an appointment with their child. The participants were caregivers of children aged under 30 months. The age group was selected because in the Kyrgyzstan national vaccination calendar, the DTP booster is given to children at 2 years of age.12 Children are expected to have completed these vaccines by 30 months of age. All children aged 0–30 months who came to primary healthcare organisations were eligible for the study, regardless of the reason for the visit. We aimed to recruit 660 participants using the standard MOV methodology of 20 participants per site. The exclusion criteria were parental or caregiver refusal, children older than 30 months and lack of documentation regarding vaccinations received or not received (in the analysis of 660 respondents, 10 did not have documentation). We excluded participants if parents or caregivers refused participation, if the child was older than 30 months or if vaccination documentation was missing (online supplemental figure 1).

Trained residents and graduates of the Central Asia Advanced Field Epidemiology Training Programme conducted interviews with caregivers in July and August 2023. Interviewers were assigned to seven to eight health facilities, and interviews lasted on average 15 min.

Data collection

We conducted computer-assisted face-to-face interviews with caregivers using the WHO MOV questionnaire, with slight adaptations for improved understanding of answer choices in the local context (eg, education groups) and to add questions about caregiver attitudes towards vaccines.9 We collected data using KoboToolbox (Cambridge, USA). The questionnaire was piloted with a small group of parents, revised based on their feedback and then used in interviews with study participants. Pilot data were excluded from the final dataset.

The questionnaire (online supplemental material) consisted of six sections:

Key definitions

A child was classified as having a missed opportunity for vaccination (MOV), if he or she was eligible for vaccination (unvaccinated, partially vaccinated or not vaccinated within the window and without contraindications to vaccination) at the start of a healthcare visit but did not receive all their missing vaccine doses by the end of their visit. We calculated eligibility to receive a vaccine using the child’s date of birth and date of interview, the national immunisation schedule (online supplemental table 1) and the presence of contraindications (as reported by the caregiver).

Vaccine history was abstracted directly from medical records. We did not rely on self-reported vaccine status or information on the vaccine cards carried by parents. Only children eligible to receive one or more doses of the vaccine when they initiated the healthcare visit were included in the calculation of MOV prevalence. Children who had aged out of eligibility for a specific vaccine were not classified as having a MOV. For example, children who became eligible for routine hepatitis B (HepB) vaccination later were not considered to have missed the birth dose of HepB. Children were considered to have a MOV if they met all the following four criteria:

They had a valid vaccination record at the clinic (including a blank if the child was never immunised).

They were missing at least one immunisation according to the national immunisation schedule for their age at the beginning of the visit.

They had no contraindications to vaccination on the day of the study.

They left the health facility without being up to date on all their vaccines, even if they received one or more vaccine doses during the visit.

We also calculated vaccine timeliness. We categorised vaccination timeliness by vaccine type and dose, as ‘vaccinated on time’, ‘vaccinated late’ or ‘overdue vaccination’ based on the child’s date of birth, age of vaccine eligibility based on the national schedule and interval of administration of each vaccine dose (online supplemental table 1). According to Kyrgyzstan’s national immunisation schedule,12 newborns receive the HepB vaccine and the BCG vaccine at birth (online supplemental table 1). At 2 months of age, children receive the first dose of the DTP-HepB-Hib vaccine (DTP containing vaccine, plus HepB and Haemophilus influenzae type b), the pneumococcal conjugated vaccine (PCV), oral polio vaccine (OPV) and rotavirus vaccine (Rota). At 3.5 months, children receive the second doses of the DTP-HepB-Hib, OPV and Rota vaccines, as well as the first dose of the inactivated polio vaccine (IPV). At 5 months of age, they receive the third dose of the DTP-HepB-Hib, OPV and Rota vaccines and the second dose of the PCV vaccine. At 9 months, they receive the second dose of the IPV. At 1 year of age, children receive the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine and the third dose of the PCV vaccine. Lastly, children receive the DTP vaccine at 2 years of age.

Caregiver attitude towards vaccines was measured by their self-reported answer to the question “What is your attitude towards vaccination?” with response options being ‘positive’, ‘neutral’ or ‘negative’. Vaccination knowledge was measured by the question, “Do vaccines protect against diseases?”

Statistical analysis

We merged data from caregiver questionnaires with data abstracted from medical charts and conducted analysis using the R Statistical program (V.4.2.0, Vienna, Austria). We performed bivariate analysis using χ2 and Fisher’s exact testing. We conducted Poisson regression to assess factors associated with a MOV. We used robust standard errors from the sandwich package for the parameter estimates. Variables known to be confounders of MOV were included in the model, as well as variables associated with MOV at p<0.05 in the bivariable analysis. When variables were highly correlated, only one was included in the model. For example, the variable indicating whether providers asked about vaccination status was included instead of the variable for whether providers asked to see the vaccination card.

Patient and public involvement

The questionnaire tools and methodology used in this study were developed by WHO, and to ensure standardisation for comparison across countries, local caregivers of patients were not involved in the methodology development or questionnaire design. The study’s results have been communicated to the participating clinics so that they can take action to reduce MOV and to vaccinate children who are missing any doses.

Results

We invited 720 parents with children to the study and recruited 660 (92%) children whose parents agreed to participate. Ten children were excluded because they lacked documentation to confirm their eligibility. Among the eligible children (n=650), 146 (22%) were not included in the MOV prevalence estimation because they were fully vaccinated before they initiated the healthcare visit, and 56 (9%) were not included because they had a contraindication for vaccination at the time of their visit (online supplemental figure 1). The remaining 448 (69%) were eligible for vaccination and were included in the MOV prevalence estimation. Of these, 86% (386/448) had a MOV.

Characteristics of the children

Among the participating children (n=448), approximately half (54%) were 0–11 months of age (median age was 11 months), and half (56%) were male (table 1). Most (92%) were previously registered at the clinic (in Kyrgyzstan, all children are geographically assigned to a clinic on birth) and 28% had visited the clinic 4–12 times in the last 12 months. The main reasons for their current visit to the health facility were to receive vaccines (30%), receive treatment or counselling (30%) or undergo preventive examinations (24%). Most (84%) did not have their vaccine cards with them, and 6% had never been vaccinated. One in five (21%) was asked by a healthcare worker to show their immunisation cards during their visit, and 47% were asked about their child’s immunisation status.

Timeliness of doses received and missed vaccination opportunities

The proportion of children vaccinated on time for each vaccine type and dose that they were eligible for ranged from 92% (598/648) for BCG to 31% (143/465) for the Rota dose 3 (figure 1). For vaccines given at birth, the missing frequency for BCG was 8% (49/468), and for HepB, it was 1% (3/599).

For vaccines given at 2 months of age, 25% (145/587) were overdue for DTP-HepB-Hib dose 1 and 24% (135/560) were overdue for Rota dose 1. These proportions increased to 31% (160/518) and 32% (158/501) for dose 2 of those vaccines, respectively, which is given at 2.5 months, and to 42% (190/456) and 44% (205/456) for dose 3, which is given at 5 months.

For polio vaccines, OPV dose 1 was overdue in 19% (115/590) of eligible children, dose 2 in 24% (126/525) and dose 3 in 35% (165/466). IPV dose 1 was overdue for 32% (170/531) of the children, and dose 2 was overdue for 48% (80/168).

One-quarter (26%, 76/290) were missing dose 1 of MMR, and 43% (123/287) were missing the PCV dose 3, both given at 12 months. Half (50%) (52/103) were missing the DTP booster given at 24 months.

Factors associated with MOV—bivariable analysis

Among children 12–30 months of age, 92% had a MOV compared with 81% of children aged 0–11 months (table 1). MOV prevalence did not differ by sex. Compared with Leninsky district, prevalence of MOV was 18% higher in Oktyabrsky district and 12% higher than in Sverdlovsk district (prevalence ratio (PR) 1.12, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.24, p=0.027, and PR 1.18, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.32, p=0.014, respectively).

Children who were not registered at the facility were more likely to have a MOV compared with registered children (PR 1.18, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.22, p=0.015), and all children who were not registered at the clinic had a MOV. Children 12–30 months of age who had visited the clinic over 4 times in the past year had the highest prevalence of MOV (93%). Among the children who specifically came to the clinic for vaccination, 49% had a MOV; among those who were at the clinic for any other reason, 100% had a MOV. All the children who had never been vaccinated had a MOV.

Children who did not have their vaccination cards with them at the time of the visit were more likely to have experienced a MOV compared with those who had a card (PR 1.48, 95% CI 1.22 to 1.78, p<0.001). However, 61% of the children who had their vaccination cards still had a MOV after their visit. The prevalence of MOV was 45% higher among children whose caregivers were not asked by healthcare providers to provide their vaccination cards compared with those who had been asked (PR 1.45, 95% CI 1.24 to 1.70, p<0.001).

The prevalence of MOV did not significantly differ by caregiver relationship with the child, education level or employment status (table 2). Having a MOV was 15% higher among children with never-vaccinated caregivers than among those with caregivers who had ever been vaccinated (PR 1.15, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.22, p=0.030). When asked about vaccine attitudes, 10% of caregivers reported having a negative view of vaccines, and 18% had a neutral view. Children of caregivers with self-reported negative or neutral attitudes towards vaccination were more likely to have a MOV than children of caregivers with positive attitudes (PR 1.18, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.26, p=0.009 and PR 1.13, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.22, p=0.013, respectively). Nearly all (98%) of children whose caregivers reported having a negative attitude had a MOV. The prevalence of MOV was higher among children whose caregivers did not know that vaccines protect against disease compared with those who knew this (PR 1.10, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.18, p=0.040).

Factors associated with MOV—multivariable analysis

In multivariable analysis (table 3), children who did not have a vaccine card with them had a higher risk of MOV than those who did (adjusted PR (aPR) 1.39, 95% CI 1.16 to 1.66, p<0.001). Children whose caregivers were not asked about their child’s immunisation status or to see their cards had a higher risk of MOV than those whose children were asked (aPR 1.13, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.22, p=0.002). Children whose caregivers self-reported negative or neutral attitudes towards vaccines had a higher risk of having a MOV than those who had positive attitudes (aPR 1.10, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.18, p=0.006, and aPR 1.11, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.19, p=0.004, respectively). Additionally, children who visited facilities in the Oktyabrsky district had a higher risk of MOV compared with those in the Leninsky district (aPR 1.23, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.37, p<0.001).

Subanalysis of vaccinated and non-vaccinated children

Among 125 children who received at least one vaccine during their visit, 50% were still missing at least one vaccine dose for which they were eligible and were classified as having a MOV by the end of their visit (table 4). Eleven per cent waited over 30 min for vaccination, 9% did not receive information about the vaccines their child received and 4% did not receive information about possible vaccine side effects or reactions.

Among children with a MOV not receiving any vaccination during their visit (n=323), the reasons given by caregivers for the child not receiving the vaccine were that: vaccination was not the purpose of the visit (71%), thought that their child was already fully vaccinated for their age (12%), did not trust vaccine safety (7%) and vaccination was against their religion (3%).

There were 71 children who still had a MOV, although they were at the visit specifically for a vaccination and received a vaccination. For all these children, the reason given by caregivers for non-vaccination was the fear of receiving several vaccines on the same day. Among these 71 children, 69% were still missing an OPV dose, 68% a DTP-HepB-Hib dose and 72% a PCV dose, with the highest rates of missed doses occurring in younger ages (online supplemental table 2).

Discussion

Our study aimed to assess the prevalence and factors associated with missed vaccination opportunities among children under 30 months of age attending primary healthcare facilities in Bishkek. We found that missed vaccination opportunities occurred in almost nine out of 10 children (86%).

The prevalence of missed vaccination opportunities in our study was substantially greater than in MOV studies that followed WHO approach among similar-aged children conducted elsewhere, including in Burkina Faso (76%), Kenya (75%), Zhejiang Province, Eastern China (54%), Ondo State in Southwest Nigeria (33%) and Western Region Cameroon (24%).1013,17 Our MOV estimate was 2.5 times greater than the pooled MOV estimate of 32% (range 1.3%–83%) from 41 studies conducted among children aged 0–18 years from 1991 to 2014.18 19 The high prevalence of MOV was unexpected given that the country has consistently reported high national immunisation coverage, with reported DTP3 coverage of 86% and a second dose of measles-containing vaccine coverage of 93% in 2023.20

Several factors may explain the high prevalence of MOV in our study. First, our study included a wider age group than most MOV studies conducted elsewhere, and MOV increases with age. In a multicountry study that included children >24 years of age, the MOV prevalence in that older age group (86%) was closer to our estimate (92%).21 However, age alone does not explain the higher prevalence found in our study because, among children under 1 year of age, the MOV was 81%, which is still higher than the MOV in the same age group reported in other studies.15 22 A second explanation for the high MOV is that, in Kyrgyzstan, children are usually only vaccinated when they attend a healthcare visit with the specific purpose of vaccination. This is evidenced by our finding that among children with a MOV who did not receive a vaccine during their appointment, 71% of caregivers said their child was not vaccinated because it was not the purpose of their appointment. WHO recommends that health providers review and assess children’s immunisation status at every healthcare visit.8

Childhood immunisations are not compulsory in Kyrgyzstan, and there has been an increase in trends in parental refusals for the vaccination of children.7 The number of children with parental refusal for vaccinations tripled from 4611 in 2016 to 12 853 in 2022. Our study highlights the role of parental hesitancy in missed opportunities to vaccinate. Nearly one in five caregivers had negative attitudes towards vaccines, and 94% of these caregivers had children with a MOV. Notably, 6% of the children in this study had never been vaccinated. Because our study excluded all the children with valid contraindications and there were no shortages of vaccines at the time of the study, it is likely that parental refusal played a role in non-vaccination among never-vaccinated children, defined as children who have never received any vaccines in their lifetime. Concerns with vaccine safety were cited as a reason for non-vaccination among 7% of caregivers. Interventions that address parental vaccine hesitancy related to trust in vaccination safety may help increase coverage.

In our study, only 21% of health workers requested a preventive vaccination card, and 26% checked the vaccination status of children during visits. Kyrgyzstan introduced vaccination cards (certificate of preventive immunisations) in 2019, requiring caregivers to bring them to healthcare facilities.23 However, only 15% of caregivers in our study brought their children’s vaccination cards. Parents are often unaware of vaccination schedules and may not realise their children have missed vaccine doses. For example, among children who missed vaccination on the scheduled day (MOV), 12% of caregivers mistakenly believed their child was fully vaccinated.

An unexpected result in our study was the high proportion of children, about half, who were at the health facility specifically for a vaccination, received a vaccination and still had a MOV after their visit. It is a common vaccine myth that too many vaccines overwhelm the immune system.18 Caregivers or clinicians might have been reluctant to provide more than one vaccination during the visit; however, from our data, we cannot determine whether this was related to vaccine hesitancy or oversight from clinicians in the correct identification of the missing doses. While we did not assess knowledge of vaccines among healthcare workers, other studies in the region have found low levels of vaccine knowledge among healthcare workers.17 18

Vaccination cards help parents and health workers track vaccination status and identify missed doses, which contributes to improving vaccination coverage. Studies show that MOV rates are lower in countries where most caregivers bring vaccination cards to healthcare visits.24 Routine verification of vaccination status and cards at every medical visit can significantly improve vaccination coverage.21 However, these cards are only useful if health workers ask caregivers to show them or provide information about their child’s immunisation status at every visit. In our study, only about half of the caregivers were asked about their child’s immunisation status or to show their vaccination cards.

Study limitations

Our study is subject to some limitations. Similar to other MOV studies that use the same WHO methodology, our study is not nationally representative of all MOV in Kyrgyzstan. Our results are specific to Bishkek and provide data from a cross-section of the population at the time of the study. Another limitation is that our study was conducted in July during a heat wave and when people often take holidays; therefore, attendance at healthcare organisations was lower than usual. This could have resulted in a greater proportion of people coming for acute illness than for preventive services, like vaccinations. Additionally, the study relies on self-reported reasons for non-immunisation and vaccine attitudes and could have been subject to social desirability bias. The proportion of caregivers with negative and neutral attitudes is likely higher than reported. Lastly, we did not include healthcare workers in our study, which limits our ability to assess how their knowledge and attitudes towards vaccines may have influenced the outcomes of MOV. Future research could provide valuable insights into this area by exploring the perspectives and experiences of healthcare professionals. Despite these limitations, our results provide a representative cross-sample of the children who sought care in the selected clinics and at the time of the study.

Conclusions

Our study, conducted in Bishkek, revealed a high prevalence of missed vaccination opportunities, with nine out of 10 children who were eligible for vaccination during a healthcare visit not receiving all due vaccines. This problem was particularly acute among children aged 12–30 months. Caregivers’ negative and neutral attitudes towards vaccines (reported by one-quarter of caregivers) were identified as a significant barrier to vaccination. Additionally, only half of the children had their immunisation status checked by a healthcare provider. Approaches where a child’s immunisation status is checked at every healthcare contact and encouraging healthcare workers to discuss vaccination with caregivers may help reduce missed opportunities and improve vaccination coverage in Kyrgyzstan.
