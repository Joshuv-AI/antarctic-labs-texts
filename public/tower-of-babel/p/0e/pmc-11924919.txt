Biology of recurrent cholesteatoma in a Romanian young patient – a case report
Authors: Ramona Andreea Bologa, Florin Anghelina, Mihaela Roxana Mitroi, Mircea Sorin Ciolofan, Carmen Aurelia Mogoantă, Alina Nicoleta Căpitănescu, Alexandru Florian Grecu, Liliana Anghelina, Mihai-Marius Botezat, Alina Andreea Mogoş, Bogdan Marius Fănuţă, Ileana Marinescu, Dan Cristian Grecu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cholesteatoma is an otologic pathology that can occur at any age and can lead to a variety of complications including facial palsy, intracranial abscess, hearing loss, venous thrombosis. Cholesteatoma, even if considered a benign condition, associates high risks of recurrency due to its invasiveness. We describe a case of recurrent cholesteatoma in a young boy who presented chronic ear discharge and hearing loss for which had undergone three surgical interventions between the ages of 16 and 19 years old, from 2019 to 2022. Pediatric cholesteatoma is more prone to recurrency. Considering surgical excision as the only treatment at the current moment, it is highly important to understand the biology of cholesteatoma lesional extension for further treatment management improvement. Good research of angiogenesis, chronic inflammation and immune infiltration correlated with surgical approach may be the future for preventing cholesteatoma recurrency.

Introduction

A rare entity of the otologic pathology is represented by cholesteatoma. It may be present in all three ear segments: external, middle and inner ear and is classically described by cystic like features surrounded by a matrix of keratinizing epithelium build up and a layer of connective subepithelial tissue, forming the perimatrix [1, 2, 3, 4].

Classification of cholesteatoma was subject to the 2017 international European Academy of Otology and Neuro-Otology (EAONO) classing implying congenital cholesteatoma (CC), affecting mostly pediatric population as a pearly mass with intact tympanic membrane and no ear discharge and acquired cholesteatoma (AC), present in both pediatric and adult patients. CC is further grouped considering the interested area in congenital middle ear cholesteatoma (CMEC) and congenital petrous bone cholesteatoma (CPBC). The amassing epithelial debris in the acquired form can appear as primary, inside a retraction pocket or secondary, related to tympanic perforation from trauma, chronic pathology or iatrogenic [1, 3, 5, 6].

Regarded as relatively rare because of variations based on age, geographic region and underlying conditions, little data is fairly known on the epidemiology, as some patients remain asymptomatic for long periods of time, thus distinguishing between its forms remains a challenge. CMEC associates an estimated incidence about 1–5% of all cholesteatomas, around 0.12 per 100 000 person-years (or nearly one in every million) while AC has an annual incidence of 3–15 cases in 100 000 children, both age groups enlisting a male predominance [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11].

Aim

The aim of this paper was to report a case of recurrent cholesteatoma in a young male patient and present the biological features that may be intricated in the relapse. This case is characterized by the aggressivity of the cholesteatoma, the necessity for the patient to undergo three surgical interventions over four years span and complications associating dehiscence of the facial nerve and trans-meatal fistulation.

Case presentation

A 19-year-old young boy was admitted in the Otorhinolaryngology Clinic of the Emergency County Clinical Hospital, Craiova, Romania between November and December 2022, for left chronic ear discharge, hearing loss and otalgia. He associated a long history of otologic pathology since the age of 12 and was first operated on the left ear at the age of 16.

The patient presented left chronic intermittent otorrhea and hearing loss from the age of 12 and was first admitted in the Hospital in 2019, at the age of 16 for chronic left otomastoiditis. Clinical examinations showed severe perforated left tympanic membrane, epitympanic cholesteatoma debris and reddish polypoid granulation tissue covered by purulent fetid secretions. Imaging evaluation at the time by temporal bone computed tomography (CT) scan described cholesteatomatous lesions in the left middle ear cavity with destruction of the lateral wall of the tympanic cavity and partial erosion of the incus. Diagnosed with severe cholesteatomatous otomastoiditis, surgical approach was necessary with modified mastoidectomy canal wall up technique, with reconstruction of the superior-posterior wall of the external auditory canal and tympanoplasty.

At 17 years, a second look surgery was performed as the patient presented with recurrent otorrhea and intraoperatively, a recurrent cholesteatoma was ablated with canal wall up mastoidectomy and tympanoplasty.

Accusing relapses of the ear discharge, two years after the second surgery, at 19 years old, the patient is presented for admission with otorrhea and otalgia, with left hearing loss, symptoms intensifying during last months. Current clinical assessment revealed left postauricular surgical scar, left mastoid area tender to painful. Partial obstruction of the nasal cavity by left deviated nasal septum and seromucous nasal secretions. Otoendoscopy showed purulent discharge in the left external ear canal, collapse of the superior posterior wall of the external ear canal with granulation tissue and congestion of the tympanic membrane.

Temporal bone CT scan indicating reduced temporo-mastoid pneumatization in left air cells with osteo-condensation and opacification of the inner ear canal (Figures 1, 2). Positive bacteriological findings of left ear discharge for Proteus spp., showing sensitivity to Ciprofloxacin.

Differential diagnosis is based on eliminating a series of pathological entities, such as middle ear benign and malignant tumors, tumors of the jugular bulb, schwannoma, through images, clinical aspects and surgical discoveries directed the diagnosis.

Based on clinical and imaging findings and differential diagnoses, the patient was diagnosed with chronic left otomastoiditis with recurrent postsurgical cholesteatoma. Conductive left hearing loss. Left nasal septal deviation.

Positive diagnosis relies on histopathological (HP) findings that confirm cholesteatoma, macroscopically and microscopically.

“Gold standard” treatment for recurrent aggressive cholesteatoma consists of revision left mastoidectomy. Surgical lesional evaluation showed giant cholesteatoma occupying the mastoid cavity, extended into the tympanic cavity with iatrogenic absence of the incus and stapes, and exposure of the facial nerve. The ablation of the cholesteatoma with the reconstruction of the superior posterior wall of the external ear canal and facial nerve and drainage was performed, followed by tympanoplasty. Tissue samples from the cholesteatoma ablation were submitted for further analysis.

Excision material was sent to the Pathological Anatomy Laboratory of the same Hospital. The specimens were fixed in a 10% neutral buffered formalin solution and then embedded in paraffin according to classical HP technique. The material was sectioned intro 4-μm thick slices using a microtome and subsequently stained with Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE).

Anatomopathological examination confirmed the clinical diagnosis of cholesteatoma. Macroscopically, is described as multiple grey pearly fragments with brown areas. Microscopic description on HE staining revealed multiple keratin lamellae, associated to necrotic debris and cholesterol granules, microcalcification and diffuse acute inflammation, while other tissue fragments presented with stratified keratinized squamous epithelium, with hemorrhage and chronic inflammation (Figure 3).

For a more accurate positive and differential diagnosis, we performed an immunohistochemical (IHC) study using antibody markers: anti-cluster of differentiation (CD)3 (monoclonal mouse anti-human CD3, clone F7.2.38, 1/50 dilution, Dako); anti-CD20 (monoclonal mouse anti-human CD20cy, clone L26, 1/50 dilution, Dako); anti-CD34 (monoclonal mouse anti-human CD34 Class II, clone QBEnd 10, 1/100 dilution, Dako); anti-CD68 (monoclonal mouse anti-human CD68, clone KP1, 1/100 dilution, Dako); anti-CD79α (monoclonal mouse anti-human CD79α, clone JCB117, 1/50 dilution, Dako); anti-tryptase (monoclonal mouse anti-human mast cell (MC) tryptase, clone AA1, 1/500 dilution, Dako).

The samples collected during surgery of the recurrent cholesteatoma presented intense markers suggestive for inflammation with great impact on the invasive characteristics of recurrent cholesteatoma related to extensive bone lesions. Samples associated alteration of the basal membrane, keratin and inflammatory infiltrate, among new patterns of vessel formation, thus promoting a local specific biology.

IHC study reveals a high vascularization in the cholesteatoma stromal level by using CD34 as a marker, highly selective for endothelial cells. CD34-positive cells are displayed in abundance, signaling new vessel formation and proliferation in the stroma of the cholesteatoma enhancing the subsequent important angiogenesis in the cholesteatoma (Figure 4).

Discussions

Cholesteatoma possesses a specific microenvironment associating inflammation, keratinocyte life cycle, angiogenesis and bone resorption. Consisting of three components, the cystic or necrotizing part of keratin debris, the matrix layer with mostly keratin epithelium of proliferation, peri-matrix layer or the stroma, mostly formed by connective tissue, blood vessels, cells and thus, these two last layers being the most involved areas in its destructive features and lesional recurrency [12, 13].

From the morphological point of view, characteristic for the cholesteatoma is the inflammatory reaction in the connective tissue of the subepithelial level and keratinization of the hyperproliferative epithelium associating formation of new vessels. Markers for these processes have been cytokeratins originating from lymphocytes, macrophages and other cells. Intensification of blood vessels appears in the context of increased cellular metabolic requirements and under the effect of inflammatory cells, mostly stimulated by keratinocytes in cholesteatoma, as much as endothelial cells. CD34 protein, localized in the endothelial cells of small blood vessels, appears in early stages of formation and development of new vascularization sources associated in evolution with microcirculation, favoring lesional extension, as much as bone destruction [13, 14, 15]. CD34 is also considered a marker for measurement of the microvessel density (MVD) [12].

Perivascular MCs positive for tryptase are also related to angiogenesis by synthetizing and secretion of angiogenic cytokines affecting endothelial cells and fibroblasts. Their enzyme tryptase represents an important regulator for invasive lesional capacity through angiogenesis [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17].

The perivascular location of the MCs in the stroma and its superficial parts, close to epithelial layer is reprehensive for the cholesteatoma samples. Studies also indicate a correlation between MCs presence and inflammation persistence, as well as their influence on bone lesions through osteoclast genesis induced by histamine release [17, 18, 19].

Inflammation is regarded as the initial step for further cholesteatoma-associated bone erosion, proliferation of epithelium and angiogenesis [18]. Large inflammatory infiltrates include macrophages, originating from myeloid-derived blood cells and identified by CD68-positive cells in the samples in the subepithelial connective tissue [18]. Considering the common precursory origin and common surface antigens that macrophages and osteoclasts share, one of the differentiating antigens is CD68 [20]. CD68-positive immunoreactivity in the perimatrix show presence of macrophages in the connective tissue that vary from uninuclear to polynuclear and giant multinuclear cells characteristic for phagocytosis [20, 21].

The inflammatory infiltrate positive for CD3, showing an increased number of T-lymphocytes in the perivascular areas of the stroma, among an abundance of B-lymphocytes identified by CD20-positive reaction, associated to a lower number of B-cells in the hemorrhage areas [18, 20, 21, 22]. Considering the potential for bone lesions induced by the tumor necrosis and the set of cells including macrophages, monocytes and lymphocytes that can produce tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), the abundance of T-lymphocytes plays an important role in pathogenesis and destructive characteristics of the middle ear cholesteatoma (MEC) [18, 20, 21, 22]. The important role played by T- and B-lymphocytes in cholesteatoma-associated bone lesions is highlighted by the capacity of these immune cells to be regarded as the main source for receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappa Β (NF-κB) ligand (RANKL), directly implicated in the bone destruction [18, 22].

Even though most literature regarding cholesteatoma consider the importance of the bacterial infection associated, emphasis on the presence of the Pseudomonas aeruginosa, some studies show a rate of 40% cases lacking any bacterial positivity in cultures [18, 21]. In our case, the recurrent presence of Proteus spp. was discovered in bacteriological findings, a Gram-negative bacterium. Studies have reported the connection between the formation of MEC and presence of Gram-negative bacteria providing a lipopolysaccharide biofilm [18, 21, 23].

Considering the importance of inflammatory lesions in cholesteatomatous disease, markers measuring the inflammation in cholesteatoma were used to evaluate it. Based on the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) defined as platelets × neutrophil/lymphocytes, as prediction marker among the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), changes in neutrophils, lymphocytes and platelets counts can be associated with inflammatory activity and with poor prognosis in many neoplasms, as much as with otologic pathologies [24, 25]. SII values appear higher in mucosal chronic otitis media than in cholesteatomatous otitis media, differentiating between a more systemic inflammation in the mucosal form than in the local inflammatory reactivity of the cholesteatoma [24, 25, 26]. Based on an SII of the first and second relapse of cholesteatoma of 436.65 and 351.91, respectively, our findings are similar to those in the group of cholesteatoma otitis media [24, 25, 26, 27].

In our case report, with an age of 12 years old for the first medical examination for chronic otitis media and 15 years old at the first cholesteatoma diagnosis, it is difficult to differentiate between congenital and acquired pediatric cholesteatoma [15, 28].

An important part of cholesteatoma lesional proliferation is the bacterial biofilm, three most isolated bacteria in pediatric patients were P. aeruginosa, Proteus mirabilis and Staphylococcus aureus [29]. Our patient was positive for Proteus spp., with bacterial finding similar to other studies that associate otological complications [29, 30, 31, 32].

We believe that the treatment of infected cholesteatoma must be complex to prevent recurrence or progression to more serious lesions [33].

Conclusions

Cholesteatoma of the middle ear associates inflammatory processes based on angiogenesis, as promoter for local inflammation extension in association with cytokines and TNF-α released by lymphocytes that contribute to local bone lesions. The presence of chronic ear discharge positive for Gram-negative bacterium and inflammatory markers may serve as foundation for the starting point for future development of alternative treatment for cholesteatoma that can slow the progression of lesions and extension with bone damage, thereby decreasing the aggressiveness of the pathology and the tendency to relapse.
