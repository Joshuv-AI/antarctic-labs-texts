Beyond Conventional Imaging: From Time-Based to Data-Driven Decision-Making in Acute Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Ichiro Deguchi, Junya Aoki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReperfusion therapy has profoundly transformed acute ischemic stroke (AIS) care. Initially, treatment decisions relied primarily on time from symptom onset. However, growing evidence has shown that the extent of irreversibly injured tissue and the presence of salvageable brain tissue determine the efficacy and safety of reperfusion therapy. This caused a paradigm shift from time-based selection toward tissue-based assessment, placing neuroimaging at the center of clinical decision-making. This narrative review traces the evolution of imaging-based stroke assessment in the reperfusion era. We review the development and clinical impact of the Alberta Stroke Program Early Computed Tomography Score (ASPECTS) and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)-ASPECTS, which translated the pathophysiological concept of the ischemic core into a practical, reproducible, routine clinical tool. We discuss the emergence of imaging and clinical mismatch concepts, including perfusion-based ischemic core–penumbra mismatch, as well as clinical–diffusion, MRA–diffusion, and DWI–FLAIR mismatches, which address the limitations of strict time-based criteria and expand treatment opportunities, particularly in patients with unknown onset time. Finally, we examine the growing role of artificial intelligence (AI)-driven automated imaging platforms in acute stroke care, enabling rapid, standardized, and quantitative assessment of ischemic core, penumbra, and vascular pathology. These tools support clinical decision-making under severe time constraints and reduce inter-reader and inter-institutional variability. This iterative process of trial and refinement has shaped the current framework of tissue-based, data-driven stroke care. We discuss how integrating established imaging concepts with emerging AI technologies may further advance precision reperfusion therapy and improve equity and outcomes in AIS treatment.Keywords: Acute ischemic stroke, Artificial intelligence, Neuroimaging, Reperfusion therapy, Tissue-based selection

Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) care has evolved substantially since the 1995 National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke trial demonstrated the efficacy of intravenous thrombolytic therapy 1) . Subsequent innovations in endovascular treatment devices, together with advances in neuroimaging technologies and expansion of their clinical indications, have further accelerated progress in this field. Consequently, reperfusion therapy has been established as a central strategy in AIS management and is now regarded as a standard treatment for eligible patients 2) .

However, mounting evidence has demonstrated that the efficacy and safety of reperfusion therapy depend strongly on the extent of irreversibly injured brain tissue, referred to as the ischemic core, and the presence of salvageable ischemic tissue, known as the ischemic penumbra. Appropriate patient selection therefore remains one of the most critical determinants of clinical outcomes 3 , 4) . Initially, eligibility for reperfusion therapy was determined primarily by the amount of time elapsed since symptom onset; this time-based selection approach is summarized in the vernacular by the phrase “time is brain.” Nevertheless, substantial inter-individual variability in the ischemic progression rate is well-established, and time alone does not reliably reflect tissue viability or damage reversibility 3 , 4) .

In response to these limitations, the concept of tissue-based assessment, where treatment decisions are guided by the biological status of brain tissue rather than by time alone, has developed progressively. This paradigm shift has led to the growing recognition that “tissue is at least as important as time,” and has positioned neuroimaging as a central component of decision-making in AIS care.

Within this framework, imaging-based ischemic core evaluation by means of non-contrast computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), identification of the ischemic penumbra by using perfusion imaging, and use of various mismatch concepts that are based on discrepancies between clinical and imaging findings have been introduced to refine treatment selection. Among these approaches, the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) and its derived metrics, which translated the pathophysiological concept of the ischemic core into a practical and reproducible scoring system for routine clinical use, are considered to be landmark tools. These methods have been pivotal in advancing AIS care from experience-based judgment toward standardized and objective assessment 5) .

More recently, rapid advances in image analysis techniques and computational power have facilitated clinical implementation of automated imaging platforms that are based on artificial intelligence (AI). These technologies facilitate rapid and standardized quantification of the ischemic core and penumbra, prompt detection of vascular pathology, and reduction of inter-reader and inter-institutional variability. Consequently, AI-assisted imaging has emerged as a promising adjunct to clinical decision-making in the highly time-sensitive setting of AIS care 6) . Nevertheless, the optimal positioning of AI-based tools relative to conventional imaging methods and established mismatch concepts, as well as their clinical roles and limitations, has yet to be clarified fully.

In this narrative review, we provide an overview of the current state and the evolution of imaging-based assessment in the era of reperfusion therapy for AIS. We focus on three key domains: (1) ASPECTS and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)-ASPECTS as the foundation of ischemic core assessment; (2) imaging and clinical mismatch concepts that have supported tissue-based decision-making; and (3) the characteristics and clinical implications of major AI-assisted automated imaging platforms that are increasingly being implemented in routine practice. We further discuss how integration of these established imaging paradigms with emerging AI technologies may drive future development of more precise and individualized precision reperfusion therapy. Imaging-Based Assessment of the Ischemic Core: ASPECTS and DWI-ASPECTS

One of the most critical factors in determining treatment eligibility in cases of AIS is the accurate assessment of the extent of irreversibly injured brain tissue, referred to as the ischemic core. In cases of extensive infarcts, reperfusion therapy has limited benefits, and the risk of hemorrhagic complications is increased 7 - 10) . Therefore, rapid and accurate evaluation of early ischemic change observed in the acute phase of ischemic stroke is critically important.

Early ischemic change is visualized on CT images as early CT signs and on magnetic resonance images predominantly as hyperintense lesions on DWI. These imaging findings reflect tissue alterations caused by acute ischemia and are associated with larger final infarct volumes and worse clinical outcomes. Accordingly, they are widely used in clinical practice as imaging surrogates for the ischemic core 11 , 12) . Indeed, current clinical guidelines state that extensive hypoattenuation on CT reflects irreversible tissue injury, which benefits little from reperfusion therapy 2) .

Against this background, the establishment of a rapid and reproducible method for ischemic core assessment has been a major challenge in AIS care. The ASPECTS was introduced as a simple, semi-quantitative, non-contrast CT-based scoring system for evaluating early ischemic changes within the middle cerebral artery territory, and it has since been widely adopted in routine clinical practice 5) . The ASPECTS concept was subsequently extended to the posterior circulation (PC-ASPECTS), which enables quantitative evaluation of ischemic changes involving the brainstem and cerebellum 13) . It should be noted, however, that ASPECTS was originally developed for the middle cerebral artery territory and is therefore not directly applicable to anterior cerebral artery infarctions, representing an inherent anatomical limitation of the scoring system.

Although the ASPECTS was originally developed for CT, the same scoring concept has also been applied to DWI, where it is now commonly referred to as the DWI-ASPECTS 14) . DWI is more sensitive than CT to ischemic changes, and the DWI-ASPECTS does not always yield results equivalent to those of the original CT-based ASPECTS. Nevertheless, in Japan, where MRI is widely available in emergency settings, the DWI-ASPECTS has been extensively used as an initial imaging assessment tool for AIS cases in routine clinical practice and in research settings 15 - 18) .

Because the ASPECTS is simple to apply and demonstrates relatively high inter-institutional reproducibility, it has been used for patient selection or stratification in numerous large-scale clinical trials of intravenous thrombolysis and endovascular therapy (EVT). In particular, in the landmark randomized controlled trials that established EVT efficacy, patients with a low ASPECTS, which represents a large ischemic core, were explicitly or implicitly excluded. Consequently, the ASPECTS has played a central role in determining patient eligibility for reperfusion therapy 19 - 24) . Given this background, recent international guidelines have explicitly recommended the use of ASPECTS-based ischemic core assessment in patient selection for EVT, positioning the ASPECTS as a standard decision-making tool not only in clinical trials but also in real-world clinical practice 2) .

More recently, the efficacy of EVT has also been reported in patients with large ischemic cores, which typically correspond to an ASPECTS of approximately 3–5 25 - 27) . Consequently, the role of the ASPECTS has expanded from being a simple exclusion criterion to providing a quantitative framework for balancing potential therapeutic benefit with the risk of complications. Even in the current era of advanced perfusion imaging and automated image analysis algorithms, the ASPECTS, which is derived solely from non-contrast CT or DWI and is rapidly assessable without requiring complex post-processing, retains exceptionally high clinical utility in the time-critical setting of AIS care. In this regard, the ASPECTS represents a landmark achievement in translating the pathophysiological concept of the ischemic core into a practical and reproducible imaging metric for everyday clinical use. Consequently, AIS care has advanced from experience-based judgment to standardized and objective assessment. Even in the modern era of EVT, the ASPECTS remains a cornerstone of treatment selection, and its contribution to the historical evolution of AIS care has been substantial.

Imaging and Clinical Mismatch Concepts in Tissue-Based Stroke Assessment

Successful treatment of AIS is highly time-dependent, implying the need for rapid treatment decision-making, which is based on limited clinical and imaging information. Traditionally, eligibility for reperfusion therapy has been determined according to time-based selection criteria, relying primarily on the time elapsed since symptom onset. However, substantial inter-individual variability has been observed in the rate of ischemic progression, and thus, time alone does not adequately reflect tissue viability or reversibility. Against this background, the concept of tissue-based assessment, i.e., treatment decision-making guided by the biological status of brain tissue rather than by time alone, has progressively been developed to address uncertainties related to onset time and lesion evolution 4 , 5) .

As one approach to tissue-based assessment, identification of the ischemic penumbra by means of CT- or MRI-based perfusion imaging has attracted considerable attention. However, perfusion imaging requires additional acquisition and post-processing time. Furthermore, access to perfusion analysis software and technical expertise may vary across healthcare systems, partly because of differences in reimbursement policies, regulatory approval, and resource allocation. These factors may limit its feasibility as a rapid decision-making tool in certain clinical settings.

Consequently, alternative approaches that are not dependent on perfusion imaging have been proposed. These include clinical–diffusion mismatch (CDM), which integrates clinical severity with findings on DWI 28 - 32) , and magnetic resonance angiography–diffusion mismatch (MDM), which combines the presence of large vessel pathology with DWI findings 33 - 35) .

In these approaches, the extent of DWI lesions has been quantified either by volumetric measurement or by more practical methods, such as the DWI-ASPECTS. By assessing discrepancies between the estimated ischemic core and clinical symptoms or vascular findings, these mismatch concepts aim to infer the presence of salvageable brain tissue. Although these strategies have been clinically meaningful, enabling treatment decision-making without the need for perfusion imaging and requiring relatively short acquisition and analysis times, CDM and MDM, particularly when based on ASPECTS, were not established as standardized standalone primary selection criteria in major randomized controlled trials. Nevertheless, the underlying concept of core–clinical or core–vascular discrepancy has substantially influenced modern trial design and tissue-based selection paradigms.

Nevertheless, the concepts of CDM and MDM have been key factors in clearly demonstrating the limitations of treatment selection based solely on time elapsed since symptom onset. Moreover, they have facilitated a broader acceptance in clinical practice of the idea that the state of brain tissue can be inferred by integrating clinical findings with non-contrast imaging findings.

Building on this conceptual framework, imaging-based selection strategies were developed further to estimate tissue age and to guide treatment decision-making even for patients with an unknown symptom-onset time. Among these, DWI–fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) mismatch has emerged as a representative approach. Among various MRI sequences, DWI can detect ischemic changes with high sensitivity within the first hour after stroke onset, whereas FLAIR imaging typically requires several hours (approximately 3–6 h) before ischemic signal changes become apparent 36 , 37) . Selection of patients with unknown onset time for intravenous thrombolysis based on DWI–FLAIR mismatch, which exploits this temporal discrepancy, was initially shown to be safe and feasible in pilot studies 38 , 39) . These findings subsequently led to randomized controlled trials that confirmed the efficacy of DWI–FLAIR mismatch-guided therapy in this patient population 40 , 41) . On the basis of this evidence, current clinical guidelines now recommend intravenous thrombolytic therapy for patients with ischemic stroke of unknown onset time who demonstrate a DWI–FLAIR mismatch 2) . Alongside these MRI-based temporal mismatch strategies, such as DWI–FLAIR mismatch, perfusion imaging–guided thrombolysis has been supported by randomized controlled trials, including EXTEND and ECASS4, which demonstrated improved functional outcomes in selected patients treated beyond the conventional time window 42 , 43) . An individual patient data meta-analysis by the Extent of Stroke collaboration further confirmed the benefit of perfusion-guided thrombolysis in patients with unknown or extended time windows 44) . Reflecting this accumulated evidence, recent ESO and AHA/ASA guidelines support the use of CT- or MRI-based perfusion imaging (and/or MRI-based mismatch criteria) to guide intravenous thrombolysis selection in selected patients with extended or unknown time windows 42 , 45) .

Collectively, these developments further consolidate the paradigm shift from rigid time-based criteria toward tissue-based decision-making, in which imaging-defined penumbral viability rather than clock time determines therapeutic eligibility. The abovementioned series of developments can be viewed as the culmination of a strong and shared clinical motivation across stroke centers worldwide to rescue viable brain tissue and to extend the benefits of treatment to patients who had previously been excluded solely because of the uncertainty regarding their symptom-onset time. Collectively, mismatch concepts symbolize the transition in AIS care from rigid time-based criteria to decision-making based on the biological status of brain tissue.

Although the clinical positioning of mismatch concepts continues to evolve with the widespread adoption of perfusion imaging, advances in EVT, and recent progress in AI-based automated image analysis, the fundamental principles underlying these approaches remain highly relevant. Specifically, the emphasis on discrepancies between clinical findings and imaging results, the integration of vascular pathology with ischemic core assessment, and the inference of temporal information based on tissue characteristics continue to provide a conceptual foundation for contemporary precision stroke care.

Data-Driven and Automated Imaging for Precision Reperfusion Therapy

In AIS care, treatment decisions must be made rapidly and accurately based on imaging evaluation under severe time and information constraints. However, particularly in rural areas or resource-limited institutions, access to experienced neuroradiologists or stroke specialists capable of providing expert image interpretation is not always available. Against this background, interest in AI-assisted tools that support diagnosis and provide standardized imaging assessments in real time has grown.

In the context of reperfusion therapy, rapid, reproducible, and reader-independent imaging evaluation is essential for appropriate patient selection. Accordingly, AI-based image analysis technologies have attracted attention as powerful tools for reducing inter-reader variability and accelerating clinical decision-making 6 , 46) . At present, a variety of AI-assisted imaging platforms are increasingly used in routine AIS care, supported by a growing body of clinical evidence. Several commercially available AI platforms are used in clinical practice for automated stroke imaging analysis. Examples include Viz.ai, RapidAI, and Brainomix e-Stroke, which offer automated assessment of large vessel occlusion detection and, depending on system configuration, estimation of ischemic core volume, perfusion parameters, ASPECTS, or collateral status. In addition, AI-based stroke imaging applications are available as workstation-based solutions or as applications embedded within imaging platforms provided by major manufacturers. Examples include Olea Sphere, Philips StrokeViewer, SYNAPSE SAI Viewer, and Siemens AI-Rad Companion. These systems enable automated or semi-automated image analysis within existing clinical infrastructures, facilitating standardized imaging assessment in routine practice.

Importantly, AI-assisted imaging has also indirectly contributed to the expansion of treatment time windows for AIS. Notably, automated perfusion software played a key role in landmark randomized trials such as DAWN and DEFUSE 3, which established the efficacy of thrombectomy beyond conventional time windows 47 , 48) . These trials operationalized tissue-based selection by incorporating standardized quantitative core and mismatch criteria derived from automated imaging analysis. Although AI itself does not redefine therapeutic time thresholds, rapid and standardized quantification of the ischemic core, penumbra, and vascular pathology enables timely identification of patients who may benefit from reperfusion therapy beyond conventional limits. In this sense, AI supports the transition from rigid time-based selection to biologically informed, tissue-based decision-making in real-world clinical practice.

However, it is also important to recognize that advanced imaging-based selection, including AI-assisted assessment, may in some cases narrow treatment eligibility by excluding patients without demonstrable salvageable tissue. For example, MRI-based or perfusion-based selection strategies may identify fewer eligible candidates compared with more permissive CT-based approaches. Thus, AI-driven precision may expand and selectively refine treatment windows, depending on clinical context and imaging strategy.

Future Perspectives

As outlined above, contemporary AI-assisted imaging platforms provide complementary strengths in supporting rapid, reproducible, and standardized imaging assessment in AIS care. These technologies contribute to workflow optimization, quantitative evaluation of the ischemic core and penumbra, and multiparametric image interpretation, thereby facilitating more consistent and efficient treatment decision-making. Importantly, AI systems are not intended to replace expert clinical judgment but to augment it, helping to reduce variability and disparities in stroke care across institutions and healthcare systems.

A central future challenge lies in the optimal integration of imaging data, clinical variables, and therapeutic strategies into cohesive, interoperable decision-support ecosystems. Rather than functioning as isolated analytical tools, next-generation platforms should aim to harmonize multimodal imaging findings with patient-specific biological and clinical information, thereby advancing truly individualized precision reperfusion therapy.

In parallel with technological development, the conceptual axes of imaging-based evaluation are also likely to evolve. This evolution may include the emergence of imaging approaches that extend beyond conventional CT and MRI paradigms, advances in acquisition and reconstruction techniques that enhance tissue characterization, and refinement of streamlined, minimal yet sufficient imaging protocols tailored to contemporary reperfusion workflows. Furthermore, AI-driven tissue-based phenotyping may enable outcome assessment not only in terms of functional disability but also in relation to the degree of brain tissue preservation and recovery. Such developments could support more biologically grounded outcome frameworks that complement, rather than rely exclusively upon, traditional clinical scales such as the modified Rankin Scale.

Conclusion

Imaging-guided decision-making in AIS has evolved through a continuous process of clinical questioning, technological innovation, and pragmatic refinement, in parallel with advances in reperfusion therapy. The introduction of the ASPECT represented a pivotal step in translating the pathophysiological concept of the ischemic core into a simple and reproducible tool that is applicable to real-world practice. Subsequently, imaging and clinical mismatch concepts have challenged the limitations of rigid time-based treatment selection and have fostered broader recognition that treatment decisions should be grounded in tissue viability, rather than based on clock time alone.

Importantly, these developments did not emerge in isolation but reflect the collective experience of stroke clinicians seeking to rescue salvageable brain tissue under increasingly diverse clinical scenarios, including patients with unknown symptom-onset time and with heterogeneous rates of infarct progression. Mismatch-based strategies, although not universally adopted as primary selection criteria in randomized trials, have played a crucial conceptual role in shaping the modern tissue-based framework of AIS care.

More recently, AI-driven automated imaging platforms have further transformed clinical practice by enabling rapid, standardized, and reproducible assessment of the ischemic core, vascular pathology, and perfusion status. Rather than replacing expert clinical judgment, these technologies serve as complementary tools that can support decision-making in time-critical environments and can help to mitigate disparities in expertise and resources across institutions. In this context, AI-assisted imaging can be viewed as a natural extension of earlier efforts to objectify and standardize imaging-based evaluation, rather than as a disruptive departure from established paradigms.

From the perspective of clinicians who have experienced the transition from time-based decision-making, through mismatch-based assessment, to the current era of AI-supported imaging, optimal AIS stroke care increasingly depends on the thoughtful integration of multiple sources of information. No metric or technology is sufficient when used in isolation. Future progress in precision reperfusion therapy will rely on harmonizing established imaging concepts, such as the ASPECTS, with emerging data-driven technologies, while preserving the central role of clinical reasoning. Ultimately, the future of AIS care will not be defined by any single imaging modality or algorithm, but by the thoughtful integration of biological insight, technological innovation, and clinical judgment. In this evolving landscape, imaging-guided decision-making continues to move beyond rigid temporal thresholds toward a truly data-informed, patient-centered paradigm of precision reperfusion therapy.
