Successful Treatment of Pigmented Purpuric Dermatoses With Abrocitinib: A Case Report
Authors: Yuping Zhang, Xiji Lu, Chen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPigmented Purpuric Dermatoses (PPD) are chronic conditions often resistant to standard treatments, creating a need for new therapies, particularly for those suffering from severe itching. This report details a patient with a year of refractory lower‐extremity PPD who failed multiple oral and topical treatments. The patient was then successfully treated with oral abrocitinib (100 mg daily). Significant relief from pruritus occurred within 2 weeks, and substantial improvement in the skin lesions followed by the sixth week. After 3 months of treatment, lesions resolved to simple brown patches, with no recurrence seen during a four‐month follow‐up and no significant side effects noted. These results suggest that the JAK1 inhibitor abrocitinib possesses potential as a safe and highly effective treatment option for refractory PPD, warranting further clinical study.

Introduction

Pigmented Purpuric Dermatoses (PPD) comprises a group of chronic, recurrent skin disorders characterized by capillaritis, with its core pathology being hemorrhage and local inflammation in the superficial papillary dermal blood vessels. Immunopathological studies have shown that erythrocyte extravasation and vascular injury result from local cell‐mediated immune inflammation [1]. Clinically, they typically present as purpuric rashes predominantly on the lower extremities. Although many patients are asymptomatic, itching can accompany the lesions, significantly affecting daily life. Currently, there is no universally effective treatment, making management challenging [1, 2]. The Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway is a widely expressed intracellular signaling pathway involved in various biological processes. Its persistent activation can induce the release of local inflammatory factors, exacerbate the aforementioned pathological damage and promote disease progression, which provides a clear theoretical basis for targeted treatment of PPD with JAK1 inhibitors, although the specific pathogenesis of PPD remains unclear [3].

Shan Cao et al. reported that two patients with intractable PPD experienced notable improvement within 1 week following treatment with Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) inhibitors [4]. Inspired by these findings, combined with drug properties, we selected abrocitinib, a highly selective JAK1 inhibitor (instead of other JAK inhibitors), and this case report details a refractory PPD patient who was successfully treated with abrocitinib.

Case History/Examination

A 51‐year‐old woman presented with multiple petechiae and ecchymoses on both lower limbs, accompanied by severe itching persisting for 1 year. She had no history of diabetes, hypertension, or prolonged standing. Baseline laboratory examinations, including complete blood count, coagulation function, HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C, were normal. Physical examination revealed multiple brownish patches on her feet and calves, surrounded by red petechiae resembling chili powder (Figure 1a,b). Histopathology showed increased basal layer pigmentation, lymphocytic infiltration around superficial dermal blood vessels, significant red blood cell extravasation, and individual cell infiltration into the epidermis (Figure 2a,b), confirming the diagnosis of PPD.

Before initiating abrocitinib treatment, a 51‐year‐old female patient was diagnosed with Pigmented Purpuric Dermatoses, characterized by the presence of petechiae and ecchymoses on both lower limbs (a,b).

Skin biopsy showed lymphocyte infiltration around superficial dermal blood vessels and significant red blood cell extravasation (a, 100×; b, 200×).

Detailed prior treatments included oral diosmin and calcium dobesilate (for 4 months), oral thalidomide (50 mg qn, for 2 weeks), and topical fluticasone propionate cream and mucopolysaccharide polysulfate cream (for 4 months). No improvement was observed with these therapies, and the pruritus severity assessed by the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS, 0–10 points; 10 = worst itching) was 8 points at baseline. As a result, the patient commenced oral abrocitinib at a dose of 100 mg daily. After 2 weeks, pruritus was notably reduced, with the NRS score decreasing to 2 points. By 6 weeks, skin lesions had substantially improved, with no new petechiae and only residual brown patches (Figure 3a,b), and the pruritus NRS score further dropped to 1 point. The patient discontinued therapy after 3 months due to financial constraints. During a four‐month follow‐up, she exhibited no recurrence of symptoms or new lesions, and no significant adverse effects were noted.

Methods (Investigations and Treatment)

Due to the poor therapeutic effect of conventional treatments, abrocitinib was administered off‐label after the patient provided written informed consent. As this report was based on clinical needs without additional research interventions, ethical committee approval was not applied for, and all treatment and follow‐up procedures were in line with clinical practice guidelines. The primary method employed was the systemic administration of the JAK1 inhibitor, abrocitinib, at a daily dose of 100 mg. Treatment response was monitored clinically over a six‐week period, followed by a four‐month observation phase post‐cessation to assess relapse rates and long‐term effects.

Conclusions and Results

Following treatment with abrocitinib, the patient's pruritus and skin lesions improved gradually, and active petechiae resolved with post‐inflammatory hyperpigmentation remaining. No symptom recurrence or drug‐related adverse reactions were observed during the 4‐month follow‐up after drug withdrawal. This case suggests that JAK1 inhibition may help rapidly control the acute inflammatory manifestations of pigmented purpuric dermatosis.

Discussion

The precise etiology of PPD remains unclear, though it is believed to involve localized immune‐mediated mechanisms. The JAK/STAT signaling pathway plays a critical role in mediating inflammatory responses, and its persistent activation is associated with various inflammatory and autoimmune diseases [3, 5]. Currently, no standardized treatment guidelines exist for PPD. Conventional therapies demonstrate limited efficacy in refractory cases, and novel therapeutic strategies are urgently needed.

The efficacy of JAK1 inhibitors in the treatment of refractory PPD has been demonstrated in two case reports before. Cao et al. reported two cases: a 27‐year‐old patient (disease duration 6 months) treated with upadacitinib 15 mg daily, with onset of effect at 3 days, complete clearance of skin lesions at 3 weeks, and maintenance therapy with 15 mg every other day for 2 months; a 12‐year‐old male patient (disease duration 1 month) treated with abrocitinib 50 mg daily for 7 days showed improvement in skin lesions [4]. Aditi Chokshi et al. reported one case of a 56‐year‐old patient with eczematoid PPD (disease duration 1.5 years) treated with upadacitinib 15 mg daily as monotherapy, with resolution of symptoms at 2 weeks and discontinuation after 2 months of maintenance [6].

This study enrolled a 51‐year‐old female patient with refractory PPD (disease duration 1 year), who was treated with abrocitinib at 100 mg/d. Pruritus was relieved at 2 weeks, and skin lesions improved at 6 weeks. No recurrence or adverse reactions were noted during 4 months of follow‐up after discontinuing the drug at 3 months. Compared with the 27‐year‐old patient treated with upadacitinib reported by Cao et al., this patient was older with a longer disease duration (1 year vs. 6 months), presenting a slightly slower onset of efficacy (2 weeks vs. 3 days) and slower clearance of skin lesions; compared with the 12‐year‐old patient treated with abrocitinib reported by Cao et al., this patient was an adult with a longer disease duration (1 year vs. 1 month) and a higher dosage (100 vs. 50 mg/d), leading to a longer onset time; compared with the 56‐year‐old patient treated with upadacitinib reported by Aditi Chokshi et al., this patient had a similar age and disease duration (1 year vs. 1.5 years) and consistent onset time (both 2 weeks), but a slightly slower improvement in skin lesions. These differences were closely associated with the patient's age, disease duration, and type of medication.

In light of the above comparative findings, the latest JADE study suggests that when abrocitinib is used in adolescents aged ≥ 12 years and adults with moderate‐to‐severe atopic dermatitis (AD), its therapeutic effect can be traced back to the initial administration stage: both populations receive an initial dose of 100 mg/d or 200 mg/d, and significant improvements in pruritus and skin lesions can be achieved within 2 weeks, with stable efficacy and minimal individual variation [7]. Both AD and PPD are inflammatory skin diseases mediated by abnormal activation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway. In this study, the 51‐year‐old female patient with PPD achieved pruritus relief 2 weeks after treatment with an initial dose of 100 mg/d, which was similar to the early efficacy observed in adult AD populations. However, it remains to be explored whether the feature of “stable traceable efficacy from the initial dose” in AD is equally applicable to PPD. AD is centered on epidermal inflammation, while PPD is dominated by vascular lesions; additionally, the efficacy of PPD is more significantly affected by age and disease duration, which is significantly different from the high stability of AD efficacy. Whether this conclusion can be extrapolated to PPD requires further verification.

This case demonstrates that targeting the JAK1 pathway with abrocitinib effectively alleviated both skin lesions and pruritus, with no new petechial hemorrhages observed. The residual brown patches suggest that pathways beyond JAK1 might contribute to PPD's pathogenesis, reinforcing the likelihood that multiple JAK‐mediated pathways are involved and interact. Although the patient remained symptom‐free during the four‐month follow‐up, longer‐term observations and additional case reports are necessary to validate these findings.

Given the chronic nature of PPD, managing symptoms effectively while minimizing recurrence, adverse drug reactions, and treatment costs remains challenging. Adjusting JAK inhibitor dosages or modifying dosing intervals may offer future optimization strategies. Despite the limited sample size and short duration of this case, abrocitinib shows promise as a potential treatment for refractory PPD.

Meanwhile, the safety of JAK inhibitors should be viewed objectively. Common potential risks associated with JAK inhibitors include infection and thromboembolic events [8, 9]. Although the overall incidence is low, high‐risk populations require vigilance. No such adverse reactions were observed in this case or previous cases of PPD treated with JAK1 inhibitors, indicating good short‐term safety. However, this study has inherent limitations: as a single‐case retrospective design, it has a small sample size, and the results are prone to be affected by individual differences. It cannot fully evaluate the long‐term safety of JAK inhibitors in the treatment of PPD, nor can it determine the optimal dosage, course of treatment, and long‐term recurrence risk of abrocitinib. Furthermore, the lack of more comparative cases with different ages and disease durations makes it impossible to clarify their impact on treatment outcomes; therefore, close monitoring of risks is still necessary in clinical application, especially for high‐risk patients.

In summary, the JAK1 inhibitor abrocitinib exhibits good short‐term efficacy and safety in the treatment of refractory PPD, which can quickly relieve symptoms. This case enriches the clinical evidence for its use in treating adult refractory PPD. In the future, large‐sample, multi‐center prospective studies are needed to verify its long‐term efficacy and safety, and to explore standardized treatment regimens.
