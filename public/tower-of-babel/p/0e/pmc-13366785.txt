Nurses’ experiences in delivering bad news to families of end-of-life patients; a qualitative content analysis
Authors: Bahman Aghaei, Mohammad Reza Heidari, Reza Norouzadeh, Somayeh Namdari, Mohammad Abbasinia, Seyed Kazem Mousavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDelivering bad news to families of end-of-life (EOL) patients is a complex ethical and emotional task for nurses, particularly within culturally specific contexts. This study aimed to explore the lived experiences of Iranian nurses when communicating bad news to families of EOL patients.MethodsA qualitative design using conventional content analysis was employed. Semi-structured, in-depth interviews were conducted with 18 purposively selected nurses from intensive care, emergency, oncology, and internal medicine units of teaching hospitals in Tehran, Iran. Data were transcribed verbatim and analyzed through open coding, categorization, and abstraction to identify latent themes.ResultsA core process emerged “Navigating the Harsh Reality” comprising three categories: (1) Preparation: assessing family readiness, creating a calm environment, and strategic timing and pacing; (2) Conveying the Bitter Truth: conveying the clinical truth, preserving truthful compassion, and facilitating intra-family communication; and (3) Providing Solace: offering professional reassurance, and communicating with empathetic language.ConclusionIranian nurses employ a structured, culturally embedded three-phase approach to bad news delivery that integrates truthfulness with compassionate, family-centered care. These findings offer a foundation for developing culturally appropriate communication guidelines and training programs in EOL care settings in Iran and comparable cultural contexts.

Introduction

Delivering bad news to families of patients at the end of life (EOL) is a complex and emotionally demanding aspect of nursing practice [1]. For nurses, this is not a discrete event but an ongoing communication process that significantly influences a family’s psychological adaptation to grief and bereavement [2]. In Iran, this task is further shaped by sociocultural and religious norms that prioritize family involvement in care, often favoring indirect or gradual disclosure to preserve hope and mitigate acute distress [3]. This creates a tension between professional ethics emphasizing truth-telling and familial expectations centered on emotional protection [4]. In this context, the family functions not as a passive observer but as a central unit of care, reflecting Islamic and Iranian cultural values that emphasize strong familial bonds, collective decision-making, and mutual emotional support during illness [5]. The Quranic concept of sakīnah (tranquility) underscores the family’s role as a source of spiritual and emotional stability during life’s trials, including terminal illness [6]. Consequently, when EOL news is communicated, the family often becomes the primary recipient, requiring nurses to act not only as information providers but also as cultural mediators and emotional anchors [7].

Despite its importance, the process of delivering bad news in Iran remains largely informal and inconsistent [4]. Western models such as SPIKES (Includes: Setting up the interview, Assessing the patient’s Perception, Obtaining the patient’s Invitation, Giving Knowledge and information, Addressing Emotions with Empathy, Strategy and Summary) and PEWTER (Prepare, Evaluate, Warn, Tell, Emotional response, Regroup / Reflect) which are widely accepted, although broadly applicable to Iran, are often not compatible with the collectivist dynamics of health care, and diverse care environments in this country and ignore ingrained cultural practices such as indirect communication, strategic timing of disclosure (e.g., avoiding late-night announcements), and the use of cultural euphemisms [8, 9]. As a result, Iranian nurses frequently rely on experiential strategies developed through practice, leading to variability in delivery and increased professional burden [10]. This gap becomes particularly pronounced in the final stage of life, a period characterized by intense psychological vulnerability, pervasive uncertainty, and the potential emergence of “near-death experiences” [9]. In this context, the manner of communication can either facilitate a dignified grieving process or deepen emotional trauma [11]. As Pergert and Lützén note, truth-telling in EOL care must be understood as a relational, ethical practice that balances honesty with the preservation of hope [12]. Nurses, as the most consistent point of contact, bear the responsibility of navigating this balance [10].

While prior qualitative studies in Iran have identified general challenges in communication with the dying patient, such as “contingency help” and “uncertainty management”, few have systematically explored the specific, culturally grounded strategies employed by frontline nurses in high-acuity settings like ICUs and emergency departments [13, 14]. Existing frameworks, including the “TAT” protocol (consists of: 1- Team formation, preparation, and coordination for delivering bad news; 2- Notification; 3- Transcending and planning for the future, and follow-up), proposed by Sadat Hosseini [15], while culturally informed, lack derivation from the lived experiences of nurses performing this task daily under pressure. To address this gap, this study employs a content analysis approach to answer: What are lived experiences and specific strategies of Iranian nurses when delivering bad news to families of EOL patients? By centering the voices of frontline clinicians, this research aims to generate a culturally resonant, empirically grounded model that can inform context-sensitive training and clinical guidelines for EOL communication in Iran and similar cultural contexts.

Methods

This qualitative study used the conventional content analysis approach outlined by Graneheim and Lundman. This method distinguishes between manifest and latent levels of text in a three-step process, enabling interpretation of meaning without loss of credibility [16]. Thus, the approach was used to provide a rich and accurate description of nurses’ experiences delivering bad news.

Participants and setting

The study was conducted in several teaching hospitals affiliated with medical universities in Tehran, Iran. Participants were 18 clinical nurses who met the following criteria: (a) a minimum of two years of clinical experience, (b) direct experience in caring for adult patients at the EOL, and (c) experience in communicating bad news to the families of these patients. Participants were purposefully selected to ensure maximum variation in age, gender, work experience, educational level, and clinical setting (Intensive Care Unit, Coronary Care Unit, Emergency Department, Internal Department, and Oncology).

Data collection

Data were collected between March 2020 to July 2021through in-depth, semi-structured, face-to-face interviews. The initial interview questions were designed by the research team through a literature review and refined and modified by conducting a pilot study on three expert nurses in the field of palliative care. The interviews were conducted at a time and place convenient for the participants to ensure privacy and comfort. The primary interview question was: “Please describe how you convey the acute or critical condition of an EOL patient to their family.” This was followed by probing questions such as, “Can you give me a specific example?” and “How did you feel during that situation?” to encourage rich, detailed narratives. Each interview lasted between 20 and 40 min (average: 30 min) and was audio-recorded with the participants’ consent. Then, data analysis was done with the help of MAXQDA 2020 software. After analyzing 17 interviews, the researchers reached saturation, so no new subcategories and categories were formed. However, one more interview was conducted to ensure saturation and confirm the findings [17].

Data analysis

The recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim in Persian by the first author. The analysis followed the steps of Graneheim and Lundman’s content analysis [16]. The entire text of each transcript was read several times to gain a sense of the whole. Meaning units—words, sentences, or paragraphs related to the aim of the study—were identified and extracted. These meaning units were then condensed and coded, creating a set of initial, concrete codes. Through a constant comparative process, these codes were sorted into sub-categories based on their similarities and differences. Finally, the sub-categories were abstracted and integrated to form three main categories that capture the latent, underlying meaning of the nurses’ experiences.

Trustworthiness

To ensure research rigor, Lincoln and Guba ‘s four criteria were used [18]. Credibility was ensured by member checking by three participating nurses, who provided both written and oral feedback. Peer debriefing further supported credibility. Transferability was achieved through rich, detailed descriptions of participant characteristics, including department of service, work experience, and type of bad news, as well as the research context. This allowed readers to judge similarities with their own context. Dependability was ensured by fully documenting research steps. This included interviews, codes, analytical notes, and the theme development process, following the Granheim and Lundman approach, and was presented to an external reviewer. Confirmability was achieved by maintaining an audit trail and ensuring that final themes were extracted directly from the raw data, specifically nurses’ quotes, rather than the researcher’s assumptions.

Preparation

All participants emphasized that delivering bad news to the families is not something to be done hastily. It requires careful and deliberate preparation:“Before delivering any news, especially bad news, you need to prepare and create it. This is something we have learned and practiced since childhood in life. It is not good to act hastily here”. (P.4). In their opinion, this preparation included three key aspects:

The first was assessing family readiness. Initially, nurses reported meticulously assessing both the patient’s clinical status and the family’s emotional state before initiating the conversation. One nurse stated, “The reality is that transferring bad news is extremely hard work… I pay attention to the severity of the patient’s condition and whether the patient is in the final moments” (P.12). They also assessed the family’s readiness, looking for signs of agitation, potential for violence, or emotional fragility. This analysis was crucial to tailor their approach. In assessing family’s psychological and physical state a participant said: “Before informing the family of the patient’s death, I first assess their psychological and emotional state… I observe whether they are agitated, prone to aggression, or ready to hear the news.” (P.15).

Nurses prioritized creating a private and calm setting to foster a sense of safety and respect before delivering distressing news. “I take the family to a quiet space—a vacant adjacent room and if possible, I go there with them. I’ve found this has a much stronger positive impact.” (P.8). Also. one of the participants describes his experience of the gradual disclosure of bad news to the families: “I don’t go straight to the point or state the reality bluntly… I proceed step by step. I say, ‘I wish your child would check on them…’ Then I add, ‘Your daughter’s condition deteriorated today…” (P.14).

Nurses also quoted of strategic timing in their disclosure, deliberately avoiding late-night hours to prevent exacerbating the family’s emotional vulnerability. “I usually avoid announcing a death after midnight. I wait until the morning and deliver the news at the earliest opportunity… If the patient passed away at midnight, we inform them the next morning and clarify that the death occurred the previous night.” (P.18).

Conveying the bitter truth

The core act of delivering the news was described as “Conveying the Bitter Truth.” Nurses strove to be honest and direct but were careful to avoid being harsh or giving false hope:” The important principle here is to be honest and tell the truth. Lying makes everything worse”. (P.2). A key strategy was “conveying the clinical truth”. Nurses translated complex medical jargon into simple, understandable terms.

Moreover, they emphasized the importance of being truthful: “I try to talk to the family honestly” (P.16). At the same time, they were adamant about “Avoiding false hope,” a common but harmful practice they observed in some inexperienced colleagues. As one nurse warned, “You shouldn’t give false or imaginary hope to the family in a very ambiguous patient situation” (P.17).

To ensure understanding, some nurses reported approach of facilitating intra-family communication. They would first explain the situation to this key person, who could then help prepare the rest of the family and act as a liaison. One participant explained, “I invite the calmest and oldest family member… and ask if they can announce the news to the others” (P.12).

Providing solace

In delivering the news, the nurses’ focus shifted to providing emotional support to help the family manage their distress. This involved communicating with empathetic language and non-offensive. They used phrases like “Unfortunately, despite our best efforts…” and avoided euphemisms like “the patient is at peace,” which they felt could be dismissive. In using non-offensive or sensitive language one nurse stated: “I make an effort to use words that clearly convey the meaning without being blunt or hurtful… I choose terms that help them understand their loved one’s condition is worsening, while ensuring they aren’t emotionally wounded.” (P.16).

Nurses also described a heightened awareness of their own demeanor, speaking in a calm tone and maintaining a humble, non-judgmental posture. A critical and unique component of this phase was offering professional reassurance. Nurses consistently reported reassuring families that the medical team had done everything possible; a strategy aimed at alleviating guilt and preventing blame. One nurse described this as a key to their approach: “I reassure them that all the specialists and the best possible care were available for their loved one… I try to calm them by saying we did everything we could” (P.13). In cases of patient death, they would often frame it as a “good death,” explaining that the patient was no longer suffering. “When the patient had passed away, I told the family: ‘Unfortunately, they were in great pain… Now they’ve been relieved of that suffering, torment, and hardship.’” (P.4).

Discussion

This study is among the first to systematically develop and validate a family-centered instrument for assessing nurses’ skills in delivering bad news to families of patients at the EOL in a non-Western, Islamic cultural context. Our findings reveal that Iranian nurses do not merely “break” bad news; they orchestrate a compassionate, three-phase process—Preparation, Conveying the Bitter Truth, and Consoling—that aligns with both professional ethics and cultural expectations.

The theme of “preparedness” showed that the process of conveying bad news begins in the nurse’s mind before any verbal interaction; this preparation is not only technical, but also an internal moral obligation to face suffering. In this context, Zolkefli stated in their study that telling the truth by a nurse who is not mentally prepared can negatively affect the course of the patient’s illness and the type of communication between the patient and the family with the doctor or other members of the treatment team [19]. Also, another study stated that nurses at this stage seek to gain confidence in their ability to remain calm while conveying the truth; the lack of this internal preparation not only negatively affects the quality of communication, but also increases the serious risk of psychological damage caused by chronic stress [20]. In line with this, Serafin et al., stated in their study that the existing literature on EOL care often focuses on teaching communication models, while it is recommended that educational focus be directed towards developing psychological resilience and emotional regulation tools for nurses to transform this readiness into a sustainable skill and reduce reliance on mere instinctive reactions [21]. Also, Laranjeira et al., believed that true readiness must begin within the nurse, not from instructions. Their view was that models such as SPIKES and PEWTER focused on the patient and family, and the role of the nurse’s situation was given less attention [22]. Ultimately, readiness is “self-care” before caring for others—an act that enables the nurse to remain both human and professional in the moment of telling the truth [20].

The Conveying the Bitter Truth phase was marked by truthful yet non-harmful communication—what one nurse termed “telling without shattering.” Nurses avoided medical jargon and catastrophic language, instead using metaphors like “the body is too tired” or “no longer in pain.” Crucially, they refrained from false hope while preserving dignity—a balance that mirrors Pergert and Lützén’s concept of “ethical truthfulness” [12]. This contrasts sharply with studies in individualistic cultures where directness is prioritized, even at the cost of emotional distress. In this regard, Aznar stated that in the conveying the bitter truth, the principle of veracity is in conflict with the principle of beneficence. Nurses often use strategies to soften the language, not to hide the truth, but to make it digestible in the moment. These strategies, which are sometimes interpreted in the medical literature as excessive softening, are in fact a conscious attempt to manage the shock process and protect the family’s cognitive capacity to absorb subsequent information [23]. Rezaei et al., also believes that these finding challenges broader ethical debates about who, when, and how should deliver bad news and suggests that the nurse’s role in this context is being upgraded from an executor of medical instructions to a “suffering translator” who has a heavy responsibility in regulating the speed and depth of information transmission [24]. Contrary to these findings, another study emphasized that the nature of truth transmission is complex, multifaceted, and difficult. Therefore, completely delegating this responsibility to nurses can not only increase their workload, but also lead to burnout and mental exhaustion [25]. In addition, one study suggested that appropriate clinical guidelines could be used in this area. These formal protocols such as SPIKES and PEWTER should focus on providing flexible and understandable guidance rather than rigid instructions [15].

In this study, the theme of “Providing Solace” was identified as the culmination of nurses’ experiences in the process of delivering bad news. This theme suggests that the nurse’s duty does not end after delivering the message of death, but rather enters a stage that requires the highest level of human presence, emotional attention, and moral forbearance. Nurses at this stage see themselves not simply as caring professionals but as witnesses to human suffering who must find and convey a sense of calm and acceptance amidst the turmoil of emotions. From a theoretical perspective, this finding closely resembles the foundations of Jean Watson’s Theory of Human Caring, which considers “consolation” to be a moral and spiritual act, not just an emotional behavior. According to this view, human contact, empathy, supportive silence, and even the nurse’s silent but conscious presence are themselves considered a type of “therapeutic intervention.” [26]. In this regard, Tornøe et al. stated in their study that consolation is not limited to the family, but is also a restorative experience for the nurse herself. Nurses refer to it as a “moment of meaning recovery”; a moment when, by being sincerely present in the pain of another, they feel that their work is still valuable, even if the patient is not saved [27]. In another study, Pereira et al., emphasized that from the perspective of the psychology of care, this is an example of “Post-Traumatic Growth”—a phenomenon in which facing death strengthens the sense of meaning in life in healthcare providers. In fact, consolation is not only a response to the suffering of others, but also a mechanism to prevent empathy burnout because it allows the nurse to redefine her role in a more human and meaningful way [28]. In another study, Osundina et al., believed that the experience of consolation, in addition to contributing to the professional growth of the nurse, has a high psychological cost for the nurse. As several participants noted, the moments after the news is given are the “hardest part” of a nurse’s job, as she must remain a source of comfort in the midst of the grief of others. This is described in the scientific literature as moral distress, where the nurse is faced with conflicting emotions, empathy fatigue, and at the same time a commitment to support [29]. Finally, it can be said that “consolation” is the last part of the ethical cycle of care in the face of death: the stage in which the nurse not only sees the suffering, but also accompanies it, and with her human presence helps the family bridge the gap between despair and acceptance. This finding suggests that in clinical settings, the nurse is the comforter of the family’s suffering—something that no formal guideline can replace [30].

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of the study was that it focused on nurses’ experiences of delivering bad news, thus addressing an important but under-researched dimension of the health system. As a result, a three-step communication process was identified for application in non-Western and Muslim societies. Also, a limitation of the study was the individual, contextual, and environmental factors specific to each clinical unit on nurses’ experiences, which may have affected the uniformity of the data.

Conclusion

The results of this research showed that Iranian nurses employ a structured, culturally embedded three-phase approach to bad news delivery that integrates truthfulness with compassionate, family-centered care. These findings offer a foundation for developing culturally appropriate communication guidelines and training programs in EOL care settings in Iran and comparable cultural contexts.
