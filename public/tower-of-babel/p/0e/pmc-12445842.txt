Linezolid-related Optic Nerve Disorders: Insight From a Pharmacovigilance Analysis of the US FDA Adverse Event Reporting System
Authors: Yuki Nakano, Motoyasu Miyazaki, Arisa Fukuyama, Hirotsugu Hasuwa, Osamu Imakyure
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLinezolid is a crucial oxazolidinone used to treat multidrug-resistant gram-positive infections; however, it is associated with optic neuropathy and other adverse events. Data regarding the usage and toxicity features of linezolid and those of tedizolid, a related agent with a potentially improved safety profile, are limited. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the signals and characterize the clinical features of optic nerve disorders related to oxazolidinones using the Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database.MethodsThis retrospective pharmacovigilance study examined FAERS data from July 2014 to April 2024. Disproportionality analyses (reporting odds ratio [ROR] and information component [IC]) were conducted to detect signals of oxazolidinone-related optic nerve disorders. Firth-type penalized logistic regression was used to further characterize the features of optic nerve disorders by comparing them with other adverse events (AEs).ResultsIn total, 15 350 730 reports were retrieved from the FAERS database, from which signals (defined as ROR > 1 and IC > 0) were detected only for linezolid, consistent with sensitivity analyses. Compared with other linezolid-related AEs, linezolid-related optic nerve disorders were characterized by younger age (<60 years; adjusted odds ratio [OR]: 6.86, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.36–26.51, P < .001) and high cumulative doses (≥720 mg/kg; adjusted OR: 7.96, 95% CI: 2.93–20.62, P < .001).ConclusionsAmong oxazolidinones, a signal for optic nerve disorders was detected exclusively for linezolid. Clinicians should ensure regular ophthalmologic monitoring during linezolid treatment, particularly in younger patients and those receiving high cumulative doses.Keywords: FAERS, linezolid, optic nerve disorder, pharmacovigilance

Linezolid is an oxazolidinone antibiotic agent that is crucial for the treatment of multidrug-resistant gram-positive bacterial infections; however, its use is associated with adverse events (AEs), such as hematologic toxicity, peripheral neuropathy, and optic nerve disorders [1]. Linezolid-related optic nerve disorders can manifest as decreased visual acuity, alterations in color vision, and defects in visual field [2]. Conversely, clinical trials and limited real-world data indicate that tedizolid, another oxazolidinone, exhibits a more favorable safety profile and tolerability than linezolid during long-term administration [3, 4]. These differences warrant continued surveillance and comparative analyses of the safety profiles of these oxazolidinones.

The clinical characteristics and time course of oxazolidinone-related optic nerve disorders remain incompletely characterized. Although prolonged exposure is generally considered a risk factor, the latency period before symptom onset varies substantially among the reported cases [5]. Moreover, the relatively low incidence of this AE has hindered the establishment of a clear clinical profile of affected patients. The current understanding of this condition remains incomplete because of the limited number of reported cases and the lack of systematic studies designed to comprehensively investigate these factors.

Hence, a pharmacovigilance approach using the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database may provide valuable insights to further characterize oxazolidinone-related optic nerve disorders. The FAERS is a large post-marketing surveillance system that collects reports of drug-associated AEs and has been widely used to detect rare or previously unrecognized safety signals [6].

This study aimed to investigate the signals of optic nerve disorders within the oxazolidinone-class drug and describe the clinical presentations of relevant reported cases. By analyzing spontaneous reports from the FAERS database, this study attempts to improve the current understanding of this AE and support safer clinical decision-making.

METHODS

Study Design

Optic nerve disorders related to oxazolidinone-class drugs were assessed through a disproportionality analysis using the FAERS database, and then, the clinical characteristics of these patients were explored. The FAERS analysis was conducted according to the REporting of A Disproportionality analysis for drug Safety signal detection using individual case safety reports in the PharmacoVigilance (READUS-PV) statement [7]. The FAERS data are anonymized, and this retrospective observational study was exempt from informed consent and conducted according to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data Source

The FAERS database, covering the period from July 2014 to April 2024, was downloaded from the US FDA in July 2024 (https://www.fda.gov/). July 2014 was selected as the start date because generic names became consistently available in the DRUG file from that point onward, enabling accurate and reproducible identification of drugs across reports. The FAERS database consists of seven files: DEMO, DRUG, REAC, OUTC, RPSR, INDI, and THER. These files names are standard FAERS dataset designations rather than abbreviations. DEMO provides basic patient information, such as sex, age, body weight, and AE occurrence date. DRUG contains details on administered drugs, including names and dosages. REAC specifies the reported AEs. OUTC records case outcomes, while RPSR indicates the source of AE information, such as the reporter. INDI describes the indications for drug use. Finally, THER specifies the start and end dates of drug administration.

Target Drugs and Definition of Outcomes

The oxazolidinones linezolid and tedizolid were targeted in this study. To account for potential variations in FAERS database entries caused by salt forms, we devised a method to extract all records based on generic names, irrespective of whether a salt form was indicated. Oral and intravenous formulations of oxazolidinone-class drugs were targeted because of their extremely favorable bioavailability (91%–100%) [8, 9]. Outcomes were defined based on the MedDRA version 27.0 as “Optic nerve disorders” (standardized MedDRA query [SMQ], narrow definition: 20000148) (Supplementary Table 1).

Data Curation

Each report in the FAERS database has a “Primary_id,” and each case is identified by a “case_id.” Because this study required a consistent follow-up of each case, we used the “case_id” term to merge the DEMO, DRUG, REAC, OUTC, INDI, and THER files and constructed an analytical crude dataset in compliance with FDA guidelines by focusing on the most recent reports and excluding duplicate reports from the same patient. The complete dataset was constructed by excluding cases with missing sex and age data; records aged >120 years were considered entry errors and were excluded [10]. Subsequently, we conducted analyses on oxazolidinone agents for which safety signals were detected in the disproportionality analysis. Specifically, we extracted AE reports related to these agents and constructed datasets to compare cases of optic nerve disorders with other AE cases related to the same drug. These datasets were then used to analyze the time to onset (TTO) and clinical characteristics of each reported case.

Statistical Analysis

The disproportionality analysis was conducted using a 2 × 2 contingency table to identify the AE signals associated with the oxazolidinone-class drug. A positive signal was defined as having all of the following items: a reporting odds ratio (ROR) with a lower limit of its 95% confidence interval (CI) > 1 and an information component (IC) by the Bayesian confidence propagation neural network with a lower limit of its 95% CI >0 [11, 12]. Details of the 2 × 2 contingency structure used in the disproportionality analysis, along with the ROR and IC formulas, are provided in Supplementary Table 2. The adjusted ROR was obtained by multivariate logistic analysis using age and sex as factors. Because a small number of target AEs may result in an inflated ROR and an increased risk of false positives, we also applied IC, which uses a statistical shrinkage transformation, to evaluate the signal. Thus, we assessed the signals using multiple metrics, including ROR and IC. To explore temporal trends in signals, we calculated the ROR and IC for each year based on the annual aggregated FAERS data. Furthermore, we performed a sensitivity analysis using the “primary suspected” term exclusively in the role code of the drug to increase the certainty of the target drug signal in optic nerve disorders.

Firth-type penalized logistic regression was used to compare linezolid-related optic nerve disorders with other linezolid-related AEs. This statistical method is useful in cases where the response variable has a low incidence and complete or quasi-complete separation occurs [14]. The variables incorporated into the multivariate model—sex, age, and cumulative dose per body weight—were selected based on their clinical relevance to optic nerve disorders and availability in the FAERS database. These three variables were entered into the model using a forced-entry approach, and the interaction term between age and cumulative dose per body weight was also included in the model to analyze whether the effect of dose varied by age group. Age was dichotomized using 60 years as a threshold, reflecting the increased risk of age-related ocular conditions above this threshold [15]. Cumulative dose was defined as the total dose per body weight (mg/kg) and calculated as follows: daily dose (mg/day) multiplied by the number of days from the start of linezolid administration to the onset of AEs. If the onset occurred after treatment had ended, the number of days was calculated up to the end of dosing. Subsequently, the resulting total dose was divided by the body weight (kg) reported in the case. A threshold of 720 mg/kg was used to classify high cumulative exposure, which was derived from the following clinical consideration: the linezolid package insert warns of optic nerve disorders associated with treatment durations of ≥1 month. Assuming a standard adult dose of 1200 mg/day and a body weight of 50 kg, this corresponds to a cumulative dose of 36 000 mg or 720 mg/kg [16].

Regarding basic statistical analyses, continuous variables were summarized using medians (interquartile range [IQR]), and between-group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test. The 95% CIs for proportions were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson method [17]. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Data analysis was conducted using the R software (version 4.4.2 R Core Team 2024; Vienna, Austria; https://www.r-project.org/) via RStudio.

RESULTS

Disproportionality Analysis of Optic Nerve Disorders Related to Oxazolidinone-Class Drugs

After excluding duplicates from 15 350 730 AE reports retrieved from the FAERS database, 13 211 265 and 7 271 058 cases were included in the crude and complete datasets, respectively (Figure 1). Disproportionality analysis detected a signal of IC for optic nerve disorders for linezolid in the crude and complete datasets, whereas tedizolid did not meet the criteria for a signal of IC (Table 1). Similarly, the sensitivity analysis detected a signal for linezolid and none for tedizolid (Supplementary Table 3). Subsequently, we evaluated the ROR and IC time series for oxazolidinone-related optic nerve disorders and found that only linezolid consistently met the signal criteria throughout the study period and demonstrated an increase in 2018 (Figure 2); the sensitivity analysis yielded similar findings (Supplementary Figure 1).

Clinical Characteristics of Linezolid-related Optic Nerve Disorders

Linezolid-related optic nerve disorders were characterized in comparison with other linezolid-related AEs for which a TTO dataset was available from the complete dataset (Supplementary Figure 3). Linezolid-related optic nerve disorders were reported in a younger patient population (age: 50.0 years [IQR: 33.0, 60.0] vs 65.0 years [IQR: 49.0, 76.0], P < .001) than other linezolid-related AEs. Moreover, a higher proportion of these optic nerve disorder cases was associated with linezolid use against Mycobacterium tuberculosis (54.17% vs 9.79%, P < .001), and the incidence of disability as an outcome was significantly greater (10.42% vs 2.15%, P = .004) (Table 2). Furthermore, optic neuritis was the most frequently reported manifestation of linezolid-related optic nerve disorders. In the TTO analysis, the occurrence of linezolid-related optic nerve disorders was significantly delayed compared with that of other linezolid-related AEs (median, 64.0 days [95% CI: 29.0, 122.0] vs median, 6.0 days [95% CI: 6.0, 7.0]; P < .001; Figure 4). To further characterize linezolid-related optic nerve disorders, we performed an analysis on a subset of the TTO dataset with available information on the cumulative dose (Supplementary Figure 3). The Firth-type penalized multiple logistic regression showed that linezolid-related optic nerve disorders were characterized by a younger patient age (<60 years; aOR: 6.86 [95%: CI: 2.36, 26.51], P < .001) and high cumulative dose (≥720 mg/kg; aOR: 7.96 [95%: CI: 2.93, 20.62], P < .001) (Table 3). Finally, we observed no interaction between age <60 years and the high cumulative dose (≥720 mg/kg), suggesting that each parameter acted as an independent factor (interaction term, P = .092; Supplementary Table 4; Supplementary Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

Our analysis of the FAERS database identified a signal for optic nerve disorders specifically for linezolid among other oxazolidinone-class drugs. Moreover, linezolid-related optic nerve disorders were characterized by age <60 years and a high cumulative dose compared with other linezolid-related AEs. These findings emphasize the importance of considering the risk of optic nerve disorders when linezolid is used for treatment and the need for careful monitoring, especially at high cumulative doses or among younger patients. In the absence of formal guidelines, clinicians may consider ophthalmologic monitoring strategies similar to those established for ethambutol-related optic neuropathy [18].

To reduce false-positive signals and improve the robustness of the findings, we evaluated optic nerve disorders among oxazolidinone-class drugs using ROR, a classical method, and IC, a Bayesian approach, combined with sensitivity analyses. Consequently, we detected signals for optic nerve disorders among the oxazolidinone-class drugs only for linezolid in the disproportionality analysis of the FAERS database. Optic nerve disorders associated with oxazolidinones are considered dose-dependent AEs, probably resulting from the inhibition of mitochondrial protein synthesis [19]. Although the literature describes case reports of optic nerve disorders associated with tedizolid administration [20], tedizolid is considered well-tolerated and safe [4]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that both linezolid and tedizolid inhibit mitochondrial protein synthesis, with IC50 values of 4.81 and 0.096 mg/L, respectively [21]. In one study, after the oral administration of linezolid 600 mg twice daily, the Cmax and Cmin were 21.2 and 6.15 mg/L, respectively [16]. Considering a 31% plasma protein binding of linezolid [16], this yields free Cₘₐₓ/IC50 and Cₘᵢₙ/IC50 ratios of 3.03 and 0.88, respectively. In contrast, oral administration of tedizolid 200 mg once daily yields Cmax and Cmin values of 1.8 and 0.41 mg/L, respectively [22]. Considering an approximately 80% plasma protein binding of tedizolid [23], the resulting free Cmax/IC50 and Cmin/IC50 ratios are 3.75 and 0.85, respectively. Although the free Cmax/IC50 ratios for tedizolid and linezolid are comparable, the twice daily dosing and higher overall exposure associated with linezolid suggest a longer duration of mitochondrial burden. Altogether, these pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic differences may help explain the disproportionately higher risk of optic nerve toxicity associated with linezolid.

Our time-trend analysis of linezolid-related optic nerve disorders revealed an upward trend in ROR and IC from 2018, coinciding with the recommendation of the World Health Organization for its use in the treatment of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) [24]. Compared with staphylococcal infections for which linezolid is typically prescribed at standard doses, MDR-TB treatment using this antibiotic tends to involve lower doses for longer periods [25]. Moreover, a previous study suggested an association between linezolid-related optic neuropathy and prolonged use [26]. Consequently, the increased prevalence of optic nerve disorders in recent years may reflect the broader and prolonged use of linezolid in the treatment of MDR-TB. Although this observation may be a manifestation of the “weber effect” [27], the increase in indications related to long-term linezolid use in certain patient populations could have resulted in an increase in the incidence of reported AEs.

Linezolid-related optic nerve disorders were frequently reported as a treatment indication in patients with tuberculosis. These cases showed a higher proportion of functional visual impairment and a longer TTO than other linezolid-related AEs. The high prevalence of tuberculosis in cases with linezolid-related optic nerve disorders confirms the increasing trend of signal detection in optic nerve disorders observed since 2018. Linezolid-related optic nerve disorders were significantly more likely to result in disability than other linezolid-related AEs; however, the reversibility of such functional impairment remains unclear [26, 28]. Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) are particularly susceptible to mitochondrial dysfunction in the central nervous system (CNS) [29]. Moreover, the fact that the optic nerve, which is classified as a component of the CNS, possesses an extremely limited capacity for axonal regeneration supports the possibility that damage resulting from linezolid use is irreversible [30]. The median TTO of linezolid-related optic nerve disorders was 2 months. Although the TTO reported in previous case studies varied across individual cases, the overall tendency toward delayed onset on a monthly timescale is consistent with the published literature [28, 31].

To further characterize linezolid-related optic nerve disorders, we conducted a Firth-type penalized multivariate logistic regression that suggested that younger age and higher cumulative dose were distinctive features compared with other linezolid-related AEs. The identification of higher cumulative dose as a factor is consistent with the previous reports indicating that treatment duration and total dose affect the occurrence of linezolid-related AEs [32, 33]. The RGCs, which are in a state of high metabolic activity because of their continuous generation of action potentials and transmission of visual signals, may be particularly susceptible to mitochondrial dysfunction. This susceptibility may contribute to the onset of optic nerve disorders in younger individuals [29]. Alternatively, younger patients may have lower thresholds of tolerance to changes in visual acuity and field, making them more likely to identify and report subtle visual disturbances.

The findings of this study should be interpreted considering several limitations. First, the FAERS database is based on spontaneous reporting, which may include incomplete information and duplicate cases originating from multiple sources. Furthermore, because the total number of patients exposed to oxazolidinone antimicrobials remains unknown, calculating the incidence rates is challenging, and causality cannot be established. Second, the observed relationship between linezolid use and optic nerve disorders may have been influenced by indication and channeling bias. This issue is particularly relevant in the treatment of MDR-TB, for which long-term linezolid use is recommended. Patients with MDR-TB often require prolonged treatment and may be exposed to higher cumulative doses of linezolid. Moreover, before or during the use of ethambutol—a neurotoxic agent frequently used in tuberculosis treatment—could not be controlled for in the FAERS database, potentially confounding the observed signal. These factors may have resulted in a disproportionate number of optic nerve disorder reports among patients receiving linezolid, irrespective of its intrinsic neurotoxicity. Third, although no signal for tedizolid-related optic nerve disorders was detected in this study, its limited use in clinical practice, particularly the lack of widespread use for indications such as tuberculosis, may have contributed to the absence of reported cases. Considering that tedizolid results in a lower systemic exposure than linezolid, the risk may in fact be lower; however, as its use expands and long-term treatment cases accumulate, potential risks of optic neuropathy may emerge over time. This limitation probably reflects an inherent sampling bias due to lower utilization of tedizolid in clinical settings, particularly the absence of use in long-term treatments such as that for MDR-TB. Fourth, although the cumulative dose emerged as a key characteristic, linezolid plasma concentrations can vary substantially based on renal function. Therefore, the actual drug exposure may not precisely correspond to the administered dose. In the FAERS database, information on comorbid conditions such as renal impairment was unavailable for the reported cases, which limited our ability to consider such factors in the analysis. This finding warrants further confirmatory studies including such factors. Fifth, when characterizing linezolid-related optic nerve disorders, we compared them with other linezolid-related AEs; however, analyses of clinical background, TTO, and cumulative dose were constrained by substantial missing data and could only be performed in a limited number of cases. Nonetheless, our findings should be considered informative in clinical practice because optic nerve disorders are not frequently reported.

CONCLUSION

Among the oxazolidinones, a signal for optic nerve disorders was detected exclusively for linezolid, and younger age (<60 years) or high cumulative dosage (≥720 mg/kg) may be contributing factors. Clinicians should be aware of these characteristics and consider implementing regular ophthalmologic monitoring during prolonged linezolid therapy, including referral to specialists. Incorporating practical monitoring strategies—such as baseline and periodic visual acuity assessments, red–green color discrimination testing (eg, Ishihara plates), and fundoscopic examinations—may help detect optic nerve disorders at an earlier stage and allow timely intervention.
