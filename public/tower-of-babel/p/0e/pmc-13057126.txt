Application of Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS) technology in malignant tumor diagnosis
Authors: Jie Chen, Pengren Luo, Shiqiang Wang, Haifeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLaser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS) represents a rapid analytical technique grounded in atomic emission spectroscopy, which has revealed considerable diagnostic capabilities within the biomedical sector in recent years. This review presents a comprehensive overview of the underlying principles, methodological advancements, clinical applications, and existing challenges related to LIBS technology in the detection of malignant tumors. By examining the disparities in elemental composition between tissue samples, LIBS proficiently distinguishes normal tissue from cancerous tissue, thereby providing valuable insights for the early detection of tumors, margin delineation, and treatment monitoring. The article further investigates the potential of amalgamating LIBS with other diagnostic modalities and deliberates on the future trajectories of this technology in translational medicine.Keywords: Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy, Malignant tumor diagnosis, Elemental analysis, Spectral imaging, Molecular pathology

Introduction

Timely and precise diagnosis of malignant tumors is essential for enhancing patient survival rates. Conventional pathological methods predominantly rely on morphological assessments, which can be time-intensive and subjective, resulting in possible misdiagnoses. In contrast, Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS) presents an innovative methodology by identifying elemental spectral lines within tissues, thus delivering quantitative biochemical data that addresses the shortcomings of traditional diagnostic approaches. LIBS is noted for its straightforward sample preparation, rapid detection capabilities (within milliseconds), and the capacity to conduct simultaneous multi-element analyses. These advantages have fostered noteworthy advancements in the diagnosis of various cancers, such as gastric cancer, breast cancer, and gliomas in recent years. This review systematically examines the technical principles of LIBS, optimization strategies for experimental design, clinical validation data, and the intrinsic limitations of the technology, thereby establishing a theoretical groundwork for its clinical application [1, 2].

One of the principal advantages of LIBS lies in its capability to simultaneously analyze multiple elements, which is vital in tumor diagnostics where alterations in elemental composition may signify pathological changes. For instance, research indicates that LIBS can effectively differentiate between healthy and cancerous tissues by assessing the elemental distribution and concentration ratios of crucial electrolytes such as calcium, magnesium, sodium, and potassium. Furthermore, the incorporation of advanced data processing methodologies, including machine learning algorithms, has significantly augmented the classification precision of tumor cells based on LIBS spectral data. This underscores the potential of LIBS not only as a diagnostic instrument but also as a means for real-time monitoring of treatment efficacy [3, 4].

Notwithstanding its promising applications, the clinical integration of LIBS confronts several hurdles. Factors such as sample desiccation and the coffee-ring effect can impact the stability of spectral signals, potentially causing detection inaccuracies. To counteract these challenges, innovative approaches like ordered microarray silicon substrates (OMSS) have been suggested to bolster spectral stability and diminish variability in LIBS measurements. Moreover, the necessity for robust calibration protocols and the standardization of procedures remain pivotal areas for future investigation. Addressing these limitations will be crucial for the wider clinical adoption of LIBS technology in oncology [5]. In conclusion, LIBS signifies a groundbreaking method within the diagnostic framework for malignant tumors, possessing the capacity to bolster early detection efforts and enhance patient prognoses. By utilizing its swift and quantitative analytical capabilities, LIBS can serve as a complementary technique to conventional histopathological approaches, thereby providing a more thorough understanding of tumor biology. Continuous research and technological developments are anticipated to refine the application of LIBS, facilitating its incorporation into standard clinical practice for cancer diagnosis and management [6, 7].

Principles of LIBS technology and fundamentals of tumor diagnosis

Mechanism of plasma generation

Figure 1 shows the schematic of the LIBS imaging instrument, which is mainly composed of laser generating, signal collecting, and imaging device. First, the laser source emits a laser beam. After passing through components like the half - wave plate, polarizer, and being reflected by mirrors, the laser is focused onto the sample placed on the 3D sample stage via lenses, ablating the sample to generate plasma.The light emitted by the plasma is then collected by lenses, transmitted through the fiber coupler, and filtered by another polarizer and half - wave plate. It enters the spectrometer, which disperses the light into spectra.The spectrometer sends spectral data to the computer, and with the help of processing software, the computer identifies elements in the sample based on spectral characteristics. The timing controller coordinates the sequence of laser emission and signal detection, enabling accurate and sequential LIBS analysis.Plasma formation in LIBS commences with high-energy laser pulses that engage with biological tissues, resulting in the creation of a high-temperature plasma capable of reaching temperatures up to 10,000 K. This intense thermal energy induces the ablation of the targeted material, leading to the ionization of atoms and molecules within the tissue. As these excited atoms revert to their ground state, they emit photons at distinctive wavelengths. This emission plays a pivotal role in determining the elemental makeup of the tissue, as the emitted light can be captured and analyzed to discern the specific elements present. The elevated temperatures achieved during this procedure not only aid in tissue breakdown but also amplify the sensitivity and specificity of elemental analysis, rendering LIBS an invaluable instrument for the diagnosis of malignant tumors. The rapid generation of plasma and the consequent light emission facilitate real-time analysis, which proves particularly advantageous in clinical environments where prompt decision-making is essential [3].

Analysis of elemental characteristic spectra

In the realm of malignant tumors, the investigation of elemental spectra derived from LIBS uncovers considerable modifications in the concentration of diverse elements when contrasted with normal tissues. For example, research indicates a substantial increase in the sodium to potassium (Na/K) ratio in malignant tissues, with breast cancer instances exhibiting elevations of 2 to 3 times compared to healthy tissues. Furthermore, alterations in the concentrations of trace elements such as iron (Fe), copper (Cu), and zinc (Zn) have been associated with metabolic anomalies linked to tumor growth and progression. These elemental variations not only act as biomarkers for cancer diagnosis but also offer insights into the fundamental pathophysiological processes. By scrutinizing the characteristic spectral lines of these elements, healthcare professionals can differentiate between malignant and benign tissues, thereby aiding in the early detection and precise diagnosis of cancers [8].

Spectral data acquisition system

A standard LIBS system typically comprises a Neodymium-doped Yttrium Aluminum Garnet (Nd: YAG) laser operating at a wavelength of 1064 nm, a medium-resolution grating spectrometer, and an intensified charge-coupled device (ICCD) detector. This configuration facilitates the collection of high-quality spectral data with a spatial resolution that may reach up to 50 μm, rendering it appropriate for micro-area analysis of tissues. The precision offered by this system is vital for distinguishing between normal and pathological tissue, especially in heterogeneous tumor environments where subtle variations in elemental composition can signify malignancy. The capability to attain such high spatial resolution enables the early identification of cancerous cells, thus promoting timely intervention. Additionally, advancements in spectral data processing algorithms enhance the evaluation of the gathered data, improving overall analysis efficiency. The overall diagnostic accuracy of LIBS in clinical applications has been demonstrated to be high, supporting its potential as a reliable diagnostic tool [9].

Application of LIBS in differential diagnosis of solid tumors

Gastric cancer tissue typing

LIBS has emerged as a promising method for classifying gastric cancer tissues, especially in differentiating between the intestinal and diffuse types of gastric adenocarcinoma. Recent investigations have shown that intestinal-type gastric cancer is characterized by a significant elevation in the calcium-to-magnesium (Ca/Mg) ratio, with statistical significance observed at p < 0.01. This increase in the Ca/Mg ratio reflects the metabolic changes associated with this subtype of gastric cancer, potentially indicating variations in tumor microenvironment and cellular metabolism. In contrast, diffuse-type gastric cancer exhibits a notable rise in chlorine (Cl) signal intensity, which is statistically significant and suggests different biochemical pathways or tumor behaviors compared to the intestinal type. LIBS’s capability to deliver swift, real-time elemental analysis of gastric tissues not only facilitates accurate typing but also enhances the understanding of the underlying pathophysiological changes linked to various gastric cancer subtypes. This advancement is vital for customizing treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes, as the therapeutic approach may vary considerably based on the tumor’s histological classification [10].

Margin definition in breast cancer

In the context of breast cancer surgery, the application of LIBS has demonstrated considerable promise in accurately defining tumor margins during surgical procedures, particularly in breast-conserving surgeries. By examining the potassium-to-sodium (K/Na) ratio gradient, surgeons can effectively delineate the tumor infiltration zone, achieving an impressive accuracy rate of 89.7%. The precise identification of tumor margins is crucial, as incomplete resection may lead to local recurrence and require further treatments. The use of LIBS-guided techniques in surgical environments has been illustrated through various case studies, where it has been utilized to inform intraoperative decisions during breast-conserving surgeries. By delivering real-time feedback on the elemental composition of the tissue, LIBS not only enhances the surgeon’s capability to achieve clear margins but also contributes to improved cosmetic outcomes for patients. The potential of LIBS to facilitate margin assessment signifies a substantial advancement in surgical oncology, introducing a novel approach to enhance the efficacy of breast cancer treatments [11].

Grading diagnosis of gliomas

The utilization of LIBS in glioma grading has gained momentum due to its capacity to provide intricate elemental analysis that correlates with tumor grade. Research has indicated that there exists a statistically significant difference in the zinc-to-copper (Zn/Cu) ratio between World Health Organization (WHO) Grade II and Grade IV gliomas, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.82, emphasizing its diagnostic potential. This elemental variation aids in distinguishing between grades while also enhancing the understanding of the biochemical milieu of gliomas [12]. Moreover, the integration of LIBS data with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) allows for three-dimensional visualization of elemental distribution within tumors, providing a comprehensive perspective on tumor heterogeneity and potentially guiding therapeutic interventions. The ability to non-invasively evaluate glioma grades through elemental composition underscores the transformative impact of LIBS in neuro-oncology, paving the way for tailored treatment strategies that align with the specific characteristics of glioma patients.

Innovations in LIBS technology in liquid biopsy

Blood sample analysis

The utilization of LIBS for the examination of blood samples has paved the way for novel colorectal cancer screening methods, particularly through the evaluation of the serum calcium to phosphorus (Ca/P) ratio. Recent investigations have highlighted that this ratio can serve as a valuable biomarker for colorectal cancer, exhibiting a sensitivity of 76% in differentiating cancer patients from healthy controls. This finding is noteworthy as it indicates that LIBS can facilitate rapid, non-invasive screening alternatives that may potentially improve early detection rates for colorectal cancer, a condition where prompt diagnosis is paramount for enhancing patient prognoses [13]. Moreover, advancements in LIBS technology have led to the development of techniques aimed at augmenting its sensitivity and detection limits, particularly through the incorporation of gold nanoparticles (AuNPs). These nanoparticles have demonstrated a remarkable ability to amplify LIBS signals, thereby enhancing detection limits by as much as 100-fold. This advancement allows for the detection of trace elements and compounds within blood samples that could signify malignancies, thereby broadening the applicability of LIBS in clinical environments [14]. The amalgamation of AuNPs with LIBS not only boosts test sensitivity but also enhances specificity, establishing it as a powerful instrument for liquid biopsy applications. This innovative methodology holds the potential to yield more precise and dependable colorectal cancer screening techniques, ultimately contributing to improved management and therapeutic strategies for patients.

Detection of trace elements in urine

The investigation of LIBS technology extends its reach beyond blood analysis to encompass the detection of trace elements in urine, particularly concerning bladder cancer. Research has indicated a troubling accumulation of cadmium (Cd) and lead (Pb) in the urine of individuals diagnosed with bladder cancer, suggesting a possible association between these heavy metals and cancer pathology. This unusual concentration of trace elements presents an opportunity for LIBS to function as a diagnostic resource by offering a non-invasive method to monitor these markers in patients predisposed to bladder cancer [15]. Additionally, the practicality of employing portable LIBS devices for community screening has been explored, revealing the potential for extensive application in early cancer detection programs. The portability of LIBS technology permits on-site testing, which could significantly improve accessibility for patients across various environments, particularly in underserved areas where conventional diagnostic methods may be less accessible [16]. This capability not only aids in early detection but also fosters public health initiatives by facilitating proactive screening measures. As LIBS technology continues to mature, its significance in liquid biopsy applications for cancer diagnostics is expected to broaden, providing new insights into disease mechanisms and enhancing patient outcomes through timely interventions.

Multimodal joint diagnostic strategies

LIBS-Raman collaborative system

The integration of LIBS with Raman spectroscopy signifies a notable advancement in the diagnostic potential for malignant tumors. This multimodal strategy enables the concurrent acquisition of elemental composition and molecular vibrational data, yielding a thorough analysis of biological samples. For example, the LIBS-Raman system can proficiently evaluate the iron state of hemoglobin and the structure of porphyrin rings, which are essential for distinguishing between various cancer types. In clinical contexts, this collaborative approach enhances diagnostic accuracy and provides a comprehensive understanding of the underlying biological processes associated with malignancies. The integrated system has shown considerable effectiveness, especially in the diagnostics of prostate cancer, where detection accuracy has improved from 85.2% with single-modality Laser-Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS) to an impressive 93.5% utilizing the combined LIBS-Raman technique. This enhancement in diagnostic accuracy is primarily due to the capability of the LIBS-Raman system to capture both elemental and molecular signatures, thus offering a more detailed insight into the biochemical milieu of the tumor. Such progress highlights the promise of multimodal diagnostic approaches in enhancing patient outcomes by enabling early detection and targeted treatment of cancers [17].

LIBS-OCT integrated platform

The fusion of LIBS with Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) establishes a potent integrated platform that amplifies diagnostic abilities for a range of malignancies, especially in accurately defining tumor boundaries in skin melanoma. OCT delivers high-resolution images of tissue microstructures, granting clinicians the ability to visualize the morphological traits of tumors in real-time. When integrated with LIBS, which provides biochemical data regarding the sample, this platform serves as a comprehensive diagnostic instrument. For instance, in clinical scenarios involving melanoma, the LIBS-OCT system has proven to be crucial in precisely delineating tumor margins, a factor vital for ensuring thorough surgical excision and reducing the likelihood of recurrence. By augmenting structural information from OCT with biochemical insights from LIBS, healthcare providers can make more informed decisions concerning treatment protocols. This multimodal strategy not only heightens diagnostic precision but also improves the overall management of patients with skin malignancies, illustrating the potential of combining advanced imaging methodologies to enhance cancer care [18].

Deep learning-assisted spectral analysis

Feature extraction algorithms

The incorporation of deep learning methodologies into spectral analysis has transformed the extraction of features from complex datasets. A notable technique is the integration of Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction followed by classification using Support Vector Machine (SVM), which has exhibited remarkable accuracy rates between 88% and 92% across various studies. PCA proficiently diminishes the dimensionality of spectral data by converting it into a series of uncorrelated variables known as principal components, thereby simplifying the data structure while preserving the most significant variance. This preprocessing phase is essential as it optimizes the efficacy of the subsequent SVM classifier, which is skilled in managing high-dimensional data and can effectively differentiate classes based on the extracted features. Moreover, the application of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) has emerged as a formidable alternative for automatic feature extraction, particularly in recognizing spectral line patterns without relying on manual feature engineering. CNNs are capable of learning hierarchical data representations, capturing intricate patterns that may elude traditional methods. For example, in the realmof tumor tissue classification, CNNs have demonstrated classification accuracies surpassing 97%, significantly outpacing shallow learning techniques. This development indicates that deep learning strategies, particularly CNNs, not only streamline the feature extraction process but also improve the interpretability and accuracy of spectral analysis, thus paving the way for more reliable diagnostic tools in medical contexts [19, 20] (Table 1).

Big data analysis platforms

The creation of comprehensive tumor element databases, which may encompass thousands of clinical sample spectra, represents a vital milestone in harnessing big data analytics within the field of cancer diagnostics. These databases act as extensive repositories of spectral data, which are instrumental in training deep learning models capable of processing substantial volumes of information. Nonetheless, the challenge posed by limited sample sizes in specific clinical situations necessitates the implementation of innovative methodologies like transfer learning, which adeptly tackles the issue of inadequate labeled data. By leveraging pre-trained models derived from larger datasets in comparable domains, researchers can modify these models to accommodate new, smaller datasets, thus bolstering predictive performance without the need for extensive new data collection. This strategy has proven especially advantageous in medical diagnostics, where acquiring large quantities of samples presents logistical and ethical difficulties. Furthermore, the incorporation of sophisticated big data platforms facilitates real-time data processing and analysis, empowering clinicians to make evidence-based decisions grounded in the most up-to-date and comprehensive information available. The synergy of extensive databases and advanced deep learning methodologies not only enhances diagnostic precision but also promotes the establishment of personalized treatment approaches, ultimately improving patient outcomes in oncology [21, 22].

Technical limitations and solutions

Matrix effect interference

The matrix effect poses a formidable obstacle in employing LIBS for the diagnosis of malignant tumors, as it can introduce variability in elemental analysis attributable to the heterogeneous characteristics of biological tissues. A viable strategy to alleviate this challenge is the standard addition method, which consists of incorporating known amounts of analytes into the sample matrix prior to analysis. This technique allows for the correction of matrix effects by compensating for fluctuations in sample composition. Through the implementation of this method, researchers can establish a calibration curve that accurately represents the true concentration of elements within the tissue, thereby enhancing the precision of LIBS measurements. Research has demonstrated that the standard addition approach can effectively address the intricate interactions between analytes and the biological matrix, resulting in more reliable diagnostic outcomes in the context of cancer detection [23].

An additional consideration in mitigating matrix effects in LIBS involves the comparative analysis of signals derived from frozen versus paraffin-embedded tissue sections. Frozen sections are frequently utilized in clinical environments for prompt diagnosis, while paraffin sections are generally employed for comprehensive histopathological investigation. Studies indicate that signal intensity and quality can vary significantly between these two preparation methods, potentially affecting the diagnostic accuracy of LIBS. For example, frozen sections may better preserve certain elemental signals due to diminished chemical alteration during the embedding procedure. Comparative investigations have revealed that frozen sections can yield elevated LIBS signal intensities, which may improve the identification of specific elements associated with malignancies. Recognizing these distinctions is essential for refining sample preparation techniques and ensuring precise LIBS analysis in clinical settings [24].

Clinical translation barriers

The clinical application of LIBS technology for cancer diagnostics is obstructed by the lack of standardized operating procedures (SOPs) that delineate optimal parameters, such as laser energy and sampling depth. In the absence of established guidelines, variability in the implementation of LIBS can result in inconsistent outcomes, thereby complicating the integration of this technology into standard clinical practice. The formulation of comprehensive protocols is thus essential to address these challenges and facilitate the effective use of LIBS in clinical settings. The establishment of comprehensive Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) that tackle these technical parameters is vital for improving the reproducibility and dependability of LIBS measurements. By standardizing operational conditions, healthcare professionals can guarantee that the outcomes are comparable across various studies and clinical environments, thus facilitating the incorporation of LIBS into diagnostic processes for malignant tumors [25].

A notable obstacle hindering the clinical use of LIBS in oncology is the sluggish advancement in acquiring FDA certification, coupled with the intricacies associated with designing multi-center clinical trials. The regulatory framework for innovative diagnostic technologies presents significant challenges, necessitating thorough validation to prove both safety and efficacy. Furthermore, formulating multi-center trials requires a consistent methodology for data collection and analysis, which can be challenging due to the variability in clinical practices among different institutions. Overcoming these hurdles demands collaboration among various stakeholders, including researchers, regulatory agencies, and healthcare practitioners, to create clear pathways for the approval and application of LIBS technology in cancer diagnostics. Initiatives aimed at streamlining the regulatory processes and fostering multi-center collaborations will be essential for progressing the clinical implementation of LIBS and ultimately enhancing patient outcomes [24].

Future development directions

Development of miniaturized devices

The progress in miniaturization technologies has unveiled new opportunities for utilizing LIBS in the diagnosis of malignant tumors. A primary focus lies in developing fiber-coupled LIBS probes for endoscopic evaluations, which can be designed to have a diameter of less than 3 mm. Such compact devices would facilitate real-time, in situ analysis of tumor tissues during endoscopic procedures, offering immediate feedback on elemental composition and potentially improving diagnostic precision. The incorporation of LIBS technology into fiber-optic systems allows for minimally invasive sampling, thus reducing patient discomfort and the risks associated with conventional biopsy techniques. Moreover, the prototype design of smartphone-compatible spectrometers signifies a groundbreaking advancement towards democratizing access to sophisticated diagnostic tools. By capitalizing on the widespread presence of smartphones, these devices can enable extensive screening and monitoring of malignancies, especially in resource-constrained environments. The capability for real-time data analysis and remote consultations via mobile platforms could significantly enhance patient outcomes by facilitating prompt interventions based on LIBS results. Continued research and development in this domain highlight the necessity of creating user-friendly, affordable solutions that align with the growing demand for precision medicine and personalized treatment approaches in oncology [26].

Applications in treatment monitoring

The utilization of LIBS technology extends beyond initial diagnosis to the continuous monitoring of treatment efficacy in malignant tumors. One promising avenue is the observation of elemental alterations following radiotherapy, particularly focusing on elements such as selenium (Se), which has been associated with radiation sensitivity. Studies suggest that tracking the concentration of specific trace elements in tumor tissues could yield insights into the biological response to radiation therapy, thereby aiding clinicians in customizing treatment regimens to meet individual patient requirements. The capability to detect these changes in real-time using LIBS might facilitate more adaptive treatment strategies, minimizing unnecessary radiation exposure and improving therapeutic outcomes. Additionally, real-time monitoring of targeted drug responses through LIBS technology could present significant advantages in managing treatment efficacy. The utilization of LIBS within the realm of metabolism signifies a pioneering application. By examining the elemental makeup of neoplastic tissues during therapeutic interventions, healthcare professionals are able to evaluate the pharmacokinetics associated with targeted treatment modalities, facilitating prompt modifications to dosage protocols tailored to individual metabolic reactions. This methodology not only maximizes therapeutic effectiveness but also reduces the likelihood of adverse effects stemming from excessive treatment. The incorporation of LIBS into standard clinical practice for the assessment of treatment responses represents considerable progress in oncology, highlighting the imperative for ongoing advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic technologies aimed at enhancing patient care [27].

Conclusion

The introduction of LIBS technology signifies a noteworthy progression in the diagnostic domain for malignant tumors, offering a unique perspective through the analysis of elemental fingerprints. This cutting-edge technique allows for the rapid and simultaneous identification of multiple elements, displaying a distinct advantage over conventional diagnostic methodologies. Current research has demonstrated that particular elemental ratios, such as Na/K and Zn/Cu, hold diagnostic significance across a range of tumor types. Additionally, the incorporation of deep learning algorithms has revealed the capability to attain classification accuracies surpassing 90%, emphasizing the potential of LIBS to enhance diagnostic accuracy [28].

Nevertheless, the path toward the broad clinical implementation of LIBS is fraught with challenges. Factors such as Matrix Effect and a lack of adequate standardization present substantial obstacles that must be overcome to guarantee the reliability and reproducibility of outcomes [29]. The intricate nature of biological samples requires thorough consideration of these aspects to prevent misinterpretation of elemental information. Therefore, a balanced approach that reconciles various research viewpoints is essential. This entails not only addressing the technical limitations associated with LIBS but also promoting collaboration among researchers, clinicians, and industry partners to refine the technology and its applications.

Looking to the future, the potential for LIBS to develop into a crucial supplementary tool in pathology is promising, particularly with forthcoming advancements in multimodal integration and device miniaturization. Such progress could enable the application of LIBS in diverse clinical environments, rendering it suitable for routine diagnostic purposes [30]. To expedite this transition, it is vital to prioritize large-scale, prospective clinical studies that can substantiate the effectiveness of LIBS across varied patient demographics. Establishing standardized diagnostic protocols will further bolster the credibility of LIBS as a diagnostic tool, paving the way for its incorporation into clinical routines.

In summary, while the current insights regarding LIBS are encouraging, the pathway ahead necessitates a unified effort to navigate the complexities associated with its application. By harmonizing the diverse perspectives within the research community and addressing existing challenges, LIBS holds the potential to transform the diagnosis of malignant tumors. The next 5–10 years will be pivotal in determining the role of LIBS in clinical oncology, and a proactive approach in research and standardization will be crucial to fully realize its capabilities.
