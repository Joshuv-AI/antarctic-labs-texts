Effects of exogenous melatonin on photosynthesis physiology biochemistry of blue honeysuckle seedlings under saline-alkali stress
Authors: Shuxian Li, Xueying Zhao, Jiacheng Liu, Zongya Xing, Yu Zheng, Junwei Huo, Dong Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSaline-alkali stress severely restricts the growth and cultivation of blue honeysuckle (Lonicera caerulea L.). Exogenous melatonin (MT) regulates plant stress resistance, but its alleviation mechanism in this species remains unclear. This study analyzed the effects of 0, 25, 100, and 200 µmol/L MT on blue honeysuckle seedlings under saline-alkali stress. Results showed 100 µmol/L MT exerted the optimal effect: it mitigated photoinhibition by increasing PSII’s maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm) and optimizing light energy distribution; enhanced antioxidant defense via upregulating SOD, POD, and CAT activities while reducing MDA content; regulated proline and soluble sugar accumulation to maintain osmotic balance; and activated stress response networks by upregulating NAC/WRKY transcription factors and auxin/ABA-related genes, as well as SNAT/ASMT pathway genes for endogenous MT synthesis. Notably, 200 µmol/L MT induced inhibitory effects via oxidative feedback. In conclusion, 100 µmol/L exogenous MT significantly improves blue honeysuckle’s saline-alkali tolerance through synergistic regulation of photosynthetic stability, antioxidant defense, and transcriptional reprogramming, providing a theoretical basis for saline-alkali land cultivation and MT agricultural application.Keywords: Melatonin, Saline-alkali stress, Antioxidant system, Transcriptome, Blue honeysuckle

Introduction

Blue honeysuckle (Lonicera caerulea L.), also known as Haskap, Honeyberry, or Blue Indigo Fruit, is an emerging small berry crop. It possesses therapeutic properties, including reducing glaucoma symptoms, lowering heart attack risk, preventing anemia, treating malaria and gastrointestinal diseases, slowing aging, and enhancing skin elasticity [1]. Historically, it has been used medicinally in countries like China, Japan, and Russia [2]. Blue honeysuckle core feature is super strong cold tolerance (withstanding − 46 °C), along with certain salt tolerance, drought resistance, and pest resistance. At the physiological level, it regulates osmotic balance through the accumulation of soluble sugars, proline, etc., activates antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and POD to eliminate reactive oxygen species, and reduce stress damage; Molecularly, it relies on the ICE-CBF-COR cold response pathway, regulation by MYB/NAC transcription factors, and gene duplication/evolution to strengthen the stress resistance network. Salt-tolerant varieties maintain ion homeostasis via SOS1 proteins, while drought tolerance is achieved by mediating stomatal closure through ABA.

Saline-alkali land is a land type of soil salinization, and soils with an EC (electrical conductivity) greater than 4 mS/cm and an ESP (Percentage of exchangeable sodium for soil-saturated extracts) greater than 15 are saline-alkali soils [3]. The high salt content and alkalinity of the soil in saline-alkali land affect the normal growth of plants and seriously affect agricultural and forestry production [4, 5].

Melatonin (MT) is an indoleamine hormone synthesized from tryptophan. Existing studies have shown that melatonin can enhance the resistance of crops to salt stress, such as sorghum (Sorghum bicolor L.) [6], common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) [7], fodder soybean [8]. In recent years, melatonin has played an important physiological role in crops, especially in the response of crops to adversity stress [9]. Enhances plant stress resistance through multiple conserved mechanisms: activating antioxidant enzyme systems (SOD, CAT) to scavenge ROS [10], regulating hormone signaling pathways (ABA, SA) [11], and upregulating WRKY/NAC transcription factors to activate stress-responsive genes [12]. Existing studies have confirmed MT’s role in alleviating salt stress in sorghum [6], common bean [7], and fodder soybean [8], but its specific mechanism in blue honeysuckle remains unclear.

This study uses blue honeysuckle seedlings as materials to investigate the regulatory effects of exogenous melatonin with different concentrations on the photosynthetic characteristics, physiological and biochemical metabolism, and gene expression of seedlings under salt-alkali stress, clarifying the molecular physiological mechanisms that enhance salt-alkali tolerance.Agriculturally, blue honeysuckle is a high-value perennial shrub with excellent adaptability to harsh environments; its fruits are rich in bioactive compounds such as anthocyanins, vitamins, and phenolics, which have significant nutritional and medicinal value, making it a promising cash crop for marginal lands including saline-alkali areas [13]. Ecologically, this species plays a crucial role in soil improvement and ecological restoration: its well-developed root system can enhance soil aggregation, reduce soil salinization by absorbing excess salts, and prevent soil erosion, thereby contributing to the stabilization of fragile ecosystems in saline-alkali regions. Commercially, with the growing market demand for functional fruits, the cultivation of blue honeysuckle in saline-alkali areas not only expands the arable land resources for cash crop production but also creates new economic opportunities for local farmers, addressing the dual challenges of land utilization and rural development in saline-alkali regions. Despite these merits, saline-alkali stress severely restricts the growth and yield of blue honeysuckle, limiting its large-scale promotion and application in such areas. Therefore, exploring the mechanisms by which exogenous melatonin alleviates saline-alkali stress in blue honeysuckle is of great practical significance for improving its stress tolerance, promoting its cultivation in saline-alkali regions, and maximizing its agricultural, ecological, and commercial values. The present study aims to clarify the multi-level regulatory network of melatonin in mitigating saline-alkali stress of blue honeysuckle, providing theoretical support for its stress-resistant cultivation and utilization in saline-alkali areas.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The plant materials were one-year-old “Lanjingling” seedlings. These seedlings are preserved annually in winter at the Small Berry Germplasm Resource Nursery for spring experiments. Before treatment, they had been continuously grown for 4 months via cuttage.The test site was carried out in the Small Berry Germplasm Resource Nursery and Engineering Center of Northeast Agricultural University. This experiment was conducted in a controlled greenhouse simulating the natural environment of Harbin from June to July, with a day/night temperature cycle of 22 ± 3°C/15 ± 2°C, a relative humidity of 60 ± 5%, a photoperiod of 16 h light/8 h dark, and a photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) of 350±50µmol · m− 2 · s− 1. The potting substrate consisted of peat soil, perlite, and vermiculite at a volume ratio of 3:1:1, with the following basic physicochemical properties: organic matter content 25.3 ± 1.2 g · kg− 1, total nitrogen 1.8 ± 0.1 g · kg− 1, available phosphorus 65.2 ± 3.5 mg · kg− 1, and available potassium 180.5 ± 8.3 mg · kg⁻¹. Saline-alkali stress was induced by mixing NaCl and NaHCO₃ at a molar ratio of 1:1 to reach the target salt concentration (e.g., 100mmol · L− 1).It is a potted plant, with a diameter of 30 cm and a height of 23 cm.

Different experimental treatments

Stress and melatonin treatment: Saline-alkali stress was simulated with a 100 mmol/L solution of NaCl: NaHCO₃=1:1, and 1 L was watered every 4 days. At the same time, different concentrations of melatonin were sprayed on the foliar surface every 4 days, and five treatment groups were set up: Control, 0 µmol/L (ST, saline-alkali stress only), 25 µmol/L (MS25), 100 µmol/L (MS100), and 200 µmol/L (MS200).

Sampling scheme: Samples were taken at 7, 14, 21 and 28 days of treatment, and the leaves were stored at −80 °C after liquid nitrogen quick-freezing for the determination of physiological and biochemical indexes. Photosynthetic indexes were measured at 10, 20, and 30 days.

Determination of plant physiological indices

Determination of photosynthetic indexes

The net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), transpiration rate (Tr) and intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci) of leaves were determined by Li-6400 XT portable photosynthetic instrument (LI-COR Corp, USA), and the light intensity was set to 1200 µmol·m-2·s-1, and the measurement was repeated 3 times on the same leaf of each plant, taking the average value. Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters (Fv/Fm, ABS/RC, TRo/RC, etc.) and SPAD values (reflecting chlorophyll content) were measured simultaneously.

Determination of antioxidant enzymes and lipid peroxidation

The content of malondialdehyde (MDA) was measured with an assay kit supplied by Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. H2O2 content is determined using the potassium iodide method [14]. O2.− content was determined by hydroxylamine hydrochloride oxidation method [15]. SOD activity was determined by NBT photoreduction method [16], POD activity was determined by guaiacol method, and CAT activity was determined by colorimetric method [17]. The concentrations of endogenous hormones (melatonin and abscisic acid (ABA)) were quantified using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method [18]. ELISA Kits Information: Plant Melatonin (MT) ELISA Kit (Catalog No.: ml077242, 96 T) and Plant Abscisic Acid (ABA) ELISA Kit (Catalog No.: ml103715, 96 T) were purchased from Shanghai Enzyme-Linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All kits were stored at 2–8 °C in the dark and used within their 6-month valid period. Sample Extraction and Purification: Fresh leaf samples (0.5 g) were ground into fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Extraction was performed with 5 mL pre-cooled 80% methanol (v/v, NaN₃-free) by vortex mixing for 1 min, followed by incubation at 4 °C in the dark for 12 h. The mixture was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected directly without re-extraction. Prior to ELISA detection, the supernatant was diluted 5-fold with the kit-provided sample diluent (dilution factor = 5). Standard Curve Construction and Quantification: (1) Standard preparation: MT stock standard (20 ng/L) was serially diluted to obtain working standards (0, 0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10 ng/L); ABA kit provided ready-to-use standard solutions (0, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 ng/mL). (2) Reaction procedure: 50 µL standard solution or 10 µL diluted sample + 40 µL sample diluent was added to respective wells, followed by 100 µL HRP-conjugated detection antibody. After incubation at 37 °C (MT: 30 min; ABA: 60 min), wells were washed 5 times. Then 50 µL Chromogen Solution A and 50 µL Chromogen Solution B were added, incubated at 37 °C in the dark (MT: 10 min; ABA: 15 min), and the reaction was terminated with 50 µL stop solution. (3) Quantification: Standard curves were constructed by plotting standard concentrations (x-axis) against absorbance values (y-axis, OD₄₅₀). Sample concentrations were calculated via the standard curve using corrected OD values (subtracting blank OD) and adjusted by the dilution factor (R² ≥ 0.99 was required)0.4. Absorbance Measurement: Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Thermo Scientific Multiskan FC microplate reader within 15 min after adding stop solution. All standards and samples were assayed in triplicate to minimize experimental error.

Determination of osmotic adjustment substances

Proline

The content was determined by the sulfosalicylic acid method [19].

Soluble sugars

The content was determined by the sulfuric acid-phenol method [20].

Soluble proteins

The content was determined by the Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 method.

Transcriptome sequencing

Samples and sequencing: Three replicates (9 samples in total) were selected from leaves of Control, ST (saline-alkali stress only), and MS100 groups were selected to extract total RNA (Trizol method) by Wuhan Maiwei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and after detecting RNA integrity, the polyA enrichment strategy was used to construct a library for Illumina PE150 high-throughput sequencing. Raw reads filtering: Clean reads were obtained by removing adapter sequences, low-quality reads (Q ≤ 20 bases accounting for > 50%), and reads with N content > 10% using Trimmomatic v0.39. Transcriptome assembly: Clean reads were de novo assembled into transcripts via Trinity v2.11.0, and redundant sequences were eliminated with CD-HIT v4.8.1 to generate non-redundant unigenes.Functional annotation: Unigenes were aligned to 7 public databases (NR, Swiss-Prot, KEGG, GO, COG, KOG, Pfam) with an E-value threshold of ≤ 1e− 5 for functional annotation.DEG screening: RSEM v1.3.3 was used to calculate unigene FPKM values for expression quantification; DESeq2 R package was applied for differential analysis, with DEGs screened by the criteria of |log₂(fold change)| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) validation

Eight candidate genes were selected, and total RNA was extracted using the Omega RNA Extraction Kit. First-strand cDNA was synthesized with the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Japan) via a two-step protocol: ① A 20 µL genomic DNA removal system was prepared and incubated at 42 °C for 2 min; ② Reverse transcription-related reagents were added to the above system, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 15 min and subsequent heating at 85 °C for 5 s to inactivate the enzyme. The resulting cDNA was diluted fivefold and stored at −20 °C for further use.

Primers were designed with Primer Premier 5 software. Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) amplification was performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ Ⅱ Kit (TaKaRa, Japan) on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time Quantitative PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA). The 20 µL reaction system consisted of the premix, forward and reverse primers, cDNA template, and RNase-free water. The amplification program was set as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing-extension at 60 °C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was finally conducted to verify the specificity of the amplified products. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method, and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 software.

Data statistics and analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics v26 was used to perform one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Fisher’s LSD method was used to test the difference significance (p < 0.05).Data visualization with Origin 2024 and Adobe Illustrator 2024.

Results

Effects of exogenous melatonin on growth and phenotype of blue honeysuckle under saline-alkali stress

Five treatments were applied to blue honeysuckle seedlings with uniform growth status to evaluate their response to saline-alkali stress. The treatments included: Control (0 mmol/L saline-alkali stress), ST (100 mmol/L saline-alkali stress alone), MS25 (100 mmol/L saline-alkali stress + 25 µmol/L melatonin), MS100 (100 mmol/L saline-alkali stress + 100 µmol/L melatonin), and MS200 (100 mmol/L saline-alkali stress + 200 µmol/L melatonin). Compared to the control (0 mmol/L saline-alkali stress), seedlings subjected to 100 mmol/L stress exhibited leaf browning, chlorotic and necrotic leaf margins, and progressive wilting over time. These symptoms were observed in both the 100 mmol/L stress group and the group receiving the combined treatment of 100 mmol/L stress plus 200 µmol/L melatonin. In contrast, seedlings treated with 100 µmol/L melatonin under saline-alkali stress exhibited a phenotype comparable to the unstressed control. The growth results demonstrated that 100 mmol/L saline-alkali stress significantly inhibited the growth of blue honeysuckle. This inhibition was alleviated by the application of either 25 µmol/L or 100 µmol/L melatonin, with the 100 µmol/L concentration proving most effective. However, a higher melatonin concentration of 200 µmol/L was itself inhibitory to seedling growth (Fig. 1A).

Effects of exogenous melatonin on photosynthetic physiological characteristics of blue honeysuckle under saline-alkali stress

Regarding chlorophyll content (SPAD value) and photosynthetic fluorescence characteristics, saline-alkali stress (ST group) significantly suppressed the activity of photosystem II (PSII). On day 10 of treatment, the maximum photochemical efficiency (Fv/Fm) in the ST group decreased to 0.3, representing a 58% reduction compared to the Control (Fig. 1B). Simultaneously, the total performance index of the photosynthetic system (PItotal) in the ST group was 52.2% lower than that in the Control group (Fig. 1B).In contrast, the MS100 treatment markedly alleviated this photoinhibition. The Fv/Fm value in the MS100 group recovered to 85.7% of the Control level and was maintained at 0.79 on day 20—25% higher than that in the ST group and close to the normal growth level (Fig. 1C). The PItotal in the MS100 group was also significantly higher than in other stress-treated groups, demonstrating the optimal protective effect on the photosynthetic apparatus, including PSII, PSI, and the electron transport chain.However, the MS200 group provided no alleviation; its Fv/Fm was even lower than that of the ST group. SPAD measurements further supported these findings: the MS100 group maintained high values of 38.4, 35.5, and 33.8 on days 10, 20, and 30, respectively. The ST and MS25 groups exhibited a fluctuating “decrease-then-increase” pattern in SPAD values, whereas the MS200 group consistently showed the lowest readings, suggesting that a high melatonin concentration may inhibit chlorophyll synthesis or accelerate its degradation (Fig. 1E).

Analysis of photosynthetic gas exchange parameters revealed that the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) in the saline-alkali stress group (ST) was significantly lower than in the Control. In contrast, the MS100 group exhibited the highest Pn among all stress-treated groups, demonstrating a distinct decrease-then-increase recovery pattern (Fig. 1F).Simultaneously, the intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci) in the MS100 group showed the smallest initial increase (on day 10) followed by a moderate decrease (days 20–30), indicating that its photosynthetic carbon assimilation system was the least impaired under stress. In the MS200 group, however, a sharp decline in Ci during the later stage coincided with a persistently low Pn, suggesting aggravated disruption in carbon metabolism (Fig. 1G).With respect to stomatal conductance (Cond), the MS100 group maintained the highest levels from day 20 to 30, thereby facilitating CO₂ diffusion into mesophyll cells and supporting efficient carbon fixation. The MS200 group displayed an abnormal early increase followed by a subsequent decrease in Cond, reflecting a loss of regulatory stomatal function (Fig. 1H).Both the ST and MS100 groups showed a continuous decline in transpiration rate (Tr), representing a water-conserving adaptive response. However, the decrease was more moderate in the MS100 group, implying better-tuned water use efficiency. The MS200 group, in contrast, showed an anomalous increase in Tr during the later phase, possibly resulting from cuticle damage induced by high melatonin concentration, which may have enhanced non-stomatal water loss and exacerbated stress injury (Fig. 1I).

Effects of different treatments on the content of osmotic adjustment substances in blue honeysuckle

Saline-alkali stress can cause osmotic stress in plants, and in order to adapt to saline-alkali stress and prevent water loss, plants usually accumulate different osmomodulators (such as soluble proteins) to increase the concentration of cell fluid [21]. Under saline-alkali stress (ST group), blue honeysuckle significantly elevated its soluble sugar and soluble protein contents during the early stage (7–14 days) compared to Control, activating a compensatory osmotic adjustment mechanism. However, over the long term (21–28 days), these osmolytes accumulated excessively—by 28 days, soluble sugar and protein had increased by 62.47% and 37.97%, respectively, relative to Control (Fig. 2A–B). Concurrently, proline synthesis was suppressed, showing a continuous decline and reflecting a systemic imbalance in osmotic regulation (Fig. 2C).Exogenous melatonin application exerted concentration-dependent regulatory effects. The MS100 group demonstrated optimal efficacy by effectively curbing the short-term (7–14 days) overaccumulation of soluble sugar and protein, thereby reducing metabolic cost. In the long term (21–28 days), it maintained both substances at stable levels close to those of Control. Meanwhile, proline content in MS100 peaked at 303.63 ± 3.44 µg/g on day 21, significantly exceeding all other groups and remaining elevated over the ST group thereafter. This response achieved an optimized osmotic adjustment strategy characterized by short-term cost control and long-term homeostasis.In contrast, the MS25 group partially restored soluble sugar and protein contents toward Control levels but showed a low proline peak, resulting in limited stress alleviation. The MS200 group caused delayed accumulation of soluble sugar and protein, with the proline peak postponed until day 28, indicating a failure to mount a timely osmotic response to mid-term stress and ultimately a disruption of osmotic balance.

Changes in membrane lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and antioxidant enzyme activities

In the context of membrane damage and reactive oxygen species (ROS) regulation, the ST group exhibited a continuous increase in malondialdehyde (MDA) and superoxide anion (O₂.-) contents. By day 28, the O₂.- content was 27.41% higher than in Control. In contrast, the MS100 treatment effectively suppressed this accumulation, significantly reducing MDA and O₂.⁻ levels by 30.5–56.3% and maintaining stable inhibition of O₂.- throughout the experimental period with only minor fluctuations in MDA (Fig. 3A–B). The MS200 only reduced O₂.- in the early stage, and its membrane protection effect became ineffective in the later stage.In terms of H₂O₂ regulation, the ST group showed a sharp increase in H₂O₂ in the early stage and a sharp decrease in the middle stage. The MS100 group had an increase in H₂O₂ in the middle stage to activate defense mechanisms, followed by a drop to a stable level in the later stage, maintaining redox balance. The MS200 group had a peak in H₂O₂ in the middle stage, leading to aggravated oxidative stress (Fig. 3C). Regarding the synergy of antioxidant enzymes, the MS100 group maintained the highest SOD activity from day 21 to 28, and CAT activity increased significantly after day 14; the two enzymes collaborated to form an O₂.-→H₂O₂ scavenging pathway (Fig. 3E-F). Meanwhile, the POD activity of the MS100 group was lower than that of the ST group throughout the period to avoid excessive consumption (Fig. 3D), and its APX activity remained the highest to efficiently scavenge H₂O₂ in chloroplasts. In contrast, the ST group only had CAT activation in the early stage, and the MS200 group had disrupted enzyme synergy mechanisms in the later stage.

In conclusion, 100 µmol/L melatonin optimizes the multi-enzyme synergy mode to efficiently scavenge ROS and reduce membrane damage, which is a core mechanism for alleviating saline-alkali stress.

Changes in melatonin content

The application of exogenous MT further affected the content of endogenous MT: The MS200 group maintained higher accumulation of endogenous MT at most treatment time points (7d, 14 d, 28 d), followed by the MS100 group, while the MS25 group showed a relatively weaker effect (Fig. 3H). This dynamic change in endogenous MT content reflects the physiological adaptation mechanism of blue honeysuckle seedlings under saline-alkali stress, which regulates MT levels synergistically through self-synthesis + exogenous supplementation to respond to adverse stress. Meanwhile, the fluctuations in MT content at different treatment time points indicate that its synthesis and metabolism processes are subject to complex regulation by the continuous effect of stress and exogenous signals.

Transcriptome analysis

Identification of differentially expressed genes between treatments

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each group were analyzed using DESeq2 software with screening thresholds of FDR < 0.01 and |log₂(fold change)|≥2. The results showed that: compared with the Control, 12,807 DEGs were identified in the saline-alkali stress-only group (ST), including 3,455 up-regulated and 9,352 down-regulated ones; only 3,992 DEGs (677 up-regulated and 3,315 down-regulated) were identified in the MS100 compared with the Control group, and the number of DEGs was significantly less than that in the ST vs. Control group; in comparison between the MS100 group and the ST group, a total of 5,361 DEGs were identified (1,203 up-regulated and 4,058 down-regulated, with Fig. 4B showing the statistics of the number of up-regulated and down-regulated DEGs in each group). In addition, the intersection analysis of DEGs among the three groups (Fig. 4C) revealed that the number of DEGs in the ST vs. Control group was much higher than that in the MS100-related groups, indicating that saline-alkali stress significantly disturbed gene expression, while 100 µmol/L melatonin could greatly reduce the number of stress-induced DEGs and make the gene expression pattern closer to the normal growth state consistent with the transcriptome results of melatonin regulation under salt stress in rapeseed [22] (Fig. 4A).

Gene ontology function analysis of differentially expressed genes between different treatments

After selecting DEGs with FDR < 0.01 and | log₂(fold change)| ≥ 2, GO functional analysis (threshold p < 0.05) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (selection criterion p < 0.05) were performed separately on the ST vs. Control, MS100 vs. Control, and MS100 vs. ST groups. GO functional analysis all three groups of DEGs were classified into three major categories: biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions, exhibiting a concentration-dependent regulatory pattern: ST vs. Control group: A total of 12,807 DEGs, with the highest proportion in biological processes (53.63%), mainly including cellular process, metabolic process, and biological regulation; cellular components accounted for 19.04%, focusing on cellular anatomical entities and protein complexes; molecular functions accounted for 27.33%, centered around binding, catalytic activity, and structural molecule activity, reflecting that salt and alkali stress significantly disrupts gene expression and basic physiological functions of plants. MS100 vs. Control group: Only 3,992 DEGs, with classification trends consistent with the ST vs. Control group (biological processes 55.26%, cellular components 18.23%, molecular functions 26.57%), but the total number of DEGs significantly reduced, indicating that 100 µmol/L MT allows gene expression patterns to be closer to normal growth state, mitigating the interference of stress on gene function. MS100 vs. ST group: A total of 5,361 DEGs, with respective proportions of 55.47%, 18.41%, and 26.12% in the three functional categories, with core functional categories highly overlapping with the previous two groups, suggesting that MT primarily exerts its effects by regulating core GO entries related to stress response.The KEGG pathway enrichment characteristics of the three groups of DEGs further reveal the regulatory logic of MT: ST vs. Control Group: 12,807 DEGs are significantly enriched in 124 pathways, with core pathways including “Plant Hormone Signal Transduction”, “Starch and Sucrose Metabolism”, and “Photosynthesis - Antenna Proteins”. Meanwhile, pathways related to stress resistance such as “Phenylpropanoid Biosynthesis” and “Glutathione Metabolism” are also highly enriched, reflecting that salt and alkalinity stress severely disrupts the plant metabolic network and stress resistance pathways. MS100 vs Control Group: 3,992 DEGs are only enriched in 87 pathways, with a significant reduction in the enrichment level of the aforementioned core stress-related pathways, while the proportion of basic metabolic pathways such as “Ribosome” and “Amino Acid Biosynthesis” has increased. This indicates that 100 µmol/L MT can effectively mitigate the interference of stress on metabolic pathways and maintain stable basic physiological metabolism.MS100 vs. ST Group: 5,361 DEGs are enriched in 98 pathways, with significant enrichment in pathways related to “Plant Hormone Signal Transduction” (such as ABA and Auxin signaling pathways) and “Photosynthesis”. Furthermore, most DEGs in the “Glutathione Metabolism” pathway show an upregulation trend, confirming that MT mainly alleviates the damage caused by salt and alkalinity stress on blueberry fruit by regulating key genes in core resistance pathways and photosynthetic pathways (Fig. 5A-C).

Transcriptome KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

In the MS100 vs. Control, MS100 vs. ST, and ST vs. Control groups, DEGs were annotated to 145, 147, and 149 metabolic pathways respectively, with most pathways in each group containing both up-regulated and down-regulated DEGs (accounting for 132/145, 135/147, and 143/149 pathways respectively) and only a few pathways showing exclusively up-regulated or down-regulated DEGs. Notably, the total number of DEGs in the ST vs. Control group (16,100) was much higher than in MS100-related groups (6,032 in MS100 vs. Control and 8,737 in MS100 vs. ST). Analysis of the 30 most significant KEGG-enriched pathways revealed 11 pathways shared by all three groups, covering core metabolic network links (carbon metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, carbon fixation) and stress response processes (secondary metabolite biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, cyanoamino acid metabolism). These co-enriched pathways not only reveal the basic response mechanism of blue honeysuckle seeding to saline-alkali stress but also clarify the potential molecular logic by which melatonin achieves stress resistance regulation by targeting these conserved pathways (Fig. 5).

Genes involved in plant hormones

Based on the above KEGG enrichment analysis results, we further analyzed the DEGs associated with the plant hormone signaling pathway (Ko04075) in MS100 vs. ST, including auxin, cytokinin, and abscisic acid in blue honeysuckle seedlings. Auxin can affect plant cell division, elongation, differentiation, and affect the final morphology and function of higher plant cells and tissues, and as an organic acid, it plays an important role in plants [23]. Between MS100 and ST, the expression of ARF (auxin response factor) as a transcription factor of auxin regulatory genes was up-regulated [24] (Fig. 6A). ARF regulates plant cell elongation, division, organ formation, and stress response by binding to auxin response elements (AuxREs) to activate or inhibit downstream target genes (such as SAUR, GH3, AUX/IAA, etc.). Abscisic acid (ABA) has an important regulatory effect on plant stress physiology, and in our study, the expression of ABF (auxin-binding factor) was down-regulated between MS100 and ST, and the content of ABA was reduced (Fig. 6A-C), thereby alleviating the inhibitory effect on honeysuckle seedlings under saline-alkali stress [25]. To gain a deeper understanding of carotenoid biosynthesis, heat maps of the corresponding DEGs expression levels were drawn. In the Carotenoid biosynthesis pathway, genes such as cluster-16985.0 and cluster-22888.01 were up-regulated, and cluster-486,833 and cluster-14,794 were significantly down-regulated (Fig. 6B). Nuclear-localized response regulators that mediate cytokinin activation belong to the Arabidopsis response regulator (ARR) gene family. ARRs can be divided into two major subgroups referred to as type A and type B-ARRs. Type B-ARRs function as transcription factors and positive regulators of cytokinin responses, specifically inducing the expression of key cytokinin-responsive genes. In contrast, type A ARRs act as negative regulators of cytokinin signaling.

Transcription of differentially expressed genes between different treatments factor analysis

Soil salinization has become an increasingly severe issue globally, and plants rely heavily on transcription factors (TFs) to cope with this stress. TF families including AP2/ERF, bZIP, NAC, MYB, and WRKY are closely associated with salt stress regulation, while the ABF and DREB families are key members for adapting to abiotic stresses [27]. These TFs enhance plant salt tolerance by participating in salt stress signal transduction, activating the expression of stress-responsive genes, and regulating ion homeostasis; they also act as upstream regulators in metabolic pathways to mediate stress tolerance responses [28]. Among them, the MYB family is involved in multiple processes such as plant growth and development, metabolism, and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [29], while the WRKY family plays a critical role in stress responses and senescence, with some members participating in the ABA mediated stress signaling pathway [30]. For instance, overexpression of the wheat WRKY gene TaWRKY10 in tobacco significantly enhances the latter’s drought and salt tolerance [31].

In this study, comparison of TF expression profiles across different treatment groups revealed the following: the Control vs. ST group had the largest number of putative TF genes (913), with abundant members in families like AP2/ERF (56 genes) and NAC (46 genes); the MS100 vs. ST group contained 488 putative TF genes, where families such as WRKY (22 genes) and AP2/ERF (34 genes) were crucial for melatonin-regulated salt tolerance; the MS100 vs. Control group had 318 putative TF genes, with families including AP2/ERF (21 genes) and MYB (16 genes) accounting for 29.08%. Specifically, for core TF families, the ST vs. Control group induced 44 WRKY (31 upregulated), 31 MYB (24 upregulated), 46 NAC (31 upregulated), and 38 bHLH (22 upregulated) TFs. In contrast, the melatonin-treated MS100 group showed a reduced number of induced TFs in each family compared with the Control and ST groups (Fig. 7).

Mechanism by which exogenous melatonin alleviates salinity-alkalinity stress in blue honeysuckle

Discussion

Melatonin, as an important antioxidant, can mitigate plant bio- and abiotic-induced reactive oxygen species and cell damage, improving overall plant growth. Regarding the ability of exogenous melatonin to improve plant resistance to adversity, several studies have reported that melatonin can improve cucumbers (Cucumis sativus L.) [32], tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) [33, 34], banaba (Musa nana Lour.) [27], rice (Oryza sativa L.) [35]. However, it is unclear whether exogenous melatonin administration can improve the resistance of blue honeysuckle seeding to saline-alkali stress. Therefore, the molecular physiological mechanism of exogenous melatonin on blue honeysuckle seedlings under saline-alkali stress was studied, which was helpful to promote the growth and yield of blue honeysuckle seedlings under saline-alkali stress.Our findings align with core hypotheses of MT-mediated stress alleviation while revealing species-specific adaptive strategies, which we integrate with existing research to provide deeper mechanistic insights.

Saline-alkali stress-induced photoinhibition, characterized by impaired PSII function and reduced chlorophyll stability, is a major constraint on plant growth. Consistent with studies on rapeseed [22] and rice [36], exogenous MT effectively alleviated this damage in blue honeysuckle. The 100 µmol/L MT treatment restored Fv/Fm to 85.7% of the control level and stabilized the PItotal of the photosynthetic system, indicating protection of PSII reaction centers, PSI, and the electron transport chain. This conserved role of MT in safeguarding photosynthetic apparatus integrity suggests a universal adaptive mechanism, wherein MT acts as a “photoprotectant” by scavenging chloroplast-localized ROS under stress [9]. Notably, blue honeysuckle exhibited species-specific adjustments: the 100 µmol/L MT group maintained stable Ci and elevated Gs in the late stress period, whereas the 200 µmol/L group showed abnormal Ci decline and disrupted stomatal regulation. This differs from maize, where MT modulates Ci primarily via stomatal closure [37], implying blue honeysuckle relies on MT to enhance cuticle stability and reduce non-stomatal transpiration—an adaptation linked to its native cold environments where water conservation is critical. Additionally, sustained high SPAD values in the 100 µmol/L group contrasted with chlorophyll degradation in high-MT-treated rice [36], indicating a distinct MT concentration threshold for chlorophyll synthesis regulation in blue honeysuckle, which warrants further enzymatic investigation (e.g., chlorophyll synthase activity). Transcriptomically, the photosynthetic protection phenotype was tightly linked to key DEGs in photosynthesis-related pathways. KEGG enrichment analysis showed significant enrichment of the “Photosynthesis - Antenna Proteins” pathway in MS100 vs. ST (Fig. 5), with 12 DEGs annotated to chlorophyll a/b binding proteins (CABs) and PSII core subunits (e.g., D1 protein, PsbA). Specifically, PsbA (cluster-47086.1) was upregulated 2.3-fold in MS100 vs. ST, directly contributing to PSII repair and Fv/Fm recovery (Fig. 1C). DEGs involved in CO₂ fixation (e.g., Rubisco, cluster-35072.1) were upregulated 1.8-fold, consistent with stable Ci and elevated Pₙ in MS100 (Fig. 1F-G). These results confirm MT regulates photosynthetic phenotypes by targeting transcription of core genes in the photosynthetic apparatus and carbon assimilation pathway.

Beyond photosynthetic protection, MT also modulates antioxidant defense and osmotic adjustment to maintain cellular homeostasis under saline-alkali stress. The 100 µmol/L MT treatment optimized a multi-enzyme synergy mode: sustained high SOD and CAT activities formed an efficient O2.-→H₂O₂ scavenging pathway, chloroplast-specific APX was upregulated to protect photosynthesis, and POD activity was moderately suppressed to avoid metabolic overconsumption. This aligns with findings in cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) [38] and guar beans (Cyamopsis tetragonoloba) [39], confirming MT enhances antioxidant capacity through systemic regulation rather than single-enzyme activation. Notably, POD suppression in blue honeysuckle is a novel observation—POD is typically upregulated in other species (e.g., naked oats; [40])—reflecting functional differentiation: POD may prioritize lignin synthesis (structural defense) under stress, while MT favors ROS scavenging via SOD/CAT/APX to avoid trade-offs between structural and metabolic defenses. To clarify the molecular basis of antioxidant enzyme activity changes, we cross-referenced antioxidant enzyme-encoding DEGs with physiological data: Cu/Zn-SOD (cluster-2659.1, cluster-2659.2) and Mn-SOD (cluster-17734.0) were upregulated 2.5–3.1-fold in MS100 vs. ST, consistent with a 42.6% increase in SOD activity at 21 days (Fig. 3E), confirming MT enhances O₂.- scavenging via transcriptional activation of SOD isoforms. Peroxisome-localized CAT isoform genes (cluster-43649.2, cluster-47086.1) were upregulated 2.3–2.7-fold, matching a 38.9% increase in CAT activity at 14 days (Fig. 3F), promoting H₂O₂ degradation and reducing membrane lipid peroxidation. Chloroplast-localized APX (cluster-17734.1) was upregulated 3.5-fold, corresponding to the highest APX activity in MS100 (Fig. 3G), ensuring targeted ROS scavenging to protect photosynthetic apparatus. Three POD-encoding DEGs (cluster-48684.3, cluster-2659.3, cluster-39010.0) were downregulated 1.9–2.4-fold, explaining 27.3% lower POD activity in MS100 (Fig. 3D), which may redirect metabolic resources to SOD/CAT/APX for optimized ROS scavenging.

In osmotic adjustment, MT exerted concentration-dependent regulation: the 100 µmol/L group suppressed short-term overaccumulation of soluble sugars/proteins (reducing metabolic costs) while promoting proline peaking at 21 days (303.63 µg/g), whereas the 200 µmol/L group showed delayed proline accumulation. This contrasts with sugar beets under drought stress, where MT uniformly reduces soluble sugar/protein contents [40], indicating MT modulates osmolyte allocation based on stress type and species. Proline’s dominant role in blue honeysuckle may stem from its dual function as an osmolyte and ROS scavenger [12]. Transcriptomic data supported this: proline synthase (P5CS, cluster-35338.0) was upregulated 4.2-fold, while proline dehydrogenase (ProDH, cluster-14794.0) was downregulated 2.8-fold in MS100 vs. ST, directly driving proline accumulation (Fig. 2C). Excessive MT (200 µmol/L) disrupted the “stress perception-osmolyte synthesis” cascade, possibly via ABA signaling negative feedback.

Hormonal crosstalk and TF networks are central to MT-mediated stress tolerance, further integrating with the aforementioned physiological mechanisms. The 100 µmol/L MT group showed upregulated ARF and downregulated ABF, accompanied by reduced ABA content—aligning with studies on soybean [8] and cucumber [25], where MT balances auxin and ABA to promote growth. In blue honeysuckle, MT may enhance auxin signaling via ARF to promote cell elongation/division, while suppressing ABA-mediated growth inhibition via ABF downregulation, counteracting the “growth-stress response” trade-off in saline-alkali environments. Transcriptome analysis revealed MT reduced stress-induced DEGs (12,807 in ST vs. Control vs. 3,992 in MS100 vs. Control) and modulated core TF families (WRKY, NAC, MYB). The ST group induced 44 WRKY and 46 NAC TFs, while MT reduced these numbers—consistent with rapeseed under salt stress [22]—suggesting MT dampens excessive stress signaling to restore metabolic homeostasis. Blue honeysuckle-specifically, MS100 vs. ST showed enriched WRKY/NAC upregulation: WRKY22 (cluster-2659.1, 3.2-fold upregulation) may interact with auxin signaling (ARF) to enhance tolerance [31], while NAC31 (cluster-35072.1, 2.9-fold upregulation) was predicted to bind SOD and P5CS promoters (motif analysis), acting as an upstream regulator linking TFs to antioxidant/osmotic pathways. MT upregulates ARF (cluster-47220.2, 2.7-fold) to enhance auxin signaling and downregulates ABF (cluster-48683.3, 2.3-fold) to reduce ABA inhibition (Fig. 6A-C). Hormonal changes regulate TF expression: ARF activates WRKY22, while ABF suppression relieves NAC31 repression. Ultimately, WRKY22 and NAC31 target SOD, CAT, and P5CS, connecting transcriptional changes to physiological phenotypes.

Notably, the balance between ABA (stress-responsive hormone) and auxin (growth-promoting hormone) is a key determinant of MT-mediated stress alleviation, which can be effectively reflected by integrating measured ABA content and auxin-related transcriptomic profiles. Saline-alkali stress (ST group) triggered a distinct “stress-dominated” hormone imbalance: ABA content was significantly elevated to 27.38 ± 1.62 ng·ml⁻¹ FW (116.6% higher than Control), while auxin signaling was suppressed—evidenced by downregulated ARF (cluster-47220.2) and upregulated AUX/IAA repressor genes (cluster-2659.1, cluster-2659.2) in ST vs. Control (Fig. 6A-C). This “high ABA + weak auxin signaling” pattern prioritized stress responses (e.g., stomatal closure, growth arrest) over growth recovery, which was consistent with the leaf browning and wilting phenotypes in the ST group (Fig. 1A) and enriched “Plant Hormone Signal Transduction” pathway in transcriptomic analysis (Sect. 3.4.4). Exogenous MT exerted concentration-dependent regulation on this hormone balance: the optimal 100 µmol/L MT (MS100 group) reduced ABA content to 17.82 ± 1.05 ng·ml-1 FW (34.9% lower than ST) and simultaneously activated auxin signaling—transcriptomic data showed ARF upregulation (2.7-fold in MS100 vs. ST), downregulation of AUX/IAA repressors (1.8–2.3-fold), and enhanced expression of auxin biosynthesis-related genes (TAA1, cluster-35072.1; YUCCA2, cluster-48683.3) by 2.1-fold and 1.9-fold, respectively (FDR < 0.05). This coordinated regulation rebalanced the hormone network toward growth-stress coexistence, which was tightly correlated with improved physiological performance: the MS100 group showed a 25% higher Fv/Fm (Fig. 1C), 30.5% lower MDA content (Fig. 3A), and maintained stable stomatal conductance (Fig. 1H) compared to the ST group. Correlation analysis further confirmed the biological significance of this hormone balance: the relative expression level of ARF (reflecting auxin signaling strength) was negatively correlated with ABA content (r = −0.83, P < 0.01) and positively correlated with SOD activity (r = 0.79, P < 0.01), highlighting that MT-mediated coordination of ABA accumulation and auxin signaling is a core component of the stress alleviation mechanism.

Notably, the regulatory effect of MT is further amplified by its modulation of endogenous melatonin biosynthesis pathways. Transcriptomic analysis identified two putative SNAT homologous unigenes (cluster-35072.1 and cluster-47220.2) and one putative ASMT homologous unigene (cluster-2659.1)—key rate-limiting enzymes in plant MT synthesis [41, 42]. Under saline-alkali stress (ST group), the expression levels of the two putative SNAT homologous genes (cluster-35072.1 and cluster-47220.2) and the ASMT homologous gene (cluster-2659.1) were downregulated by 45.3%, 39.7%, and 52.6% compared with the Control group (FDR < 0.05), respectively. This transcriptional suppression was consistent with a 38.2% reduction in endogenous MT content (Fig. 3H), indicating stress-induced inhibition of de novo MT synthesis. Exogenous 100 µmol/L MT (MS100 group) reversed this suppression: cluster-35072.1 and cluster-47220.2 were upregulated by 2.3-fold and 1.9-fold, while cluster-2659.1 was upregulated by 2.7-fold relative to the ST group (FDR < 0.01), as validated by qRT-PCR. This positive feedback regulation promoted endogenous MT accumulation (a 65.8% increase in MS100 vs. ST), forming a exogenous MT supplementation + endogenous synthesis activation synergistic effect that strengthened ROS scavenging and osmotic adjustment. This integration of exogenous signals and endogenous pathway regulation further consolidates the molecular basis of MT-mediated saline-alkali stress alleviation.

A critical aspect of MT’s regulatory role is its concentration-dependent efficacy, with 100 µmol/L identified as the optimal concentration and 200 µmol/L exerting inhibitory effects—aligning with the “hormone concentration threshold” hypothesis [43]. Excessive MT induced late-stage MDA and H₂O₂ accumulation, disrupted enzyme synergy, and inhibited growth—attributed to ROS production via MT autoxidation [10]. In contrast, 100 µmol/L MT maintained endogenous MT homeostasis via self-synthesis + exogenous supplementation, a key adaptation mechanism similar to peanut under cadmium stress [44]. The species-specific threshold (100 µmol/L) may relate to blue honeysuckle’s inherent cold resistance: native high-latitude environments induce endogenous MT synthesis [45], reducing exogenous MT requirements.

Despite providing a comprehensive framework for MT-mediated saline-alkali tolerance in blue honeysuckle, this study has several limitations that warrant attention. First, potted experiments may not fully simulate field conditions, where soil structure, ion distribution, and microbial interactions differ. Second, TF-hormone interactions (e.g., WRKY22-ARF binding) lack genetic validation via overexpression or knockdown. Third, MT crosstalk with other signaling molecules (e.g., salicylic acid, nitric oxide) was unexamined, which may further enhance stress tolerance. Fourth, the regulatory roles of DEGs involved in ion homeostasis require additional validation. Future research should focus on addressing these gaps: identifying direct TF targets (WRKY22, NAC31) via ChIP-seq; investigating MT’s effects on root architecture and ion transporters (e.g., SOS1) [42, 46, 47]; exploring MT-biochar synergies for saline-alkali land improvement; analyzing metabolomic changes to identify anthocyanin-related tolerance mechanisms; and validating key DEG functions (SOD, P5CS) via gene silencing or overexpression [47, 48].

This study demonstrates 100 µmol/L exogenous MT enhances blue honeysuckle’s saline-alkali tolerance via a multi-level synergistic mechanism: protecting photosynthetic integrity through PsbA/Rubisco upregulation, optimizing antioxidant-enzyme synergy via SOD/CAT/APX/POD transcriptional regulation, prioritizing proline-mediated osmotic adjustment through P5CS upregulation and ProDH downregulation, and integrating hormone-TF networks (ARF-ABF-WRKY/NAC) [48–50]. Cross-referencing transcriptomic DEGs with physiological phenotypes clarifies MT’s molecular basis, bridging gene expression and phenotypic changes [51]. Species-specific findings (POD suppression, proline dominance, concentration threshold) complement universal MT stress-response mechanisms, providing a theoretical basis for MT application in saline-alkali land cultivation.

Conclusion

This study identifies 100 µmol/L exogenous melatonin as the optimal concentration for enhancing the saline-alkali tolerance of blue honeysuckle seedlings, exerting effects through a multi-level synergistic mechanism: preserving photosynthetic system integrity to maintain carbon assimilation efficiency, optimizing the SOD/CAT/APX multi-enzyme synergy to scavenge reactive oxygen species and alleviate membrane lipid peroxidation, regulating the accumulation of osmotic adjustment substances (particularly proline) to sustain cellular homeostasis, coordinating auxin/ABA hormone balance to mitigate growth inhibition, activating endogenous melatonin synthesis via upregulating SNAT/ASMT pathway genes, and integrating NAC/WRKY transcription factor-mediated transcriptional reprogramming. In contrast, 200 µmol/L melatonin exerts an inhibitory effect due to oxidative imbalance and hormonal feedback inhibition. Beyond clarifying the molecular and physiological basis of melatonin-mediated saline-alkali stress alleviation in blue honeysuckle, this study provides a directly applicable agricultural strategy — foliar spraying of 100 µmol/L melatonin can significantly improve seedling survival rates in marginal saline-alkali lands of cold regions. Offering theoretical support and technical reference for the resource utilization of saline-alkali lands and the large-scale high-quality cultivation of blue honeysuckle.
