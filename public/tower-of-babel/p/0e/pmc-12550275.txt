Leveraging Telemedicine to Manage Adolescent Obesity: A Systematic Literature Review
Authors: Clemens Kruse, Gerardo Pacheco, Haylee Roe, Sydney Caperton, Raven Krips
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsObesity and adolescent obesity are international problems that have increased three‐fold since 1975. Adolescent obesity is highly correlated with long‐term health consequences. Sedentism and diet are mostly to blame for this condition. Telemedicine offers solutions, but other systematic reviews do not link telemedicine efforts to health outcomes. The objective of this systematic literature review is to analyze the effectiveness of telemedicine interventions in adolescent obesity through analysis of data over the last 10 years internationally. Adolescents were defined as ages 10 to 19. Interventions of telemedicine included mHealth, eHealth, and video conferencing.MethodsFour research databases were queried with a Boolean search string. The systematic literature review was conducted in accordance with a published protocol and reported in accordance with PRISMA (2020). Research articles were accepted if they used a telemedicine intervention to address adolescent obesity.ResultsThree interventions (mHealth, eHealth, and video call) were identified in 17 RCTs that originated in nine countries. Interventions reported improvements in BMI, zBMI, weight loss, waist and hip circumference, physical activity, cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein, triglycerides, fasting blood sugar, symptoms of food addiction, health knowledge, muscle mass, strength, agility, body fat, behavior change, adherence to weight‐loss programs, life perspectives, global lifestyles, nutrition, psychological impacts, stress, and depression.ConclusionTelemedicine offers interventions in the management of adolescent obesity that address both physical and mental health, however, not all interventions reported statistically significant or long‐term results. Successful designs engaged parents, the school system, and adolescents through mHealth and eHealth apps.Keywords: adolescent obesity, mHealth, obesity

Rationale

Obesity is an international problem that has tripled since 1975 [1], but it is particularly prevalent in the United States. While the population of the U.S. only comprises 3.3% of the world population, it houses 7.3% of the world′s overweight population [2]. Adolescent obesity is one of the fastest growing conditions in the world, and it is highly prevalent among adolescents from diverse and economically challenged households [3, 4]. In the United States, the prevalence ranged from 19.7% to 22.2% in age groups 6–19 between 2017 and 2020 among all adolescents, [5]. The prevalence of adolescent obesity decreased between 2011 and 2014 as the head of the household′s level of education increased [6]. Prevalence of adolescent obesity increased as household income decreased [6]. Exceptions for these rules were seen for non‐Hispanic black females (no statistical difference between income), and among males, there was a lower obesity prevalence in the highest income group for non‐Hispanic Asian youths [6].

There is a high correlation between children with obesity and both adolescents and adults with obesity. About one half of school‐age children with obesity become adolescents with obesity; about 80% of adolescents with obesity will become adults with obesity; about 70% of adolescents with obesity will become adults over age 30 with obesity; about 33% of children with obesity become adults with obesity [7, 8]. The odds of developing into an adult with obesity is twice that for children with obesity than for nonchildren with obesity [9].

Adolescent obesity is highly correlated with long‐term health consequences on both mortality and morbidity. Adolescents with obesity develop high blood pressure, dyslipidemia, impaired glucose metabolism, breathing problems (asthma and sleep apnea), Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH), and joint problems [5]. Obesity is also highly correlated with overall cancer increases [10]. Adolescents with obesity develop cardiovascular diseases, increased mortality, premature death, disability, mental health and mood disorders, polycystic ovarian syndrome, and metabolic syndrome [11, 12]. Obesity adversely affects school performance and educational attainment for adolescents due to negative effects that stigmas and discrimination incur on cognitive functioning [13].

There is a worldwide economic burden associated with childhood obesity. There is an increased annual cost of $237.55 per capita as well as an increase of 0.28 days in length of stay in hospitals [13]. This totals to an annual increase of $13.62 billion [14].

Many researchers identified sedentarism and unhealthy diet as causes for adolescent obesity [15, 16]. Screen time has supplanted physical activity, unhealthy food preferences have developed largely due to the advertisements on television, and a combination of automatic eating and overconsumption occurs during TV viewing [16]. These effects were magnified during the pandemic [17]. Nothing short of 60 min per day of physical activity will stem the trend of obesity in adolescents [18].

Telemedicine offers a solution because it extends the clinic without extending the physical plant, and it meets adolescents where they spend large amounts of time (on electronic devices). The World Health Organization (WHO) defines telemedicine as healing at a distance [19]. The WHO defines mHealth as telemedicine delivered through mobile technologies such as a cellular phone [20]. The age of the definition of telemedicine is problematic because it has not kept up with the speed of technological advance. The lines between mobile devices and computers have blurred. Telemedicine includes eHealth interventions such as web applications. Due to the proliferation of smart phones, many web apps can be accessed through a phone. However, many mobile apps cannot be accessed by computer. Both mHealth, eHealth, and video were included because not all people have smart phones. In the United States, 95% of teens have access to a smartphone, and about 45% of them report they are online almost instantly [21]. Due to the prevalence of mobile technology, smartphone apps have the opportunity to extend school‐based programs to encourage healthy diet and physical activity [22]. Additionally, telemedicine might appeal to those struggling with obesity and might feel uncomfortable in a doctor′s office. This offers great opportunity to target population‐based issues, such as adolescent obesity.

A review of applications was published in 2023 [23]. It analyzed the apps in the market and proposed guidelines for the inclusion of user interface design patters as new apps are developed. This review was not a randomized control trial (RCT), but only a review of the literature concerning popular apps from a snapshot in time. It reviewed 17 apps, but it did not evaluate their effectiveness in terms of health outcomes which is a necessary element of a systematic review [24].

A systematic review was published in 2023 that analyzed physical activity surveillance in children and adolescents using mHealth [25]. It analyzed interventions over 15 years, but it also did not analyze effectiveness of the apps as seen in medical outcomes. A large limitation was that the evidence was insufficient to make conclusions due to a knowledge gap. Our systematic literature review addresses the gap in the literature concerning health outcomes and its association with the use of ehealth apps.

Objectives

The purpose of this systematic literature review was to analyze the effectiveness of telemedicine applications in the management of adolescent obesity compared with traditional treatment through an analysis of peer‐reviewed, published articles from the last 10 years. Effectiveness was measured in terms of health outcomes and patient satisfaction.

Methods

The authors of this systematic literature review chose to follow an established protocol [26]. The results were reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyzes (PRISMA, 2020) standard [27].

Eligibility Criteria

Articles were selected according to a set of eligibility criteria. Articles must have been part of a RCT, must have been published in peer‐reviewed academic journals over the last 10 years, must have included adolescents as participants, and must have used apps to monitor obesity or behavior. The RCT was chosen to maximize rigor and minimize bias with the methodologies. The threshold of 10 years was chosen due to the rapidly advancing field of telemedicine. Authors chose to eliminate other systematic reviews to prevent confounding the results, which follows established precedent [28, 29, 30].

Information Sources

Four research databases were queried using the same Boolean search string: PubMed (MEDLINE), the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Web of Science, and Science Direct. Authors chose these databases because they are readily availability to most researchers and the exhaustiveness of their contents. Authors chose to only include published literature to ensure the work had been peer‐reviewed. Authors also queried the trial registry Clinicaltrials.gov to ensure the search did not miss any relevant studies.

Search Strategy

Authors used terms from the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) from the U.S. Library of Medicine, and they created the following Boolean search string: (mhealth OR ehealth OR telemedicine OR apps) AND adolescent AND obesity. “Sedentary lifestyle” was not included as this would limit the search. Authors used similar search strategies in all databases for consistency. Not all databases offered the same filtering tools, so the exact search strategy was not possible.

Selection Process

Following the Kruse Protocol [26], authors searched the four research databases, filtered the results, and screened the abstracts to ensure they were germane to the objective statement. Authors chose the Kruse Protocol because it has been used in 52 other published works from nine countries. The Cochrane Library was searched for similar systematic reviews.

Data Collection Process and Data Items

A standardized Excel spreadsheet was established by the Kruse Protocol. This was used as a data extraction tool. This spreadsheet collects and screens data incrementally. A series of three consensus meetings was used to share observations and ensure consistency of effort [26].

The standardized spreadsheet collected the following data fields in the following steps: the Google Scholar step (date of publication, authors, study title, journal, impact factor study design, key terms, experimental intervention, results, observations); the database filtering step (results with no filters, results after excluding MEDLINE (from all but PubMed), results after inserting the date range, results after applying the other filters, the number of duplicates); the abstract screening step (database source, date of publication, authors, study title, journal, screening decision by each coauthor, notes about rejection, consensus meeting screening decision, reason for rejection, kappa statistic calculations); the analysis step (database source, date of publication, authors, study title, participants, experimental intervention, results compared with the control, medical outcomes, study design, sample size, bias observed, effect size reported, country of origin, statistics used, user satisfaction reported, facilitators to adoption, barriers to adoption, strength of evidence, quality of evidence, and observations noted by each coauthor). Cochran′s Q was checked for all studies to ensure the results reported surpassed the rejection criteria. Because Zotero was used to track references, it checked to ensure all articles selected had not been retracted.

Study Risk and Reporting Bias Assessment

Authors used the Johns Hopkins Nursing Evidence Based Practice tool (JHNEBP) to record observations of bias and assessed the quality of each study [31] The JHNEBP tool assesses Strength of Evidence as levels I–V and quality of evidence as Levels A–C. Strength of Evidence includes Level I studies such as RCTs (with controls and randomization), Level II studies such as quasi‐experimental (a control group, but no randomization), Level III studies such as observational, qualitative, or other nonexperimental methods, and Levels IV and V such as opinions. Authors chose only RCTs, so they eliminated all levels except Level I. The quality of the evidence is based on the consistency of results and sufficient sample size. Authors chose to eliminate Level C studies. Authors considered the instances of bias in how to interpret the results because bias can limit external validity [32].

Effect Measures

Authors preferred to use Cohen′s d as the measure of effect; however, other measures of effect were recorded such as Wald′s w, beta, F, and Odds Ratio [33, 34].

Synthesis Methods

Authors conducted a thematic analysis on the observations to identify themes [35]. When they identified the same or similar observation, they identified the set as a theme. They tabulated themes into affinity matrices for further analysis.

Additional Analyzes and Certainty Assessment

Authors transformed, combined, and tabulated effect sizes [33, 34]. They performed a certainty assessment by combining the narrative analysis with the effect sizes. The frequency of observations were tabulated. Frequency of themes is not intended to imply importance: The frequency simply provides the probability of encountering the theme in the group of articles for analysis.

The authors screened and filtered the original 3662 search results. They filtered the group and screened abstracts down to 17 studies that fit the objective statement. The study selection process is illustrated in Figure 1.

Study Characteristics

Following the PRISMA checklist, authors extracted data fields for PICOS (participants, intervention, results comparison to the control, medical outcomes, and study design); see Table 1. This was organized to summarize study characteristics in a manner established by the literature [29, 53, 54]. Studies were sorted by year and then by author. Of the 17 studies analyzed over the 10‐year period, four were from 2023 [36, 37, 38, 39], three were from 2022 [40, 41, 42], two were from 2021 [43, 44], two were from 2020 [45, 46], two were from 2019 [47, 48], one was from 2018 [49], two were from 2017 [50, 51], and one was from 2016 [52]. All 17 studies involved adolescents. One of the studies also included children as young as 8 [50], and one included an adult as old as 21 [43]. Most included mHealth interventions. Studies were generated from nine different countries, but a large percentage came from the USA. All 17 included RCTs, but one also included a qualitative study, so it was classified as mixed methods.

Risk of Bias and Reporting of Bias in Studies

In all studies, authors noted a risk of bias. All but one study was performed in only in one region of one country, and the included one region and a small island. The most often noted bias was selection bias. In 15/17 studies (88%), samples were mostly one sex or race [36, 37, 38, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 50, 51, 52]. The JHNEBP quality assessment tool identified 100% of the studies as strength Level I and quality Level A. These are the highest strength and quality outcomes the tool measures. Authors suspect the reason for this high assessment was due to the rigor of the RCT, the adequate sample size, and the consistency of the results.

In addition to the JHNEBP, authors made note of bias identified within each study. The types of bias identified were: Selection bias (17/17, 10%) and sample bias (15/17, 88%). The dangers of selection and sample bias in research are in external validity. Researchers and practitioners must carefully examine their population along with the sample in the study to ensure each study results can be appropriately applied to their population. The dangers this imposes on research is on both internal and external validity.

Cohen′s Q was evaluated for each study to ensure all results were below the rejection criteria. Because none of the articles selected were of low quality, Cohen′s Q was satisfactory indicating no heterogeneity observed in the results of individual articles.

Results of Individual Studies

Three interventions (mHealth, eHealth, and video call) identified positive results in areas of physical and mental health. The interventions (17/17) were consistently identified as easy to use and therefore acceptability was high. All interventions noted positive results, but not all were statistically significant (5/17) [38, 43, 49, 50, 51]. In these cases, authors noted the interventions offered additional options for those who were technically inclined or requested a technically based intervention, but because the improvements were not statistically better than traditional care, there was not an urgency to change. Results of Syntheses

Only two studies reported an effect size, so a meta‐analysis was not possible.

Additional Analysis and Certainty of Evidence

Authors created four affinity matrices to conduct additional analysis of the data extracted. Authors sorted each matrix from most often cited to least. Authors did not intend to imply importance with this exercise. It only states the statistical probability of each theme occurring in the literature from the last 10 years.

Interventions

Three intervention themes were identified in the literature: mHealth (82%, 14/17), eHealth (12%, 2/17), and video call (6%, 1/17). These three interventions qualify under the definition of telemedicine.

Results (Compared With the Control)

Authors summarized the results of the intervention compared with the control group in Table 3. Seven themes plus three individual observations were identified and tallied for a total of 41 occurrences of observations. The theme “Improvement in health” was identified the most often (14/41, 34%) [38, 39, 41, 42, 44, 46, 50]. This theme included weight loss, decrease in waist and hip circumference, reduced cholesterol, improved low‐density lipoprotein, improved triglycerides, improved fasting blood sugar, increase in strength, increase in muscle mass, increase in agility, reduced body fat, improvement in nutrition, and reduced triceps and subscapular skinfold thickness. Multiple observations were noted in the same study for this theme. In five studies (8/41, 20%), there was improvement, but it was not statistical different from the control group [37, 38, 42, 43, 49, 50, 51]. Four of the five studies involved mHealth and the other involved a video call. Weight, BMI, physical activity, and sedentary time were measured. The remainder of the findings comprise less than 50% of the occurrences. These can be found in Table 3. The studies that reported “Improvement in psychological impacts” all used mHealth as the intervention. Studies reporting “decrease in BMI” all used mHealth as the intervention, and the differences in BMI were not statistically significant. Studies that reported a “decrease in zBMI” used mHealth as the intervention, and in all but one, findings were statistically significant. The study that identified a decrease in waist and hip circumference used mHealth. In the two studies that identified an increase in physical activity one study used eHealth while the other used mHealth. The studies that noted a change in eating behaviors also used mHealth as the intervention, but neither study noted statistically significant differences in weight or BMI. Of the two studies that reported an increase in treatment adherence one used mHealth while the other used eHealth. One observation could not be fit into themes: increase in health knowledge [40].

Medical Outcomes

Table 3 summarizes the medical outcomes observed. In 2018, it was established that systematic reviews should identify medical outcomes to differentiate themselves from scoping reviews [24]. Medical outcomes are differentiated from results. For instance, increased physical exercise over the control group is a successful result, but a decrease in cholesterol over the control is both a result and a medical outcome. Four themes plus “not reported” were identified and tallied for a total of 32 occurrences of observations. These four themes were the same observations as the “results compared with the control group” section. The remaining themes will be mentioned but not described. The theme, “Improvement in health” was identified in 16/32 occurrences (50%) [37, 38, 39, 41, 42, 44, 46, 50]. Six studies did not report medical outcomes (6/32, 19%) [36, 43, 45, 47, 49, 50, 51]. An improvement in psychological impacts was observed in 4/32 occurrences (13%) across three studies [40, 42, 46]. A decrease in BMI [37, 48, 50] and zBMI [37, 48, 52] were identified in 3/32 occurrences (9%).

Patient Satisfaction

Observations of patient or user satisfaction were collected. Across the 17 studies, all 17 reported that acceptability of the intervention was high. One study also identified “ease of use” which may explain the high acceptability [37].

Facilitators

Table 3 summarizes the facilitators identified in the literature for the adoption of mHealth, eHealth, or video calls for the management of adolescent obesity. Four themes, eight individual observations, and “not reported” were identified for a total of 31 occurrences. “Improved health” was a combination of physical and mental health categories, and it occurred in 9/31 occurrences (29%) [39, 40, 41, 42, 44, 46, 50]. These studies all implemented mHealth interventions. Four studies identified mHealth interventions as beneficial over traditional care [39, 41, 46, 48]. This theme included observations that it was medically effective, clinically effective to reduce BMI and zBMI, and that a multidisciplinary approach may show better performance over a single‐intervention approach. The latter combined the mobile app with education, computer based therapy, and aerobic exercise [41]. Four articles did not report any facilitators [43, 45, 49, 51]. These studies used one of three interventions. Three studies offered mHealth as an alternative intervention for those who want it [38, 39, 40], but results were only statistically significant in two of the three studies [39, 40]. Two articles noted the mHealth intervention changed behaviors which resulted in reduced weight as well as waist and hip circumference [39, 41]. Two studies noted only a short‐term benefit to the mHealth intervention [38, 52]. The improvements were noted in both weight and zBMI, but these improvements were not sustained over time. Acceptability was only overtly noted in one study as a facilitator due to its ease of use [37], but user acceptability was noted across all studies. Five other individual observations were unable to be fit into themes: greater physical activity [36], improved cost of intervention over traditional care [48], improved food addiction symptoms [42], improved retention [48], and direct and positive impact on obesity [40].

Barriers

Authors summarized the barriers identified in the literature in Table 3. Eight studies did not identify barriers [36, 39, 40, 41, 44, 46, 47, 48]. Seven studies did not identify statistically significant results over traditional care [37, 38, 42, 43, 49, 50, 51]. Six observations could not be fit into themes: conflicts with other activities [45], inconvenient [45], labor intensive [37], no long‐term benefit [52], no perceived relevance [45], and takes up too much time [45].

Discussion

Summary of Evidence

Through a filtered search of four research databases, three telemedicine interventions (mHealth, eHealth, and video call) were identified in 17 studies from nine countries that met the objective statement. Seven mHealth [39, 40, 41, 44, 46, 48, 52] and two eHealth [36, 45] interventions reported statistically significant results in the areas of BMI [48], zBMI [52], weight loss [39, 41], waist and hip circumference [41], physical activity [38], cholesterol [41], low‐density lipoprotein [41], triglycerides [41], fasting blood sugar [41], symptoms of food addiction [42], health knowledge [40], muscle mass [44], agility [44], body fat [44], behavior change [42, 46], strength [44], adherence to weight loss programs [45, 47], life perspectives [46], global lifestyles [46], nutrition [46], psychological impacts [40, 42, 46], stress [42], and depression [42].

Adherence to weight control programs was negatively correlated to the weight of the subjects [45], and it increased with an SMS intervention [47]. Adolescents′ displayed greater physical activity when their parents had completed more web‐based content sessions on weight control [36]. A school based program showed a direct and positive impact on increasing health knowledge of adolescents [40]. The only intervention that reported an increase in physical activity was eHealth, and that included involvement and education of parents on adolescent obesity, healthy eating, and positive parenting [36].

Our findings are congruent with another systematic literature review published in 2023: mHealth can positively influence behavior change [23, 42, 46]. Our findings are also consistent in that it found direct and positive results from mHealth interventions [25, 39, 40, 41, 44, 45, 46, 48, 52]. Our research differs in that it also reports the medical outcomes, the positive physical and mental effects, the facilitators, barriers, and user satisfaction, and countries of origin.

Clinicians should look to mHealth and eHealth interventions. They should involve both schools and parents in the intervention. They should recognize that obesity interventions will conflict with other activities. They should be confident that acceptance and usability of developed apps is high. Healthcare administrators should feel confident investing in this technology for their clinicians to treat adolescent obesity. Policy makers should dedicate resources to public campaigns that stress the importance of healthy eating and lifestyle to the adolescents to help build perceived relevance.

Future research should focus on leveraging the successful interventions in terms of BMI and weight loss. Telemedicine offers several solutions, but not all have demonstrated statistically significant results. eHealth has only been successful increasing physical activity by engaging and educating parents on healthy habits and positive parenting [36]. Another shortfall of previous research is achieving long‐term results in BMI and zBMI [37, 52]. Future research should explore ways to achieve long‐term results.

Limitations

One limitation of our systematic literature review was that we limited the timeframe to 10 years. We felt that 10 years was sufficient to capture the essence of research in this area, however, a longer timeframe may have yielded additional interventions with positive and significant results. Another limitation was that we developed our Boolean search string from MeSH terms, but this may have omitted studies that did not index with the MeSH hierarchy. One more limitation was that we focused on studies with strong methodologies (the RCT). Had we included studies with other methodologies (qualitative, etc.) we may have found additional interventions that were effective. Lastly, we could have included gray literature in our study. We chose to omit gray literature because they were not data based.

Conclusion

Telemedicine offers interventions that affect both physical and mental health improvements in adolescent obesity. mHealth appears to offer the most robust results in this area, but not all interventions resulted in long‐term benefits. Successful interventions educate both adolescents and their parents and attempt behavior change to achieve significant and long‐term results.
