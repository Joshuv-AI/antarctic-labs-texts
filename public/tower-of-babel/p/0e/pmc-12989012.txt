Patterns of failure following stereotactic radiosurgery salvage for recurrent high-grade glioma
Authors: Shivani Bindal, Brandon Gonzalez, Antonio Dono, Roy Riascos, Jay-Jiguang Zhu, Sigmund Hsu, Nitin Tandon, Angel Blanco, Yoshua Esquenazi, Mark Amsbaugh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesRe-irradiation with stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is an increasingly used treatment for recurrent high-grade glioma (HGG). We analyzed the patterns of failure after salvage SRS to better identify which patients would most benefit.MethodsWe reviewed all patients who received SRS for recurrent HGG over 12 years at our institution. Pattern of failure was defined by the distance of recurrence from the SRS isodose line. Overall-survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and local-failure-free survival (LFS) were estimated using Kaplan-Meier methods, and regression model analysis was performed to analyze clinical predictors of failure.Results146 patients with HGG underwent SRS treatment to 410 target lesions. The median target volume was 3.3 cm3 and median total dose was 18 Gy. Sixty-one (41.8%) patients developed progression following SRS. PFS at 6 and 12 months were 65.8% and 37.3% respectively. Median OS was 12.4 months from salvage SRS. There were 113 (79.5%) local failures, 2 (1.4%) distant, and 27 (19%) concomitant local and distant failures. LFS at 1 and 2 years was 65.1% and 40.4%, respectively. Non-white race (HR = 0.60), SRS dose ≥ 20 Gy (HR = 0.34), and KPS ≥ 70 (HR = 0.44) were associated with longer time to local failure, and age ≥ 60 years (HR = 2.29) was associated with shorter time to local failure. Non-white race (HR = 5.55) and SRS dose ≥ 20 Gy (HR = 5.62) were associated with more distant failure.ConclusionThe dominant pattern of failure remains local and in-field despite conformal dose distribution with SRS and infiltrative tumor histology. This supports the role of reirradiation with SRS in well-selected patients with recurrent HGG.Keywords: Glioblastoma, Malignant glioma, Radiosurgery

Introduction

High grade gliomas (HGG) are the most common primary malignant brain neoplasms and confer a poor prognosis with high rates of progression following treatment. Standard of care for newly diagnosed HGG consists of maximal safe resection followed by external beam radiotherapy with concurrent temozolomide (TMZ) and adjuvant TMZ with or without adjuvant tumor treating fields [1, 2]. Despite standard treatment, almost all patients experience progression at the initial site of disease.

The treatment of recurrent disease represents a management challenge with the majority of patients having received previous surgical resection and high dose radiotherapy, often near critical regions. The standard of care for recurrent HGG is poorly defined so far, with options including repeat surgical resection, salvage systemic treatment, and reirradiation, with the latter limited by the cumulative brain radiation dose. Despite these concerns, recent studies have shown promising results for consideration of reirradiation in recurrent HGG [3, 4].

The use of stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) as a salvage treatment for recurrent HGG has been studied in recent years, though the diffuse, infiltrative nature of HGG raises questions about its efficacy in disease control. Nevertheless, recent studies suggest improvement in outcomes with acceptable toxicity profile [5–9]. Studies evaluating patterns of failure after SRS for recurrent HGG have been published with small patient cohorts with variable results, suggesting that a specific subset of patients benefit the most from this therapy [10, 11]. In this study, we analyzed the patterns of failure and outcomes of salvage SRS for recurrent HGG at our institution to identify demographic and clinical factors that could help predict the greatest benefit from SRS.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study is an analysis of patients treated using SRS from January 2008 to May 2020 at a single academic institution. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) histologically confirmed diagnosis of HGG, defined as World Health Organization (WHO) Grade III and IV gliomas, following the 2016 WHO Classification of Tumors of the CNS, (3) at least one documented recurrence following initial standard of care treatment, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation, based on the Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) criteria, and (4) single fraction salvage Gamma Knife (GK) SRS performed at recurrence [12, 13]. Patients without histological diagnosis or missing data for independent variables of interest were excluded from the study. All patients were evaluated for further treatment at recurrence through a multidisciplinary conference including neuro-oncology, neurosurgery, neuroradiology, and radiation oncology.

Study data were collated using a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) web-based application. Data collection and analyses of de-identified data were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of our institution and after study approval by the Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects (HSC-MS-17-0917).

Radiosurgery treatment technique

All patients were treated with Gamma Knife SRS (Perfexion Unit). Patients underwent placement of a Leksell G stereotactic head frame with the use of local anesthetic or general anesthesia if indicated. A high-resolution thin cut T1-weighted magnetic resonance image (MRI) with gadolinium contrast agent administration was obtained after frame placement, and the target volume was selected after deliberation between the radiation oncologist and neurosurgeon to target areas of enhancement as a marker of recurrence. Treatment was delivered in all instances in a single fraction. Patients were discharged the same day and follow-up imaging was performed every 2 months until criteria for tumor recurrence or progression was met.

Study variables

Tumor and clinical characteristics included glioma grade (III or IV), isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation status, extent of tumor resection (gross-total resection (GTR), near-total resection (NTR), subtotal resection (STR), biopsy without resection (BWR), thermal ablation (TA)) and radiosurgery dose selection. Karnofsky performance status (KPS) was determined at time of initial glioma diagnosis as well as at time of SRS. The lesion target enhancing volume (cm3) was contoured by the treating radiation oncologist. Patient race/ethnicity was self-reported (non-Hispanic White, Hispanic, Black, Asian). The use of bevacizumab at any time since diagnosis was noted, if available. Treatment toxicity was classified and graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.

The location of failure following SRS was also assessed by RANO criteria and defined according to the distance from the prescription isodose line on contrast-enhanced T1 MRI. Local failures included in-field (within the prescription isodose line), marginal (< 2 cm), and regional (2–5 cm), and distant failures were > 5 cm [9, 11, 14] Imaging studies were loaded within the Leksell GammaPlan (LGP) software and scored by overlaying isodose lines from original treatment. Failures were adjudicated by two reviewers. Local failure-free survival was defined as time from SRS to local failure or last follow-up. Progression-free survival was defined as time from SRS to date of disease progression or last follow-up, and overall survival was defined as time from SRS to date of death or last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and local failure-free survival (LFS) from salvage SRS were analyzed at the patient level and characterized graphically with Kaplan-Meier survival curves. Failure-related outcomes were analyzed at the lesion (target) level, as some patients had multiple treated lesions. Because lesions within the same patient are not statistically independent, lesion-level models incorporated intra-patient clustering using multilevel mixed-effects regression with a random intercept for participant, which accounts for within-patient correlation and unmeasured patient-level heterogeneity.

In lesion-level longitudinal analyses including all treated lesions, failure and failure location were modeled using multilevel mixed-effects parametric and ordered logistic regressions, respectively, which included a random participant intercept. Failure location was modeled as an ordinal outcome using a multilevel mixed-effects ordered logistic regression (random participant intercept) and was restricted to lesions in patients with SRS failure. Local failure was additionally analyzed with a Fine-Gray competing-risks regression model, including categorical dose (SRS dose < 20 and ≥ 20) and age (age < 60 and ≥ 60 years) predictors at SRS. For Fine–Gray competing-risks regression models, the proportional sub-distribution hazards assumption was assessed using graphical diagnostics (log–log plots) and by testing covariate-by-time interaction terms. Patients lost to follow-up were removed from the analysis, and analyses were performed using complete-case methods without data imputation. Analyses were performed in Stata (Version 17.0, StataCorp, LLC, College Station, Texas).

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 146 patients treated with single fraction salvage SRS for HGG were included in the study. A summary of patient demographics is shown in Table 1. The median age of patients was 58 years (range 20 to 83 years) and 60 years (range 21–84) at time of diagnosis and time of SRS, respectively. There were 83 males (56.9%) and 63 females (43.2%). The majority of patients were diagnosed with histologic WHO Grade IV gliomas (92.5%, 135/146 patients). The IDH-mutation status was wild-type in 94 patients (64.4%), IDH-mutant in 15 patients (10.3%), and unknown in 37 patients (25.3%). Of the patients included, 71.9% identified as non-hispanic white, 17.2% as hispanic, 7.5% as black, and 3.4% as asian. The median Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score at time of SRS treatment was 60 (range 20–90). With regards to the resective surgery, a gross total resection was achieved in 58 patients (39.7%), a near total resection in 17 patients (11.6%), subtotal resection in 64 patients (43.8%), biopsy without resection in 5 patients (3.4%), and thermal ablation in 2 patients (1.4%). The median time from diagnosis to salvage SRS treatment was 12.2 months (range 2.99 to 175.8 months). Of the patients for whom data on the use of bevacizumab was collected, 95 (65.1%) received bevacizumab during their overall treatment course, 12 (8.2%) did not receive bevacizumab, and 39 (26.7%) are unknown.

Treated lesions

In our cohort of 146 patients, 410 targets were treated with SRS. A single focus of recurrence was treated in 53 patients (36.3), 2 foci in 27 patients (18.5%), 3 in 28 patients (19.2%), 4 in 17 patients (11.6%) and greater than 4 lesions in 21 patients (14.4%). The median number of lesions treated at each session was 2 (range 1–11 lesions). The median number of times patients underwent treatment was 1 (range 1–5). Of all treated lesions, 10.7% had IDH mutations (44 out of 410 targets in 15 patients).

The median enhancing tumor targeted for each focus was 3.3 cm3 (range 0.0–85.5). The median target volume treated was less than 2.5 cm3 in 190 lesions (46.34%), between 2.5 cm3 and 7.0 cm3 in 86 lesions (20.98%), and greater than 7.0 cm3 in 134 lesions (32.68%). The median total dose was 18 Gy (range 10–24 Gy) to the 50% isodose line. The SRS dose was less than 18 Gy for 174 treated lesions (42.44%), between 18 Gy and 20 Gy for 135 lesions (32.93%), and greater than 20 Gy for 101 lesions (24.63%). A summary of the characteristics of treated lesions is shown in Table 2.

Patterns of failure

The median follow-up after salvage SRS was 8.2 months. A total of 61 (41.8%) patients continued with progression following first SRS treatment, with a median PFS of 8.28 months (95% confidence interval [CI]: 6.9–10.3). PFS at 6 and 12 months were 65.8% (95% CI: 57.4–73.0) and 37.3% (95% CI: 29.3–45.3), respectively (Fig. 1). Median OS from time of diagnosis was 26.6 months (95% CI: 23.8–34.4) and 12.4 months (95% CI: 10.2–15.4) following first SRS (Fig. 2). Median OS for patients with and without progression after first SRS treatment was 16.6 months (95% CI: 13.0-22.1) and 9.33 months (95% CI: 6.8–12.2) respectively. One patient was lost to follow-up and removed from further analysis.

Of the total number of lesions treated (N = 410), 142 treated lesions had imaging demonstrating failure. There were 113 local failures (79.5%), 2 distant failures (1.4%), and 27 concomitant local and distant failures (19%). Of the local-only failures, 60 (53.1%) were considered in-field, 51 (45.1%) were marginal, and 2 (1.8%) were regional. A summary of the patterns of failure is shown in Table 3, and exemplar images are shown in Figs. 3 and 4. Median time to local failure after first SRS treatment was 17.1 months (95% CI: 13.3–24.1). Local failure-free survival at 1 and 2 years were 65.1% (95% CI 55.1–73.4) and 40.4% (95% CI: 28.8–51.7) respectively (Fig. 5).

Predictors of failure

Table 4. shows the clinical and demographic variables predicting local failure following salvage SRS treatment. Age ≥ 60 years at SRS (HR = 2.29, 95% CI: 1.18–4.48) was significantly associated with local failure, with a median of 4.8 months to local failure in patients ≥ 60 years vs. 6.14 months in patients < 60 years. KPS ≥ 70 at time of first SRS (HR = 0.44, 95% CI: 0.24–0.80) and non-white race (HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.38–0.97) was also significantly associated with local failure (median time to local failure of 11.3 months in patients with KPS ≥ 70 vs. 3.6 months in patients KPS < 70, and 10.6 months in non-white race vs. 4.6 months in those who identified as white). SRS dose ≥ 20 Gy (HR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.16–0.76) was an additional predictor of local SRS failure with longer median time to failure (9.7 months vs. 3.7 months in those who received 18–19 Gy and 6.4 months in those who received < 18 Gy). After adjusting for age, race, and KPS at recurrence, SRS dose ≥ 20 Gy remained significantly associated with longer time to failure (HR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.17–0.75). The cumulative incidence of local failure was highest amongst patients ≥ 60 years treated with an SRS dose < 20 Gy (Fig. 6). The use of bevacizumab was not associated with local failure (P > 0.05).

Sub-analysis in patients with confirmed IDH wild-type mutation status

Sub-group analysis was performed on patients in whom IDH wild-type mutation status was confirmed (i.e. excluding patients with IDH mutation or unknown). A total of 93 patients with 248 targets were included. The median target enhancing volume was 3.15 cm3 and median total dose was 18 Gy to the 50% isodose line. Forty-eight (51.6%) patients developed progression following SRS. PFS at 6 and 12 months were 64.1% and 40% respectively. Median OS from time of diagnosis was 22.2 months and 12.94 months following first SRS. Analysis of failure pattern demonstrated 68 (77%) local failures (53% in-field, 44% marginal, 2% regional), 2 (2%) distant failures, and 18 (21%) concomitant local and distant failures. Local failure-free survival at 1 and 2 years was 42.2% and 15.6%, respectively.

Analysis of clinical and demographic variables predicting local failure following salvage SRS treatment found age < 60 years (HR = 0.37) and KPS at time of first SRS ≥ 70 (HR = 0.38) to be associated with longer time to local failure. Median time to local failure was 7.4 months in patients ≥ 60 years vs. 9.6 months in patients < 60 years and 17.4 months in patients with KPS ≥ 70 vs. 5.8 months in patients with KPS < 70. SRS dose, use of bevacizumab, and race were not statistically significant. Additionally, there were no clinical and demographic variables found to be statistically significantly associated with salvage SRS failure location.

Discussion

The management of recurrent HGG remains a challenge with no single established standard of care. Analysis of treatment failures for HGG have shown that up to 80% of recurrences occur within 2 cm of the tumor margins, supporting the use of focal re-irradiation modalities [15, 16]. Prior studies examining the use of re-irradiation as a salvage treatment for recurrent HGG have suggested that SRS is safe and effective in this patient population [3, 15]. However, the evidence in favor of the use of SRS is weighted in mostly retrospective studies with small, heterogeneous patient populations, making it difficult to determine which patients are most likely to benefit [10, 11]. In the present study, we analyzed a larger patient cohort with more treated lesions than any prior published study. We found that local failure was the most frequent pattern of recurrence, substantiating the prior evidence that most patients treated with SRS for recurrent HGG are SRS responsive. Additionally, due to our larger cohort, we were able to analyze factors that predicted longer time to local failure (SRS dose, age, KPS score, and race), and a more distant failure pattern (race and SRS dose).

Our analysis showed that SRS dose was a significant predictor of local failure when accounting for other variables, with longer time to failure for patients treated with greater than or equal to 20 Gy compared to patients treated with a dose less than 20 Gy. While higher re-irradiation doses generate concern for brain toxicity, current data suggests a relatively quick recovery capacity of normal brain to re-irradiation, supporting the use of biologically equivalent total dose normalized to 2 Gy/fraction (EQD2) of up to 120 Gy, especially for small lesions far from eloquent areas [3, 17]. In our cohort, no grade 4 or 5 toxicity was seen in any patients.

In addition to SRS dose, we found that age, KPS score at recurrence, and race were significant predictors of local failure following SRS, with patients over 60 years of age, non-Hispanic white race, and KPS score < 70 associated with shorter time to local failure. Patients over the age of 60 treated with lower doses faired the worst. Ene et al. similarly found that older patients had shorter time to failure after SRS for recurrent HGG and proposed that this was due to the different molecular signature of HGG in older patients vs. younger patients [11]. Although these factors are not modifiable, patients who meet these criteria may be candidates for SRS dose escalation to counteract their predilection for shorter time to local failure. Additionally, a recent study by Zhang et al. found that the combined use of bevacizumab with SRS can improve survival in patients with recurrent HGG compared to monotherapy [18]. Thus, patients at risk of shorter time to local failure may benefit from combined SRS therapy with systemic treatments, such as bevacizumab.

In evaluating the overall pattern of failure, our study showed that non-White race and SRS dose greater than or equal to 20 Gy were associated with more distant failure pattern. While prior studies have noted that a more distant failure pattern suggests SRS resistance, these findings may reflect better local control in this group of patients. Furthermore, the total number of lesions with a distant failure pattern alone was very small (1.4%) in our patient cohort, thus suggesting most patients are SRS sensitive. Due to the overall small number of patients with distant failure in our study, a larger patient population would be needed to clarify this.

Sub-group analysis of IDH wild-type only patients re-demonstrated local failure as the most frequent pattern of recurrence, thus demonstrating SRS responsiveness in this patient cohort. Age and KPS score remained significant predictors of local failure, and while SRS dose was no longer a statistically significant predictor, the trend remained for longer median time to failure for patients treated with higher dose. This sub-group analysis was limited by a smaller patient cohort which could have resulted in the loss of statistical significance seen.

Finally, this analysis is limited by the retrospective, single-institution nature of the patient cohort, where patient selection may affect the outcomes of overall survival and progression-free survival. Additionally, race/ethnicity has been shown previously to impact survival in patients with HGG; thus, this factor is likely impacted by unmeasured confounders that limit interpretation of predictive value in our cohort [19]. Our study is also limited by the lack of a matched control group as well as an analysis of molecular characteristics and mutations associated with failure patterns.

Conclusion

Re-irradiation with SRS for patients with progression of HGG is safe and effective in well selected patients. In addition to several non-modifiable risk factors, SRS dose is a modifiable risk factor for local failure, with our data suggesting longer time to local failure in patients receiving greater than or equal to 20 Gy. Patients who can tolerate higher doses may have higher rates of local control and improved overall survival and progression-free survival. Overall, the majority of failures are local and in-field despite conformal SRS despite concerns of the infiltrative nature of glial neoplasms, supporting the role of this treatment in the care of these complex patients.

Study data were collated using a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) web-based application. Data collection and analyses of de-identified data were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of our institution and after study approval by the Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects (HSC-MS-17-0917).
