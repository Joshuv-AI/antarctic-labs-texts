Pioneering microbial synthesis of gangliosides in the filamentous fungus Ashbya gossypii
Authors: Javier F. Montero-Bullón, Javier Martín-González, Rodrigo Ledesma-Amaro, Alberto Jiménez, Rubén M. Buey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Gangliosides are essential glycosphingolipids critical in neurodevelopment and cell signaling. Traditionally sourced from animal tissues, their production raises ethical concerns and faces challenges in scalability and cost. Chemoenzymatic methods have emerged as alternatives but lack flexibility and broad industrial applicability of microbial systems. However, complete microbial biosynthesis remains challenging due to the complexity of reconstructing the biosynthetic pathway in non-native hosts. We report the first successful complete microbial synthesis of gangliosides by engineering the industrial filamentous fungus Ashbya gossypii. Using modular metabolic engineering, we heterologously expressed human and yeast enzymes to reconstruct a functional ganglioside biosynthetic pathway. Pathways for producing activated N-acetylneuraminic acid, lactosylceramide, and sialylated intermediates were integrated, yielding GM3 and GD3 at milligram-per-liter levels. These titers were further enhanced by introducing a heterologous Leloir pathway for galactose metabolism. This work represents a foundational advance in microbial glycoengineering, offering a scalable, animal-free microbial platform for ganglioside production with broad applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13068-025-02697-4.

Introduction

Gangliosides are a subclass of glycosphingolipids that contain one or more sialic acid residues attached to their oligosaccharide chains [1] (Fig. 1). These molecules are crucial components of vertebrate cell membranes, particularly in neural tissues, where they are involved in cell signaling, neurodevelopment, immune response modulation, and have also been reported as tumor-associated antigens [2]. Among these molecules, the predominant gangliosides are GM3 and GD3, which constitute around 50% of the total lipid-bound sialic acid in most non-neuronal tissues [3]. In chordates, ganglioside biosynthesis is a tightly regulated process involving a series of glycosyltransferase-mediated reactions that assemble the characteristic glycosphingolipid structure (Fig. 1A). This biosynthetic pathway is built upon a conserved core shared by all glycosphingolipids: a ceramide backbone composed of a sphingoid base—formed by the condensation of palmitoyl-CoA and serine—and an amide-linked fatty acid. The ceramide moiety serves as the hydrophobic anchor for the subsequent stepwise addition of monosaccharides, which define the structural and functional diversity of gangliosides.

The glycan assembly begins with the formation of glucosylceramide (GlcCer), catalyzed by UDP-glucose:ceramide glucosyltransferase, which transfers a glucose unit to the ceramide backbone. This is followed by the action of beta-1,4-galactosyltransferase (β4GalT), which transfers a galactose from UDP-galactose to GlcCer, generating lactosylceramide (LacCer), a key precursor for complex glycosphingolipids including gangliosides (Fig. 1B). GlcCer and LacCer are evolutionarily conserved and widely distributed across eukaryotes—including animals, plants, and fungi—where they function as integral components of cellular membranes and signaling platforms. However, LacCer has not been detected in Ashbya gossypii, the microbial host organism employed in this study. This absence may be attributed to the species’ limited capacity for galactose utilization, as previously reported in a genome-wide metabolic analysis [6]. This metabolic constraint underscores the need to introduce the LacCer biosynthetic branch as part of the engineering strategy. Consequently, GlcCer serves as the starting endogenous intermediate in our approach to reconstruct the full ganglioside biosynthetic pathway in A. gossypii.

The production of sialylated gangliosides such as GM3 and GD3 is unique to chordates and relies on the availability of N-acetylneuraminic acid (NANA) in its activated form, CMP-NANA. The NANA biosynthetic pathway involves three key steps: (i) UDP-N-Acetyl-α-D-glucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc) is converted to N-acetyl-D-mannosamine 6-phosphate by UDP-N-acetylglucosamine 2-epimerase/N-acetylmannosamine kinase (GNE); (ii) N-acetyl-D-mannosamine 6-phosphate is then transformed into N-acetylneuraminic acid by N-acetylneuraminic acid synthase (NANS); and, finally, (iii) NANA is activated to CMP-NANA by CMP-N-acetylneuraminic acid synthetase (CMAS), enabling its use as a donor molecule for sialylation reactions (Fig. 1B).

The first ganglioside in the biosynthetic pathway, GM3, is synthesized by the action of the enzyme GM3 synthase (ST3Gal5), which transfers a single sialic acid residue from CMP-NANA to LacCer. GM3 is a crucial intermediate and serves as the substrate for GD3 synthase (ST8Sia1), which adds an additional sialic acid residue to produce GD3 (Fig. 1B). This sequential addition of sialic acid residues underscores the hierarchical and modular nature of ganglioside biosynthesis, which ultimately leads to the production of more complex gangliosides depending on the specific enzymes expressed in a given tissue or cell type [7, 8].

Traditionally, gangliosides are obtained from animal tissues, such as bovine brain and milk, as these are some of the richest natural sources available [9]. However, the extraction process is hindered by limited availability, high costs, and complex purification procedures [10], all of which pose significant barriers to their broader therapeutic application. In response to the increasing demand for gangliosides, alternative production strategies, primarily chemical and chemoenzymatic methods performed in vitro, have been explored [11–13]. While these approaches offer improved specificity and control, they remain limited in terms of efficiency, scalability, and sustainability when compared to a fully engineered microbial production platform.

Genetically engineered microorganisms and plants have shown promise by successfully producing ganglioside precursors such as sialic acid [14], ceramide [15], GlcCer [16], and LacCer. However, the complete biosynthesis of gangliosides such as GM3 and GD3 in microbes has yet to be achieved. This challenge is likely due to the complexity of glycosyltransferase enzymes and the need to carefully balance lipid metabolism within the cell to support complete ganglioside molecule biosynthesis. Thus, achieving a fully functional microbial ganglioside biosynthesis pathway would represent a significant biotechnological breakthrough, offering a renewable source of high-purity gangliosides while addressing environmental concerns associated with animal-derived sources.

Among microorganisms, filamentous fungi represent a promising platform for ganglioside production due to their distinct metabolic and structural characteristics [17]. Unlike bacterial or yeast systems, filamentous fungi naturally produce high levels of sphingolipids, and their membrane lipid composition closely resembles that of mammalian cells, making them particularly well-suited for producing mammalian-like compounds [18]. Notably, A. gossypii, a filamentous hemiascomycete known as a natural overproducer of riboflavin, is extensively used in large-scale industrial bioprocessing. The fermentation-based production of riboflavin, which has replaced traditional chemical synthesis, serves as a key example of biotechnology’s environmental and economic benefits [19]. Additionally, A. gossypii’s small genome and robust genetic manipulation tools [20] have facilitated the development of engineered strains that have proven effective for the bioproduction of valuable metabolites such as folates [21], lipids [22], gamma-lactones [23] and terpenes [24], further demonstrating the species’ versatility and its potential as a biotechnological host.

Beyond its metabolic capabilities, A. gossypii offers significant advantages in downstream processing [20]. Its filamentous growth morphology simplifies biomass separation, facilitating product recovery, while its ability to utilize low-cost feedstocks like agricultural byproducts and waste cooking oil enhances the economic feasibility of industrial-scale production [25–27]. These attributes, combined with its proven success in fermentation processes [19], make A. gossypii an ideal candidate for scalable production of high-value biomolecules.

In this study, we report, for the first time, the complete microbial biosynthesis of GM3 and GD3 gangliosides in the industrial filamentous fungus A. gossypii. By engineering this fungal host to express genes encoding key mammalian and yeast enzymes, we developed a strain capable of producing a fully functional ganglioside biosynthetic pathway. We characterized the production yields and structural integrity of the fungal-derived gangliosides, demonstrating that they are comparable to their mammalian-derived counterparts. These findings mark a significant achievement in microbial biotechnology and establish a novel platform for the production of gangliosides for therapeutic, biotechnological, and research purposes.

Results

To achieve complete biosynthesis of gangliosides GM3 and GD3 in the filamentous fungus A. gossypii, we implemented a modular metabolic engineering strategy (Fig. 1B). This involved the stepwise introduction of expression cassettes carrying heterologous genes grouped into four functional modules, each corresponding to a specific stage of the biosynthetic pathway: synthesis of activated N-acetylneuraminic acid (NANA), production of lactosylceramide (LacCer), and subsequent formation of the monosialylated (GM3) and disialylated (GD3) gangliosides.

Because human-type gangliosides are the most biomedically and biotechnologically relevant forms, our long-term objective is to reconstitute their native biosynthetic route in a non-native host. To this end, we selected human enzymes with well-documented catalytic roles in the corresponding native pathways, based on their substrate specificity and expected compatibility with the structural features of human gangliosides. Where multiple isoforms were available, we consistently selected the full-length variant as the initial candidate for heterologous expression. This enzyme selection strategy was intended to establish a functional baseline and provide a clear proof-of-concept. While not exhaustive, it represents a rational starting point for reconstituting the pathway. We acknowledge that further optimization will benefit from systematic testing of alternative isoforms, engineered variants, and potentially non-human orthologs to improve efficiency and product yields.

Each section below provides detailed results for the functionality of each of these metabolic modules, as well as their effects on precursor and ganglioside yield.

Production of CMP-NANA precursor

To ensure a sufficient supply of NANA for ganglioside biosynthesis, we furnished A. gossypii strains with a pathway that converts endogenous UDP-GlcNAc into CMP-NANA (shadowed in blue in Fig. 1B), the activated form of sialic acid required for downstream ganglioside synthesis. An integrative overexpression cassette (NANA module) was constructed to simultaneously express codon-optimized versions of the human enzymes hGNE, hNANS, and hCMAS [28] (see Materials and methods for details, Table S1 and Table S2). This cassette was targeted to the intergenic region between the ABL210C and ABL211W genes (Fig. S1).

Using UPLC–MS/MS, we monitored UDP-GlcNAc as the initial substrate, NANA as a critical intermediate, and CMP-NANA as the final product in a method developed ad hoc (Fig. S2 and Table S3). In our engineered strain, endogenous UDP-GlcNAc levels remained stable, while NANA-related products were successfully biosynthesized de novo (Fig. 2). Notably, NANA and CMP-NANA accumulated at an approximate 1:2 ratio (Fig. 2, Fig. S3, Fig. S4, and Table S4). Production of LacCer from endogenous GlcCer

A. gossypii’s native glycosphingolipid metabolism supports the production of GlcCer but is unable to convert it into LacCer, which is a crucial step in the ganglioside biosynthetic pathway (shadowed in green in Fig. 1B). We therefore used a biosynthetic module for the production of LacCer (hGAL module), consisting of codon-optimized ORFs encoding the human enzymes UDP-galactose-4-epimerase (hGALE) and beta-1,4-galactosyltransferase 6 (hß4GalT6). hGalE catalyzes the epimerization of UDP-glucose to UDP-galactose [29], while hß4GalT6 catalyzes the synthesis of LacCer through the transfer of galactose from UDP-galactose to GlcCer [30] (Fig. 1B). This integrative cassette was targeted to the intergenic region of ADL397C and ADL397CA genes (Materials and methods, Fig. S1, Table S1, and Table S2).

Additionally, as wild-type A. gossypii strains cannot naturally uptake or metabolize galactose, we introduced the yeast Leloir pathway [31] as an attempt to optimize the assimilation of exogenous galactose (shadowed in grey in Fig. 1B). This was achieved by introducing an integrative cassette (yGAL module) for overexpression of the following Saccharomyces cerevisiae genes: (i) ScGAL2, coding for a galactose permease, which is also able to transport glucose; (ii) ScGAL1, encoding a galactokinase that phosphorylates alpha-D-galactose to yield alpha-D-galactose-1-phosphate; and (iii) ScGAL7, coding for a galactose-1-phosphate uridyl transferase that synthesizes glucose-1-phosphate and UDP-galactose from UDP-D-glucose and alpha-D-galactose-1-phosphate. All these transcriptional units were assembled using recombinogenic flanks for genomic integration between AER348C and AER347W genes (Materials and methods, Fig. S1, Table S1, and Table S2).

Production of monosialo- and disialo-gangliosides

After confirming the availability of CMP-NANA and LacCer in our engineered strains, we introduced an additional overexpression cassette (GX3 module) containing codon-optimized sequences of two human sialyl transferases: hST3Gal5 and hST8Sia1 [32] that enabled the production of gangliosides GM3 and GD3, respectively (highlighted in orange in Fig. 1B). The GX3 module was integrated into the intergenic region between the AGL366C and AGL367WA genes (Materials and methods, Fig. S1, Table S1, and Table S2).

The resulting recombinant strain exhibited robust growth comparable to the wild type. This observation is supported by cell dry weight measurements taken at 120 h, the time point used for metabolite quantification, which showed no significant difference between the ganglioside-producing strain (5.8 ± 1.0 g/L) and the wild-type strain (5.5 ± 0.4 g/L) under identical conditions in MA2 culture medium.

Mass spectrometry analysis confirmed the production of GM3 and GD3 gangliosides, with titers reaching about two hundred micrograms of gangliosides per gram of mycelia (cell dry weight: CDW) when glucose was used as the carbon source (Fig. 4). However, the abundance of GD3 was notably lower than that of GM3, suggesting reduced efficiency in the addition of the second CMP-NANA required to form the disialo-ganglioside. Interestingly, GM3 titers exhibited a consistent upward trend upon galactose supplementation, consistent with activation of the heterologous Leloir pathway and its beneficial effect on ganglioside biosynthesis. A further increase was observed with palmitic acid supplementation, likely due to enhanced ceramide availability. High-resolution accurate mass spectrometry (HRAMS) analysis with MS/MS fragmentation analysis allows detection of GD3 and GM3 pools in A. gossypii and unequivocal identification of both GD3 and GM3 [33], inferred from an MS/MS fragmentation pattern that exhibits sequential neutral loss of the monosaccharide units and the sphingoid core (Fig. S7). Accordingly, it also provided insights into the composition of sphingoid bases, which largely mirrored the natural proportions found in wild-type A. gossypii, with d18:2 (sphingadienine) and dm18:2 (4-methyl-sphingadienine) as the most abundant species (Fig. S8, S9, and Table S4), followed by d18:1 (sphingosine). Notably, gangliosides containing d18:0 (sphinganine) and t18:0 (4-hydroxysphinganine) sphingoid bases were not detected. Similarly, the fatty acyl chains were predominantly hydroxylated, aligning with the typical profile seen in wild-type A. gossypii endogenous sphingolipid profile (Table S4).

Discussion

This study marks the first successful microbial biosynthesis of the gangliosides GM3 and GD3, representing a groundbreaking advancement in the field of microbial biotechnology. By engineering A. gossypii to express key human and yeast enzymes, we have demonstrated the feasibility of producing these complex glycosphingolipids in a microbial host. Despite the modest production yields achieved in this proof-of-concept study, the work establishes a solid foundation for scalable, animal-free ganglioside production. Microbial synthesis enables the use of inexpensive carbon sources, standard fermentation processes, and continuous strain improvement via genetic engineering, offering clear advantages over chemoenzymatic approaches, which rely on costly enzymes, multi-step protocols, and often animal-derived substrates. While microbial systems face challenges such as lower early-stage titers and complex pathway balancing, they present a flexible and convenient alternative. Although a detailed cost or efficiency analysis is beyond the scope of this study, our findings highlight the potential of microbial platforms to overcome limitations inherent to chemoenzymatic synthesis.

The current yields, in the milligrams-per-liter range, are insufficient for industrial application but highlight the opportunity for metabolic engineering to improve production efficiency. A key step toward optimization will involve identifying metabolic bottlenecks, elucidating the mechanisms driving these constraints, and minimizing flux through competing pathways that divert precursors away from the desired biosynthetic route. In this context, our findings highlight notable metabolic dynamics associated with carbon source utilization. Introduction of the Leloir pathway enabled A. gossypii to metabolize galactose, as previously demonstrated in yeast [34, 35]. Interestingly, however, strains harboring the yeast Leloir pathway did not exhibit a significant increase in LacCer accumulation when grown in galactose compared to glucose-containing media (Table S4). In contrast, GM3 titers showed a consistent upward trend upon galactose supplementation, while GD3 levels exhibited a less pronounced and more variable response (Fig. 4), suggesting that additional downstream steps may be limiting. This divergence points to a possible inhibitory mechanism or specific metabolic bottleneck affecting LacCer, which may be rapidly consumed during downstream ganglioside synthesis. Alternatively, the enhanced ganglioside production may reflect a broader reconfiguration of metabolic flux in response to galactose.

Notably, the increase in LacCer levels observed in strains carrying the hGAL module also correlated with a substantial rise in total ceramide-derived lipids, suggesting a metabolic pull effect that may drive increased ceramide biosynthesis to support both LacCer production and GlcCer homeostasis. In this regard, endogenous GlcCer plays a key role in fungal cell physiology, contributing to membrane structure, hyphal development, and cell wall integrity. Similarly, UDP-GlcNAc, a shared precursor for ganglioside biosynthesis, is also essential for fungal cell wall construction via chitin synthesis and glycoprotein formation. The diversion of these native metabolites toward noncanonical pathways through heterologous modules may therefore impose new metabolic demands or trade-offs that could affect fungal physiology.

Although growth and production kinetics were not systematically evaluated across all strains, the ganglioside-producing strain exhibited cell dry weight levels comparable to those of the wild-type at the time of metabolite quantification. This suggests that the introduced genetic modifications did not markedly impair overall physiological performance under the tested conditions. Future studies involving time-resolved profiling of both biomass accumulation and product formation will be essential to elucidate the dynamic relationship between synthesis efficiency and strain fitness, and to inform targeted optimization strategies.

Another promising approach to improving ganglioside production through metabolic engineering is enhancing the availability of CMP-NANA. In this study, the CMP-NANA yields were significantly lower compared to other microorganisms, such as E. coli, where production levels of dozens of grams per liter have been reported [14]. An indicative sign of this limitation is the exhaustion of activated CMP-NANA observed under high-yield conditions, such as when the ganglioside-producing strain is cultured in MA2-Gal-PA medium (Table S4). To address this, increasing the expression of genes involved in NANA activation, such as hCMAS or hGNE, by integrating multiple copies into the fungal genome could boost CMP-NANA production and enhance overall ganglioside synthesis. In addition to precursor limitation, the lower production of GD3 compared to GM3 may also reflect suboptimal localization or reduced activity of the human sialyltransferase hST8Sia1, which is normally Golgi-localized in mammalian systems. Mislocalization or improper membrane association in A. gossypii could hinder its function, and we are currently investigating enzyme localization in follow-up studies.

Similarly, although GM3 and GD3 were successfully extracted from mycelial biomass, we did not perform subcellular localization studies. Based on their biosynthetic pathway in mammalian cells, it is likely that these gangliosides are synthesized in the ER–Golgi network and retained in intracellular membranes rather than secreted. Future work will address the localization, trafficking, and membrane integration of gangliosides in A. gossypii, which may influence both enzyme function and product recovery.

Improving endogenous ceramide biosynthesis, the foundational precursor for all glycosphingolipids, represents another critical area for optimization. Ceramide biosynthesis begins in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and involves four sequential steps catalyzed by cytosol-associated enzymes. In the first and rate-limiting step, serine palmitoyltransferase (SPT) catalyzes the pyridoxal phosphate-mediated condensation of L-serine and palmitoyl-CoA, forming 3-ketosphinganine. This intermediate is subsequently reduced to sphinganine by 3-ketosphinganine reductase, acylated with an activated fatty acid by ceramide synthase to generate dihydroceramide and finally desaturated to ceramide by a desaturase. Notably, SPT activity has been identified as a bottleneck in ceramide biosynthesis [36], making it a prime target for metabolic engineering to boost precursor availability. Enhancing SPT expression or introducing orthologs with higher catalytic efficiency could improve the metabolic flux toward ceramide production, directly supporting increased production of GlcCer and downstream ganglioside intermediates. Furthermore, optimizing ceramide production could involve modulating lipid metabolism to prioritize ceramide synthesis, with palmitic acid, a critical substrate for ceramide formation and the most abundant saturated fatty acid in A. gossypii. In this regard, our previous work demonstrated that the modification of both the fatty acid elongation and desaturation systems of A. gossypii can provide a tunable profiling of fatty acids [37].

Another important area of improvement lies in tailoring the sphingoid base profile of the engineered strains. Currently, the gangliosides produced in A. gossypii reflect the natural sphingolipid profile of the wild-type strain, with d18:2 and dm18:2 as the predominant sphingoid bases. While these profiles are characteristic of fungal systems [38, 39], they differ from those of mammalian cells, where d18:1 predominates [40]. Modifying the sphingoid base composition to better match mammalian profiles would enhance the functional relevance and applicability of fungal-derived gangliosides for biomedical applications and therapeutic use. Our ongoing work demonstrates the feasibility of such modifications, with preliminary unpublished results indicating that the deletion of the desaturase (SLD1) and hydroxylase (SUR4) genes shifts the sphingoid base profile toward d18:1 and non-hydroxylated fatty acids, respectively. While preparing this manuscript, a similar approach was successfully demonstrated in Yarrowia lipolytica to produce human-like sphingoid bases and GlcCer [16].

The potential applications of this platform extend far beyond the biosynthesis of GM3 and GD3 gangliosides. While this study establishes a proof-of-concept for producing these two molecules, the engineered strain demonstrates significant promise for synthesizing a wider array of gangliosides. To diversify the oligosaccharide moiety, co-expression of specific glycosyltransferases could enable the tailored synthesis of 0-, a-, b-, and c-series gangliosides. By incorporating additional glycosyltransferases and sialyltransferases, the platform could potentially produce higher-order gangliosides such as GM1, a molecule with considerable therapeutic relevance [4] Critically, in addition to UDP-Gal, the introduction of its N-acetylated counterpart, UDP-GalNAc, would be essential to meet precursor requirements in a proposed recombinant strain. Notably, the hGalE enzyme introduced in this study catalyzes the epimerization of UDP-N-acetylglucosamine to UDP-N-acetylgalactosamine, simplifying the metabolic pathway—an aspect not depicted in Fig. 1B for clarity. This functionality will facilitate the expansion to potential metabolic pathways as the aforementioned GM1 biosynthesis. In this regard, a recent study described an integrated chemoenzymatic approach to systematically synthesize a comprehensive in vitro library of 65 ganglioside glycans, encompassing complete series with diverse patterns of sulfation and sialylation [41]. These types of strategies highlight the potential to employ similar methods in vivo, expanding the diversity of gangliosides that can be biosynthesized within engineered microbial strains.

In addition to metabolic engineering, further improvements in ganglioside yield and scalability will depend on optimizing cultivation strategies. Key factors include refining culture media composition and fermentation conditions, particularly in controlled bioreactor systems. For instance, galactose supplementation has proven beneficial for enhancing endogenous GlcCer biosynthesis and its subsequent conversion to LacCer, while palmitic acid supplementation supports ceramide formation by increasing precursor availability. Tailoring these inputs in conjunction with process parameters—such as pH, temperature, and aeration—could significantly enhance ganglioside production. Moreover, improvements in downstream processing, including more efficient extraction and purification of glycosphingolipids, will be critical for reducing production costs and improving product quality. Incorporating cost-effective, renewable carbon sources from agroindustrial and domestic residues—such as sugar cane molasses [24] and waste cooking oil [27]—further underscores the potential of A. gossypii as a microbial host. Together, these efforts will advance the development of a robust, scalable platform for the microbial production of gangliosides and other complex sphingolipids.

In summary, we demonstrate that A. gossypii fermentation represents a scalable alternative to current ganglioside production methods. By potentially reducing or eliminating the need for animal-derived tissues and complex chemoenzymatic processes, this fungal platform addresses key limitations related to cost, environmental impact, and ethical considerations. The successful history of A. gossypii in industrial riboflavin production [19] exemplifies the power of microbial engineering to transform the manufacturing of high-value biomolecules. If similar advancements are realized in ganglioside biosynthesis, A. gossypii could significantly reshape the production landscape, making these complex lipids more accessible for biomedical, pharmaceutical, and research applications. This work underscores the broader potential of fungal biotechnology and establishes a compelling framework for future innovation in the biosynthesis of complex lipids.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

D-glucose (Thermo Scientific), D-galactose (Formedium), palmitic acid (Acros organics), tergitol (Merck), bactopeptone (Condalab), yeast extract (Condalab) and myo-inositol (Thermo Scientific) were purchased for media formulations. D-Erythro-sphingosine C17 base, N-heptadecanoyl-D-erythro-sphingosine, N-(2’-(R)-hydroxyheptadecanoyl)-D-erythro-sphingosine, D-glucosyl-b-1,1’-N-heptadecanoyl-D-erythro-sphingosine, D-lactosyl-b-1,1’-N-Heptadecanoyl-D-erythro-sphingosine, and deuterated C18:0 GM3 Ganglioside-d5 from Avanti® Polar Lipids, Inc. (Alabaster, AL, USA) were used as internal standards. Uridine 5’-diphospho-N-acetylglucosamine sodium salt, N-acetyl-neuraminic acid, and cytidine-5’-monophopho-N-acetylneuraminic acid sodium salt were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) as external standards.

A. gossypii strains and growth conditions

A detailed description of all A. gossypii strains used in this study is provided in the Supplemental Materials (Table S1 and Table S2). Strains were cultured in MA media (20 g/L bactopeptone, 2 g/L yeast extract, and 0.6 g/L myo-inositol, adjusted to pH 6.8) with varying carbon sources: MA2 (2% glucose), MA2-Gal (1% glucose and 1% galactose), and MA2-Gal-PA (1% glucose, 0.5% galactose,0.5% palmitic acid, 0.5% tergitol). Cultures (100 mL) were grown in 500-mL Erlenmeyer flasks at 28 °C with shaking at 200 r.p.m. Geneticin (G418) was added to a final concentration of 250 mg/L (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) for the isolation of G418-resistant clones. Mycelia were harvested for analysis by vacuum filtration after 120 h.

Combinatorial assembly of integrative overexpression cassettes

For the overexpression of the heterologous genes, integrative cassettes were assembled using a Golden Gate method as previously described [42]. All the integrative cassettes comprised recombinogenic flanks, a loxP-KanMX-loxP (G418R) marker, and the transcriptional units for gene overexpression (Fig. S1). The NANA module consisted of the codon-optimized sequences for hGNE, hNANS, and hCAMS (see Table S2 for sequence details), controlled by the strong constitutive promoters PSEH1, PTSA1, and PSED1 [43], respectively. The NANA module contained recombinogenic flanks for genomic integration between ABL210C and ABL211W genes (Fig. S1). The hGAL module comprised the codon-optimized sequences (Table S2) for the human enzymes UDP-galactose-4-epimerase (hGALE) and beta-1,4-galactosyltransferase 6 (hß4GalT6) with the strong constitutive promoters PSED1 and PTSA1, respectively. This module was targeted for integration to an intergenic region between ADL397C and ADL397CA genes (Fig. S1). The yGAL module was assembled with the codon-optimized sequences of the yeast GAL1, GAL2, and GAL7 genes (Table S2), under the control of the promoters PSEH1, PTSA1, and PSED1, respectively. The recombinogenic flanks target the intergenic region between AER347W and AER348C. Finally, the GX3 module comprised two transcriptional units (Table S2): a codon-optimized sequence of the human alpha-N-acetylneuraminide alpha-2,8-sialyltransferase isoform 1 (ST8SIA1) under the control of the promoter PSED1, and a codon-optimized sequence of the human lactosylceramide alpha-2,3-sialyltransferase isoform 1 (ST3GAL5) under the control of the promoter PTSA1. The GX3 module contained recombinogenic flanks for genomic integration between AGL366C and AGL367WA genes. The codon-optimized heterologous genes were synthesized by either Integrated DNA Technologies (USA) or Genscript (Netherlands). The correct integration of the modules was confirmed by analytical PCR.

UPLC–MS/MS analysis of UDP-GlcNAc, N-acetyl neuraminic acid and CMP-NANA

10 mg of lyophilized mycelia was suspended in 1 mL of methanol 70% and lysed in benchtop Minilys homogenizer (Bertin Technologies, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France) with 1.4 mm ceramic beads. Supernatant was collected for UPLC–MS/MS analysis by monitorization of specific MS2 transitions in an Orbitrap Q-Exactive Focus mass spectrometer. A biphasic gradient with diethylamine 50 mM / formic acid 50 mM aqueous phase with increasing organic solvent acetonitrile was used for separation in a PGC Hypercarb column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Parallel reaction monitoring was used in MS2 transitions 87.008 < 308.099 for N-acetyl-neuraminic acid, 384.985 < 606.070 for UDP-N-acetyl-glucosamine and 322.045 < 613.140 for CMP-N-acetyl-neuraminic acid. Quantitative values were obtained by comparison of areas under the curve with pure external standards and normalized by the lyophilizate dry weight extracted.

UPLC–MS/MS analysis of ceramides, GlcCer, LacCer and mono- and disialogangliosides

10 mg of lyophilized mycelia was suspended in 1 mL of methanol:CHCl3:H2O (4:1:1) and lysed in a benchtop Minilys homogenizer (Bertin Technologies) with 1.4 mm ceramic beads. Supernatant was collected for UPLC–MS/MS analysis by monitorization of specific MS2 transitions in an Orbitrap Q-Exactive Focus on a biphasic gradient, as previously published [33]. Quantitative values, normalized by lyophilizate weight, were obtained by the ratio of areas under the curve to internal standards with odd-chain fatty acids for ceramides, GlcCer and LacCer, and a stable isotope monosialoganglioside for ganglioside species. Assignation of the hexose moiety motif glucose-galactose is only based on enzymatic specificity of the reconstructed pathway. For gangliosides, fragmentation patterns showed characteristic neutral losses of NANA and two hexose units, along with the detection of the sphingoid base and NANA fragments, unambiguously confirming the identity of the GM3 and GD3 species (Fig. S9). The MS2 transitions utilized for metabolite detection in this study are comprehensively listed in Table S3. Quantitative data for all metabolites measured in both wild-type and engineered strains are presented in Table S4.
