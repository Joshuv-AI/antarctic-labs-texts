Benefits of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists versus pioglitazone for cardio-hepatic outcomes: a territory-wide target trial emulation
Authors: Lanlan Li, David Tak-Wai Lui, Carol Ho-Yi Fong, Wing-Sun Chow, Ivan Chi-Ho Au, Xi Xiong, Brian Hung-Hin Lang, Carlos King-Ho Wong, Chi-Ho Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBoth glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist (GLP1RA) and pioglitazone are associated with cardiovascular and hepatic benefits in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D). However, studies that directly compare their cardio-hepatic effects are lacking. We emulated a target trial to compare their effects on adverse liver and cardiovascular outcomes in T2D patients.MethodsWe adopted an “active comparator, new user” design involving T2D patients newly prescribed GLP1RA or pioglitazone between January 2008 and December 2022. The primary outcomes were major adverse liver outcomes (MALO) and cardiovascular events (MACE), with their individual outcomes and heart failure (HF) as secondary outcomes. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) via intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses.ResultsA total of 8922 patients (N = 4461 each group) were included. Compared to pioglitazone users, GLP1RA users had comparable risks of incident MALO (ITT: HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.66–1.34; PP: HR 1.13, 95% CI 0.60–2.15) and MACE (ITT: HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.80–1.22; PP: HR 1.10, 95% CI 0.77–1.58). The risk of HF was significantly lower in GLP1RA users in ITT analysis (HR 0.65, 95% CI 0.51–0.83). The results were consistent across most subgroup and sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsThe effects on incident major adverse liver and cardiovascular outcomes were comparable between GLP1RA and pioglitazone, although the risk of incident HF was lower with GLP1RA. Treatment decisions for T2D patients at risk of adverse cardio-hepatic events should be individualized, considering HF risk and the need for weight loss, which if present, GLP1RA may be preferred.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-025-02973-5.

Introduction

Patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D), particularly those with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), are at risk of both adverse cardiovascular and hepatic events [1]. Glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonist (GLP1RA) and pioglitazone, when used as monotherapy, have both been shown to possess cardiovascular and hepatic benefits in previous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [2–6]. Consistently, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommends GLP1RA or pioglitazone for treatment of hyperglycemia in T2D adults with MASLD [7, 8]. The European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL), European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) and European Association for the Study of Obesity (EASO) guidelines, however, recommend GLP1RA, but not pioglitazone, in treating T2D among patients with co-existing MASLD 9. The difference in these recent recommendations introduces controversy among clinicians about whether pioglitazone, an oral medication as compared to the injectable GLP1RA, should remain an anti-diabetic agent of choice for patients with co-existing MASLD [10], which constitute over 70% of T2D patients [11].

A recent retrospective cohort study of over 1.8 million T2D patients in the United States (US) demonstrated that the use of GLP1RA, as compared to other non-GLP1RA anti-diabetic agents, was associated with a significantly lower risks of developing hepatic decompensation and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [12]. However, the effects of smoking status and glycemic control, both of which are important determinants of HCC and adverse hepatic events, were not considered in that study [13]. A recent cohort study compared sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) with other oral anti-diabetic drugs (OADs), including glitazones, on adverse liver outcomes among T2D patients with MASLD. However, the study did not include GLP1RA users and hence direct comparison between GLP1RA and pioglitazone remains unavailable [14].

Therefore, we designed this hypothetical target trial using the territory-wide electronic cohort data in Hong Kong to estimate the causal effects of GLP1RA and pioglitazone on the risks of major adverse cardiovascular and liver outcomes in T2D patients.

Methods

Data source

This study assembled data from the Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (CDARS), a territory-wide database of the Hospital Authority (HA) in Hong Kong. HA serves as the sole statutory body that oversees all public hospitals and ambulatory clinics in Hong Kong. Therefore, CDARS comprehensively captures a wealth of patient information, including demographic data, diagnoses, procedures, medication prescriptions, laboratory investigations, clinical appointments, and death records from all public clinics and hospitals, representing inpatient and outpatient care of approximately 7.4 million of the local population. Accordingly, the utilization of CDARS has facilitated several large-scale population-based studies among patients with diabetes locally [15, 16], and the high coding accuracy of CDARS with positive and negative predictive values of over 90% has been described previously [16]. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institutional Review Board of the University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (Reference Number: UW 15-613). Informed consent was not required as all data were anonymous.

Target trial specification and emulation

A hypothetical "target trial" using cohort data enables assessment of causal inferences when evidence from RCTs is not available [17]. In this cohort study, we first specified the protocol of a target trial comparing the cardio-hepatic benefits of GLP1RA and pioglitazone. Next, we emulated the target trial using CDARS data, with all protocol components summarized in Supplemental Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

All adult new users of GLP1RA and pioglitazone with T2D managed in HA between January 2008 (when GLP1RA first became available locally in HA) and December 2022 were identified. In addition to the prescription of oral anti-diabetic medication, T2D was also identified by using the International Statistical Classification of Disease, 9th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) codes of ‘250. × 0’ and ‘250. × 2’ or by physician diagnosis based on clinical history, biochemical findings, or immunological evidence [18]. The baseline date was defined as that of the first GLP1RA or pioglitazone prescription. Patients were excluded if they had: (i) co-initiation of GLP1RA and pioglitazone. (ii) prescription of other drugs within the class of thiazolidinedione within 12 months before baseline; (iii) end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 15 ml/min/1.73 m2, or history of kidney replacement therapy (dialysis or kidney transplantation) on or before baseline; (iv) contraindications of GLP1RA or pioglitazone, including pancreatitis and heart failure (HF), respectively, on or before baseline; (v) cirrhosis, HCC, liver failure or liver transplantation on or before baseline.

Treatment strategies

Two treatment strategies were compared: i) initiation of GLP1RA which was continued during follow-up; and ii) initiation of pioglitazone which was continued during follow-up. Continuous drug exposure was defined as consecutive drug prescriptions without a prescription gap of more than 60 days, a commonly used definition in diabetes research as recommended by the checklist for medication compliance and persistence studies [19, 20].

Treatment assignment

An “active comparator, new user” study design was adopted, and patients were classified into two groups based on the above two treatment strategies [21]. To emulate randomization, we assumed groups were exchangeable at baseline conditional on their baseline covariates, which included age, sex, smoking status, alcohol status, diabetes duration, body mass index (BMI), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), fasting glucose, systolic and diastolic blood pressure (BP), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride, eGFR, albuminuria status, alanine transaminase (ALT), albumin, bilirubin, platelet, pre-existing atrial fibrillation, diabetic retinopathy, diabetic neuropathy, cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and chronic liver diseases, use of anti-diabetic agents, anti-hypertensive medications, anti-coagulants, anti-platelets and lipid-lowering medications for 6 months prior to baseline, as well as the baseline year.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were major adverse liver outcomes (MALO) and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). MALO was defined as HCC, compensated and decompensated cirrhotic events, which included hepatic encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome, ascites, or variceal bleeding, liver failure, liver transplantation, or liver-related mortality [22]. MACE was defined as non-fatal myocardial infarction (MI), non-fatal stroke, and cardiovascular mortality [23]. The secondary outcomes included HF, as well as individual outcomes within MACE and MALO. The complete list of codes for disease diagnosis, procedures, drugs, and laboratory criteria for each clinical diagnosis is shown in Supplemental Table S2.

Follow-up

Eligible patients were followed from baseline until the occurrence of any outcome of interest, date of death, or 31 December 2023, the end of the observation, whichever occurred first. For per-protocol (PP) effects, patients were additionally censored when they had treatment crossover (i.e. patients crossing over from GLP1RA to pioglitazone, or vice versa) or treatment discontinuation.

Causal contrasts

We estimated observational analogues of the intention-to-treat (ITT) and PP effects.

Statistical analysis

Multiple imputation by chained equations was adopted to account for missing baseline characteristics. Each missing datum was imputed five times using other complete variables, followed by multiple-imputation linear prediction applying the Rubin’s combination rules [24]. One to one propensity score matching (PSM) without replacement and with a caliper width of 0.05 was performed to match GLP1RA and pioglitazone new users. We used a multivariable logistic regression model adjusting for all baseline covariates and the year of treatment initiation to estimate the propensity-scores of all included patients. Balance of baseline covariates between the two groups was assessed by absolute standardized mean difference (ASMD), and an ASMD < 0.1 indicated a good balance [25]. Both ITT and PP analyses were applied in the emulated target trial. The ITT analysis compared the risks of outcomes between GLP1RA and pioglitazone, based on their treatment strategies at baseline, whereas the PP analysis compared the risks of outcomes between continuous GLP1RA and pioglitazone users during the follow-up period. In contrast to ITT, the PP analysis artificially censored those patients who had deviated from their assigned treatment strategy. The cumulative incidence and crude incidence rate of each outcome event were calculated. Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to compare the effects of GLP1RA and pioglitazone on the outcomes of interest with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Cumulative incidence curves of outcomes among two groups were plotted and log-rank test was used to estimate the p for trend.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted stratified by sex (male vs. female), age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), eGFR (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 ml/min/1.73 m2), HbA1c (< 8 vs. ≥ 8%), duration of diabetes (< 10 vs. ≥ 10 years), and the presence of pre-existing CVD. Similar to the main analysis, PSM was conducted within each subgroup to re-match new users of GLP1RA with those of pioglitazone. P-value for interaction was used to assess if there were significant differences in the associations among subgroups. Several sensitivity analyses were also conducted to assess the robustness of the findings, which included: (1) redefining drug discontinuation as the absence of dispense of the index drug within 30 days after the estimated last day of pill supply from the previous drug prescription [20]; (2) excluding the adverse liver events or major adverse CVD that occurred within 6 months after start of follow-up when investigating each outcome of interest to reduce reverse causation bias. This approach aligns with the recommended 6–12 month interval for HCC surveillance imaging and accounts for the likelihood that events occurring in this early period are less likely to be associated with new medication use [26]; (3) excluding saxagliptin users at baseline as the medication is associated with an increased risk of HF exacerbation [27]; (4) applying Fine-Gray competing risk analysis by treating non-cardiovascular mortality as competing events for MACE outcomes and non-liver-related mortality as competing events for MALO; (5) adjusting the use of SGLT2i as a time-varying covariate in PP analysis to consider the reported effect of SGLT2i on the outcomes [28, 29]; (6) employing inverse probability censoring weighting (IPCW) to adjust for both baseline and time-varying covariates related to treatment adherence and account for the potential selection bias due to artificial censoring in PP analysis [30]. The IPCW models incorporated factors including age, sex, diabetes duration, pre-existing comorbidities, and use of medications at baseline, along with time-varying covariates including atrial fibrillation, diabetic retinopathy, diabetic neuropathy and use of medications, as detailed in the Table 1 below. We structured our dataset into monthly intervals and updated the relevant comorbidities and medication information every month; (7) including only patients with MASLD at baseline, defined as a hepatic steatosis index (HSI) > 36 [31]; (8) Lastly, comparing the primary outcomes between pioglitazone and each individual GLP1RA drug. Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 4617 GLP1RA and 82,548 pioglitazone new users were identified after applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The data completion rates for baseline characteristics of eligible patients are presented in Supplemental Table S3. After multiple imputation and PSM, 8922 patients (4461 in each group) were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Of the GLP1RA new users, 1982 (42.9%), 638 (13.8%), 1256 (27.2%), 81 (1.8%), and 660 (14.3%) were treated with dulaglutide, exenatide, liraglutide, lixisenatide and semaglutide, respectively. Baseline characteristics of patients before and after PSM are shown in Supplemental Tables S4 and 1, respectively. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The results of the subgroup analyses are depicted in Supplemental Figures S1 and S2. Consistently, the effects of GLP1RA and pioglitazone on MALO and MACE were comparable across most subgroups stratified by sex, age, HbA1c and eGFR levels, and the presence of CVD at baseline. No interaction was found between subgroups in ITT analyses (all p for interaction > 0.05). In PP analysis, diabetes duration interacted with the comparison of GLP1RA and pioglitazone regarding MALO outcomes (p = 0.029). However, the number of MALO events were only 2 and 8 among pioglitazone and GLP1RA users with diabetes duration < 10 years, respectively. Importantly, no significant differences were observed in the outcomes of MALO and MACE between pioglitazone and each individual GLP1RA, including semaglutide, exenatide, dulaglutide, and liraglutide. The results of the sensitivity analyses are shown in Supplemental Figures S3–S10 and were again largely consistent with the main findings.

Discussion

In this cohort study adopting a target trial emulation framework, the use of GLP1RA and pioglitazone demonstrated comparable effects on risks of MALO and MACE in both ITT and PP analyses. Importantly, these results were consistent across multiple subgroups and sensitivity analyses, regardless of their baseline HbA1c and eGFR levels, the presence of pre-existing CVD and MASLD. However, we noted that patients started on GLP1RA had a significantly lower risk of incident HF compared to those started on pioglitazone. Therefore, for T2D patients who are at risk of both adverse cardiac and hepatic events, such as those with MASLD, treatment decisions should be individualized, taking into consideration the need for weight loss and the risk of HF, which if present, GLP1RA may be a more favourable choice.

Studies that directly compare the cardio-hepatic effects of GLP1RA and pioglitazone have been lacking. A recent nationwide cohort study assessed the hepatic effectiveness of SGLT2i, pioglitazone, and GLP1RA in patients with MASLD, and found that SGLT2i was associated with a significantly lower risk of hepatic decompensation compared to pioglitazone and was similar as GLP1RA. However, the study did not compare pioglitazone and GLP1RA [32]. In a network meta-analysis comparing GLP1RA and pioglitazone for treating MASLD, there was no difference in their effects on hepatic steatosis based on vibration-controlled transient elastography [33]. The latest EASL-EASD-EASO guidelines recommend GLP1RA and SGLT2i over pioglitazone in treating T2D among patients with co-existing MASLD [9]. In this context, our findings suggested that, from the liver perspective, both GLP1RA and pioglitazone are similarly effective in preventing the development of MALO. The results remained consistent with the main analysis when evaluating individual MALO components as endpoints, despite the small number of events. Notably, our conclusions were robust across most subgroup and sensitivity analyses, including accounting for SGLT2i use as a time-varying covariate or restricting the cohort to patients with MASLD.

For MACE, GLP1RA is a well-recognized class of anti-diabetic agent with cardio-protective effects [6, 34]. Pioglitazone, on the other hand, is also a potent insulin sensitizer and addresses multiple aspects of metabolic dysfunction [35]. Indeed, in the PROspective pioglitAzone Clinical Trial In macroVascular Events (PROactive) trial, although pioglitazone did not achieve statistical significance with its primary outcome [4], it significantly reduced the risk of MACE compared to placebo [36]. Consistently, our study demonstrated that the effects of GLP1RA and pioglitazone on incident 3-point MACE were comparable. Notably, for both anti-diabetic agents, previous studies showed that their cardiovascular benefits were more apparently observed among patients with established CVD than those without [37, 38]. However, we found no significant difference when analyses were stratified based on the presence of CVD at baseline or restricted to patients with MASLD. Indeed, the ADA guidelines also recognize the cardiovascular benefits of pioglitazone, particularly on stroke risk [7, 8].

Owing to the framework of a target trial emulation, patients with history of HF at baseline, which precluded their use of pioglitazone, had already been excluded. Nonetheless, our study found that GLP1RA remained associated with a lower risk of HF than pioglitazone. Previous studies had already reported that, despite its cardiovascular benefits on atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), pioglitazone is associated with an elevated risk of HF and related hospitalizations, primarily due to fluid retention [4, 39]. On the other hand, a recent pooled analysis of participants with HF from the series of semaglutide trials (SELECT, FLOW, STEP-HFpEF, and STEP-HFpEF DM trials) demonstrated that semaglutide was efficacious in reducing the risk of clinical HF events in patients [40]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis of 10 RCTs involving over 68000 patients also reported that GLP1RA reduced the risk of HF hospitalization among patients without a history of HF [34]. Taken together, whether the lower HF risk associated with GLP1RA in our study was attributed to an adverse side-effect profile of pioglitazone, or the benefits of GLP1RA in a study population without baseline history of HF, remains to be further elucidated. Indeed, in addition to this differential HF risk from GLP1RA, other important adverse effects of pioglitazone should also be acknowledged and discussed during the prescription process, which include weight gain (in contrast to weight reduction with GLP1RA), increased fracture risk and osteoporosis particularly among postmenopausal women, and the previously reported potential association with bladder cancer [41]. A personalized approach, taking into consideration all the clinical characteristics and patient-specific factors, should be adopted in T2D management.

The strengths of our study include the large, territory-wide database of T2D patients and the emulated target trial design, which can overcome bias such as immortal time bias, that are commonly present in observational studies [17]. Notably, in addition to MACE, our study evaluated hard hepatic outcomes which are clinically meaningful, patient-centered and more long-term endpoints that could be difficult to capture in RCTs given the extended follow-up periods required to observe for events. The use of target trial emulation study design therefore leveraged the large-scale real-world data while maintaining rigorous methodological standards comparable to RCTs. However, our study also had limitations. First, an observational study is limited in its ability to imply causal inference and the impact of residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Despite using PSM and an active-comparator, new-user design to closely approximate randomization, unmeasured factors, such as socioeconomic Moreover, channeling bias might occur if clinicians selectively avoided pioglitazone in T2D patients perceived to be at higher HF risk or in obese patients due to its side effect of weight gain. In addition, clinicians may monitor pioglitazone users more closely for symptoms and signs of HF, which could potentially introduce detection bias and magnify the observed difference in HF between the two groups. Nonetheless, by comparing two active treatments with broadly similar clinical indications, it is likely that the distribution of such unmeasured variables was balanced between groups, thereby mitigating their potential impact. Secondly, although HSI is a validated surrogate marker with favorable sensitivity and specificity for identifying MASLD [42], it could be confounded by fibrosis and inflammation of the liver [43], which are also more commonly found in T2D patients and cannot be confirmed histologically in our study. Furthermore, not all new users had complete baseline parameters for HSI calculation to determine the presence of MASLD. Nonetheless, it is well recognized that over 70% of T2D patients have MASLD, and our sensitivity analyses demonstrated that our findings should also apply to all T2D patients regardless of the presence of MASLD. Thirdly, the relatively short duration of follow-up in PP analysis limited both the number of events and the ability to fully assess long-term cardio-hepatic outcomes, including detailed analyses of individual MALO and MACE components. While this constraint necessitates caution in interpreting the durability of treatment effects beyond the study period, the consistent main findings obtained from ITT with longer follow-up period and PP analyses provide support to the overall robustness, validity and reliability of the conclusion of our study. Future studies with extended follow-up periods are warranted to validate our findings. Lastly, the study population was predominantly Chinese, which might limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups.

Conclusions

To conclude, our target trial emulation study, which directly compared GLP1RA and pioglitazone, demonstrated similar effects on major adverse liver and cardiovascular events, but a lower risk of incident HF with use of GLP1RA than pioglitazone. Therefore, for T2D patients who are at risk of both adverse cardiac and hepatic events, such as those with MASLD, treatment decisions should be individualized, taking into consideration the need for weight loss and the risk of HF, which if present, GLP1RA may be a more favourable choice.
