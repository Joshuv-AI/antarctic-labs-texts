The role of satisfaction with the way you look in 10 to 12-year-olds’ health and subjective well-being across genders and cultures
Authors: Mònica González-Carrasco, Ferran Casas, Sara Malo, Xavier Oriol, David Cámara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Few studies have examined the relationship between health and well-being in the general population of children and adolescents from a psychosocial perspective. The present article therefore addresses the following unprecedented twofold objective: (1) to conduct an in-depth analysis of differences in satisfaction with the way you look, satisfaction with health and a global measure of subjective well-being (SWB), by age group (10- and 12-year-olds), gender and country; and (2) to explore to what extent satisfaction with the way you look mediates the relationship between satisfaction with health and a global measure of SWB, and how the relationship between these variables is related to gender and country of origin in the two mentioned age groups. Using a cross-sectional and correlational approach, analyses were conducted using data obtained from a self-administered questionnaire on a sample of 91,076 adolescents from 35 countries belonging to the Children’s Worlds 3rd wave dataset. Satisfaction with the way you look was found to mediate the relationship between satisfaction with health and a global measure of SWB, and the variables age, gender and country were identified as being important in this mediation. These findings further our knowledge on the contribution of SWB-related evaluations in these age groups for different countries and genders, but also on the adoption of a broader and more preventive approach to improving them. The relevance of implementing interventions focused on improving self-perception at these ages is discussed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22515-z.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity (Preamble to the World Health Organization Constitution1. Moore and Oberklaid2 highlighted the definition of health added by the WHO a few years later, as it awards even more importance to social and personal resources and not only to physical capacities. According to this definition, health is understood as the extent to which an individual or group is on the one hand able to realize aspirations and security needs, and on the other change or cope with the environment3.

Thanks to this unprecedentedly broad concept of health, which has since become intertwined with that of well-being, health researchers have since considered health as an aspect or domain that falls within the general construct of quality of life. However, with the exception of epidemiological studies, scholars have tended to limit their research to those dimensions affected by disease or its treatment that have the potential to be modified by therapeutic interventions4–6, leading to the formulation of the term “health-related quality of life”. Within the tradition of health-related quality of life, children’s and adolescents’ satisfaction with health has led to numerous articles on the impact of various diseases on quality of life, such as cancer and diabetes, and satisfaction with health services in general or mental health support during the Covid-19 pandemic in particular (in many cases from the perspective of their parents). The above contrasts with the approach to health taken by social scientists, which is the one adopted in the present article to investigate children’s and adolescents’ subjective evaluations of health and well-being from their own perspective. The article is framed within the 3rd wave of the Children’s Worlds Project (https://isciweb.org), an international survey aimed at furthering understanding of the determinants of children’s and adolescents’ subjective well-being (SWB) and promoting children’s policies based on empirical evidence collected on this issue worldwide.

SWB has been conceptualized as part of the subjective dimension of the quality-of-life construct and is understood as the way in which people evaluate their lives, regardless of age, both in general (overall life satisfaction) and in relation to specific life domains (family, friends, leisure, health, etc.)7. These two types of evaluations constitute the cognitive component of SWB, which is the focus of this article.

According to Holte et al.8, SWB measurement instruments can be classified as follows: (1) single-item scales based on responses to a global or generic question; (2) unidimensional multiple-item scales based on the assumption that all items load on a single component. The latter scales can be of two types, context-free, which include generic items on overall life satisfaction, and scales based on satisfaction domains; or (3) multidimensional, multiple-item scales that refer to different components of the SWB construct.

There are few studies that have examined the relationship between health and well-being in the general population of children and adolescents from a psychosocial perspective9, and even fewer if we exclude those focusing on self-rated health. See, for example, the recent work by Kroh et al.10, with 9- to 12-year-old children, the report by Cosma et al.11, which used data from the most recent Health Behaviour in School-Aged Children -HBSC- survey of adolescents aged 11, 13 and 15 in 44 countries, and the latest results from the OECD Program for International Student Assessment -PISA- published in 2023 with the optional well-being questionnaire administered to 15-year-old students in 13 countries.

The lack of research in this area is consistent with the fact that, in terms of specific satisfaction with life domains, satisfaction with health has generally not been included in instruments aimed at measuring children’s and adolescents’ SWB, despite the importance that children and adolescents themselves attribute to health for overall well-being, as observed in the context of qualitative studies (see, for example, the qualitative meta-synthesis of 13 qualitative articles by Carrillo et al.12, which shows that healthy activities are among the most important activities that influence children’s and adolescents’ experience of well-being). An exception to the above is the Personal Well-Being Index-School Children (PWI-SC) scale, a multiple-item and domain-based instrument developed by Cummins and Lau13, which uses an 11-point bipolar scale and is designed for self-administration. Parallel to the adult version, this instrument consists of seven items corresponding to different life domains, which are theoretically embedded as representing the first-level deconstruction of the global question “How satisfied are you with your life as a whole?”14. These domains are: standard of living, personal health, life achievement, personal relationships, personal safety, feeling part of the community and future security.

Tomyn and Cummins15 conducted a study to examine the psychometric properties of the PWI-SC in two samples of Australian adolescents aged 12–20, with a total of 351 participants. For the PWI-A (the adult version of the PWI), they confirmed that the seven domains of the PWI-SC loaded on only one factor. They also observed that only five domains contributed to explaining life satisfaction scores, with health being the fourth largest contributor according to its regression weight. This shows the relative importance of health satisfaction among adolescents compared to other life domains. In terms of gender and age, girls scored higher in health satisfaction than boys, and the oldest participants scored lower than the youngest in the same life domain.

In another publication aimed at testing the comparability of the PWI-SC with the PWI-A, Tomyn et al.16 again confirmed the one-dimensionality of the PWI-SC. They also found that the mean for satisfaction with health ranked 6th for an Australian adolescent sample aged 11–18 (the authors added an 8th dimension of satisfaction with school to the seven dimensions already described) and 5th for the Australian adult sample (adding an 8th dimension of satisfaction with religion/spirituality). In that study, satisfaction with health was among the indicators with the lowest loading value in the principal component analysis.

In contrast to the aforementioned two studies, in research conducted by Alfaro et al.17 aimed at examining the psychometric properties of the PWI-SC in a sample of Chilean 10–12-year-olds, satisfaction with health had the second highest mean score, as well as being the second most contributing domain to overall SWB. Scores did not vary by gender or age. This important contribution of satisfaction with health in relation to other life domains was also observed by Gross-Manos et al.18 when administering different versions of the PWI-SC to Israeli children aged 11–13 (girls scored higher than boys), by Singh et al.19 with Indian adolescents aged 13–18 using the same instrument (males and younger participants scored higher), and by Vaqué et al.20 with 10–12 year-old Spanish adolescents responding to the PWI-SC plus an additional item on satisfaction with food (no differences were found by gender or age).

The above findings suggest cultural differences in the importance attached to satisfaction with health for overall well-being, as well as in children’s and adolescents’ evaluations of satisfaction with health, and that these interact with gender and age. This observation is supported by results from the 35 countries participating in the 3rd wave of the Children’s Worlds Project. Specifically, satisfaction with family, home, material possessions, health and safety ranked high in most of the participating countries, although high variability was detected among them. In contrast, satisfaction with classmates, neighbourhood, the way you look - we discuss this life satisfaction domain in more detail below - freedom, and being listened to ranked low, again displaying a fairly wide range of mean scores21.

According to Alexandrova-Karamanova22, “the importance of body image and appearance self-attitudes in adolescence is higher than in other developmental periods due to the increased body consciousness related to the many changes that happen in this period: intense physical development and physical changes associated with puberty, identity formation, the development of sexuality and the increased importance of gender roles, the need for being liked by peers and being a valued member in peer groups” (p. 70). As far as satisfaction with the way you look is concerned, it is very indicative of current trends that a search of articles using these keywords or, alternatively, satisfaction with one’s appearance, will yield quite a large number of articles addressing the impact of orthodontic treatment and craniofacial malformations on children’s and adolescents’ self-image, or negative body image perceptions and dissatisfaction with specific body parts due to overweight or obesity, with an important focus on gender and age differences in the latter case, but hardly anything else. As with satisfaction with health, satisfaction with the way you look is generally not included in multidimensional, multi-item scales referring to different components of the SWB construct.

One exception to this is the CW-DBSWBS (Children’s Worlds Domain Based Subjective Well-Being Scale), which consists of five items measuring domain-based cognitive SWB and is inspired by Seligson et al.’s23 Brief Multidimensional Student Life Satisfaction Scale (BMSLSS). The CW-DBSWBS includes the following items: satisfaction with the people you live with, with your friends, with your life as a student, with the area where you live and with the way you look. It is important to note that while the BMSLSS considers a dimension related to the self (satisfaction with oneself), its corresponding indicator within the CW-DBSWBS is formulated in a very different way, namely, as satisfaction with the way you look. An analysis conducted by Casas and González-Carrasco24 on the 35 countries that collected data from 10- and 12-year-olds during the 3rd wave of the Children’s Worlds project showed that the CW-DBSWBS fitted very well with the five items and that the explained variance corresponding to satisfaction with the way you look, measured by means of squared multiple correlations, was lowest for some countries, although not for most of them.

If it is difficult to find studies that have related satisfaction with health or satisfaction with the way you look to SWB in children and adolescents from the general population, it is even more difficult to find research that has used both indicators at the same time. A notable exception to this is the study by Savahl et al.25, who, using the Children’s Worlds 3rd wave dataset, showed that both satisfaction with health and with the way you look clearly contribute to a latent factor of domain-based cognitive SWB. At the same time, together with positive affect, negative affect, and a fourth latent factor related to context-free cognitive SWB, this latent factor formed part of a second-order factor model of SWB that also showed a good fit.

Satisfaction with health and satisfaction with the way you look are not only included as questions in the Children’s Worlds project, but also in questionnaires administered to children and adolescents in the UK by The Children’s Society and published regularly in their successive Good Childhood Reports, the latest of which26 shows that participants are less satisfied with their appearance compared with previous years, girls scoring lower than boys in this regard. This report also stresses that satisfaction with appearance is a strong contributor to feelings of unhappiness. In contrast, satisfaction with health is the second most important life domain for children and adolescents, just behind satisfaction with family. Both satisfaction with health and with the way you look have been included in the optional PISA well-being questionnaire since 2018. The latest 2022 report, which has just been published, shows that, after controlling for student and school characteristics, satisfaction with health and the way they look are among the best predictors of students’ life satisfaction27.

However, despite all the above, we did not find any articles from any of the aforementioned projects that examined the role of satisfaction with the way you look in health and well-being evaluations among 10- to 12-year-olds. The present article therefore proposes the following unprecedented twofold objective: (1) To conduct an in-depth analysis of differences in satisfaction with the way you look, satisfaction with health and a global measure of SWB, by age group (10 and 12 year olds), gender and country, and (2) to explore to what extent satisfaction with the way you look mediates the relationship between satisfaction with health and a global measure of SWB, and how the relationship between these variables is related to gender and country of origin in the two mentioned age groups (10 and 12 year olds).

Based on the above aims, it is hypothesized that satisfaction with the way you look will mediate the relationship between satisfaction with your health and the global measure of SWB used here. Given the existing evidence that satisfaction with health and appearance varies significantly across countries, even when using standardized instruments21,24, the influence of cultural norms must be considered. Moreover, consistent gender differences have been reported, with girls generally expressing lower satisfaction with their appearance. These findings, along with age-related trends highlighted in previous studies, provide a strong rationale for including age, gender and country as key variables when analysing this phenomenon.

Method

The present study adopts a cross-sectional and correlational approach, whereby data were gathered at one time point with the aim of identifying patterns of association among variables of interest.

Sample

A total of 91,076 adolescents, mainly 10-year-olds (N = 48,053, M = 10.06, SD = 0.73) and 12-year-olds (N = 43,023, M = 11.99, SD = 0.75) from 35 countries and belonging to the Children’s Worlds 3rd wave dataset were included. As gender is a key variable in this article, cases where this variable was not reported were excluded from the analysis. The percentage of girls was slightly higher than that of boys in the pooled sample (50.91%), but also below 51% in both age groups (50.89% in the 10-year-old age group and 50.93% in the 12-year-old age group).

Instruments

The respective country teams were responsible for translating the agreed-upon original English version of the questionnaire into their local language and providing a back-translation, enabling the international core team to conduct a rigorous analysis of item equivalence across both languages. The analysis presented in this article was based on data collected using the two measures included in the questionnaire. These were:

Two items on how satisfied you are with the way you look and with your health, both measured on a 0 to 10 point scale, where 0 signifies “not at all satisfied” and 10 “totally satisfied”. The item measuring satisfaction with the way you look is one of five items on the Children’s Worlds Subjective Well-Being Scale (CW-DBSWBS), which is based on the Brief Multidimensional Student Life Satisfaction Scale by Seligson et al.23. Although only one item was used here, it is worth mentioning that the scale fit the 10- and 12-year-old age groups very well with the original five items, although it was not possible to directly compare the means between countries, since neither metric nor scalar invariance was supported for this scale in either of the two age group models24. Satisfaction with health formed part of a modified version of the Personal Well-Being Index – School Children (PWI-SC), which was originally formulated by Cummins and Lau13 and used in the Children’s Worlds project. The modified scale fit moderately well with both age groups, but there were some limitations when comparing means across countries24.The Children’s Worlds Subjective Well-Being Scale (CW-SWBS), which consists of six items measuring cognitive subjective well-being based on the Student Life Satisfaction Scale28. The latter was developed within the context of the Children’s Worlds Project and its reliability and cultural comparability have already been tested using the same dataset of 35 countries that was used for this article24. The items on the CW-SWBS were measured on a 0-to-10-point scale, where 0 means “strongly disagree” and 10 means “strongly agree”, formulated as a response to the question of how much you agree with the following statements about your life as a whole. These statements are as follows: I enjoy my life; My life is going well; I have a good life; The things that happen in my life are excellent; I like my life; and I am happy with my life. Procedure

The procedure was the same for all countries involved in the Children’s Worlds project. The participants completed an anonymous, school-based, self-administered questionnaire that was online or paper-based, depending on the country, in their regular classroom, during school hours, with the assistance of the researchers involved. As this was a human study, the ethical norms of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments were followed, including the voluntary cooperation of schools and the adolescents themselves and the informed consent from guardians. Approval was obtained from the relevant ethics committees in each participating country prior to the survey being conducted. Since no single committee holds the authority to issue guidance on data collection and processing in countries other than their own, several ethics committees had to be involved in the process, each of which issued its own resolution.

The schools were selected in such a way as to form a representative sample at the national or regional level, according to the parameters considered most relevant by each national research team, such as territorial distribution or school characteristics. In each selected school, a second sampling unit was the class corresponding to the target age group, thus allowing for two-stage probability sampling (see the Children’s Worlds’ website: https://isciweb.org/, for more details). Inclusion criteria for participating in the data collection were: having provided informed consent; possessing sufficient cognitive and language comprehension; and being willing to complete the questionnaire.

Data analysis

Prior to it being made available for analysis, the dataset had been thoroughly cleaned and prepared within the context of the international project. This process involved detecting and removing cases with excessive missing data, as well as those showing consistent or patterned responses that could compromise data quality. The following criteria were considered (Rees et al., 2020): (1) The participant was more than two years outside the target age range for the age groups surveyed, (2) more than 50% of the participant’s responses were missing data, (3) there was evidence of systematic responding (the participant gave exactly the same answers to a set of 14 time-use questions). Cases were omitted from the international data set if they met any of these three criteria. On average, this resulted in around 3% of cases being excluded. An additional data cleansing procedure was applied to the study sample. For each scale, participants who failed to respond to three or more items were excluded from the analysis – that meant less than 1% of additional excluded cases. In contrast, missing responses for one or two items were addressed through multiple imputation using regression, as implemented in the AMOS29 software.

The general measure of SWB used here, the CW-SWBS5, had already been subjected to Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) in a previous study with the same age groups (10- and 12-year-olds) to provide evidence of the internal structural validity of this multi-item scale24. The five-item version (without the item “I like my life”) was found to show an excellent fit for both age groups. Several multi-group models were computed to examine the extent to which the CFA model was equivalent across countries (measurement invariance). Three steps are required to test for measurement invariance: (a) configural invariance (unconstrained variables); (b) metric invariance (constrained factor loadings); (c) scalar invariance (constrained loadings and intercepts). Both configural and metric invariance were achieved, meaning that correlations and regression weights could be compared among countries. However, only partial scalar invariance was observed, because four items could be compared across countries in terms of means but one could not: “The things that happen in my life are excellent”.

Descriptive statistics, including independent samples t-test and Cohen’s d to measure effect size, were calculated for satisfaction with your health, satisfaction with the way you look, and the CW-SWBS5, considering gender, age group (10- vs. 12-year-olds), and country using SPSS v29. According to Cohen29, a d-value of around of 0.2 is considered a small effect, 0.5 a medium effect, and 0.8 a large effect. Structural equation modelling (SEM) was then used to examine the mediating role of satisfaction with your health and satisfaction with the way you look on a global measure of SWB such as the CW-SWBS5. SEM is a multivariate technique that seeks to explain the relationship between multiple variables (including unobservable or “latent” variables), including measurement error in the model30. The AMOS v29 software with Maximum Likelihood Estimation was used. Two models were calculated separately, one for the 10-year-olds and one for the 12-year-olds, both including gender.

Once the goodness of fit of both models had been tested, multi-group SEM was performed to test for measurement invariance across countries. The fit indices considered were the CFI (Comparative Fix Index), the RMSEA (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation) and the SRMR (Standardized Root Mean Square Residual). Results above 0.950 for CFI and below 0.05 for RMSEA and SRMR were considered to be excellent, in line with Arbuckle31 and Byrne30. However, it should be noted that some other authors consider RMSEA values of up to 0.08 to represent acceptable approximation errors in larger samples30,32,33, while a CFI above 0.90 reflects an acceptable fit to the data33. Each multi-group SEM model was tested in three steps (configural invariance, metric invariance and scalar invariance). When a constraint was added to a model, a change in any fit index greater than 0.01 was considered unacceptable34,35.

Squared Multiple Correlations (SMC) were calculated with each model, because they indicate how accurately each variable is predicted by the other variables in the model30,31. Additionally, the remaining % variance is accounted for by its unique factor error. If error represents measurement error only, we are able to say that the estimated reliability of the variable is the value displayed for each variable SMC. Therefore, each SMC value is an estimate from the lower band of reliability relating to its variable30,31.

The next step was to calculate multi-group models for each age group separately. According to the results of the goodness of fit indices shown in Table 3, the correlations and regression weights were comparable across countries (Models 3 and 7, respectively), but not the means (Models 4 and 8). Tables 4 and 5 show the standardized regression weights and the SMC (Squared Multiple Correlations) by country and for each age group, while Figs. 3 and 4 visually present the standardized regression weights with constrained loadings for the two age groups, respectively. According to the results observed, the effects of gender on SWB of adolescents in the 10-year age group were not significant in most countries, the only exceptions being Algeria, Indonesia and Russia, where girls had significantly higher scores than boys at the p < 0.001 level. However, there were a few more countries where their contribution appeared significant at the p < 0.01 or p < 0.05 level, probably due to the large sample effect; girls’ SWB scores were higher in some (Albania, Bangladesh, and Taiwan), while boys’ were in others (Belgium, Norway).

The effects of gender on adolescents’ satisfaction with the way you look were not significant in most countries (10-year-old group), the only exceptions being Algeria and Malaysia, where girls had significantly higher mean scores than boys at the p < 0.001 level, and France, Hungary, South Korea and Switzerland, where boys had significantly higher scores than girls at the p < 0.001 level. However, there were a few more countries where its contribution appeared significant at the p < 0.01 or p < 0.05 level, probably due to the large sample effect; girls’ SWB scores were lower in some (Belgium, Taiwan and Wales), while in South Africa they were lower for boys. The effects of gender on satisfaction with health among adolescents from the 10-year group were not significant in most countries, the only exceptions being Indonesia and Wales, where girls displayed significantly higher scores then boys at the p < 0.001 level. However, there were a few more countries where its contribution appeared significant at the p < 0.01 or p < 0.05 level, probably due to the large sample effect, with higher SWB scores for boys in all cases (France, Hong Kong, Hungary, Italy, Nepal and Spain).

The effects of satisfaction with the way you look on adolescents’ satisfaction with health were significant in all countries in this sample for both the 10-year-old group (ranging from 0.257 in Albania to 0.518 in Norway) and the 12-year-old group (ranging from 0.257 in Nepal to 0.652 in Sri Lanka). The effects of both satisfaction with the way you look and satisfaction with health on adolescents’ SWB were also significant in all countries for both age groups. The effects of satisfaction with health appeared to be higher in West Asian countries (Vietnam, South Korea, Hong Kong and Sri Lanka) and developing countries (Algeria) than in Western countries, whereas the effects of satisfaction with the way you look on CW-SWB5 appeared to be lower in South Asian countries (India, Indonesia, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Vietnam), Albania and Malta. Effects of satisfaction with the way you look on the CW-SWB5 ranged from 0.193 in Albania to 0.659 in Chile for the 10-year age group, and from 0.239 in Albania to 0.682 in Chile for the 12-year age group. The effects of health satisfaction on the CW-SWBS5 ranged from 0.114 in Chile to 0.436 in Indonesia for the 10-year age group, and from 0.148 in Malta to 0.459 in Vietnam for the 12-year age group.

The explained variance in the CW-SWBS5 (using SMC) taking the effects of satisfaction with the way you look and satisfaction with health together ranged from 0.067 in Albania to 0.338 in Spain for the 10-year age group and from 0.143 in Albania to 0.496 in Wales for the 12-year age group. The explained variance of satisfaction with health by the effects of satisfaction with the way you look ranged from 0.092 in Albania to 0.515 in Chile for the 10-year age group and from 0.106 in Croatia to 0.427 in Sri Lanka for the 12-year age group.

Discussion

This article is organized around two objectives. The first was formulated as exploring gender, age group and country differences in satisfaction with the way you look, satisfaction with your health and the CW-SWBS5 as a global measure of SWB. The second was to examine the extent to which satisfaction with the way you look mediates the relationship between satisfaction with your health and the CW-SWBS5, and how the relationship between these variables is related to gender and country in the two age groups considered (10- and 12-year-olds). With respect to the first objective, it is observed that satisfaction with your health displays the highest mean, followed by the score for the CW-SWBS5, and then satisfaction with the way you look in both age groups and genders.

Satisfaction with the way you look was expected to display the lowest scores in many countries, as previous research indicates that there is a high prevalence of adolescents expressing body dissatisfaction36,37. At the same time, the country differences in relation to scores for satisfaction with the way you look observed in this article could be explained by Blum et al.’s38 consideration that the gendered nature of body dissatisfaction may not be as universal as expected. Specifically, they stated that “results from bivariate analysis stratified by gender show that generally young adolescents who perceived more traditional gender roles and traits reported greater body satisfaction…” (p. S42). Consistent with the results observed here, and according to the most recent PISA report27, although both satisfaction with the way you look and satisfaction with your health are among the best predictors of life satisfaction among 15-year-olds, the change in life satisfaction when students reported being satisfied or completely satisfied with your health was higher compared to satisfaction with the way you look.

When gender is considered as an isolated variable, it does not seem to play a significant role. Rather, it interacts with age differences, as the decreasing tendency of SWB with age is faster for girls than for boys39,40. Being a female and being older (15-year-olds vs. 13-year-olds) were also found to be associated with poorer self-rated health, multiple health complaints and lower life satisfaction in the HBSC survey11,41. Satisfaction with the way you look has a greater impact on the decline of SWB among girls compared to boys in industrialized countries (not only in Western countries), which is consistent with previous research showing that negative body image and concerns about thinness, especially for women, are much less common among populations living in subsistence economies [Anderson-Fye, 2012, cited in Holmqvist et al.,43].

Although Paxton et al.42 had already made the observation that body dissatisfaction increases in early adolescence, especially for girls, no cross-national analysis was conducted in this case. This can be explained by objectification theory, according to which girls in particular are socialized to see their bodies as objects through a mechanism called objectified body consciousness, which is the internalization of others’ perspectives regarding one’s own body, thus objectification becomes self-objectification (for more details, see the chapter by Holmqvist et al.43.

In relation to the second objective, the effects of satisfaction with the way you look on satisfaction with health were found to be significant in all countries included in the survey for both the 10- and 12-year-old groups. These findings can be seen as being related to those of Neumark-Sztainer et al.37, who concluded that lower body satisfaction does not serve as a motivator for healthy weight management behaviours among adolescents. Rather, it predicts the use of behaviours that may put adolescents at risk of weight gain and poorer overall health. They are also related to Blum et al.‘s38 observation that perceived health status was positively associated with body satisfaction among boys and girls aged 10–14 years in poor urban settings in three different countries (China, Ecuador and Indonesia), a finding they interpreted in terms of Field and Thomson’s (2004, cited in Blum et al.38 suggestion that body satisfaction may be a proxy for an overall sense of health and well-being.

The results observed here are also related to those of Alexandrova-Karamanova22 who, using data from the HBSC survey in Bulgaria, found that 13- and 15-year-old adolescents’ satisfaction with appearance and positive feelings and attitudes toward body and appearance were significantly associated with better self-rated health, absence or low levels of psychosomatic complaints and better “psychological well-being” (in the author’s terms), the latter measured using the KIDSCREEN-10 index. According to the analysis of the Norwegian HBSC data by Meland et al.36, the risk of reporting negative health perceptions increases significantly with dieting or expressing dissatisfaction with weight, perceived need for body change, perceived being too fat and perceived not looking good. These authors also observed that perceived negative health was associated with body dissatisfaction in early and mid-adolescence (11-, 13- and 15-year-olds were included in this analysis). Another study conducted in Brazil44 seems to go in the same direction, as the authors found that satisfaction with oneself, positive health perceptions, happiness with one’s health and gender (being a boy) were the most important predictors of body image satisfaction among adolescents aged 12 to 19 years.

The effects of both satisfaction with the way you look and satisfaction with your health on children’s and adolescents’ SWB (measured by the CW-SWBS5) are also significant in all countries for both age groups. This observation is the main contribution of the present article, since no previous literature has been identified using the same indicators and sharing the same objectives. It also confirms Field and Thomson’s hypothesis formulated within the context of eating disorders. From a country perspective, the effects of satisfaction with health seem to be higher in West Asian countries (Vietnam, South Korea, Hong-Kong, Sri Lanka) and developing countries (Algeria) than in Western countries, while the effects of satisfaction with the way you look on the CW-SWB5 seem to be lower in South Asian countries (India, Indonesia, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Vietnam), Albania and Malta. This latter finding seems to be consistent with the fact that body and appearance dissatisfaction is very common in industrialized countries22 and that “in more sociocentric societies, the body is an entity for shared care by close social groups rather than an individual entity that can be changed individually”43.

Since, as hypothesized, satisfaction with the way you look mediates the relationship between satisfaction with your health and a global measure of SWB, and the variables age, gender and country are important in this mediation, it is hoped that the observed results will not only increase our knowledge of the contribution that satisfaction with the way you look has for health and well-being evaluations in these age groups and for different countries and genders, but also lead to the adoption of a broader and more preventive approach to improving such evaluations. Interventions aimed at feeling better about oneself should therefore be included in public policies to improve health and well-being at these ages. Specifically, Meland et al.36 stated that body image is an important target for interventions to improve subjective health in adolescents because of the importance of physical appearance to their social identity. This same opinion is shared by Lemes et al.44, who believe that adopting a positive body image approach can help health care services to be more accessible to adolescents and more effective in their work.

This idea is further supported by studies analysing adolescent development, which highlight the extensive structural and functional brain changes that take place during this stage and their impact on self-perception, emotional regulation, and sensitivity to social evaluation45,46. According to Sawyer et al.47, adolescence spans from age 10 to 24 and represents a distinct developmental period characterized by profound biological, psychological, and social transitions. Within this framework, the importance of physical appearance increases significantly, as adolescents begin to construct their identity in relation to the peer group and become especially responsive to feedback regarding their social image.

This study is not exempt from limitations. First, given the cross-sectional design, no causal conclusions can be drawn from the mediation analysis. Longitudinal research would be required to assess the directionality of the observed relationships. Second, satisfaction with the way you look and satisfaction with your health may be interpreted differently not only across countries but also among adolescents within the same cultural context, which limits the comparability of responses. In this regard, a qualitative approach could enrich our understanding of how appearance satisfaction mediates the relationship between health and overall subjective well-being (SWB). Third, the use of single-item measures for satisfaction with health and appearance, while efficient in large-scale international surveys, may reduce the reliability and depth of the constructs measured. Moreover, although cultural differences were acknowledged, the analyses were carried out solely at the individual level. Future studies should consider multilevel modelling to better account for context-level influences. Finally, the mediation model would benefit from a more comprehensive theoretical framework -not yet available in the scientific literature- to explain why satisfaction with one’s appearance might play a mediating role in this developmental stage. Future avenues for research could also include the affective components of SWB (e.g., positive and negative affect) and indicators of psychological well-being in order to address a more holistic understanding of adolescent well-being.
