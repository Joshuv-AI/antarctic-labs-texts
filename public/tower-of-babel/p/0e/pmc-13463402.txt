The effect of Orem’s self-care model on self-esteem and resilience among hospitalized patients with major depressive disorder: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Amir Mohammad Dorosti, Amin Soheili, Zeinab Habibpour, Mir Amirhossein Seyednazari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMajor Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a debilitating psychiatric condition that severely impairs patients’ self-care capabilities, self-esteem, and resilience. Dorothea Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Theory offers a systematic nursing framework to transition patients from passive care recipients to active self-managers. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of a nursing intervention based on Orem’s self-care model on self-esteem and resilience among hospitalized patients with MDD.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial was conducted from February to May 2026 among 60 patients diagnosed with MDD who were hospitalized in the psychiatric ward of Shahid Madani Hospital in Khoy, Iran. Patients were selected via convenience sampling and randomly assigned to either the intervention group (n = 30) or the control group (n = 30) using block randomization. The intervention group received 5 sessions of a tailored self-care program based on Orem’s supportive-educative system, alongside routine clinical care. The control group received only standard hospital care. Data were collected at baseline (pre-test), immediately post-intervention (post-test), and at a one-month follow-up using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES) and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC). Data were analyzed using SPSS software via descriptive statistics and Repeated Measures ANOVA.ResultsBaseline demographic and clinical characteristics were homogeneous between the two groups (P > 0.05). Mauchly’s test confirmed sphericity for both self-esteem (W = 0.992, P = 0.788) and resilience (W = 0.953, P = 0.252) variables. The Repeated Measures ANOVA demonstrated a significant time-group interaction effect for both self-esteem (F = 10.11, P < 0.001, Partial Eta Squared = 0.15) and resilience (F = 6.59, P = 0.002, Partial Eta Squared = 0.10). At post-test, the intervention group exhibited significantly higher self-esteem (Mean = 18.33, 95% CI: [17.08, 19.58]) and resilience (Mean = 55.70, 95% CI: [52.00, 59.40]) compared to the control group (Self-esteem Mean = 12.03, 95% CI: [10.78, 13.28]; Resilience Mean = 42.07, 95% CI: [38.37, 45.77]) (P < 0.001), which remained stable at the one-month follow-up.ConclusionPsychiatric nurses can integrate this structured, low-cost, and empowering model into routine clinical practice to foster autonomy and reinforce essential psychological capacities—specifically self-esteem and resilience—in hospitalized psychiatric patients.Trial registrationIranian Registry of Clinical Trials, IRCT20250723066609N3. Introduction

One of the most common, limiting, and serious mental illnesses in the world is Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) [1]. Persistent depression, anhedonia, widespread feelings of worthlessness, cognitive deterioration, and a variety of crippling physical symptoms are its clinical characteristics [2]. Recent estimates from the Global Burden of Illness study indicate that MDD is one of the major causes of the world’s illness burden and is expected to overtake all other causes of disability by 2030 [3]. In Iran, similarly, epidemiological surveillance highlights an extremely alarming increasing trend in this disorder’s incidence, placing a significant financial and socioeconomic burden on the country’s healthcare system [4].

A significant percentage of patients with MDD do not receive an efficient or ongoing continuum of care, despite the availability and development of many therapeutic and psychopharmacological protocols [5, 6]. Systemic disparities in accessing specialized psychiatric services, a lack of mental health literacy, and ongoing social stigma are the main causes of this therapeutic gap [7, 8]. As a result, these people experience lengthy, incapacitating symptom trajectories and high rates of recurring relapses [9, 10]. In light of these difficulties, modern mental health research has made it a top goal to find and develop strong intrapersonal psychological mechanisms that can serve as protective limitations against the intensity of acute symptoms [11].

Self-esteem and resilience are two of these protective intrapersonal variables commonly acknowledged as fundamental elements influencing the long-term therapeutic management of MDD and predicting recovery trajectories [12, 13]. Self-esteem, which reflects an individual’s global evaluation of their inherent worth and competence, plays a vital role in maintaining psychological integrity [14]. Chronically low levels of self-esteem not only serve as an established cognitive vulnerability factor for the onset of depressive episodes but also exacerbate symptom severity by trapping the patient in maladaptive cycles of self-blame [15]. Conversely, psychological resilience is conceptualized as a dynamic capacity for adaptive functioning and cognitive flexibility, enabling individuals to regain psychological equilibrium after encountering severe life stressors and acute crises [16]. Patients possessing higher baseline resilience experience substantially lower psychological burnout and demonstrate superior clinical and therapeutic outcomes [17, 18]. Current empirical evidence confirms a synergistic, bidirectional interplay between self-esteem and resilience, suggesting that interventions targeted at elevating either construct can effectively disrupt the vulnerability cascade in depression, fostering sustained clinical recovery [19].

Given the chronic and highly recurrent nature of MDD, contemporary psychiatric care paradigms increasingly advocate for a crucial shift—moving away from models that view patients as passive care recipients toward evidence-based strategies that emphasize active, structured empowerment [20]. In this context, Dorothea Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Theory stands out as one of the most conceptually coherent and well-structured nursing frameworks focused on fostering client autonomy, personal responsibility, and enhancing self-care agency [21]. This theoretical framework posits that while individuals inherently possess the potential to develop and maintain adaptive self-care patterns, acute health crises—such as a severe episode of major depression—precipitate a profound “self-care deficit” where the individual’s therapeutic self-care demands vastly exceed their current self-care agency. Under these clinical conditions, psychiatric nursing interventions become essential, functioning as a “supportive-educative system” to act as a catalyst, mobilizing and rebuilding the patient’s latent psychological and behavioral capacities to systematically restore independence.

Although Orem’s self-care model is clinically effective in improving quality of life and reducing distress in a variety of chronic somatic illnesses, its practical application to deeply ingrained psychological constructs like resilience and self-esteem among patients with MDD—particularly during the acute inpatient hospitalization phase—remains largely unexplored. The vast majority of historical studies have focused almost exclusively on physiological parameters or superficial symptom metrics, leaving a distinct gap in the literature regarding how theory-driven, nursing-led self-care protocols affect internal psychological capacities. To create evidence-based, extremely economical psychiatric nursing procedures that can be easily incorporated into current mental health settings, this empirical gap must be filled.

While Orem’s self-care model has traditionally been applied to somatic conditions, its theoretical components are highly relevant to psychiatric populations, particularly those with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). In acute depression, patients experience a profound self-care deficit characterized by learned helplessness, low motivation, and diminished personal agency. Within Orem’s framework, the supportive-educative nursing system does not merely address physical needs; it acts as a structured mechanism for behavioral activation and cognitive restructuring. By mobilizing the patient’s latent capacities through progressive, goal-directed daily routines, the intervention systematically rebuilds a sense of mastery and autonomy. Consequently, this increase in self-care agency directly reinforces the patient’s global self-esteem and enhances psychological resilience, allowing them to counter depressive cognitive distortions adaptively.

To provide a robust, evidence-based response to this clinical challenge, the present study was designed and executed to evaluate the explicit effects of a structured nursing intervention based on Orem’s Self-Care Model on self-esteem and resilience among hospitalized patients diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted as a parallel-group randomized controlled trial (RCT). The primary objective was to investigate the longitudinal therapeutic effects of a theory-driven nursing intervention, meticulously designed based on Dorothea Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Theory, on intrapersonal psychological mechanisms—specifically self-esteem and resilience—among hospitalized psychiatric patients diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). The trial was conducted between February 10, 2026, and May 20, 2026, within the acute psychiatric inpatient unit of Shahid Madani Hospital, a major referral medical center affiliated with Khoy University of Medical Sciences, located in Khoy, Iran.

The target population comprised adult patients hospitalized with a definitive clinical diagnosis of MDD. Participants were recruited utilizing convenience sampling based on the following pre-established inclusion criteria: a primary diagnosis of MDD established by a board-certified psychiatrist based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria; age ranging between 18 and 65 years; ability to communicate effectively in Persian or Azerbaijani; and a baseline clinical stability sufficient to actively participate in cognitive and educational sessions as determined by the attending treatment team. Patients were systematically excluded if they presented with secondary comorbid neurodevelopmental disorders, acute cognitive impairment, or active substance dependence (excluding nicotine). Furthermore, patients who experienced acute clinical deterioration requiring transfers to intensive care, or those who chose to withdraw their informed consent at any point, were excluded from the final analysis.

Sample size

The sample size for this randomized controlled trial was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7) for a two-tailed independent-samples t-test to compare the means of two independent groups. Due to the paucity of identical clinical trials implementing Orem-based models for self-esteem and resilience specifically among acute psychiatric MDD inpatients, a conservative medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.55) was assumed. Sampling and randomization

Sampling was initially conducted using a convenience sampling approach from eligible patients with major depression admitted to the psychiatric ward of Shahid Madani Hospital in Khoy. After selecting 60 eligible patients, participants were randomly allocated into two groups: intervention (n = 30) and control (n = 30). To ensure an exact balance of members between the two groups throughout the recruitment process, a block randomization technique with a block size of 4 was used.

Allocation concealment and blinding

To guarantee allocation concealment and prevent selection bias, sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE) were used. These envelopes were prepared by an independent researcher who had no involvement in patient recruitment or clinical care. This study adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. Due to the educational and interactive nature of Orem’s self-care model intervention, blinding the patients and the intervention provider was not feasible; however, a single-anonymized design was rigorously maintained. The outcome assessor (the individual administering the questionnaires) and the data analyst were kept completely blinded to the group assignments. To maintain assessor blinding, the post-intervention and follow-up data collection sessions were conducted in a separate counseling room by an independent nurse who was not present during the intervention delivery and did not have access to the randomization log.

Ethical considerations and trial registration

This clinical trial was strictly conducted in adherence to the ethical tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol received formal institutional authorization and ethical approval from the Regional Research Ethics Committee of Khoy University of Medical Sciences (Approval Code: IR.KHOY.REC.1404.058). Before initiating any study procedures, the primary investigator provided comprehensive oral and written explanations regarding the study objectives, risk-benefit ratios, and the absolute confidentiality of personal data. All eligible participants provided written, voluntarily signed informed consent forms. Additionally, the trial was prospectively registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (Trial ID: 88937, Registry ID: IRCT20250723066609N3) on February 6, 2026.

Intervention Protocol

Participants allocated to the control group received only the standard, routine psychiatric ward care, which included prescribed psychopharmacotherapy, basic clinical nursing care, and standard hospital discharge planning.

In contrast, participants in the intervention group received the standard ward care supplemented by a structured, theory-driven self-care program based directly on Orem’s supportive-educative system. This specialized intervention was delivered through 5 structured, face-to-face, interactive sessions conducted over consecutive days. Each session lasted approximately 45 to 60 min and was conducted individually in a quiet counseling room within the psychiatric ward. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0). Quantitative demographic and clinical data were summarized using descriptive parameters, including mean values, standard deviations (SD), frequencies, and percentage distributions. Baseline homogeneity between the intervention and control arms for categorical demographic variables was assessed using the Chi-Square or Fisher’s Exact test, and independent-samples t-tests were used for continuous variables.

To examine the primary hypotheses regarding the clinical efficacy of the Orem-based intervention over time, Repeated Measures Analysis of Variance (RM-ANOVA) was performed for both primary outcomes (self-esteem and resilience). The within-subject factor was Time (comprising three levels: baseline, post-test, and 1-month follow-up), and the between-subject factor was Group (Intervention vs. Control). Before conducting the RM-ANOVA, essential parametric assumptions were verified: normality of distribution was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the assumption of sphericity was explicitly evaluated using Mauchly’s test of sphericity. In cases where the sphericity assumption was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied to adjust the degrees of freedom. Pairwise post-hoc comparisons were executed using Bonferroni corrections to locate specific time-point differences. A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was established as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

A total of 60 hospitalized patients with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) completed the trial. It was included in the final analysis (n = 30 in the intervention group and n = 30 in the control group) (Fig. 1). The overall mean age of the sample was 34.50 ± 9.30 years in the intervention arm and 36.07 ± 9.27 years in the control arm, demonstrating no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.516).

The effect of Orem’s Self-Care Intervention on Self-Esteem

The parametric Repeated Measures ANOVA revealed a significant within-subjects main effect of Time (F = 19.24, P < 0.001, ηp² = 0.25), indicating that global self-esteem scores changed substantially across the three temporal milestones. Crucially, a robust and statistically significant Time x Group interaction effect was observed (F = 10.11, P < 0.001, ηp² = 0.15), establishing that the structural trajectory of change in self-esteem differed fundamentally between the intervention and control arms over time. The between-subjects analysis further demonstrated a significant main effect for the Group factor (F = 59.04, P < 0.001, ηp² = 0.50). As displayed in Table 3, pairwise post-hoc comparisons using Bonferroni corrections confirmed that while baseline self-esteem scores were statistically equivalent between the two groups (P > 0.05), the intervention group exhibited a marked and clinical increase in self-esteem immediately post-intervention (18.33 ± 3.62) compared to the control group (12.03 ± 3.09). This therapeutic benefit was effectively sustained at the one-month longitudinal follow-up interval (18.00 ± 3.90 vs. 12.67 ± 4.24).

The effect of Orem’s self-care intervention on resilience

Regarding the psychological resilience domain, Mauchly’s test indicated that the baseline assumption of sphericity was satisfied (Chi-Square = 2.755, P = 0.252); hence, sphericity-assumed values were reported. The univariate analysis revealed a significant within-subject main effect for Time (F = 12.94, P < 0.001, ηp² = 0.18) and a significant Time x Group interaction effect (F = 6.59, P = 0.002, ηp² = 0.10). The between-subjects test verified a significant main effect for Group (F = 29.35, P < 0.001, ηp² = 0.34). As illustrated by the clinical data in Table 4, the mean resilience scores in the Orem-based intervention group increased from a baseline of 41.03 ± 9.31 to 55.70 ± 10.24 at the immediate post-test. This psychological adaptation remained stable through the one-month follow-up period (54.00 ± 11.99). In contrast, the control group exhibited negligible fluctuations across all assessment intervals (40.30 ± 8.13 → 42.07 ± 9.68 → 43.53 ± 10.29).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that an intervention grounded in Orem’s self-care model significantly improved self-esteem and resilience in patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) compared to a control group. Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a statistically significant time x group interaction effect for both self-esteem (F = 10.11, P < 0.001, ηp² = 0.15) and resilience (F = 6.59, P = 0.002, ηp² = 0.10). These findings suggest that an empowerment-based approach can yield meaningful psychological benefits even in severe psychiatric conditions. Thus, Orem’s model may extend beyond physical disorder management to improve psychological and adaptive outcomes in psychiatric settings, as evidenced by the substantial group effect sizes observed here (ηp² = 0.50 for self-esteem and 0.34 for resilience).

The theoretical foundations of Orem’s self-care paradigm may account for the observed improvements [24, 25]. The paradigm places a strong emphasis on each person’s ability to recognize their own needs, take an active role in providing care, and adopt habits that promote health and wellbeing. Deep helplessness, low motivation, low self-esteem, and a sense of losing control over their everyday life are common symptoms among MDD patients [26]. Interventions that transition patients from passive recipients to active participants in their care can therefore restore autonomy, enhance perceived control, and reinforce personal value. Accordingly, the marked increase in self-esteem within the intervention group (from 12.27 ± 2.82 at baseline to 18.33 ± 3.62 post-test) likely stems from strengthened self-care agency, greater behavioral independence, and consistent engagement in health-promoting routines.

These findings regarding self-esteem align with previous literature [24, 27]. However, a crucial conceptual distinction emerges when contextualizing these results within the broader research literature. While prior applications of Orem’s model have predominantly targeted physiological outcomes in chronic somatic conditions such as asthma and hemodialysis [24, 25], the present study demonstrates its efficacy in a psychiatric context. In physical illnesses, self-care primarily facilitates disease management and physiological regulation; conversely, in MDD, Orem’s core components—particularly need recognition and corrective action—appear to function as cognitive-behavioral restructuring mechanisms. This interpretation aligns with self-efficacy theory, which posits that perceived personal competence is a prerequisite for sustained emotional and behavioral change in mood disorders. Consequently, self-care should not be viewed merely as a set of physical or habitual behaviors, but as a dynamic process that positively influences deeper psychological dimensions, including self-worth and emotional regulation [27, 28]. The effect sizes observed here are comparable to those reported in somatic populations, indicating that Orem’s framework is sufficiently adaptable to transcend physical disorder management and serve as a robust empowerment tool for psychological adaptation.

Another key finding was the significant improvement in resilience within the intervention group. Mauchly’s test indicated that the baseline assumption of sphericity was satisfied (Chi-Square = 2.755, P = 0.252); hence, sphericity-assumed values were reported. The analysis confirmed a significant time x group interaction effect (F = 6.59, P = 0.002, ηp² = 0.10). The intervention group’s resilience score rose from 41.03 ± 9.31 at baseline to 55.70 ± 10.24 post-test and remained stable at follow-up (54.00 ± 11.99), whereas the control group exhibited minimal change across assessments (40.30 ± 8.13 → 42.07 ± 9.68 → 43.53 ± 10.29). This trajectory holds considerable clinical relevance, as resilience is central to adapting to chronic psychiatric conditions, tolerating psychological distress, and restoring daily functioning. The observed gains likely reflect enhanced adaptive coping, increased perceived mastery, and reduced learned helplessness.

The improvement in resilience can also be understood within a broader conceptual framework in which self-care and resilience mutually reinforce one another [29–31]. Greater engagement in self‑care may increase adaptive capacity and coping ability, while stronger resilience may help maintain self‑care despite psychological challenges. The sustained effect observed at follow‑up in our study supports this bidirectional perspective [28, 30].

These findings are consistent with previous research showing that improved mental health indicators and decreased psychological vulnerability are associated with stronger self-care skills [25, 28, 32]. The observed increases in resilience and self-esteem are consistent with the broader paradigm linking self-care to psychological well-being, even though depression severity was not measured as an outcome, thereby limiting causal inference about symptom reduction [26, 30]. Standardized depression severity scores should be used as variables in future research to see if these psychological improvements are unrelated to improvements in mood symptoms.

The current results are also indirectly consistent with intervention studies in chronic physical illnesses [24, 29]. Thus, Orem’s model may extend beyond physical disorder management to improve psychological and adaptive outcomes in psychiatric settings, as evidenced by the substantial group effect sizes observed here (ηp² = 0.50 for self-esteem and 0.34 for resilience).

These findings have significant clinical implications for psychiatric nurses. Low motivation, poor treatment adherence, and social/behavioral withdrawal are common symptoms of MDD. Patients can successfully regain a feeling of personal agency by actively participating in goal-setting, daily self-monitoring, and skill-building through structured, step-by-step self-care protocols. With the help of Orem’s concept, nurses can promote active recovery and long-term self-management in addition to symptom management. Therefore, these findings can directly influence empowerment-based therapies, psychoeducational programming, and nurse care planning in psychiatric facilities [29]. It is important to note that the proposed relationship between improved self-care, cognitive-behavioral restructuring, and actual depressive symptom reduction remains speculative in this study. Since depression severity and self-care agency were not directly measured using standardized scales, these mechanisms are presented as plausible hypotheses rather than definitive causal conclusions. Future research is required to evaluate these mediating pathways objectively.

Limitations

Despite these promising outcomes, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the study was conducted at a single center with a relatively modest sample size (n = 60), which may constrain the generalizability of the findings. Second, the follow-up period was short; extended longitudinal tracking is necessary to confirm the durability of improvements in self-esteem and resilience. Third, baseline depression severity was not objectively quantified using a standardized psychiatric rating scale. Since depression severity is a critical clinical variable that can significantly influence a patient’s baseline self-care agency and subsequent capacity to engage in supportive-educative sessions, the lack of this measurement limits our ability to analyze how the intervention’s efficacy might vary across strata of clinical severity. Future multi-center clinical trials should explicitly integrate standardized depression severity indices as potential moderators or covariates.

In conclusion, the implementation of a structured nursing intervention based on Orem’s Self-Care Deficit Theory successfully enhanced global self-esteem and psychological resilience among hospitalized patients diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). These significant psychological improvements remained stable over a one-month longitudinal follow-up period. Psychiatric nurses can integrate this practical, low-cost, and empowering model into clinical routines to foster autonomy and reinforce essential psychological capacities in acute inpatient mental health settings.
