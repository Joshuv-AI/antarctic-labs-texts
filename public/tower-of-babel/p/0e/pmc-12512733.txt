Unexpected necrotizing fasciitis in first day post laparoscopic hernia repair caused by rare species of Aeromonas; case report
Authors: Khaled Albedah, Ahad Alotaibi, Sohail Alelaiyan, Anfal Altamimi, Raed Alshalfan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

She had a history of incisional hernia and underwent open repair of an incisional hernia in 2021. She had a recurrence and underwent laparoscopic incisional hernia repair with mesh in 2022. Two years later, she presented to the Emergency Department with features of adhesive small bowel obstruction and recurrence of incisional hernia, which was soft and reducible, managed conservatively, and planned for elective repair on the same admission. Introduction

Necrotizing fasciitis (NF) is a rare skin and soft tissue infection (SSTI) that is a rapidly progressive life-threatening condition secondary to sepsis, systemic toxicity, and multi-organ failure ([1]). Historically, the first modern description of necrotizing fasciitis was described by Confederate medical officers of the American Civil War, Joseph Jones, following the American Civil War (1861–1865) [1] [2]. In 1990, NF became more popular and described as “flesh-eating bacteria” in patients with certain risk factors such as impaired immunity, diabetes mellitus, and intravenous drug abuse, with mortality rates up to 50% (2). The causative organisms in necrotizing fasciitis could be polymicrobial infection due to the synergistic effect of aerobic and anaerobic bacteria or mono-microbial infection caused by group A Streptococcus ([3]). Aeromonas is a Gram-negative bacteria comprising 36 species of Gram-negative, non-spore-forming, facultatively anaerobic, oxidase, and catalase-positive bacilli. These bacteria grow in aquatic environments of fresh and salt water, food such as fish, meat, dairy products, and fresh vegetables ([3, 4]). A retrospective study conducted over 18 years, from 2001 to 2019, found that most Aeromonas SSTIs are associated with environmental exposure and commonly occur during summer ([5]). Poor outcomes and significantly high mortality have been reported in patients with monomicrobial Aeromonas NF compared with polymicrobial Aeromonas or Aeromonas with other Clostridial species ([5]). Monomicrobial Aeromonas were reported in immunocompromised patients such as cancer patients and patients with liver cirrhosis ([5]). Furthermore, a higher mortality rate was reported in NF patients who have cancer, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and hepatic dysfunction ([5]). Few reported cases in the literature describe Aeromonas species as causative organisms of NF, and most affected patients were immunocompromised and had risk factors for exposure to freshwater fishing ([6–8]). Aeromonas infection is rare SSTI and is mainly presented in patients with multiple comorbidities, hematological malignancy, or hepatobiliary diseases ([9–11]). All reported cases in literature with Aeromonas veronii had active malignancy or exposure to aquatic environments or seafood ([12, 13]). We are reporting a rare presentation of Necrotizing Fasciitis in first-day laparoscopic Hernia Repair with no apparent risk factor, as the patient has been cancer-free since 2018.

Case presentation

We are presenting a 60-year-old female patient, obese patient with BMI of 30, known to have high-grade serous ovarian cancer stage 3 C, who received three cycles of neo-adjuvant chemotherapy, underwent total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy in June 2018, and received three cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy that was completed in September 2018. The patient has a strong family history of breast cancer with an affected mother and sister; genetic testing revealed a BRCA 1 mutation. The patient was referred to the breast surgery clinic for further care. Initially, she refused prophylactic bilateral mastectomy as it would reduce the risk of developing breast cancer, and she preferred to follow with images, and she continued following till 2022. During this period, she developed an incisional hernia and underwent open repair of an incisional hernia with mesh in July 2021. Then developed a recurrence and underwent laparoscopic incisional hernia repair in November 2022. The patient then agreed to go for prophylactic mastectomy, and she underwent bilateral nipple-sparing risk-reducing mastectomy with immediate reconstruction with bilateral latissimus dorsi flap and implant insertion in October 2023.

On the 24th of May 2024, she presented to the Emergency Department with a complaint of generalized abdominal pain and vomiting for five days, passing bowel motion and flatus a day before her presentation after taking laxatives. She was clinically stable, with a soft abdomen and reducible soft, non-tender recurrent incisional hernia; digital rectal examination revealed an empty rectum. The laboratory workup was normal, except for lactic acid of 2.1, which responded to fluid resuscitation. The patient was admitted, treated conservatively, kept nil per os (NPO), on intravenous fluid (IVF), a nasogastric tube (NGT) inserted, and kept on low intermittent suction. She improved clinically, passing normal bowel motion and flatus on the 3rd day of the admission. The patient prepared for an elective incisional hernia repair on the same admission. The endocrinology team was involved as the patient gave a history of subclinical hypothyroidism, and her repeated thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) during admission was 10.8 mlU/L; she started on levothyroxine 25 mcg and cleared for surgery with low-risk stratification. Cardiac echocardiography (Echo) was unremarkable. Albumin pre-operatively was 40 g/L.

The patient underwent laparoscopic incisional hernia repair with mesh on the 29th of May, 2024—intraoperative findings of adherent small bowel to the abdominal wall and previous mesh dissolved with tacks in place. Two hernial defects were identified, one in the epigastric area measuring around 2 × 2 cm, containing a small bowel. Another suprapubic defect of about 4 × 4 cm did not contain bowel, omentum, or fat. Adhesiolysis is carried out using sharp dissection. Soft adhesions were taken down, then the bowel was taken down completely, and the hernia sac was taken down as well. Blunt dissection was then carried out. The abdominal wall was cleared completely, no bleeding, no bowel injury, and both hernia defects were identified. The epigastric hernia defect was closed with the PDS #1 EndoClosure device in a figure-of-eight manner, and the suprapubic defect was also closed. Planned to apply permanent mesh, two SYNECOR intraperitoneal meshes 12 cm circle were prepared. The first mesh was used at the suprapubic previous hernia site and was deployed nicely, fixed with tacks, avoiding injury to the urinary bladder and bowel. Then, another mesh was applied to the epigastric hernia defect with the same fixation and was deployed nicely. The patient tolerated the surgery without immediate complications, extubated, and returned to the surgical ward.

On 1 st day postoperatively, the patient became hypotensive with a low blood pressure of 93\46 mmHg and low urine output since post-op, tachycardia of 121, low saturation of 93% in room air, no fever, and no tachypnea. She denied abdomen pain but had active central chest pain. An electrocardiogram (ECG) showed sinus tachycardia, no ischemic changes, and Troponin I 0.016 pg/L; the cardiology team was involved and asked for serial ECG and Trop I. A bedside echo showed nothing significant apart from a collapsed inferior vena cava (IV). IVF Boluses were given, and a Foley catheter was inserted. The Abdomen was soft, with surgical site tenderness, wound dressing exposed, and there was a hematoma-like over one of the port sites, marked to be monitored if extended. The patient did not respond to resuscitation; venous blood gases (VBG) test was done, which showed PH 7.14, PCO2 42, HCO3 14.3, Base deficit − 14.7, Hgb 12, lactic acid 6.3, Intensive care team (ICU) involved immediately, initial impression hypovolemic shock secondary to hematoma/bleeding, pulmonary embolism (PE), septic shock, or bowel ischemia.

Sodium Bicarbonate infusion started; Norepinephrine started peripherally while preparing for the central line. A septic workup was sent, and Meropenem, Vancomycin, and Caspofungin were started. The patient was sent to Chest CT angio to rule out pulmonary embolism and Abdomen and pelvis CT to rule out bowel ischemia. The patient clinically deteriorated and was placed on a non-rebreather mask 15 L as she was desatting. Increased requirement of inotropes. She was then intubated. The patient was re-examined; the previously noted hematoma extended and developed subcutaneous gases, wound clips opened, and dishwater-like fluid emerged. The patient was taken immediately to the operating room for debridement. Extensive debridement was done for the affected area till it reached healthy tissues and fat. The camera scope was introduced through an 11 mm port from one of the previous surgical ports. The bowel was healthy, with no pus, and the mesh was intact and clean. The patient’s hemodynamics improved, and inotropic support was tapered down. The patient planned for 2nd session of debridement. Unfortunately, she deteriorated the next day and developed non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) and acute kidney injury. She started on Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy (CCRT), and the requirement for ventilation increased. Discussion and conclusion

Necrotizing fasciitis is one of the most dreadful infections as it is rapidly progressive and.

carries a high mortality rate [14]. Early diagnosis of NF is vital for the outcome. However, diagnosis at the presentation time is challenging and reached in only 13–34% of the cases [1].

In 80% of NF, there is skin disruption either by trauma or wound incision [1]. Aeromonas veronii can ferment D-glucose to produce acid and gas, and synthesizes aerolysin, which has hemolytic and cytotoxic activities, which can cause disseminated intravascular gas production and may cause death [3]. Symptoms of NF vary based on the time of infection. Early symptoms in the first 24 h usually are pain out of proportion, dehydration, and flu-like symptoms. Within 3 to 4 days, advanced symptoms are swelling, purplish rash, blisters with blackish fluid, and mottled skin—critical presentation including a severe drop in blood pressure, septic shock, and drop in consciousness [15]. In our case, the patient was critically ill within 24 h postoperatively; she showed signs of septic shock earlier in the first day with a severe drop in blood pressure, low urine output, and alerted level of consciousness.

Necrotizing fasciitis is classified into two types based on the type of the organisms: type.

I is polymicrobial, and type II is monomicrobial, either gram-positive or gram-negative.

organisms [1]. Our patient’s tissue culture came positive for Aeromonas vironii, a rare organism. Aeromonas are gram-negative bacteria widely present in aqueous environments and are usually associated with gastrointestinal symptoms [16]. Nonetheless, it has also been identified as a causative agent of cellulitis and soft tissue infections [16]. Most of the reported cases have been associated with risk factors such as liver cirrhosis, chronic kidney disease, immunocompromised, and hematological malignancies [9–16]. In contrast, our patient did not have chronic comorbidities, apart from a history of ovarian cancer that was treated in 2018 with no recurrence in surveillance. Our patient did not have an apparent risk factor explaining her presentation and rapid deterioration. We hypothesized that the source of infection could be related to the ingestion of unknown contaminated food or water with obstruction, which predisposed the organism to translocate after it was relieved conservatively. Similarly, few reported cases have supported this hypothesis, given that this organism is invasive and usually presents.

with negative blood cultures [17]– [18].

In Literature, postoperative sepsis is considered a surgical complication associated with any type of infection that can lead to sepsis, severe sepsis, or septic shock, with a mortality rate reaching up to 25% [19].

Early initiation of empirical antibiotics and extensive surgical debridement are the cornerstones for NF management. Despite early management, mortality and morbidity rates remain high. In this case, the patient experienced a severe fulminant infection that rapidly deteriorated, even with early detection and intervention. Similar instances have been reported in two other cases involving the exact causative organism, where rapid deterioration led to no survival [7, 20].

In conclusion, Aeromonas veronii is a very atypical and rare organism for NF with.

unexpected presentation and outcome. Significantly high mortality has been reported. Early detection, administration of appropriate antibiotics, and extensive surgical debridement are crucial in any suspected skin and soft tissue infection.
