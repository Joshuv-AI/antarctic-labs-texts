Global lessons: a comparative analysis of nuclear gamification's impact in American and British students
Authors: Sarah Elizabeth Lu, Shaun Hemming, Jamie Purkis, Stephen P. LaMont
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The global nuclear skills shortage demands urgent educational investment, particularly for students aged 11–18. To help address this, we have introduced RAD Ratings, an interactive card game designed to improve the teaching of nuclear concepts to students, which includes subjects such as half-lives, types of radiation, and radionuclide applications. RAD Ratings has already been successfully piloted in the United Kingdom and in this paper, we present a comparison in selected American schools for the first time. Snapshot survey results show RAD Ratings improve interest in nuclear science and careers, offering insights into gamification’s potential to improve engagement across different geographic locations.

Introduction

As the global demand for skilled professionals in the nuclear sector continues to grow, there is an urgent need to inspire the next generation to want to work in nuclear science and related areas [1–4]. This includes making complex topics such as radioactivity, radionuclides, and isotopes, which students typically struggle to understand [5–7], more accessible and engaging. Traditional approaches to nuclear skills, such as summer schools and structured enrichment programs like the ‘Harper Academy 4 Future Nuclear Engineers’ [8] and Los Alamos Physics Summer Camp [9], can help fill this need, but they can be expensive to run and typically attract high performing and/or students who are already interested in science. Additionally, outreach activities conducted by national laboratories and academic institutions are insufficient to effectively disseminate knowledge evenly across a large student population outside structured programs [10, 11]. Safety concerns with radioactivity further limit hands-on activities [12, 13]. Alternative approaches are therefore needed to help teach nuclear skills to students aged 11–18.

One emerging approach is to use games, or ‘gamification’ [12–17]. We recently created RAD Ratings [15], to improve students’ conceptual knowledge and foster early interest in careers related to nuclear science. RAD Ratings is designed for students aged 11–18 (Fig. 1) and provides an accessible, interactive learning experience with two modes of play: a Top Trumps® style game for small groups of 3 to 6 students, and a “best fit” game designed for whole-class participation, facilitated by a teacher or instructor. Both games encourage active participation and help simplify abstract scientific concepts. However, we wished to test the game’s effectiveness with different audiences, especially internationally. Accordingly, we present this study which evaluates the effectiveness of RAD Ratings in improving students' knowledge and interest in nuclear science in a test area of the United States (USA), compared to a similar study in the United Kingdom (UK). To our knowledge, this is the first nuclear gamification study to compare outcomes between two different geographical locations: a pilot study conducted in New Mexico, USA, and a study in the UK.

Methods

The study surveyed 50 students (aged 11–18) in New Mexico, USA, who provided feedback on the RAD Ratings game. These results were compared with publicly available data from 98 students in the UK [15] who participated in a similar study. Questions were asked using a closed Likert scale (from 1 to 5, 1 = Strongly Disagree and 5 = Strongly Agree). To ensure questions were accessible to all students we used simple language, which also helped us ensure thoughtful responses. We also avoided asking too many questions, recognising that time is limited in a classroom. While teacher feedback and alternative qualitative methods (focus groups, interviews) were used for the UK cohort, these were not incorporated in the USA Cohort due to time and logistical constraints. The timing of data collection also varied slightly between the two cohorts; in the USA Cohort, questionnaires were administered immediately before and after gameplay, whereas in the UK, questionnaires were completed only once (after gameplay), with students asked to retrospectively assess their prior knowledge. To minimise social desirability bias we emphasised that responses were anonymous, and that there were no “right” or “wrong” answers. Despite these measures, social desirability bias cannot be ruled out. No formal follow-up surveys were conducted beyond these time points, limiting insights into longer-term retention or attitude shifts.. We recognise the limitations of the data collection methods, and so we comment on general trends rather than provide detailed statistical analysis. Our aim is to provide preliminary insights into the effectiveness of gamification (to teach nuclear skills) across two different geographies. Detailed methods on game mechanics, materials and lesson plans that were used in this study can be found in Lu et al. 2024 or RAD Ratings website [15].

Results and discussion

A summary of changes in student scores before and after playing is given in Table 3. For the first set of (learning outcome) questions, playing RAD Ratings appears to enhance student self-reporting of their own learning. Prior to playing RAD Ratings, students in the USA Cohort exhibited low awareness of the term “radionuclides” with an average score of 1.92 (Table 3), which increased to an average of 3.93 after playing RAD Ratings. This follows similar trends for students in the UK Cohort (before: 1.96, after: 2.86). Similarly, student understanding of what radionuclides are (question 2) and providing examples of radionuclides and how they could be used (question 3) improved for both cohorts, though less of an increase was seen for the UK cohort. There was little difference before and after playing for students interested in learning more about radioactivity and its uses (question 4). The increase in awareness and understanding scores (questions 1, 2 and 3) following the RAD Ratings activity suggests that the game provides modest improvements in supporting student learning about radionuclides after just one session. The USA group exhibited a more pronounced improvement than the UK group, which may reflect differences in baseline knowledge or the timing/method of data collection. One possibility is that the USA participants had lower level of prior knowledge on radioactivty, leaving more room for measurable progress during the study period. Alternatively, the timing and method of data collection could have influenced the results. For instance, the USA group was assessed after a longer instructional period of 70–90 min (due to availability of lesson blocks) and UK students were assessed after 45–50 min, this could have contributed to their greater gains. Differences in the contextual relevance of the material may also have played a role; for example, in New Mexico, nuclear science has particular historical significance, which may have heightened students' engagement with the topic. Further work to study the impact of pre-intervention scores and the structure of the learning environments would help clarify the reasons behind this disparity but is beyond the scope of this study. The UK and USA educational systems differ significantly in how they structure and deliver curricula [19]. The UK operates under a National Curriculum, which is a set framework of subjects, content, and attainment levels that most state schools must follow [20], therefore in the UK, there is more consistency across schools nationwide. In contrast, the USA has a decentralized system [21] where local school districts and even individual schools often determine their own curriculum, leading to variations even within the same state. The game experience was also positively received, with students across all questions in the second set (Questions 5–9) agreeing with the questions above a neutral score of 3. Students agreed mostly strongly with question 6 (liking card design; USA Cohort average 4.45, UK Cohort 3.86), while also suggesting agreement with question 5 (enjoying playing RAD Ratings; USA Cohort 4.28, UK Cohort 3.79) and question 9 (understanding game instructions; USA Cohort average 4.25, UK Cohort 3.78). A similar trend was seen with question 7 (repeat game play; USA Cohort average 4.35, UK Cohort 3.65). There was more limited agreement for question 8 (that playing the game increased understanding of radionuclides and used; USA Cohort average 3.95, UK Cohort 3.39). While UK students gave favorable responses, their mean scores were consistently lower than the USA cohort, suggesting somewhat less enthusiasm or perceived educational impact.

These differences may reflect varying levels of prior familiarity, cultural factors, or differences in how the game was facilitated across sites. For example, students with more or less prior knowledge about radioactivity might engage differently with the game’s content, leading to different gains in understanding and interest. Cultural attitudes toward science and education can also influence how students perceive and respond to the material, and some groups may view radioactivity with more curiosity or concern, affecting their motivation to learn. Additionally, the way facilitators guide the game, explain concepts, and encourage discussion can significantly impact student engagement and comprehension. These factors together highlight that educational interventions are not one-size-fits-all; their effectiveness can depend heavily on context and delivery. This suggests that although students’ familiarity with radionuclides increased, there is still room to assess how the conceptual understanding of radioactivity has improved. The game showed potential in shifting perceptions towards a less negative average in Question 4 across both UK and USA groups, particularly by increasing interest in learning about radioactivity. However, given the limited scope of the study conducted with limited student numbers (50–98) from one day after one gameplay session, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further research, ideally involving larger and more diverse samples over multiple sessions, is needed to substantiate these initial results. Furthermore, the modest gains in student interest suggest that while the current version of the game is engaging, it may require further development to more effectively foster sustained enthusiasm for the topic. Future iterations could benefit from incorporating additional content or interactive elements to deepen engagement and reinforce learning.

Public attitudes toward nuclear energy in both the UK and the USA are shaped not only by concerns over safety and waste, but also by deeper psychological associations [22]. Research shows that in the USA nuclear energy is often subconsciously linked to nuclear weapons, evoking imagery of danger and contamination rather than innovation or sustainability [22]. These associations, reinforced by events such as Chernobyl and Fukushima and their ongoing portrayal in the media, continue to shape public perceptions [22–25]. However, studies also suggest these views are malleable [22], especially when educational interventions provide clear and engaging information, attitudes toward nuclear have been shown to shift in a more positive direction [26]. This highlights the value of tools like RAD Ratings, which aim to improve understanding of nuclear science through interactive, gamified learning, especially at a young age when students are starting to decide which subjects to pursue further. With the nuclear industry facing a growing demand for skilled professionals, increasing student interest in nuclear science is essential to building the future workforce. By presenting this game and comparing its impact on UK and USA audiences, we provide preliminary data supporting its usefulness as an engaging educational resource. Therefore, RAD Ratings shows promise as a tool to help attract and prepare the next generation of nuclear professionals.

Conclusions

These results are consistent with existing literature on the use of educational games in STEM, demonstrating that interactive approaches such as gamification can improve understanding and engagement for the teaching of nuclear skills [12–17]. The RAD Ratings game showed impact in the USA cohort and presents a promising tool for nuclear science outreach as part of a wider set of comprehensive resources.

Further work is needed to provide insights into how cultural and historical factors influence educational engagement and perceptions of nuclear science. Understanding these differences can inform the design of more effective, culturally sensitive educational interventions and guide policymakers in addressing nuclear literacy challenges in diverse communities. Future work should also focus on assessing the ability to collect longitudinal data to assess the impact of increasing long-term interest. The observed variations in this study underscore the importance of tailoring educational tools like this game to local knowledge, cultural context, and facilitation style to maximize their impact.
