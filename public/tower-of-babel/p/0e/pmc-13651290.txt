Sex and occupational differences in long COVID prevalence among healthcare workers: a multicentre registry-based study in three Spanish hospitals
Authors: Pia Delano, Fernando G. Benavides, Consol Serra, José María Ramada, Mª. Teresa Gea-Velázquez de Castro, Roberto Botella Mira, Itria G. Mirabella, Marina Fernandez Escribano, Magdalena Blasco López, Alfonso Muriel, Gemma Navarro, Rosa Serrano, Claudia Palma-Vasquez, Alejandra Vives, Mireia Utzet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsTo estimate the prevalence of long COVID identified through hospital registry data and examine associated occupational and demographic factors among healthcare workers (HCWs) in Spain.MethodsA multicentre study was conducted between 2020 and 2023 in three Spanish hospitals (Madrid, n = 1; Barcelona, n = 2). Long COVID prevalence and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated overall and by sex, age group, and occupational category. Associations between long COVID and occupational category were assessed using Poisson regression models with robust variance, stratified by sex and hospital, and adjusted by age and occupation.ResultsRegistry data included 8,439 HCWs. Long COVID prevalence ranged from 3.0% to 5.0% and increased with age, with an adjusted prevalence ratio (PR) increase of 3–9% per year. Occupational differences were more pronounced among men. In University Hospital Ramon y Cajal, male nurses (PR 2.56; 95% CI 1.13–5.81) and other HCWs (PR 3.67; 95% CI 1.12–12.08) had higher prevalence compared with physicians. In Hospital Parc Tauli, male nurse assistants (PR 20.33; 95% CI 3.64–113.42) and support staff (PR 8.30; 95% CI 1.46–47.19) showed markedly elevated prevalence, although confidence intervals were wide. Among women, occupational associations were weaker and less consistent. Overall sex differences were modest, but sex modified the association between occupational role and long COVID prevalence.ConclusionsLong COVID affected approximately 3–5% of HCWs across participating hospitals. Occupational differences in long COVID prevalence were observed, although estimates varied across centres. These findings highlight the importance of occupational health surveillance and follow-up strategies that consider both occupational role and potential sex-specific patterns.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28415-9.

Introduction

Six years after the start of the COVID-19 pandemic and with over 700 million confirmed cases worldwide [1], a substantial proportion of individuals who have been infected with SARS-CoV-2 continue to experience long-lasting symptoms that affect daily functioning and their ability to work [2]. This condition, known as long COVID, is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a “post-COVID-19 condition occurring usually three months after acute infection, with symptoms lasting at least two months and not explained by an alternative diagnosis. Common symptoms included fatigue, shortness of breath, cognitive dysfunction, but also others, and generally have an impact on everyday functioning “ [3].

There has been increasing interest in the impact of long COVID on vulnerable populations and key occupational groups, particularly healthcare workers (HCWs), including both clinical and non-clinical staff. Globally, more than 8.9 million HCWs have been infected with SARS-CoV-2 [4], often under conditions of sustained occupational exposure, raising concerns about an elevated risk of long COVID [5]. A recent rapid review reported that many affected HCWs experience reduced capacity to perform everyday tasks, concerns about patient safety, and difficulties coping with workplace demands [6].

At the policy level, the occupational recognition of COVID-19 and long COVID among HCWs remains heterogeneous across Europe [7]. Although the European Commission recommended recognising COVID-19 as an occupational disease in cases of workplace exposure, long COVID is not uniformly acknowledged. In Spain, COVID-19 (and long COVID) was classified as a work-related accident rather than an occupational disease, with adverse implications for access to long-term compensation as it only covers up to 5 years, difficulty to access workplace accommodations and occupational health follow-up [7–9].

Estimates of long COVID prevalence among HCWs vary widely, ranging from 15% to over 70%, depending on the study period, case definition, recruitment strategy and data collection method [10–23]. In the general population, prevalence estimates are considerably lower, around 6% [24] Reported risk factors for long COVID among HCWs have been inconsistent, with several studies suggesting associations with female sex, older age [14, 25], and greater severity of the acute infection [26, 27], while evidence regarding occupational risk factors remains limited and heterogeneous. Some studies have reported higher risk among nurse assistants [18, 19], but many investigations have focused primarily on physicians and nurses and have not consistently included non-clinical hospital staff [21, 26].

Recent population-based evidence from Spain has highlighted the role of occupational determinants of long COVID, including close contact with colleagues and the public, limited opportunities for social distancing, and inadequate use of personal protective equipment at work [28]. However, evidence specifically focusing on HCWs and based on clinically diagnosed long COVID identified through routine hospital registry data remains scarce.

The primary aim of this study was to estimate the prevalence of long COVID among healthcare workers in three Spanish hospitals using registry-based data, and to examine its association with sociodemographic and occupational characteristics, including potential differences by sex.

Methods

Study design and setting

A multicentre study was conducted using hospital registry data from three public hospitals in Spain, recruited through the national research network Centre for Biomedical Research in Epidemiology and Public Health (CIBERESP). Participating centres included University Hospital Ramon y Cajal (HRC, Madrid), Hospital del Mar (HM, Barcelona), and Hospital Parc Tauli (HPT, Sabadell).

Participants

Participants were healthcare workers (including clinical and non-clinical staff) employed at the participating hospitals who had been diagnosed with COVID-19 between 2020 and 2023. Eligible participants were healthcare workers with information available on long COVID status. Records with missing information on age, sex, or occupational group were excluded from regression analyses. Overall, missing data were infrequent (< 5% for all variables across centres). Because data were anonymised before transfer, it was not possible to identify potential overlap of workers across hospitals. However, employment at multiple hospitals simultaneously is uncommon and any resulting duplication is expected to be limited.

Data collection

Data sources and case ascertainment methods varied across hospitals according to the availability of institutional records. At HM, the dedicated long COVID clinical unit ceased activity in 2022 following the end of specific programme, and therefore registry data were only available up to that year. Both in HM and HRC, long COVID cases were identified through occupational health registries containing workers with a clinical diagnosis of long COVID following SARS-CoV-2 infection. Acute COVID-19 infection had been previously recorded by the corresponding hospital occupational health services according to local procedures in place during the pandemic. In HPT, long COVID cases were not clinically recorded in a dedicated registry; instead, workers with prolonged COVID-19-related sickness absence were identified and used as a proxy for long COVID. The study relied on routinely collected registry variables and did not have access to detailed information regarding symptom profiles, diagnostic procedures, laboratory confirmation of acute infection, reinfections, recovery status, or subsequent changes in long COVID status. As such, the research team had access only to anonymised registry data provided by each participating hospital. Detailed information regarding local clinical procedures for acute COVID-19 diagnosis, long COVID case identification, and internal case ascertainment protocols was not systematically available across centres.

Participating hospitals were contacted to resolve missing information in core variables whenever possible prior to database harmonisation. As a result, no missing data were present for long COVID status, age, sex, or occupational category in the final analytical dataset.

Case definition and variables

The definition of long COVID was guided by the WHO, referring to symptoms persisting for at least two months and usually three months after acute SARS-CoV-2 infection. Given differences in data sources, the operational definition of long COVID varied by hospital (Table 1).

The primary outcome was long COVID status (yes/no). The main exposure variable was occupational group, categorised as physicians, nurses, nurse assistants, other HCWs with direct patient contact (e.g. physiotherapists, speech therapists), and non-clinical staff (administrative and maintenance workers). Age (continuous, in years) was included as adjustment variable, and analyses were stratified by sex.

Statistical analysis

Prevalence of long COVID and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were estimated by sociodemographic and occupational variables and stratified by sex using hospital registry data. Confidence intervals for prevalence estimates were calculated using Wilson’s method.

Effect modification by sex was evaluated by including an interaction term between sex and occupational category, adjusting for age and hospital in models combining data from multiple centres. As evidence of effect modification was observed, sex-stratified Poisson regression models were subsequently fitted.

Due to heterogeneity in long COVID case definitions across centres, primary regression analyses were conducted separately for each hospital. In analyses with sparse data or separation issues, bias-reduced Poisson regression was applied to obtain stable estimates.

Prevalence ratios (PRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were obtained by exponentiating model coefficients. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using RStudio version 2024.04.2 + 764.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Hospital del Mar (reference HM 2022/10544), the Ethics Committee of Consorci Corporació Sanitària Parc Taulí (reference 2023/6063), and the Ethics Committee of Hospital Universitario Ramón y Cajal (reference 177/23). Clinical trial number: Not applicable.

The data used in this study were anonymised prior to analysis. Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of routinely collected registry data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the respective ethics committees.

Results

Registry data included 8,441 HCWs across three hospitals (Table 2). Missing data were limited. In HM, approximately 2% of records lacked information on occupational group. In HRC, missingness was 4% for age and sex and 3% for occupational group. No missing data were present in HPT. Overall prevalence of long COVID was 4.9% at HM, 5.0% at HRC and 3.0% at HPT. Among women, prevalence was 5.9% in HM, 5.5% in HRC, and 2.9% in HPT. Among men, prevalence was 1.6%, 4.0%, and 3.4%, respectively. Across hospitals, prevalence increased with age in both sexes, with the highest values generally observed among HCWs aged > 50 years.

Prevalence of long COVID among healthcare workers by hospital, sex, age group and occupational category

Occupational patterns also varied by centre and sex. In HM, prevalence among women was highest in support staff (10.2%; 95% CI 6.0–16.8) and nurse assistant (7.7%; 95%CI 5.2–11.3), whereas among men most occupational estimates were imprecise due to small numbers. In HRC, prevalence among women was relatively high in nurse assistants (6.1%; 95%CI 4.5–8.2) followed by nurses (5.9%; 95% CI 4.6–7.5), and among men the highest estimates were observed in other HCWs (8.3%; 95%CI 2.9–21.8) and nurses (7.4%; 95%CI 4.0–13.1). In HPT, higher prevalence was observed among nurse assistants in both sexes (women 5.1%; 95%CI 3.7–7.0, men 6.5%; 95%CI 3.5–11.6) and among male support staff (5.5%; 95% CI 2.7–11.0).

In sex-stratified Poisson regression models fitted separately for each hospital (Table 3), age was consistently associated with a higher prevalence of long COVID in both females and males. The increase in prevalence per additional year of age ranged from 3% to 9%, with the strongest association observed among males at HPT (PR = 1.09; 95% CI 1.05–1.13).

Among women, adjusted occupational associations varied by hospital. In HM, all occupations showed significantly higher adjusted prevalence ratios compared with physicians, although confidence intervals were wide. In contrast, in HRC no statistically significant occupational differences were observed among women after adjustment for age. In HPT, women nurse assistants showed a significantly higher adjusted prevalence of long COVID compared with physicians (PR 2.97; 95% CI 1.29–6.86), whereas no significant associations were observed for other occupational groups.

Among men, occupational differences were more pronounced, particularly in HRC and HPT. In HRC, nurses (PR = 2.56; 95% CI 1.13–5.81) and other HCWs (PR = 3.67; 95% CI 1.12–12.08) had a significantly higher prevalence of long COVID compared with physicians. In HPT, markedly elevated prevalence ratios were observed among nurse assistants (PR = 20.33; 95% CI 3.64–113.42) and support staff (PR = 8.30; 95% CI 1.46–47.19), although estimates were imprecise. In HM, regression estimates among men were unstable due to very small numbers of long COVID cases in several occupational strata and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Bias-reduced Poisson regression was applied to account for sparse data and potential separation. Despite wide confidence intervals in some hospital- and sex-specific strata, the results consistently indicate heterogeneity in long COVID prevalence by occupational role and sex, with stronger and more consistent occupational associations observed among men.

To assess the robustness of the findings, pooled analyses combining HM and HRC, as well as all three hospitals with adjustment for hospital, were conducted as sensitivity analyses. Results were broadly consistent with the hospital-specific analyses and are presented in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

Discussion

In this multicentre study among Spanish healthcare workers (HCWs), the prevalence of long COVID identified through hospital registry data ranged from 3% to 5%. Increasing age was consistently associated with higher long COVID prevalence across all analyses. Occupational differences were also observed, with nurse assistants showing the most consistent pattern of elevated prevalence across centres and in pooled analyses. Although some sex-specific differences were identified, these estimates were often imprecise and should be interpreted cautiously.

The prevalence of long COVID observed in this study was lower than that reported in many previous studies of HCWs, where estimates have ranged widely from 15% to over 70% [10–23]. This heterogeneity across studies likely reflects differences in case definitions, diagnostic practices, study periods, and data sources [5, 6]. Similarly, heterogeneity in long COVID prevalence was observed across hospitals in our study, particularly between centres using clinically diagnosed long COVID data (HM and HRC) and the centre using suspected long COVID cases (HPT). This variability highlights the importance of using comparable, clinically based definitions in analytical models to centres applying harmonised diagnostic criteria.

In addition to differences in case definitions, the lower prevalence observed in this study may reflect the use of clinically diagnosed registry-based cases rather than self-reported symptoms. Registry-based approaches are likely to identify more severe or medically recognised cases and may therefore yield lower prevalence estimates. Furthermore, a proportion of long COVID cases occurring during the earliest phases of the pandemic may not have been systematically captured because diagnostic pathways and recognition of the condition evolved substantially over time.

Age was positively associated with long COVID prevalence in both women and men. In adjusted analyses, each additional year of age was associated with a 3–9% increase in prevalence, consistent with previous findings in HCWs and in the general population [15, 25, 26, 29]. This association may reflect higher comorbidity burden, age-related vulnerability to prolonged recovery, or differences in clinical recognition of long COVID among older individuals [14, 25].

Women showed slightly higher crude prevalence of long COVID, but this difference diminished after adjusting for age. This pattern aligns with some previous studies, although findings on sex differences have been inconsistent or heterogeneous [14, 15, 18, 19, 21, 23, 25, 28–32]. Importantly, sex appeared to modify the association between occupational role and long COVID prevalence, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of cases in some strata.

Some occupational differences in long COVID prevalence were observed across hospitals, although several estimates, particularly among men, were based on small numbers and wide confidence intervals, indicating substantial uncertainty. Compared with physicians, higher prevalence ratios were observed in some occupational groups, although patterns were not entirely consistent across centres. In supplementary pooled analyses combining hospitals with comparable case definitions, nurse assistants showed the most consistent pattern of elevated prevalence, suggesting that occupational role may contribute to long COVID prevalence among healthcare workers. Although these findings should be interpreted cautiously, they may reflect differences in exposure intensity, task allocation, duration of patient contact, physical workload, and working conditions. Such differences in occupational exposures within the same job categories may vary by sex, reflecting gendered divisions of labour in healthcare settings [11, 18, 19, 33]. In addition, sex-related differences in symptom recognition, healthcare-seeking behaviour, and diagnostic practices may contribute to the observed patterns. Although some sex-specific associations, particularly among men, suggested elevated prevalence in certain occupational groups, these findings were imprecise and should be interpreted cautiously. Future occupational health research may benefit from incorporating gender-sensitive and intersectional perspectives.

The elevated prevalence observed among support staff in some hospital- and sex-specific analyses, although not consistently statistically significant after adjustment, suggests that non-clinical occupational exposures may also contribute to long COVID prevalence and warrant further investigation [28, 33].

Our findings are consistent with recent evidence from the population-based COVICAT cohort in Spain, which identified several occupational determinants of long COVID, including close interpersonal contact, limited social distancing and inadequate use of protective equipment at the workplace [28], all present in HCWs and non-clinical staff.

Finally, differences in long COVID prevalence and occupational associations across hospitals may partly reflect heterogeneity in case identification, with clinically diagnosed cases in two centres and suspected cases in one centre. Residual heterogeneity related to unmeasured temporal factors, diagnostic practices, and healthcare access cannot be excluded. Future studies using harmonised clinical definitions, standardised data collection, and longitudinal designs are needed to better characterise occupational and sex-specific patterns of long COVID among HCWs.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is its multicentre design, based on hospital registry data from three large Spanish hospitals, which increases the robustness of the findings and allows the inclusion of HCWs from a wide range of clinical and non-clinical occupational groups. The use of routinely collected registry data reduces recall bias and enables the identification of clinically diagnosed long COVID cases within real-world occupational health settings.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, the lack of individual-level information on the timing of acute COVID-19 infection and long COVID diagnosis precluded adjustment for calendar time, pandemic waves, vaccination rollout, or dominant viral variants. These factors may be associated both with the risk of developing long COVID and with occupational group, potentially contributing to residual confounding in the observed associations. Similarly, the absence of information on calendar time, pandemic wave, and circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants may have influenced both prevalence estimates and occupational comparisons.

Given the substantial changes in diagnostic practices and long COVID risk over time, this represents an important source of potential residual confounding. To mitigate this limitation, all main analyses were restricted to registry-based data and for each hospital, and centre-specific prevalence estimates were reported.

Second, heterogeneity in case ascertainment and registry periods across hospitals may have affected comparability of prevalence estimates. In one centre, long COVID cases were inferred indirectly from prolonged COVID-related sick leave, which may have resulted in some misclassification and under- or overestimation of prevalence. Third, registry data did not systematically capture relevant individual-level factors such as pre-existing comorbidities, reinfections, infection severity, or vaccination status, limiting the ability to explore underlying mechanisms and residual confounding.

Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into occupational and sex-specific patterns of diagnosed long COVID among HCWs and highlights the need for harmonised data collection and longitudinal follow-up in future research.

Implications

The prevalence observed in this study indicates that a non-negligible proportion of the healthcare workforce may experience prolonged health consequences following SARS-CoV-2 infection. Given the high occupational exposure experienced by HCWs during the COVID-19 pandemic, it is plausible that a proportion of these infections occurred in the workplace, although this cannot be formally assessed with the available data.

From an occupational health perspective, these findings highlight the relevance of considering long COVID within occupational health surveillance frameworks for HCWs, particularly in hospital environments with sustained exposure risk. In Spain, long COVID is not currently recognised as an occupational disease, which may have implications for access to compensation, workplace accommodations, and long-term follow-up within occupational health systems.

In this context, our results highlight the importance of strengthening occupational health monitoring and follow-up strategies for both clinical and non-clinical hospital workers. The observed differences by occupational role and sex suggest that prevention and follow-up approaches should consider the diversity of job tasks and working conditions within hospital settings. Future research should incorporate detailed information on infection severity, reinfections, vaccination history, and workplace exposure patterns to better understand individual and job-level determinants of long COVID among HCWs.
