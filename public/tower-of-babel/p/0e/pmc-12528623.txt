Effect of extrinsic and intrinsic factors on colour stability of 3D-printed, thermoplastic, and conventional resin materials: an in vitro study
Authors: Azita Mazaheri Tehrani, Arash Zarbakhsh, Alireza Shafigh, Somayeh Hashemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe colour stability of occlusal devices is a critical factor in their long-term aesthetic performance. This in vitro study aimed to evaluate and compare the colour stability of 3D-printed, thermoplastic, and conventional resin materials used in occlusal devices under extrinsic and intrinsic factors. In vitro – comparative study. Sixty rectangular specimens (15 × 15 × 2 mm) of Dentaclear (CAD/CAM 3D-printed), IMPAK (CAD/CAM milled thermoplastic), and ProBase Hot (heat-polymerised) resins were fabricated. Each material group consisted of 20 specimens, with 10 assigned to a control group (immersed in artificial saliva(serving both as the control group and as the intrinsic factor) and 10 to a test group (subjected to 5000 thermocycles in coffee). Baseline colour measurements (L*, a*, b*) were obtained using a spectrophotometer with D65 standard illumination. Colour changes (ΔE00) were calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula, and statistical analysis was performed using 2-way ANOVA (α = 0.05). 2-way ANOVA (α = 0.05). Significant colour changes (ΔE00) were observed in all materials following coffee thermocycling (P < .001). ProBase Hot exhibited the lowest ΔE00 values, indicating the highest colour stability, while IMPAK and Dentaclear showed higher ΔE00 values, with no significant difference between them (P = .287). The control group showed minimal colour change, indicating the negligible effect of intrinsic factors like saliva. Coffee thermocycling significantly impacted colour stability, with ProBase Hot demonstrating superior colour stability compared to IMPAK and Dentaclear. Intrinsic factors like Saliva had minimal influence on colour stability when compared to extrinsic factors like coffee.Keywords: Colour stability, CAD/CAM, Thermocycling, Extrinsic factors, 3D-printed materials

Introduction

Occlusal devices are important in the field of dentistry, particularly for the management of temporomandibular disorders (TMD) and bruxism. These devices, which include night guards and splints, are designed to protect teeth and the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) by distributing occlusal forces evenly, thereby preventing excessive dental wear and mitigating joint pain caused by continuous microtrauma (Maranhão et al. 2019; Heydecke et al. 2003). The effectiveness of occlusal devices in reducing symptoms associated with TMD and bruxism has been well-documented, making them a cornerstone of conservative dental management strategies (Keyf and Etikan 2004).

Traditionally, occlusal devices have been fabricated using a range of materials, with polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) being the most commonly used conventional rigid material. PMMA has long been widely used due to its favourable mechanical properties, such as high fracture resistance (Nam 2014; Jang et al. 2015). These rigid materials are preferred for their enhanced retention and relatively low porosity, qualities that are crucial in the effective management of TMD and bruxism (Nam 2014; Jang et al. 2015). However, the lack of flexibility in PMMA can present challenges, such as discomfort for patients and difficulty in making chairside adjustments (Gregorius et al. 2012). Conversely, thermoplastic materials, not typically used in CAD/CAM milling processes, provide a unique combination of flexibility and rigidity. These materials, like IMPAK, are rigid at oral temperatures but become more flexible at higher temperatures, facilitating easier adjustments and relining procedures (Imirzalioglu et al. 2010; Uzun and Keyf 2011).

The advent of computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technologies has revolutionized the fabrication of occlusal devices, introducing both subtractive (milling) and additive (3D printing) methods. These technologies have brought about significant advancements in precision, speed, and overall efficiency of dental prosthesis production (Checherita et al. 2013; Issar-Grill et al. 2013). The subtractive method, typically involving the milling of CAD/CAM blocks, produces devices with consistent density and mechanical properties. On the other hand, the additive method, commonly associated with 3D printing, is more cost-effective and generates less material waste compared to CAD/CAM milling. This method allows for the creation of complex geometries that would be difficult to achieve with subtractive techniques, further expanding the possibilities in dental prosthesis design (Negrutiu 2005; Keyf and Güngör 2003).

Despite these technological advancements, the color stability of occlusal devices, particularly those produced using digital technologies like CAD/CAM and 3D printing, as it directly impacts the aesthetic appearance of dental prostheses and, consequently, patient satisfaction (Raptis et al. 1982). Various factors, including the type of material used and the manufacturing method employed, can influence the long-term colour stability and mechanical durability of occlusal devices (Cook and Chong 1985; Nordbo et al. 1983; Goiato et al. 2013).

While previous studies have primarily focused on the mechanical properties of occlusal devices, such as fracture resistance and wear. comprehensive research comparing the colour stability of devices produced through different fabrication methods remains limited (Goiato et al. 2013). Given the importance of aesthetics in patient acceptance and satisfaction with occlusal devices, it is crucial to understand how different materials and manufacturing processes respond to common environmental factors, such as exposure to staining agents (Raptis et al. 1982; Nordbo et al. 1983).

This study aims to evaluate the impact of extrinsic factors, particularly thermocycling in staining solutions, alongside intrinsic factors such as artificial saliva, on the colour stability of occlusal devices fabricated using both conventional and digital methods. The null hypothesis of this study posits that neither the type of material used nor the one same cycle processes of coffee thermocycling (an extrinsic factor) and saliva immersion (an intrinsic factor) would significantly affect the colour stability of materials utilized in occlusal devices. By systematically comparing the performance of conventional rigid materials, CAD/CAM materials, and CAD/CAM 3D-printed materials under these conditions, this research aims to provide valuable insights that could guide the selection of materials and fabrication methods in clinical practice.

Material and methods

Sample size and power analysis

In alignment with the methodology described by Imirzalioglu et al. (2010) and based on calculations performed using PASS 11 software, the required sample size was determined considering α = 0.05, β = 0.2, a mean standard deviation of ΔE = 0.83, and an effect size of 0.61. This analysis established that a minimum of 10 specimens for each of the six subgroups under investigation was sufficient to detect statistically significant differences. Specimen preparation

Probase hot and impak speciment were fabricated using a molding technique (Swaney et al. 1953). Rectangular high-density polyethylene (HDPE) models with dimensions of 15 × 15 × 2 mm were milled using a CNC machine (Radox, Karaj, Iran) and served as templates. The molds were created by layering Type II and Type III dental stones within a brass flask, ensuring consistent mold cavities. For IMPAK, a powder-to-liquid ratio of 2:1 was used, whereas ProBase Hot was prepared by mixing 23.4 g of powder with 10 mL of liquid, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The resin mixtures were packed into the molds at a dough-like consistency, covered with a thin cellophane sheet, and pressed at 5 MPa to eliminate air bubbles and ensure uniform filling. The curing process involved gradually increasing the temperature to 100 °C over 45 min, followed by maintaining 120 °C for an additional 30 min (Choi and Kwon 2013) (Fig. 2).

Mold shape and dimensions used for specimen fabrication. Sequence shows before milling, addition of the mold material, and after milling; final cavity size 15 × 15 × 2 mm

For Dentaclear specimens produced via digital fabrication, the designs were generated in 3D Max software and saved as STL files before being transferred to a digital light processing (DLP) 3D printer (Asiga, Australia) (Table 1). Each layer was printed with a thickness of 50 μm under a UV light source (wavelength 385 nm) at a build speed of 30 mm/s. This approach aimed to ensure complete curing of the resin and prevent the presence of uncured that could compomise colour stability (Barizon et al. 2013). The printed samples were mounted on temporary supporting structures. Which were removed after fabrication, finishing pricedures included their removal, followed by polishing and finishing steps consistent with standard protocols. Initial cleaning was performed in reusable ethanol (3 min), then fresh ethanol (2 min) in an ultrasonic bath, and finally post-polymerization in a UV curing box (Sunlu UV resin curing box, China) for 2 × 20 min (Ergün and Çekiç Nagaş 2007) (Table 4).

Baseline color measurement

Sixty specimens were prepared in total, with 20 assigned to each material group: ProBase Hot, IMPAK, and Dentaclear. Each group was split into a test subgroup (subjected to coffee thermocycling) and a control subgroup (immersed in artificial saliva), with 10 specimens per subgroup. Baseline colour measurements were taken for each specimen using a spectrophotometer (SP62, X-Rite Inc., Michigan, USA) under D65 standard illumination, with readings of L*, a*, and b* recorded against a white background to avoid interference (Barizon et al. 2013). Each specimen was placed in a custom mold, and three measurements were taken per specimen, with the mean value recorded as the baseline parameter (Fig. 3).

Schematic diagram of the study workflow

Thermocycling and aging simulation

The test group underwent thermocycling in coffee solution to simulate clinical conditions (Yan-Fang Ren et al. 2012). The solution was prepared by dissolving 1 g of Nescafe Classic coffee in 100 mL of water. Thermocycling involved 5000 cycles between 5 °C and 55 °C, with each cycle including a 10-s transfer time and 25 s in each bath. The coffee solution was replaced every 12 h. After thermocycycling, the specimens were rinsed under running tap water at room temperature (~ 23 °C) for 30 s and dried with absorbent paper to remove any surface residues (Alexandria et al. 2017). Control groups underwent the same thermocycling process but in artificial saliva (Ca 1.5 mmol/L, P 0.9 mmol/L, KCl 150 mmol/L, F 0.05 mg/mL in Tris buffer, pH = 7.0). while the test group specimens were immersed in coffee. The same thermocycling process was applied to both groups to simulate oral thermal changes under clinical conditions (Alexandria et al. 2017). Following the thermocycling procedure, post-thermocycling colour measurements were recorded using the same protocol as the baseline measurements (Alexandria et al. 2017).

Post-thermocycling color measurement and ΔE calculation

The colour change (ΔE) for each specimen was calculated using the CIEDE2000 equation, derived from the differences in L*, a*, and b* values measured before and after thermocycling. The ΔE00 values were assessed on a neutral gray background (L = 93.7; a = − 0.3; b = 2.9) using the following formula:

ΔEoo=√ΔL/K_LS_L2+ΔC/K_CS_C2+ΔH/K_HS_H2+R_TΔC/K_CS_CΔH/K_HS_H

In this equation, the parametric factors K_L, K_C, and K_H were set to 1. Clinical thresholds for perceptibility and acceptability were based on established literature, with a 50:50% perceptibility threshold (PT00 = 0.81) and an acceptability threshold (AT00 = 1.81) (Alexandria et al. 2017).

Both the test and control group specimens were polished after thermocycling using silicon carbide abrasive papers of decreasing grit sizes (600, 800, 1200, 1500, 2000) under running water for 15 s each, to standardize surface finish and remove superficial changes caused by thermocycling (Takahashi et al. 2011). This process was performed by a single investigator to ensure uniformity in surface texture. The final thickness of each specimen was adjusted to 1 ± 0.02 mm using digital calipers (Mitutoyo CD-15DCX; Mitutoyo, Japan), followed by ultrasonic cleaning in distilled water for 10 min to remove any residual particles (Takahashi et al. 2011).

After thermocycling, colour measurements were repeated using the same protocol as at baseline. The colour change (ΔE) was calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula, with parametric factors KL, KC, and KH set to 1, and the thresholds for perceptibility (PT00 = 0.81) and acceptability (AT00 = 1.81) adopted from previous literature (Alexandria et al. 2017) (Table 3). All specimens were then polished using silicon carbide abrasive papers (grit sizes 600 to 2000) for 15 s each under running water to standardize surface finish and remove superficial changes from thermocycling (Takahashi et al. 2011). Final thickness was adjusted to 1 ± 0.02 mm, followed by ultrasonic cleaning in distilled water for 10 min.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using NCSS 2007 software. Descriptive statistics (median, minimum, maximum) were computed for each group. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess normality. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney U tests were used. Comparisons between pre- and post-thermocycling values were conducted with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and Spearman’s correlation was used to explore relationships between ΔE values, with significance set at α = 0.05 (Alexandria et al. 2017).

For the a* and b* values, ProBase Hot showed the least colour shift along the green–red axis, while Dentaclear demonstrated the most substantial shift along the yellow-blue axis, especially after coffee thermocycling (P < 0.001). These colour shifts resulted in significant differences in ΔE values between the materials after both saliva and coffee thermocycling, with ProBase Hot displaying the lowest ΔE values, indicating superior colour stability, while IMPAK and Dentaclear exhibited significantly higher ΔE values, surpassing the clinical acceptability threshold (AT00 = 1.81) (Fig. 1, Table 3).

Discussion

Colour stability is a critical property for occlusal splints, as these appliances are worn for extended periods in a dynamic oral environment. Intrinsic factors such as saliva can gradually alter material colour through hydrolytic degradation, while extrinsic factors like coffee accelerate staining due to strong pigments, acidic composition, and tannins, which can penetrate polymer networks and bind to surface irregularities (Um and Ruyter 1991). This study evaluated three commercially available occlusal splint materials—Dentaclear (3D-printed nylon-based resin), Impak (CAD/CAM thermoplastic resin), and ProBase Hot (conventional heat-cured PMMA)—which differ in polymer composition, manufacturing method, and surface characteristics. By simulating clinical ageing through thermocycling in coffee (external factor) and artificial saliva (internal factor and control), the study aimed to provide a comparative, evidence-based assessment of their long-term colour stability.

In the oral cavity, intrinsic factors arise from constant exposure to saliva, temperature variation, and humidity, which can lead to water sorption, surface changes, and pigment breakdown (Ruyter et al. 1987). Water sorption acts as a plasticiser, softening the polymer matrix and facilitating pigment penetration over time. Artificial saliva in this study replicated these intrinsic conditions while serving as the control medium.

Extrinsic factors involve staining from external sources such as dietary chromogens or beverages like coffee, which contains tannins, acidic compounds, and hydrophilic/hydrophobic pigments capable of penetrating polymer networks and binding to surface irregularities (Um and Ruyter 1991). Coffee immersion was selected as the primary extrinsic challenge due to its strong staining potential and clinical relevance.

To avoid subjectivity in visual assessment, colour changes were measured using the CIELAB system, where L* denotes lightness, a* represents the red-green axis, and b* the yellow-blue axis. The total colour difference (ΔE) was calculated from these coordinates. ΔE values below 1 are considered imperceptible, values between 1 and 3.7 perceptible but clinically acceptable, and values above 3.7 unacceptable for most prosthetic uses (R. Liberman et al. 1995). Spectrophotometric analysis enabled precise ΔE measurements for each resin before and after exposure, allowing consistent and objective comparisons.

After thermocycling in artificial saliva, measurable ΔE changes occurred in all materials (Fig. 1). ProBase Hot showed the least change (ΔE < 1), indicating high resistance due to its dense cross-linked PMMA structure, low residual monomer content, and minimal porosity, which reduce water uptake and pigment breakdown. Dentaclear displayed perceptible but acceptable changes (1 < ΔE < 3.7), likely linked to its more hydrophilic, photopolymerised network and micro-porosities from printing (Shin et al. 2020). Impak recorded values above the acceptability threshold (> 3.7), consistent with its lower cross-link density and greater moisture absorption, which facilitate internal pigment degradation (Hamedi Rad et al. 2017) (Table 3).

These findings confirm that even without extrinsic staining, intrinsic ageing through moisture and temperature cycling can cause clinically relevant discolouration, especially in materials with higher porosity and lower network density (Table 4).

Coffee immersion combined with thermocycling caused greater ΔE changes across all materials than artificial saliva alone. Thermal stress likely weakened silane coupling agents within the resin matrix, increasing surface porosity and facilitating pigment adherence.

ProBase Hot maintained ΔE within the acceptable range (1 < ΔE < 3.7), benefiting from low water sorption, a dense polymer matrix, and a smooth surface that limited stain penetration. Dentaclear exceeded acceptable limits (> 3.7), consistent with reports that nylon-based 3D-printed resins absorb more water and develop greater surface roughness (Shin et al. 2020). Impak exhibited similarly high staining, attributable to lower cross-linking and greater chain mobility, which enable both surface adsorption and subsurface diffusion of pigments (Alp et al. 2019).

When comparing both intrinsic and extrinsic conditions (Fig. 1), ProBase Hot consistently had the greatest colour stability, followed by Impak, while Dentaclear performed worst overall. These differences are primarily dictated by material composition, polymer network density, water sorption rate, and manufacturing method rather than by the staining medium alone. The combined effect of high moisture uptake and thermal cycling was particularly detrimental to the 3D-printed resin, confirming earlier reports that such materials are more vulnerable to both intrinsic ageing and extrinsic staining. Clinically, material choice should balance mechanical properties with aesthetic longevity, especially for patients with high coffee consumption or limited oral hygiene compliance.

The in vitro design allowed precise control over intrinsic and extrinsic variables, eliminating confounding factors such as patient diet, oral hygiene, and saliva composition. Standardised thermocycling and spectrophotometric methods ensured reproducibility. However, artificial saliva cannot fully mimic the biochemical complexity of natural saliva, and the absence of mechanical wear, plaque accumulation, and pH variation may underestimate clinical staining (Um and Ruyter 1991; Ferracane 2006). The study was limited to specific brands of PMMA, CAD/CAM thermoplastic, and 3D-printed nylon-based resins, which may limit generalisability.

Future research should include in vivo or in situ designs with mechanical loading, pH cycling, and natural salivary flow, as well as exposure to multiple staining agents (tea, red wine, coloured mouthrinses). Assessing the role of surface finishing, glazing, or protective coatings could further inform clinical recommendations for maximising colour stability.

Conclusions

Based on the findings of this in vitro study, the following conclusions were drawn:

Coffee caused discolouration in all three types of resins, with ProBase Hot (ΔE = 1.00) showing less change compared to the IMPAK (ΔE = 11.44) and Dentaclear (ΔE = 11.76) resins. In contrast, exposure to saliva resulted in minimal colour changes across all resin types, further highlighting the significant effect of coffee on discolouration.
