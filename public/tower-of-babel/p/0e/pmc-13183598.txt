Design and baseline findings of the NEODEM prospective cohort on early‐onset neurodegenerative dementia
Authors: Sophie Auriacombe, Emmanuelle Durand, Marion Kret, Leslie Cartz‐Piver, Frédérique Etcharry‐Bouyx, Mathilde Sauvée, Serge Belliard, Adrien Julian, Benoit Le Bail, Eric Frison, Vincent Planche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONFunctional prognosis of early‐onset neurodegenerative dementia (EOD) remains poorly understood.METHODSThe Neurodegenerative Early‐Onset Dementia (NEODEM) study is an ongoing prospective multicenter cohort study investigating factors associated with decline in daily activities in EOD. Detailed information on behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) is collected as prognostic factor.RESULTSBetween May 2020 and May 2023, 162 patients with EOD, including 111 patients with early‐onset Alzheimer's disease (AD) and 40 with frontotemporal dementia (FTD) variants, were enrolled in seven tertiary memory clinics in France. The median time from symptom onset to diagnosis was 3 years. By design, Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale score was 0.5 or 1 at inclusion. Baseline level of autonomy was similar in early‐onset AD and FTD, but MMSE scores were lower in AD. Baseline BPSD results are presented in the manuscript.CONCLUSIONFour‐year longitudinal data, available from 2027, will help clarify the impact of baseline factors on future functional status, institutionalization, morbidity, and mortality.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, early‐onset, frontotemporal dementia, prospective cohort

BACKGROUND

Early‐onset dementia (EOD) occurs before 65 years old and is less common than late‐onset dementia. 1 Yet, EOD causes a substantial burden on patients, their families, and the healthcare system. 2 , 3 The primary causes of EOD are neurodegenerative diseases, with Alzheimer's disease (AD) being the most prevalent, followed by frontotemporal dementia (FTD). 4 , 5 Research on early‐onset AD (EOAD) is underrepresented compared to late‐onset AD (LOAD), focusing on rare monogenic presentation of AD in the Dominantly Inherited Alzheimer Network (DIAN) 6 or in the French ECASCAD cohort 7 for instance. However, monogenic forms are uncommon, and even before 65 years of age, sporadic cases remain by far the most prevalent. 7 Other research has focused on very specific aspects of EOD, such as patients’ needs and quality of life, as investigated in the NeedYD study in the Netherlands. 8 The field has recently gained momentum with the first publications from the Longitudinal Early‐Onset Alzheimer's Disease Study (LEADS consortium) in the United States. 9 Although LEADS includes 100 individuals with non‐AD EOD, the specific diagnostic categories for this subgroup are not detailed. Indeed, the LEADS study aims specifically at improving the characterization of EOAD.

The natural history of non‐AD EOD remains poorly understood, particularly in terms of functional prognosis. Current knowledge is limited for early‐onset FTD, except for monogenic forms in the ALLFTD Consortium and the Genetic FTD Inititiative (GENFI). 10 It may be due to the heterogeneity of the large FTD clinical spectrum, which includes various phenotypes such as the behavioral variant (bvFTD), nonfluent variant primary progressive aphasia (nfvPPA), semantic variant primary progressive aphasia (svPPA), and also 4R tauopathies such as progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) and cortico‐basal degeneration (CBD). 11 However, in the field of sporadic FTD, some studies suggest that bvFTD and PPA may be associated with longer survival compared to PSP and CBD. 12 Finally, it is worth mentioning that the prevalence of potential early‐onset dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) remains unknown, as these patients are generally not included in EOD cohorts.

Functional dependency represents a critical clinical milestone, which has major implications for care planning and resource allocation. Additionally, evaluating cognitive performance alone as a marker of disease severity in patients with EOD may be misleading, particularly in aphasic patients, because language deficits interfere with cognitive evaluation. Notably, non‐amnestic variants of AD are more prevalent in EOAD than in LOAD. 13 In these cases, cognitive test results may not accurately reflect disease severity, whereas functional status may provide a more reliable indicator. Similarly, in FTD, cognitive and functional impairments are often poorly correlated. 14

To address these gaps, the Neurodegenerative Early‐Onset Dementia (NEODEM) study aimed to establish a multicenter cohort of patients with EOD to prospectively investigate the factors associated with incidental functional dependency. Focus is put on behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD), with a particular emphasis on depression. Indeed, depression is common in EOD 8 and is associated with poor quality of life. 9 While functional decline is linked to depression and anxiety in LOAD, 15 the association between BPSD and functional outcomes has not been specifically studied in EOD. The main rationale for studying BPSD in EOD is their potentially modifiable nature through non‐pharmacological intervention or medications, with the aim to improve quality of life.

Secondary objectives of NEODEM include exploring the potential prognostic value of other variables such as cognitive reserve proxies, gender, living conditions, baseline cognitive performance, clinical phenotypes of AD and FTD. We also aim at investigating the prognostic significance of blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers and neuroimaging features in an ancillary study. In addition to functional dependency, other key disease milestones in EOD, such as institutionalization and mortality, are also documented.

In this manuscript, we present the design and baseline characteristic of the NEODEM cohort. We also analyzed initial differences in BPSD and associated variables across the different etiologies and clinical presentations of EOD.

METHODS

Study design

NEODEM is an ongoing prospective, multicenter, longitudinal, observational clinical study (NCT04254094). Seven French tertiary memory clinics (in Bordeaux, Grenoble, Limoges, Lorient, Angers, Poitiers, and Rennes), with recognized expertise in the diagnosis and care management of EOD, participated in this study. The study protocol was approved by the Île‐de‐France VI Ethics Committee at the Pitié‐Salpêtrière Hospital on December 11, 2019 (approval no. 60‐19 HPS Cat 2).

In France, the identification of cognitive impairment is carried out in primary care, with referral by the general practitioner to a local specialized memory clinic. For rare and young‐onset dementias, a network of tertiary memory clinics is also available in University Hospitals. Participation in the NEODEM study was offered to all consecutive patients consulting in the 7 participating tertiary centers who met both clinical and biomarker‐based international criteria for neurodegenerative EOD. The refusal rate for participation in NEODEM was not systematically recorded. The clinico‐biological criteria of the International Working Group (IWG) were used to diagnose AD, 16 , 17 the Rascovsky criteria to diagnose bvFTD, 18 the Gorno‐Tempini criteria for nonfluent and semantic variant PPA 19 and the McKeith criteria for DLB. 20 CSF biomarkers were available for all participants to confirm (or rule out) the diagnosis of AD according to the clinico‐biological definition of the disease. 16 , 17 The inclusion period was initially set at 2 years but was extended to 3 years due to reduced recruitment activity during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic. Each participant will be followed for a total duration of 4 years.

Two ancillary studies to NEODEM were conducted exclusively at the Bordeaux center. One aimed to investigate MRI sequences not used in routine clinical care. MRI were performed at baseline and after 18 months of follow‐up using a 3‐T Vantage Galan ZGO scanner (Canon Medical Systems, Japan) with a 32‐channel phased‐array head coil. We collected a 3D‐T1‐weighted magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE); a 3D white‐matter‐nulled (WMn) MPRAGE; a 3D‐T2 weighted multiplanar voxel imaging; two shells of diffusion tensor imaging (DTI, with b = 1000 s/mm2 in 34 directions, b = 2500 s/mm2 in 34 directions and two sets of b = 0 s/mm2) ; quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) and resting‐state functional MRI. The other ancillary study aimed to establish a biobank for the development of future blood biomarkers (BioNEODEM). All biological samples were stored at the Centre de Ressources Biologiques of Bordeaux University Hospital under validated laboratory procedures (NF S96‐900). Fluid samples including plasma (7 mL), serum (5 mL), whole blood (4 mL), and CSF (15 mL) were stored in −80°C freezers. Initial results on blood ptau217 have recently been published. 21

Eligibility criteria

Participation in the NEODEM study was proposed to all patients newly diagnosed with a neurodegenerative disease within the previous six months, whose first symptoms related to the diagnosis had started before the age of 65 years. Participants had to be able to undergo cognitive and psychiatric assessments, and the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) global score had to be 0.5 or 1 at baseline. Patients without a caregiver were not excluded, as caregiver status was a variable of interest. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient; when available, a non‐opposition form was signed by the caregiver. All participants were required to be affiliated to a social security system.

Patients with dementia of non‐neurodegenerative origin (e.g., vascular, alcohol‐related, toxic, infectious, post‐traumatic,…), of unknown origin, or associated with Down syndrome were excluded. Patients residing in nursing homes or other institutional care facilities, those who were completely dependent for dressing and bathing at baseline, or those with a severe or life‐threatening medical condition were also excluded.

Data collection

The NEODEM clinical assessments included demographics, social situation (living conditions, employment), past medical history including risk factors, family history, concurrent medication (with particular interest in acetylcholinesterase inhibitors and psychotropic medication), non‐pharmacological interventions, and detailed neurological examinations. At inclusion, patients underwent a comprehensive neuropsychological examination required for establishing the diagnosis, including: the Free and Cued Selecting Reminding Test (FCSRT), literal and semantic fluencies, Frontal Assessment Battery (FAB), Trail‐Making Test (TMT) A and B, Digit span, Rey‐Osterrieth complex figure (ROCF) test, Boston naming task and praxis. Additional tests to support the diagnosis of a specific focal syndromic presentation could be performed at the discretion of each neurologist (e.g., tests of semantic memory, social cognition, visuospatial skills, etc.), but they were not collected in the cohort database. The following relevant diagnostic information was collected: date of diagnosis; date of first symptoms; clinical phenotype of AD (amnestic vs. non‐amnestic presentations) or clinical variants of FTD (bvFTD, svPPA, nfvPPA, and PSP/CBD). The search for an autosomal dominant cause of AD or FTD was not systematic and genetic testing was performed at the discretion of the physicians and the participants or their families.

The functional and cognitive status was assessed with the CDR, the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), the basic activities of daily living (bADL) and the instrumental activities of daily living (iADL) scores. Neuropsychiatric and mood assessment included the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI), a well‐known and validated scale that describes the most frequent and significant BPSD in AD 22 ; the Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia (CSDD), a 19‐item clinician‐administered instrument that uses information from interviews with both the patient and an informant, specifically designed for the rating of symptoms of depression in patients with dementia, 23 that was previously validated in French. 24

Variables related to CSF biomarkers (Aβ42, Aβ40, total‐tau, ptau‐181) and brain imaging (Scheltens medial temporal atrophy score, Fazekas scale for white matter changes) performed as part of routine clinical care for EOD were also systematically recorded.

Follow‐up

The longitudinal follow‐up of the patients in the cohort is ongoing, every 6 months over a total duration of 4 years. When on‐site visits are not feasible, assessments are conducted by telephone: during remote assessments, only selected outcomes are collected, including iADL, bADL, treatments, use of social resources, information on institutionalization and death. Statistical analyses

Assuming an anticipated baseline prevalence of depression of 65% and a 3‐year incidence of total dependency for bathing and dressing of 25%, we aimed to recruit 260 participants to detect a relative risk of 2 (15% vs. 30%) with a power of 80% and a two‐sided type I error of 5%. Due to recruitment difficulties, the final sample size was determined by the end of the extended recruitment period. Only participants meeting all eligibility criteria and with signed informed consent were included in the analysis.

No imputation of missing data was performed and descriptive analyses were based on all available baseline data. Qualitative variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and quantitative variables as mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum, and quartiles. Due to the limited number of patients with DLB, comparisons were restricted to patients with EOAD and FTD: differences in proportions were presented with exact or Wald 95% confidence intervals, and differences in means with t‐distribution‐based 95% confidence intervals. No formal statistical tests (p‐values) were performed, and all comparative analyses are descriptive and exploratory. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2 or higher).

One participant initially enrolled was excluded due to the absence of CSF biomarkers and an initial clinical profile suggestive of non‐neurodegenerative etiology. DISCUSSION

Between 2020 and 2023, 162 patients with EOD were enrolled in the NEODEM study across seven tertiary memory clinics in France. The cohort included 111 patients with EOAD, 40 with FTD, and 11 with DLB. Participants will be followed for 4 years, until 2027. Regarding baseline characteristics, NEODEM confirms the predominance of AD over FTD in the EOD population, as well as the relative rarity of DLB (< 10%), in line with previous studies. 4 , 5

Baseline findings regarding cognition and function were consistent with an early stage of disease, as expected given NEODEM inclusion criteria (patients newly diagnosed within the past six months; global CDR = 0.5 or 1). Two‐thirds of patients had a CDR of 0.5, and the mean CDR‐sb score was < 4, in both EOAD and early‐onset FTD groups. At these relatively early stages, the mean MMSE was lower in EOAD compared with FTD. At these relatively early stages, the mean MMSE was lower in EOAD compared with FTD, as expected given that the MMSE is poorly sensitive to the early dysexecutive and language deficits seen in FTD. 25 , 26 It is also possible that the EOAD cases included in NEODEM exhibit genuinely more pronounced global cognitive impairment than FTD, whose deficits may be purely behavioral at onset. In general, in EOD, language and visuospatial impairments, as well as executive dysfunction, often challenge global cognitive assessment scales, which are primarily designed for LOAD.

In our tertiary memory clinics, the delay between the first symptoms and the diagnosis was long (almost 3 years on average) but shorter than in some general population studies, where it can reach up to 5 years. 27 This highlights the need to increase awareness of EOD in the general population, as well as among general practitioners. The high proportion of patients initially diagnosed with occupational ‘burnout’ (22%), also highlights the importance of training occupational physicians in this diagnosis. Burnout is a work‐related syndrome of chronic stress, marked by emotional exhaustion and reduced personal accomplishment, which impair professional functioning. It was reported by the patients and their relatives at cohort entry after having been initially mentioned by the primary care physician, occupational physician, or a psychiatrist. This psychiatric diagnosis is likely “inaccurate”, probably reflecting cognitive or behavioral difficulties from EOD that overwhelmed the patient in their professional environment. Interestingly, the frequency of burnout misdiagnosis was significantly higher in EOAD than in early‐onset FTD (22% vs. 5%). This is consistent with the observation that FTD patients remain in employment longer than EOAD patients (18% vs. 8%), whereas EOAD patients are more frequently on long‐term sick leave (41% vs. 15% of FTD patients). This could suggest that, in the early stages of the disease, the memory, praxis, language, and orientation impairments of EOAD have a greater impact on work ability than the behavioral impairment of FTD.

The prevalence of BPSD was high in NEODEM, although the overall severity remained relatively mild, as reflected by the global NPI score. Comparison between EOAD and early‐onset FTD revealed significantly higher total NPI scores in FTD patients, with symptoms including euphoria, apathy, disinhibition, nighttime behavioral disturbances, and eating disturbances, consistently with bvFTD diagnostic criteria, 18 which represent half of the FTD population in the cohort. Neither the NPI depression subscore nor the total CSDD score differed significantly between EOAD and FTD groups. Similarly, in the LEADS study, the total NPI‐Q score was higher in the early‐onset non‐AD group compared to EOAD, with no difference in depressive symptoms. 28

Patients with early‐onset FTD were more frequently treated with psychotropic medication than those with EOAD (73% vs. 53%), in line with their higher NPI scores and findings from previous studies. 28 Compared to the LEADS study, patients with EOAD in NEODEM were more likely to receive psychotropic medication (33% vs. 53%, respectively). This is not necessarily specific to EOD, but reflects the generally high prescription rates of this medication in France. 29 In both LEADS and NEODEM, antidepressants were the most frequently prescribed psychotropic medication, reflecting similar medical practices in the United States and France. 28 In NEODEM, over 90% of patients with EOAD and FTD taking psychotropic medications received an antidepressant, in accordance with usual recommendations for the pharmacological management of BPSD. The prescription of antipsychotics was rare (< 10% of patients on psychotropic medications), because of a questionable risk–benefit ratio in EOD. 30

In NEODEM, 35% of patients with EOAD had a non‐amnestic clinical presentation. This is consistent with previous studies in France, 31 but the LEADS cohort initially reported a notably lower rate of non‐amnestic presentations (approximately 20%). 25 However, a data‐driven analysis of neuropsychological data in LEADS suggests that the amnestic‐predominant presentation may actually be much less frequent than what is described based on clinical judgment. 32 EOAD is indeed known to be more clinically heterogeneous than LOAD, with a greater proportion of atypical, non‐amnestic phenotypes such as posterior cortical atrophy (PCA) or logopenic PPA. 13 The dysexecutive and/or behavioral presentation of AD was reported only once in NEODEM. 33 , 34 It is indeed recognized by the IWG as an uncommon AD phenotype. 17 Since aphasia and “posterior” symptoms such as simultanagnosia are frequently reported in the dysexecutive variant, 34 it is possible that some patients were classified as PPA or PCA according to the judgement of the clinician including the patient in NEODEM. However, it seems unlikely that patients were misclassified as “amnestic”, as we routinely use in France the FCSRT to assess memory. 35 This test allows the distinction between encoding, storage, and retrieval processes, and a patient is classified as “amnestic” only if a storage deficit is present, consistent with hippocampal impairment. In contrast, encoding and (uncued) retrieval deficits are associated with a dysexecutive syndrome.

In NEODEM, baseline characteristics did not significantly differ between amnestic and non‐amnestic EOAD patients, except for a higher proportion of women in the amnestic group. Notably, there were no differences in iADL, in contrast to findings from LEADS and the Amsterdam Dementia cohorts. 36 This may be linked to NEODEM's inclusion criteria, which required low baseline CDR. Consequently, tracking the longitudinal changes in iADL according to syndromic presentation will be particularly informative in future NEODEM studies.

The NEODEM cohort has several limitations. The sample size is smaller than the target set for the recruitment period, partly due to the COVID‐19 pandemic. Subgroup descriptions should be considered exploratory, not powered for formal hypothesis testing. Biobanking and ancillary imaging studies were performed only at the Bordeaux center. Information on genetic causes is incomplete, as it was left to the discretion of clinicians and patients. Moreover, recruitment in expert centers may limit the generalizability of the results. The use of international criteria for bvFTD and PPA variants, established prior to the first inclusions in NEODEM, limits the possibility of describing more recently defined FTD phenotypes, such as right temporal variants. 37 The cohort also has numerous strengths that help complement our limited knowledge of EOD: precise diagnoses based on rigorous neuropsychological assessment and biomarkers, systematic evaluation of BPSD, iADL, and personal and professional living conditions. The European care environment, including non–English‐speaking patients, also provides insights regarding the generalizability of findings from ongoing American cohorts.

In conclusion, our initial findings highlight the high frequency of burnout misdiagnosis in EOAD, as well as the higher prevalence of BPSD and psychotropic use in early‐onset FTD compared with EOAD. Longitudinal data over 4 years, available from 2027, will allow a better understanding of the impact of these factors on functional outcomes, institutionalization, morbidity and mortality.
