Evaluating CASS and BATSS scores for predicting laparotomy in blunt abdominal trauma: meta-analysis from low- and middle-income countries
Authors: Chris Lovis Bwambale, Elna Owembabazi, Jackson Kakooza, Bienfait Vahwere Mumbere, Theoneste Hakizimana, Prosper Akankwasa, Catherine R Lewis, Michael Mugenyi, Demoz Abraha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly identification of intra-abdominal injury (IAI) following blunt abdominal trauma (BAT) is critical to reducing mortality in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where access to contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) is limited. The Clinical Abdominal Scoring System (CASS) and the Blunt Abdominal Trauma Scoring System (BATSS) are used to guide triage and operative decision-making; however, their diagnostic accuracy in LMICs remains uncertain and inconsistently reported. The objective of this study was to evaluate the pooled diagnostic accuracy of CASS and BATSS in predicting the need for laparotomy or the presence of IAI in adult BAT patients in LMICs.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Lens.org were searched from January 2009 to January 2025. Eligible studies evaluated adult patients with BAT in LMICs and reported diagnostic accuracy of CASS or BATSS for predicting IAI or need for laparotomy using CECT, laparotomy, or composite reference standards. Random-effects meta-analyses were used to pool sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic odds ratios (DORs). Heterogeneity and publication bias were formally assessed.ResultsTwelve studies encompassing 2,148 patients met inclusion criteria, including 7 BATSS studies and 5 CASS studies. For BATSS, pooled sensitivity was 57.9% and specificity 86.5%, with a pooled DOR of 70.1 (95% CI: 9.38–520.61). For CASS, pooled sensitivity was 77.2%, specificity was 90.6%, and pooled DOR was 50.4 (95% CI: 6.59–384.91). Substantial heterogeneity was observed for both systems (I²=88.5% for BATSS; I²=74.6% for CASS). BATSS showed better pooled performance at a cut-off ≥ 12; however, variation in thresholds across studies limited direct comparability of pooled estimates. Publication bias was detected for BATSS but not for CASS, indicating that BATSS estimates may be vulnerable to small-study effects or selective publication.ConclusionsBased on a limited evidence base, both CASS and BATSS appear to have diagnostic value for identifying IAI in adult BAT patients in LMICs, although neither should be used as a standalone decision tool. CASS may be more useful for initial rule-out triage where imaging is unavailable, whereas BATSS may support rule-in decisions for definitive imaging or surgical intervention; however, these interpretations should be made cautiously because of high heterogeneity, small study numbers, variable thresholds, and publication bias affecting BATSS. Local validation, threshold optimization, and prospective multicenter evaluation are essential before routine implementation. When cautiously integrated into structured trauma protocols, these scoring systems may support triage and imaging prioritization in resource-constrained settings, but current pooled estimates should be interpreted as preliminary.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00423-026-04101-7.Keywords: BATSS, Blunt abdominal trauma, CASS, Diagnostic accuracy, Intra-abdominal injury, Laparotomy, Low- and middle-income countries, Meta-analysis

Background

Traumatic injury is recognized as a global health crisis and an “unsolved epidemic,” serving as the leading cause of mortality in individuals under 45 years of age [1, 2]. Blunt abdominal trauma (BAT) is the most prevalent pattern of abdominal injury, accounting for approximately 75% to 80% of all abdominal trauma cases [3, 4].

The clinical management of BAT is exceptionally challenging because serious intra-abdominal injuries (IAI), such as solid organ lacerations or hollow viscus perforations, are often initially occult or masked by distracting extra-abdominal injuries and altered mental status [5, 6]. Abdominal injuries contribute to 15% to 20% of all trauma-related deaths, with a significant portion of these fatalities being preventable if diagnosed within the “golden hour” [4, 7]. Diagnostic delays remain a primary cause of morbidity and avoidable death, leading to life-threatening complications such as exsanguination, sepsis, and multiorgan failure [8]. Consequently, rapid and accurate identification of surgical candidates is fundamental to improving global survival outcomes [5, 9].

In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), the management of BAT is further complicated by severe resource constraints and limited healthcare infrastructure [8, 10]. While contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) is the established gold standard for detecting IAI, its routine application is hindered in LMICs by high costs, limited accessibility, and the logistics of transporting hemodynamically unstable patients to imaging suites [1, 11]. Furthermore, the risks of ionizing radiation exposure, particularly in younger populations, and the potential for contrast-induced complications necessitate a more selective use of advanced imaging [12]. In these settings, clinicians often rely on clinical judgment and basic diagnostic adjuncts, but physical examination alone has demonstrated moderate to poor sensitivity for identifying internal hemorrhage [5, 13]. Therefore, there is a critical need for cost-effective, bedside clinical decision tools that can standardize risk stratification, optimize resource allocation, and reduce the rate of non-therapeutic laparotomies [14, 15].

To address these diagnostic gaps, several clinical scoring systems have been developed and adopted for rapid triage, most notably the Clinical Abdominal Scoring System (CASS) and the Blunt Abdominal Trauma Severity Score (BATSS). CASS is a simplified physiological instrument that integrates five to six variables, including the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), systolic blood pressure, pulse rate, and clinical abdominal findings such as pain, tenderness, or guarding [13]. It was primarily designed to predict the necessity of urgent laparotomy in environments where advanced imaging may be entirely absent [16]. In contrast, BATSS is a more comprehensive 24-point weighted system that incorporates physiological parameters, mechanism of injury, and the results of a Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) scan [17, 18]. While these systems aim to prioritize patients into low-, moderate-, and high-risk categories, their diagnostic purpose is often hampered by wide variability in reported accuracy across primary studies [17, 19].

A significant evidence gap persists due to the inconsistency of diagnostic metrics reported in existing literature. Sensitivity estimates for CASS vary dramatically, often confounded by the use of different scoring thresholds such as 8, 8.5, or 12 and varying reference standards like CT or exploratory laparotomy [2, 18]. Furthermore, recent preliminary research suggests that BATSS may fail to safely “rule out” injury in pre-selected, high-risk patients due to a dangerously low negative predictive value [19]. The lack of pooled diagnostic accuracy estimates specifically for LMIC settings, combined with significant heterogeneity in study design and patient selection, hinders the implementation of standardized, evidence-based trauma protocols in resource-limited regions.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the pooled diagnostic accuracy of CASS and BATSS for predicting the need for laparotomy or the presence of IAI in adult patients with BAT in LMICs. By synthesizing data from diverse geographical and clinical environments, this study seeks to identify optimal diagnostic thresholds and determine the comparative efficacy of these two scoring systems. The findings will provide a validated framework for surgical decision-making and imaging prioritization, ultimately bridging the gap between initial clinical assessment and definitive trauma care. By reducing reliance on scarce diagnostic technology while minimizing preventable deaths, this research has the potential to significantly enhance trauma triage and patient outcomes in the global population.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines and the Cochrane Handbook for Diagnostic Test Accuracy Reviews. The protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261303033).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they: (1) evaluated adult patients (≥ 18 years) with BAT; (2) assessed the diagnostic accuracy of CASS or BATSS for predicting the need for laparotomy or presence of IAI; (3) were conducted in LMICs as defined by the World Bank classification; (4) used laparotomy findings, CECT, or a composite reference standard as the diagnostic gold standard; (5) provided sufficient data to construct 2 × 2 contingency tables; and (6) were published in English between 2009 and 2025. Studies evaluating penetrating trauma, pediatric populations, or conducted in high-income countries were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed across four electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Lens.org) from January 2009 through January 2025. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords related to BAT, clinical scoring systems, laparotomy, IAI, and LMICs. No language restrictions were initially applied during the search, though only English-language studies were ultimately included. Full search strategies for each database are provided in the Supplementary Appendix. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Study selection

Search results were imported into reference management software, and duplicates were removed. Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts against eligibility criteria. Full-text articles of potentially relevant studies were retrieved and independently assessed for inclusion. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data extraction

Data were independently extracted by two reviewers using a standardized, pilot-tested extraction form. The following information was recorded: first author, publication year, country, study design, sample size, patient demographics, trauma mechanism, scoring system evaluated, threshold scores, reference standard, true positives, false positives, true negatives, false negatives, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. Corresponding authors were contacted via email when data were unclear or missing. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or third-party adjudication.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality and risk of bias were assessed independently by two reviewers. Cohort and prospective studies were evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), while cross-sectional studies were appraised using the Appraisal Tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS). The NOS assesses selection, comparability, and outcome domains, with scores of 7–9, 4–6, and 0–3 indicating high, moderate, and low risk of bias, respectively. The AXIS evaluates 20 quality domains including study design, sample selection, measurement validity, and reporting [20].

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using jamovi version 2.6.4.4 with the metafor package in R version 4.4. For each scoring system, pooled sensitivity and specificity with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using random-effects models with the restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) estimator. Effect sizes were computed using logit-transformed proportions. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran’s Q statistic, I² statistic, τ² (between-study variance), and H² (ratio of total to sampling variance). I² values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively.

Publication bias was assessed using three complementary methods: visual inspection of funnel plots, Kendall’s rank correlation test, and Egger’s regression test. The fail-safe N was calculated using Rosenthal’s method to estimate the number of null studies required to nullify significant findings, with robustness defined as exceeding the 5k + 10 threshold (where k represents the number of included studies). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Forest plots were generated to visualize individual study estimates and pooled effects for each scoring system.

Synthesis methods

Separate meta-analyses were conducted for CASS and BATSS. When studies reported multiple thresholds, the most commonly used or clinically recommended threshold was selected for primary analysis. Sensitivity analyses explored the impact of threshold selection, study design, sample size, and risk of bias on pooled estimates. Due to insufficient studies per subgroup, formal subgroup analyses or meta-regression were not feasible. Results are presented descriptively with narrative synthesis where quantitative pooling was inappropriate.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The systematic search identified 817 records across four databases (Web of Science: 277, Scopus: 227, Lens.org: 214, PubMed: 99). After removing 137 duplicates, 680 records were screened. Following title and abstract screening, 507 records were excluded. Of the 173 reports sought for retrieval, 32 could not be retrieved. The remaining 141 reports underwent full-text assessment for eligibility. After applying inclusion criteria, 129 reports were excluded for the following reasons: conducted in high-income countries (n = 47), evaluated penetrating trauma (n = 23), used different scoring systems (n = 31), lacked adequate reference standards (n = 18), and provided insufficient data for meta-analysis (n = 10). Ultimately, 12 studies met inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis represents the first comprehensive pooled evaluation of CASS and BATSS diagnostic accuracy specifically in LMIC populations with BAT. Across 12 studies encompassing 2,148 patients from Pakistan, Indonesia, India, Iran, and Egypt, we demonstrated that both scoring systems possess statistically significant diagnostic value (p < 0.001), though with clinically important differences in performance characteristics. BATSS exhibited a pooled DOR of 70.11 with 57.92% sensitivity and 86.52% specificity, while CASS demonstrated a pooled DOR of 50.40 with notably higher sensitivity (77.22%) but comparable specificity (90.62%). These findings carry critical implications for trauma triage in resource-constrained settings where CECT remains inaccessible or prohibitively expensive for the majority of patients presenting with BAT.

The divergent sensitivity-specificity profiles of BATSS and CASS suggest fundamentally different clinical applications within trauma care pathways. CASS, with its higher sensitivity, appears better suited as a rule-out instrument during initial emergency department triage, particularly when the clinical objective is to minimize missed IAIs that could progress to life-threatening complications. This aligns with the original design intent of CASS as a rapid bedside tool for settings lacking advanced imaging infrastructure [26]. Conversely, BATSS demonstrates superior specificity, positioning it as a more appropriate rule-in tool for identifying patients who genuinely require definitive imaging or surgical intervention. The incorporation of FAST ultrasonography findings into BATSS likely contributes to this enhanced specificity by providing objective anatomical evidence of free fluid or solid organ injury. However, the modest overall sensitivity of BATSS raises substantial concern about its capacity to safely exclude significant injury in patients presenting with equivocal clinical findings, a limitation noted in recent Indonesian validation studies [19].

Substantial between-study heterogeneity (I²=88.45% for BATSS; I²=74.60% for CASS) emerged as a dominant finding, reflecting considerable methodological diversity across the included primary studies. Subgroup analyses revealed that this heterogeneity stemmed primarily from three sources: variable cut-off thresholds, inconsistent study designs, and heterogeneous reference standards. For BATSS, the ≥ 12 threshold demonstrated optimal combined performance (sensitivity 89.58%, specificity 86.43%) compared to the ≥ 8 threshold (sensitivity 49.61%, specificity 86.92%), suggesting that lower thresholds sacrifice sensitivity without meaningfully improving specificity. These threshold effects are consistent with receiver operating characteristic analyses from high-income settings demonstrating that optimal BATSS performance occurs at scores exceeding 10 to 12 points [2]. The prevalence of IAI also varied dramatically across studies from 28% to 94% in CASS cohorts and 31% to 92% in BATSS cohorts, indicating profound differences in patient selection, trauma mechanism distribution, and injury severity that inevitably influence test performance characteristics. Studies employing composite reference standards (CT plus laparotomy) generally reported higher diagnostic accuracy than those relying exclusively on CT imaging or ward observation, likely because composite verification reduces incorporation bias and captures injuries that evolve beyond the initial imaging timepoint.

Comparison with existing literature

This meta-analysis is situated within a substantial body of review-level evidence documenting profound gaps in trauma and diagnostic capacity in LMICs. Systematic reviews of LMIC trauma systems show that most assessments focus on in-facility resources and delays in receiving care, consistently highlighting shortages of imaging, operative capability, and organized trauma pathways as major drivers of preventable mortality [11, 27]. Parallel reviews of trauma severity measures and scoring tools in LMICs report that models developed in high-income settings often demonstrate only moderate calibration, wide variability in discrimination, and limited feasibility when advanced investigations or specialist staff are unavailable, reinforcing the need for simple, context-appropriate scores based on readily available clinical variables [28, 29].

Evaluations of critical care delivery and prognostic models in LMIC intensive care units (ICUs) further underscore this pattern, describing heavy reliance on in-hospital care, frequent data gaps, and dependence on tests that are not routinely accessible, alongside a paucity of interventional and cost-effectiveness research to guide resource allocation [29, 30]. Quality-improvement–focused trauma system reviews demonstrate that structured protocols, audits, and standardized processes can reduce mortality, but also emphasize that robust, locally validated triage and severity tools are rarely embedded within these system-level interventions [27].

Within BAT specifically, no prior systematic review or meta-analysis has synthesized the diagnostic accuracy of CASS or BATSS. Existing evidence consists of single-center evaluations of BATSS and other isolated decision tools for IAI, rather than pooled, multicenter estimates, while review-level diagnostic work has focused on individual components such as point-of-care ultrasonography or clinical examination rather than integrated scoring systems [12, 31].

Against this backdrop, the present meta-analysis provides the first pooled, LMIC-specific diagnostic accuracy benchmarks for CASS and BATSS in adult BAT. By moving beyond generic or mortality-focused trauma and ICU scores to organ-specific decision support, it offers empirically derived thresholds and performance estimates that can guide laparotomy decisions and imaging prioritization in settings where CT capacity is constrained. In doing so, it operationalizes the broader calls from trauma systems, critical care, and prognostic-model reviews for simple, feasible, and locally validated tools to bridge the gap between initial clinical assessment and definitive trauma care in LMICs [27–29].

From a clinical implementation perspective, several pragmatic recommendations emerge from this meta-analysis. First, CASS should be considered for initial triage in emergency departments lacking reliable access to FAST, given its higher sensitivity and simpler variable set (GCS, blood pressure, pulse, abdominal examination). Second, in facilities with available ultrasonography, BATSS may offer superior rule-in capacity for identifying patients who genuinely warrant CT imaging or operative intervention, thereby reducing non-therapeutic laparotomy rates. Third, neither system should operate as a standalone decision rule; instead, they should complement rather than replace serial clinical assessments, particularly during the first 24 h when occult injuries may declare themselves. Fourth, local calibration studies are essential before widespread implementation, as optimal thresholds appear to vary substantially across different prevalence settings and trauma mechanisms. Finally, these scoring systems must be integrated into structured trauma protocols that include defined pathways for observation, repeat examination, selective imaging, and surgical consultation, as isolated implementation without institutional commitment to trauma care infrastructure is unlikely to improve outcomes.

Limitations

This meta-analysis has several limitations that warrant acknowledgment. First, publication bias was detected for BATSS studies (Kendall’s τ = 0.810, p = 0.011; Egger’s test p = 0.043), suggesting that null or negative findings may be underrepresented in the published literature. While the fail-safe N exceeded robustness thresholds, the possibility of inflated pooled estimates cannot be dismissed. Second, the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies limits the precision and generalizability of our pooled estimates, though we addressed this through random-effects modeling and comprehensive subgroup analyses. Third, most included studies carried high risk of bias in selection domains, reflecting the challenges of conducting prospective diagnostic accuracy studies in emergency trauma populations where randomization is neither ethical nor feasible. Fourth, composite reference standards were applied inconsistently, with some studies using CT imaging alone and others employing clinical follow-up or laparotomy, potentially introducing verification bias. Fifth, we were unable to perform diagnostic test accuracy meta-analysis using hierarchical models due to limited data granularity, constraining our ability to precisely estimate summary receiver operating characteristics. Finally, the geographic distribution of studies was concentrated in South Asia and Southeast Asia, with minimal representation from Africa or Latin America, potentially limiting applicability to other LMIC regions with different trauma epidemiology.

Future research should prioritize large-scale, prospective, multicenter validation studies across diverse LMIC settings to establish region-specific performance benchmarks and optimal thresholds. Particular emphasis should be placed on standardizing cut-off values. Currently, practice varies widely, complicating inter-study comparisons and clinical implementation. Investigation of hybrid scoring approaches that combine clinical variables, point-of-care ultrasonography, and laboratory markers (such as lactate, base deficit, or hemoglobin trends) may yield superior diagnostic accuracy compared to existing unidimensional systems. Integration of these scoring tools into clinical decision pathways alongside selective CT imaging protocols could optimize resource utilization while maintaining patient safety. Additionally, implementation science research is needed to understand barriers and facilitators to scoring system adoption in LMIC emergency departments, including training requirements, inter-rater reliability, documentation burden, and alignment with existing trauma care workflows. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses comparing scoring system-guided selective imaging versus universal CT protocols would inform policy decisions in resource-constrained health systems.

Conclusions

In conclusion, both CASS and BATSS demonstrate statistically significant diagnostic value for identifying IAI in LMIC populations with BAT, though neither achieves sufficiently high sensitivity and specificity to function as a standalone decision rule. CASS offers superior sensitivity for rule-out applications during initial triage, while BATSS provides enhanced specificity for rule-in scenarios when FAST is available. Substantial heterogeneity in diagnostic performance across studies underscores the importance of local validation and threshold optimization before implementation. When thoughtfully integrated into structured trauma protocols alongside clinical judgment and selective imaging, these scoring systems hold considerable promise for improving triage efficiency, reducing diagnostic delays, and optimizing scarce imaging resources in LMICs. However, realization of this potential will require sustained investments in trauma system infrastructure, provider training, quality assurance mechanisms, and ongoing research to refine and validate these instruments across diverse LMIC populations and practice environments.
