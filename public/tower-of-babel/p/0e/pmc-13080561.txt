Paradoxical Pulmonary Congestion After Mavacamten in Obstructive HCM
Authors: Noriaki Tabata, Yasuhiro Izumiya, Noriko Nomura, Naoto Kuyama, Eiichiro Yamamoto, Kenichi Tsujita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Mavacamten, a first-in-class cardiac myosin inhibitor, has demonstrated robust efficacy in reducing left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction and improving symptoms in patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM).1 Consequently, LVOT gradient reduction is often considered a surrogate for hemodynamic recovery.2 However, HCM is not solely a disease of obstruction but also of diastolic dysfunction, abnormal ventricular-arterial coupling, and impaired atrial-ventricular interaction.3 Hemodynamic profiles may evolve dynamically once a dominant pressure gradient is relieved.2 While deterioration of systolic function associated with excessive myosin inhibition has been described, paradoxical pulmonary congestion in the setting of preserved systolic function and reduced LVOT obstruction remains poorly characterized.3 We describe a patient who developed pulmonary congestion shortly after successful LVOT relief with mavacamten and propose a mechanistic framework to explain this hemodynamic transition.

Take-Home Messages

•Rapid LVOT relief can paradoxically trigger congestion, even with preserved LVEF; this “functional dam” effect reflects a mismatch between increased preload and limited diastolic reserve.

History of Presentation

A 51-year-old man was referred for progressive exertional dyspnea. He had been diagnosed with HCM 4 years earlier after detection of a cardiac murmur. Despite treatment with a class Ia antiarrhythmic drug and beta-blocker, symptoms persisted. Echocardiography at presentation revealed asymmetric septal hypertrophy with systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve and marked left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction (blood pressure: 110/70 mm Hg). His plasma B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) level was elevated.

Investigations

Owing to persistent symptoms, mavacamten was initiated at week 0. Clinical improvement occurred within several days. By week 2, the patient's BNP level had decreased and his NYHA functional status had improved to class I. At week 4, follow-up echocardiography demonstrated significant improvement in LVOT obstruction with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction, with concomitant increase in Doppler-derived stroke volume and cardiac output (blood pressure: 120/60 mm Hg) (Figure 1A). Left-sided diastolic indices suggested no worsening: E/e′ remained stable (from 15 to 14.7), while left atrial volume index decreased from 58.9 to 46.3 mL/m2. Notably, the tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient (TRPG) had increased from 29 to 40 mm Hg, while the inferior vena cava (IVC) diameter remained normal (13.1 mm). Transmitral inflow analysis at this time demonstrated an increase in the E/A ratio to 2.8, while E/e′ and left atrial volume index did not show overt deterioration. The patient was asymptomatic at this time. At week 6, exertional dyspnea recurred (blood pressure: 110/60 mm Hg). His BNP increased to 159 pg/mL and IVC diameter increased to 21 mm, with estimated right atrial pressure rising from 3 mm Hg at baseline to 8 mm Hg based on American Society of Echocardiography criteria. Ejection fraction remained unchanged. Chest radiography showed new blunting of bilateral costophrenic angles (Figure 2B). Given a working diagnosis of pulmonary congestion due to hemodynamic redistribution after LVOT relief, an SGLT2 inhibitor was initiated.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At week 8, follow-up evaluation demonstrated further hemodynamic stabilization, with normalization of IVC diameter (11 mm), estimated right atrial pressure (3 mm Hg), and transmitral E/A ratio (1.46). The TRPG decreased to 31.6 mm Hg, BNP declined to 97.9 pg/mL, and E/e′ improved to 12.5, while the LVOT gradient remained reduced (65.3 mm Hg) and ejection fraction was preserved (62.9%). The left atrial volume index increased to 68.6 mL/m2, suggesting adaptive remodeling rather than recurrent congestion. Chest radiography obtained after the initiation of adjunctive therapy demonstrated improvement of pulmonary congestion (Figure 2C). Overall, these findings were consistent with sustained clinical and hemodynamic improvement following adjunctive therapy. Discussion

This case demonstrates that relief of LVOT obstruction does not necessarily translate into uniform hemodynamic improvement. Although mavacamten effectively reduced the LVOT gradient and preserved systolic function, pulmonary congestion paradoxically progressed during the early treatment phase of obstructive HCM. The most striking feature of this case is the dissociation between the left-sided diastolic parameters and the clinical trajectory. At week 4, E/e′ and left atrial volume index appeared improved, yet pulmonary pressures were already rising, as evidenced by TRPG elevation. BNP and IVC enlargement manifested only at week 6, coinciding with symptoms. At this time, estimated right atrial pressure increased transiently, supporting a flow-driven increase in venous return rather than progressive intrinsic myocardial dysfunction. This temporal separation underscores the limitations of resting left-sided indices in predicting hemodynamic shifts after mavacamten initiation.

We hypothesize that severe LVOT obstruction may function as a dynamic restraint on pulmonary venous return in selected individuals. Once this “functional dam” is removed, pulmonary venous inflow may increase abruptly, overwhelming a hypertrophied and noncompliant left ventricle. This may lead to an elevation of left atrial pressure and pulmonary venous congestion despite preserved systolic contraction. The observed deterioration occurred in the absence of systolic dysfunction and despite sustained reduction of LVOT gradient, making drug-related myocardial suppression unlikely. Instead, the temporal association of worsening pulmonary congestion with gradient reduction supports the concept of hemodynamic imbalance caused by sudden changes in ventricular loading conditions.

A similar phenomenon has been reported after relief of other forms of fixed-outflow obstruction, such as pulmonary edema after transcatheter aortic valve replacement in patients with severe aortic stenosis, where restoration of forward flow unmasks latent diastolic dysfunction.4 These analogies support the plausibility that abrupt flow normalization can expose limited cardiac compliance.

Our conceptual model highlights this hemodynamic transition as a systemic interaction between pulmonary circulation and left heart compliance, rather than a purely intraventricular phenomenon. This perspective expands the clinical framework by suggesting that LVOT relief should be viewed as a physiologic transition rather than a simple correction of obstruction. Importantly, pulmonary congestion improved with adjunctive heart failure therapy without cessation of mavacamten. This suggests that recognition of early pulmonary congestion and timely intervention may permit continued disease-specific therapy. Instead of simply increasing loop diuretics, we initiated an SGLT2 inhibitor (dapagliflozin) to manage the congestion. This strategy was chosen not only for its osmotic diuretic effect but also for its potential benefits in improving diastolic function and mitigating the acute hemodynamic transition from pressure to volume overload.5,6 Notably, follow-up assessment at week 8 demonstrated improvement in pulmonary and filling pressure indices, including reductions in BNP, TRPG, and E/e′, while LVOT relief and systolic function were maintained. These findings indicate that the observed hemodynamic transition was not progressive but reversible, and that appropriate adjunctive therapy can restore hemodynamic balance without discontinuation of mavacamten. This reversibility was reflected by normalization of transmitral inflow pattern, IVC diameter, and estimated right atrial pressure at follow-up. Clinicians should therefore monitor not only LVOT gradient but also pulmonary congestion indices in the early phase of therapy.

Although definitive predictors cannot be identified from a single patient, several baseline features may plausibly predispose patients to this hemodynamic transition. These include substantial left ventricular hypertrophy with limited diastolic reserve, as well as elevated left atrial pressure or increased left atrial volume, which may indicate chronic diastolic loading. Patients with borderline pulmonary hypertension may also be vulnerable, as even modest increases in flow can disproportionately raise pulmonary pressures. Furthermore, an early rise in TRPG after initiating mavacamten, as observed in this case, may represent a subtle warning sign of impaired compliance. Finally, blunted improvement in myocardial relaxation indices such as e′, even when E/e′ appears stable, may signal subclinical diastolic stiffness not captured by standard resting measures. In this context, the subsequent increase in left atrial volume index observed at week 8 may reflect adaptive remodeling to accommodate increased preload and reduce filling pressures, rather than recurrent decompensation. This interpretation is supported by the concomitant improvement in BNP, TRPG, and E/e′, indicating relief of pulmonary congestion despite volumetric adaptation of the left atrium. This observation further supports the concept that LVOT relief initiates a dynamic process of hemodynamic re-equilibration rather than a linear recovery. Although Doppler-derived stroke volume and cardiac output suggested an increase in forward flow, invasive hemodynamic confirmation was not available, and the proposed mechanism should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Conclusions

Relief of LVOT obstruction should not be equated with universal hemodynamic improvement. In selected patients with obstructive HCM, mavacamten therapy may reveal latent diastolic limitation and provoke pulmonary congestion through hemodynamic redistribution. Careful surveillance for congestion parameters may permit timely intervention without discontinuation of disease-specific therapy.
