Dementia progression with GLP‐1 receptor agonists in people with mild cognitive impairment and type 2 diabetes: target‐trial emulation study
Authors: Meir Schechter, Dvora R Sehtman‐Shachar, Alisa Fishkin, Ofri Mosenzon, Tali Cukierman‐Yaffe, Ronit Sharon, Gil Leibowitz, David Arkadir, Genya Aharon‐Hananel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONIn people with type 2 diabetes (T2D) without dementia, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs) improved cardiovascular, kidney, and mortality outcomes, with multiple real‐world studies suggesting reduced risk of dementia onset. However, the EVOKE/EVOKE+ randomized‐controlled trials (RCTs) found that oral semaglutide did not slow cognitive decline in individuals with early Alzheimer's disease (AD), most (86%) without T2D. It is unclear whether GLP‐1 RA therapy influences progression to dementia in people with both T2D and mild cognitive impairment (MCI).METHODSIn a target‐trial emulation hypothesis‐generating study using TriNetX global collaborative network data, we compared incident dementia (including AD) in adults with MCI and T2D without dementia, who were new users of GLP‐1 RAs versus dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors (DPP4i).RESULTSWe included 1320 propensity‐score‐matched (1:1) individuals who initiated GLP‐1 RAs or DPP4i in the period 2010 to 2021 (702 women; mean age 67.2 years). Over up to 5 years of follow‐up, incident dementia occurred in 101 and 132 participants initiating GLP‐1 RAs and DPP4i, respectively (Cox proportional hazard ratio 0.74 [95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.57 to 0.95]). At a mean follow‐up of 3.9 years, the respective Kaplan‐Meier‐derived cumulative incidences were 15.5% and 20.4% (absolute difference −4.9% [95% CI −9.4 to −0.4], number needed to treat of 21 [11 to 234] individuals to prevent one event). Various sensitivity analyses supported the primary analysis: comparing GLP‐1 RAs with basal insulin, using alternative outcome definitions, repeating the analyses across baseline subgroups, and using a negative control outcome.CONCLUSIONIn this hypothesis‐generating study, initiation of GLP‐1 RAs versus DPP4i in people with T2D and MCI without dementia was associated with a lower risk of incident dementia.Keywords: mild cognitive impairment, dementia, type 2 diabetes, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, target‐trial emulation, real‐world data, multinational cohort, TriNetX

BACKGROUND

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) often reflects a transition phase from normal aging to dementia. Disease‐modifying options to reduce the risk of dementia progression are limited to anti‐amyloid agents, and only in individuals with brain amyloid accumulation. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Even with these therapies, the residual risk of progression to dementia remains high. Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a major risk factor for MCI, 5 and 13% to 39% of individuals with MCI or dementia have T2D. 6 Individuals with coexisting MCI and T2D face an even higher risk of progression to dementia, 7 , 8 yet there are no risk‐modifying therapies specifically approved to mitigate dementia progression for this group.

Besides their glucose‐lowering effects, 9 , 10 glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist (GLP‐1 RA) therapy in people with T2D improves body weight, 9 , 10 , 11 blood pressure, 12 cardiovascular, 9 , 13 kidney, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 liver, 17 and stroke outcomes 13 – all of which are dementia risk factors. Some post hoc analyses of randomized‐controlled trials (RCTs) in people with T2D provided evidence of improved neurocognitive outcomes with GLP‐1 RAs compared with placebo. 18 , 19 These findings, although not confirmatory, were supported by preclinical data 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 and by multinational retrospective real‐world cohort studies of people with T2D and without a history of neurodegeneration. 25 , 26

Studies in people with pre‐existing neurodegeneration yielded conflicting evidence regarding disease progression with GLP‐1 RAs versus placebo. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 The EVOKE and EVOKE+ trials 34 and the ELAD trial 33 assessed neurological outcomes with oral semaglutide and liraglutide, respectively, versus placebo, in people with early Alzheimer's disease (AD). Both trials failed to meet their primary neurological endpoints but reported positive findings in their pre‐specified secondary outcomes. These trials focused mainly on people without T2D. Accordingly, it remains unclear whether GLP‐1 RAs influence dementia progression in people with coexisting MCI and T2D, who often have a high burden of GLP‐1 RA‐modifiable dementia risk factors. To address this knowledge gap, we used a multicenter real‐world data registry to emulate a target trial comparing the risk of dementia progression between GLP‐1 RA versus dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitor (DPP4i) therapy in people with MCI and T2D.

METHODS

Target trial specification and emulation strategy

We specified the protocol of a hypothetical target trial and emulated it using observational data. In the trial, eligible participants would be adults with MCI and T2D who newly initiated either a GLP‐1 RA or a DPP4i. In the target trial, we would exclude individuals with a prior diagnosis of dementia, AD, or evidence of use of drugs for dementia. We would also exclude individuals with a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes or severely uncontrolled T2D, defined as a glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) >10.5%. Participants would be randomly assigned to initiate either a GLP‐1 RA or a DPP4i and followed under an intention‐to‐treat principle. Follow‐up continued for up to 5 years to minimize biases associated with prolonged follow‐up, including treatment crossover and informative censoring. The primary outcome would be time to a new diagnosis of any dementia or AD. The causal estimand would be the intention‐to‐treat effect of initiating GLP‐1 RA therapy versus DPP4i on the risk of progression to dementia (Table S1).

We emulated this target trial using longitudinal observational data from the TriNetX LIVE global federated research network, which supports analyses of de‐identified electronic health record data from multiple countries. Eligibility criteria, baseline covariates, and outcomes were operationalized using prespecified International Classification of Diseases (ICD)‐10 codes, and treatment strategies were operationalized using Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) codes in a new‐user, active‐comparator design (Tables S1 and S2). To approximate random treatment assignment, we used propensity‐score matching to balance baseline characteristics before treatment initiation. Participants were followed under an intention‐to‐treat framework for up to 5 years, until outcome occurrence, death, or end of follow‐up.

Study specifications

We included adults living with T2D and a history of MCI and an ambulatory visit. In the main analysis, we included those who initiated a GLP‐1 RA or a DPP4i between March 1, 2010 and December 31, 2021, a period chosen to reflect the availability of these drug classes and to allow up to 5 years of potential follow‐up. We excluded those with a history of dementia, AD, or an indication of treatment with anti‐dementia drugs or those with type 1 diabetes (Table S2). We required an HbA1c measurement within the past 18 months and excluded those with HbA1c > 10.5%. To support a new‐user, active‐comparator design and reduce potential immortal‐time bias, we excluded those who had used the comparative drug within 6 months of treatment initiation. The index date was the date of treatment initiation. Baseline variables were collected in the year before the index date. The main study composite outcome was time to diagnosis of dementia or AD (Table S3).

Sensitivity analysis

We performed a set of sensitivity analyses aiming to address potential confounders and biases. To account for potential detection biases, we assessed an additional composite outcome that included dementia, AD, or treatment initiation for dementia. Due to limitations in the TriNetX platform, we could not account for the competing risk of death; instead, we assessed the composite outcome of dementia, AD, or all‐cause death. Additional outcomes of interest were a new diagnosis of any dementia, AD, or vascular dementia (Table S3). In another sensitivity analysis, we restricted the outcome definition only to those with an indication of the event on two separate occasions (confirmed‐event analysis) to increase specificity. We used upper respiratory tract infection incidence as a negative control outcome. We also assessed, in each cohort, the number of prescriptions received for the treatment and comparator drugs during follow‐up to evaluate treatment adherence.

Outcome assessments were performed in the whole cohort and in subgroups defined by age (≤70 or >70 years), sex (men or women), year of treatment initiation (≤2018 or ≥2019), race (White or others), HbA1c (≤7.5 or >7.5%; measured within 6 months before treatment initiation), body mass index (BMI; <32 or ≥ 32 kg/m2), blood albumin (≤4 or >4 g/dL), and by specific GLP‐1 RA initiated (semaglutide, dulaglutide, or liraglutide). In the subgroup analysis, each category was propensity‐score‐matched and analyzed separately. We also performed a sensitivity analysis excluding those with Parkinson's disease at baseline.

In separate analyses, we compared those initiating GLP‐1 RA versus basal insulin, GLP‐1 RA versus sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), and SGLT2i versus DPP4i using a similar approach. In analyses that included an SGLT2i arm, we included only those who started treatment after January 1, 2015, to align with the time when the first SGLT2i received regulatory approval.

Statistical analyses

Propensity‐score matching 1:1 was performed using TriNetX platform's built‐in algorithm, which performs nearest‐neighbor, greedy matching without replacement with a default caliper of 0.10 standard deviations of the propensity score. We considered the following 37 variables. In the demographics category, individuals were matched on age, sex (female and male), race, and ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino, White, Black or African American, and Asian). In the diagnosis category, individuals were matched on ischemic heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, essential (primary) hypertension, chronic kidney disease (CKD) diagnosis, atrial fibrillation and flutter, heart failure, and hyperlipidemia. In the medication category, individuals were matched on beta blocking agents, diuretics, statins, calcium channel blockers, anxiolytics, agents acting on the renin‐angiotensin system, aspirin, biguanides, sulfonylureas, thiazolidinediones, and clopidogrel. In the laboratory category, individuals were matched on blood hemoglobin, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, plasma glycated hemoglobin A1c, BMI, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, plasma alanine aminotransferase, blood albumin, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (Modification of Diet in Renal Disease formula). Depending on the specific analytic comparison, we also included use of SGLT2i, basal insulin, GLP‐1 RAs, and DPP4i (if not used to define the treatment arm).

Baseline variables were presented as frequencies for categorical variables or as means with standard deviation for continuous variables. We used standardized mean differences before and after propensity‐score matching to assess the balancing process.

To estimate the intention‐to‐treat effect specified in the target trial, we compared outcomes between treatment groups using Cox proportional hazards regression models. We also calculated the E value with the lower limit of the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the main outcome. 35 We fitted cumulative incidence functions to estimate the incidence of the main outcome at the mean follow‐up time point with 95% CIs. Using these estimates, we calculated the absolute risk difference and the number of patients needed to treat to prevent one event of the main outcome during the study's mean follow‐up. For the confirmed event analyses, we used aggregate data output from the TriNetX system without timestamps; we therefore calculated the risk ratio and the 95% CI using the Wald method with log transformation as previously described. 26

Being a retrospective emulation study, the analyses were performed to generate hypotheses without hierarchical testing. Accordingly, estimates are presented with 95% CIs, and no adjustment for multiple testing was performed. Analyses were conducted using the TriNetX LIVE platform, which uses R‐based functions.

Ethics approval

We performed the analyses in November 2025. The analyses used aggregate data extracted from the TriNetX network, and the investigators did not have access to patients' data; therefore, the Helsinki Committee of Hadassah Medical Center granted an exemption from Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval and from patients' informed consent.

RESULTS

After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria and propensity‐score matching, we included 1320 individuals (702 women) who initiated GLP‐1 RAs or DPP4i (Figure S1), with a mean age of 67.2 years and a mean HbA1c of 7.6%. Most baseline variables, including the proportions of patients with a history of hypertension (81.4%), hyperlipidemia (76.0%), cerebrovascular disease (19.1%), or ischemic heart disease (31.4%), were comparable across groups after matching (SMD < 0.10). However, the mean baseline BMI was 34.2 and 32.9 kg/m2 (SMD 0.17), and the mean baseline blood albumin was 4.0 and 3.9 g/dL (SMD 0.15) with GLP‐1 RAs and DPP4i, respectively (Table 1). Among the GLP‐1 RA group, 278 (42.1%), 182 (27.6%), 143 (21.7%), and 49 (7.4%) initiated dulaglutide, semaglutide, liraglutide, and exenatide, respectively (Table S4). Individuals had a mean follow‐up of 3.9 years and a total follow‐up of 5159 person‐years (Table S5). Dispensation patterns suggested continued use of the initiated therapy and limited switching to the comparator during follow‐up (Figure S2).

This finding was supported by a series of sensitivity analyses. First, we considered expanded definitions of the composite outcomes. Those initiating GLP‐1 RAs versus DPP4i had a HR of 0.76 [0.60 to 0.97] for the composite of dementia or AD diagnoses or initiation of drug for dementia. The between‐group HR for incident dementia, AD, or death was 0.69 [0.56 to 0.84] (Figure 2). Similar results were observed in the confirmed‐event analysis, showing a lower risk of dementia or AD onset with GLP‐1 RAs than with DPP4i (72 [10.9%] vs 99 [15.2%] events, respectively; risk ratio 0.72 [95% CI: 0.54 to 0.96]; Figure 3). Results were in the same direction when assessing the risk of the main composite outcomes across baseline subgroups – defined by age, sex, year of treatment initiation, race, HbA1c, BMI, blood albumin, and the specific GLP‐1 RA initiated – although most did not reach separate statistical significance (Figure 4). There was no evidence of a difference in the incidence of the negative control outcome, upper respiratory tract infection, between those initiating GLP‐1 RAs and those initiating DPP4i (Figure S3).

DISCUSSION

In a target‐trial emulation, hypothesis‐generating study of adults with MCI and T2D without dementia, initiation of GLP‐1 RAs compared with DPP4i was associated with a 26% relative reduction in the risk of dementia or AD onset. At a mean follow‐up of 3.9 years, the estimated absolute risk reduction was 4.9% (95% CI: −9.4 to −0.4), corresponding to a number needed to treat of 21 (95% CI: 11 to 234) individuals. The results were supported by several sensitivity analyses: When considering initiation of dementia drugs as an outcome, counting only confirmed events at a second visit and assessing a negative control outcome. Differences were observed when comparing GLP‐1 RAs with basal insulin, but not when comparing with SGLT2 inhibitors. These hypothesis‐generating data support conducting a dedicated randomized controlled clinical trial to test the effect of GLP‐1 RAs on neurodegeneration outcomes in people with MCI and T2D.

Several post hoc analyses of RCTs assessed neurocognitive outcomes with GLP‐1 RA therapy in people with T2D, mostly without prior evidence of neurodegeneration. In some analyses 18 , 19 but not others, 18 , 36 GLP‐1 RA therapy was associated with improved neurocognitive outcomes. Studies using real‐world data also highlighted the potential benefits of GLP‐1 RAs in reducing the risk of neurodegeneration onset. 25 , 26 , 37 , 38 , 39 However, given the relatively low baseline risk of dementia, the observed absolute risk differences with GLP‐1 RAs were small. One study found a 2.9% incidence of any neurodegenerative disorder during a mean follow‐up of 4.0 years in people with T2D starting DPP4i, with an absolute risk reduction of −0.6% in those initiating GLP‐1 RAs. 26 Another study found a rate difference of 2.2 dementia events per 1000 person‐years with GLP‐1 RAs versus other glucose‐lowering agents. 39 Here, we sampled people with pre‐existing MCI and T2D. In this high‐risk population, the estimated cumulative incidence of dementia or AD at 3.9 years (mean follow‐up) was 20.4% with DPP4i. GLP‐1 RA initiation was associated with an absolute risk reduction of 4.9%, corresponding to 21 individuals needing treatment to prevent one event, highlighting potential clinical significance, although the wide confidence interval (11 to 234) reflects considerable imprecision.

In preclinical models of AD, GLP‐1 RAs attenuated microglial and astrocytic activation, reduced amyloid plaque burden, suppressed neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, preserved neuronal structure and synaptic integrity, and restored central insulin signaling via insulin receptor substrate‐1 phosphorylation. 22 , 23 , 24 However, there is ongoing controversy over whether GLP‐1 RAs can effectively cross the blood–brain barrier in humans, with evidence suggesting that penetration occurs mainly in the brainstem regions where the blood–brain barrier is less tight. 40 , 41 Accordingly, while direct neural mechanisms cannot be ruled out, it seems that indirect mechanisms may explain most of the effect, including the cardiovascular benefits, 9 , 13 kidney effects, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 glycemic control, 9 reduction in adiposity, 9 , 11 and blood pressure. 12 GLP‐1 RAs have also been shown to exert anti‐inflammatory effects, both systemically 42 and in the central nervous system. 43 Such anti‐inflammatory effects, which appear to operate independently of GLP‐1 RA metabolic actions, 43 may also explain potential neurocognition benefits.

In the target‐trial emulation, we compared those initiating GLP‐1 RA with those initiating DPP4i. This comparison was selected because, at the time of the study, both were comparable options, and those using one class did not simultaneously use the other, making the emulation more robust. In a sensitivity analysis, we found that initiation of GLP‐1 RA was associated with a lower risk of dementia or AD compared with basal insulin, but not with SGLT2i (HR 0.88 [0.70 to 1.11]). The lack of a difference relative to SGLT2i may reflect limited power, effect attenuation against this comparator, or residual confounding. Although SGLT2i, compared with DPP4i, were not associated with a lower risk of dementia (HR 1.03 [0.78 to 1.34]), other real‐world studies have described an association between SGLT2i use and a lower incidence of dementia in populations with T2D without evidence of neurodegeneration, 39 , 44 warranting further investigation.

It is under debate whether GLP‐1 RA therapy may reduce the risk of dementia in people with evidence of neurodegeneration without T2D. In the ELAD trial, 33 12‐month liraglutide therapy mitigated cognitive function decline and brain volume loss compared with placebo in people with AD. However, these were secondary outcomes, and there was no evidence of a difference in the trial's prespecified primary outcome of cerebral glucose metabolic rate. 33 The EVOKE and EVOKE+ RCTs compared oral semaglutide versus placebo in 3808 adults aged 55 to 85 years with MCI or mild dementia due to AD with confirmed amyloid abnormalities detected by imaging or cerebrospinal fluid analysis. 34 The trial was terminated early as the 2‐year interim primary analysis found no evidence of semaglutide superiority over placebo in reducing Alzheimer's clinical progression, although semaglutide therapy was associated with improvements in some AD‐related biomarkers. Several potential explanations exist for the differences between our results and EVOKE's. Two years may be too short to detect a between‐group difference at this disease stage. Supporting this hypothesis, our cumulative incidence function showed ongoing separation between the study arms over time. It is also possible that GLP‐1 RAs could be beneficial in a subpopulation of people with early‐phase neurodegeneration and pre‐existing T2D, where they modify additional key risk factors. While we cannot exclude the possibility that our study is subject to confounding and biases inherent in real‐world data, these findings raise the question of whether GLP‐1 RAs may modify neurocognitive outcomes in individuals with co‐existence of MCI and T2D – a question that could be addressed in a dedicated trial in this population.

The study has several limitations. It includes only people with coexisting MCI and T2D, and results should not be extrapolated to people with AD without diabetes. Although aiming to emulate a target trial, it is limited by retrospective data collection and is subject to unmeasured confounding and potential biases; accordingly, the results can only be used to generate hypotheses and should not be viewed as confirmatory. Residual confounding may include differences in socioeconomic status, disease duration, and standards of care across healthcare organizations. Patients initiating a newer injectable therapy may also undergo more frequent monitoring, raising the possibility of surveillance bias. We did not have access to cognitive tests, cerebrospinal fluid, or imaging markers; thus, MCI at baseline and dementia and AD incidence were detected using ICD‐10 codes that can be misused, resulting in potential ascertainment biases. To partially address this limitation, we performed a confirmed‐event sensitivity analysis using a more specific outcome definition. After matching, baseline BMI was higher among those starting GLP‐1 RAs versus DPP4i. Residual imbalance was also present for baseline albumin, with limited clinical relevance (4.0 vs 3.9 g/dL, respectively). TriNetX platform limitations precluded a doubly robust analysis to adjust further for these covariates. We addressed this limitation by performing a subgroup analysis stratified by baseline BMI and albumin, which showed a directionality similar to that of the main analysis. For the same reason, we could not fit subdistribution models (e.g., Fine‐Gray) to account for the competing risk of death, which limited our ability to provide unbiased, population‐level risk estimates. To partially account for it, we assessed mortality events as part of a composite outcome; however, this does not fully substitute for a formal competing‐risk analysis. Exposure was defined at the class level, and although agent‐specific subgroup analysis was directionally consistent, it cannot substitute for agent‐ or dose‐specific assessment. We could not account for changes in treatment regimen using time‐varying adjustment or as‐treated analyses (an alternative to the intention‐to‐treat analysis conducted). However, in a control analysis, we found that, although some participants switched treatments during follow‐up, most participants continued the study drug and did not initiate the comparator.

In conclusion, in a target‐trial emulation, hypothesis‐generating, retrospective study using a multinational representative cohort of people with coexisting MCI and T2D, initiation of GLP‐1 RAs versus DPP4i was associated with a lower risk of dementia onset during a mean follow‐up of 3.9 years. These hypothesis‐generating findings should not be viewed as confirmatory and require validation in a dedicated trial.
