Xylooligosaccharide interferes with the cell cycle and reduces the antibiotic tolerance of avian pathogenic Escherichia coli by associating with its potential antimetabolic actions
Authors: Lulu Ren, Hui Ye, Jiarong Fang, Qingyun Cao, Changming Zhang, Zemin Dong, Dingyuan Feng, Jianjun Zuo, Weiwei Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to probe if xylooligosaccharide (XOS) could act as an antimetabolite to impact the cell cycle and antibiotic tolerance of avian pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC). We firstly measured the bacteriostasis of XOS against APEC O78 and its effect on the growth of APEC O78 growing on different medium. Afterwards, the effects of XOS on xylose operon activation along with the cell cycle and antibiotic tolerance of APEC O78 were analyzed. The results showed that XOS caused no inhibitory circle against APEC O78 and did not affect (P > 0.05) the growth of APEC O78 growing on LB medium. Besides, APEC O78 was unable to grow on M9 medium (carbon-free) added with XOS. However, XOS exerted a similar role as xylose in increasing (P < 0.05) the expression of certain xylose operon genes including xylose isomerase (XylA)-encoding gene (xylA) and xylose-binding periplasmic protein (XylF)-encoding gene (xylF) in APEC O78. The molecular docking simulation revealed that the major monomer components (xylobiose, xylotriose and xylotetraose) of XOS had stable binding potentials to both XylA and XylF proteins of E. coli, as supported by the low binding free energy and the formation of considerable hydrogen bonds between them. The subsequent analysis showed that XOS altered certain cell cycle-related genes expression, especially elevated (P < 0.05) nrdB expression and decreased ihfB expression to a degree. Moreover, XOS played a similar role as 2-deoxy-glucose (a glucose analogue serving as a typical antimetabolite) in lowering (P < 0.05) the number of ampicillin-tolerant APEC O78. Collectively, XOS had no direct bacteriostasis against APEC and could not be metabolized/utilized by APEC O78. However, it might become an analogue of xylose and then activate xylose transport- and metabolism-related proteins in APEC O78, thus functioning as a potential antimetabolite and exerting antimetabolic actions. This could at least partially interpret the observed roles of XOS in interfering with the cell cycle and diminishing the antibiotic tolerance of APEC O78. The above findings expand the knowledges about the functions of XOS and provide a basis for exploring novel strategies to reduce the antibiotic tolerance of APEC.

INTRODUCTION

Avian pathogenic Escherichia coli (APEC) strains (e.g. O1, O2, and O78 serogroups) usually represent the most common pathogenic bacteria in poultry and account for various diseases, known as colibacillosis, causing a huge economic loss in poultry production worldwide. Besides, APEC strains are considered as potential zoonotic pathogens that threaten public health (Logue et al., 2017; Kathayat et al., 2021), because they share high similarities in the genome sequences and pathogenic mechanisms with human extra-intestinal pathogenic Escherichia coli (Logue et al., 2017; Hu et al., 2023). The treatment or control of APEC infection is relatively difficult, due to the high insensitivity of APEC to antibiotics particularly the β-lactam antibiotics (Karacan-Sever et al., 2022). Furthermore, the exchange of mobile genetic elements between APEC and other bacteria can fortify the tolerance or resistance of APEC to antibiotics (Yoon et al., 2022), thereby contributing to the disease burden of APEC infection and seriously restricting the efficacy of antibiotic treatment for APEC infection (Kathayat et al., 2021). This is of great concern to the poultry industry and results in an interest to explore novel natural approaches to combat the antibiotic tolerance of APEC (Li et al., 2024).

Xylooligosaccharide (XOS) represents a typical kind of functional oligosaccharides prepared from the natural ingredient xylan, which is widely spread in the cell wall of multifarious plants. In general, XOS is used as a prebiotic to selectively facilitate the growth of certain beneficial bacteria (probiotics) and may subsequently antagonize the harmful bacteria in the hindgut of animals (Pang et al., 2021; Yadav et al., 2024), because it is a polymer comprising of β-D-(1,4)-xylopyranosyl units that can be degraded by certain probiotics having an ability to express the transporters and degrading enzymes of functional oligosaccharides (Broekaert et al., 2011; Singh et al., 2022). Unlike specific probiotics, wide types of Escherichia coli are unable to utilize XOS due to the lack of certain genes encoding corresponding degrading enzymes (Wang et al., 2017; Singh et al., 2022). However, our previous study discovered that XOS could directly inhibit the expression of adhesion and invasion-related genes of Salmonella (Zhang et al., 2024). Furthermore, it is interesting that the monomer components (xylobiose and xylotriose) of XOS can be sensed and binded by xylose operon-encoded proteins (xylose isomerase and xylokinase) in probiotics (Skariyachan et al., 2021). In fact, numerous sugar transporters of bacteria have a certain universality, that is, low affinity and nonspecificity (Ren et al., 2009; Kohlmeier et al., 2023). It has been verified that the nonmetabolizable analogues (such as 2-deoxy-glucose (2-DG), methyl-glucoside and methyl-mannoside) of monosaccharides could interact with the corresponding monosaccharide transporters and enter into the cells of certain bacteria (e.g. E. coli) to act as the antimetabolites, thereby triggering perturbations of certain physiological phenotypes such as biofilm formation that benefits the antibiotic insensitivity of bacteria (Sutrina et al., 2016; Poosarla et al., 2017). Based on the above findings, we assumed that the major components of the nonmetabolizable oligosaccharide XOS might act as xylose analogues to activate xylose transport- and/or metabolism-related proteins encoded by xylose operon in APEC, thus functioning as a potential antimetabolite and exert antimetabolic actions, by which XOS might disturb the cell cycles and subsequently elevate the antibiotic sensitivity of APEC (Macheboeuf et al., 2006; Morrison et al., 2024). In keeping with this, the present study was conducted to investigate if XOS could interfere with the cell cycle and diminish the antibiotic tolerance of APEC O78, the most prevalent strain of APEC in poultry production (Paixao et al., 2016), by associating with its potential antimetabolic actions. The results of this study would broaden our knowledges about the functions of XOS and drive the development of XOS application as a novel strategy to reduce the antibiotic tolerance of APEC, thus being anticipated to improve the efficacy of antibiotic treatments for APEC infection that represents a momentous concern in poultry production.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Measurement of the Bacteriostasis of Different Treatments Against APEC O78

The single colony of APEC O78 strain (CVCC1570, China Veterinary Culture Collection Center) was revived and inoculated into LB broth medium. A total of 200 μL of APEC O78 inoculum (1 × 109 CFU/mL) in logarithmic growth phase was inoculated into 200 mL of LB agar medium at about 50°C and mixed thoroughly. Thereafter, 20 mL of APEC O78 inoculum was poured into the culture dish until cooled and solidified. The sterilized hole puncher was used to punch holes on the solid medium and the medium in the holes was discarded. A total of 10 μL ampicillin antibiotic or XOS solution with different concentrations was respectively dropped into the above holes and cultured at 37°C overnight. Finally, the bacteriostasis of ampicillin and XOS against APEC O78 was evaluated by detecting the diameter of the inhibitory circle. The XOS preparation (> 95% purity) provided by the Longlive Bio-Technol. Co. Ltd. (Dezhou, China) was derived from corncob and contained 40% xylobiose, 33% xylotriose, 12% xylotetraose, 5% xylotentaose, 5.5% xylhexaose and xylheptaose.

Determination of the Effect of Different Treatments on APEC O78 Growth

Medium Preparation. The M9 medium (carbon-free) was prepared according to the method of Elbing and Brent (2002). Briefly, the inorganic salt solutions (containing 12.8 g/L Na2HPO4·7H2O, 3 g/L KH2PO4, 0.5 g/L NaCl and 1 g/L NH4Cl) were prepared with ultra-pure water and then added with MgSO4 and CaCl2 solutions (the final concentrations were 2 and 0.1 mmmoL/L, respectively), followed by adjustment of the pH value to 7.0 and autoclaved sterilization. Before usage, the M9 medium was added with sterile BME vitamins (Merck Corporation, Darmstadt, Germany).

APEC culture. The single colony of APEC O78 strain was revived and inoculated into LB broth medium or with added XOS at a final concentration of 4 mg/mL. Meanwhile, APEC O78 strain was inoculated into M9 medium added with glucose or XOS at a final concentration of 4 mg/mL. The above inocula were then cultured in a shaker (37°C, 220 r/min), followed by determination of the absorbances at 600 nm every 2 hours, based on which the growth curves of APEC O78 were drew.

Determination of the Effect of Different Treatments on Gene Expression in APEC O78

After cultured in M9 medium (carbon-free) or with added different carbon sources for 3 h, APEC O78 inocula were centrifuged and the bacterial cells were collected. RNA samples were then extracted from bacterial cells using the VeZol-Pure Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted RNA samples were dissolved in RNase-free ddH2O. The confirmation of RNA concentration and quality, reverse transcription as well as quantitative real-time PCR were then implemented according to our previous study (Wang et al., 2023). Primer informations of the reference gene namely RNA polymerase subunit alpha-encoding gene (rpoA) and the target genes associated with xylose operon and cell cycle of APEC O78 are presented in Table 1. Molecular Docking Simulation

The major monomer components (xylobiose, xylotriose and xylotetraose) of XOS were used as the ligands, while the xylose isomerase (XylA) and xylose-binding periplasmic protein (XylF) of E. coli served as the potential receptors. The molecular structures of xylobiose, xylotriose and xylotetraose were obtained from the Pubchem database and their spatial structures were optimized using the Chem3D. The crystal structures of XylA and XylF proteins of E. coli were acquired from the Uniport database. The PyMOL2.3.0 was used to remove the water molecules, irrelevant protein chains and primary ligands from the protein structures, followed by processing of chemical bonds using the AutoDock Tools1.5.6. The molecular docking models of the interactions of XOS components (xylobiose, xylotriose and xylotetraose) with XylA and XylF proteins were analyzed using the Auto Dock Vina 1.2.0 and further visualized using the PyMOL2.3.0. Generally, the binding free energy less than -5.0 kcal/moL indicates a stable binding model between the ligands and the potential receptor proteins.

Evaluation of the Effect of Different Treatments on the Sensibility of APEC O78 to Antibiotic

Because of the high tolerance or resistance of APEC to β-lactam antibiotics (Karacan-Sever et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2023) in chicken industry, ampicillin that represents a typical β-lactam antibiotic was selected for the subsequent research.

Effect of different treatments on the bacteriostasis of ampicillin against APEC O78. APEC O78 was precultured in M9 medium or with added different carbon sources (glucose and XOS, 4 mg/mL) until reached the logarithmic growth phase, followed by adjustment of the concentration to a same level. The above APEC O78 inocula were respectively added into LB agar medium at about 50°C and mixed thoroughly, followed by pouring into the culture dish until cooled and solidified. The above agar plates were then placed with ampicillin-containing filter paper (Solarbio Biotechnol., Beijing, China) at the central location. After incubation at 37°C overnight, the inhibitory circle was measured.

Effect of different treatments on the tolerance of APEC O78 to ampicillin. The tolerance of APEC O78 to ampicillin was evaluated based on the method of Gefen et al. (2017). Firstly, 10 μL of ampicillin (3 mg/mL) and different carbon sources (glucose or XOS, 4 mg/mL) were respectively dropped onto the blank (carbon-free) filter paper (Solarbio Biotechnol., Beijing, China) and subjected to air dry. Then, 100 μL of APEC O78 inoculum was evenly coated on LB agar. The ampicillin-containing filter paper was placed in the center of LB agar and cultured at 37°C to form an inhibitory circle. With the duration of culture time, the carbon source in LB agar was exhausted and the bacteria could not continue to reproduce. Thereafter, the ampicillin-containing filter paper in LB agar was removed and replaced with blank filter paper, glucose- and XOS-containing filter papers, respectively, followed by culture at 37°C for 9 h. Finally, APEC O78 colonies re-growing in the inhibitory circle (represented ampicillin-tolerant bacteria) were enumerated using the spread plate method.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (n = 6). All data were analyzed by the Student's t test (for comparisons between 2 groups) or 1-way ANOVA (for comparisons among 3 or more groups) of SPSS 25.0. Differences among groups were identified by the Duncan's multiple comparisons. Significance was defined as P < 0.05.

As presented in Figure 3, the major monomer components (xylobiose, xylotriose and xylotetraose) of XOS could stably bind to XylA and XylF proteins of E. coli, as supported by the relative low binding free energy that is less than -5.0 kcal/moL. The binding conformation analysis visualized that the above monomers could penetrate deep into the active pocket of XylA and XylF proteins, as visualized in Figure 4. DISCUSSION

As a functional oligosaccharide, XOS has long been accepted as a kind of prebiotics to selectively stimulate the growth of certain beneficial bacteria (probiotics) and then competitively repress the growth of harmful bacteria in animal gut (Pang et al., 2021; Yadav et al., 2024). However, it remains largely unknown whether XOS could directly inhibit pathogenic bacteria. In this study, we found no direct bacteriostasis of different concentrations of XOS against APEC O78, as evidenced by the absence of inhibitory circle. This was contrary to the observed effect of ampicillin antibiotic which elicited a distinct bacteriostasis against APEC. The further analysis revealed that supplementing XOS to LB medium did not affect the growth curve of APEC O78. In order to avoid the potential interference induced by glucose in LB medium, M9 medium (carbon-free) was then used, through which we detected that APEC O78 rapidly grew on medium with glucose as the sole carbon source, but failed to grow when XOS served as the sole carbon source. This result verified a role of XOS as a nonmetabolizable oligosaccharide for APEC O78, probably due to the absence of genes encoding corresponding transporter or degrading enzymes (Broekaert et al., 2011; Singh et al., 2022).

Generally, XOS is degraded by certain beneficial bacteria (e.g. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacteria) which possess the complete utilization system for XOS (Broekaert et al., 2011; Singh et al., 2022). However, it is of interest that XOS may also interact with xylose transport- and/or metabolism-related proteins of bacteria, since the monomer components (xylobiose and xylotriose) of XOS displayed a potential to act as the analogue of xylose and in turn effectually activate xylose operon-encoded proteins (xylose isomerase and xylokinase) in Lactobacillus probiotic (Skariyachan et al., 2021). Moreover, it has been indicated that quite a few sugar transporters of bacteria have a degree of universality, that is, low affinity and nonspecific targets (Ren et al., 2009; Kohlmeier et al., 2023). Accordingly, it could be speculated that XOS may also function as a ligand to elicit an affinity with certain proteins encoded by xylose operon in APEC, despite its inability to be metabolized/utilized by APEC. Indeed, we herein found that APEC treated with XOS showed an upregulation in the expression of certain xylose operon genes such as the xylA and xylF, which respectively encode XylA and XylF proteins that are critical for xylose transport and metabolism of bacteria (Liu et al., 2015). The subsequent comparative analysis disclosed that both XOS and its major monomer components (xylobiose, xylotriose and xylotetraose) played a similar role as xylose in activating the expression of xylA and xylF genes in APEC, although their efficacy existed some differences. These results implied that XOS might activate xylose operon of APEC, to some extent, by serving as an analogue of xylose. In order to reinforce these findings, we then conducted the molecular docking simulation, which deciphered low binding free energy (−5.0 Kcal/moL) of XOS monomer components (xylobiose, xylotriose and xylotetrose) interacting with both XylA and XylF proteins in E. coli. In support of this view, binding conformations analysis visualized that both xylobiose, xylotriose and xylotetrose could penetrate deep into the active pocket of XylA and XylF proteins, forming plentiful hydrogen bonds and a small number of carbon-hydrogen bonds with the amino acid residues of these 2 proteins in E. coli. These results further demonstrated that XOS components probably exerted stable binding potentials towards xylose transport- and metabolism-related proteins in APEC O78. Likewise, some previous studies have revealed a role of the nonmetabolizable analogues (such as 2-DG, methyl-glucoside and methyl-mannoside) of monosaccharides in activating the corresponding saccharide transporters in some harmful bacteria including E. coli, by which these analogues could enter into bacterial cells, subsequently serving as the antimetabolites and disturbing the cell metabolism of these bacteria (Sutrina et al., 2016; Poosarla et al., 2017). Overall, the binding potentials of XOS components to xylose transport- and metabolism-related proteins probably allowed an entry of XOS into the cells of APEC O78, through which XOS might play antimetabolic actions, on account of its nonmetabolizable property.

It has been well-established that the cell metabolism of bacteria is closely associated with certain physiological phenotypes including cell cycle progression and antibiotic sensitivity in bacteria (Wang and Levin, 2009; Morrison et al., 2024). Therefore, antimetabolites-induced antimetabolic actions may disturb the cell cycle and fortify the antibiotic sensitivity of bacteria (Wang and Levin, 2009; Morrison et al., 2024). To date, no information was available regarding whether XOS can impact the cell cycle and antibiotic sensitivity of bacteria. The present study showed that APEC O78 treated with XOS had an increased expression of cell cycle-related gene nrdB that encodes the β-subunit of ribonucleoside-diphosphate reductase 1 (Kolberg et al., 2004; Odsbu and Morigen, 2009). This enzyme catalyzes the transformation of ribonucleoside diphosphate into deoxyribonucleoside diphosphate (dNTP), which is viewed as a rate-limiting step in intracellular dNTP production and determines the movement of replication fork in bacteria (Martin and Imlay, 2011), subsequently influencing the chromosome replication period (C period) that represents a key stage of bacterial cell cycle (Wang and Levin, 2009; Zhu et al., 2017). Consequently, we deduced that XOS-induced increased expression of nrdB might alter intracellular dNTP substrate pools and then interfere with the cell cycle of APEC O78. In addition to the nrdB gene, the expression of some other cell cycle-related genes such as ihfB was also affected by XOS to a certain extent, as exhibited by the mitigating effect of XOS on carbon free-induced increase in the expression of ihfB. This gene encodes the β-subunit of integration host factor, which serves as a DNA-binding transcriptional regulator complex essential for the quiescent state of E. coli (Nicolau and Lewis, 2023) and favorable for the antibiotic insensitivity of pathogenic E. coli (Chittò et al., 2020; Nicolau and Lewis, 2023). Notably, deletion of ihfB gene has been reported to diminish the tolerance of pathogenic E. coli to ampicillin (Morrison et al., 2024). Based on the above findings, it could be supposed that the alterative expression of selected cell cycle-related genes (nrdB and ihfB) in APEC O78 arising from XOS-induced antimetabolic actions would conduce to the sensitivity of APEC to antibiotics. Thus, we then compared the antibiotic sensitivity of APEC O78 responding to XOS and 2-DG, a classical antimetabolite having an ability to hamper biofilm formation that can favor the antibiotic insensitivity of E. coli (Sutrina et al., 2016; Poosarla et al., 2017). The above comparative analysis manifested that XOS played a similar role as 2-DG in reducing the number of ampicillin-tolerant APEC O78, although it did not impact the bacteriostasis (evidenced by the absence of inhibitory circle) of ampicillin against APEC O78. These results had not been reported before and validated a capacity of XOS to weaken the tolerance of APEC O78 to ampicillin, which could greatly benefit the effectiveness of controlling APEC infection in poultry production, since antibiotic tolerance serves as a precursor to antibiotic resistance and renders the survival of pathogenic bacteria exposing to antibiotics (Windels et al., 2019; Stower, 2020). Similar to our findings, some previous studies have indicated that certain marine oligosaccharides (such as alginate oligosaccharide and chito-oligosaccharide) especially those with low molecular weight could improve the efficacy of antibiotics to suppress the growth of pathogenic bacteria, as well as diminish the antibiotic tolerance of these bacteria (Khan et al., 2012; He et al., 2014; Yuan et al., 2020).

CONCLUSIONS

XOS had no direct bacteriostasis against APEC O78 and was unable to be utilized/metabolized by this strain. Nevertheless, the major components of the nonmetabolizable oligosaccharide XOS had potentials to bind to and activate xylose transport- and metabolism-related proteins encoded by xylose operon, by which XOS might serve as an antimetabolite and exert antimetabolic actions for APEC O78. This could be at least partially responsible for the observed ability of XOS to interfere with the cell cycle and reduce the antibiotic tolerance of APEC O78. The findings in this study expand our fundamental knowledges concerning the functions of XOS and provide a basis for exploring novel strategies to lower the antibiotic tolerance of APEC, thus being anticipated to better control APEC infection in poultry production.
