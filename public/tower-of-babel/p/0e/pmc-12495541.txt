Evidence on oral tranexamic acid versus intravenous tranexamic acid for perioperative blood management in total knee arthroplasty: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yongjian Wang, Xinyu Na, Tong Chen, Lingqin Huang, Cong Chen, Hainan Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of oral versus intravenous tranexamic acid (TXA) in total knee arthroplasty (TKA).MethodsThe review process was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library were searched. Data extraction and quality assessment were performed independently by two investigators. The primary outcomes were hemoglobin (Hb) decrease and blood loss, while secondary outcomes included transfusion rate, operation time, hospital stay, and complications. The analysis used random-effects models and assessed heterogeneity with I2 values.ResultsNine studies were included in the meta-analysis, comprising a total of 1,227 participants. Across the included studies, oral TXA was most commonly administered as 1.95–2 g given 1–2 h before surgery, with some regimens including postoperative doses. Intravenous TXA was typically given as 1 g before surgery, sometimes with additional doses before wound closure or after surgery. The results showed no significant difference between oral and intravenous TXA in terms of Hb decrease and transfusion rates. Similarly, there was no significant difference in complications, operation time, and length of hospital stay. Comparable findings were observed in both RCTs and non-RCTs. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the overall results remained robust, with no single study exerting a substantial influence on the pooled estimates.ConclusionsBased on available evidence, there is no significant difference observed between oral and intravenous TXA in patients undergoing TKA. However, the wide confidence intervals for several outcomes indicate important uncertainty, and further high-quality studies are needed to confirm the comparative effectiveness and safety.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is an effective treatment for end-stage knee joint diseases, widely used in the management of osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and other related conditions (1, 2). With the aging population and changing lifestyles, the incidence of knee joint diseases has been rising annually, leading to a rapid increase in the number of TKA procedures (3). However, bleeding during and after TKA has remained a significant challenge for orthopedic surgeons (4). Excessive blood loss can lead to anemia and increase the demand for blood transfusions, which in turn carries the risk of transfusion-related complications (5). As such, effective control of perioperative bleeding in TKA has become a major focus of clinical research.

Tranexamic acid (TXA), a synthetic antifibrinolytic drug, exerts its hemostatic effects by competitively inhibiting the activation of plasminogen, thereby reducing fibrin degradation (6). Since its introduction in the 1960s, TXA has been widely used across various medical fields, including trauma, neurosurgery, and obstetrics, for the prevention and treatment of bleeding (7, 8, 9, 10). In the orthopedic field, particularly in TKA, TXA has significantly reduced perioperative blood loss and transfusion requirements, thereby enhancing surgical safety (11, 12, 13). Although concerns persist regarding the potential for TXA to increase thromboembolic risk, robust evidence from Taeuber et al. demonstrates no significant overall increase in such events (14, 15). Their findings underscore the importance of appropriate assessment time windows and vigilant follow-up when evaluating TXA safety. Traditional methods of TXA administration include intravenous injection, intra-articular application, and local application to surrounding tissues, with intravenous administration being favored for its rapid onset and stable blood drug concentration (16, 17). However, intravenous administration has certain limitations, such as the need for professional medical personnel to administer it, potential infusion reactions, and increased healthcare costs.

In recent years, oral TXA has gained attention as a potential alternative (11, 18, 19). The advantages of oral TXA include ease of use and lower costs, although its absorption and bioavailability may be affected by gastrointestinal function, food intake, and other factors. Consequently, the comparison of efficacy and safety between oral and intravenous TXA in TKA has become a critical area of clinical research. Previous meta-analyses comparing the efficacy of oral and intravenous TXA in total joint arthroplasty (TJA) have primarily focused on patients undergoing hip or combined hip and knee arthroplasty (20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25). Even when knee arthroplasty patients were included, these analyses lacked subgroup analysis specific to different joints (hip vs knee). Therefore, there is currently a lack of studies specifically evaluating the efficacy and safety of oral vs intravenous TXA in TKA patients.

Thus, we conducted a meta-analysis to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of oral and intravenous TXA in TKA patients, providing evidence-based guidance for clinical decision-making. This study seeks to advance the understanding of TXA use in TKA by providing a more targeted and robust comparison, ultimately contributing to the optimization of perioperative bleeding management in TKA. The findings intend to offer clinicians a clearer picture of the effectiveness and safety of oral TXA as a potential alternative to intravenous administration, with the aim of improving patient outcomes while reducing healthcare costs. Our hypothesis is that oral TXA and intravenous TXA will demonstrate comparable efficacy in patients undergoing TKA.

Methods

The study conforms to the principles outlined in the Handbook of the Cochrane Collaboration (26), along with the guidelines established by the PRISMA 2020 (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) statement (27). The protocol was registered on PROSPERO (Registration No: CRD42025648141).

Outcomes

The primary outcomes include the drop in hemoglobin (Hb) and blood loss. The secondary outcomes include transfusion rate, length of hospital stay, operation time, and complications.

Search strategy

Two of the authors performed the search in PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from the inception dates to January 2025, using the keywords ‘oral’, ‘tranexamic acid’, ‘intravenous’, ‘knee’, and ‘arthroplasty’. The detailed search strategy is provided in the Supplement (see section on Supplementary materials given at the end of the article). No language restrictions were applied during the search. Researchers were blind to each other’s decisions and assessments to minimize the risk of bias.

Study selection

After duplicates were removed, two independent researchers screened titles and abstracts and applied the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full texts of potentially eligible studies were reviewed. Disagreements were resolved by the senior researcher after group discussion.

Data collection process

Two investigators independently extracted relevant data from the included studies using a standardized form, with discrepancies resolved through consensus.

Assessment of risk of bias and quality of evidence

Two researchers independently assessed the quality of RCTs using the Cochrane risk-of-bias criteria (28). Two researchers independently assessed the quality of non-RCTs based on the Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS) (29). Based on three factors: selection, comparability, and exposure or outcome. The checklist graded articles from 0 to 9 stars. Studies of 7 to 9 stars, 5 or 6 stars, 4 stars, and 0 to 3 stars were considered very good, good, satisfactory, and unsatisfactory, respectively. Studies with 6 stars can be regarded as of high quality. Researchers determined whether risk-of-bias assessors were blinded to study details. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis was conducted using a leave-one-out approach, where each study was sequentially excluded to examine its influence on the overall effect size. The results remained stable throughout, indicating that no single study significantly affected the overall estimates.

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis used Stata software (version 17; StataCorp, USA, 2021). Heterogeneity was assessed via the Q test and calculation of the I2 value. Our analysis employed the random-effects model. Odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used for count outcome assessment. Continuous outcomes were evaluated using mean, standard deviation (SD), and sample size from each study. Weighted mean difference (WMD) was calculated when all included studies reported outcomes using consistent units and measurement scales, allowing for direct comparison. When studies employed different measurement scales for the same outcome, standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to enable meaningful comparison across studies. For count outcomes, studies in which both arms reported zero events (double-zero studies) were excluded from the meta-analysis as they provide no information for estimating relative effects. For studies with zero events in a single arm (intravenous group), a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to enable calculation of ORs. Statistical significance was denoted by a P-value below 0.05.

Results

A total of 441 records were screened after identifying 62 from PubMed, 280 from EMBASE, 97 from Web of Science, and 2 from Cochrane. After removing 154 duplicate records, 287 records were screened, and 270 were excluded based on title and abstract. Subsequently, 17 reports were sought for retrieval, all of which were successfully retrieved. These reports were assessed for eligibility, and eight studies were excluded. Exclusions were made for conference abstract (30), studies in which TKA patients could not be separated (31, 32), UKA studies (33), unspecified TKA patients (34), and studies in which both groups received either intravenous or oral TXA (11, 35, 36). Finally, nine studies were included in our meta-analysis (18, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43, 44). The search flow diagram is shown in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

Of the nine studies included, six were RCTs (18, 38, 40, 42, 43, 44) and three were cohort studies (37, 39, 41), with a total of 1,227 participants (604 in the oral TXA group and 623 in the intravenous TXA group). Five studies (18, 38, 39, 40, 41) were conducted in the USA, two in China (42, 43), one in Israel (44), and one in India (37). Of these studies, one (41) did not specify whether a tourniquet was used during the procedure, and one (42) clearly stated that a tourniquet was not used, while the remaining studies (18, 37, 38, 39, 40, 43, 44) all used a tourniquet. Regarding the administration of oral TXA, seven studies (18, 37, 38, 39, 40, 42, 43) administered it 2 h before surgery, and two studies (41, 44) administered it 1 h before surgery, with doses ranging from 1 to 2 grams. Seven studies compared single preoperative doses of oral TXA (18, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42), and two studies administered additional oral TXA postoperatively (43, 44). Among patients receiving oral or intravenous TXA, no additional local TXA was used. Detailed characteristics of the included studies are shown in Table 1.

Hb decrease

A total of seven studies reported on the decrease in Hb (37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43). Pathan et al.’s study found that the decrease in Hb was significantly higher in the oral TXA group compared to the intravenous group (41). The remaining six studies showed no difference between the two groups. The pooled results indicated no significant difference between oral and intravenous TXA in terms of Hb decrease in both RCTs (WMD: −0.02, 95% CI: −0.11 to 0.07, I2 = 0%, P = 0.690; Fig. 3) and non-RCTs (WMD: 0.23, 95% CI: −0.04 to 0.50, I2 = 59.7%, P = 0.095; Fig. 3).

Forest plot comparing the hemoglobin decrease between oral and intravenous tranexamic acid in patients undergoing TKA. Seven studies were included (37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42, 43). The results are presented as WMD with 95% CIs.

Blood loss

Four studies reported on blood loss (18, 38, 40, 42), with two studies (18, 40) reporting calculated blood loss, one study (42) reporting intraoperative blood loss, and one study (38) reporting total blood loss. DeFrancesco et al. (18) found that oral TXA significantly reduced calculated blood loss compared to intravenous TXA, while Hootsmans et al. (40) and others showed no difference between the two groups. Wang et al. (42) reported no difference in intraoperative blood loss between oral and intravenous TXA. Fillingham et al. (38) found that intravenous TXA significantly reduced total blood loss compared to oral TXA. Due to limited reports of blood loss in the included studies, it was not possible to pool data for this outcome.

Transfusion rate

Six studies (18, 38, 41, 42, 43, 44) reported on the transfusion rate, all of which showed no difference between the two groups. The pooled result indicated no difference in transfusion rates between oral and intravenous TXA after TKA in both RCTs (OR: 0.93, 95% CI: 0.50–1.71, I2 = 0%, P = 0.806; Fig. 4) and non-RCTs (OR: 1.24, 95% CI: 0.36–4.31, P = 0.731; Fig. 4).

Forest plot comparing the transfusion rate between oral and intravenous tranexamic acid in patients undergoing TKA. Six studies were included (18, 38, 41, 42, 43, 44). The results are presented as ORs with 95% CIs.

Complications

Five studies (18, 38, 41, 42, 43) reported on the occurrence of complications, with two studies (38, 41) having zero events and therefore excluded from the forest plot. Finally, a total of three RCTs were included in the forest plot. The pooled result indicated no significant difference in postoperative complications between oral and intravenous TXA (OR: 0.72, 95% CI: 0.30–1.73, I2 = 0%, P = 0.467; Fig. 5).

Forest plot comparing postoperative complications between oral and intravenous tranexamic acid in patients undergoing TKA. Three studies were included in the forest plot (18, 42, 43). The results are presented as ORs with 95% CIs.

Operation time

Five RCTs (18, 38, 40, 42, 44) compared operation time, with one study (38) showing that oral TXA significantly reduced operation time compared to intravenous TXA in TKA. The remaining four studies (18, 40, 42, 44) showed no difference between the two groups. The pooled result indicated no significant difference in surgical time between the two groups (WMD: −0.08, 95% CI: −4.40 to 4.23, I2 = 47.3%, P = 0.970; Fig. 6).

Forest plot comparing operation time between oral and intravenous tranexamic acid in patients undergoing TKA. Five studies were included (18, 38, 40, 42, 44). The results are presented as WMD with 95% CIs.

Length of hospital stay

Five studies (38, 40, 41, 42, 44) reported on length of hospital stay. The pooled result indicated no significant difference between oral and intravenous TXA in terms of hospital stay in RCTs (WMD: 0.04, 95% CI: −0.23 to 0.31, I2 = 0%, P = 0.751; Fig. 7) and non-RCTs (WMD: 0.18, 95% CI: −0.13 to 0.49, P = 0.261; Fig. 7).

Funnel plots comparing length of hospital stay between oral and intravenous tranexamic acid in patients undergoing TKA. Five studies were included (38, 40, 41, 42, 44). The results are presented as WMD with 95% CIs.

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Sensitivity analysis showed that excluding any single study did not significantly affect the results. Given that fewer than 10 trials were included, publication bias was not assessed using funnel plots.

Discussion

Our meta-analysis results indicate that there is no significant difference in the efficacy between oral and intravenous TXA administration, particularly regarding Hb decrease and transfusion rates, where no notable differences were observed. In addition, oral TXA did not lead to an increase in postoperative complication rates, and no significant differences were found between the two routes of administration in terms of operation time and length of hospital stay. Due to limited reports of blood loss in the included studies, it was not possible to pool data for this outcome. Future studies should include more relevant data for further validation.

Poursalehian et al. (21) conducted a network meta-analysis, which concluded that oral TXA, especially when combined with intra-articular administration, was significantly more effective in reducing blood loss and transfusion rates than other routes of TXA administration in patients undergoing TJA, with comparable safety to other routes of administration. Ling et al. also conducted a network meta-analysis, which demonstrated that all tranexamic acid regimens effectively reduced blood loss after TKA (45). The combination of intravenous infusion and oral administration provided the greatest reduction in blood loss without increasing the risk of deep vein thrombosis (45). However, it is important to note that these conclusions are primarily based on indirect comparisons, and network meta-analyses, due to their methodological nature, may introduce bias that could exaggerate the reliability of the results. Moreover, Sun et al.’s (23) study demonstrated that, compared to intravenous TXA, oral TXA has similar efficacy in reducing Hb decrease, total blood loss, transfusion rates, drainage volume, and length of hospital stay, without increasing the risk of calf intermuscular venous thrombosis or deep vein thrombosis in patients receiving total knee and hip arthroplasty. Ye et al.’s (24) meta-analysis also suggested that oral and intravenous TXA administration have the same efficacy in TJA patients. Notably, oral TXA offers more convenient access and administration, significantly reducing the workload for nurses, and may provide cost-effective benefits for the healthcare system. However, most existing studies focus on TJA patients, and there are physiological and surgical differences between TJA and TKA patients. For example, knee arthroplasty typically requires the use of a tourniquet, which may influence the efficacy of TXA and require adjustments to its dosage. Therefore, although our findings align with previous studies, this research focuses specifically on TKA patients, providing a more comprehensive and in-depth analysis of this patient population.

Yuan et al. found that all three routes of TXA administration (oral, intravenous, and local) significantly reduced postoperative Hb decrease, transfusion frequency, and transfusion costs after TKA (43). Oral TXA, however, was the least costly compared to the other two administration routes. In terms of safety, a meta-analysis showed that the use of TXA in TJA, regardless of administration route, does not increase the risk of venous thromboembolism (46). The results of our meta-analysis are consistent with previous studies. For TKA, oral TXA is as effective as intravenous TXA in terms of blood conservation and safety. The primary advantage of oral TXA lies in its lower cost compared to injectable forms and its ease of administration, aligning with the restrictive fluid strategies of enhanced recovery after surgery (47, 48). Kayupov et al. reported that the cost of a complete oral TXA dose (1.95 g, three tablets, administered preoperatively) was approximately $14, whereas the cost of an equivalent intravenous dose (1 g, administered preoperatively) ranged from $47 to $108, depending on the availability of a generic formulation (49). Wang et al. also reported a mean total cost per patient of $75 for oral TXA versus $581 for intravenous TXA (50). Therefore, oral TXA may represent a more cost-effective alternative to intravenous TXA, given its lower drug acquisition cost. However, as cost outcomes were not directly analyzed in this review, its potential to reduce overall healthcare costs requires further confirmation through dedicated cost-effectiveness analyses.

The common intravenous TXA dosage is approximately 10–20 mg/kg, which has been shown to maintain the minimum therapeutic level for about 3 h (51, 52). The most common oral TXA dosage is 2 g, administered 2 h before surgery (53, 54). Despite the slower and less complete absorption of oral TXA, with an absorption rate of 30–50% (55), studies have confirmed that perioperative oral TXA provides similar efficacy to intravenous TXA (53, 56, 57). The oral TXA dosage and timing are based on pharmacokinetic studies, which show that 2.0 g of oral TXA reaches its peak concentration about 2–3 h after administration, and its levels drop below the therapeutic threshold 6 h post-administration (31). For TKA patients, the surgery time usually falls within the duration of tourniquet use (approximately 1.5 h), so taking oral TXA 2 h before surgery ensures that blood drug concentrations remain within therapeutic range during the procedure and the early postoperative phase, thereby effectively preventing bleeding. This dosing schedule, which combines pharmacokinetic characteristics with surgical features, provides scientific support for the application of oral TXA in TKA.

Limitation

Our study has the following limitations. First, in the analysis of results, we only combined the data on Hb decrease, while specific blood loss outcomes (including intraoperative blood loss, postoperative drainage, and total blood loss) were not included in the meta-analysis due to the limited number of included studies. Second, the use of oral TXA varied across studies. Although most studies administered approximately 2 g of oral TXA 2 h before surgery (18, 37, 38, 39, 40, 42), some studies used a 1 g dose or administered it 1 h before surgery (41, 44), which may have increased the heterogeneity of the results. Similarly, the intravenous TXA dosage also varied across studies. Third, the study by Pathan et al. (41), which we included, involved patients undergoing revision surgery, potentially contributing to the heterogeneity of the results.

Conclusion

Based on the available evidence, no significant differences were observed between oral and intravenous TXA in patients undergoing TKA regarding efficacy and safety outcomes. However, given the current uncertainty and wide CIs, further high-quality studies are needed to confirm these findings.
