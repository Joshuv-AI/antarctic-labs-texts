Debonding mechanisms of fully grouted rock bolts driven by adhesive-ring cracking
Authors: Dongxu Liang, Nong Zhang, Feng Lin, Dongjiang Pan, Haoyu Rong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interface debonding is the primary failure mode of fully grouted rock bolts, yet the role of adhesive-ring cracking remains insufficiently understood. This study proposes a mechanics-based framework coupling bolt–adhesive interfacial shear with crack initiation and propagation, deriving stage-dependent load–displacement relations. We identify four evolutionary stages: initial elastic coupling, transient shearing hardening, hardening with radial crack growth, and softening after full-depth cracking. A criterion is established to determine the governing interface: debonding occurs at the bolt–adhesive interface if rock tensile strength surpasses that of the adhesive, whereas it shifts to the adhesive–rock interface (or rock detachment) if the adhesive is stronger. The predicted debonding propagation is qualitatively validated via laboratory pull-out tests and acoustic emission monitoring. Our findings demonstrate that adhesive tensile strength and bolt–borehole geometry govern stage-wise crack evolution and load-transfer behavior. The proposed framework provides valuable insights into the debonding mechanisms of fully grouted rock bolts, which could inform adhesive selection and the design of anchorage systems under specific conditions. Further empirical validation is needed to confirm its applicability in a wider range of geological and dynamic environments.

Introduction

Rock bolting is a widely used support method in coal mine roadways and other underground excavations. With increasing mining depth, anchorage systems are more frequently subjected to large deformation, strong disturbance, and complex stress conditions, which increase the possibility of support failure, as shown in Fig. 1. Among the various failure modes of fully grouted rock bolts, interface debonding is one of the most common in engineering practice. However, the underlying mechanism of adhesive-ring cracking and its role in debonding evolution remain insufficiently understood. Because cracking in the adhesive ring directly affects radial confinement, interfacial shear transfer, and the propagation of debonding, it should be treated explicitly in mechanical modeling.

Anchor failure and large deformation of the tunnel.

Research into the shear stress distribution and bond strength in rock anchors is crucial for the stability and safety of underground constructions. Studies on resin-grouted rock anchors have shown significant discrepancies between theoretical predictions and experimental results in softer materials such as limestone and clay1. Hyett and Bawden2 investigated the bond strength of fully-grouted cable bolts and proposed a frictional-dilatational model for failure analysis. Collectively, these studies highlight the urgent need to better understand the dynamics of anchor systems under various rock types and the debonding failure mechanisms under real mining conditions. Theoretical analyses are crucial for understanding the behavior of rock anchors, optimizing their design, and enhancing the safety of underground projects, which is a fundamental aspect of geotechnical and geological engineering.

Researchers like Li et al.3 and Yue et al.4 have conducted in-depth analyses of rock bolt behaviors, identifying significant gaps in understanding the complex interactions between bolt, grout, and rock under various loads and deformations. A theoretical model was proposed to simulate the coupling and decoupling behavior of the bolt/grout (B/G) interface in fully encapsulated rock bolts, validated through physical experiments. The findings reveal that the loading phases of the B/G interface are independent of the encapsulated length, and that an increased modulus of the grouting material leads to more brittle performance of the rock bolt5. Zhou et al.6 further advanced our understanding of bond-slip behavior through their studies on FRP (fiber-reinforced polymer) sheet-concrete interfaces. However, these studies reveal significant limitations in existing models, particularly for short joints, and underscore the need for models that can accurately predict bond behavior over all joint lengths.

Moreover, researchers7–10 have developed theoretical models for anchor pullout mechanisms and debonding, addressing the oversimplifications in previous studies. Numerical modeling and constitutive models for anchorage systems, as studied by Van Steen et al.11 and Chen et al.12, emphasize the need for further research on various rebar types, configurations, and geological conditions. While these contributions have enriched research on anchorage systems, they also highlight the ongoing gaps in knowledge, calling for more comprehensive studies to explore various anchorage systems and geological contexts.

Laboratory experiments offer precise control over environmental variables such as temperature, humidity, and stress, facilitating detailed analysis of the interactions between anchors or tendons, the surrounding media, and their mechanical properties. Unlike studies on damage and fracture in rock materials, anchorage research often overlooks interfaces composed of multiple media13–15. The exploration of anchoring interface debonding failure mechanisms is divided into critical thematic areas, reflecting the complex and multifaceted nature of this research topic. The first category includes studies on the mechanical behavior and various failure modes of anchoring systems. This includes research on fully bonded rock bolts in mudstone and bolt-reinforced rock joints, revealing insights into load distribution, stress adaptation, and mechanical responses under shear stress. Studies also explore different bolt types and their behaviors under various conditions, highlighting the complexity of bolted rock joint systems and the need for more research on effective reinforcement designs16,17.

The second category investigates the bond strength between FRP and concrete, examining factors influencing bond strength and debonding. Experimental and numerical studies in this field have supported existing bond strength models and offered new insights into crack propagation and stress transfer mechanisms. These findings underscore the importance of optimizing joint design and analyzing debonding behavior to improve the strength of reinforced beams18–20. A substantial portion of research focuses on the design and performance of anchorage systems, especially in terms of CFRP tendons and rock bolts. This involves both experimental and theoretical analyses of debonding failure mechanisms, the constitutive law of the bolt-grout interface under axial loads, and the mechanical properties of novel anchorage systems. These studies provide valuable insights into load-transfer mechanisms and load-carrying capacity, while also highlighting gaps in theoretical modeling and the need for improved anchorage technology21–24.

He et al.25 investigate the impact of rock hardness and rock mass integrity on the anchorage performance of mortar bolts in jointed rock. Through pull-out experiments, they identify the failure modes of jointed rock and bolt-grout composite structures, proposing calculation formulas for the ultimate pull-out load and displacement of mortar bolts under various conditions. Lastly, research into novel materials and techniques to enhance anchorage efficiency includes studies on using carbon fiber-reinforced polymer (CFRP) in construction and developing innovative anchorage solutions, such as additional rib (AR) anchorage. These studies demonstrate significant advancements in materials science, providing performance superior to traditional systems, and highlight the importance of design parameters in bond strength26,27.

Ueda et al.28 and Ana et al.29 have contributed significantly to clarifying the bonding properties of fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) sheets with concrete substrates, as well as exploring debonding phenomena in ground anchorages with fixed anchor lengths. These studies emphasize the need to understand the mechanics behind bond strength and debonding effects on load distribution and dynamic behavior. Ma et al.30 have focused on analyzing the mechanical behaviors of structures reinforced with methods ranging from rock bolts to FRP interactions with base materials. Their findings reveal the subtleties of reinforcement mechanisms, such as strain-softening at interfaces and load transfer processes, emphasizing the need for ongoing research into the resistance capabilities of various reinforcement materials. Sun et al.31 developed a new numerical model to analyze the tension-shear coupling fracture behavior of rock bolts, validated by experimental tests. The results indicate that the model accurately predicts rock bolt performance with a maximum shear displacement difference of approximately 2 mm during yielding and breaking. When applied to a coal mining roadway, the original support scheme failed under tensile-shear coupling but was effectively controlled using grouting anchorage.

In reviewing these contributions, it is evident that, despite significant progress in understanding debonding mechanisms, further empirical studies are required to validate existing models and theories. Moreover, research into non-linear material behaviors, three-dimensional peeling, and the durability of interfaces under varying environmental conditions remains a critical frontier in this field.

Theoretical and numerical simulations have led to the development of various load-displacement models for analyzing the debonding failure process in full-length anchoring systems. However, prevalent models often oversimplify the dynamic aspects of crack propagation within the anchoring agent ring and the concomitant coupled effects on interfacial debonding phenomena. A major deficiency lies in the lack of definitive criteria to discern the primary debonding mechanisms operating at distinct interfaces, namely the bolt-adhesive and adhesive-rock interfaces. Accordingly, the present study aims to formulate a detailed mechanical model that accounts for both the nucleation and propagation of cracks within the anchoring agent ring. The overarching goal is to explicate the ‘progressive propagation’ debonding mechanism inherent in full-length anchorage systems under a range of conditions, and subsequently propose appropriate criteria for interfacial failure. This study offers valuable insights for constructing high-quality anchorage systems. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. “Theoretical framework” presents the theoretical framework, including the interfacial load-transfer mechanism, the crack initiation and propagation model of the anchoring agent ring, and the debonding-control criterion with the corresponding piecewise analytical model. “Materials and methods” describes the materials, experimental setup, and loading scheme used in the pull-out tests. “Results” reports the experimental results and compares them with the theoretical analysis, with emphasis on load–displacement response, debonding evolution, and stress distribution characteristics. Finally, “Discussion” and “Conclusion” discuss the model applicability and limitations, and summarize the main conclusions of this study.

Theoretical framework

Interfacial load transfer and neutral-point evolution

Fig. 2Anchorage interface failure model.

Anchorage interface failure model.

In rock engineering, the primary types of anchor failure include rock bolt breakage, debonding at the rock bolt-adhesive interface, adhesive ring crushing, adhesive-rock interface debonding, and the dislodgment of the anchored rock mass. The most common failure mode is debonding at the rock bolt-adhesive interface, as illustrated in Fig. 2. This type of failure occurs without the breakage of the rod itself. The entire length of the anchored rod is bonded with adhesive or grouting material, which will henceforth be referred to as the “adhesive ring” .

The shear strength of the anchor interface consists of three key components: bonding, mechanical interlocking, and friction. Debonding failure at the anchor interface occurs due to incoherent deformation among the rock bolt, adhesive ring, and surrounding rock. Initially, the bonding component of shear strength is activated, followed sequentially by the mechanical interlocking and friction components. As the process progresses, the shear strength of the interface gradually decreases, ultimately leading to what is known as residual shear strength after partial strength loss. In grouted rock bolts, similar to rebar, all three components—bonding, mechanical interlocking, and friction—are present at the bolt interface. However, in fully frictionally engaged bolts, such as split-set bolts (Fig. 3a), only friction operates at the contact interface. Conversely, expansion bolts (Fig. 3b) rely on both mechanical interlocking and friction to provide shear strength. Although split-set bolts and expansion bolts are not the main focus of this study, they are briefly discussed here to provide a broader context for understanding the different mechanical anchoring systems used in rock bolting. The failure mechanisms and load-transfer characteristics of these bolt types offer insights that are relevant to the subsequent analysis of fully grouted rock bolts.

Split-set and expansion rock bolts. (a) split-set, (b) expansion.

Pull-out tests are widely utilized to evaluate the anchoring capacity of rock bolts. I.W. Farmer’s study1 on the mechanical behavior of rock bolts under pull-out loads demonstrated that axial and shear stresses at the bolt interface exponentially decrease from the loading point to the tail. Figure 4a shows the results of a typical pull-out test, where curve a illustrates the axial stress distribution of the rock bolt under a lighter load. At this load, compatible deformations on both sides of the interface suggest a complete bond between the rock bolt, the anchoring agent ring, and the surrounding rock. In contrast, curve b shows the axial force distribution under a heavier load, where partial debonding occurs at the interface. Figure 4b shows the axial stress distribution in rock bolts within underground mine tunnels. Unlike the pull-out test, the axial stress distribution around the borehole is uneven. However, stress variation near the tail-end of the bolt mirrors that observed in the pull-out test. These differences are due to the neutral point in the in-situ rock bolt, which divides it into free and anchored lengths, unlike the solely anchored length used in pull-out tests.

The rock bolt initially exhibits elastic behavior, as shown in Fig. 7, with shear stress distribution along the bolt. Near the tunnel wall, the rock bolt experiences negative shear stress directed towards the tunnel. At a certain distance from the tunnel surface, this stress becomes zero. Beyond this neutral point, the shear stress turns positive, extending towards the bolt’s far end. At this stage, components like shear strength, bonding, mechanical interlock, and friction become influential.

High confining pressure results in partial debonding at the anchoring agent-surrounding rock interface, leading to interface failure. As the rock bolt slides further, axial displacement increases, leading to more radial cracks in the anchoring agent ring. When the crack extends to the outer circumference of the cylindrical anchoring agent ring, it fractures through the entire thickness of the ring. According to formulas (20) to (23), the softening stage commences once the radial crack fully fractures. During the softening stage, the confining pressure at the rock bolt-anchoring agent interface is constant. The bonding force at the rock bolt-anchoring agent interface is lost, leaving only mechanical interlock and friction. As the rough angles decrease, the mechanical interlock fails quickly, leaving only friction, and the rock bolt’s load-bearing capacity decays with increasing sliding displacement. Since the rock bolt’s lateral ribs lack deep protrusions and are not designed for friction, they provide minimal frictional force. Materials and methods

Materials

A pull-out test was conducted in the laboratory to verify the cyclic diffusion-type debonding failure mechanism, as identified in the theoretical derivations. However, it is important to note that this method is limited to verifying the failure mechanism and cannot track the changes in confining pressure resulting from rock bolt sliding. The research focused on a full-length anchoring type rock block, and the pull-out experiment of the rock bolt anchoring specimen was combined with acoustic emission monitoring to analyze the debonding failure process of the anchoring interface.

Figure 17 illustrates the physical model used in the pull-out experiment. According to research by Che et al.33, boundary effects become negligible when the model width is nine times the rock bolt’s diameter. The anchoring system in the experiment had a full length of 400 mm, with a rock bolt diameter of 22 mm, and model dimensions of 200 mm × 200 mm × 400 mm. To monitor stress distribution, strain gauges were bonded to the rock bolt using anchoring glue. This adhesive was chosen over resin anchoring agents due to its similar bonding capacity and favorable usage conditions. The high-strength, injection-type anchoring glue gelled in 60 min at 20 °C and 40 min at 30 °C, with curing times of 90 and 80 min, respectively. Simulative rock materials were created using a mixture of cement, sand, and gypsum. These materials were selected because of their ability to mimic the properties of natural rock. Researchers often incorporate additional admixtures to meet specific requirements34,35. The simulative rock was cast using cement and sand as the matrix, with water-reducing and early-strength agents added to improve fluidity and early strength for easier mixing and quicker demolding. The mix ratio was 1:0.300:0.600:0.015:0.010, using C425 silicate cement and quartz sand with a particle diameter of 2 mm. The specimens were cured in a YH-40B standard constant temperature and humidity curing box for 28 days before undergoing uniaxial compression testing. The uniaxial compressive strength reached 54.30 MPa, and the elastic modulus was recorded at 3.90 GPa. Figure 18 shows the corresponding compressive strength curve.

Nine specimens were cast, and nine pull-out tests were performed. The mold was made from transparent acrylic plates to form a rectangular box. Waterproof glue was applied to the inner walls to prevent leakage, and hot melt glue was used to secure the outer walls. A mold release agent was applied to ease demolding. To remove air bubbles from the cast specimens, a vibrating rod was used for two minutes after mixing. Apparatus and loading scheme

The pull-out test system consisted of a pull-out force testing machine and an acoustic emission monitoring system. The pull-out device used an LW-1000 type horizontal tester, capable of exerting up to 1000 kN with a maximum error of 1%. It offers a stretch space of 1000 mm and a piston speed ranging from 0 to 100 mm/min. The acoustic emission monitoring system was based on a PCI-2 acoustic emission instrument, with a frequency range of 1 to 3 MHz. This system can display, collect, store, and replay multi-channel signals, recording hit counts, amplitudes, frequencies, and positioning points in real time. Figure 21 illustrates the equipment used in the pull-out test.

The horizontal pull-out tester was controlled by a computer system. Strain gauges were connected via wires to a dedicated recording computer, and acoustic emission sensors were attached to the specimen surface and linked to the PCI-2 acoustic emission host. Specimens were cleaned in pre-drilled holes using a brush, followed by the injection of anchor adhesive to fill three-quarters of the hole depth using a caulking gun. After inserting the rock bolt into the pre-drilled hole, any excess adhesive was removed. A fixing device was placed onto the rock bolt to ensure complete encapsulation with the adhesive, bonding securely to the surrounding rock. Specimens were left to cure at room temperature for three days before testing. The test employed a displacement-controlled loading technique at a rate of 2 mm/min until the rock bolt failed by pulling out.

Debonding and crack evolution along the anchorage

As illustrated in Fig. 25, the rock bolt is extracted, revealing tensile crack damage on the surface and longitudinal tensile spalling along the sides of the surrounding rock. These tensile cracks extend only partway to the bottom and do not fully penetrate the rock. Following the specimen’s failure, observations of the debonding interface, as shown in Fig. 25a, suggest that the failure resulted not from the complete extraction of the rock bolt but from the debonding at the rock bolt-adhesive interface due to adhesive fragmentation. The progression of acoustic emission positioning from the pull-out experiment is depicted in Fig. 26. The 5 mm difference between the rock bolt-adhesive interface and the adhesive-surrounding rock interface on the radial cross-section of the anchorage body precludes acoustic emission positioning from accurately determining whether fractures occur at the rock bolt-adhesive interface or at the adhesive-surrounding rock interface. Although fragmentation of the adhesive leading to debonding at the rock bolt-adhesive interface cannot be confirmed through radial cross-sections, the spatial pattern of acoustic emissions along the 400 mm axial anchoring length illustrates the progressive debonding process. Figure 26a shows that the initial acoustic emission points are located 125–150 mm from the loading end. With increasing pull-out load, as depicted in Fig. 26b, the acoustic emissions extend towards both ends of the loading point due to increased axial and shear stress on the rock bolt, which causes the debonding to expand bidirectionally. As the debonding progresses towards the loading end, the adhesive ring fractures and swells completely. This leads to increased confining pressure at the interface between the adhesive ring and surrounding rock, ultimately causing the rock to fail. With increasing pull-out load, the extension and density of acoustic emission positioning points along the axial direction both increase significantly, as illustrated in Fig. 26c. As the positioning points extend downward, both their number and density decrease with distance, suggesting a gradual reduction in anchorage debonding and surrounding rock damage. During the pull-out failure, depicted in Fig. 26d, there is a sudden increase in acoustic emission positioning points, although their downward extension does not continue. The rock bolt and the adhesive are concurrently extracted, aligning with the observations in the experimental photographs (Fig. 25).

Limitations of the theoretical model and future refinements

The theoretical model developed in this study offers a valuable framework for understanding the influence of cracking within the anchoring agent ring on the overall debonding process of full-length anchorages. The model’s ability to capture the sequential stages of failure, from initial elastic behavior through hardening due to crack initiation and propagation, to eventual softening and complete debonding, aligns qualitatively well with the experimental observations from pull-out tests. Specifically, the incorporation of confining pressure changes at the bolt-adhesive interface, triggered by the shear bulging effect and subsequent crack development in the adhesive, provides a more nuanced explanation for the observed load-displacement characteristics compared to models that neglect these phenomena. The concept of a “progressive propagation” debonding, characterized by the migration of the neutral point, is also well-supported by both the theoretical derivations and the progressive nature of acoustic emission (AE) localization observed experimentally.

However, certain limitations inherent in the current model and experimental setup warrant further discussion and suggest avenues for future research. A principal simplification within the theoretical framework is the assumption of a logarithmic relationship \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r_{\mathrm{e}}}={\mathrm{ln}}{u_z}{\mathrm{(}}{r_1}\le {r_{\mathrm{e}}} \le {r_2}{\mathrm{)}}$$\end{document} for the crack propagation rate within the anchoring agent ring. While this assumption facilitates analytical tractability and captures the general trend of decelerating crack growth as it approaches the outer boundary of the adhesive, its universal applicability, particularly under complex in-situ stress conditions or for different anchoring agent materials, remains to be rigorously validated. Real-world crack propagation is influenced by a multitude of factors, including local stress intensity, material heterogeneity, fracture toughness, and the presence of existing micro-defects, which are not explicitly accounted for in this simplified rate law. Consequently, discrepancies between the model’s predictions and actual field performance might arise, especially when extrapolating to scenarios involving high stress gradients, dynamic loading, or long-term creep effects where crack growth mechanisms can be significantly more intricate. Future work could explore incorporating more sophisticated fracture mechanics principles or empirical data-driven models to define the crack propagation rate with greater fidelity.

Furthermore, the experimental validation, while providing valuable qualitative insights, faced constraints in precisely differentiating the failure locus between the bolt-adhesive interface and the adhesive-rock interface using the current acoustic emission (AE) monitoring setup. As acknowledged, the 5 mm radial difference between these interfaces, relative to the inherent localization accuracy of the AE system, introduces ambiguity in pinpointing the exact origin of detected micro-fracturing events. Although the macroscopic failure mode observed post-test predominantly indicated debonding at the bolt-adhesive interface (consistent with the theoretical conditions where the anchoring agent’s tensile strength is the limiting factor), a more definitive, real-time distinction during the loading process would be beneficial. This limitation highlights the need for future experimental investigations employing higher-precision monitoring techniques. For instance, advanced AE systems with denser sensor arrays and improved localization algorithms, or the integration of distributed fiber optic sensing (DFOS) along the interfaces, could offer enhanced spatial resolution. Alternatively, meso-scale or micro-scale numerical simulations, perhaps using distinct element methods (DEM) or cohesive zone models (CZM) within a finite element framework, could provide a powerful complementary tool to meticulously explore the stress distribution and damage evolution at each interface, thereby helping to deconvolve the contributions of different failure mechanisms under various parametric conditions. Such numerical studies could also serve to calibrate and refine the simplified assumptions made in the analytical model, particularly regarding crack propagation and interfacial constitutive laws. Besides, the experiments were conducted using synthetic rock materials that simulate the mechanical properties of natural rocks. However, these materials cannot fully capture the heterogeneity, natural fractures, moisture effects, or complex stress environments of real-world geological conditions, limiting the generalizability of the findings to actual mining environments.

Additionally, the current study primarily focused on quasi-static loading conditions. The behavior of full-length anchorages under dynamic or cyclic loading, which is common in mining environments due to blasting or a live stress redistribution, could present different debonding characteristics and crack propagation dynamics. The “progressive propagation” debonding might be accelerated or exhibit path-dependent behavior under such conditions. Therefore, extending the theoretical model and experimental investigations to encompass these loading regimes would be a valuable contribution to the field.

In summary, while the proposed model represents a significant step towards a more comprehensive understanding of debonding in full-length anchorages by explicitly considering adhesive cracking, future refinements focusing on the crack propagation law and enhanced experimental validation techniques will further strengthen its predictive capabilities and broaden its applicability.

Conclusion

This study investigated the debonding failure mechanisms in full-length anchorage systems, focusing on crack propagation within the adhesive ring. Through the development of a refined mechanical analysis model and corroborating experimental validation, the following primary conclusions are drawn:A “progressive propagation” debonding mechanism, characterized by progressive neutral point transfer and distinct evolutionary stages (elastic adhesion, partial debonding with hardening, and complete debonding with softening), governs the bolt-adhesive interface failure. This is driven by the interplay of interfacial shear stresses and adhesive ring cracking.Adhesive ring crack propagation, critical to anchorage behavior, is significantly influenced by bolt-borehole geometry, adhesive/rock mechanical properties, and surrounding rock stress state.Crucially, a criterion for determining the primary debonding interface was established: debonding favors the bolt-adhesive interface if rock tensile strength surpasses adhesive tensile strength; conversely, it favors the adhesive-rock interface (or rock detachment) if adhesive tensile strength is greater. The adhesive’s tensile strength is thus a key determinant of initial failure locus.Adhesive ring integrity directly dictates anti-pull capacity, transitioning from adhesive action and mechanical interlocking in intact rings to primarily residual friction in fragmented rings.
