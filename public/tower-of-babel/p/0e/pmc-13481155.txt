Preparation and MR Imaging Study of a Recombinant Surface Antibody Superparamagnetic Iron Oxide Nanoparticle–EPC1 Molecular Probe for Hepatic Alveolar Echinococcosis
Authors: Weili Tian, Wenchao Ji, Wenya Liu, Peng Chen, Jun Li, Xueqi Sheng, Yaomiao Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo establish a new hepatic alveolar echinococcosis (HAE) model, construct an EPC1 antibody–conjugated superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticle (SPION) molecular probe and evaluate the probe’s efficacy in the targeted MRI of HAE.MethodsThirty C57 female mice (9–10 weeks; 17–22g) were used to establish an HAE model via hepatic portal vein infection. Twenty successfully HAE-modeled mice were randomly divided into a control group, which was given a simple SPION tracer, and an experimental group, which was given a complex SPION–EPC1 tracer. In addition, a blocking group (n = 5), in which mice received an excess dose of free EPC1 antibody prior to injection of the SPION–EPC1 tracer, was included to assess EPC1-dependent targeting, and a gadolinium–diethylene triamine pentaacetic acid (Gd-DTPA) group (n = 5), which received 0.1 mmol/kg of Gd-DTPA, was included to provide a reference clinical contrast agent. MRI was performed using an animal MRI scanner, and T1- and T2-weighted imaging sequences were utilized to assess lesion characteristics before and after tracer injection at 1 and 4h time points. Subsequently, T2 signal intensity, the lesion SNR, and the lesion-to-liver contrast-to-noise ratio were measured by 2 blinded physicians (>5 years experience) using ImageJ software. Comparisons were made between plain and post-injection scans at 1 and 4h. Independent sample t-tests and non-parametric tests were applied to compare signal intensities and SNR values.ResultsThe SPION–EPC1 probe significantly decreased T2 signal intensity and lesion SNR in the experimental group at both 1 (T2 intensity: –24.3%; SNR: –35.2%) and 4h (T2 intensity: –23.5%; SNR: –43.1%) compared with plain scans. By contrast, the control group showed only transient decreases, with no significant differences at 4h. In the EPC1-blocking group, the T2-signal decrease at 4h was markedly attenuated compared with that of the SPION–EPC1 group and comparable to that of the SPION group, supporting EPC1-dependent targeting.ConclusionThe SPION–EPC1 molecular probe demonstrated improved sensitivity and long-lasting effects in the MRI detection of HAE lesions, offering a promising tool for early diagnosis and the potential advancement of clinical practice in HAE imaging.

Introduction

Hepatic alveolar echinococcosis (HAE) is a chronic and severe parasitic disease caused by the Echinococcus multilocularis parasite, primarily affecting the liver. The disease is characterized by the formation of invasive, tumor-like lesions that can metastasize to other organs, including the lungs and brain. The pathogenesis of HAE involves the proliferation of parasitic vesicles that leads to tissue destruction and fibrosis.1 The disease is characterized by strong invasiveness, slow progression and a long course, often leading to severe complications if not diagnosed early. Patients in the early stages of the disease typically present with nonspecific symptoms, such as upper abdominal pain, fever and hepatomegaly, making early diagnosis challenging.2 The lack of specificity among current diagnostic methods, including ultrasound (US), CT, and MRI, for small lesions highlights the need for more sensitive imaging techniques.3,4 Imaging technology plays a key role in the treatment and efficient evaluation of HAE. With the development and application of molecular imaging, early and clear diagnosis of HAE lesions <1 cm in diameter is possible.5

Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) are widely used in biomedical applications due to their unique magnetic properties, biocompatibility, and ease of surface functionalization. These nanoparticles can be conjugated with antibodies, peptides, and other targeting ligands to achieve specific bindings that target tissues or cells. In MRI, SPION injected intravenously acts as a T2 contrast agent by shortening the transverse relaxation time of water protons, thereby enhancing image contrast. The conjugation of SPION with targeting ligands, such as antibodies, enables its precise delivery to specific tissues, making it a powerful tool for molecular imaging. Additionally, due to its passive accumulation in the liver, SPION has garnered increasing attention as a liver-specific T2 contrast agent.6 The recombinant surface antigen of echinococcosis, EPC1, is a serological diagnostic marker for echinococcosis. The cDNA sequence encoding EPC1 has a high amino acid sequence homology (as high as 86%) with that of Taenia solium (the pork tapeworm), which causes neurocysticercosis (NCC); however, in actual serological tests, only 9% of the sera from patients with T. solium-related NCC exhibit a cross-reaction with EPC1.7 The application of SPION in HAE imaging remains underexplored. Therefore, in this study, SPION was conjugated with the recombinant surface antibody EPC1 to develop a molecular probe for the targeted imaging of HAE lesions with the aim of improving the sensitivity and specificity of MRI in the detection and early diagnosis of HAE.8

Materials and Methods

Experimental materials

Thirty clean-grade C57 female mice (aged 9–10 weeks, weighing 17–22 g) were selected from the Xinjiang Medical University experimental animal center and treated with 20 nm of carboxylated Fe3O4 nanoparticles (Y001071; Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) dissolved in 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC). All experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Medical University (Ethics Approval No. 2023-0123). The mice were housed under standard conditions with free access to food and water.

Experimental methods

This study’s design is a prospective randomized controlled trial. Mice were randomly divided into control and experimental groups using a random number table. The inclusion criteria for the mouse model were based on successful hepatic portal vein infection and confirmed lesion formation via abdominal US. Exclusion criteria included any pre-existing liver conditions or failure to establish the HAE model.

Sample size was calculated based on previous studies using similar models. A power analysis was performed with an alpha level of 0.05 and a power of 0.8, indicating that a minimum of 10 mice per group was required to detect significant differences in T2 signal intensity.9

Preparation of a SPION–EPC1 complex tracer

An LMES buffer (P0745; Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology) solution with a concentration of 30 mmol/L was first prepared. The solution was then weighed, and its pH value was adjusted to 6.5 with Tris alkaline solution. An appropriate amount of water was then added to achieve the required concentration.

Carboxylated Fe3O4 nanoparticles with a concentration of approximately 1 mg/mL and a dosage of approximately 4 mL were extracted. Following ultrafiltration, the LMES solution was replaced at a dosage of 15 mM and with a pH value of 6.5. The solution was diluted to 4 mL, and approximately 400 μL of EPC1 antibody (the purity of the detection antibody was prepared using the SDS-PAGE [sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis] method) solution with a concentration of 1 mg/mL was added. After thorough vortex mixing, the resultant solution was shaken and adsorbed at 37℃ for 1h. Then, 40 μL of EDC at a concentration of 10 mg/mL was added to activate the carboxyl groups. The solution was shaken thoroughly at 37℃ and stored overnight and then separated using a magnetic separation column. The clear liquid produced was collected, and the absorption light testing method (Oriel 77700 Multi-spec 257) was used for the test. The actual coupling rate of EPC1 antibody was calculated (coupling efficiency = (optical density [OD]0 – OD1)/OD2 × 100%; wavelength = A260/A280 nm), and the antibody magnetic beads were finally dispersed in a solution of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 0.1% triton X-100 (P0222; Shanghai Beyotime Biotechnology) containing 1% bovine serum albumin at a dose of 15 mM. The solution was finally diluted to 4 mL.

Establishment of a HAE mouse model

A total of 30 mice were included in this study. Of these, 20 mice were successfully modeled, and the remaining 10 were euthanized. After successful induction of the animal model,10 20 mice were randomly divided into a control group and an experimental group using a random number table, with 10 mice in each group. In addition, a blocking group (n = 5), in which mice received an intravenous injection of excess free EPC1 antibody (100 μg) 15 mins prior to injection of SPION–EPC1, was included to assess EPC1-dependent targeting, and a gadolinium–diethylene triamine pentaacetic acid (Gd-DTPA) group (n = 5), which received 0.1 mmol/kg of Gd-DTPA, was included to provide a reference clinical contrast agent. The randomization process ensured that both groups were comparable in age, weight, and lesion size. Due to SPION’s unique magnetic properties, biocompatibility and ease of surface functionalization, it is widely used in biomedical applications. When used as a contrast agent in MRI, SPION shortens the transverse relaxation time of water particles, thereby enhancing image contrast. MRI shows HAE lesions. The control group received a simple SPION tracer, whereas the experimental group received the complex SPION–EPC1 (SPION conjugated with EPC1) tracer.

During the experiment, the mice were required to fast for 12h before the operation and were deprived of water for 1h before anesthesia. After isoflurane anesthesia was administered using a special anesthesia machine for mice, the mice were fixed on the operating plate, their abdomens were disinfected with medical iodine and an incision was made along the midline to expose the hepatic portal vein. Alveolar cysticercosis was induced through the injection of Echinococcus granulosus originating from a laboratory culture. A 200–300 μL suspension of procariae was injected through hepatic portal vein puncture to prevent a large amount of fluid from entering the circulatory system of the mice in a short time and causing acute heart failure. After hemostasis, the abdomens were sutured. The mice were placed on a heating table for resuscitation and then continued to be raised in separate cages. Their general condition was closely observed. Nine weeks after infection with E. granulosus, abdominal US examinations were performed on the mice to observe the formation of liver lesions.

Liver tissue sections were taken and stained with hematoxylin solution for 10 mins, then rinsed with tap water and washed once with distilled water. The sections were then stained with eosin solution for 3 mins, and the excess dye was rinsed off with tap water. Finally, the sections were washed once with distilled water, a neutral resin was applied for sealing and microscopic examination was conducted. The cell nuclei appeared blue, and the cytoplasm was red or pink.

SPION and SPION–EPC1 complex tracer in mouse MRI

Using a 7T German Bruker BioSpin (Rheinstetten, Germany) animal MRI scanner (model: BioSpec70/20USR) and a mouse body coil for scanning, the successfully anesthetized mice were fixed in a supine position inside a small-animal, high-field MRI device. The liver surfaces of the mice were projected close to the middle position of the coil, and the height of the coil was adjusted to ensure that the abdomens were at the center of the magnetic field. Simultaneously, the respiratory monitoring computer was observed. If a mouse’s respiratory rate fell below 60 breaths/min, the experiment could begin. The parameters of the MR scanner were adjusted, and the described conditions were adjusted to the optimal state. Before formal scanning began, a pre-scan was required to screen for the best scanning conditions, and the scanning sequence was set. Before injecting the contrast agent, axial T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) and T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) sequence MR scans of the mouse livers were performed to observe the locations of liver lesions and the signal characteristics of different sequences. For T1WI, RARE (rapid acquisition with refocused echoes] sequences were used (TR: 1500 ms, TE: 8 ms, flip angle: 90°, FOV: 32 × 32 mm, matrix: 256 × 256, slice thickness: 0.8 mm and number of slices: 32), whereas for T2WI, the TurboRARE sequence was adopted (TR: 3500 ms, TE: 33 ms and flip angle: 90°). Axial T2WI MR scans of the mouse livers were performed at 2 time points (1 and 4h) after tracer injection. After the scans were completed, the mice were placed back in their cage for continued feeding. All original images obtained from the scan were copied, and 2 physicians with >5 years of experience in imaging conducted image processing using ImageJ software. The SNR is calculated as the ratio of the signal intensity of a lesion to the signal intensity of the background. The contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) was calculated as follows: CNR = (SI_lesion − SI_liver)/SD_noise, where SI represents signal intensity and SD represents standard deviation. The T2 signal intensities of the HAE lesions were measured, and the SNR values of the lesions (lesion SNR = lesion SI/background SI) were calculated. The average between the 2 values measured by the physicians was selected as the final evaluation result. The T2 SIs and lesion SNR and CNR values of the control and experimental groups at 1 and 4h after tracer injection were compared with each other and with the plain scans (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Preparation of the fluorescein isothiocyanate–EPC1 fluorescent probe

Next, 500 mg of antibody was dispersed in 1.5 mL of 0.1 monochlorobimane (pH 9.0) to prepare a 0.25 mg/mL solution. Then, 10 mL of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC; 10 mg/mL, dissolved in N,N-dimethylformamide; F106837-50 mg, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China) were added, and the mixture was shaken on a 37°C shaker for 2h. Pure water and a 30 KD ultrafiltration tube were used to ultrafilter the supernatant until it became colorless. Finally, the FITC-labeled EPC1 antibody was diluted to 500 μL (1 mg/mL) with 0.02 M PBS. The stage of the in vivo fluorescence imaging system was cleaned with an alcohol cotton ball. The parameters of the fluorescence instrument were set according to the maximum excitation wavelength (488 nm) and maximum emission wavelength (525 nm). The fluorescent drugs (FITC and FITC–EPC1) were slowly injected into the tail veins of the mice at a dose of 100 μL per mouse. The experimental mice were weighed and anesthetized with 7% chloral hydrate (0.5 mL/100 g) intraperitoneally, and after satisfactory anesthetization, their abdomens were opened at the designated time points to remove their livers. The excess blood was wiped off, and in vivo fluorescence photos were taken for detection 30 and 60 mins after administration.

Ex vivo fluorescence imaging to corroborate MRI findings

In a subset of mice that underwent MRI scanning (n = 3 from the SPION–EPC1 group), ex vivo fluorescence imaging was performed immediately after the 4h MRI scan to enable correlation between the MRI and fluorescence findings in the same animals. Fluorescence intensity was quantified by measuring the mean fluorescence signal within ROIs placed over HAE lesions and adjacent normal liver tissue. The lesion-to-liver fluorescence ratio was calculated for each animal.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 statistical software. Measurement data conforming to a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and analysis was conducted mainly with an independent sample t-test. A one-way analysis of variance was used for analysis among multiple groups, and Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for post hoc testing. When the data did not conform to a normal distribution, with median and interquartile spacing, analysis was conducted using a non-parametric test, with statistical significance established at P < 0.05.

Results

SPION–EPC1 coupling results

Table 1 details the process and results of the binding of the recombinant surface antibody EPC1 to SPION. In the experiment, the concentration of the original EPC1 antibody was 1.228 mg/mL, the input amount was 400 µL, and the calculated total amount of EPC1 was 0.491 mg. After the reaction was completed, no EPC1 antibody was detected in the supernatant, indicating that all antibodies were successfully adsorbed onto the SPION surface. The binding rate reached 100%, indicating that the binding of antibodies to nanoparticles was highly efficient. Furthermore, the adsorption capacity of the magnetic microbeads for the EPC1 antibody was 122.8 µg/mg, indicating that SPION has a good surface adsorption capacity and can effectively load the antibody. These results provide a solid foundation for subsequent MRI experiments, ensure the preparation quality of the SPION–EPC1 composite probe, and lay a reliable foundation for targeted imaging applications.

Size and potential of the SPION and SPION–EPC1

Carboxylated Fe3O4 has a Z-average size of 49.26 nm, with an intensity and number size of 61.63 and 30.09 nm, respectively, and a polydispersion coefficient of 0.23 (Fig. 1a, b). The hydrodynamic size of carboxylated Fe3O4 nanoconjugated to the EPC1 antibody has a Z-average of 54.53 nm, an intensity of 51.04 nm, a number size of 32.47 and a polydispersion coefficient of 0.29 (Fig, 1c, d). Moreover, Z-average values between Fe3O4 and SPION–EPC1 showed significant differences (P < 0.05) (Table 2), with values increasing after coupling; however, the number value comparison showed no difference (P > 0.05). Polydispersion coefficients differed significantly (P < 0.05), increasing post-coupling. The zeta potential of carboxylated Fe3O4 nanoparticles was –24.91 mV, whereas that of the conjugated nanoparticles was –11.96 mV (Fig. 1e, f) (Table 3). Carboxyl group consumption enabled antibody conjugation, resulting in a positive shift in zeta potential, and the SPION–EPC1 nanoparticles exhibited a more regular nanoscale quadrilateral morphology (Fig. 1g, h) and had uniform particle size distribution, good dispersion and no obvious aggregation through the use of denoising filters (Fig. 2).

Establishment of a HAE mouse model

A total of 30 mice were selected to establish an HAE model in this study. Twenty successfully modeled mice were screened using abdominal US (Fig. 3a, b). Within the model group, clear lesions could be observed on the liver. The echoes within the lesions were uneven, the boundaries were unclear, the shapes were irregular, and there were variously sized and differently shaped strong echoes mixed in among them. In addition, this study conducted hematoxylin and eosin staining on the livers of normal mice and of mice in the model group, which showed that the liver tissue structure of mice in the control group was normal, with intact liver lobules, clear liver cell boundaries, orderly arrangement and no inflammatory cell infiltration. In the model group, the fibrous capsule wall of the echinococcal cysts in the mice was enlarged, and there was a large degree of fibroblast proliferation and inflammatory cell infiltration around the cyst wall. The liver structure was damaged, and the liver lobule structure could not be distinguished. A significant inflammatory cell reaction zone was formed between the liver cells and the cyst wall of the echinococcal cyst (Fig. 3c, d). The successfully modeled mice were randomly divided into a control group and an experimental group using a random number table, with 10 mice in each group.

MRI detection of differences in HAE lesions between experimental and control group mice

To obtain accurate fiber bundle images when performing diffusion-weighted imaging of the liver and skeletal muscles, an image SNR of at least 30 is recommended. At 1h after tracer injection, the T2 signal intensity and SNR value of HAE lesions in the control group decreased compared with the plain scan, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05). However, at 4h, the T2 signal intensity and SNR value of the HAE lesions increased, and there was no statistical difference compared with the plain scan (P > 0.05) (Table 4). At 1 and 4h after tracer injection, the T2 signal intensity and SNR value of the HAE lesions in the experimental group were lower than in the plain scan, and the differences were statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Table 4, Figs. 4 and 5).

Lesion-to-liver CNR increased from 2.31 ± 0.42 at baseline to 4.87 ± 0.68 at 1h and remained elevated at 4h (5.12 ± 0.73) in the SPION–EPC1 group, whereas CNR in the SPION group increased to 3.56 ± 0.54 at 1h but returned to near-baseline levels at 4h (2.68 ± 0.51) (Fig. 6). This dynamic pattern indicates that the SPION–EPC1 probe provides sustained enhancement of lesion contrast compared with unconjugated SPION.

EPC1-blocking experiment and comparison with Gd-DTPA

In the EPC1-blocking group, the T2 signal decrease at 4h was markedly attenuated compared with that of the SPION–EPC1 group (T2 intensity reduction: −8.7% vs −23.5%) and was comparable to that of the SPION group (−6.2%), supporting EPC1-dependent targeting (Fig. 7a). This result indicates that the retention of SPION–EPC1 in HAE lesions is at least partially EPC1 specific. Compared with Gd-DTPA, SPION–EPC1 produced a higher and more persistent lesion-to-liver CNR at 4h post-injection (5.12 ± 0.73 vs 2.14 ± 0.38, P < 0.05, Fig. 7b), suggesting potential advantages for the delayed imaging of HAE lesions.

Fluorescence imaging verifies the targeting specificity of EPC1 antigens in HAE lesions

The experimental group was administered the FITC–EPC1 complex tracer, whereas the control group received the simple FITC tracer. The imaging effects between the 2 groups were observed, and differences in alveolar echinococcosis were compared at 2 time points (30 and 60 min) after the injection of the tracer. The lesion imaging intensities in the experimental group were significantly higher than those in the control group at the 2 time points. Quantitative analysis revealed that the lesion-to-liver fluorescence ratio was 3.42 ± 0.56 in the FITC–EPC1 group compared with 1.28 ± 0.31 in the FITC control group at 60 mins (P < 0.001). This indicates that the FITC–EPC1 complex tracer can be combined with the recombinant surface antigen of hepatic echinococcosis, EPC1, to achieve targeted imaging, making lesion imaging more concentrated, improving the imaging effect and making smaller lesions easier to visualize (Fig. 8).

In a subset of mice that underwent both MRI and ex vivo fluorescence imaging, lesion-to-liver CNR on T2-weighted MRI at 4h correlated positively with ex vivo fluorescence intensity (r = 0.87, P = 0.024), supporting concordance between MRI and EPC1-targeted fluorescence signals (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Alveolar echinococcosis is a chronic, severe and potentially life-threatening parasitic disease caused by E. multilocularis. Infection with E. multilocularis is relatively widespread, mainly distributed in the Northern Hemisphere, including the United States and Canada and the central and northern parts of Eurasia (including China).11 The liver is the most commonly affected area, but parasites may spread from the liver to other organs such as the lungs and brain. Clinical symptoms include upper abdominal pain, fever, jaundice, and liver enlargement.12,13

MRI has the advantages of being non-invasive and free of ionizing radiation and has been widely used in medical diagnosis. Compared with other clinical imaging techniques, MRI demonstrates high imaging flexibility and spatial resolution as well as effective soft tissue contrast and can produce relevant information regarding blood circulation and blood vessels. However, its main disadvantage is its low sensitivity.14,15 In recent decades, several types of contrast agents have been developed to improve the sensitivity of MRI and enhance the information conveyed in MRI images, namely through the use of nano- and magnetic particles.16,17 Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles were developed and approved as a possible alternative to Gd contrast agents because of their unique advantages, which include good biocompatibility, easy metabolism, a high saturation magnetic moment (controllable by adjusting particle size and composition) and easy surface functionalization.18,19 Currently, SPION is widely referred to as a T2 contrast agent in clinical practice owing to its ability to shorten the T2 relaxation time20 and has received increasing attention and research focus as a liver-specific T2 contrast agent due to its passive accumulation in the liver.21

Compared with traditional materials, nanomaterials have many unique physical and chemical properties. These special properties have enabled nanoparticles to become widely used, studied and valued in biomedicine. Compared with other magnetic materials, SPION has been most extensively studied for its biomedical applications. The degree of atomic order, or crystallinity, in its iron oxide lattice, as well as the dispersion in the size and shape of its nanoparticles, affect its performance in certain applications. Among oxides and pure metals, iron oxide has better biocompatibility. Therefore, the Fe3O4 selected for this study belongs to SPION. Polyethylene glycol–modified Fe3O4 was chosen and coupled with the recombinant surface antibody of echinococcosis, EPC1, to improve magnetic field stability and achieve the targeted imaging of antigen–antibody binding for HAE.

A total of 30 C57 mice were included in the experiment. A mouse model of HAE was established by hepatic portal vein infection, and the formation of liver lesions was detected by abdominal US. Twenty mice were found to have been successfully modeled using this method. The success rate of establishing a mouse model using this method is relatively high and provides a reliable basis for experimental modeling.20 This study used SPION coupled with EPC1 to obtain a SPION–EPC1 complex tracer. The results show that after injection with the SPION–EPC1 complex tracer, the T2 signal intensity and SNR value of the lesions in mice in the experimental group decreased compared with plain scans at both the 1 and 4h time points. After injection with SPION, the T2 signal intensity and SNR value of the lesions in mice in the control group at the 1h time point decreased compared with plain scans. Compared with the 1h time point, the same values were increased in the control group at the 4h time point, and there was no statistically significant difference between the scans of lesions in the control group and plain scans at the 4h time point. Thus, the SPION probe exhibited a similar effect to that of SPION–EPC1 after 1h, indicating that SPION–EPC1 may not exert its effect until 1h after injection. This could provide guidance for clinical applications. After the injection of the tracer, although the signal intensity of lesions in the experimental and control groups changed, their duration was not the same. This indicates that the SPION–EPC1 composite tracer can target the recombinant surface antigen EPC1 of HAE, thereby shortening the T2 relaxation time and making liver lesions easier to detect. The longer duration can provide a reliable basis for early clinical diagnosis and experimental research. The reduced enhancement observed in the EPC1-blocking group further indicates that the retention of SPION–EPC1 in HAE lesions is EPC1 specific rather than due to nonspecific accumulation. The positive correlation between the MRI CNR and ex vivo fluorescence intensity in the same animals strengthens the validity of using MRI as a non-invasive method to assess probe accumulation. Compared with Gd-DTPA, SPION–EPC1 demonstrated superior and more persistent lesion enhancement at 4h, suggesting potential advantages for delayed imaging protocols that may improve lesion detection in clinical practice.

This study has certain limitations. First, the number of mice included in the experiment was relatively small; thus, the sample size should be increased to make the results more robust. Second, the MRI scan time was relatively short; rather than injecting the SPION–EPC1 composite probe and immediately conducting the scan, the scan time and frequency should be increased to observe the subsequent development of lesions and the retention time of the probe in the body. Third, although we included a Gd-DTPA comparison group, comprehensive pharmacokinetic studies including additional time points (e.g. 0.5, 2, 8, and 24h) are needed to fully characterize the biodistribution and clearance profiles of SPION–EPC1. At present, there are no reports on the in vivo pharmacokinetics of SPION–EPC1, and we will consider focusing on this aspect in future research.

This study successfully prepared an EPC1 recombinant surface antibody and SPION molecular probe and explored its application in MRI for HAE. In addition to US, other imaging examination methods can be used when evaluating echinococcosis lesions, including CT, MRI, and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography. These examination methods are all helpful in distinguishing active from inactive lesions by evaluating the metabolic activities around echinococcosis.22 The results show certain advantages and potential. The EPC1 recombinant surface antibody was obtained through genetic engineering technology and accurately coupled with SPION. The innovation of this method provides reference for the development of similar probes in the future. Compared with previous studies using conventional contrast agents, the SPION–EPC1 probe demonstrates superior targeting ability and longer-lasting imaging effects. Although other studies have explored the use of SPION in liver imaging, the application of EPC1 as a targeting ligand for HAE is novel. The results of this study align with previous findings on the efficacy of SPION in enhancing MRI sensitivity, but the specific targeting of HAE lesions with EPC1 represents a considerable advancement in the field.23,24 In terms of MRI, T2WI is particularly prominent and produces clear delineations of lesion boundaries and delicate internal structures, including a high differentiation between necrotic and calcified areas and active lesions. This is superior to conventional contrast agent imaging and is due to the unique magnetic properties of the probe, which aggregates at the lesion site to alter the local magnetic field and amplify imaging signal differences.25,26

Conclusion

In summary, SPION can bind with the recombinant surface antigen of HAE, EPC1, to create a complex tracer, SPION–EPC1, that achieves targeted imaging, thereby shortening T2 relaxation time and making intrahepatic lesions easier to detect. This molecular probe has many advantages, including a good imaging effect and a long duration, and provides a reliable basis for early clinical diagnosis in the future.
