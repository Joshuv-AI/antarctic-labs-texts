Subgroups With Metabolic Syndrome Show Different Association With Ischemic Heart Disease and Stroke; Findings of Iran STEPS Survey 2021
Authors: Sogol Koolaji, Sina Azadnajafabad, Moein Yoosefi, Naser Ahmadi, Ali Golestani, Erfan Ghasemi, Arezou Dilmaghani‐Marand, Negar Rezaei, Ameneh Kazemi, Nazila Rezaei, Yosef Farzi, Kamyar Rezaee, Arefeh Alipour Derouei, Maryam Nasserinejad, Elham Abdolhamidi, Mohammad‐Mahdi Rashidi, Rosa Haghshenas, Azadeh Momen Nia Rankohi, Shirin Djalalinia, Farideh Razi, Bagher Larijani, Farshad Farzdafar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMetabolic syndrome (MetS) is defined as clustering of 3 or more of 5 metabolic risk factors in an individual. It has been greatly using in clinical practice; although some research illustrated that it might not act as a homogenous disorder in association with cardiovascular diseases. Therefore, we aimed to investigate its prevalence and association with ischemic heart disease (IHD) and stroke.MethodsUsing Iran STEPS (STEPwise Approach to NCD [Noncommunicable Diseases] Risk Factor Surveillance) Survey 2021, the prevalence and association of MetS, with prevalence of IHD and stroke was assessed. Given the definition of MetS, it includes 16 combinations (clusters) of 5 risk factors; we aggregated them into 6 groups and evaluated their association with prevalence of IHD and stroke.ResultsOverall, 54.19% of total population had MetS, where the most prevalent criterion was elevated waist circumference. MetS was associated with 1.70 times greater adjusted odds ratio (OR) of IHD prevalence (95% CI, 1.40–2.07). A closer look reveals that the greatest OR of IHD prevalence was observed in individuals with all 5 risk factors or 4 risk factors including both elevated blood pressure and fasting plasma glucose with ORs of 6.69 (3.52–12.68) and 6.38 (3.38–12.03), respectively, compared with individuals with none of the risk factors (0 components).ConclusionsThe association of MetS cluster with IHD and stroke prevalence varied. In addition to the dose–response relationship, the clusters characterized by both elevated BP and FPG are the most critical for targeting preventive and therapeutic measures.Keywords: ischemic heart disease, metabolic syndrome, metabolic syndrome components, strokeSubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease, Epidemiology, Obesity, Risk Factors, Primary Prevention

Nowadays, metabolic syndrome (MetS) is widely recognized in research and clinical practices with a general agreement upon its association with cardiovascular disease (CVD), the global leading cause of mortality. 1 MetS is a combination of at least 3 of 5 risk factors including elevated waist circumference (WC), low level of high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and elevated fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglyceride, and blood pressure (BP). 2 Therefore, in line with the growing prevalence of diabetes, obesity, and hypertension, its prevalence is also raising and attracting more global attention. 2 , 3 , 4

Recent data indicate that the global prevalence of MetS ranges from 12.5% to 31.4%, depending on the definitions and criteria used. 5 However, the prevalence of MetS and its components remains high worldwide, with alarming rates observed in certain regions and populations. 5 Pooled estimates from studies suggest that MetS increases the risk of CVDs nearly 2‐fold and the risk of all‐cause mortality by approximately 1.5‐fold, highlighting the significance of the rising trends in MetS epidemiology. 1 , 6 Furthermore, evidence shows that each component of MetS is independently associated with an elevated risk of CVD to some extent, underscoring the growing prevalence and disease burden attributable to MetS. 7 The pathophysiology of MetS involves core features such as insulin resistance, visceral adiposity, dyslipidemia, and endothelial dysfunction, with endothelial damage serving as the common pathway linking these factors to increased CVD risk, particularly when elevated FPG and BP coexist. 8 , 9

A closer look reveals that the definition includes different clusters of the risk factors; for instance, a person with elevated WC, low HDL‐C and high FPG is considered to have MetS just as one with elevated WC, low HDL‐C, high FPG, high TG, and high BP. Given that each of the clusters can potentially increase the risk of CVD considering this prominent syndrome as 1 homogenous disorder might be controversial 10 ; some studies illustrated a difference in the risk of ischemic heart disease (IHD) and stroke incidence in some clusters. 11 , 12 Accordingly, we decided to investigate the association of different MetS clusters association with IHD and stroke events. Furthermore, Iran is located in the second rank of global age‐standardized IHD incidence rate based on the Global Burden of Diseases study in 2019 and is experiencing an upward trend of obesity, diabetes, and hypertension prevalence in recent decades, 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 which emphasizes the importance of focusing preventive and therapeutic strategies on the most significant MetS clusters.

To better evaluate the noncommunicable diseases (NCD) risk factors such as MetS, the World Health Organization STEPS (STEPwise Approach to NCD Risk Factor Surveillance) framework has been developed previously. 18 The eighth round of the STEPS survey in Iran was conducted in 2021, which provided national representative data on the NCD risk factors prevalence that could be applicable in other regions around the world. 19 We applied the Iran STEPS Survey 2021 data in this study, which is the first one to investigate the national and subnational prevalence of MetS and its clusters in Iran in 2021 to the best of our knowledge.

METHODS

Due to the nature of the data collected during the STEPS survey, requests to access the data set from qualified researchers trained in human subject confidentiality protocols may be sent to National Institute of Health Research at Tehran University of Medical Sciences.

Study Design and Participants

The protocol of the 8 cross‐sectional STEPS study was published elsewhere. 19 As a summary, a systematic cluster random sampling frame which was proportional to the population of each province, was applied, resulting in gathering 3176 clusters from rural and urban areas of 31 provinces. Iranian adults >18 years of age were eligible for enrolment in the survey except pregnant women and individuals with severe mental or physical illness preventing them from answering the questionnaire or providing a blood sample. Individuals who agreed to participate and signed the informed consent were interviewed through a systematic questionnaire and underwent physical measurements (anthropometry) and blood and urine sampling (limited to the participants ≥25 years). Because MetS is consisted of laboratory components, in the current study we analyzed only the data from individuals ≥25 years of age due to the World Health Organization STEPS framework criteria, which necessitate laboratory workup in the population aged ≥25. 19 , 20

The information was collected via the following 3 steps. In the first step, a validated version of the latest standard World Health Organization questionnaire for STEPS (version 3.2) was applied to gather demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle (such as smoking, physical activity, and alcohol consumption), and NCD risk factors such as history of CVD, stroke, diabetes, and hypertension. 19 In the second step, anthropometric variables like weight, height, and BP were measured based on the protocol of STEPS manual. 19 A standard tape was used to measure the WC as halfway between the edge of the lower ribs and the hip, almost crossing the umbilicus. Systolic and diastolic BP were measured by a standard Beurer sphygmomanometer 3 times at a time interval of 15 minutes in sitting position, based on the World Health Organization STEPS guidelines. 19 The mean of the second and third reading was assumed as the systolic and diastolic BP. 19 The third step was obtaining biological samples of blood and urine after overnight fasting, which was analyzed for FPG, HDL‐C, triglyceride, total cholesterol, and other serum biomarkers listed in the study protocol 19 ; however, only the mentioned MetS‐related biomarkers were used in the current study.

Definition of MetS and Clusters

MetS was defined based on the Joint Interim Statement (JIS) published in 2009 in which the WC criteria for the Middle East and Mediterranean region was provided by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF). 2 , 21 Therefore, individuals with >3 of the following components were considered to have MetS: (1) WC ≥94 cm in men or ≥80 cm in women, (2) FPG ≥100 mg/dL or receiving pharmacological therapy for diabetes, (3) HDL‐C<40 mg/dL in men or <50 in women (low HDL‐C), (4) triglyceride ≥150 mg/dL (elevated triglyceride); and (5) mean systolic BP≥130 mm Hg or mean diastolic BP≥85 mm Hg or receiving pharmacological therapy for hypertension (elevated BP). 2

Accordingly, MetS consists of 16 different combinations of these components, or 16 clusters. We categorized these clusters into 6 groups based on the clinical importance of components similar to a previous study. 12 The main idea behind these combinations was presence of elevated BP or elevated FPG and numbers of components. In this order, the first group was a cluster of 3 components not including elevated BP and FPG (high triglyceride+low HDL+high WC), and the sixth group consisted of the cluster with all 5 components (high triglyceride+low HDL+high WC+high BP+high FPG). The full details are described in Figure 1. In this order, we also managed the low frequency of some clusters.

Definition of Other Variables

IHD is defined as any previous events of ACS including unstable angina, non‐ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction, and ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction in the past that was ever told to the patient by a physician or health care provider. The exact translation of the question that was asked of the patients was “Has a doctor or health care professional ever told you that you have had a heart attack or angina? And have you undergone medical procedures such as bypass surgery (coronary artery replacement), angioplasty (balloon), or stenting?” Therefore, it included all types of acute and chronic angina (stable angina, unstable angina, non‐ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction and ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction). Stroke was also defined as any previous episode of stroke told to the patient by a physician or health care provider.

Alcohol drinking was measured using a standard scale of 1 unit of each alcoholic drink through self‐reported data, which were categorized into different levels of alcohol drinking. Heavy drinkers were assessed in this study, defined as drinking >6 normal glasses of wine or beer or 6 small glasses of vodka, tequila, and alcoholic beverage in 1 drinking episode during the past 12 months. Current cigarette smoking was defined as current daily use of cigarette. Total cholesterol reported as mean and 2 groups of < and >240 mg/dL. Low physical activity is defined as physical activity <600 metabolic equivalent of task, which was defined in detail elsewhere. 22 The questionnaire was filled out by the survey executive member based on patient‐reported data. 19 The data were collected by trained surveyors who were not physicians but were experienced allied health professionals working within the regional health care system.

Statistical Analysis

Data were gathered through electronic questionnaires and after cleaning and curation of data by 2 independent biostatisticians, the R version 4.0.2 was used for analysis. Afterward, to prepare more valid and reliable results, a 4‐step process of data weighting was applied. 19 P value <0.05 was considered as statistically significant. All estimates of prevalence values were reported with 95% CIs.

In descriptive analysis, MetS prevalence at the national and provincial level was assessed and presented. Age standardization of provincial results was performed according to the National Population and Housing Census conducted by Iran's Statistical Center. In the next step, the association of MetS with IHD and stroke incidence was analyzed by logistic regression. Afterward, we applied 2 models to assess the association of 6 MetS groups of component clusters with IHD and stroke incidence; in the first reference group were people who did not completely fulfill the MetS definition (model 1: have 0 or 1 or 2 components of MetS) and in the second were people without any MetS components (model 2: 0 components). Crude and adjusted logistic regression were performed for both populations as in model 1 (0, 1 or 2 components) and 2 (0 components). In the adjusted model, the odds ratios (ORs) were adjusted for a series of relevant sociodemographic variables (age, area of residence, education level, wealth index) and lifestyle risk factors (cigarette smoking [current], alcohol [heavy drinker], low physical activity), selected based on a literature review of evidence on the risk factors of CVDs.

Ethical Considerations

The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the National Institute for Health Research (ID: IR.TUMS.NIHR.REC.1398.006). The patients/participants provided their written informed consent to participate in this study.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

We enrolled 18 083 >25‐year‐old people, of whom 55.64% were female. MetS prevalences were 54.19%, 57.77%, and 49.69% in the total population, women, and men, respectively. Overall, the prevalence of MetS increased by age; the lowest and greatest prevalences were observed in 25‐ to 34‐year‐old and ≥75‐year‐old groups (32.16% (95% CI, 29.84%–34.58%) and 69.88% (95% CI, 65.04%–74.32%), respectively). Moreover, women aged >75‐year‐old had the greatest prevalence (79.24% [95% CI, 71.91%–85.05%]) among age and sex groups.

People living in urban areas showed higher MetS prevalence than rural ones (55.59% [95% CI, 54.31%–56.86%] versus 49.91% [95% CI, 54.31%–56.86%], respectively, P value <0.001). Regarding literacy, and marital status, the highest prevalence was observed in people who were illiterate and widowed; 68.73% and 73.68%, respectively (Table S1). In people with hypertension, diabetes, and cholesterol >240 mg/dL the prevalence was 82.24% (95% CI, 80.98%–83.43%), 91.92% (95% CI, 90.49%–93.15%), 81.14% (95% CI, 79.07%–83.05%) respectively. Moreover, 50.58% (95% CI, 47.07%–54.11%) of current cigarette smokers and 49.42% (95% CI, 31.97%–67.01%) of nonsmokers had MetS (Table 1). Mean of body mass index, total cholesterol, and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol in the group with MetS was 29.15 kg, 177 mg/dL, and 101.08 mg/dL, significantly higher than the group with no MetS (95% CI, 29.01–29.28 kg, 175.76–178.24 mg/dL, and 99.99–102.17 mg/dL, P value <0.001) (Table 1).

Prevalence of MetS, Its Components, and Groups

Among people with metabolic syndrome, elevated WC was the most prevalent criteria; 91.41% (95% CI, 90.51%–92.22%). However, in men with MetS, low HDL‐C occupied this position with prevalence of 85.55%. The lowest prevalent criteria were FPG in the total population (60.59% [95% CI, 59.18%–61.98%]), and men (60.72% [95% CI, 58.38%–63.01%]) and elevated triglyceride in women (58.11% [95% CI, 56.31%–59.89%]) (Table 2). The most prevalent group of MetS was group 2, which included clusters with 3 components including high BP or high FPG with 25.21% (95% CI, 24.01%–26.42%). Ranking next were groups 5 and 6 with quite similar prevalences: 16.18% (95% CI, 15.16%–17.2%) and 17.98% (95% CI, 16.83%–19.12%), respectively. The lowest prevalence was observed in group 3: 8.34% (95% CI, 7.53%–9.16%) (Table S2). A similar pattern was observed at the subnational level where group 2 and group 3 had the highest and lowest prevalence in almost all provinces, respectively (Figure 2).

Association of MetS With IHD and Stroke Prevalence

Among total people with MetS, 10.64% (95% CI, 9.79%–11.57%) experienced at least 1 IHD event, which was significantly greater than 4.32% (95% CI, 3.76%–4.97%) of people without MetS (P value <0.001). Overall, MetS was associated with 1.70 (95% CI, 1.40–2.07) times greater adjusted odds of IHD. In men and women with MetS the adjusted ORs were (2.02 and 1.43 [95% CI, 1.57–2.60 and 1.06–1.93], respectively). Regarding stroke, 1.92% of people with MetS had at least 1 stroke event, which was 2.09 (95% CI, 1.47–2.97) times greater than the group with no MetS in univariate analysis. However, after adjustment, the presence of MetS did not statistically significantly increase the odds of stroke (P value, 0.178). Adjusted ORs for stroke in women and men also were not statistically significant (P value, 0.509 and 0.129, respectively) (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

We illustrated that not all the subgroups with MetS have similar association with IHD. People with all 5 components of MetS or groups of 4 components including both high BP and high FPG demonstrated the greatest ORs of IHD; the odds of IHD were nearly 2.5 times when comparing with people without MetS (≤2 components). It was even considerably greater (>6 times) when compared with people without any MetS components (0 components). Furthermore, a dose–response relationship was observed between the number of MetS components and IHD. Regarding stroke and MetS, despite the significant correlation in the univariate analysis, multivariable analysis was not statistically significant.

Our estimation according to STEPS 2021 revealed that more than half of ≥25‐year‐old Iranian people (54.19%) have MetS based on JIS criteria, which is higher than the 47.6% that was estimated in STEPS 2016 study in Iran. 23 JIS criteria were selected in this study because of its WC value that was based on the Middle East and Mediterranean region measure provided by the IDF. 21 , 24 The estimated prevalence of MetS in our study is higher than a previously meta‐analysis reporting prevalence of MetS in Iranian adults aged >20 years about 24%. 25 The observed discrepancy could be due to differences in definitions of MetS in various studies and considering the growing prevalence of MetS during past years.

A closer look reveals that multiple diagnostic criteria for MetS have been developed since 1998. In this regard, a meeting between several major organizations as a Joint Interim in 2009 tried to harmonize the definition and selected a single set of cut points for all components except WC. 2 Therefore, the difference between JIS and some other definitions is in the WC criteria; it has lower cut point for WC (94 cm and 80 cm for men and women, respectively) than ATP III (Adult Treatment Panel III; 102 cm and 88 cm for men and women, respectively), while similar to IDF. The difference between JIS and IDF is the necessity to include the obesity domain in all MetS clusters in IDF. 2 , 23 These differences can cause the IDF and ATP III to have higher level of restriction and show lower MetS prevalence, which might be the reason for considerably greater prevalence in our study than the records from IDF (20%–25% of the world population), the prevalence in Asia (12%–37%), Europe (12%–26%), the United States (34.3%, modified National Cholesterol Education Program ATP III), 26 , 27 , 28 and Middle East countries such as Qatar (33.7%) based on IDF and Turkey (33.9%) based on ATP III. 29 , 30 Previous studies also showed the highest MetS prevalence when applying the JIS definition. 23 Furthermore, this difference might partly be due to the variation in distributions of sex, age, race, ethnicity, and sociocultural situations. 31 , 32

People >25 years with MetS demonstrated 1.7 times greater odds of having at least 1 IHD event compared with those without MetS according to our findings, which is consistent with previous studies in the world and in Iran. 11 , 33 , 34 , 35 The Framingham Spring and Hoorn Study that used the National Cholesterol Education Program ATP III MetS definition, and the Hong et al study that applied ATP III revealed nearly 2 to 3 times greater risk of developing coronary heart disease in people with MetS. 11 , 34 , 35 Although not all previous studies applied the same MetS definition, a community‐based study in Iran showed that the risk of CVD is not significantly different between various definitions 33 and not dependent on the WC threshold including 94/80 and 102/88, 36 which are the thresholds used in JIS and American Heart Association/National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute or ATP III. Furthermore, previous studies stated that cardiometabolic complications developed at lower amounts of adiposity in Asian than western populations, 37 which resulted in lower recommended cutoffs for body mass index and WC in Asian populations. 24

One of the aims of this study was to criticize recognition of MetS as a homogenous disorder or syndrome, which is also controversial in different studies. 38 In this order, the findings illustrated that the combination of elevated FPG, and elevated BP either in 3‐, 4‐, or 5‐component MetS possess the most significant correlation with IHD. To do this, we categorized MetS clusters into 6 groups according to the 2 models, with a difference in their reference group; people with no MetS for the first model and people with no components of MetS for the second model (Figure 1). Speaking of the first model, the only groups that were significantly correlated with IHD were the ones with both elevated FPG and elevated BP. The second model illustrated that all groups of MetS clusters are correlated with IHD except the THW cluster, individuals with both elevated FPG and elevated BP, like the first model. The difference between clusters could be related to the role of each risk factor in the pathophysiology of MetS and emphasis on the significance of elevated FPG and BP.

Multiple studies investigated the pathophysiology of MetS, which demonstrated that insulin resistance, visceral adiposity, dyslipidemia, and endothelia dysfunction are among the core features. 8 , 9 To be specific, endothelial damage is the shared common pathway between risk factors like insulin resistance, visceral adiposity, and dyslipidemia to increase risk of cardiovascular diseases. 8 Furthermore, high BP is a factor to ensure that these risk factors affected the endothelium severely enough to cause damage. Given that there is a theory that insulin resistance is a cause of MetS, 21 it could be concluded that elevated FPG is more a preliminary risk factor for CVD and when it is concomitant with elevated BP shows more severe endothelial damage and higher risk of CVD, consistent with our findings. Nevertheless, there is a more complex correlation between high FPG, high BP, and IHD, 39 which has been studied previously. Therefore, MetS provides an excellent basis to identify high‐risk patients but we must consider that it is not an IHD scoring system because the risk varies between clusters. 9

The THW cluster showed the lowest IHD event in both models and did not significantly increase the odds of IHD. However, the Framingham Offspring Study illustrated that THW increases the risk of CHD by nearly 2 times. It is probably due to their different reference group who were people without THW and their study group that included individuals without diabetes or CVD. 40 Another study also revealed that THW is associated with nearly 2 times greater risk of IHD, in which the reference group was people without MetS components. 11 Accordingly, their reference group is similar to our study's second model and the difference in the results might be due to the more restricted WC criteria used in their study (National Cholesterol Education Program ATP III, and ATP III). To be clearer, our WC criterion that is present in the THW cluster had a lower limit of circumference than the mentioned study, which results in enrolling people with lower WCs who have lower risk of CVDs according to previous studies. This might be the reason for no significant correlation with IHD.

In terms of the 2 applied models, some studies used methodologies similar to model 2 in our study; they compared the relative risk of IHD in different MetS clusters with the general population who had no component of MetS 11 or had only diabetes. 41 However, we compared the risk with people without MetS who can have 2 or fewer components of MetS. We believe that it could provide a more comprehensive vision for clinicians because they are seeing people who meet or do not meet the MetS definition, and it is vital to know which clusters could differentiate these people regarding the risk of IHD. Therefore, they can provide individualized long‐term management and treatment plans.

History of stroke was not associated with any MetS cluster in the multivariable analysis. Regarding prevalence rate of stroke, there was also no association with groups with MetS in our study and previous study. 12 However, another study that applied a similar method to our second model demonstrated a significant association in groups with elevated BP, or elevated FPG, with the greatest significance in groups with both risk factors. 12 They adjusted their findings with age, sex, and race, whereas we did adjustment with age, area of residence, education level, cigarette smoking (current), wealth index, alcohol (heavy drinker), and low physical activity. It might be one of the reasons for the observed difference, because our univariate findings indicated significant association in respect of stroke like the aforementioned study.

Policy Implications

Our study suggests that MetS may not be a precise indicator of health status in relation to IHD and stroke. Elevated FPG and high BP were identified as significant contributors to IHD and stroke in patients with MetS, highlighting the need for targeted policies. Although no direct link was found between other MetS components (elevated WC, triglycerides, and low HDL‐C) and IHD or stroke, these factors are still important as they can lead to hypertension and diabetes. 42

Limitations and Strengths

One of the strengths of this study was being nationally representative, which reduces the selection bias and enlightens the pathways to make health policies in the future. Furthermore, the results criticized considering MetS as a unique syndrome, which points toward the importance of individualized risk assessment through more developed risk scoring systems.

One of the limitations that we faced in this study was not having access to the chronologic data of the individuals to perform more advanced analysis on the association of MetS clusters with incidence of IHD and stroke. In addition, IHD and stroke were self‐reported, despite explanations and questions being administered by study coordinators, which can introduce reporting bias. A health data recording system can address these limitations.

CONCLUSIONS

According to the JIS definition, more than half of the people >25 in Iran have MetS, which is significantly greater than global records and slightly greater than 2016 record in Iran. It emphasizes the importance of improving NCD risk factor assessment and preventive measure in Iran. Not all MetS clusters illustrated the same association with IHD and stroke. The findings illustrated that MetS clusters with both high BP and high FPG are the most critical ones to focus the policies on them.
