Prevalence of transfusion-transmissible infections among blood donors in a tertiary health institution in South-west, Nigeria: a three-year retrospective study

Background

Blood transfusion is an essential medical intervention used to manage conditions such as severe blood loss, hematologic disorders, malignancies, surgical procedures, and obstetric complications. As a therapeutic product, human blood has no sustainable or functionally equivalent synthetic substitute [1]. Although scientific efforts have explored artificial blood alternatives, these innovations remain limited in clinical utility [2]. Thus, the donation of human blood continues to be the most reliable and effective source of lifesaving components required in healthcare delivery. However, transfusion practice is not without risk, as donated blood may carry infectious agents capable of being transmitted to recipients [1].

These infections, referred to as transfusion transmissible infections (TTIs), represent a major challenge to blood transfusion safety. The risk of such infections has increased due to the emergence and persistence of various pathogens that may escape detection during routine screening processes [3]. To mitigate this threat, global and national health authorities such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the National Blood Transfusion Services (NBTS) recommend mandatory pre-donation screening for key TTIs, including Hepatitis B virus (HBV), Hepatitis C virus (HCV), Human Immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and syphilis [4], in line with Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3, which promotes good health and well-being. The prevalence of these infections among blood donors differs across countries and regions. Globally, HBV prevalence ranges from 0.6% to 18.6%, HCV from 0.05% to 8.69%, and HIV from 0.02% to 7.8% [5], while syphilis continues to be a serious concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where diagnostic infrastructure is often underdeveloped [6].

Several studies in African countries such as Cameroon, Ethiopia, Mozambique, Eritrea, and Nigeria have consistently reported HBV as the most prevalent infection among blood donors [7–11], although other infections have been reported as more dominant in certain settings. Cwinyaai et al. [12] identified HCV as the leading infection in their study, while Kanyike et al. [13] found syphilis to be most common among donors in Uganda. Sub-Saharan Africa continues to bear a disproportionate burden of these infections, primarily due to high background disease prevalence, limited access to quality healthcare, inadequate donor screening, and the persistence of unsafe medical practices. A recent meta-analysis placed the pooled prevalence of TTIs in the region at approximately 2% [14], with HBV most frequently observed, followed by HIV, syphilis, and HCV, although the exact burden may vary based on local epidemiological trends, donor demographics, and testing methods. A prevalence of 6.0%, 4.0%, 3.0% and 3.0% was reported in Gabon, while a meta-analysis in Ethiopia found 5.2%, 0.9%, 2.8% and 1.5% for HBV, HCV, HIV and Syphilis respectively [15, 16].

In Nigeria, studies have reported varying prevalence rates across different regions. In the South-East region, HBV prevalence among blood donors ranges from 2% to 4%, HCV from 0.5% to 2%, HIV around 1.7%, and syphilis between 0.1% and 1% [17, 18]. In the federal capital city Abuja, Otu et al. [19] reported an overall prevalence of 11.88%, predominantly among young adults and family or replacement donors. In a southwestern facility in Ekiti State, Daramola et al. [20] documented a total prevalence of 22.7%, with HBV accounting for 12%, HCV 6.7%, syphilis 2.7%, and HIV 1.3%. In Osun State, multiple studies have assessed TTI prevalence among donors. Buseri et al. [11] found HBV positivity at 18.6%, HIV at 3.1%, HCV at 6.0%, and syphilis at 1.1%. Similarly, Oladele et al. [21] reported HBV and HIV prevalence rates of 19.9% and 3.0% respectively, while a large-scale retrospective study by Salawu et al. [22] involving 14,500 donors in Ile-Ife reported rates of 7.5% for HBV, 0.96% for HIV, 0.86% for HCV, and 2.61% for syphilis.

Given the significant public health implications of TTIs and the changing patterns of their occurrence, continuous surveillance and data analysis are essential. This study, therefore, seeks to assess the burden and trends of TTIs among prospective blood donors in Osun State, Nigeria, over a three-year period (2020 to 2022), with the aim of generating context-specific evidence to guide targeted interventions and improve transfusion safety in the region.

Methodology

Study setting

This study was conducted at Uniosun Teaching Hospital (UTH), located in Osogbo, the capital city of Osun State, Nigeria. Osogbo has an estimated population of over 800,000 [23]. UTH is a tertiary healthcare facility that provides a wide range of medical services, including comprehensive blood banking and transfusion services to residents of Osogbo and surrounding communities.

Study design

This was a retrospective, cross-sectional study spanning a three-year period from January 2020 to December 2022. The study involved a total of 11,386 prospective blood donors who presented at the blood bank of Uniosun Teaching Hospital during the study period. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. Where applicable, the RECORD (REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data) checklist was followed to ensure transparency in data source description and handling.

Data retrieval and inclusion criteria

Data were retrieved from the blood bank’s donor records and included information on donor age, gender, type of donation, year of donation, and results of TTI screenings. In this study, prospective blood donors refer to individuals who presented at the blood bank with the intention to donate blood and who had successfully passed the initial pre-donation eligibility assessment. This assessment involved evaluation of age (18–60 years), body weight, general physical wellness, vital signs, frequency of prior donations, and medical interview, in accordance with standard donor selection guidelines. It also included a laboratory evaluation of packed cell volume (PCV) to assess haemoglobin adequacy. For the purpose of this study, records were manually retrieved for all individuals who met the basic pre-donation criteria and had undergone PCV assessment, regardless of whether they were ultimately deferred or accepted for donation based on haemoglobin levels. These records were included to ensure completeness of TTI screening data where available.

Screening procedure

All prospective donors who meet the minimum PCV requirement were screened for at least one TTI using rapid immunochromatographic test kits. At UTH blood bank, the screening approach is sequential and stepwise, guided by the relative prevalence of TTIs in the region as reported in previous studies. This strategy is employed to optimize resource use during donor screening. Screening begins with HBV detection via Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) testing. If a donor tests reactive for HBV, they are immediately deferred, and no further TTI screening is conducted. If non-reactive, screening proceeds to HCV using anti-HCV antibody rapid tests. A reactive result at this stage also leads to donor deferral. If non-reactive, the donor is then screened for HIV types 1 and 2 using anti-HIV-1/2 antibody rapid tests and syphilis, using the Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) test. Only individuals who test non-reactive to all four TTIs are allowed to proceed with blood donation. The rapid test kits used in this study included DiaSpot® (EVANCARE Medical, Nantong, China) for HBsAg (sensitivity > 99.9%, specificity 99.7%), anti-HCV (sensitivity 99.1%, specificity 99.5%), and syphilis (sensitivity > 99.9%, specificity 99.7%). For HIV-1/2 screening, the Determine™ HIV-1/2 test (Abbott Point of Care, Abbott Park, Illinois, USA) was used, with reported sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 99.75%. Following donation, confirmatory testing using the Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) technique is routinely performed for all donated units. However, the data utilized in this study were limited to results from the initial rapid screening tests and did not include ELISA confirmatory outcomes.

Data management

Collected data were entered into Microsoft Excel for organization, cleaning, and preparation for statistical analysis. To ensure data accuracy and prevent overestimation of prevalence, all donor records were screened for duplicates before analysis. Duplicate records were identified by matching donor number, demographic details (age, gender, blood group), date of donation, and TTI screening results. Where multiple entries were found for the same donor on the same date, only a single record was retained, while donations from the same donor on different dates were treated as separate entries. Records without TTI screening results (primarily from donors deferred due to low PCV) were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), version 22.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data and were represented as frequencies and percentages. Data was presented in tables and charts.

Results

A total of 11,386 blood donor records were analysed across the three-year period. Of these, 2,899 (25.5%) were from 2020, 4,141 (36.4%) from 2021, and 4,346 (38.1%) from 2022. The majority of the donors were aged 25–34 years (42.6%), followed by those aged 35–44 (28.9%), 18–24 (17.3%), and 45–60 (11.2%). The mean age of the donors was 33.09 ± 8.66 years. Males constituted the predominant group, accounting for 9,245 (81.2%) of donors, while females represented 2,141 (18.8%). For Hepatitis B, 2021 recorded the highest prevalence with 296 (8.6%) positive cases out of 3,455 donors, followed by 2022 with 286 (8.0%) out of 3,569, and 2020 with 179 (7.1%) out of 2,505. Overall, while HIV showed consistently low prevalence, Hepatitis B remained the most prevalent TTI across all three years.

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence and distribution of TTIs among blood donors over a three-year period at a tertiary health facility in Osun State, Southwestern Nigeria. The findings revealed that hepatitis B infection was the most prevalent TTI, affecting 8.0% of the donor population. This was followed by hepatitis C at 4.1%, syphilis at 2.7%, and HIV at 1.1%.

These rates indicate a notable decline in the prevalence of HBV, HCV, and HIV compared to previous studies conducted in the same region. Buseri et al. [11] reported substantially higher prevalence rates of 18.6% for HBV, 6% for HCV, and 3.1% for HIV, although syphilis in that study was observed at 1.1%, which is lower than the 2.7% identified in our study. Similarly, Oladele et al. [21] found a 19.9% prevalence of HBV and 3% for HIV, further emphasizing the decreasing trend of certain TTIs in Osun State. Contrarily, a study by Muhibi et al. [24] found a lower prevalence of HCV at 2.07%, which is below the 4.1% observed in our data, suggesting variability even within the same region. These differences may reflect the impact of public health interventions that have taken root over the past decade. Government and non-governmental efforts ranging from hepatitis B vaccination campaigns, targeted awareness programs, and increased access to screening and treatment for viral hepatitis and HIV may have contributed significantly to the observed reduction in disease burden.

Differences in study methodologies might also explain the variations in prevalence. The study by Buseri et al. [11] employed both immunochromatographic diagnostic kits and ELISA, a more sensitive and specific method capable of detecting low-level pathogen antibodies, including during the window period [25]. In contrast, our study utilized retrospective records based solely on immunochromatographic screening methods, which may be less sensitive. The retrospective design of our study, however, enabled the inclusion of a larger sample size, which improved statistical power [26].

When compared to other retrospective studies across Nigeria, such as that by Otu et al. [19] in Gwagwalada, Abuja, which reported lower prevalence rates of 4.4% for HBV and 3.52% for HCV, our study reflects a relatively higher burden, particularly for hepatitis B. Durowade et al. [27] also reported lower prevalence rates in Ekiti State, 4.4% for HBV, 2.7% for HCV, 1.6% for syphilis, and 1.7% for HIV. Conversely, a cross-sectional study by Motayo et al. [28] in Ogun State reported higher prevalence for HBV at 10% and HIV at 6.2%, though syphilis was not detected at all.

The year-by-year analysis of TTIs revealed that hepatitis B remained the most prevalent infection throughout the three-year period, with rates of 7.1% in 2020, 8.6% in 2021, and 8.0% in 2022. Although there was a slight decline in 2022, the overall burden remains high. Hepatitis C showed a fluctuating pattern of 4.7% in 2020, decreasing to 3.7% in 2021, and rising again to 4.0% in 2022, indicating inconsistent control efforts and the need for continued preventive strategies, especially given the lack of a vaccine [29]. Syphilis prevalence declined from 3.5% in 2020 to 2.3% in 2021 but increased to 2.6% in 2022. This is in disagreement with the study of Kanyike et al. [13] in Uganda which reported the highest prevalence of syphilis among blood donors (5.1%) in 2021. This finding aligns with global concerns on the rise of syphilis infection rate and reinforces the need for public health interventions that promote early detection and treatment of cases [30–32]. HIV prevalence on the other hand, though lower than other infections, increased from 0.8% in 2020 to 1.2% in both 2021 and 2022. This steady rise signals a need to reinforce prevention and testing strategies. The irregularity observed in the TTI trend across the three years may be attributed to variations in donor selection practices, particularly during the COVID-19 and immediate post-pandemic periods when there was greater reliance on family/replacement donors. Additional factors such as shifts in donor demographics and changes in the intensity or focus of donor recruitment campaign strategies in the region may also have contributed to the fluctuations.

These findings strengthen the notion that hepatitis B infection remains a significant public health concern among blood donors in Osun State, with a prevalence of 8 in every 100 donors mirroring the overall estimated prevalence in Nigeria [33], a rate that still warrants urgent attention. The relatively high prevalence calls for sustained and enhanced preventive strategies, including expanded hepatitis B vaccination coverage, and community education programs. Effective implementation of these interventions could further reduce the burden of TTIs and improve transfusion safety in the region.

This study revealed a marked predominance of male donors over female donors, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 4:1. This finding is similar to previous research in Nigeria and other sub-Saharan Africa countries [7, 8, 10, 11, 19, 27, 28], which frequently report male dominance in blood donor populations. Several physiological and sociocultural factors may contribute to this disparity. Women are more likely to be deferred from donation due to menstruation, pregnancy, lactation, and lower hemoglobin levels, conditions that may render them temporarily or permanently unfit for donation [34, 35]. Additionally, cultural perceptions and misconceptions about blood donation may further discourage female participation [34]. Addressing these gender disparities would require targeted awareness campaigns and health system interventions that focus on expanding donation eligibility among women and dispelling myths surrounding female blood donation.

The donor population in this study was predominantly composed of family or replacement donors rather than voluntary donors, a trend similarly observed in the study by Otu et al. [19]. This reflects a systemic reliance on patient-linked donations within Nigerian healthcare settings, where individuals in need of transfusion are often required to bring replacement donors. While this approach may sustain blood availability in the short term, it has long been associated with higher risks of TTIs and diminished overall safety of the blood supply [36]. Voluntary non-remunerated blood donors are widely recognized to have a lower risk profile, primarily because they are more likely to be motivated by altruism rather than compulsion and less likely to conceal risky health behaviors [37]. Therefore, the continued dominance of replacement donation highlights the urgent need to strengthen voluntary blood donation systems in Nigeria through donor education, incentives, and nationwide blood mobilization strategies.

Our findings showed that family or replacement donors were significantly more likely to have HBV infection compared to voluntary donors. This buttresses longstanding concerns about the relative safety of blood obtained through non-voluntary means. Family or replacement donors, under pressure to provide blood for a relative, may withhold critical information about risk behaviors or health status, leading to increased chances of transmitting infections through donated blood. These findings strongly support the global call for a transition towards a system of 100% voluntary, non-remunerated blood donation, which has been shown to enhance the safety, sufficiency, and sustainability of national blood supplies [38].

This study has a few limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the retrospective design relied on secondary data, which inherently limits the control over data quality and completeness. Additionally, the data used in this study were derived from donor screening records based solely on immunochromatographic techniques, a method which while rapid and widely used in routine blood banking, may have lower sensitivity and specificity compared to more advanced methods such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay or nucleic acid testing. As such, the actual burden of TTIs may be under- or over-estimated. Despite these limitations, the large sample size, multi-year data coverage, and relevance to the regional public health context provide valuable insights into the trends and burden of TTIs, making the findings highly informative for policy and intervention planning.

Conclusion

This study highlights the persistent burden of TTIs among prospective blood donors in Osun State, Southwest Nigeria, with hepatitis B consistently identified as the most common infection throughout the three-year period assessed. While the prevalence of some infections appears to have decreased compared to earlier reports, the presence of these TTIs among donors remains a significant concern for blood transfusion safety. To improve blood safety in the region, there is a pressing need to expand hepatitis B vaccination coverage, implement the use of more sensitive screening technologies such as nucleic acid testing, and strengthen public health education on safe blood donation practices and donor eligibility. Additionally, efforts must be intensified to build a robust system of voluntary non-remunerated blood donation, thereby reducing reliance on family or replacement donors who are often associated with higher infection risks. Achieving a safer and more reliable blood supply will require continuous investment in transfusion infrastructure, policy reform, and community engagement strategies that support ethical and sustainable donor recruitment.
