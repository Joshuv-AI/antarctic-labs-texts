The effects of bleaching products on the color stability of ion-releasing restoratives
Authors: Jian Sheng Lee, Noor Azlin Yahya, Azwatee Abdul Aziz, Adrian U-Jin Yap
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContemporary ion-releasing restoratives were developed to prevent secondary caries while maintaining the mechanical strength and esthetic qualities of conventional composite resins. Dental bleaching procedures may affect the physical properties of restorations. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of bleaching agents on the color stability of these ion-releasing restoratives.MethodsForty-five disc-shaped specimens of Activa Bioactive, Beautifil II LS, Cention-N, Riva LC HVGIC, and Luna were fabricated using customised cylindrical Teflon molds. Baseline CIE-Lab color measurements were recorded using a spectrophotometer. Specimens were assigned to three subgroups (n = 15) and exposed to take-home bleaching (Pola Night), in-office bleaching (Pola Rapid), or artificial saliva (control) per manufacturers’ protocols. Color measurements were repeated at 24 h (T1), two weeks (T2), and one month (T3), with color change (ΔE*) calculated. Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA, two-way repeated measures ANOVA and post-hoc tests (p < 0.05).ResultsΔL* ranged from − 0.02 ± 1.23 to 4.94 ± 1.37; Δa* from − 0.31 ± 0.12 to 0.65 ± 0.13; Δb* from − 3.08 ± 1.24 to 0.31 ± 0.86; and ΔE* from 0.94 ± 0.48 to 5.99 ± 1.33. At T1, Riva LC HVGIC showed the highest ΔE*, while Luna had the least. Both bleaching products had similar effects on ΔE*. Activa Bioactive’s ΔE* increased significantly from T1 to T3, reaching the highest value at T3.ConclusionsThe impact of bleaching on ion-releasing restoratives’ color stability was material-dependent.

Background

Direct tooth-colored restorations are widely used to replace damaged tooth structures and enhance dental esthetics. Composite resin (CR) remains the preferred restorative material in modern dentistry due to its excellent mechanical and esthetic properties. However, systematic reviews have identified recurrent caries as a major cause of CR restoration failures [1, 2]. Recurrent caries often occurs at restorative margins due to their susceptibility to plaque formation. These areas are more difficult to clean, and the presence of an interfacial gap at the tooth–restoration margin promotes bacterial colonization [3].

Ion-releasing restorative materials (IRMs) were developed to address this issue. These materials release and recharge remineralizing ions that promote fluorapatite crystal formation, which is more acid-resistant than hydroxyapatite [4, 5]. Conventional IRMs, such as glass ionomer cement (GIC), are limited by poor mechanical and optical properties, restricting their use in stress-bearing and esthetic zones. Advances in IRM technology have incorporated resin monomers and fluoroaluminosilicate fillers, enhancing mechanical strength, optical appearance, and remineralizing ability for use in esthetic areas [6]. Newer IRMs feature innovative fillers, such as Isofiller in alkasites (Ivoclar Vivadent) and surface pre-reacted glass ionomer (S-PRG) in giomers [7, 8]. Additionally, handling properties have improved in high-viscosity GICs and in Activa Bioactive Restoratives [9, 10].

As public awareness of dental esthetics increases, the global tooth-bleaching market continues to expand rapidly [11]. Vital tooth bleaching is categorized into in-office, take-home, and over-the-counter products, which primarily contain hydrogen peroxide or carbamide peroxide. These agents release free radicals that break down staining molecules within tooth structures [12]. However, this nonspecific reaction can produce adverse effects, including crown fractures, dentin hypersensitivity, restoration discoloration, and debonding [13, 14]. Several studies have reported changes in restoration color following exposure to bleaching agents [15–17]. The severity of color change is assessed using perceptibility and acceptability thresholds. One study reported that ΔE* values exceeding 1.2 are visually perceptible, while those above 2.7 are considered clinically unacceptable [18]. The ongoing ionic exchange and dissolution of functional filler particles in IRMs create internal porosities, making them more susceptible to the effects of bleaching agents [19].

Given the limited research on the impact of bleaching products on contemporary IRMs, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of in-office and take-home bleaching agents on the color stability of IRMs and to assess their color stability over time. The null hypotheses were: (i) in-office and take-home bleaching products do not affect the color stability of IRMs; (ii) there are no differences in color stability among IRMs; (iii) the color of IRMs does not change over time following exposure to bleaching agents.

Methods

Evaluated materials and determination of sample size

The technical profiles of the materials evaluated in this study are presented in Table 1. These included four IRMs of shade A2: a bioactive composite (Activa [AB], Pulpdent Corporation, Watertown, MA, USA), a giomer (Beautifil II LS [BI], Shofu, Kyoto, Japan), a bulk-fill alkasite (Cention-N [CN], Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Schaan, Liechtenstein), and an encapsulated high-viscosity GIC (Riva LC HVGIC [RV], SDI Limited, Bayswater, Victoria, Australia), along with a conventional CR control (Luna [LN], SDI Limited, Bayswater, Victoria, Australia). An in-office bleaching product (Pola Rapid [PR], SDI Limited, Bayswater, Victoria, Australia) and a take-home bleaching product (Pola Night [PN], SDI Limited, Bayswater, Victoria, Australia) were used in this study.

A minimum total sample size of 90 specimens (n = 6 per group) was determined using G*Power software version 3.1.9.4, based on an ANOVA model with an effect size of 0.6, an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 95% across 15 groups [15, 20]. However, the total sample size was increased to 225 specimens (n = 15 per group) to enhance the robustness and reliability of the results.

Specimen preparation

Forty-five disc-shaped specimens were prepared for each material, following the manufacturers’ instructions, using a customized cylindrical Teflon mold (8 × 2 mm). A cellulose acetate matrix strip and a glass slide were placed on the top and bottom surfaces of the mold to remove excess material under finger pressure and ensure a smooth finish. The specimens were then light-cured through the glass slide for 20 s using a calibrated LED curing unit (Radii Cal LED Curing Light, SDI, Bayswater, Victoria, Australia) with an output irradiance of 1200 mW/cm² and a wavelength of 468–470 nm. The light-curing unit was recharged and calibrated after every 15 specimens to maintain consistent output. Specimen thickness was measured using a digital caliper (Mitutoyo Corporation, Kawasaki, Japan).

The specimens were stored in artificial saliva (SAGF) [21] and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow post-irradiation polymerization (Memmert Incubator IN750, Schwabach, Germany). Following this, the specimens were finished and polished using medium, fine, and superfine Sof-Lex discs (3 M ESPE, St. Paul, Minnesota, USA) at 15,000 rpm, with 10 strokes applied in a single direction for each disc. All specimens were polished by a single operator, J.S.L., to ensure consistency in applied pressure. Specimen dimensions were verified using the digital caliper to ensure a standardized thickness (2.0 ± 0.2 mm). The polished specimens were then stored in artificial saliva at 37 °C in the incubator for two weeks to allow water stabilization before the commencement of bleaching procedures [22]. The artificial saliva was refreshed weekly to prevent deposit buildup.

Bleaching procedure

The specimens of each material were randomly distributed into three groups (n = 15) as follows:

Group A (control): Immersion in artificial saliva for seven days.

Group B (take-home bleaching): Exposed to PN for 45 min daily for seven days.

Group C (in-office bleaching): Exposed to PR for three cycles of 8 min on day seven.

All specimens were rinsed with distilled water and dried before the bleaching procedures. When not undergoing bleaching, they were stored in artificial saliva at 37 °C.

Color measurement

The color of each specimen was measured using the Commission Internationale de l’Éclairage (CIE Lab*) color system with a spectrophotometer (CM-5, Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan). The standard observer angle was set at 10°, and each specimen was positioned within the 3 mm diameter illumination area. Reflectance values were recorded after 1 s of exposure to the D65 standard illuminant, against a standardized black background.

Three readings per specimen were taken at baseline (T0), 24 h (T1), two weeks (T2), and one month (T3) after bleaching. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 25 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied to assess data normality. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the interaction between IRMs and bleaching products on ΔE. Tukey’s post hoc test was used to analyze differences between IRMs and bleaching products. A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to assess the interaction between IRMs and time on ΔE.

Discussion

Effects of bleaching products on IRMs

As bleaching products significantly affected the ΔE* values, the first null hypothesis was rejected. In general, the L* and a* values increased, while the b* value decreased after bleaching. Changes in the L* and b* dimensions were more pronounced than those in the a* dimension. When mapped to the Munsell color space, the specimens’ colors shifted toward lighter, bluish hues, resulting in a lightening of the restorative materials [23]. Furthermore, the a* and b* values approached the neutral axis—specifically, negative a* values increased and positive b* values decreased—causing desaturation and a duller appearance [24]. These findings align with studies by Karamangar et al. and Korać et al., which identified an increase in L* as the primary contributor to color changes [17, 25]. RV and AB specimens bleached with PN and PR exhibited a larger change in the b* value compared to the L* value. This is consistent with the findings of Yu et al., who reported significant declines in the b* values of resin-modified GIC and compomer after bleaching [15].

The bleaching mechanism involves hydrogen peroxide oxidizing surface pigments and amides that affect the restoration’s shade [12]. In resin-based materials, discoloration is promoted by hydrolytic degradation, driven by water sorption and the elution of residual monomers, especially in matrices with high resin content and low filler load [16, 26]. For IRMs, the glass matrix functions as a permeable hydrogel, allowing ion and water diffusion and enabling dissociated peroxides to penetrate the material [27]. Reactive oxygen species and perhydroxyl ions then attack the resin–filler interface, leading to plasticization, matrix dissolution, and micropore formation, all of which alter reflectance and optical properties [16]. Additionally, bleaching-induced erosion is influenced by the acidity of bleaching products. With pH values ranging from 3.6 to 7.3, fluoroaluminosilicate fillers dissolve more rapidly under acidic conditions, resulting in higher particle elution [28–30].

Interestingly, artificial saliva (AS) caused greater color change in LN and AB compared to bleaching. This could be due to bleaching agents partially reversing discoloration caused by saliva. While artificial saliva promotes staining through hydrolytic degradation, bleaching can break down superficial chromophores and dissolve surface irregularities, improving reflectance and reducing visible discoloration [26, 31–33]. As a result, bleached specimens may appear less discolored than unbleached ones, even after equal exposure to saliva.

Both PR and PN exhibited comparable effects on all IRMs, which is consistent with prior research [25, 34, 35]. The similarity in outcomes may be due to differences in bleaching protocols and formulations. PR contains a high concentration of hydrogen peroxide (38%) and requires a short application time (24 min). In contrast, PN uses a lower concentration (6.6%) but requires a much longer application time (315 min).

Comparison between restorative materials

Different IRMs exhibited varying color stability, indicating that the second null hypothesis was rejected. Variations in color stability among materials can be attributed to differences in filler content, filler size, and the type of organic monomer used. Consistent with previous studies, the resin-based composite LN showed superior color stability against bleaching agents compared to IRMs [15, 16, 36]. Methacrylate-based composites form tightly cross-linked, high–molecular-weight polymers that limit the penetration of oxidizing agents into the material [16].

Among the IRMs, RV and AB were significantly affected by bleaching. This may be due to the degradation of metal polyacrylate salts within their matrices [36]. AB showed the highest ΔE* values at T3. With the lowest filler content (55.4%), AB contains a higher proportion of resin matrix, which is more prone to water sorption and discoloration. In contrast, BI showed comparable ΔE* values to LN, likely due to its high filler content (83% by weight) and the use of hydrophobic monomers such as UDMA [37]. CN’s color stability is supported by its hydrophobic resin matrix, which includes UDMA, DCP, aromatic–aliphatic UDMA, and PEG-400 DMA, along with a high filler content. Additionally, CN uses thiocarbamide as a photoinitiator instead of oxidation-prone tertiary amines, which helps reduce long-term discoloration [38].

Comparison between time intervals

The third null hypothesis was rejected, as there were significant changes in ΔE* values over time. For most IRMs, the ΔE* values increased slightly but not significantly, likely due to water absorption and color stabilization occurring within the first 7–14 days of artificial saliva immersion [22, 39]. However, AB showed a significant increase in ΔE* over time, possibly because its larger resin component promotes greater water sorption [40].

Clinical perceptibility and acceptability of color change

Perceptibility and acceptability thresholds of color changes are essential for evaluating the clinical significance of restoration discoloration. Reported threshold values in the literature range from 0.4 to 2.6 for perceptibility and from 1.7 to 5.5 for acceptability [41]. In this study, most groups exceeded the perceptibility threshold (ΔE* = 1.2) at T1, except for LN bleached with PN, which only slightly exceeded it at T3 (ΔE* = 1.22). RV bleached with both PN and PR, and CN bleached with PR, exceeded the acceptability threshold (ΔE* = 2.7) at T1. Notably, AB showed significant and progressive color deterioration over time, leading to clinically unacceptable color changes in the AS, PN, and PR groups by T3.

Ugurlu et al. investigated the color change of bleached natural tooth structure in vivo. The ΔE* was 14.22 immediately after bleaching and decreased to 7.51 after two years [42]. Li et al. reported that the color of bleached restorations improved after the withdrawal of carbamide peroxide bleaching [16]. These studies demonstrated that natural tooth and restorations show different degrees of color change after bleaching, with the potential for regression over time [16, 42].

Clinical implications

This study demonstrated that exposure to artificial saliva and bleaching products can cause discoloration of restorative materials. Care should be taken to avoid exposing restorations to bleaching agents. IRMs with poor color stability are not suitable for use in the esthetic zone of the dentition. Clinicians are advised to postpone restorative procedures for at least one week after bleaching to allow for color stabilization [42]. Patients should also be informed of the potential need to replace existing restorations following bleaching to achieve optimal esthetic outcomes.

Limitations of the study

This study does not fully replicate the oral environment, as it lacks factors such as biofilm adhesion and thermal variations. Although artificial saliva contains ions that mimic natural saliva, it does not contain glycoproteins. These glycoproteins influence viscosity and affect the diffusion and reaction rates between biomaterials and the immersion media [21]. Future studies could improve aging protocols by incorporating thermocycling to simulate thermal changes.

A limitation of the CIE-Lab color space used in this study is that it measures only three parameters: lightness (L*), red-to-green (a*), and yellow-to-blue (b*). As discussed, human color perception is influenced by lighting and surrounding objects. People are generally more sensitive to changes in the red-green axis (a*) than in the yellow-blue axis (b*) [43, 44]. The CIEDE2000 (ΔE₀₀) formula provides a more accurate assessment of clinically relevant color changes. It accounts for variations in lightness, chroma, hue, and illumination conditions [45]. Future research should adopt ΔE₀₀ for more clinically meaningful evaluations, noting that its thresholds for perceptibility and acceptability differ from those of ΔEab.

This study did not evaluate the correlation between color stability and other material properties such as surface roughness, microhardness, water sorption, and solubility. Future investigations should examine these factors, along with optical properties like gloss, translucency, fluorescence, and opalescence. This would offer a more comprehensive understanding of the performance of restorative materials [46].
