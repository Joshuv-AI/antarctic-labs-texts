Development of high-grade glioma as a second malignant neoplasm in patients treated for primary brain tumors
Authors: Cheng Li, Chuan Zhao, Jingjing Ge, Chi Zhao, Shaopei Qi, Fengjun Xue, Jingjing Zhang, Junping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeSecond malignant neoplasms (SMNs) represent a devastating late complication of central nervous system (CNS) tumor, particularly in pediatric and young adult populations. While improved treatments have increased survival rates, the risk and characteristics of SMNs in contemporary cohorts remain poorly characterized. The purpose of this study is to characterize the clinicopathological and molecular features of therapy-related high-grade gliomas(t-HGGs), and to explore their multifactorial etiology involving radiation, chemotherapy, and potential genetic susceptibility.MethodsWe analyzed seven cases of histologically confirmed SMNs developing after treatment for primary CNS tumors. We collected and analyzed key clinical information from patients, including gender, age, time to second tumor onset (latency), treatment modalities, and outcomes. Additionally, we examined imaging findings, histopathological features using hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry, as well as molecular marker characteristics.ResultsThe cohort consisted of seven patients (5 males, 2 females). The median age at primary tumor diagnosis was 5 years (range: 3–21 years), and at SMN diagnosis was 12 years (range: 7–27 years), with a median latency of 6 years (range: 2–9 years). All patients received radiotherapy. Exploratory analysis revealed no significant relationship between radiation dose and latency period. Histological transformation was universal, most commonly from medulloblastoma to high-grade glioma (3/7 cases) and germ cell tumors (3/7 cases, including 2 germinomas and 1 non-germinomatous germ cell tumor (NGGCT)). Molecular analysis in sequenced cases revealed distinct profiles, including NF1/PDGFRA alterations. Outcomes were poor.ConclusionThis study delineates a rare yet distinct clinicopathological entity—therapy-related high-grade glioma, which may arise from the complex interplay of prior radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and underlying genetic susceptibility. Recognition of this multifactorial etiology is critical for risk stratification and long-term surveillance.

Introduction

The development of second primary tumors following radiation or chemotherapy for primary brain tumors represent one of the most serious complications in the treatment of malignant brain tumors [1, 2]. Although their incidence is generally considered low, they are a well-documented entity in both clinical practice and previous studies [1–4].

With advances in the complete surgical resection of tumors, improved medical management, and more effective chemotherapy, the survival of patients with central nervous system (CNS) malignancies has been significantly prolonged. Due to the typically long latency between radiotherapy and the development of second tumors, extended survival inevitably leads to an increased incidence and a growing number of cases requiring clinical attention. The pathogenesis of second tumors is likely multifactorial, depending not only on radiation dose but also on irradiation techniques, pathological substrate, additional treatments, and patient-specific factors such as age (reflecting the biological vulnerability of neural tissue). Among second tumors in the CNS, meningiomas are relatively common, while high-grade gliomas (HGG) are comparatively rare, warranting further analysis of their clinical characteristics [1, 5–8]. Radiation-induced glioblastomas or gliomas, collectively termed radiation-induced gliomas, develop in approximately 3% of pediatric cancer survivors following cranial radiotherapy, as the most aggressive late complication, however, it seriously compromises the long-term prognosis of survivors [9, 10].For this study, a diagnosis of therapy-related tumor required considering the classic Cahan criteria and tumor predisposition syndromes. The purpose of this study is to characterize the clinicopathological and molecular features of therapy-related high-grade gliomas, and to explore their multifactorial etiology involving radiation, chemotherapy, and potential genetic susceptibility.

Materials and methods

We collected and analyzed key clinical information from patients, including gender, age, time to second tumor onset (latency), treatment modalities, and outcomes. All cases met stringent diagnostic criteria, including: (1) pathologically confirmed primary CNS malignancy, (2) multimodal therapy including radiotherapy, and (3) development of histologically distinct secondary tumors after ≥ 1 years latency.

Diagnostic Criteria for Radiation-Induced Neoplasms(the Cahan criteria): (1) the tumor must occur within the irradiated field, (2) a sufficient latency period must exist between irradiation and tumor incidence, (3) the radiation-induced tumor must be proven to be of a different histological type from that of the original neoplasm, and (4) the patient must not have any pathologies favoring the development of tumors such as Li-Fraumeni disease, tuberous sclerosis, xeroderma pigmentosum, retinoblastoma, or neurofibromatosis. We applied a modified version of the Cahan criteria, adhering to the requirements regarding radiation field, latency, and histological distinction. However, given the study’s aim to explore multifactorial etiology including genetic susceptibility, patients with potential cancer predisposition syndromes were not excluded from this analysis.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

After surgical resection, tumor specimens were fixed by immersion in 10% formalin at room temperature for 24–48 h. The fixed tissues were then embedded in molds filled with liquid paraffin, which were subsequently cooled and solidified to form paraffin blocks. Sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared from these blocks using a microtome. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, the sections were stained with hematoxylin for 1 min, rinsed under running water, and differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol for several seconds. After another rinse with running water, the sections were blued in 1% ammonia water for 1 min, followed by a brief rinse. Subsequently, the sections were counterstained with eosin solution for several seconds, rinsed again, and finally mounted. All procedures were performed at room temperature. The stained sections were examined under a light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tumor tissue samples were fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin. Sections were then deparaffinized and rehydrated. Following antigen retrieval, immunohistochemical staining was performed using the following primary antibodies: anti-Olig-2(ZA-0561;1:200; ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China), anti-GFAP(ZA-0529;1:200; ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China), anti-ATRX(ZA-0016; ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China), anti-H3K27M(ZA-0321;ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China), anti-CK(RT00301A; Newlife Science, Weifang, China), anti-IDH1 R132H(clone:H09;1:500; Dianova, Hamburg, Germany). Some of these primary antibodies were provided as ready-to-use working solutions by the supplier, corresponding approximately to the following stock solution dilutions: anti- ATRX (1:100), anti-CK (1:100), anti-H3K27M (1:100). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, diluted at 1:200, were applied and incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Detection was carried out using diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen, followed by rinsing with PBS. After dehydration and drying, the sections were mounted. Slides were examined under a light microscope (Leica DM3000, Mannheim, Germany), and images were acquired using a NanoZoomer Slide Scanner (2.0-RS). All immunohistochemical procedures were performed by an experienced pathology technician.

Statistical analysis

R (version 4.5.2) was used for statistical analysis. Given the small sample size and heterogeneous nature of the cohort, all analyses were descriptive and exploratory. All visualizations were created using packages in R, with statistical annotations directly embedded in the figures where applicable.

Latency period

The median latency between primary and secondary malignancies was 6 years (IQR 3–7 years). Latency periods ranged from 2 to 9 years (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Further molecular characterization was performed on Cases 1, 2, and 3 using next-generation sequencing. In Case 1, next-generation sequencing of the secondary high-grade glioma identified the following somatic alterations: NF1 p.Q985Kfs*7 (frameshift mutation), homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/CDKN2B, and PDGFRA amplification. Germline testing revealed a TP53 c.659A > G (p.Y220C) variant. In Case 2, sequencing of the secondary high-grade glioma detected MET amplification, a TP53 c.376-1G > C splice-site variant, FAT1 p.A1863V, and JAK3 p.S769P mutations. In Case 3, sequencing of the secondary glioblastoma reported NF1 p.Glu524Ter (nonsense mutation), PDGFRA amplification, CDKN2A/CDKN2B homozygous deletion, ATM p.Arg3008Cys, MYCN amplification, and MTAP deletion.

Case 1 was found to carry a germline TP53 c.659A > G (p.Y220C) pathogenic variant. Her mother, who also carried this variant, died of breast cancer. Based on this germline mutation and family history, Case 1 meets the diagnostic criteria for Li-Fraumeni syndrome.

Discussion

The development of second primary brain tumors is a multifactorial process, with radiation exposure being a well-established and significant risk factor [2, 8, 11, 12] In pediatric and adolescent patients, the inherent vulnerability of developing neural tissue may further amplify this risk. However, a singular attribution to radiation alone is often insufficient [13]. Chemotherapeutic agents, particularly alkylating agents and topoisomerase inhibitors, possess intrinsic DNA-damaging and mutagenic potential, contributing to genomic instability and carcinogenesis [14, 15], Although some epidemiological studies have not identified a strong independent association between chemotherapy and second CNS tumors [16], its synergistic or additive role in the context of other risk factors cannot be discounted, as evidenced by the chemotherapeutic exposures in our cohort. Critically, underlying tumor predisposition syndromes form a crucial foundational layer of risk. Germline mutations in genes such as TP53 (as identified in Case 1 of our series, confirming Li-Fraumeni syndrome) dramatically increase lifetime susceptibility to multiple primary malignancies. In such a genetically susceptible background, the DNA-damaging effects of both radiotherapy and chemotherapy may synergistically overwhelm DNA repair mechanisms, accelerating the accumulation of oncogenic hits [15]. This multifactorial interplay—genetic susceptibility, chemotoxicity, and radiation injury—likely converges to drive the genomic instability observed in therapy-related neoplasms [9]. Molecular alterations commonly detected, such as PDGFRA amplification, may thus represent a final common pathway activated by this convergent oncogenic stress, rather than a specific signature of radiation alone.

The classic Cahan criteria provide a valuable clinical framework for associating a second tumor with prior radiotherapy [17]. Indeed, the cases of second malignant neoplasms in this series cannot be simply attributed to radiation alone. Some cases in our series fulfill these criteria, including location within the prior radiation field, sufficient latency, and histological distinction from the primary tumor. However, it is imperative to note that while these criteria support a therapy-related etiology, they do not establish radiation as the sole causative agent, especially when patients have also received potentially mutagenic chemotherapy or harbor germline cancer predisposition syndromes, as seen in our cohort. The molecular profile of our cases, including PDGFRA alterations, shows consistency with the patterns reported in radiation-associated gliomas [18]. This molecular overlap, however, may reflect shared downstream oncogenic pathways activated by diverse genotoxic insults (including radiation and chemotherapy), rather than specifying a unique radiogenic origin.Ionizing radiation is a well-established carcinogen, and its core mechanism for inducing tumorigenesis lies in damaging the DNA of normal cells and subsequent misrepair [19, 20]. However, the difference in the incidence proportion between RIGs and Radiation-Induced Meningiomas (RIMs) suggests potentially distinct pathogenic pathways and inducing conditions behind them [21]. Existing evidence indicates that the occurrence of RIMs is often associated with low-to-moderate dose radiation exposure [22]. In contrast, the occurrence of RIGs, particularly glioblastomas, is closely linked to high-dose radiotherapy.

The predominance of RIGs in our study cohort likely profoundly reflects the unique composition of patients treated at our center. Our center receives a large number of patients with primary intracranial malignant tumors (e.g., high-grade glioma, medulloblastoma, germ cell tumors) who require high-dose, large-volume brain radiotherapy [23, 24]. All patients with second tumors in this study received primary tumor radiotherapy doses consistent with the high-dose characteristics known to induce RIGs. Therefore, our patient population itself represents the highest risk group for developing RIGs regardless of whether radiotherapy is the sole contributing factor.

Furthermore, the following factors may have also contributed to our observations: First, survival and follow-up bias: High-grade gliomas progress rapidly and are more likely to cause clinical symptoms and be detected during the follow-up period. In contrast, many RIMs are slow-growing and asymptomatic. If patients have a short overall survival due to poor prognosis from their primary disease, these latent RIMs might remain undetected and thus be underestimated in the statistics [5–7, 22]. Second, diagnostic and reporting bias: A new, space-occupying lesion with malignant imaging features (like a RIG) immediately attracts significant clinical attention and is pathologically confirmed via surgery or biopsy. Conversely, a stable, small meningioma might only be recorded for observation and not included in the analysis as a significant event, potentially leading to its underreporting compared to RIGs.

The findings of this study emphasize the necessity of lifelong surveillance for survivors of childhood CNS malignancies who have received high-dose radiotherapy, with particular vigilance for therapy-related high-grade gliomas. In clinical decision-making, it is essential to comprehensively consider genetic background, optimize radiotherapy techniques to spare normal tissue, and explore chemotherapy-based radiotherapy de-escalation strategies where oncologically appropriate. Future efforts should focus on developing integrated risk-stratification models that incorporate genetic profiles and treatment history to guide personalized follow-up.

Study limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective, single-center design and small sample size. The heterogeneity in primary diagnoses and treatment regimens, along with incomplete germline and somatic molecular profiling for all patients, precludes definitive causal inference or precise quantification of individual risk factors. These constraints highlight the need for prospective, multi-institutional cohorts with comprehensive molecular characterization to better elucidate the etiology of therapy-related second malignancies.

Conclusion

In summary, this case series underscores that therapy-related high-grade gliomas likely arise from a multifactorial interplay of genetic susceptibility, prior chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. Moving forward, a holistic risk-management approach—integrating genetic assessment, treatment optimization, and structured long-term surveillance—is crucial for mitigating this severe late effect in susceptible populations.
