CD40 agonism in renal cell carcinoma enhances immune checkpoint activity through myeloid cells
Authors: Dijana Djureinovic, Jacqueline E. Mann, Jasmine I. Caulfield, Dorin Bar-Ziv, Lin Zhang, Kelly Olino, Kathryn Miller-Jensen, Susan M. Kaech, Marcus Bosenberg, Lucia Jilaveanu, David A. Schoenfeld, Harriet M. Kluger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryImmune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has improved survival for patients with metastatic renal cell carcinoma (RCC), but there remains a need to overcome resistance mechanisms. CD40 agonists increase anti-tumoral immunity, but limited data are available on their activity in RCC. We evaluated CD40 agonism in combination with ICB in preclinical RCC models. Adding CD40 agonism to anti-CTLA-4, but not anti-PD-1, improved survival in Renca-bearing mice. CD40 agonism and anti-CTLA-4 upregulated CCL2 and increased intra-tumoral macrophages and type 1 conventional dendritic cells (cDC1), whereas polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs) decreased compared with control. Neutralizing CCL2 with CD40 agonism and CTLA-4 blockade partially abrogated the anti-tumoral effect and led to less increase in cDC1 and less decrease in PMN-MDSC populations. Our studies suggest that CCL2, at least partially, mediates the anti-tumoral effects of CD40 agonism, and more importantly, that the addition of CD40 agonism to ICB, particularly anti-CTLA-4, might be beneficial in RCC.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) targeting PD-1 with or without anti-CTLA-4 or anti-angiogenic agents is the backbone for the treatment of advanced renal cell carcinoma (RCC).1,2,3 While some patients have durable responses, a significant portion of them have insufficient or no response and the majority develops resistance over time.4,5 Thus, there is an unmet need to overcome resistance mechanisms to further improve survival in this disease. CD40 is a co-stimulatory receptor found on both immune and non-immune cells, with high expression levels on antigen-presenting cells.6 The CD40 ligand (CD40L) is mainly expressed on activated CD4 T cells.6 Activation of CD40 through CD40-CD40L binding induces proinflammatory responses and stimulates anti-tumor immunity.7 CD40 activation on dendritic cells (DCs) and monocytes increases antigen presentation, leads to DC maturation, and activates CD8 T cells.8 On macrophages and mature DCs, CD40 activation promotes IL-12 secretion that is important for cytotoxic T cell effects, T helper type 1 phenotype polarization, and natural killer (NK) cell-mediated anti-tumoral effects.9,10 In response to CD40 activation, macrophages produce cytokines such as TNF-α, CCL5, and IL-6.9,10 While TNF-α and CCL5 are associated with improved survival and tumor regression following CD40 therapy in preclinical models, more recent studies on IL-6 in patients with RCC have indicated an association with worse outcome in response to ICB.10,11 CD40 is also expressed on several types of tumor cells, including RCC in which higher expression has been associated with better survival.12,13 Some studies have shown that DCs and CD8 T cells are crucial for successful CD40-mediated anti-tumor immunity.14,15 However, in some tumor models, tumor regression was reported to be independent of T cells and largely driven by macrophages and other monocyte-derived cells.16 CD40 activating therapies are under evaluation in early phase clinical trials. The various methods to activate CD40-CD40L signaling have included recombinant CD40L, gene therapy, and agonistic antibodies, with the latter being the most common approach. To augment efficacy, CD40 agonists have been evaluated in combination with other therapies, such as DC vaccination, chemotherapy, chemoradiation, and immunotherapy including anti-PD-1.17,18,19,20,21 We have previously reported a phase 2 trial evaluating a combination of CD40 agonist (sotigalimab) with a PD-1 inhibitor (nivolumab) in patients with advanced melanoma that had progressed on anti-PD-1, showing durable responses in a subset of patients.22 We also conducted a phase 1 trial using two myeloid-targeting drugs, sotigalimab and an antibody against CSF1R (cabiralizumab) with or without PD-1 inhibitor (nivolumab) in melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and RCC patients who progressed on anti-PD-1/PD-L1. While both regimens were safe and some patients obtained durable and prolonged responses, the anti-tumor activity in these trials and other early phase studies evaluating CD40 agonism with chemotherapy and/or anti-PD-1 has generally been modest.23,24

Here, we focused on RCC and analyzed the expression of CD40 and CD40L in human samples. We evaluated the effect of CD40 agonism with single and dual ICB in preclinical models of RCC and analyzed immune-related changes after therapy in tumor tissue and in circulation. Our specimen-profiling studies suggest that CCL2 might be playing a key role, and CCL2 neutralization appears to abrogate the effects of CD40 agonist-containing regimens.

Results

CD40 and CD40L expression in human RCC and association with clinical outcome

We utilized data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) to analyze mRNA expression of CD40 and CD40L in human cancers. The mRNA expression of both CD40 and CD40L was highly elevated in both non-clear cell and clear cell RCC compared with other tumor types (Figures 1A and 1B). We used digital spatial profiling (DSP) to analyze CD40 expression levels in RCC tumor tissue. Primary RCC tumors had significantly higher expression of CD40 than adjacent histologically normal kidney in patient-matched tissues (p < 0.05, Figure 1C). There was no significant difference either in CD40 expression between patient-matched primary and metastatic tumors (Figure 1D) or in tumors before and after treatment with ICB (Figure 1E). This suggests that immune checkpoint inhibition alone might not be enough to increase CD40 expression in RCC tumors. We then analyzed the levels of CD40 and CD40L in plasma obtained from RCC patients treated with ipilimumab and nivolumab in the frontline setting (Table S1). Higher baseline levels of soluble CD40 were observed in patients with disease who were responsive to or stable on immune checkpoint inhibitors, although this difference did not reach statistical significance in this small patient cohort (n = 19) (p = 0.09, Figure 1F). CD40 agonism in combination with immune checkpoint inhibition improves survival in murine RCC models

We hypothesized that CD40 signaling pathway stimulation might synergize with ICB and increase survival in RCC. Anti-CD40 (αCD40) was evaluated as monotherapy and in combination with ICB in two syngeneic immunocompetent murine RCC models, Renca and RAG. Treatment schedule, spider plots, and tumor growth over time and survival are shown in Figures S1 and 2A for Renca and in Figure S2 for RAG. We observed relative insensitivity to αPD-1 alone and limited sensitivity to αPD-1 + αCTLA-4 in Renca in the survival analysis (10% complete response, p < 0.05 for the comparison between PBS and αPD-1 + αCTLA-4) (Figure 2A; Figures S1B and S1C). The addition of αCD40 enhanced survival (Figure 2A; Figures S1B and S1C). Of note, αCD40 monotherapy resulted in survival in 20% of mice, and the addition of αCTLA-4 to αCD40 improved the survival rates to 35%, whereas no significant difference in survival was observed upon the addition of αPD-1 to αCD40 (Figure 2A). These results suggest that αCTLA-4 might be a better partner for αCD40 than αPD-1. RAG tumors are known to be highly sensitive to ICB alone.25 RAG-bearing mice treated with PD-1 blockade alone exhibited survival in 50%. The survival rate reached 90% with the addition of αCTLA-4 (Figure S2B). CD40 agonism, alone or in combination with immune checkpoint inhibition, induces a pro-inflammatory profile in the circulation and strong upregulation of CCL2

To investigate the augmented immune response associated with αPD-1, αCTLA-4, αCD40, and all combinations of two or three drugs, we collected peripheral blood from mice (n = 3 per treatment group) 24 h after one treatment and analyzed systemic cytokine and chemokine secretion. Clustering analysis revealed the upregulation of cytokines and chemokines involved in innate and adaptive immune responses, with the most dramatic changes seen in treatments that contained αCD40 (Figure 2B). We analyzed the fold change of cytokines and chemokines in mice treated with αCD40 (alone or in combination) versus mice not treated with αCD40 (αPD-1 and αCTLA-4 alone or in combination). A fold change of >3 was observed for RANTES (CCL5), IL-6, G-CSF, IFN-γ, IP-10 (CXCL10), and CCL2 (MCP-1) (p < 0.05). The highest fold change was observed for CCL2 (7.17-fold change, Figure 2C). Levels of each analyte in respective treatment group are shown in Figure S3.

CD40 agonism in combination with immune checkpoint inhibition increases macrophage recruitment to the tumor

Following αCD40 treatment, we found large increases in cytokines and chemokines that were involved in the recruitment of macrophages/monocytes and T cells. To analyze treatment-related changes in tissues, we collected tumors 24 h after one treatment. We stained for F4/80 that is highly expressed on cells of the monocyte and macrophage lineage in mice. Tumor tissue from the mice treated with αCD40, in combination with αCTLA-4, αPD-1 or the combination, had the highest percentage of F4/80+ cells in the tumor microenvironment (Figures 2D and 2E).

Changes induced by CD40 agonism in cells in the tumor microenvironment using single-cell RNA sequencing

We performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of Renca tumors collected 24 h after two treatments (Figure 3A). The different cell types identified are illustrated in Figure 3B. A comparison of the proportion of different infiltrating immune cell types revealed a relative increase in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and granulocytes after αCD40 treatment with αPD-1 or αCTLA-4 (Figure 3C). These granulocytes were characterized by a high expression of Ccr2 (one of the receptors for Ccl2), Apoe, and Mrc1 (Cd206). Cd40 was found to be predominantly expressed on B cells, a subset of TAMs, and RCC tumor cells, whereas Ccl2 had a broader expression (Figure 3D). TAM clusters are shown in Figure S4A. All TAM clusters expressed Cd68, Cd14, Apoe, Mrc1, Ccr2, and Ccl2 except for the mitochondrial subsets not included in Figure S4A. A relative increase in TAM subsets encoding key components of MHC class II involved in antigen presentation (H2-Ab1, H2-Eb1, and H2-Aa), TAMs expressing genes involved in extracellular matrix remodeling (Ctsk and Mmp9), and TAMs with high expression of C1Q genes, Ccl8 and Ccl12, were observed after treatment with αCD40-containing double therapies (αCD40 + αPD-1 and αCD40 + αCTLA-4). Interferon-activated TAMs had relative high expression in mice treated with αPD-1 + αCTLA-4 and all three drugs together (Figure S4A). A relative increase in CD8 T cells, CD4 T cells, and T regulatory cells (Tregs) was observed with αPD-1 + αCTLA-4 and the triple combination. To analyze changes in T cell subsets in greater depth, we employed the annotation tool “ProjecTILs.” The proportion of CD8 naive-like, CD4 naive-like, and CD8 effector memory cells was the highest in tumors treated with αPD-1 + αCTLA-4 and the triple combination. The majority of T cells were Tregs in all samples (Figure S4B). NK cells were increased with αPD-1 + αCTLA-4 and were characterized by genes encoding cytotoxic effector molecules (Gzma, Gzmb, and Prf1). Neutralizing CCL2 partially abrogates the anti-tumoral effect of CD40 agonism with CTLA-4 blockade

αCD40 induces the expression of CCL2 in blood in both mice and humans.23,26,27 We observed strong upregulation of CCL2 following αCD40 therapy, both in the circulation and at the transcript level in murine tumors, along with increased macrophage infiltration, which prompted us to analyze whether CCL2 is a critical component of the αCD40-mediated anti-tumor response. Adding a CCL2 blocking antibody to the combination of αCD40, αPD-1, and αCTLA-4 did not significantly impact survival, likely due to the potent effects of these three drugs in combination and the substantial effects of these drugs on T cells (data not shown). Tumor and immune cell changes resulting from CCL2 inhibition

Tumors collected 24 h after the second treatment with αCD40 and αCTLA-4, with or without αCCL2 were stained for hematoxylin and eosin, CD3, and FOXP3. Tumor tissue from both treatment groups had a significantly higher necrotic component than untreated tumors (p < 0.05, Figure 4B). An example of a tumor tissue with predominantly necrotic tumor is shown in Figure 4C. There is a clear difference in the CD3 positivity between the intact tumor and the necrotic tumor (Figure S5A). Percentage of CD3+ cells and FOXP3+ cells increased in both treatment groups compared with untreated tumors (not significant) (Figure S5B).

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) collected after the two treatments and at endpoint were analyzed via flow cytometry for myeloid cell populations in untreated mice and the mice treated with αCD40 and αCTLA-4, with or without CCL2 inhibition. There were no significant differences in myeloid cell populations between the control and treatments with αCD40 + αCTLA-4 and αCD40 + αCTLA-4 + αCCL2 after the two treatments. There was a trend toward increases in total DCs, conventional type 2 DCs (cDC2), and plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs) in both treated groups compared with untreated mice (p = 0.07 and p = 0.06, Figure S6A). A trend was observed toward a decrease in polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs; CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6Clow/intermediate) and activated PMNs (Ly6G+CD11b+) after αCD40 + αCTLA-4 treatment compared with the control (p = 0.08 and p = 0.06, Figure S6A). At endpoint, there was a significantly higher percentage of conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1) (XCR1+CD172a−) after αCD40 + αCTLA-4 treatment compared with the control (Figure 4D) (p < 0.05). Treatment with αCD40 + αCTLA-4 significantly reduced PMN-MDSCs and activated PMNs compared with those in untreated mice (p < 0.05, both comparisons). There was a trend toward increases in monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) after αCD40 + αCTLA-4 + αCCL2 treatment compared with untreated mice (p = 0.06) (Figure 4D). There were no significant differences in myeloid cell populations between the αCD40 + αCTLA-4 and αCD40 + αCTLA-4 + αCCL2 treatment groups (Figure 4D; Figure S6B).

Discussion

Overcoming resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors is critical for extending survival in patients with RCC. Activation of the CD40-CD40L pathway using CD40 agonists in combination with checkpoint blockade has been studied in several cancer types, but little is known about the importance of this pathway in RCC.

Combining dual checkpoint blockade (αPD-1 and αCTLA-4) with CD40 agonism has previously been evaluated in murine melanoma, where additive activity was shown in the αPD-1- and αCTLA-4-resistant B16 tumor model.28 Here, we analyzed the activity of CD40 agonists with ICB in the Renca model, which has relative resistance to αPD-1, αCTLA-4, and the combination thereof. CD40 agonism significantly improved survival when combined with αCTLA-4 and was effective with or without αPD-1. The effects were less striking in the immune checkpoint inhibitor-sensitive kidney cancer model, RAG. CD40 agonism with αCTLA-4 has been evaluated in a phase 1 trial in melanoma patients, where it resulted in 9.1% complete response and 18.2% partial response.29 The majority of clinical studies with CD40 agonism and ICB have targeted the PD-1/PD-L1 axis. Our data suggest that αCTLA-4 might be a better combination partner than αPD-1 in RCC. This might be due to the enhanced activity of both αCD40 and αCTLA-4 acting early on in the immunity cycle and resulting in stronger response than with PD-1 blockade that acts later.

Following initial treatment with CD40 agonist therapy and ICB, we observed an increase both in circulating CCL2 and at the mRNA transcriptional level in tumors. Both Ccl2 and its receptor Ccr2 were globally expressed on TAMs, as shown in our scRNA-seq data, and not restricted to a specific subset of macrophages. Ccl2 was also expressed on tumor cells and granulocytes. CCL2 has pleiotropic functions, with broad and complex roles in the tumor microenvironment. Consistent with its potent monocyte/macrophage recruitment ability, we observed an increase in macrophages in Renca tumor tissue after initial treatment with CD40 agonists. Increase in CCL2 following CD40 agonist therapy has previously been observed in a preclinical model of melanoma.14 We and others have reported an upregulation of CCL2 in humans following a CD40 agonist-containing regimen.23,27 Renca tumors exhibited a greater degree of necrosis after treatment with CD40 agonism and CTLA-4 blockade. Tumor necrosis following CD40 agonist treatment alone or in combination with other immune-based strategies has been observed in murine pancreatic and melanoma models.14,27 The necrotic component and improvement in survival of tumor-bearing mice were significantly reduced with the neutralization of CCL2, indicating that CCL2 could be an important mediator of anti-tumor activity of CD40 agonists. Previously, CCL2 has been implicated in CD40 agonist-mediated anti-fibrotic activity in murine pancreatic carcinoma.27 Degradation of extracellular matrix was dependent on CCL2 and IFN-γ and resulted from the recruitment of Ly6C+ inflammatory monocytes/macrophages.27

CCL2 polarizes macrophages to pro-inflammatory phenotypes and promotes immune tumor infiltration.30 Others, however, have shown that in certain contexts, CCL2 can tilt the balance of macrophage activity toward immune suppression.31 These pleiotropic effects require further investigation. In our study, we observed that the treatment with αCD40-containing double therapies increased TAM subsets involved in antigen presentation as well as TAMs that are involved in extracellular matrix degradation and remodeling, which primarily have been associated with pro-tumoral effects.32 However, in a phase 1b/2 study that evaluated a CD40 agonist in combination with chemotherapy in patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, baseline tumors from patients with better outcomes overexpressed genes involved in extracellular matrix degradation and a gene signature involved in monocyte chemotaxis that includes Ccl2 and complement activation.18 In a phase I study of a CD40 agonist and CSF1R blockade with or without nivolumab in patients resistant to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, we observed higher baseline CCL2 levels in plasma from patients with better outcome after CD40 agonist-containing treatment (data not published).

Our preclinical studies show, however, that in the setting of CD40 agonism and CTLA-4 inhibition in RCC, CCL2 plays a key pro-inflammatory role in macrophage activation. While our study did not explore the precise mechanism by which CCL2 is involved in anti-tumor responses driven by CD40 agonism, our data highlight the importance of myeloid cells and their polarization to an active state by CD40 agonism. We show that CCL2 signaling affects type 1 DCs and MDSCs, as the levels of both were changed following CCL2 blockade. While we do not show data to support T cell immune suppression, our flow cytometry data indicate that myeloid cells are the main cell population involved.

Additional studies with a CCL2-knockout mouse model are necessary to investigate CCL2 signaling in the context of CD40 agonism. Here, we used a syngeneic model in which the necrotic component was quite high following CD40 agonism. Using an orthotopic model and models of distant metastases might give additional insights into the tumor microenvironment that more closely mimics the human setting and would be less necrotic. Moreover, emerging technologies such as spatial transcriptomics might enable us to analyze small tumors at different time points, focusing on necrotic vs. non-necrotic areas. These results may further guide myeloid targeting strategies to enhance CD40 agonism. Importantly, our results suggest that CD40 agonism with ICB should be further explored in RCC as it has the potential to improve response rates and survival in patients treated with these combinations.

Limitations of the study

We show that the neutralization of CCL2 with CD40 agonism and CTLA4 blockade partly abrogates the anti-tumoral effect of CD40 agonism in Renca models and that the neutralization of CCL2 impacts the numbers of DCs and MDSCs in the circulation. Additional studies with a CCL2-knockout mouse model or the knockout of CCL2 are necessary to investigate the more precise role of CCL2 signaling in the context of CD40 agonism. Here, we used the syngeneic Renca model whose genetics differ from those of human RCC. Using an orthotopic model and models of distant metastases might give additional insights into the tumor microenvironment that more closely mimics the human setting, as subcutaneous injections do not represent metastatic dissemination. Additionally, we used only male mice in these studies, which can impact the translation of our results to human patients.

Experimental model and study participant details

In-vivo animal studies

Seven-to nine-week-old healthy male BALB/c mice of normal weight were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Five mice were housed per cage in appropriate environment with specific pathogen-free conditions. For establishment of a subcutaneous Renca tumor model, 5 × 105 Renca cells were resuspended in 100 μL of PBS and subcutaneously injected into the mice. For establishment of the RAG tumor model, 1 × 106 RAG cells were resuspended in 100 μL of PBS and subcutaneously injected into the mice.

Ten days after tumor cell injection, tumors were 30 mm3–100 mm3. Mice were excluded if tumor was smaller than 30 mm3. The remaining mice were randomized to 10 groups (ctrl (PBS), anti-PD-1, anti-CD40, anti-CTLA-4, anti-CD40 and -CTLA-4, anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4, anti-PD-1 and anti-CD40 and anti-PD-1, anti-CD40 and anti-CTLA-4). For experiments with CCL2 neutralizing antibody, mice were randomized to 3 groups: ctrl (PBS), anti-CD40 and anti-CTLA-4 and anti-CD40, anti-CTLA-4 and anti-CCL2. Treatment started at day 8-day 10 post tumor cell injection and was administered intraperitoneally twice a week for a total of 5 doses with the following antibodies and doses: 200 μg αPD-1 (Bio X Cell, Clone RMP1-14), 200 μg αCD40 (Bio X Cell, Clone: FGK4.5/FGK45), 200 μg αCTLA-4 (BioXCell, clone 9H10) and 200 μg αCCL2 (Bio X Cell, Clone: 2H5). Control groups were injected with sterile PBS. Power calculations (80% power) indicated that 10–15 mice per group would be required to reach significance level 0.05 based on the expected effect size and variability. Tumors were measured twice weekly with a digital caliper and tumor volume estimated with the ellipsoid volume calculation formula. Endpoint was defined as the time until tumors reached 1000 mm3 or if tumors ulcerated. The investigators were not blinded to the treatment allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Since we only used male mice we have addressed this as a limitation of the study under “Limitations of the study”. All animal studies were approved by Yale University Institutional Animal care and Use Committee (IACUC) approval #2020-20152.

Cell lines

Renca cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 plus 10% FBS, 1× MEM nonessential amino acids, sodium pyruvate (1 mM), L-glutamine (2 mM), and 1× antibiotic-antimycotic. RAG cells were grown in EMEM with 1.5 g/L sodium bicarbonate, nonessential amino acids, L-glutamine, and sodium pyruvate plus 10% FBS and 1× antibiotic-antimycotic. Cells were cultured in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37°C. All cells were injected at passage <10 and tested negative for mycoplasma.

Human samples

Human EDTA-plasma samples were collected at Yale University (New Haven, Connecticut, USA) from patients with renal cell carcinoma treated with ipilimumab and nivolumab. Samples used for plasma analysis were collected at baseline (pretreatment) and at the beginning of the third cycle of therapy (approximately 6 weeks later). For two of the patients later on-treatment timepoints were collected (C4D1 and C5D1).Patient characteristics are noted in Table S1. Informed consent was obtained from all patients using a Yale University approved Investigational Review Board protocol. Patient characteristics are shown in Table S1.

Method details

The cancer genome atlas data analysis

mRNA data from PanCancer Atlas was downloaded from the cBioPortal. mRNA expression z-scores relative to normal samples (log RNA Seq V2 RSEM) data, processed and normalized was analyzed in GraphPad Prism. See the cBioPortal User Guide for more information on the RNA data available.

Digital spatial profiling

Tissue microarrays (TMAs) were generated as previously described.33,34,35 YTMA-84 consists of matched adjacent normal kidney and primary tumor pairs from renal tumors, YTMA-166 consists of matched primary tumor and metastasis pairs and YTMA-528 consists of tissue from patients who developed brain metastases. Digital spatial profiling (DSP) was performed as previously described.33,34 In brief, TMAs were incubated at 4°C overnight in a humidity chamber with fluorescently labeled antibodies for visualization of tumor-infiltrating leukocyte compartment (CD45), tumor associated macrophage compartment (CD68) and tumor compartment (pan-cytokeratin) (CK). A panel of 52 oligonucleotide-labeled antibodies with a UV-cleavable DNA barcodes was included for protein analysis (including anti-CD40). Tissue fixation and nuclear staining were performed according to manufacturer’s instructions before the slides were loaded into GeoMx DSP instrument in which the slides were scanned to obtain fluorescent images of the stained tissue cores. Based on the fluorescent staining, each region was segmented into three compartments: tumor, tumor-infiltrating leukocytes, and tumor-infiltrating macrophages. Protein expression values in each compartment were obtained, and quality control was performed as previously described.33,34 Data were normalized in DSP software (Nanostring) and obtained normalized protein expression values for CD40 were obtained in the different compartments. CD40 protein levels from the tumor compartment (CK + cells) were extracted and analyzed for this study in Graphpad Prism 10.

Cytokine/chemokine profiling

Human samples: Plasma was analyzed for cytokine/chemokine expression using the 48-plex Human Cytokine/Chemokine Discovery assay (cat#: HD48A, Eve Technologies Corp.) and single-plex analysis for soluble CD40. Murine samples: Plasma samples were collected from control or treated mice 24 h after the first treatment and profiled using the 31-plex Mouse Cytokine/Chemokine Array (cat# MD31, Eve Technologies). A fluorescence intensity value was obtained for each analyte. Concentrations for sCD40L and sCD40 were calculated with a standard curve to yield observed concentration (pg/mL). Cytokine/chemokine levels (pg/mL) were analyzed in GraphPad Prism 10. Heatmap was plotted using “pheatmap” in R Studio (version 3.6.2).

Immunohistochemistry

Tumors were collected 24 h after the 1st and 2nd treatments, and at endpoint, fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin. Five μm FFPE tumor sections were stained with antibodies targeting F4/80 (clone D2S9 R, Cell Signaling), CD3 (clone E4T1 B, Cell Signaling) and FoxP3 (clone FJK-16s, e-Bioscience). Slides were scanned by Leica AT2 scanner at 20× magnification and analyzed in QuPath. Two representative square regions (0.25mm2 each) were analyzed. The mean percentage value from each sample was analyzed in GraphPrism 10 and used to represent percentage of positive cells per sample. The percentage of positivity in each tumor area was automatically scored.

scRNA-sequencing

Tumor tissue was collected 24 h after the second treatment from three mice per treatment group and single cell suspension was prepared for RNA sequencing.24 Cells were incubated for 30 min at 4°C with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies using anti-CD45 (clone 30-F11, BD Biosciences) and anti-CD3 (clone 17A2, BD Bioscience). Samples were sorted using a BD FACSAriaII into 3 populations: T cells (CD45+CD3+), non–T immune cells (CD45+CD3–); and tumor and stromal cells (CD45– CD3–). AmCyan Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for live/dead staining. Sorted cells for each subset were counted manually and combined in a 2:1:1 ratio of T cells/non-T immune cells/tumor and stromal cells, with an equal contribution from each biologic replicate from an experimental condition. Ten thousand cells from each of the mixed sorted samples for each condition were loaded onto the 10× Genomics Chromium System. Library preparation for scRNA-Seq was performed using the 3′ Reagent Kit from 10× Genomics according to the manufacturer’s instructions by the Yale Center for Genome Analysis (YCGA) and passed quality control. cDNA libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq instrument (Illumina). CellRanger was used to align reads to the mouse reference transcriptome (mm9) to generate cell-by-gene matrices for each sample library.

Digital count matrices were analyzed to identify cell types using Seurat package (version 4.0.5) in R Studio (version 4.1.2). To remove low quality cells, the following thresholds were applied: >500 nUMI,>250 genes,>0.8 log10GeneperUMI and <0.2 mitochondrial gene ratio and only genes expressed in 10 or more cells. Cell cycle scoring was performed using the CellCycleScoring command using mouse gene sets orthologous to gene sets previously described. Cell cycle factors were regressed out using the “sctransform” function, and the data were normalized and integrated on the 3,000 most variable features. Principal component (PC) scores from the first 40 PCs were used for clustering with the FindClusters command, and a resolution of 0.6. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used for dimensionality reduction. Cell type assignments of clusters were performed using SingleR, analysis of expression patterns of canonical markers and by assessment of the top 5 conserved markers per cluster from the FindConservedMarkers function. Gene expression UMAP plots were generated using the FeaturePlot command. Cluster frequencies by experimental condition were normalized to the total number of cells per condition. The top differentially expressed genes comparing a single cluster to all other clusters were computed using the FindAllMarkers function and the data were scaled. Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using EnrichR. T cell subsets were annotated using ProjecTILS and a reference mouse tumor-infiltrating T cell dataset.

Flow cytometry

PBMCs collected 24 h after the 2nd treatment and at endpoint were used for immune-profiling using flow cytometry as previously described.36 In brief, primary-conjugated antibodies were used to stain the cells for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. Data were analyzed using Cytobank (10.7).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Mann–Whitney test and unpaired t test were used to analyze mean CD40 protein levels obtained by digital spatial profiling in normal and adjacent ccRCC primary tumors and levels of soluble CD40 and soluble CD40L in plasma from RCC patients (∗p < 0.05).

Log rank statistics were used for survival analysis (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p=<0.005, ∗∗∗∗p=<0.0001). Mixed effect analysis was used to analyze tumor growth over time, comparing the mean tumor volume of each treatment with the mean tumor volume of every other treatment with Tukey’s correction for multiple comparisons (∗∗p = 0.001,∗∗∗p=<0.001, ns = not significant). Unpaired t test was used to analyze the difference in necrosis and positive cells stained for IHC analysis in tumors (∗p < 0.05). Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare mean rank of each group with the mean rank of every other group to analyze differences in PBMC populations with Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗∗∗p=<0.0001. Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test was used for scRNAseq data to identify distinct cell types. A p value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
