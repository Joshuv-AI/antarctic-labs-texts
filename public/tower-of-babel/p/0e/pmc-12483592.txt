Peritoneal dialysis-associated peritonitis due to Nocardia farcinica: A case report
Authors: Mieko Tokano, Norihito Tarumoto, Hisashi Furukawa, Yuya Kusuyama, Mizuya Ito, Haruko Oba, Hikaru Yamazaki, Ryosuke Arika, Masahiro Suda, Minako Tanioka, Hirokazu Okada, Shigefumi Maesaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nocardia spp. are Gram-positive aerobic actinomycetes that are widely found in everyday environments, such as soil, vegetables, and water. They are known to cause infections in the lungs, central nervous system, and skin. However, peritonitis caused by Nocardia spp. is rare. We report a case of peritoneal dialysis-associated peritonitis caused by Nocardia farcinica in a 53-year-old woman. She had been undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD) for approximately two years because of diabetic nephropathy. Gram staining of the PD effluent revealed the presence of Gram-positive rods. Because species-level identification could not be achieved using and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry, next-generation sequencing was performed and the isolate was identified as N. farcinica. The PD catheter was removed and hemodialysis was initiated. She received intravenous amikacin, sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (SMX/TMP), and imipenem/cilastatin for approximately two months, followed by oral SMX/TMP and minocycline for four months after discharge. The N. farcinica isolate from our patient showed good antimicrobial susceptibility to various antimicrobials, including SMX/TMP (minimum inhibitory ≤40 µ g/mL). This case highlights that N. farcinica can cause peritoneal dialysis catheter–associated peritonitis. Although reports of N. farcinica-related peritonitis are extremely limited, the species is associated with a higher mortality rate than other Nocardia spp. Therefore, appropriate source control and at least six months of antimicrobial therapy are recommended. Additionally, SMX/TMP monotherapy should be avoided as empiric therapy because N. farcinica may exhibit resistance.

Introduction

Peritoneal dialysis (PD)-associated peritonitis can lead to a decreased peritoneal membrane function, catheter removal, transition to hemodialysis, progression to encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis (EPS), and even death. Therefore, the prevention and early treatment of infections are essential [1], [2]. The most common routes of infection are touch contamination and extension from exit sites or tunnels. Occasionally, translocation from the gastrointestinal tract or hematogenous spread causes endogenous infections [3]. Gram-positive cocci are the most common pathogens causing PD-associated peritonitis, followed by Gram-negative rods [4], [5].

Nocardia spp. are Gram-positive aerobic actinomycetes commonly isolated from soil, vegetables, and water [6], [7]. Nocardia spp. infect the lungs (39 %), followed by the central nervous system (9 %), and skin (8 %) [8]. Infection with Nocardia spp. has been reported in immunocompromised patients; however, PD-associated peritonitis due to Nocardia spp. is extremely rare. We herein report a case of PD-associated peritonitis caused by Nocardia farcinica.

Case

A 53-year-old woman presented to our hospital with abdominal distension, vomiting, and headache. She had been undergoing PD for approximately two years because of diabetic nephropathy. Ten days prior to admission, while bathing, the cap at the end of her PD catheter was accidentally detached while she was soaking in the bathtub. She also reported impaired outflow of the PD effluent. On admission, she was alert and oriented, with a body temperature of 38.9°C. No meningeal irritation was observed. The patient’s blood test results are presented in Table 1. The PD effluent was cloudy with a white blood cell count of 634/μL (57.4 % polymorphonuclear leukocytes). A non-contrast CT scan revealed encapsulated fluid collection at the tip of the PD catheter, with surrounding fat stranding, suggestive of intra-abdominal infection (Fig. 1). She was admitted with PD-related peritonitis. She did not present with any respiratory symptoms, and chest CT revealed no abnormalities. Gram staining of the PD effluent revealed Gram-positive rods, and the culture became positive on day 5. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Bruker, Germany; Library 11) performed on colonies obtained from the PD effluent culture identified the organism as Nocardia spp., although species-level identification was not achieved. The results of the susceptibility testing are shown in Table 2. Blood cultures yielded no pathogenic organisms, and a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination showed no abnormalities. Initially, she received intraperitoneal cefazolin and ceftazidime in the dialysate, targeting the common skin flora and Escherichia coli. After detection of Nocardia spp., she was switched to intravenous amikacin, sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim (SMX/TMP), and imipenem/cilastatin. We performed next-generation sequencing (NGS) using colonies obtained from the PD effluent culture and de novo assembly was performed using SPAdes (https://cab.spbu.ru/software/spades/) based on the sequencing data obtained via the iSeq 100 system (Illumina, USA). The obtained sequences (DDBJ accession no. DRR658037) were compared with the NCBI nt/nr database using BLASTn. The 16S rRNA, gyrB, hsp65, SOD, and sacA1 genes of the cultured pathogen were 100 % identical to N. farcinica (NCBI accession No. NC_006361.1). Furthermore, a comparison of the de novo assembled whole genome with the reference sequence of N. farcinica (NCBI accession no. NC_006361.1), using average nucleotide identity, confirmed a 98.87 % match. On the basis of these findings, the isolate was identified as N. farcinica.

The patient received intravenous amikacin (200 mg after each dialysis session), SMX/TMP (160 mg/day as trimethoprim), and imipenem/cilastatin (0.25 g/day) for approximately two months. Her serum amikacin levels were maintained within the therapeutic range, and she did not experience any adverse effects. Her general condition improved, and she was discharged in good health. After discharge, the patient continued oral SMX/TMP and minocycline (100 mg/day) for four months. After treatment initiation, the patient’s PD effluent culture remained negative.

Discussion

Nocardia spp. includes more than 100 different organisms, with the most important species involved in human disease being those previously grouped under the Nocardia asteroides complex, such as N. farcinica and Nocardia nova. In addition, species such as Nocardia brasiliensis are also frequently isolated as causative pathogens of human infections [9], [10]. Previous studies have shown that the distribution of Nocardia spp. varies geographically. In contrast to other countries, N. farcinica is the most commonly isolated species in Japan [11].

Although the overall resistance rate to SMX/TMP among Nocardia spp. was reported to be 16.2 %, N. farcinica showed a significantly higher resistance rate of 45.3 % [12]. A previous study reported that patients infected N. farcinica had a higher mortality rate than those infected with other Nocardia spp. [13]. There are reports indicating that the mortality rate among patients infected with N. asteroides was 17.6 %, whereas that among patients infected with N. farcinica was 57.1 % [14]. Therefore, treatment should be approached with caution when N. farcinica is isolated from patient specimens. As clinical outcomes and antimicrobial susceptibilities vary by species, species-level identification of Nocardia is important. Although Nocardia spp. are generally identifiable by MALDI-TOF MS, the identification score was low in our case, making it unreliable for definitive species-level identification. Therefore, next generation sequencing (NGS) was performed. The isolated N. farcinica strain was susceptible to SMX/TMP. However, she presented with severe peritonitis, and susceptibility results were not available at the time of initial treatment. More than 90 % of N. farcinica are susceptible to both imipenem/cilastatin and amikacin [11]. Furthermore, in severe cases, combination therapy with multiple antimicrobial agents is recommended [15]. Therefore, amikacin, SMX/TMP, and imipenem/cilastatin were administered.

Peritonitis caused by Nocardia spp. is extremely rare. Most of these cases were due to N. asteroides or involved Nocardia spp. without species-level identification [16], [17], [18], [19]. The mechanism of PD-associated peritonitis caused by Nocardia spp. is thought to be, in most cases, bacterial invasion into the peritoneal cavity via the catheter. However, there have been case reports suggesting the involvement of non-catheter routes, such as hematogenous dissemination [16]. In the present case, no lesions were detected outside the peritoneal cavity, and therefore, as in many other cases, the infection was considered to have occurred via the catheter.

Nocardia spp. are slow-growing and difficult to identify, which means that they may be overlooked as a cause of culture-negative peritonitis. Some reports have suggested extending the culture incubation period to improve detection [18]. Peritonitis caused by N. farcinica has been reported in only three cases, including ours (Table 3) [10], [20]. Two previously reported cases of N. farcinica peritonitis were associated with peritoneal dialysis. One patient died during treatment. In that case, a mass was also observed in the lungs; however, linezolid was the only antimicrobial agent administered systemically. In another reported case, no lesions were found outside of the abdominal cavity. The patient improved with the administration of SMX/TMP and removal of the catheter, and was subsequently transitioned to hemodialysis. However, as the case dates back to 1992, and neither genetic analysis nor mass spectrometry-based species identification was performed, the possibility that the causative organism was a different Nocardia species cannot be ruled out. Treatment for Nocardia infections generally requires a duration of six months or longer [21], and our patient received six months of therapy. This case has a limitation. By the time the patient was admitted to our hospital, the bathtub at her home had already been cleaned, and therefore, environmental sampling could not be performed. Clinically, however, the infection of the PD catheter was considered to have resulted from contamination during bathing. This case highlights the potential of N. farcinica infection to cause peritonitis. Although reports of N. farcinica peritonitis are rare, this species is known to be associated with higher mortality in comparison to other Nocardia spp. Therefore, appropriate source control and antimicrobial therapy for at least six months are recommended. As N. farcinica can exhibit resistance to SMX/TMP, monotherapy with SMX/TMP should be avoided. Moreover, if the incubation period is too short, Nocardia may cause culture-negative PD-associated peritonitis, potentially leading to inappropriate empiric therapy. Therefore, when no organisms are detected during the standard incubation period, extending the incubation time or taking other appropriate measures is necessary.
