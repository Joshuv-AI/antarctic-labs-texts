Implementation of multisectoral interventions to improve nutrition in Uganda: stakeholder perceptions and experiences
Authors: Edgar Agaba, Peter Rukundo, Per Ole Iversen, Xikombiso Mbhenyane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalnutrition in sub-Saharan Africa remains a persistent public health challenge driven by food insecurity, inadequate health services, suboptimal caregiving practices, and weak livelihoods. Addressing these complex determinants requires multisectoral interventions that integrate nutrition, health, agriculture, and community development. The Uganda Community Connector (UCC) programme was designed to address these challenges through integrated, community-based approaches. This study explored stakeholder perceptions of UCC implementation strategies using the RE-AIM framework.MethodsA cross-sectional qualitative study grounded in Participatory Action Research was conducted in six UCC implementation districts in Uganda. Data were collected through key informant interviews with policymakers, health workers, and implementers, and focus group discussions with community members, including lactating mothers, youth, and community leaders. Thematic content analysis was conducted using a combined deductive approach based on the RE-AIM framework and inductive coding for emerging themes.ResultsThe UCC programme achieved broad reach among vulnerable populations, particularly women of reproductive age, children under five years, and youth, through community mobilisation and use of local leadership and existing local systems. Study participants reported high adoption of integrated nutrition, health, WASH, agriculture, and livelihood practices among participating households, supported by participatory training and group-based platforms such as Village Savings and Loan Associations.Stakeholders reported perceived improvements in dietary practices, hygiene behaviours, health-seeking practices, women’s empowerment, and household livelihoods, alongside strengthened linkages between communities and health services. However, implementation and adoption were constrained by environmental shocks, weak infrastructure, limited resources, gender norms, and inconsistent incentives for community volunteers. Sustainability of gains was strongest where community ownership, institutional capacity, and integration into district systems were present.ConclusionThe UCC programme demonstrates that integrated, community-led, and multisectoral approaches can improve nutrition and health-related behaviours in rural settings. However, sustaining and scaling such gains requires stronger institutional integration, continued capacity strengthening, adaptive implementation strategies, and inclusive engagement of marginalized groups, particularly men and vulnerable households.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40795-026-01433-x.

Introduction

Malnutrition in sub-Saharan Africa remains a major public health challenge driven by food insecurity, weak health systems, poverty, and climate-related shocks. Despite global and regional commitments, progress in reducing malnutrition has been slow. According to FAO et al., undernourishment in sub-Saharan Africa has remained persistently high over recent decades. Over the past twenty years, the prevalence of undernourishment increased slightly from 22.0% in 2005 to 22.3% in 2024. Severe food insecurity also rose from 18.4% in 2015 to 24.4% in 2024. In contrast, stunting declined modestly from 36.0% in 2012 to 32.2% in 2024 [1], although this progress remains below the African Union target of a 40% reduction set under the Africa Regional Nutrition Strategy 2015–2025 [2].

Addressing malnutrition in this context requires multisectoral approaches that integrate agriculture, health, nutrition, and community development systems. Evidence suggests that nutrition outcomes are shaped by interconnected determinants such as food access, caregiving practices, health service delivery, and household livelihoods, making single-sector interventions insufficient to achieve sustainable impact [3–6]. As a result, integrated and nutrition-sensitive interventions have increasingly been promoted as more effective approaches to addressing the underlying causes of malnutrition [7].

However, although several studies have documented the effectiveness of multisectoral nutrition interventions in improving nutrition and health outcomes in Uganda and other low- and middle-income countries [8–11], limited evidence exists on how the Uganda UCC programme was implemented in practice and how different stakeholders experienced its implementation. Most published studies have focused primarily on programme outcomes, with less attention given to implementation processes, stakeholder experiences, and the contextual factors influencing programme delivery, adoption, and sustainability. Understanding these implementation processes is essential for explaining programme successes, challenges, and long-term sustainability beyond measured outcomes [12, 13].

In Uganda, the UCC programme was implemented as a multisectoral intervention to address malnutrition through integrated actions in nutrition, agriculture, health, and livelihoods. The programme emphasised community engagement, behaviour change communication and strengthened linkages between households and service delivery systems.

The UCC was implemented between 2012 and 2016 as a multisectoral initiative in 15 districts of western and northern Uganda [7]. The programme targeted pregnant and lactating women, children under five years, youth, and vulnerable households through integrated nutrition-specific and nutrition-sensitive interventions, including maternal and child health, agriculture, water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH), livelihoods, financial inclusion, and social behaviour change communication. Interventions were delivered through existing government and established community platforms. The programme was underpinned by a theory of change that integrated multisectoral interventions within community and government systems to strengthen household knowledge, improve access to services and productive resources, promote healthy nutrition and livelihood practices, and ultimately improve maternal and child nutrition outcomes while enhancing community and institutional capacity.

The RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance) framework, developed by Glasgow and colleagues, was designed to improve the translation of research into practice by providing a comprehensive approach for evaluating the public health impact of interventions across multiple dimensions. Reach assesses the extent to which the intended population is engaged; Effectiveness examines the intervention’s outcomes; Adoption evaluates uptake by organisations and stakeholders; Implementation considers the fidelity, quality, and consistency of programme delivery; and Maintenance assesses the sustainability of interventions over time. The framework has been widely applied in public health and implementation research to evaluate complex interventions and identify factors influencing their successful implementation and long-term sustainability [12].

The RE-AIM framework was selected because it provides a comprehensive approach for examining both programme implementation processes and perceived outcomes across multiple levels, making it particularly suitable for evaluating complex multisectoral nutrition interventions such as the UCC programme.

Although multisectoral nutrition interventions have demonstrated positive nutrition outcomes, evidence on their implementation in real-world settings remains limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. By applying the RE-AIM framework, this study contributes implementation science evidence on the factors influencing the implementation and sustainability of the UCC programme.

This study therefore explored stakeholder perceptions of the implementation and effectiveness of the UCC programme using the RE-AIM framework. By capturing experiences from policymakers, implementers, and community members, the study provides insights into facilitators and barriers of implementation and generates lessons for strengthening multisectoral nutrition programming in similar contexts [13].

Materials and methods

Study design

A cross-sectional qualitative design was used to capture differences in stakeholder perspectives. The study was grounded in Participatory Action Research (PAR), which fosters collaboration and mutual learning to explore complex realities and generate actionable insights that enhance intervention relevance, sustainability, and responsiveness [14].

Study setting

The study was conducted in six districts in Uganda where the Uganda Community Connector (UCC) programme was implemented: Agago, Dokolo, Kole, Lira, Kamwenge, and Kisoro. These districts represent diverse geographical and socio-economic contexts, including both rural and semi-rural settings, and were selected to capture variation in programme implementation and community experiences [15].

Study population and sampling

Participants were purposively selected to capture diverse perspectives across multiple stakeholder groups. Community participants included women with children under five years, men, youth, and community leaders, while key informants comprised policymakers, health workers, and programme implementers at district and national levels. Women with children under five years were purposively selected because they were the primary beneficiaries of the maternal and child nutrition interventions under the UCC programme. Purposive sampling ensured inclusion of individuals directly involved in or affected by the UCC programme.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible participants were community members and key informants with direct experience of the UCC programme. Individuals without direct programme involvement, those unable to provide informed consent, or those with symptoms consistent with COVID-19 during data collection were excluded.

Recruitment procedures

Participants were identified and approached through established community and administrative structures, including Village Health Teams (VHTs), Community Connector Officers (CCOs), parish-level leadership, and existing community groups such as savings groups and farmer groups. These structures served as entry points for identifying eligible participants, with support from UCC focal persons and Parish Chiefs.

A total of 26 stakeholders were approached for key informant interviews (KIIs), of whom 24 participated (92%). For focus group discussions (FGDs), 168 individuals were recruited, and 151 participated (90%). Participant recruitment continued until thematic saturation was achieved, with no new themes or insights emerging from subsequent interviews and focus group discussions.

Data collection

Data were collected using KIIs and FGDs to explore stakeholder perceptions and experiences. The KII and FGD guides were developed by the research team based on the RE-AIM framework and relevant literature. English-language versions of the interview guides are provided as Supplementary Files 1 and 2. Eight research assistants were trained over two days on the study objectives, research ethics, informed consent procedures, and administration of the KII and FGD guides in English and the relevant local languages. Following a competency assessment, six research assistants who demonstrated proficiency in qualitative interviewing and facilitation were selected to conduct field data collection. The KII and FGD guides were pretested with 14 participants in two non-study communities to assess clarity, contextual relevance, and usability, and were subsequently refined before data collection. The research assistants had no prior relationship with the participants, and daily debriefing meetings were conducted throughout the data collection period to review interview quality, discuss emerging issues, and ensure consistency in data collection. Data collection was conducted between June and July 2022. KIIs lasted approximately 45–60 min, while FGDs comprised eight to nine participants and lasted between 45 and 120 min. FGDs were conducted separately for lactating mothers, youth, and community leaders to encourage open discussion and ensure that participants were comfortable sharing their experiences. All interviews and discussions were audio-recorded with participants’ consent, transcribed verbatim by the trained research assistants, and translated into English where necessary. COVID-19 prevention protocols were observed throughout the data collection process [16].

Data analysis

Transcripts were cross-checked against the audio recordings for accuracy before analysis. Thematic content analysis was conducted using Dedoose software. A combined deductive and inductive approach was applied, whereby initial coding was guided by the RE-AIM framework, while additional codes and themes were allowed to emerge inductively from the data. The lead researcher conducted the primary coding in collaboration with an experienced qualitative data analyst, who reviewed the coded transcripts to ensure consistency, completeness, and accurate interpretation of the findings. The codebook was developed iteratively, beginning with the RE-AIM constructs and refined as new themes emerged during analysis. Coding discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus during regular debriefing meetings [18]. Reflexivity was enhanced through regular discussions within the research team, maintenance of analytic memos, revisiting transcripts during analysis, and ensuring that interpretations remained grounded in participants’ quotations.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval was obtained from the Stellenbosch University Health Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences, Stellenbosch University, South Africa (Reference S20/08/216), the Makerere University School of Public Health Research Ethics Committee, Makerere University, Kampala, Uganda (REC No. 940), and the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (UNCST) (HS1549ES). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before participation in the study. For participants unable to read or write, the consent form was read aloud in the local language, and consent was documented using a thumbprint in the presence of an independent witness. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed that they could withdraw at any stage without consequences. Confidentiality and anonymity were ensured by removing personal identifiers from transcripts and restricting access to data to the research team only.

Consent to publish

Not applicable.

Availability of data and materials

The datasets generated and/or analysed during the current study are not publicly available because they contain qualitative information that could compromise participant confidentiality but are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request and subject to approval by the relevant ethics committees.

Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was ensured through multiple strategies, including triangulation of data sources (KIIs and FGDs), member checking, maintenance of audit trails, reflexivity, and detailed documentation of the research process. These approaches addressed key dimensions of qualitative rigour, including credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [19].

Results and discussion

Reach of the UCC interventions

Reach refers to the extent to which the UCC intervention engaged its intended priority populations and the representativeness of those reached. Five subthemes emerged under the Reach dimension: (1a) inclusion and targeting, (1b) community entry and mobilisation, (1c) communication channels and training methods, (1d) partnerships and community engagement, and (1e) coverage and accessibility.

Inclusion and targeting

Participants consistently reported that the UCC programme reached its intended target groups, including pregnant and lactating women, children under five years, and households at heightened risk of malnutrition, while also engaging men, youth, and other community members through community-based platforms. Respondents described beneficiary identification as being guided by local implementation systems and local leaders to prioritise vulnerable households, indicating that the programme was perceived to have appropriately targeted populations with the greatest nutrition and health needs. Women of reproductive age and children under five years were consistently identified as the primary beneficiaries, reflecting the programme’s deliberate focus on populations most vulnerable to malnutrition. These findings are consistent with evidence that prioritising high-risk groups is essential for achieving meaningful nutrition outcomes [20].

Community entry and mobilization

Reach was achieved through multiple, overlapping community entry points. Beneficiaries were mobilised through nutrition education sessions, Village Savings and Loan Associations (VSLAs), farmer learning platforms, and livelihood-strengthening activities designed to improve household food security and income. These platforms enabled both individual and group-level engagement and ensured that intervention messages were delivered through trusted, pre-existing local governance structures.

The programme’s reach was further strengthened through established community governance and health systems. Participant identification and mobilisation were coordinated through VHTs, CKWs, CCOs, and local leadership structures. These actors played a central role in identifying eligible households, mobilising participants, coordinating programme activities, and supporting ongoing community engagement. By working through established and trusted community-based platforms, the programme facilitated community participation and acceptability. As highlighted by a district official:“Leaders chose Community Connector Officers and Community Knowledge Workers to coordinate and monitor the different activities going on in the various groups.” [KII, District Official, Dokolo]

“Leaders chose Community Connector Officers and Community Knowledge Workers to coordinate and monitor the different activities going on in the various groups.” [KII, District Official, Dokolo]

Communication channels and training methods

Engagement through mixed-gender groups and community dialogue platforms further facilitated collective learning, with couple-based participation emerging as an important pathway for reinforcing shared decision-making and household-level behaviour change. This group-based approach enhanced interaction within trusted local delivery mechanisms and improved uptake of recommended practices, consistent with evidence that structured community platforms enhance participation in nutrition interventions [21].

However, households targeted at the individual level sometimes required additional follow-up to achieve full participation and adoption, as household power dynamics influenced the translation of knowledge into practice. Similarly, individual-level engagement sometimes required repeated follow-up, as intra-household dynamics influenced the translation of knowledge into practice. In some cases, women reported resistance from male partners when attempting to adopt recommended nutrition behaviours, necessitating additional community-level mediation and support to strengthen acceptance. As one participant noted:“In situations where my husband expressed disbelief when I shared the knowledge I had acquired, I found it necessary to seek the assistance of a community knowledge worker to visit and help persuade him.” [FGD, lactating mother, Lira]

“In situations where my husband expressed disbelief when I shared the knowledge I had acquired, I found it necessary to seek the assistance of a community knowledge worker to visit and help persuade him.” [FGD, lactating mother, Lira]

Community engagement strategies further expanded programme reach by combining multiple communication and delivery channels, including training sessions, outreach activities, radio programmes, drama performances, music, and printed materials. These approaches were reinforced by leveraging existing village-level systems such as VSLAs and youth groups, which acted as platforms for mobilisation and dissemination. Integrated outreach services that combined nutrition education with immunisation, family planning, and livelihood support increased opportunities for contact with target populations and improved exposure to key messages. As one participant described:“Community Connector initiative appealed to all community members… fathers and mothers in our community because we were being trained on how to eat a well-balanced diet, such as vegetables and nutritious porridge, to reduce malnutrition.” [FGD, lactating mother, Agago]

“Community Connector initiative appealed to all community members… fathers and mothers in our community because we were being trained on how to eat a well-balanced diet, such as vegetables and nutritious porridge, to reduce malnutrition.” [FGD, lactating mother, Agago]

The use of culturally relevant communication channels and trusted community platforms enhanced acceptability and participation. Evidence from similar integrated interventions shows that multi-channel community engagement strategies improve awareness and uptake of health and nutrition practices [22, 23].

Coverage and accessibility

Despite these strengths, several contextual factors constrained full reach. Persistent gender norms were reported to limit equitable participation in programme activities within some households. Women described situations where resistance from male partners required additional support from community knowledge workers before recommended nutrition practices were accepted [24]. As one participant explained: “Men in the community did not fully embrace these interventions, as seen in other health programs like family planning. There is a need for targeted efforts to change men’s attitudes toward health services.” [FGD, community member, Dokolo]

“Men in the community did not fully embrace these interventions, as seen in other health programs like family planning. There is a need for targeted efforts to change men’s attitudes toward health services.” [FGD, community member, Dokolo]

These findings indicate that prevailing gender norms constrained equitable participation in programme activities.

Participants also identified geographical and infrastructure-related barriers that constrained equitable programme reach. Limited transport and inadequate infrastructure made it more difficult for community workers to reach some villages and conduct follow-up activities. As one community health worker explained:“We were not given significant amounts of money for jobs or transportation such as bicycles to help carry out grassroots intervention activities.” [Community Health Worker, Lira district]

“We were not given significant amounts of money for jobs or transportation such as bicycles to help carry out grassroots intervention activities.” [Community Health Worker, Lira district]

These logistical and infrastructure constraints reduced access to some communities and limited equitable programme coverage.

Overall, the UCC intervention achieved strong reach among priority populations through a combination of targeted beneficiary identification, embedded community delivery systems, and multi-channel engagement strategies. However, gender norms, uneven male participation, and geographic access constraints limited full equity in reach. These findings highlight the importance of leveraging community-based platforms while addressing structural and social barriers to ensure inclusive and equitable programme coverage. Effective nutrition interventions in such contexts require not only targeted outreach but also broad community engagement mechanisms that extend beyond traditional maternal and child health delivery channels [22, 25] (Table 4 summarises the major findings on reach dimension).

Effectiveness of UCC interventions

Effectiveness was defined as the extent to which the UCC interventions contributed to improvements in nutrition, health, and related livelihood outcomes, as perceived by stakeholders. Six subthemes emerged under the Effectiveness dimension: (2a) child and maternal health outcomes, (2b) nutritional knowledge and dietary practices, (2c) water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) practices, (5d) women’s empowerment and household decision-making, (2e) integrated service delivery and community outreach, and (2f) agricultural practices and livelihood outcomes.

Child and maternal health outcomes

Participants reported improvements in children’s growth and nutritional status, alongside enhanced maternal nutrition knowledge and increased maternal health-seeking behaviour following UCC implementation. Caregivers attributed perceived reductions in undernutrition among children under five years to improved infant and young child feeding practices.“We were educated about a balanced diet and how to feed children properly. We also learned the importance of antenatal care, and this helped reduce malnutrition among our children.” [FGD, lactating mother, Kamwenge]

“We were educated about a balanced diet and how to feed children properly. We also learned the importance of antenatal care, and this helped reduce malnutrition among our children.” [FGD, lactating mother, Kamwenge]

These findings are consistent with evidence demonstrating that integrated nutrition education and maternal health interventions improve child nutrition and survival outcomes in low-resource settings [5].

Nutritional knowledge and dietary practices

UCC interventions enhanced household-level nutrition knowledge and dietary diversity through community education sessions, cooking demonstrations, and peer learning platforms. Respondents reported increased consumption of diversified foods, including legumes, vegetables, and animal-source foods.Before the project, we used to eat whatever was available, but now we know how to mix foods and ensure children get a balanced meal.[FGD, caregiver, Kole]

Before the project, we used to eat whatever was available, but now we know how to mix foods and ensure children get a balanced meal.

[FGD, caregiver, Kole]

These improvements in dietary practices are consistent with evidence from nutrition-sensitive programmes that combine behaviour change communication with practical demonstrations [26].

Water, sanitation, and hygiene practices

Improvements in hygiene and sanitation behaviours were reported, particularly in relation to handwashing, latrine use, and safe water handling. However, participants noted variability in the consistency and sustainability of these practices across communities.“We were taught to wash hands and keep our homes clean, and many households changed, though some practices reduced when follow-up became less frequent.” [KII, community leader, Agago]

“We were taught to wash hands and keep our homes clean, and many households changed, though some practices reduced when follow-up became less frequent.” [KII, community leader, Agago]

This pattern reflects broader evidence indicating that WASH-related behaviours are more likely to be sustained when reinforced through continuous community engagement and follow-up [27].

Women’s empowerment and household decision-making

UCC activities strengthened women’s participation in household and community decision-making, particularly in relation to food choices, childcare, and use of income. Women reported increased confidence as a result of group-based learning and participation in community platforms.“Through the women’s groups, I gained confidence to make decisions about feeding my children…” [FGD, community member, Lira]

“Through the women’s groups, I gained confidence to make decisions about feeding my children…” [FGD, community member, Lira]

These findings support evidence that women’s empowerment is a critical pathway through which nutrition-sensitive interventions achieve sustained health and nutrition outcomes [28].

Integrated service delivery and community outreach

The integration of health, nutrition, agriculture, and social services enhanced access to information and services at the community level. Community outreach activities strengthened linkages between households and integrated health services by increasing access to family planning, nutrition screening, immunisation, and other preventive health services delivered through collaborative outreach platforms. As one health worker explained:“At the health facilities family planning services were also given coupled with screening for malnutrition for target populations, and immunization of children. Generally, at the health facility, a comprehensive health program was given to the entire community with the help of government and implementing partners.” [Health worker, Agago]

“At the health facilities family planning services were also given coupled with screening for malnutrition for target populations, and immunization of children. Generally, at the health facility, a comprehensive health program was given to the entire community with the help of government and implementing partners.” [Health worker, Agago]

These findings suggest that integrating community outreach with facility-based services enhanced access to essential maternal and child health services and reinforced coordinated service delivery. This aligns with evidence that integrated community–facility delivery models improve service uptake and continuity of care, particularly for maternal and child health outcomes.

Agricultural practices and livelihood outcomes

Households reported improvements in agricultural practices, including crop diversification and small livestock rearing, which contributed to increased food availability and household income.“We learned how to rear poultry and goats, and this helped us improve food at home and earn some income.” [FGD, Community Member, Kamwenge]

“We learned how to rear poultry and goats, and this helped us improve food at home and earn some income.” [FGD, Community Member, Kamwenge]

These findings are consistent with evidence showing that nutrition-sensitive agriculture approaches that link food production with nutrition education improve dietary diversity and household food security [26, 28] (Table 5 summarises key findings on effectiveness dimension).

Adoption of UCC Interventions

Adoption refers to the extent to which individuals, households, and communities implemented recommended nutrition, health, WASH, and livelihood practices introduced through the UCC programme. Adoption was examined across three levels—individual, household, and community—reflecting the progression of behaviour change from personal uptake to broader social diffusion. Five subthemes emerged under the Adoption dimension: (3a) individual-level adoption, (3b) household-level adoption, (3c) community-level adoption, (3d) enabling factors for adoption, and (3e) barriers to adoption.

Individual-level adoption

Pregnant and lactating women

Participants consistently reported adoption of recommended infant and young child feeding (IYCF), dietary, and WASH practices among pregnant and lactating women following participation in the UCC programme. Nutrition education, emphasising the use of locally available foods, enabled many women to translate knowledge into practice within resource-constrained settings.“Most mothers practiced improved nutrition and WASH behaviours, including dietary diversity through kitchen gardens and using latrines, which reduced illness.” [KII, District Official, Kisoro]

Youth and adolescents

Among youth and adolescents, adoption was primarily driven by economic empowerment interventions delivered through Village Savings and Loan Associations (VSLAs) and income-generating activities such as poultry farming, bricklaying, and beekeeping.“UCC helped us, the youths in our community, to become more creative… through bricklaying and poultry farming… it gives us money to support our basic needs.” [FGD, youth, Lira]

Economic empowerment reinforced engagement in nutrition and health practices by addressing underlying financial constraints. This aligns with evidence that integrating livelihood interventions into health programmes strengthens sustained behaviour change and improves consumption patterns [30].

Household-level adoption

Individual-level changes translated into broader household practices, particularly in relation to nutrition, food production, and hygiene behaviours. Participants reported adopting home gardening, more diverse diets, and improved sanitation practices following participation in the UCC programme. These changes were attributed to practical training, household demonstrations, continued support from community-based platforms, and the provision of agricultural inputs.

These accounts illustrate how beneficiaries translated programme knowledge and support into improved household nutrition and food production practices.

Households reported increased production and consumption of nutrient-rich foods, including vegetables and fruits, supported by the provision of agricultural inputs and training. Improved hygiene practices, such as latrine use and handwashing, were also widely reported. These changes contributed to better health outcomes and reduced illness, consistent with evidence from integrated nutrition-sensitive interventions [29].

However, adoption at the household level was sometimes influenced by intra-household dynamics, including decision-making power and gender norms. In some cases, additional engagement was required to ensure full uptake of recommended practices. These findings highlight the importance of engaging all household members, particularly men, to facilitate sustained behaviour change.

At the community level, adoption was characterised by the diffusion and normalisation of recommended practices through formal and informal platforms. Implementation of UCC Interventions

Implementation structures and actors

Implementation refers to how the UCC programme delivered integrated nutrition, health, agriculture, and livelihood interventions through coordinated systems and actors. Overall, Implementation was characterised by coordinated community delivery systems, multisectoral partnerships, and participatory implementation approaches. Five subthemes emerged under the Implementation dimension: (4a) implementation structures and actors, (4b) multisectoral partnerships and coordination, (4c) integrated service delivery, (4d) capacity building and implementation support, and (4e) implementation constraints.

Implementation structures and actors

Community Knowledge Workers (CKWs), Village Health Teams (VHTs), and Community Connector Officers (CCOs) were central to programme delivery, providing technical support in nutrition, agriculture, financial literacy, and savings group facilitation. Local government actors, particularly agriculture extension workers and CCOs, played a key operational role in implementation and were instrumental in mobilising communities and coordinating activities.“Local government and community actors also played a key role, especially agriculture extension workers and CCOs in programme implementation and were influential” [FGD, community members, Kole]

“Local government and community actors also played a key role, especially agriculture extension workers and CCOs in programme implementation and were influential” [FGD, community members, Kole]

Multisectoral partnerships and coordination

Implementation was supported by a network of institutional partners, including FHI360, BRAC, district local governments, Gulu University, and local civil society organisations. These partnerships provided technical expertise and facilitated coordinated multisectoral implementation across health, agriculture, and livelihood domains.

This collaboration enhanced technical quality, contextual relevance, and accountability, consistent with evidence that multi-stakeholder engagement strengthens implementation fidelity in integrated nutrition programmes [33]. By combining expertise across sectors, the programme was able to address the complex and interconnected determinants of malnutrition.

Furthermore, the integration of technical knowledge across sectors—health, agriculture, and economic empowerment—enabled a more holistic response to the underlying determinants of malnutrition.“Many volunteers, mentors, and facilitators had in-depth knowledge of the different intervention areas to support the UCC program.” [KII, Implementing Partner, Lira]

“Many volunteers, mentors, and facilitators had in-depth knowledge of the different intervention areas to support the UCC program.” [KII, Implementing Partner, Lira]

This reinforces existing evidence that cross-sectoral coordination is essential for addressing the complex and interconnected drivers of malnutrition and poor health outcomes [26, 34].

Integrated service delivery

Service delivery was characterised by the integration of sectoral roles and coordinated outreach mechanisms. Health professionals focused on maternal and child health services, agriculture specialists supported improved farming practices, and VSLA facilitators strengthened financial literacy and savings behaviour. This division of roles ensured that interventions were delivered comprehensively and reinforced across sectors.

Routine monitoring and supervision, conducted by CCOs and local government staff, supported implementation quality and responsiveness. Community feedback mechanisms, including radio talk shows, further enabled adaptive implementation.“Field monitoring by CCOs and local government staff, including radio talk shows for community feedback, ensured timely oversight and adaptive implementation.” [FGD, lactating mother, Kamwenge]

“Field monitoring by CCOs and local government staff, including radio talk shows for community feedback, ensured timely oversight and adaptive implementation.” [FGD, lactating mother, Kamwenge]

This participatory approach, combining supervision with community feedback, enhanced programme responsiveness and alignment with local needs [32].

Capacity building and implementation support

Capacity-building was a central component of implementation. Strategies included training of trainers (ToT), community sensitisation through radio platforms, and continuous skills development for both volunteers and beneficiaries. Volunteers received periodic training to strengthen competencies in nutrition, agronomy, and financial management.During project implementation, some of the volunteers were taken for further training to update their knowledge on the different intervention areas.[FGD, community member, Kole]

In addition, school nutrition clubs and demonstration gardens provided hands-on learning opportunities, particularly for children and youth, fostering early engagement in nutrition and agriculture practices.

These participatory and skills-based approaches strengthened implementation fidelity and reinforced the adoption of recommended practices [35].

Overall effectiveness

Overall, stakeholders perceived improvements in child and maternal nutrition practices, increased nutrition knowledge, enhanced health and WASH behaviours, strengthened women’s empowerment, and improved livelihoods following UCC implementation. These findings suggest that the multisectoral design of the programme contributed to broad and interrelated outcomes, consistent with evidence that integrated interventions are more effective in addressing the multiple determinants of malnutrition than single-sector approaches [5, 28].

An important unanticipated finding was that, despite the overall positive perceptions of the UCC programme, contextual factors such as prolonged drought, poor adaptation of agricultural inputs, transport limitations, and weak group cohesion constrained the effectiveness of some intervention components. These findings underscore the importance of adapting multisectoral nutrition interventions to local environmental, logistical, and social contexts.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides in-depth insights into the implementation of a large-scale multisectoral nutrition programme using the RE-AIM framework, drawing on diverse stakeholder perspectives across multiple districts. The use of a participatory action research approach enhanced contextual relevance and strengthened the validity of the findings. Inclusion of both community members and policy-level stakeholders allowed for a comprehensive understanding of programme processes across different levels.

However, several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional qualitative design does not allow for assessment of changes over time or causal attribution of outcomes to the intervention. Findings are based on self-reported perceptions and may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. In addition, the absence of quantitative outcome measures limits the ability to directly measure programme effectiveness. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable implementation insights and practical lessons for similar multisectoral nutrition programmes.

Conclusion

This study explored stakeholder perceptions of the implementation and contribution of UCC programme using the RE-AIM framework. The findings indicate that the UCC programme implemented a holistic, multisectoral approach integrating nutrition, health, agriculture, and livelihood interventions. The programme achieved broad reach among vulnerable populations through community-based platforms and participatory delivery mechanisms, and supported adoption of key nutrition, health, WASH, and livelihood practices.

Implementation was strengthened by multisectoral collaboration, engagement of community-level actors, and alignment with local government systems. These factors contributed to perceived improvements in nutrition knowledge, health behaviours, women’s empowerment, and household livelihoods. However, environmental shocks, resource constraints, gender norms, and limited engagement of some stakeholders constrained programme effectiveness and equitable participation.

Maintenance of programme activities was facilitated by integration into government systems, transfer of assets, and community-level capacity building. Nevertheless, gaps in sustained training, funding, and institutional support pose risks to long-term sustainability.

These findings demonstrate that multisectoral nutrition programmes are most likely to achieve sustained impact when community ownership, institutional integration, and adaptive implementation strategies are strengthened simultaneously.
