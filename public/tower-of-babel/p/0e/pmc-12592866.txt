Interdisciplinary Management of an Infected Dentigerous Cyst Associated with an Impacted Maxillary Canine: A Case Report with a 6-year Follow-up

Introduction

Dental anomalies and pathologies in children pose a significant challenge in pediatric dentistry, often necessitating invasive minor oral surgical procedures for effective management.1 These challenges are further compounded by factors such as poor oral hygiene, low socioeconomic status, lack of awareness, parental negligence, poor patient compliance, and the presence of developing adjacent dental structures, all of which complicate treatment planning and execution. One such condition is impacted teeth, which, if left untreated, can lead to crowding, malocclusion, and resorption of adjacent teeth, eventually requiring more complex orthodontic or surgical interventions.2,3 Additionally, untreated cystic or pathological lesions associated with impacted teeth can progressively enlarge, resulting in bone destruction, infection, and compromised jaw development.4 Delayed intervention due to socioeconomic and behavioral factors further increases the risk of postsurgical complications.5 Early diagnosis, parental education, and a multidisciplinary treatment approach are essential for improving prognosis and ensuring optimal long-term outcomes in pediatric patients.1

Dentigerous cysts are the second most common type of odontogenic cysts, typically associated with unerupted or impacted teeth, predominantly the mandibular third molars and maxillary canines.6,7 These cysts often remain asymptomatic until they attain a significant size, leading to bone expansion, root resorption of adjacent teeth, and potential infection.8 The diagnosis and management of such lesions require a comprehensive clinical, radiographic, and histopathological approach to prevent complications and ensure optimal oral function and esthetics.8 Impacted maxillary canines pose a significant challenge in clinical practice due to their high prevalence and potential complications, such as cystic transformation, resorption of adjacent teeth, and associated infections. A variety of factors, including genetic predisposition, dilaceration, and ectopic eruption pathways, contribute to canine impaction, necessitating an early and precise diagnosis for effective management.9 Advanced imaging modalities, such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), play a crucial role in assessing the three-dimensional (3D) relationship of impacted teeth with adjacent anatomical structures, thereby guiding appropriate treatment strategies.10

This case report highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges encountered in a 13-year-old patient presenting with pain in the maxillary anterior region, ultimately diagnosed with an infected dentigerous cyst associated with an impacted maxillary canine (tooth #13) and a dilacerated tooth #12. The case underscores the importance of an interdisciplinary approach, integrating surgical enucleation, cyst management, and prosthetic rehabilitation to restore both function and esthetics. By detailing the clinical presentation, diagnostic considerations, and treatment strategies, this report aims to provide valuable insights into the management of dentigerous cysts associated with impacted teeth in pediatric patients. Emphasizing the significance of early diagnosis and intervention, it contributes to the existing literature on pediatric odontogenic lesions and highlights best practices for improved patient outcomes.

Case Description

A 13-year-old boy reported to our Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry in the year 2019 with a chief complaint of pain in the upper right front teeth region for the past 2 weeks. The child provided a history of spontaneous, dull aching, throbbing, and nocturnal pain in the affected area. He also reported a past dental history of similar pain in the same region one month back, for which he had undergone an extraction of tooth #53, which was associated with pus discharge. Medical history and family history were noncontributory, with no reported drug allergy. On general examination, the patient was moderately built and well-nourished, with no facial asymmetry. Intraoral examination revealed a missing tooth #13 (Fig. 1).

Intraoral picture showing missing tooth #13

The orthopantomogram (OPG) revealed the altered root morphology of malformed tooth #12, characterized by a short, stunted, and wide root and an impacted tooth #13 in the alveolar bone with an open apex (Fig. 2A). The dilacerated tooth #12 was causing a hindrance to the eruption of tooth #13. The patient was advised to undergo a CBCT evaluation, followed by the extraction of tooth #12 and orthodontic extrusion of tooth #13; however, he was reluctant to proceed with the recommended treatment.

(A) Preoperative OPG revealing the altered root morphology of malformed tooth #12 and impacted tooth #13 in the alveolar bone with an open apex taken in the year 2019; (B) Preoperative OPG revealing a large, ill-defined, diffused radiolucency extending from the periapical region of dilacerated tooth #12 to the mesial root surface of tooth #11, also involving the impacted tooth #13 taken in the year 2024

In February, 2024, the patient reported back with the same chief complaint. He had previously consulted another dentist for the same concern, where an OPG was performed, and he was subsequently referred back to our department for further evaluation and management. No extraoral or intraoral swelling or any facial asymmetry was noted during this visit.

The OPG revealed a large, ill-defined, diffused radiolucency extending from the periapical region of dilacerated tooth #12 to the mesial root surface of tooth #11, also involving the impacted tooth #13 (Fig. 2B).

Further, a CBCT was advised (Fig. 3A), revealing an expansile radiolucent lesion measuring approximately 20 × 28 mm, extending from the periapical area of tooth #15 to tooth #11 (Fig. 3B). Superiorly the lesion extended up to the floor of the nasal cavity and medial wall of the right maxillary sinus and inferiorly up to the alveolar crest of teeth #11 and #12 region. The root of tooth #12 appeared to be dilacerated, with the root curving distally. The lesion was also associated with a vertically impacted tooth #13. The crown appeared close to the apical portion of tooth #12 and tooth #14, while the root appeared to be in close proximity with the medial wall of the right maxillary sinus and nasal floor, causing perforation (Fig. 3C). Axial cross-sections (Fig. 3D) were given at every region of tooth #13 for every 3 mm on the scale. Mucosal thickening was seen at the right maxillary sinus. An expansile radiolucent lesion measuring 20 × 17 mm was noted associated with an impacted tooth #13. The tooth appeared to be labially positioned with respect to the lesion. The lesion caused expansion and perforation of the labial and lingual cortical plate, the floor of the nasal cavity on the right side, as well as the floor of the right maxillary sinus. The crown of impacted tooth #13 appeared close to the apical portion of teeth #12 and #14, while the root appeared to be in close proximity with the medial wall of the right maxillary sinus and nasal floor, causing perforation.

A provisional diagnosis of a dentigerous cyst with respect to tooth #13 was given, with a differential diagnosis of an adenomatoid odontogenic tumor (AOT) with respect to tooth #13.

A treatment plan was formulated to be performed under local anesthesia (LA) on an outpatient basis, including the extraction of teeth #13 and #12, enucleation of the cyst, and subsequent prosthetic rehabilitation for the missing teeth. A complete routine blood investigation was done, and all parameters were within normal limits.

On the following appointment, the patient's written informed consent was obtained and surgical enucleation of the cyst was done. During the surgical procedure, LA was achieved using 2% lignocaine with 1:1,00,000 epinephrine. A full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap was raised from the distal surface of tooth #15 to the distal surface of tooth #21. Upon flap reflection, the crown of the impacted tooth #13 was exposed (Fig. 4). The surrounding bone was compromised. Tooth #13 was luxated and extracted, which was associated with a large soft tissue attachment (Fig. 5A). Tooth #13 was cleaned with copious saline irrigation and preserved in Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS) with the plan of autologous replantation. However, replantation was not feasible due to the compromised integrity of the surrounding bone structure. Complete enucleation of the cystic lesion was done, and dilacerated tooth #12 (Fig. 5B) was also extracted. After the hollow bony cavity was irrigated, sterile gauze soaked in freshly prepared Carnoy's solution was placed there for 5 minutes (Fig. 6). Post that, the cavity was curetted and irrigated. A sterile gauze soaked in betadine was packed inside the cavity and suturing was done. The patient was prescribed prophylactic antibiotics, analgesics, and mouthwash. The soft tissue and hard tissue specimens were sent for biopsy (Fig. 5C), confirming the diagnosis of an infected dentigerous cyst. Two days after surgery, the patient was recalled and the gauze was removed, followed by copious irrigation and suture placement. After 1 week of recall, the sutures were removed, and postoperative healing was satisfactory.

After 1 month of healing, an alginate impression was taken for the fabrication of a removable partial denture (RPD) with respect to teeth #12 and #13 to address the patient's esthetic concerns. However, the treatment option of bone grafting and implant placement in the concerned region was discussed, to which the patient did not agree. Hence, the RPD satisfied the patient's esthetic concern (Fig. 7). The patient was kept under follow-up once in 3 months for the next 1 year.

Discussion

Dentigerous cysts are the second most common type of odontogenic cysts, arising from the accumulation of fluid between the reduced enamel epithelium and the crown of an unerupted or impacted tooth.6,7 They are frequently associated with impacted mandibular third molars, maxillary canines, and, less commonly, supernumerary or premolar teeth.11 The pathogenesis of dentigerous cysts is believed to involve the expansion of the dental follicle due to increased hydrostatic pressure, leading to the separation of the follicle from the crown of the tooth.12 If left untreated, these cysts can result in complications, such as displacement of adjacent teeth, root resorption, cortical bone expansion, and, in rare cases, malignant transformation.4

In the present case, the cystic lesion involved an impacted maxillary canine (tooth #13). The lesion extended significantly, affecting the adjacent maxillary lateral incisor (tooth #12), central incisor (tooth #11), and the surrounding bony structures. The lesion's expansile nature, extending superiorly to the nasal cavity and the medial wall of the right maxillary sinus while also causing perforation of the cortical plates, highlights the aggressive potential of dentigerous cysts when left untreated. The presence of mucosal thickening in the right maxillary sinus suggested an inflammatory response secondary to cystic expansion.

A notable feature of this case was the presence of a dilacerated maxillary lateral incisor (tooth #12). Dilaceration is an abnormal angulation of the tooth structure, often resulting from trauma or developmental disturbances during odontogenesis. The altered root morphology of tooth #12, characterized by a short, stunted, and wide root, likely contributed to the failure of the spontaneous eruption of the maxillary canine.13 Additionally, the close proximity of the dilacerated tooth #12 to the impacted canine created a mechanical obstruction, preventing its eruption and favoring the development of a dentigerous cyst. The decision to extract the dilacerated lateral incisor was crucial, as there was no sound bony structure.

The differential diagnosis of the lesion included an AOT, which is a benign epithelial odontogenic tumor commonly associated with impacted maxillary canines in young patients. AOTs exhibit similar radiographic presentations to dentigerous cysts but often present with fine, radiopaque flecks within the lesion.14 Given the radiolucent nature of the lesion in this case, a dentigerous cyst was the more probable diagnosis, confirmed postoperatively through histopathological examination of the specimen which included one bit of hard tissue that had a coronal and radicular portion resembling a permanent maxillary canine with incomplete root formation measuring 0.7 × 2 × 0.5 cm and three bits of soft tissue that appeared brown to black, lumen noted in one bit, soft in consistency and largest bit measured 1.7 × l × 1.5 cm (Fig. 5C). Histopathological examination of the H&E-stained submitted sections showed cystic lumen lined by epithelium and surrounded by connective tissue capsule. The epithelium was stratified squamous nonkeratinized, 2–5 cell layer thick (Fig. 8A) with flat rete ridges and showed hyperplasia in few areas. An arcade pattern (Fig. 8B) was also noted in one area of the section. The connective tissue capsule is composed of loosely arranged collagen fibers interspersed with fibroblasts, numerous budding capillaries, and dense mixed inflammatory cells. One area of the section also showed small globular basophilic calcifications. Muscle fibers, nerve bundles, peripheral bony trabeculae, blood vessels, and extravasated red blood cells were also seen in the specimen. A diagnosis of an infected dentigerous cyst was made.

The treatment of dentigerous cysts typically involves enucleation15 with removal of the associated impacted tooth. In this case, a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap was raised, allowing complete exposure of the impacted canine and the cystic lesion. The compromised bone surrounding tooth #13, along with the soft tissue attachment, necessitated careful luxation and extraction of the impacted tooth. Given the extensive nature of the lesion, enucleation was performed meticulously to minimize trauma to the adjacent structures.

One of the key aspects of surgical management in this case was the application of Carnoy's solution as an adjuvant therapy following cyst enucleation. Carnoy's solution is a chemical cauterizing agent composed of ethanol, chloroform, acetic acid, and ferric chloride.16 It has been widely used to reduce the recurrence rate of odontogenic cysts and tumors by promoting the chemical necrosis of residual epithelial remnants within the bony cavity. The application of Carnoy's solution for 5 minutes ensured the eradication of potential residual cystic lining, thereby minimizing the risk of recurrence. This step is particularly relevant in cases of aggressive dentigerous cysts with extensive bony involvement.16

The impacted canine was stored in HBSS to preserve its viability with the intent of autologous replantation. However, due to extensive cystic involvement and compromised bone structure, the decision for prosthetic rehabilitation was favored over replantation. The patient was given the option of bone grafting and implant placement, which he refused, leading to the fabrication and delivery of an RPD for esthetic and functional rehabilitation.

The postoperative period was uneventful, with satisfactory healing observed during follow-ups. The placement of a gauze dressing soaked in betadine for 2 days ensured proper healing of the enucleated cavity. The 6-month follow-up period confirmed no signs of recurrence (Fig. 9), emphasizing the effectiveness of complete cyst enucleation and Carnoy's solution application.16

Early diagnosis and intervention are critical in preventing the progression of dentigerous cysts, particularly in pediatric patients, where cystic expansion can have long-term developmental consequences on the maxillofacial skeleton.8 This case underscores the importance of periodic radiographic evaluation in young patients with unerupted maxillary canines, as delayed diagnosis can result in more extensive surgical interventions. Additionally, the role of CBCT in determining the 3D extent of the lesion proved invaluable in treatment planning,10 particularly in assessing its relationship with adjacent anatomical structures such as the maxillary sinus and nasal floor.

Conclusion

This case highlights the interplay between an impacted maxillary canine, a dilacerated lateral incisor, and the development of a dentigerous cyst, illustrating the complexity of managing such cases. Comprehensive treatment planning, incorporating advanced imaging modalities and surgical interventions tailored to the patient's needs, remains the cornerstone of successful management in such challenging pediatric dental cases.

Clinical Significance

Early diagnosis and intervention are vital in preventing complications of dentigerous cysts, such as bone loss and infection. CBCT enables accurate evaluation, while a multidisciplinary approach ensures effective functional and esthetic restoration. Long-term follow-up is key to detecting recurrence and maintaining successful outcomes.
