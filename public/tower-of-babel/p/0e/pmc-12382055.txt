Antiresorptive agents in the management of bone metastatic disease: a multicenter mixed-methods study in Palestinian clinical practice
Authors: Neama Daoud, Adan Abu Assa, Bayan Abu Obed, Adan Abu Rabea, Mazen Abdalla, Mohammad Mazoz Jaber, Ramzi Shawahna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntiresorptive agents are recommended for patients with bone metastatic disease in order to treat hypercalcemia of malignancy, to reduce skeletal-related events, and to alleviate bone pain. Background

Bone is a common site for cancer metastases [1, 2]. Bone metastasis represents one of the most complex pathologies, involving multiple phases and a wide range of interactions among the host and both primary and disseminated cancer cells [3–5]. The occurrence of bone metastases often depends on the type of primary tumor, with incidences as high as 80% observed in patients with advanced breast or prostate cancer [6, 7]. Globally, bone metastases affect millions of individuals, with nearly 450,000 cases diagnosed annually in the United States alone, making bone the third most common site for metastasis after the lung and liver [8, 9].

Cancer cells detach from the primary tumor, invade the extracellular matrix, and are predominantly disseminated hematogenously to the bone and other distant organs [5]. In bone metastasis, a disruption in the balance between osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and osteoblast-driven bone formation results in lesions that are radiologically classified into three types: predominantly osteolytic (the most common), osteoblastic, and mixed osteolytic-osteoblastic lesions [10].

Bone metastasis can manifest as bone pain, anxiety, depression, reduced performance status, hypercalcemia, and pathological fractures, all of which significantly diminish a patient’s quality of life [11–13]. Various therapeutic strategies have been introduced to manage bone metastasis, including analgesics, radiation therapy, and antiresorptive agents, which have become standard treatments [14–17].

Antiresorptive agents act by attenuating osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and are classified into two principal categories: bisphosphonates and receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) inhibitors [18, 19]. These agents work by binding to hydroxyapatite in the bone, after which they are internalized by osteoclasts, leading to inhibition of osteoclast activity through the induction of apoptosis and impairment of osteoclast differentiation. This mechanism helps to improve bone mineral density, reduce the risk of pathological fractures and other skeletal-related events (such as spinal cord compression, the need for radiation therapy, or surgical stabilization), and alleviate pain in patients with bone metastasis [15, 17]. Osteoclast-mediated bone resorption is effectively inhibited by antiresorptive agents, including zoledronate, ibandronate, pamidronate, and alendronate [10]. Moreover, these agents are also employed in the treatment of hypercalcemia of malignancy, Paget’s disease, osteoporosis, and various other bone disorders.

International guidelines continue to endorse the use of antiresorptive agents in patients with bone metastatic disease as they effectively reduce skeletal-related events and treat hypercalcemia of malignancy, despite the potential for adverse effects [20–24]. Long-term adverse effects shared by both bisphosphonates and denosumab include osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femur fractures; bisphosphonates also carry a risk of renal impairment, whereas stopping denosumab can trigger rebound-associated multiple vertebral fractures [25, 26].

A literature search on the use of antiresorptive agents in patients with metastatic bone disease revealed that no studies had been conducted within the Palestinian healthcare system. Consequently, there is limited knowledge regarding the utilization of antiresorptive agents in this context. Therefore, this study was undertaken to describe the prescribing practices for antiresorptive agents among patients with metastatic bone disease in the Palestinian healthcare system. Additionally, the study aimed to document the complications associated with the use of these agents and to explore the perspectives of oncologists and orthopedists regarding the benefits and risks of antiresorptive therapy in patients with metastatic bone disease.

Methods

Study design and settings

In this study, we employed a mixed-method approach that combined a retrospective analysis of prescription data for antiresorptive agents in patients with metastatic bone disease with a cross-sectional survey of the views and opinions of oncologists and orthopedists practicing within the Palestinian healthcare system regarding the benefits and risks of using antiresorptive agents in these patients. The study was conducted between March 2024 and December 2024 in the following hospitals: Makassed Hospital, National Hospital, An-Najah National University Hospital, Palestine Medical Complex, and H-clinic Specialty Hospital.

Study populations, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

As this study used a mixed-method approach, the target populations included patients with metastatic bone disease and oncologists and orthopedists treating these patients in the Palestinian practice.

The patients were eligible for inclusion if they had metastatic bone disease, were older than 18 years of age, had complete medical records, and received treatment within the Palestinian healthcare system. Patients were excluded if their medical records were incomplete or if they had a primary bone tumor.

For the survey component of the study, oncologists and orthopedists treating patients with metastatic bone disease at the study hospitals were included. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating clinicians prior to collecting their views and opinions.

Sample size and sample technique

According to previous studies, the prevalence of metastatic bone disease was estimated at 18 per 100,000 people [27, 28]. This rate was used to determine the sample size required for this study. According to statistics from the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics, the population of the West Bank is 3.5 million, which translates to an estimated 630 patients with bone metastatic disease. Using a 95% confidence interval, a margin of error of 5%, and a response distribution of 50%, we calculated the sample size to be 239 patients with bone metastatic disease. The medical and prescription records of the patients were retrieved from the electronic systems of the study hospitals.

Research tools, validity, and reliability

For the retrospective analysis, data were collected using a structured data collection form. Patients included in the analysis were diagnosed with bone metastatic disease using imaging modalities such as skeletal scintigraphy, single-photon emission computed tomography, and positron emission tomography.

For the retrospective portion of the study, patient age, weight, and height were collected, and body mass index was calculated using these measurements. Furthermore, data on sex, hospital or site of treatment, residency, insurance status, employment status, educational level, and marital status were also gathered. In addition, information on comorbidities, past surgical history, family history of cancer, type and grade of cancer, time elapsed since cancer diagnosis, time elapsed since the diagnosis of bone metastatic disease, treatment modalities received, and laboratory values including calcium (albumin-corrected), potassium, and creatinine levels were collected. The data collection form is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

To assess the views and opinions of oncologists and orthopedists treating patients with bone metastatic disease, a two-section questionnaire was administered. The first section gathered demographic and professional information about the clinicians, including sex, hospital affiliation, the average age of patients treated for bone metastasis, years of practice in managing metastatic bone disease, the percentage of their patient load comprising bone metastasis cases, and the frequency with which they encountered newly diagnosed patients with bone metastasis. The second section explored clinical practices and decision-making regarding the prescription of antiresorptive agents for patients with metastatic bone disease. The questionnaire is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

The questionnaire was developed after a thorough review of the literature. Its face and content validity were confirmed by a panel of five experts, including oncologists, orthopedists, and pharmacologists with expertise in using antiresorptive agents for patients with metastatic bone disease.

Interviews with the oncologists and orthopedists were conducted by final-year medical students. During these interviews, participants were given the opportunity to ask questions and clarify any ambiguities, ensuring that their responses accurately reflected their clinical practices.

Statistical analysis

The IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0, was used to analyze the data. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges. The distribution of categorical variables was compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Differences in continuous variables were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Associations among continuous variables were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlations. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with international and local ethical guidelines, including those outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of An-Najah National University, and necessary approvals were obtained from the participating hospitals. For the survey, all oncologists and orthopedists provided written informed consent before participating. All collected data were securely stored, and participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time.

Results

Demographic and health characteristics of the patients

In this study, 239 patients with metastatic bone disease were included. The median age of the patients was 61.0 [52.0–69.0] years, the median duration of cancer was 3.0 [1.0–6.0] years, and the median duration of bone metastatic disease was 1.0 [1.0–3.0] years. Among the patients, 159 (66.5%) were female, 156 (65.3%) resided in urban areas, 126 (52.7%) were either overweight or obese, 90 (37.7%) were employed, 143 (59.8%) had a school-level education, and 188 (78.7%) were married. Additionally, 131 (54.8%) patients had other comorbidities, 71 (29.7%) had a prior surgical history, 84 (35.1%) had a family history of cancer, and 158 (66.1%) had breast cancer. Treatments received by the patients

With regard to treatment, a total of 72 (30.1%) patients received surgery, 142 (59.4%) underwent chemotherapy, 133 (55.6%) received hormonal therapy, 6 (2.5%) had biological therapy, 54 (22.6%) underwent radiotherapy, 21 (8.8%) received immunotherapy, 32 (13.4%) were administered targeted therapy, and 191 (79.9%) were given analgesics. Among the patients, 139 (58.2%) received antiresorptive agents. Of these, 20 (8.4%) received only a single dose while 47 (19.7%) received doses at inconsistent intervals. The median duration since initiation of antiresorptive therapy was 1.3 [0.7–2.3] years, and the median number of doses administered was 6.5 [2.0–12.0]. Associations between the use of antiresorptive agents and the variables of the patients

Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests indicated that antiresorptive agents were significantly more likely to be prescribed to patients who were unemployed (p-value = 0.024), who received hormonal therapy (p-value < 0.001), and who underwent radiotherapy (p-value = 0.007). Additionally, patients prescribed antiresorptive agents were more likely to experience hypocalcemia (p-value = 0.005) than those who were not prescribed these agents.

Conversely, no significant associations were observed between the prescription of antiresorptive agents and other variables, including age, body mass index, duration of cancer, duration of bone metastatic disease, sex, hospital, place of residence, health insurance status, educational level, marital status, presence of comorbidities, past surgical history, family history of cancer, type of cancer, grade, treatment method, potassium levels, and creatinine levels. Among the oncologists and orthopedists, 40% considered the prescription of antiresorptive agents for patients with bone metastatic disease to be routine; 60% reported that they would recommend these agents even if the patient experienced little or no pain; 40% indicated that they would recommend an antiresorptive agent even in cases of blastic metastasis; and 60% endorsed the use of antiresorptive agents even when the patient’s prognosis was less than 6 months due to another metastasis. Regarding first-line therapy, zoledronic acid was the antiresorptive agent of choice for 70% of respondents.

Moreover, 40% of the surveyed clinicians believed that antiresorptive agents could reduce the risk of skeletal-related events by 25–50%. Additionally, 50% stated that they would discontinue antiresorptive therapy if a patient developed skeletal-related events after 6 months of regular use, and 30% indicated that they would switch the patient to clodronate. In a related scenario, 40% reported that they would discontinue antiresorptive therapy if a patient’s bone pain worsened after 6 months of consistent treatment, while 50% stated that they would switch the patient to clodronate.

Furthermore, 60% of the clinicians believed that the continued use of antiresorptive agents in patients who experienced skeletal-related events despite ongoing therapy is supported by level Ia evidence (randomized double-blind trials). Among the respondents, 20% reported discontinuing antiresorptive agent therapy in fewer than 5% of patients with bone metastasis, and 30% indicated that unacceptable side effects were the most common reason for complete discontinuation of these agents.

Finally, the vast majority (90%) expressed comfort in encouraging patients with bone metastasis who experienced progression of their bone disease while on antiresorptive therapy to participate in clinical trials. Discussion

In accordance with guidelines from the American Society of Clinical Oncology and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network, antiresorptive agents are recommended for the management of hypercalcemia of malignancy, reduction of skeletal-related events, and alleviation of bone pain in patients with bone metastatic disease [29–32]. This study represents the first investigation into the prescribing of antiresorptive agents for patients with metastatic bone disease in Palestinian clinical practice. This novel inquiry, set within a unique and challenging context, provided important insights into current prescribing practices, evaluated patient‐associated variables, and examined the perspectives of oncologists and orthopedists. The most important findings of this study include a notable prescription rate of antiresorptive agents (58.2%) among patients with metastatic bone disease, substantial variations in dosing frequencies, and significant associations with unemployment, hormonal therapy, and radiotherapy. Similarly, most surveyed oncologists and orthopedists advocated the use of antiresorptive agents even for cases with minimal or no pain and unfavorable prognoses, with zoledronic acid identified as the most frequently prescribed agent. Overall, these findings are significant and informative for healthcare providers and decision‐makers aiming to align prescribing practices with evidence‐based guidelines to enhance patient outcomes. Furthermore, decision‐makers can leverage these findings to optimize resource allocation, improve the quality of care for patients with metastatic bone disease, reduce the burden of skeletal‐related events, and ultimately improve health‐related quality of life.

In this study, the prevalence of prescribing antiresorptive agents was 58.2%. These findings are consistent with current evidence-based practices and guidelines regarding the role and benefits of antiresorptive agents in patients with metastatic bone disease. Prescription trends—such as variable dosing frequencies and the predominant use of zoledronic acid—reflect current clinical practices and preferences [33–35]. Studies have demonstrated that antiresorptive agents effectively reduce skeletal-related events and alleviate bone pain, with prescription rates in similar populations ranging from 50 to 80% worldwide [36–38]. Research from Europe and North America has reported similar rates, with a notable preference for zoledronic acid owing to its superior efficacy in preventing skeletal-related events [30, 32–35, 39]. Variations in adherence to standardized dosing regimens and differences in treatment selection suggest potential shortcomings in information dissemination or resource accessibility within the Palestinian healthcare system [40–43]. These findings underscore the necessity for updated clinical protocols and continuous education to standardize prescribing practices in line with global recommendations.

Antiresorptive agents offer evidence-supported advantages, such as a decreased risk of fractures and hypercalcemia; however, these benefits must be balanced against potential hazards, including osteonecrosis of the jaw, renal toxicity, among other potential adverse effects [44–46]. The findings of this study revealed that irregular dosing was observed in 19.7% of patients, raising concerns about suboptimal treatment that may compromise efficacy or elevate risks. Evidence-based guidelines, including those from the American Society of Clinical Oncology and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network, recommend personalizing therapy based on individual patient factors [29–32]. The discrepancies between local practices and established standards observed in this study underscore the need to adopt guideline-compliant methods to ensure both safety and efficacy. Enhancing adherence carries broader implications by offering societal benefits through reducing healthcare costs associated with managing preventable complications.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients in this study offer insight into the population receiving antiresorptive agents. The majority of patients were female (66.5%), suggesting that breast cancer was the predominant malignancy (66.1%) associated with metastatic bone disease [16, 17, 21, 22, 31, 36]. The median patient age of 61 years aligns with the worldwide average, suggesting that metastatic bone disease primarily affects older individuals [47, 48]. A substantial proportion of patients were unemployed (62.3%), reflecting socioeconomic barriers that could hinder access to care and treatment adherence. The high prevalence of hypocalcemia among patients receiving antiresorptive agents likely reflects the mechanism of these agents, which reduce osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and consequently decrease calcium mobilization from bone [49, 50]. This effect is often compounded by insufficient calcium and vitamin D supplementation, necessitating close clinical monitoring [51]. In contrast, hypercalcemia, though less frequent, is indicative of a paraneoplastic process sometimes observed in patients with extensive osteolytic metastases [52]. These findings underscore the need for regular, albumin-corrected calcium monitoring in patients with bone metastatic disease to promptly identify and manage metabolic disturbances. Such vigilance is crucial not only for mitigating the risks of symptomatic hypocalcemia and hypercalcemia but also for optimizing overall patient management in the context of complex, multimodal therapy—particularly in resource-limited and conflict-affected settings where comprehensive biochemical monitoring is challenging.

The correlation between demographic and clinical characteristics and antiresorptive agent prescription provides additional insights. Unemployed individuals and patients receiving hormonal therapy or radiotherapy were more frequently prescribed antiresorptive agents, perhaps indicating the severity of disease progression and specific treatment approaches [30, 32–35, 39–43]. The lack of significant correlations with factors such as body mass index, comorbidities, or specific cancer types suggests that prescribing decisions may rely more on clinical judgment and immediate clinical needs rather than on standardized criteria. This finding contrasts with research from other regions, where adherence to predictive algorithms is more common. Addressing these discrepancies requires targeted interventions, including the development of localized standards that incorporate global insights while accounting for the unique features of the Palestinian healthcare system.

The study examined the clinical decision-making processes of oncologists and orthopedists. Although 40% reported regularly prescribing antiresorptive agents, the majority endorsed their use based on broader criteria, such as the presence of minor pain or poor prognosis. These approaches represent a proactive strategy to minimize skeletal-related events; however, they also underscore the potential for overuse in situations where the risks may outweigh the benefits [30–35, 39–43, 45]. Comparisons with international practices suggest that, although Palestinian practitioners acknowledge the efficacy of antiresorptive agents, inconsistencies remain in adhering to evidence-based criteria for initiating or discontinuing treatment. For example, discontinuation rates attributable to adverse effects (30%) and changes in therapy reveal shortcomings in patient monitoring and management protocols. Improving adherence to international standards through targeted training and comprehensive policy reforms may help address these deficiencies, ensuring both effectiveness and safety.

It is noteworthy that 40% of the surveyed oncologists and orthopedists indicated they would discontinue antiresorptive therapy if a patient’s bone pain increased after 6 months of continuous use, and 50% expressed a preference for switching to clodronate. This practice may reflect a misunderstanding of the approved indications for antiresorptive agents. Although intravenous bisphosphonates and other antiresorptive agents can indeed help alleviate pain in patients with bone metastases, their use is primarily supported by evidence demonstrating an increased time to skeletal-related events and a reduction in the incidence of these events, rather than serving as direct analgesics [53–55]. Therefore, discontinuing or altering therapy solely in response to an increase in bone pain might result in suboptimal management. Our findings underscore the need for targeted educational interventions and standardized protocols to ensure that these agents are used in accordance with their proven clinical benefits as outlined in international guidelines.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This research boasts several strengths that underscore its significance. Firstly, it is the first study to assess antiresorptive agent use within the Palestinian setting, thereby addressing a significant gap in knowledge. Secondly, its mixed-methods approach integrates quantitative data with qualitative insights from healthcare practitioners, offering a comprehensive perspective. Thirdly, the inclusion of multiple healthcare institutions enhances the generalizability of the findings within the Palestinian healthcare system. Fourthly, by emphasizing both patient outcomes and provider behaviors, the study adopts a multidimensional approach. Finally, the considerable sample size, especially for the retrospective component, strengthens the reliability of the results.

On the other hand, the following limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the retrospective approach limits the ability to deduce causation between prescription practices and outcomes. Secondly, the survey’s cross-sectional design captured practitioners’ perceptions at a single point in time, potentially failing to reflect long-term trends. Thirdly, reliance on electronic medical records raises the possibility of data omissions or documentation inaccuracies. Fourthly, the relatively small sample of oncologists and orthopedists may not adequately capture the full range of clinical approaches. Fifthly, this study did not assess patient-reported outcomes, such as pain alleviation and quality of life, which are critical endpoints in the management of metastatic bone disease. Sixth, although our study included potassium measurements as part of the routine electrolyte panel, the role of potassium abnormalities in the context of antiresorptive therapy remains unclear. Future studies could examine in greater detail whether fluctuations in potassium levels have any specific clinical implications in patients with bone metastatic disease. Seventh, it is important to note that the dosing frequencies observed in our study—such as the ‘once’ or ‘every 6 months’ regimens—are also characteristic of antiresorptive treatment protocols for osteoporosis or osteopenia. Although our cohort consisted exclusively of patients with metastatic bone disease, some physicians may have adapted their dosing strategies to address concomitant low bone mineral density. This dual consideration could potentially explain some of the variability in dosing intervals. Nonetheless, the primary aim of antiresorptive therapy in our cohort was the prevention of skeletal-related events rather than the treatment of osteoporosis per se. Future studies should aim to delineate clearly the rationale behind dosing regimens and assess the extent to which underlying bone health influences these decisions, particularly in resource-limited and conflict-affected healthcare systems where multifactorial challenges can affect clinical practice. Finally, although our study provides valuable insights into the use of radiation therapy and antiresorptive agents in managing bone metastatic disease, detailed time-stamped data regarding the interval between the initiation of radiation therapy and antiresorptive treatment were not consistently available. Consequently, we could not perform a comprehensive subgroup analysis to evaluate the impact of treatment sequencing on patient outcomes. Future studies incorporating more granular data on the timing of these interventions are needed to better understand any potential differences related to the sequence of administration.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the use of antiresorptive agents within the Palestinian healthcare system, revealing significant trends in prescription behaviors, patient demographics, and clinical practices. Our study demonstrated substantial variability in the prescribing patterns and dosing schedules of antiresorptive agents, highlighting the need for the adoption of standardized, evidence‑based protocols to improve patient outcomes and optimize resource allocation in the management of bone metastatic disease. Future research should focus on evaluating patient-centered outcomes, long-term safety profiles, and the impact of educational and legislative changes on practice patterns. Such research is essential for improving the quality of treatment and outcomes for patients with metastatic bone disease in Palestine.
