Higher vegetable intake is associated with lower diabetes risk: cross-sectional evidence from the STEPS 2016 survey in Iran
Authors: Mehran Nouri, Zainab Shateri, Shiva Faghih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence on the association between dairy products, fruit, and vegetable (FV) intake and the risk of diabetes and pre-diabetes is inconsistent across populations, particularly in Middle Eastern countries. Therefore, this study examined the association between dairy products and FV intake with pre-diabetes and diabetes risk in Iranian adults.MethodsThis nationwide Stepwise Approach to Surveillance (STEPS) survey conducted from April to November 2016 in 30 provinces of Iran with 30,541 participants over 18 years of age living in rural or urban Iran were selected for further investigation. Fasting blood sugar (FBS) was assessed by an autoanalyzer. Dietary intake was assessed using the world health organization (WHO) STEPwise questionnaire. Food intakes were categorized as follows: fruit and dairy product consumption: less than 1, 1, 2, and more than 2 servings; and vegetable intake: more than 3, 3, 2, and less than 2 servings/day. Multinomial logistic regression was used to evaluate associations between FV and dairy products intake with FBS levels, adjusting for body mass index, age, gender, physical activity, education, marital status, area of residency, smoking history, occupation, and wealth index.ResultsIn the adjusted model, the inverse relationship between vegetable intake and diabetes remained significant. Specifically: two servings/day (odds ratio (OR) = 0.46; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.31–0.66), three servings/day (OR = 0.52; 95% CI: 0.36–0.76), more than three servings/day (OR = 0.55; 95% CI: 0.36–0.85) were each associated with significantly reduced odds of diabetes. No significant associations were observed between diabetes risk and fruit or dairy intake in the adjusted model.ConclusionsHigher vegetable consumption was inversely associated with diabetes risk in this cross-sectional study, whereas no significant associations were observed for fruit or dairy intake. These findings suggest a potential protective role of vegetable intake; however, causal inferences cannot be made, and longitudinal studies are required to confirm these associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01327-5.

Introduction

Originally documented in Egypt, diabetes mellitus (DM) is a prevalent illness distinguished by polyuria and weight loss [1]. This metabolic disorder is defined by increased fasting or postprandial blood sugar [2]. Nephropathy, neuropathy, and retinopathy are important complications of DM [3]. According to the 11th edition of the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) Diabetes Atlas, approximately 589 million adults aged 20–79 years are living with diabetes worldwide, and this is projected to rise further by 2050 [4]. Projections suggest that, without effective interventions, this figure could reach approximately 853 million by 2050 [4]. These updated prevalence numbers substantially exceed earlier projections and highlight a rapidly escalating diabetes epidemic, reinforcing the critical need for preventive research and population-specific interventions. In recent years, the occurrence of DM has risen significantly in Iran [5]. By 2030, approximately 9.2 million Iranians will probably suffer from DM [6].

Between diabetes and normal blood glucose levels is a reversible intermediate condition known as pre-diabetes [7]. In this situation, there is either impaired glucose tolerance or impaired fasting glucose present [8, 9]. A study showed that 29.2% of Iranian adults have pre-diabetes [10].

One of the most important variables associated with diabetes is diet [11]. As a basic element of a healthy diet, it is recommended to eat sufficient amounts of fruits and vegetables (FVs) [12]. FVs contain useful substances such as minerals, polyphenols, flavonoids (which maintain antioxidant properties), vitamins, saponins, isothiocyanates, carotenoids, and fibers [11]. The new guidelines for adults recommend seven to ten servings of FV consumption per day [13].

Some research has found that higher FV intake is correlated to a lower diabetes risk [12]. Recent evidence highlights the potential protective role of FV consumption in the development of type 2 DM (T2DM). A dose–response meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies reported that each additional serving of fruit per day was associated with a reduced risk of T2DM, while an increase of 0.2 servings/day in green leafy vegetable intake conferred an even stronger benefit [14]. Another updated meta-analysis of 23 cohort studies found a weak inverse association between FV intake and T2DM risk [15]. Importantly, a separate meta-analysis focused on FV, and their fiber highlighted that certain subtypes—especially berries, green leafy vegetables, yellow vegetables, and cruciferous vegetables—as well as both FV fiber, were significantly linked with lower T2D risk [12]. Consistent findings emerged from the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer-InterAct (EPIC-InterAct) cohort study across eight European countries, where only root and green vegetables showed inverse associations, while fruit or vegetables separately were not associated with diabetes [16].

Mechanistically, fruits and vegetables may protect against T2DM through multiple pathways. Their high fiber content delays carbohydrate absorption, lowers glycemic index, and improves insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake [17]. In addition, bioactive compounds such as flavonoids and carotenoids exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects by modulating inflammatory signaling pathways, including the down-regulation of nuclear factor-kappa-B (NF-κB) and pro-inflammatory cytokines [18].

Beyond FV, other major components of habitual diets, such as dairy products, may also influence glucose metabolism and diabetes risk [19]. Dairy consumption is also considered necessary for a healthy diet [20]. Dairy products contain important substances that appear to be useful for T2DM prevention, including calcium, trans-palmitoleic acid, medium-chain fatty acids, polyunsaturated fatty acids, flavonoids, vitamin K, phylloquinone, and branched-chain amino acids [21, 22]. However, there are substances in dairy products that may be harmful to T2DM, including sodium, trans-fatty acids, and saturated fatty acids [23–25]. A study found that higher consumption of dairy is linked to a lower risk of T2DM [26]. Another study identified a reverse association between pre-diabetes risk and the intake of fermented and skimmed dairy products [27]. Conversely, another study identified no link between pre-diabetes and milk consumption [28]. Research has shown that greater consumption of cheese is related to higher diabetes risk, while greater consumption of yogurt is linked to a reduction risk [29].

Although several prospective cohort studies and meta-analyses have examined the association between FV and dairy intake and T2DM, the evidence remains inconclusive, with variations depending on the type of food, the amount consumed, and the population studied. While a limited number of studies have been conducted in Iran [30, 31], most available evidence is still derived from Western or East Asian populations, and nationally representative data from Middle Eastern countries remain scarce. Given the distinct dietary patterns, lifestyle factors, and rising prevalence of diabetes in Iran, the present study builds on previous Iranian research by providing large-scale, population-based evidence that can inform region-specific dietary guidelines and diabetes prevention strategies.

Methods

Research design

This cross-sectional study was conducted among Iranian adults participating in the world health organization (WHO) STEPwise approach to Surveillance (STEPS) 2016 survey. From April to November 2016, the Tehran University of Medical Sciences’ Non-Communicable Diseases (NCD) Research Center conducted STEPS in 30 provinces throughout Iran, with the exception of Qom [32]. An adapted nationwide Stepwise Approach to Surveillance (STEPS) questionnaire, inspired by the STEPwise approach of the World Health Organization for monitoring, was utilized in this research [33]. The questionnaire’s reliability and validity have already been assessed [32]. Applying stratified cluster random sampling, 31,050 persons were chosen, and a total of 30,541 individuals answered the STEPS questionnaire in the 1st stage (509 participants were excluded due to incomplete data or missing information). After physical examination in the second stage, 30,042 participants over 18 years of age living in rural or urban Iran were selected for further investigation (499 participants were removed due to missing information on diabetes status or essential covariates (e.g., BMI, physical activity, socioeconomic status)). Participants over the age of 25 were then asked to undergo laboratory evaluations (19,734 participants), and all samples were sent to the center’s laboratory [32].

In the first phase, questions were asked of the participants regarding their way of life, including smoking history, physical activity, and food intake, as well as medical risk factors, demographic characteristics, and history of non-communicable diseases. Then, anthropometric indices were evaluated in the second phase. After that, urine and blood samples were collected in the third phase. Written informed consent was completed and signed before the first stage of the study. The research details (setting, sampling, data collection, etc.) have been previously published [32]. All methods were conducted in compliance with the applicable regulations and guidelines.

The Medical Research and Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.SCHEANUT.REC.1400.035) approved this study, and informed consent was secured from every participant. Additional information regarding the study has been reported in previous publications [34–36].

Measurements

Blood samples were gathered after a 12-hour fasting period. Fasting blood sugar (FBS) was assessed by an autoanalyzer [32]. Weight was recorded applying a standard scale, while height was recorded with a height gauge. To assess the participants’ physical activity, the global physical activity questionnaire of the world health organization was used [32].

Dietary assessment

Dietary intake of fruits, vegetables, and dairy products was assessed using the WHO STEPwise questionnaire. Participants’ food intakes were extracted from the questionnaire of the first stage of the study [32]. Participants reported their average daily servings of each food group. The cutoff points (> 2, 2, 1, < 1 servings for fruits and dairy; >3, 3, 2, < 2 servings for vegetables) were defined in accordance with WHO dietary recommendations for healthy eating. Although the WHO STEPwise questionnaire provides standardized dietary assessment across populations, dietary intake was self-reported and therefore subject to recall bias.

Statistical analysis

The Wealth Index (WI) was computed based on access to various amenities, including water and gas pipelines, a cooler, dishwasher, vacuum cleaner, washing machine, internet, mobile phone, TV, personal computer, telephone, freezer, central heating system, oven, kitchen, bathroom, house, and car applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA). For data reduction, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test were utilized. We then converted the WI into quintiles.

Diabetes was classified based on laboratory-measured FBS levels only. FBS was categorized as follows: ≥126 mg/dL, 100–125 mg/dL, ˂100 mg/dL, which were classified as diabetes, prediabetes, and normal, respectively. The baseline characteristics of the participants, organized by FBS, are shown as percentages for categorical variables and as median (interquartile range [IQR]) or mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Additionally, the chi-square test was utilized for categorical variables, while Kruskal-Wallis’s test or one-way ANOVA was utilized for continuous variables. Multinomial logistic regression was then utilized to assess the odds ratio (OR) and the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) between dairy products, vegetable, and fruit consumption and FBS classification in crude and adjusted models. Demographic variables were entered in the adjusted model as confounders. All analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25). Also, all figures’ depictions were presented by R software (version 3.0.2). A p-value ≤ 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

In both crude and adjusted models, no significant associations were observed between the intake of fruits or dairy products and the odds of pre-diabetes compared to individuals with normal FBS, as shown in Tables 2 and 3. In crude analyses, higher dairy intake was associated with increased odds of diabetes; however, this association was fully attenuated after adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, and lifestyle factors, suggesting potential confounding effects. In contrast, vegetable intake demonstrated a consistent inverse association with diabetes across both crude and adjusted models, with a graded reduction in odds observed across intake categories. No significant associations were observed between fruit intake and diabetes or pre-diabetes in adjusted analyses.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that higher consumption of vegetables (more than three servings per day) was associated with a 45% lower odds of T2DM. In contrast, fruit intake and overall dairy consumption were not significantly related to the odds of diabetes or pre-diabetes. These findings are partly consistent with previous research. For example, Cooper et al. reported that higher vegetable intake, but not fruit consumption, was inversely related to diabetes risk [37]. Similarly, other studies and meta-analyses have highlighted the inverse association between green leafy and cruciferous vegetables and diabetes [14, 38]. Nevertheless, some studies observed null associations [39, 40]. Differences in dietary assessment methods, intake ranges, or adjustment for confounders, variability in vegetable types and preparation methods, as well as population-specific dietary patterns likely contribute to these discrepancies. Our study adds to the literature by providing evidence from a Middle Eastern population, where dietary habits differ from Western or East Asian cohorts, thereby reinforcing the potential role of vegetables in diabetes prevention across diverse settings.

The inverse association between vegetable intake and diabetes risk, but not fruit intake, may be partly explained by the lower sugar content of vegetables and their higher fiber and polyphenol composition. In addition, in the Iranian context, fruit consumption often includes processed or sweetened forms, which may attenuate potential benefits. These dietary intakes may contribute to the observed differences between food groups [41].

Vegetables may help prevent T2DM due to their high fiber content, which slows glucose absorption and aids in maintaining normal blood sugar levels [11]. Soluble fiber, in particular, has been shown in randomized clinical trials to reduce postprandial glycemic responses, whereas insoluble fiber has a smaller effect [42]. In addition, bioactive compounds in vegetables, such as flavonoids and carotenoids, exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects that may mitigate chronic low-grade inflammation, a key contributor to insulin resistance and diabetes development [43, 44]. Evidence also suggests that increased vegetable intake can support weight management, improve plasma glucose levels, and enhance insulin sensitivity, further contributing to the reduced risk of diabetes [45, 46]. For example, every 0.2-serving daily increase in green leafy vegetable consumption was associated with a 13% lower risk of developing T2DM [14].

In contrast to vegetables, our study did not find a significant association between overall fruit or dairy intake and the odds of diabetes or pre-diabetes. Several other studies have reported similar null findings. For instance, some research has shown no correlation between total dairy consumption—including low-fat or high-fat milk—and T2DM risk [47–49]. Conversely, a few studies have suggested an inverse association between dairy and diabetes [50, 51], highlighting heterogeneity in findings. Similarly, fruit intake was not significantly related to diabetes risk in our population [31, 52], consistent with prior meta-analyses [39, 40]. However, several recent meta-analyses have reported an inverse association between fruit consumption and the risk of T2DM. For example, a systematic review and dose–response meta-analysis of prospective studies reported a weak protective effect of higher fruit and vegetable intake on T2DM incidence [41]. Similarly, another systematic review and meta-analysis found that higher fruit or green leafy vegetables intake is associated with a significantly reduced risk of T2DM [14]. The lack of association observed in our study may therefore reflect differences in fruit types consumed, preparation methods, or cultural dietary patterns.

Several factors may explain the lack of significant associations between fruit or dairy intake and diabetes odds in our study. Self-reported dietary assessments are prone to recall bias and measurement error, which can attenuate true associations. Additionally, heterogeneity in food types, preparation methods, and nutrient composition may dilute potential effects, as certain subtypes of fruit or dairy might confer greater benefits than others. Cultural dietary patterns in Iran, including high consumption of refined grains, may further overshadow the potential protective effects of fruit and dairy [53]. Finally, residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be ruled out, highlighting the need for cautious interpretation and further research using precise dietary assessments in larger, diverse populations.

This study has several strengths, including a large sample size and adjustment for multiple potential confounders. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, dietary intake was self-reported, which is subject to recall bias and measurement error. For example, individuals without diabetes may over-report the intake of healthy foods such as vegetables due to social desirability bias, whereas individuals with diagnosed diabetes may have intentionally increased vegetable consumption following medical advice, potentially biasing the observed associations. Moreover, although the WHO STEPwise questionnaire is a standardized instrument developed by the WHO and widely used in population-based surveys, its dietary components have not been formally validated for Iranian adults, which may have introduced additional measurement error. Second, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as genetic predisposition or unrecorded lifestyle habits, cannot be ruled out. Third, our findings are based on a sample of Iranian adults, which may limit generalizability to other populations with different dietary habits and cultural contexts. Fourth, due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, causality cannot be inferred. Reverse causation is a major concern in dietary studies of diabetes, as individuals diagnosed with diabetes or pre-diabetes may have modified their dietary habits, particularly by increasing vegetable intake, after diagnosis, which may partially explain the observed inverse associations. Fifth, the broad categorization of food servings and lack of information on preparation methods (e.g., raw, cooked, fried, boiled), vegetable types (leafy, cruciferous, starchy), fruit types (high vs. low glycemic index), and dairy subtypes (fermented, high-fat, low-fat) may have resulted in misclassification bias, potentially diluting the observed associations between diet and diabetes risk. This limitation is particularly important because the introduction highlights evidence suggesting differential effects of specific vegetable subtypes, which could not be examined in the present study. Finally, another limitation is that energy adjustment could not be performed because data on total energy intake were not available.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study shows that higher vegetable intake is associated with lower odds of T2DM. In contrast, no significant associations were observed between fruit or dairy intake and the odds of diabetes or pre-diabetes. These null findings may reflect cultural dietary patterns, differences in nutrient composition, or limitations inherent to self-reported dietary assessment. Future research should prioritize longitudinal cohort studies and randomized controlled trials to clarify the temporal and causal nature of these associations. In particular, subtype-specific investigations examining different categories of vegetables, as well as high-fat versus low-fat dairy products, are warranted across diverse populations. Such studies would help to better characterize potential diet–diabetes relationships and inform evidence-based dietary guidance. At the population level, our findings suggest that vegetable intake may be an important dietary factor associated with glycemic health among Iranian adults.
