Mechanical ventilation in ARDS: navigating the fine line with real-time monitoring
Authors: Lorenzo Ball, Giulia Benzi, Denise Battaglini, Chiara Robba, Nicolò Patroniti, Pedro L Silva, John J Marini, Patrícia Rieken Macedo Rocco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a heterogeneous and rapidly evolving condition in which mechanical ventilation must balance adequate gas exchange with lung protection and hemodynamic stability. Over the past decades, the traditional one-size-fits-all approach to ARDS management has been progressively replaced by precision strategies guided by patient-specific physiological characteristics. However, conventional ventilatory and hemodynamic management continues to rely on intermittent measurements that fail to capture the physiological fluctuations typical of ARDS. Recent advances, including sensor miniaturization, high-resolution continuous data acquisition, advanced waveform analysis, and expanded computational capabilities, now enable real-time assessment of respiratory mechanics and cardiopulmonary interactions. These technologies allow clinicians to track the dynamic interactions among ventilation, lung mechanics, and cardiovascular function, supporting more precise and timely adjustments in ventilatory support. Artificial intelligence systems can integrate these high-density data streams, enhance data quality assessment, and provide decision support; however, they also raise concerns regarding information overload and the need to ensure that outputs translate into meaningful, patient-centered decisions. Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a dynamic condition defined by acute hypoxemia, bilateral pulmonary infiltrates, and the need for escalating respiratory support [1]. Although its etiologies and biological pathways are diverse, the current definition rests on the premise that patients who meet these criteria share key physiological features that justify the use of similar ventilatory strategies [2]. This assumption supported major therapeutic advances in the early 2000s establishing the modern paradigm of lung-protective ventilation: low tidal volume (VT) ventilation [3], appropriate levels of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) [4], and prone positioning [5]. Since then, however, few interventions, ventilatory or pharmacological, have shown additional mortality benefit. This plateau has prompted a re-examination of the one-size-fits-all approach and strengthened the rationale for precision medicine [5]. Efforts to individualize care now span a broad range of strategies: titrating ventilation according to driving [6] or transpulmonary [7] pressure; using mechanical power as an integrated measure of ventilatory stress [8]; identifying biologically distinct subphenotypes through inflammatory and molecular markers [9]; and characterizing patterns of lung injury using chest computed tomography (CT), ultrasound, or electrical impedance tomography (EIT) [10]. In parallel, growing recognition of the central role of hemodynamics, particularly right ventricular function, has emphasized the necessity of integrating ventilatory management into a comprehensive cardiopulmonary perspective [11].

Bedside monitoring remains predominantly intermittent: arterial blood gases, respiratory mechanics, and sporadic echocardiography provide only brief snapshots of a syndrome that changes over hours, limiting the precision with which therapy can be tailored (see Fig. 1). Recent technological advances in monitoring devices and signal processing are reshaping this landscape, offering real-time physiological data and allowing clinicians to move from isolated measurements to dynamic, feedback-guided titration of ventilation and hemodynamic support [12, 13]. However, despite their substantial potential, these tools may entail the risk of overwhelming clinicians with large volumes of data, potentially complicating the decision-making process, particularly in time-sensitive settings. Computational methods, including machine learning, can help by reducing noise, highlighting meaningful physiological trends, and supporting clinical decisions [14]. Their use is not fully implemented in clinical practice, and still requires full transparency, robust validation, and a design that strengthens rather than substitutes for clinical judgment (Fig. 2). This review summarizes the current state and recent advances in moment-by-moment monitoring of respiratory mechanics, regional ventilation, hemodynamics, and ventilation–perfusion interactions in ARDS. We aim to provide anunderstanding of available monitoring tools, their physiological basis, practical limitations, and evidence for clinical application. We highlight how emerging technologies may support individualized therapeutic strategies, an essential step toward true precision medicine in ARDS.

Establishing the diagnosis of ARDS

Alterations in lung imaging and gas exchange have been central to the definition of ARDS since its original description [15]. The most recent global definition continues to require documentation of hypoxemia, preferably assessed by arterial blood gas analysis, though pulse oximetry may be used when arterial sampling is not possible, and demonstration of bilateral pulmonary opacities on chest radiography or, when unavailable, lung ultrasound [1]. A careful assessment of hemodynamics and fluid status, although often underappreciated in clinical practice, remains essential to estimate the contribution of hydrostatic edema or cardiogenic dysfunction and to exclude alternative causes of acute hypoxemic respiratory failure [2].

Role of lung imaging

In research, CT has been instrumental in elucidating ARDS pathophysiology [16], characterizing responses to PEEP [17, 18], and identifying imaging-based subphenotypes [10] that may respond to distinct ventilatory strategies. In high-resource clinical settings, CT is frequently used when chest radiographs are inconclusive [20] and can provide additional radiomics-derived physiological information [19, 20]. Recent guidelines from the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine [21] also endorse CT for patient subphenotyping and stratification. Nonetheless, all conventional imaging modalities – CT, chest radiography, or lung ultrasound – offer only static assessments in a rapidly evolving disease. These must be complemented by monitoring tools capable of tracking dynamic changes in aeration, mechanics, and perfusion.

Monitoring gas exchange

Combining intermittent ABG sampling with continuous pulse oximetry and conventional capnography remains essential for early detection of gas-exchange deterioration and timely adjustment of ventilatory support.

Role of arterial blood gas analysis and pulse oximetry

Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis remains the gold standard for assessing pH, arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO2), arterial carbon dioxide partial pressure (PaCO2), electrolyte disturbances, and acid–base balance in patients with ARDS [22]. Severity classification continues to rely on the Arterial oxygen partial pressure to fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2 ratio) [2]. In recent years, pulse oximetry–derived indices have been proposed as alternatives when ABG is unavailable [1]. The ratio of the peripheral oxygen saturation to the FiO2 (SpO2/FiO2), which correlates with PaO2/FiO2, is a practical non-invasive tool for screening and for resource-limited settings [23]. However, its reliability declines once SpO2 exceeds approximately 97%, at which point the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve flattens, limiting estimation of PaO2 and risking misclassification of severity [24]. Both SpO2/FiO2 and PaO2/FiO2 are influenced by PEEP [25] and FiO2, with particularly large effects in patients with substantial intrapulmonary shunt, making them imperfect markers of disease trajectory [26].

Automated, real-time, and estimated ABG

Routine ABG sampling increases workload due to repeated arterial punctures and specimen handling. Moreover, ABG provides only discrete snapshots, often limited to several measurements per day depending on disease severity and institutional practice [29], and therefore may miss rapid physiological fluctuations or the immediate effects of therapeutic interventions. Automated sampling systems integrated into ICU monitors can reduce nursing workload [30], but they are costly, not widely available, and still provide intermittent data.

Continuous intra-arterial sensors for pH, PaO2, and PaCO2 were developed in the 1990s [31], but were abandoned due to sensor drift, recalibration needs, and high costs. Newer technologies have renewed interest in continuous ABG monitoring [32], but none has entered routine clinical practice. Parallel efforts aim to estimate ABG values from venous [33] or capillary [34] samples, or through data-driven algorithms using non-invasive inputs such as SpO2 and end-tidal CO2 (PetCO2). Although these methods perform reasonably well at a population level [35], individual discrepancies, particularly for PaO2, remain substantial, and large-scale validation is ongoing [36].

Continuous pulse oximetry

Pulse oximetry is a cornerstone of ARDS monitoring [37], providing continuous detection of desaturation and helping clinicians assess responses to changes in ventilatory support. Current guidelines define both lower and upper SpO2 limits to avoid unnecessary exposure to high FiO2 and excessive PEEP, each of which carries potential adverse effects [38]. The traditional conservative target (88–92%) has been questioned: a recent randomized trial found no mortality benefit compared with a more liberal strategy targeting SpO2 > 96% [39]. SpO2 values also show substantial temporal variability, and reliance on intermittent manual documentation may overestimate a patient’s true oxygenation status [40]. High-frequency SpO2 monitoring allows real-time assessment of oxygenation dynamics and improves adherence to prescribed targets, facilitating more precise titration of ventilatory support.

Capnography

Capnography, which displays exhaled CO2 over time, remains part of standard ARDS monitoring. It is typically obtained through a mainstream sensor in the ventilator circuit or a sidestream system connected to the patient monitor. Capnogram inspection helps detect patient–ventilator asynchrony [27], airway obstruction, circuit disconnection, and apnea. PetCO2 is the concentration of CO2 in the exhaled gases and is related to, but does not directly represent, PaCO2. PetCO2 systematically underestimates PaCO2 in the presence of ventilation–perfusion mismatch, which is commonly extensive in ARDS. While absolute PetCO2 values should not be interpreted as a surrogate for PaCO2, changes in PetCO2 over time still provide useful information regarding trends in ventilation and CO₂ elimination.

The PaCO2 − PetCO2 gradient reflects ventilatory inefficiency and is related to the physiologic dead-space fraction (VD/VT), namely the proportion of tidal volume that does not participate in gas exchange [28]. Formal quantification of VD/VT is based on the Bohr equation, which requires the measurement of the average exhaled CO2 and alveolar partial pressure of CO2, and on the Enghoff modification, which replaces alveolar with arterial partial pressure of CO2.

However, since average expired CO2 is not available in conventional monitoring, a surrogate index using PetCO2 instead of average exhaled CO2 has been proposed as a rough bedside estimate of VD/VT ()[28] and it has been associated with mortality in ARDS [29].

Volumetric capnography

Volumetric capnography combines exhaled CO2 with expiratory flow to plot CO2 concentration against volume, allowing real-time assessment of ventilation–perfusion mismatch [30], more accurate quantification of dead space [31], and evaluation of PEEP effects on lung mechanics [32]. It provides continuous tracking of ventilatory inefficiency, a parameter recently associated with mortality in a dose–response manner [33]. During PEEP titration, volumetric capnography can identify increases in dead space caused by regional hyperinflation, supporting individualized PEEP selection [34]. Furthermore, integrating dead-space measurements–particularly through AI-driven methodologies–could refine non-invasive estimation of PaCO2. Although present on many high-end ventilators and some metabolic monitors, broader use is expected as data-integration platforms that combine flow and CO2 signals become more widely available.

Capnography during non-invasive respiratory support

Non-invasive respiratory support presents challenges for conventional capnography. In bilevel non-invasive ventilation, efforts are underway to achieve reliable mainstream CO2 monitoring, even in single-limb circuits with intentional leaks [35]. For patients receiving conventional oxygen therapy or high-flow nasal oxygen, alternatives include transcutaneous CO2 monitoring [36] and specialized sidestream interfaces [46], although their use in ARDS remains limited.

Monitoring respiratory mechanics

Assessment of respiratory mechanics is important to quantify the extent of lung involvement in ARDS [37], guide ventilator settings [38], and detect patient–ventilator interactions [39], all of which help minimize ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) [40].

Several key measurements still require intermittent maneuvers, such as inspiratory or expiratory holds and stepwise PEEP adjustments, to obtain static respiratory parameters. Recently, attention has shifted to ventilator-derived indices, including the recruitment-to-inflation ratio [41], which can estimate lung recruitability and support individualized PEEP titration without complex methods like dual-PEEP CT scans [17]. Despite their clinical value, these operator-dependent maneuvers remain underused; even plateau pressure, the simplest measurement, is documented in only 40% of ARDS patients [42]. Continuous, real-time monitoring can identify patients who may benefit from targeted static measurements or more advanced physiological assessments.

Ventilator waveform analysis

Modern ventilators continuously record airway pressure, flow, and volume, allowing clinicians to visualize and calculate multiple physiological parameters at the bedside. Routine waveform inspection enables rapid detection of incomplete exhalation on the flow–time curve [43], visual estimation of the stress index during volume-controlled ventilation [44] to identify tidal recruitment or overdistension, and assessment of volume–time curves for tidal volume drift, leaks, or artefacts. Combined waveform analysis is particularly valuable for recognizing patient–ventilator asynchrony [39]. Pressure–volume and flow–volume loops provide complementary information, helping to identify hysteresis, air trapping, and expiratory flow scooping [43], the latter indicating expiratory flow limitation [45].

Beyond waveform analysis, most ventilators continuously estimate respiratory-system compliance and resistance during tidal breathing. These dynamic estimates are generally reliable in passive, fully controlled ventilation and have improved with updated algorithms [46], but should be interpreted cautiously in assisted or hybrid modes [47]. When available, esophageal pressure monitoring [63] allows separation of lung and chest wall mechanics and can guide PEEP titration [7]. Despite technical feasibility using dedicated esophageal balloons with compatible ventilators or standalone systems, adoption remains extremely limited, below 1% in ARDS patients [42].

The high temporal resolution of ventilator signals generates a volume of data that exceeds what can be interpreted reliably at the bedside. This has created opportunities for AI-assisted decision systems, which have demonstrated strong performance in detecting patient–ventilator asynchrony and are expected to expand as real-time computational tools advance [48].

Driving pressure and mechanical power

Driving pressure (ΔP), defined as the difference between plateau pressure and PEEP, reflects the pressure required to deliver a given tidal volume relative to the compliance of the respiratory system. In ARDS, ΔP has been proposed as a marker of disease severity, a safety threshold, and a potential target for ventilatory management [49]. However, strategies that titrate PEEP solely to minimize ΔP have not shown mortality benefit in randomized trials [4, 50] and have been criticized because PEEP-induced changes in ΔP do not reliably indicate lung recruitment [51]. Nonetheless, higher ΔP consistently correlates with increased mortality and can help identify patients who may benefit from tidal volume reduction [52].

Mechanical power (MP) quantifies the total energy delivered to the respiratory system during mechanical ventilation and models ventilator-induced lung injury as a process of energy transfer [8]. MP is calculated using routinely available ventilator parameters, including tidal volume, PEEP, inspiratory flow pattern, and airway resistance [53]. Although methodological aspects of MP computation remain debated [54], MP has been repeatedly associated with mortality in ARDS [55].

Together, ΔP and MP provide complementary, real-time information on respiratory mechanics, changes in lung aeration, and the overall mechanical load imposed by ventilation [56].

Monitoring patient effort during assisted ventilation modes and non-invasive respiratory support

During assisted and assisted–controlled ventilation, patient-generated effort contributes to transpulmonary pressure, causing airway pressure measured at the ventilator to underestimate actual lung stress [57]. Visual inspection of ventilator waveforms can suggest excessive inspiratory drive (e.g. prominent negative deflections at inspiratory onset, double triggering, premature cycling, or breath stacking), but these signs are neither sensitive nor specific [39]. Many patients may generate high transpulmonary pressures despite minimal waveform abnormalities.

Esophageal pressure (Pes) monitoring remains the gold standard for quantifying inspiratory effort [57], providing continuous measurements of Pes swings and, when combined with dedicated software, the pressure–time product. Diaphragmatic activity can also be measured invasively via specialized esophageal catheters that record the electrical activity of the diaphragm (EAdi) [58], or estimated non-invasively using surface electromyography (sEMG), although this method is less validated [59].

Several intermittent techniques estimate inspiratory drive and effort without direct Pes monitoring. These include airway occlusion pressure at 100 ms (P0.1), the pressure–muscle index [60], and the occlusion-derived ΔPocc [61], each requiring brief or prolonged occlusion maneuvers (see Fig. 3). Among non-invasive continuous methods, the “flow index” evaluates the concavity of the flow–time curve during pressure support ventilation and correlates with inspiratory effort [62]. Additional approaches use mathematical modeling of the equation of motion [63] or deep-learning algorithms to infer patient effort from ventilator waveforms [64].

Monitoring inspiratory effort during non-invasive respiratory support (NRS) is more challenging, as many tools validated in intubated patients cannot be reliably applied [65]. In this context, Pes and EAdi remain the most accurate options [66], while clinical scores are being developed to identify patients at risk of excessive effort and potential patient self-inflicted lung injury (P-SILI). Continuous measures such as pressure swings at the nostril using a dedicated probe [67], or fluctuations in central venous pressure [68], may provide additional insights. Accurate monitoring of effort during NRS and assisted ventilation is critical to guide timely adjustment of support or escalation of therapy, potentially mitigating the risk of P-SILI [69].

Monitoring regional distribution of ventilation with electrical impedance tomography

The techniques described above assess global respiratory mechanics, but ARDS is characterized by marked regional heterogeneity, with areas of consolidation, ground-glass opacities, atelectasis, overdistension, and normally aerated lung coexisting [70]. EIT provides dynamic bedside monitoring of regional ventilation using a thoracic electrode belt that detects cyclical changes in electrical impedance. Modern systems can integrate ventilator-derived pressure and flow data [71]. In ARDS, EIT has been used to guide PEEP titration by balancing alveolar recruitment and overdistension [72], detect pendelluft phenomena and airway opening pressures [73], and assess responses to prone positioning or during weaning trials [74]. Recent devices can also provide information on regional perfusion and selected hemodynamic variables, broadening the potential applications of EIT beyond ventilation alone [75].

Although EIT delivers unique physiological insights and allows continuous real-time monitoring, prolonged use may be limited by skin injury from belt pressure. As a result, EIT is generally applied as an intensive monitoring tool during specific ARDS phases or for individualized ventilatory optimization, rather than for continuous long-term monitoring.

Monitoring the hemodynamic effects of ventilation

Hemodynamic monitoring in ARDS is essential for two closely related reasons [11]. First, sepsis and severe pneumonia—the leading causes of ARDS—are often accompanied by circulatory dysfunction [42]. Second, positive-pressure ventilation alters intrathoracic pressure, venous return, and right ventricular (RV) afterload, adding an additional hemodynamic burden [76]. High-intensity ventilation can precipitate RV dysfunction, a complication consistently linked to increased mortality [77]. Although the pulmonary artery catheter remains the reference standard, its invasive nature and intermittent measurements limit its use to fewer than 5% of ARDS patients [42]. Current practice therefore prioritizes less invasive techniques that provide continuous or near–real-time physiological monitoring.

Minimally invasive monitoring

Radial or femoral arterial catheterization is routinely used in ARDS for continuous blood pressure monitoring and repeated ABG sampling. Beyond measuring pressure, pulse-contour systems can estimate cardiac output and derived parameters, including systemic vascular resistance [78]. Moreover, AI-based algorithms have attempted to predict hypotension episodes based on continuous waveform analysis of the arterial pressure [79]. Dynamic indices, such as respiratory variations in pulse pressure or stroke volume, may indicate fluid responsiveness, though their reliability can decrease during low tidal volume ventilation [80]. While pronounced respiratory oscillations in the arterial waveform can provide a qualitative estimate of preload dependence, visual assessment alone is imprecise; algorithm-based indices are generally preferred [81].

In addition to macrocirculatory monitoring, interest in the microcirculation has renewed following evidence that capillary refill time–guided resuscitation improves outcomes in septic shock [82]. Sublingual imaging remains the reference standard for microcirculatory assessment, while near-infrared spectroscopy provides a practical, real-time bedside alternative [83]. Integrating macrocirculatory and microcirculatory assessments may help clinicians detect fluid imbalances and evaluate the hemodynamic effects of ventilatory adjustments in a timely and patient-specific manner.

Integrated assessment of ventilation and hemodynamics

Mechanical ventilation affects circulation through two opposing mechanisms: changes in intrathoracic pressure, which alter venous return and RV preload, and changes in lung volume and transpulmonary pressure, which impact RV afterload and pulmonary vascular resistance. The interplay of these forces creates a narrow physiological window in which gas exchange can be optimized without jeopardizing hemodynamic stability [84].

During PEEP titration, it is crucial to identify the hemodynamic threshold at which increases in alveolar and capillary pressures begin to impair RV function [76]. Abrupt changes in airway pressures, whether from PEEP adjustments or recruitment maneuvers, require close monitoring of pulse pressure, mean arterial pressure, and, when available, cardiac output.

Heart–lung interactions are equally important during assisted ventilation. Spontaneous inspiratory efforts can generate marked negative intrathoracic pressure swings, causing abrupt changes in venous return and RV afterload, which may result in unstable hemodynamics [85].

Integrating ventilator-derived data with real-time hemodynamic monitoring allows clinicians to define a patient-specific safe operating window for mechanical ventilation (Fig. 5). This approach may enhance the safety of ventilatory adjustments in ARDS by identifying the limits of individual hemodynamic tolerance.

From multimodal data integration to intelligent decision support

Advances in sensor miniaturization, continuous data acquisition, high-frequency waveform analysis, and computational processing are transforming bedside monitoring in critically ill patients. Artificial intelligence has the potential to integrate information from multiple monitoring systems, ensure data quality, and provide decision-support tools that may improve clinical care [86]. However, the very features that make the ICU a fertile environment for innovation, dependence on organ support, rapid physiological fluctuations, and substantial patient heterogeneity, also complicate the development of reliable AI-driven systems [87]. A key challenge remains that, in critical care in general and in ARDS in particular, no specific monitoring strategy has consistently demonstrated direct impact on clinical outcomes. However, it could be questioned whether validation of monitoring techniques should require improved outcomes per se, rather than improved physiological understanding that enables more individualized decision making. The latter can ultimately translate into better outcomes, but as part of a longer-term process of knowledge improvement and complex refinement of clinical practice.

Practical barriers to the implementation of advanced monitoring techniques include technical complexity, the need for specialized training, high costs, limited resource availability, challenges in workflow integration, and competing clinical priorities. On the other hand, technical obstacles to translating data “from byte to bedside” consist of poor interoperability among devices, limited adoption of standardized data formats across manufacturers, variability in sampling frequencies, and the need for seamless integration with life-support systems. Emerging solutions, such as unified dashboards (Fig. 5) and open-format data platforms, aim to consolidate respiratory, hemodynamic, and metabolic data, enabling real-time, physiology-driven decision making at the bedside [88]. Although such integrated dashboards are currently confined to specialized centers with advanced digital infrastructure, they illustrate a potential future model for scalable, system-level ICU monitoring.

Conclusions

Advances in respiratory and hemodynamic monitoring have generated an unprecedented volume and diversity of physiological data in ARDS patients. While these developments enhance our ability to characterize individual physiology, they also risk overwhelming clinicians with information that is challenging to integrate into coherent, patient-specific management strategies. AI-assisted multimodal data integration offers a potential solution but requires rigorous validation and practical implementation before widespread clinical adoption. Ultimately, continuous real-time monitoring enables clinicians to move beyond population-based targets and engage with each patient’s dynamic physiology, supporting truly individualized, precision-based care in ARDS.
