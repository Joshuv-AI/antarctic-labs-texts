Working through a pandemic: The mediating effect of nurses' health on the relationship between working conditions and turnover intent
Authors: Farinaz Havaei, Xuyan Tang, Nassim Adhami, Megan Kaulius, Sheila A Boamah, Kimberly McMillan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimWhile research has demonstrated that nurses' health and working conditions are important predictors of turnover in COVID‐19, the relationship between these factors is not well understood. Our study investigated the mechanism through which working conditions and nurses' physical and mental health could impact intent to leave the nursing profession.DesignSecondary data from a cross‐sectional survey of 3478 nurses in British Columbia administered in May 2021 were analysed using structural equation modelling.MethodsTwo models were assessed utilizing workplace conditions as the predictor, nurses' health as the mediator, and reported turnover intent (Model 1), and anticipated time to turnover (Model 2) as the outcomes.ResultsNurses' health partially mediated the relationship between working conditions and turnover intent, where poorer workplace conditions were directly and indirectly associated with greater likelihood of leaving the profession. Nurses' health fully mediated the relationship between working conditions and nurses' anticipated time to turnover, after controlling for age. The findings from this study underscore the importance of enhancing working conditions and improving nurses' mental health and safety on the job.Patient or Public ContributionThe British Columbia Nurses' Union provided the data for this study; survey data from 3478 nurses were utilized in our study.Keywords: COVID‐19, employee turnover, mental health, nursing, structural equation modelling

BACKGROUND

The COVID‐19 pandemic has resulted in a greater nursing supply–demand gap; one that the nursing workforce has never experienced before (Buchan et al., 2022). Since the start of the pandemic in 2020, nurses have been exposed to increasingly challenging working conditions including significant staffing shortages, work overload, limited access to personal protective equipment (PPE), and fears of exposure to COVID‐19 and spreading the virus to friends, family, and colleagues (Galanis et al., 2021; Havaei, Ma, et al., 2021). As a result of chronic exposure to these workplace stressors, nurses have experienced a rise in negative mental and physical health symptoms, absenteeism, and turnover, resulting in a wider nursing supply–demand gap (Galanis et al., 2021; Havaei, Ma, et al., 2021; Havaei, Smith, et al., 2021; International Council of Nurses, 2021). According to a recent report from the International Council of Nurses, an additional 4% of nurse turnover due to the pandemic would increase the outflow of nurses by one million, further increasing the estimated global shortage of nurses from six to seven million (Buchan et al., 2022).

Given this forecast, our healthcare systems cannot afford to lose more nurses to premature or avoidable turnover. Pre‐pandemic research has identified working conditions as one of the most important contributing factors to nurse turnover (Hayes et al., 2012; O'Brien‐Pallas et al., 2010). Despite this evidence, it is unclear how working conditions, particularly during the third wave of the pandemic (i.e., spring 2021), have impacted nurse turnover behaviour. Given the unprecedented changes in pandemic working conditions worldwide, it is essential to re‐evaluate the impact of working conditions on nurse turnover in this context. More specifically, this study seeks to evaluate the mediating effects of nurses' health on the relationship between their working conditions and turnover outcomes, particularly intent to leave and anticipated time to leave the profession, and ultimately, help policy makers close the existing supply and demand gap by preventing or delaying nurse turnover.

Literature review

Nurse turnover is a costly phenomenon for individual nurses, patients under their care, and healthcare organizations. A pan‐Canadian study of nurses found that higher nurse turnover rates were associated with an increased likelihood of medical errors (O'Brien‐Pallas et al., 2010). Other research has shown that the total cost of turnover per nurse for healthcare organizations varied between approximately $21,000 USD in the United States to $49,000 USD in Australia, with a cost of $27,000 per nurse in Canada (Duffield et al., 2014).

Conceptualizations of turnover are developed based on two grounds: (a) whether the job move is internal (e.g., between nursing units within the same organization) versus external (e.g., leaving the organization or the profession all together), and (b) if it is on a voluntary (e.g., due to illness or poor working conditions) versus involuntary (e.g., due to ward rotation or termination of employment) basis (Hayes et al., 2012). Voluntary turnover is frequently preceded by turnover intention, defined as staff's likelihood of internal or external turnover, which is considered an important proxy for actual turnover behaviour (Brewer et al., 2012; Hayes et al., 2012). This paper focuses on intent to leave the profession (i.e., external turnover), as this type of turnover has the greatest impact on widening the nurse supply and demand gap (Buchan et al., 2022).

Pre‐pandemic research on the antecedents of nurse turnover has illuminated the complexity and multifaceted nature of the issue. A systematic review of 68 studies examining the predictors of nurse turnover (intent and actual) across the healthcare spectrum found that workplace (e.g., staffing, workload) and individual (e.g., age, lack of kinship) factors were among the most important predictors (Hayes et al., 2012). The COVID‐19 pandemic worsened nurses' working conditions, their state of mental and physical health, and their turnover intentions (Falatah, 2021; Havaei, Ma, et al., 2021; Havaei, Smith, et al., 2021). Mental health symptoms such as stress, anxiety and fear of COVID‐19 (Falatah, 2021), as well as compassion fatigue, burnout, and post‐traumatic stress disorder symptoms (Tolksdorf et al., 2022) were identified as important predictors of the nurse turnover. Other important predictors included working conditions in relation to workplace health and safety (e.g., PPE) (Cole et al., 2021; Lotfi et al., 2022), and COVID‐19 specific factors in the workplace such as nurses' deployment to COVID‐19 divisions (Falatah, 2021; Tolksdorf et al., 2022).

Despite these findings, the mechanism through which nurses' working conditions and their state of mental and physical health impact their turnover outcomes is less clear, particularly during the third wave of the pandemic. In this context, most of the literature has examined nurse turnover intent, with fewer studies evaluating antecedents of length to turnover. Examining nurse turnover outcomes is an urgent undertaking in the context of a severe nursing supply and demand gap, which requires extensive nursing human resource planning and intervention to keep healthcare systems functioning.

METHODS

Data collection and sample

This is a secondary analysis of cross‐sectional survey data from 3478 actively working regulated nurses across the healthcare spectrum in British Columbia (BC), collected by the BC Nurses' Union (BCNU) (British Columbia Nurses Union, 2021). The BCNU represents nearly 48,000 nurse members who were invited to participate in a 10‐minute electronic survey by email between May 11 and May 31, 2021, during the third wave of the COVID‐19 pandemic in BC. The BCNU members were invited to access the survey from the union's member portal. Certain strategies were used to promote the survey including advertisement through the BCNU e‐news and weekly reminders as well as a raffle draw for an Apple Watch Series 6. A total of 3479 nurses responded to the survey yielding a response rate of 7.2%. After excluding nurses eligible for retirement, a final sample of 3011 nurses were included for this analysis. Based on Kline's (2015) recommended 10 to one ratio of response to parameter for structural equation modelling (SEM), the study sample size was deemed adequate for the two study models with 16 and 17 free parameters. There were no missing values for the variables included in the SEM analysis. Harmonized ethics approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Boards of the University of British Columbia.

Variables and measurements

The survey included a series of researcher‐developed questions, classified into outcome, mediating and predictor variables, and content validated by BCNU subject matter experts.

Outcomes

In Model 1, the observed outcome variable asked nurses about the likelihood of leaving nursing for a different occupation post‐pandemic. Nurses' responses were rated using a four‐point and dichotomized for SEM analysis: ‘not at all likely’ and ‘not very likely’ = 0, ‘somewhat likely’, ‘extremely likely’ = 1. In Model 2, the observed outcome variable asked nurses to indicate how many more years they planned to continue to work as a nurse. There were eight options for this question: (1) leaving nursing immediately, e.g., within 1–2 months; (2) leaving nursing as soon as the pandemic is over; (3) 1 year; (4) 2 years; (5) 3 years; (6) 4 years; (7) 5–10 years; (8) more than 10 years. Nurses' responses were treated as a continuous variable when conducting SEM, with the options (1) and (2) recoded as 0, option (7) recoded as 5, and option (8) recoded as 10. The original values that represented the number of years in options (3) to (6) were retained. Of note, this outcome variable is based on a limited number of response options, and thus, is not a true continuous measure. However, it could be considered interval data, as it has a natural numerical scale (as opposed to Likert scales measuring the level of agreement) and a meaningful zero point, which allows for meaningful distance between consecutive response options. Therefore, this variable was treated as continuous to maintain the information gained from the numerical scale and to apply statistical models that assume a continuous scale.

The mediator

Nurses' health was operationalized by three questions. One of the questions asked nurses to rate the level of stress experienced in their job due to the COVID‐19 pandemic on a five‐point response scale ranging from ‘very low’ to ‘very high’. The other two questions asked about changes in mental and physical health during the COVID‐19 pandemic with a five‐point response scale ranging from ‘worsened a lot’ to ‘improved a lot’. Responses to the stress question were reverse scored so that higher scores represented better health (e.g., lower stress level, increased mental health, and increased physical health). Adequate internal consistency reliability was obtained for the health indicator in our study sample (Cronbach's alpha = 0.72).

The predictor

Workplace conditions were indexed with three indicators: (a) workplace health and safety, (b) staffing and workload, and (c) exposure to COVID‐19 patients. Workplace health and safety was measured by the mean of four questions rated on a four‐point response scale from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’ (See Table 2 for measure details). These four questions demonstrated good internal consistency reliability, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.80 for our study sample. To enhance model parsimony and the stability of model parameters, nurses' responses to these questions were reverse‐scored and averaged into a single indicator, with higher mean scores reflecting unsafe working conditions. Staffing and workload was measured by obtaining the mean score of three questions that asked nurses to rate the adequacy of nurse staffing, the degree to which inadequate staffing impacted their ability to meet practice standards, and whether the overall workload had increased since the pandemic (See Table 2 for measurement details). Higher scores reflected inadequate staffing and excessive workload. These three questions exhibited adequate internal consistency reliability, as evidenced by a Cronbach's alpha of 0.70 for our study sample. The third indicator, exposure to COVID‐19 patients, was a single item question that asked about the frequency of direct contact with suspected or confirmed COVID‐19 patients over the last 3 months. Responses were scored on an eight‐point response scale ranging from ‘never’ to ‘daily’, with higher scores indicating more frequent exposure.

Demographics

A series of questions were included to measure the nurse demographic characteristics including the age, nursing experience, professional designation (licensed practical nurses [LPN], registered nurses [RN] and/or registered psychiatric nurses [RPN]), work status (full‐time, part‐time, casual) and healthcare sector (acute care, community care and long‐term care).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis and SEM were performed using R software (V.4.0.4 R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). The two mediation models were tested with SEM using the lavaan package (Rosseel et al., 2022). Because of the strong correlations between the health indicators and the weak to moderate correlations between workplace conditions indicators, a latent variable accounting for the common variance of the indicators was created as a strategy to address multicollinearity (Ramlall, 2016). Our preliminary analyses included demographic variables in both models, but none of the variables had a significant impact, with the exception of nurses' age on time to turnover. The inclusion of these non‐significant demographic variables reduced model parsimony and resulted in a significantly worse fit of the model to our data. Hence, only age was included as a control variable in Model 2, and all the other demographic variables were excluded from further analyses.

The two models were tested using maximum likelihood estimation. The model fit was evaluated using Wang et al.’ (2011) cut‐off criteria for a good fitting model: comparative fit index (CFI) ≥ 0.90, Tucker–Lewis index (TLI) ≥ 0.90, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) < 0.08, and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) < 0.08. Of note is that the estimates of indirect effect are often non‐normally distributed (Ng & Lin, 2016); therefore, the mediating effect of health was tested with the bootstrap procedure, which does not require the normality assumption, with 2000 iterations (Efron & Tibshirani, 1986). This non‐parametric bias‐corrected method is known to produce more accurate confidence intervals and standard errors, and accordingly was used for statistical significance testing of the mediating effects (MacKinnon et al., 2004).

Table 2 shows the descriptive statistics of the key study variables. The means of the health‐related variables were all close to 2 on a five‐point scale (Mstress=2.01;Mmental=1.87;Mphysical=2.22). Nearly one‐third (32.8%) of nurses reported that they were ‘somewhat’ or ‘extremely likely’ to leave nursing for a different occupation post‐pandemic. Most respondents (67.4%) planned on leaving nursing in 10 or more years, whereas 20.3% reported an anticipated turnover time of five to 10 years, and 12.4% in four or fewer years.

Parameter estimates of the two mediation models can be found in Table 4 and Figures 1 and 2. For Model 1, health status had a significant direct effect on turnover intent. Specifically, healthier nurses (higher levels of mental and physical health and lower levels of stress) were less likely to intend to leave the profession of nursing (β = −0.19, p < 0.001). In addition, workplace conditions had significant direct and indirect effects on nurse turnover intent; the direct effect showed nurses in poorer workplace conditions (inadequate staffing and excessive workload, unsafe workplaces, and frequent exposure to COVID‐19 patients) were more likely to intend to leave the profession of nursing (β = 0.22, p < 0.001). The indirect effect of workplace conditions on nurse turnover intent was partially mediated by nurses' health status. More specifically, poor workplace conditions were associated with lower level of health in nurses (β = −0.76, p < 0.001), which were subsequently related to greater turnover intentions. The indirect effect was significant (β = 0.15, p < 0.001) and accounted for 42% of the total effect of workplace conditions on nurse turnover intent.

For Model 2, after controlling for nurses' age, health status had a significant direct effect on the anticipated time to turnover. Specifically, healthier nurses were more likely to report a longer anticipated turnover time (β = 0.66, p < 0.01). Unlike Model 1, workplace conditions only had a significant indirect effect on the anticipated time to turnover, after controlling for age, which means the relationship between workplace conditions and turnover time was fully mediated by nurses' health status (β = −0.48, p < 0.01). Specifically, nurses with poorer ratings of workplace conditions were more likely to report an expedited time to leave the profession because poor workplace conditions compromised their health (β = −0.72, p < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

This study had two key findings: (1) poor working conditions directly and indirectly increased nurses' reported likelihood of leaving the profession post‐pandemic; (2) poor working conditions also expedited nurses' anticipated time to leaving the profession only indirectly through compromising their health.

The direct and indirect relationship between working conditions and nurses' intent to leave is unsurprising. The link between (a) nurses' working conditions and their health (Havaei, Ma, et al., 2021; Lake et al., 2019); (b) nurses' health and their turnover intent (Falatah, 2021; Hayes et al., 2012; Tolksdorf et al., 2022) and (c) working conditions and turnover intent (Falatah, 2021; Hayes et al., 2012; Tolksdorf et al., 2022) has been well established in the literature. Poor working conditions also directly increased nurse turnover intent without impacting nurses' health. We speculate that this direct relationship may be attributed to new nurses who have not yet been chronically exposed to poor working conditions and subsequently have not yet had their health negatively impacted. Approximately one‐third of our survey respondents had five or fewer years of experience. For this group, the reality of nursing under severe working conditions during the pandemic was likely so strong that it defeated their sense of commitment to the profession and increased their thoughts of leaving the profession altogether, even under circumstances of intact health (Labrague & Santos, 2020).

Poor working conditions shortened nurses' anticipated time to leave the profession through compromising their health. It is reasonable that nurses would more promptly leave the profession when their mental and physical health is deteriorated as a result of strenuous working conditions. A recent cross‐sectional study of 549 nurses from the Philippines found that psychological stress, burnout and job satisfaction affected turnover intent (Labrague et al., 2020). Interestingly, working conditions were not directly related to nurses' anticipated time to leaving the profession, which speaks to nurses' personal and professional commitments buffering against time to turnover, as long as their health is not impacted.

In predicting turnover outcomes, certain aspects of nurses' work environments such as staffing, workload and workplace safety were stronger indicators of working conditions than COVID‐19 exposure. This finding is consistent with a plethora of research evidence supporting the importance of working conditions for nurses and their patient care delivery (Lake et al., 2019; Smith et al., 2021). COVID‐19 exposure was a less important factor for nurses in this study, potentially due to the timing of the data collection (i.e., in May 2021), when most healthcare workers were fully vaccinated (British Columbia Ministry of Health, 2020), which likely alleviated their concerns regarding exposure to COVID‐19 (Patelarou et al., 2022). Furthermore, mental health and stress were more important indictors of nurses' health compared to physical health. This finding is consistent with emerging research evidence showing the worsening of nurses' mental health during the pandemic (Havaei, Smith, et al., 2021).

Age was the only demographic variable related to turnover outcomes and was only associated with nurses' anticipated time to leave the profession. Specifically, we found that older nurses were more likely to intend to leave the profession in a shorter time span. This unsurprising finding could be explained by older nurses' age being closer to retirement and/or their more chronic exposure to poor working conditions dating back to pre‐pandemic.

This study is one of the few studies on antecedents of professional turnover during the third wave of the pandemic and conducted with a relatively large and diverse sample of BC nurses representing all healthcare sectors. Despite this strength, the non‐probability sampling strategy and the low response rate are the study limitations particularly for the generalizability of the findings from this study sample to BC nurses. We believe that survey fatigue combined with fatigue related to work overload and high prevalence of overtime and burnout were the main contributing factors to nurses' low response rate to the study survey (Galanis et al., 2021; Havaei, Ma, et al., 2021; Tolksdorf et al., 2022). In addition to low response rate, the sample did not include nurses outside of BC, and therefore the findings should be cautiously generalized to nurses in other jurisdictions and contexts. Additionally, the study utilized self‐reported intention‐to‐leave survey responses as the outcome; future studies should assess objective turnover data when available.

IMPLICATIONS FOR NURSING MANAGEMENT

The study findings have important implications for decision makers and nursing leaders in practice. First and foremost, there is ample evidence supporting an urgent need to invest in nurses' working conditions, particularly in terms of staffing, workload, and workplace health and safety. Our study findings indicate that these aspects of nurses' workplace, when suboptimal, increase their likelihood of turnover. In this context, staffing and workload are difficult aspects of nurses' work environments to address immediately and require long‐term planning and intervention. The BC government has made commendable efforts to increase the nursing supply through the addition of 602 new seats in nursing post secondary institutions throughout the province, and an investment of approximately $9 million to support internationally educated nurses with updating their nursing licensure (BC Ministry of Health, 2022). These laudable efforts, however, will not have the intended impact if nurses continue to leave the profession at a high rate. Therefore, short term interventions are also needed to improve working conditions. Potential interventions include the use of evidence‐based workload management tools such as the Safer Nursing Care tool (Griffiths et al., 2020) or the Synergy tool (Udod et al., 2021) that inform staffing decisions based on patient acuity and dependency needs. Self‐scheduling systems could also be implemented to better promote work‐life balance (Wynendaele et al., 2021).

Another strategy to reduce nurse turnover is through improving workplace health and safety. Nurse leaders can do this through first ensuring that nurses have regular access to accurate and up‐to‐date health and safety information in their workplace, and through creating opportunities for nurses to serve on Joint Occupational Health and Safety Committees. Nurses' needs and expertise must be sought and used to inform health and safety resources and training opportunities in the workplace. A 2019 study found the most important predictor of nurses' perceptions of workplace safety is a leader that listened to nursing staff's recommendations and input (Havaei et al., 2019).

Finally, there is a need for strategies that improve nurses' health, particularly their mental health. Long‐term strategies include more frequent access to mental health resources (e.g., the Employee Family Assistance Program in BC) and more flexible sick time policies that recognize mental health as an equally important aspect of health. We also recognize that the nature of nurses' work puts them at greater risk for poor mental heath outcomes (Canadian Federation of Nurses Unions, 2020), and for this reason, supports and resources must be provided to nurses whose health has been compromised as a result of exposure to poor working conditions. Mental injury presumptive legislation, which assumes that a worker's physical or mental injury is a result of their work and subsequently provides an opportunity for allocation of appropriate supports and resources to affected individuals, include nurses in the list of eligible occupations in some (e.g., BC, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Ontario) but not all (e.g., Alberta, New Brunswick, Yukon) Canadian provinces (Canadian Federation of Nurses Unions, 2020). This provides a unique opportunity for nurse leaders and mangers to advocate for the consistent implementation of the legislation across the country.

In the short‐term, nurse leaders can also implement evidence‐based interventions aimed at enhancing nurses' well‐being (see Melnyk et al. (2020) for a full review). Specifically, interventions that include mindfulness‐based techniques or programs based on cognitive behavioural therapy have been successful at reducing nurses' anxiety, stress, and depression (Melnyk et al., 2020). Although regular use of such interventions could markedly enhance nurses' ability to cope with workplace stressors at an individual‐level in the short‐term, we would like to emphasize that they are inappropriate for dealing with system‐level issues, such as chronic exposure to unethical and severely stressful working conditions, and therefore must not be a substitute for large‐scale efforts towards and investments in creating healthier workplaces.

CONCLUSIONS

Poor working conditions were found to increase nurses' likelihood of leaving the profession and compromises to their physical and mental health further accelerated this likelihood. Poor working conditions also directly increased nurses' turnover intent even in circumstances where their health was not impacted. The findings support a critical need for further investing in system‐level interventions that improve nurses' working conditions and interventions to support their mental and physical health.
