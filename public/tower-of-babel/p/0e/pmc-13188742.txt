Illness severity, treatment motivation and personality as predictors for the treatment outcome in adolescents and young adults with anorexia nervosa
Authors: Konstantin Kopp, Stefanie Truttmann, Tanja Wittek, Julia Klang, Helene Krauss, Carina Aichinger, Isolde Kostner, Ulrike Schmidt, Andreas Karwautz, Michael Zeiler, Gudrun Wagner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnorexia nervosa (AN) is a severe psychiatric disorder with high chronicity, particularly affecting adolescents. Existing treatments for adolescents do not always lead to full recovery. Recently, the developmentally adapted Maudsley Model of Anorexia Nervosa Treatment for Adolescents (MANTRa) has emerged as a promising alternative, warranting investigation into its outcome predictors.MethodsThis secondary analysis is based on data from a multi-center cohort study conducted in several Austrian clinical settings. The study included 92 participants aged 13–21 years diagnosed with AN who received MANTRa (n = 45) or treatment-as-usual (TAU, n = 47). Statistical analyses focused on identifying baseline predictors and moderators of treatment success (change in eating disorder symptomatology) for the MANTRa and TAU groups at end-of-treatment and 18-month-follow-up. In addition to univariate linear regression models associating various predictor variables (BMI, eating disorder severity, depressive symptoms, weight suppression, illness duration, age of onset, motivation for change, and personality traits) with the long-term eating disorder outcome, we used a cluster analysis approach to categorize patients into high vs. low illness severity and “favorable” vs. “unfavorable” treatment motivation/personality profiles. Differences between clusters regarding treatment outcome were explored further.ResultsIn the MANTRa group, none of the examined baseline variables significantly predicted outcomes at 18-month follow-up in univariate models. In contrast, in the TAU group, higher initial eating-disorder psychopathology and lower motivation were associated with poorer outcomes. In MANTRa, there was a trend suggesting that patients with both high and low baseline illness severity, as well as those with unfavorable and favorable motivation/personality profiles, demonstrated sustained symptom reduction. By contrast, in the TAU group, sustained improvements were observed primarily among patients with low baseline illness severity and a favorable motivation/personality profile, whereas those with high illness severity and an unfavorable profile tended to show less benefit.ConclusionsThese results provide preliminary evidence for MANTRa as an effective, individualized treatment for adolescents with AN, including those exhibiting severe pathology, low baseline motivation and personality traits, e.g. low self-directedness, high harm avoidance and low cooperativeness. Future randomized controlled trials comparing MANTRa with established treatments are warranted to validate these findings and refine patient selection criteria.Trial registration: The original trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (Identifier NCT03535714).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-026-01598-7.Keywords: Eating disorders, Adolescents, MANTRa, Outcome moderators, Motivation for change, Psychotherapy, Temperament, Character, Motivational interviewing

Introduction

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a serious psychiatric disorder with peak prevalence during adolescence, marked by high rates of chronicity, mortality and long-term adverse consequences [1, 2]. While various psychotherapeutic approaches – such as cognitive–behavioral therapy (CBT), specialist-supportive clinical management (SSCM) and the Maudsley Model of Anorexia Nervosa Treatment (MANTRA) – are recommended for adults [3], treatment options for adolescents remain limited. Family-based treatment (FBT) currently represents the best-established approach in this group. However, outcomes remain modest, and long-term recovery rates indicate substantial room for improvement [4]. In this context, developmentally adapted approaches such as the Maudsley Model of Anorexia Nervosa Treatment for Adolescents (MANTRa) have recently been developed and are increasingly attracting clinical and research interest [5].

The present study is a secondary analysis building on the 2023 cohort study, which found that adolescents who received MANTRa therapy showed a significant larger increase in body mass index (BMI), decrease in eating disorder symptomatology and higher remission rates at 18-months follow-up compared to patients having received treatment-as-usual (TAU) [5]. According to Treasure & Schmidt [6] the theoretical foundation of MANTRa lies in the cognitive-interpersonal maintenance model of AN, which posits that cognitive rigidity, emotional avoidance, pro-anorexic beliefs and maladaptive interpersonal dynamics work synergistically to sustain the disorder. MANTRa directly targets these factors through individualized case formulation and workbook-guided interventions. Therapists´ case formulation letter addressed to patients have been described as particularly valuable in strengthening the therapeutic alliance and supporting the recovery process. Likewise, the exercise in which patients write a letter to anorexia nervosa, portraying the illness as both a “friend and a foe” to externalize it and increase motivation, has also been highlighted by therapists as a valuable therapeutic intervention [7]. In addition, exercises addressing rigid thinking styles aim to improve cognitive flexibility, while motivational and reflective techniques address emotional regulation and pro-anorexic beliefs. This integrated approach helps patients understand and disrupt the “maintenance cycles” of their illness. By targeting these cognitive and interpersonal mechanisms MANTRa aims to facilitate more sustainable behavioral and psychological change beyond mere weight restoration. Moreover, MANTRa’s flexible structure allows it to be tailored to patients’ unique needs, making it especially valuable for older adolescents and young adults who may require a more individualized therapeutic approach [8].

Given the chronic and relapsing nature of AN, the identification of factors that predict treatment response or moderate the outcomes of established treatments remains essential for improving clinical outcomes and to determine which treatment approach may fit best to which patients.

Motivation and readiness to change have emerged as consistent predictors of positive treatment trajectories. These processes are directly addressed within MANTRa, which incorporates motivational and reflective exercises aimed at reducing ambivalence toward recovery and strengthening engagement with treatment. Across multiple studies, higher pretreatment motivation has been linked to stronger engagement, lower dropout rates, and greater symptom reduction [9, 10]. Conversely, low intrinsic motivation frequently predicts poorer adherence and limited therapeutic progress. Although motivational approaches such as motivational interviewing have been developed to enhance engagement, evidence from a recent systematic review and meta-analysis indicates that available data remain limited and that effects on eating-disorder psychopathology, BMI, and motivation to change were small and statistically non-significant [11]. These findings underscore the importance of systematically assessing motivation early in treatment while highlighting that the empirical support for motivational interventions in eating disorders remains limited.

Personality traits also play a significant role. Research has linked certain characteristics, such as high self-criticism, perfectionism, and cognitive rigidity, with less favorable outcomes, whereas more flexible and adaptive personality profiles, respectively cognitive styles, tend to predict better recovery [12, 13]. In line with this, meta-analytic findings indicate that individuals with anorexia nervosa tend to show elevated levels of harm avoidance and persistence compared with healthy controls, reflecting increased anxiety-related avoidance and perseverant behavioral tendencies [14]. Although findings regarding the influence of comorbid personality pathology remain mixed, evidence suggests that patients without pronounced personality disorders or maladaptive traits achieve more sustained improvements [15, 16]. Recent longitudinal work in adolescents further indicates that therapeutic change in AN is closely tied to personality development, with increases in self-directedness and decreases in persistence predicting better outcomes and lower reward dependence or cooperativeness being associated with poorer adherence and less favorable symptom trajectories [17]. In addition, personality and temperament traits are conceptualized as underlying mechanisms that shape eating disorder symptoms and behavioral responses rather than merely representing statistic risk factors and may therefore contribute to differences in treatment outcome [18]. These findings are particularly relevant in the context of MANTRa, which aims to address perfectionistic tendencies linked to persistence, reduce anxiety-related avoidance associated with harm avoidance, and strengthen self-directedness and interpersonal functioning, including cooperativeness, as part of the therapeutic process.

Evidence on baseline symptom severity as a predictor of treatment outcome in eating disorders remains inconsistent. Meta-analytic findings suggest that lower baseline psychopathology and higher BMI, as well as shorter illness duration, are modestly associated with more favorable outcomes, including greater weight restoration and lower relapse risk [10]. However, these effects are heterogeneous and often lose predictive significance once other clinical factors such as comorbidity or treatment intensity are taken into account [19–21]. Overall, these findings suggest that illness-severity indicators may function as partial rather than independent predictors oftreatment outcome.

This study aims to determine which patient characteristics are significantly associated with successful outcomes in MANTRa compared to TAU among adolescents with AN. Specifically, we examined whether baseline indicators of illness severity (eating disorder symptomatology, BMI, weight suppression, depression and age of onset/illness duration) as well as personality characteristics and treatment motivation can predict the 18-month eating disorder outcome in each treatment group. Furthermore, to reduce the complexity of information and avoid multiple univariate analyses, we used a cluster analysis approach to determine whether patients can be clustered into high vs. low illness severity, respectively favorable vs. unfavorable personality / treatment motivation groups. We then aimed to evaluate whether these clusters can moderate the outcome trajectories in AN patients and whether the clusters differ regarding the eating disorder outcome at end-of-treatment and last follow-up.

Identifying which adolescents benefit most from specific treatment approaches represents an important step toward more personalized and effective interventions for anorexia nervosa. By clarifying which patient characteristics predict response to MANTRa compared with treatment-as-usual, the present study may contribute to improving treatment allocation and optimizing long-term recovery in this high-risk population.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study is a secondary analysis of data originally collected as part of a multi-center cohort study evaluating MANTRa in comparison to TAU [5]. This study was conducted in clinical settings across several Austrian centers and involved adolescents and emerging adults diagnosed with AN and atypical AN. Ethical approval for the original studies was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna (EK 2005/2017), and all participants (and legal guardians for those under 18) provided written informed consent. A comprehensive study protocol was published by Wittek et al. [22]

Participants and procedure

Participants met the following inclusion criteria: a diagnosis of full-syndrome or atypical AN (or weight‐restored AN following inpatient treatment) according to DSM‐5 criteria [23]; an age range between 13 and 21 years; and willingness to engage in outpatient psychotherapeutic treatment. Exclusion criteria were acute medical instability, insufficient cognitive or language abilities, the presence of other severe psychiatric or physical illnesses that required separate treatment and pregnancy. Participants in the MANTRa group received 24 to 34 individual therapy sessions depending on illness severity, while the TAU group underwent standard individual psychotherapy provided in nationally recognized therapeutic approaches in Austria which include CBT, systemic family therapy, humanistic therapy and psychodynamic therapy. Consequently, some overlap with MANTRa may have occurred, as MANTRa incorporates therapeutic techniques used across psychotheraoeutic approaches, such as case formulation, motivational interviewing, and therapeutic writing. However, MANTRa is distinctive in that it was specifically developed from the cognitive-interpersonal model targeting the core maintaining factors of anorexia nervosa, as described above [8]. Most patients from the TAU group received weekly therapeutic sessions, which is comparable to the MANTRa group. However, treatment length and number of sessions were not regulated in the TAU group and thus, reflect the naturalistic psychotherapeutic treatment duration for eating disorders in Austria (which is usually about one year). Both groups also received medical monitoring, nutritional counselling, and caregiver skills training. Group allocation was not randomized but was primarily based on availability of therapy openings and therapists trained in MANTRa and TAU approaches​. Patients could not choose their preferred treatment approach. Outcome assessments were collected at baseline, after 6-months, 12-months (end-of-treatment) and 18-month follow-up [22].

Measures

A range of instruments was used to assess treatment outcomes and potential predictors for the treatment outcome. The total score of the Eating Disorder Examination Interview (EDE; [24]) was selected as the primary outcome variable as it represents the gold-standard assessment of eating disorder pathology. Trained psychologists conducted the EDE interview at baseline, 6, 12 and 18-months follow-up. Higher EDE total scores represent higher EDE symptomatology (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.93).

For the present study, the following predictor variables were considered (obtained at baseline). BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m²) and then converted into an age- and sex‐specific standard deviation score (BMI‐SDS) using normative data [25], while weight suppression was defined as the difference between the highest self-reported premorbid weight and the current weight [26]. Baseline eating disorder psychopathology was assessed using the total scores of the EDE (interview) described above and the Eating Disorder Inventory-2 (EDI-2; [27]), a self‐report questionnaire capturing cognitive and behavioral symptoms (total score: Cronbach’s alpha = 0.90). Depressive symptoms were measured with the total score (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.92) of the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI; [28]). Information on the age of onset and duration of the eating disorder was obtained via the clinical interview. In addition, treatment motivation prior to treatment start was evaluated with the Anorexia Nervosa Specific Outcome Questionnaire (ANSOCQ [29, 30]), with higher values indicating higher treatment motivation (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.76). Moreover, temperament and character traits were assessed using the Junior Temperament and Character Inventory (JTCI; [31]). Four pre-selected dimensions were obtained in the present study (harm avoidance, persistence, self-directedness, cooperativeness) with internal consistencies of these subscale scores ranging between 0.82 and 0.86.

The selection of instruments for this study was based on their established psychometric properties and widespread use in eating disorders research. The EDE and EDI-2 provide complementary clinician- and self-report perspectives on eating disorder psychopathology, while the BDI assesses a common comorbidity that may influence treatment outcomes. BMI and weight suppression are objective indicators of physical health and nutritional status, and the inclusion of age of onset and illness duration allows for an exploration of developmental factors. The ANSOCQ was chosen to capture aspects of treatment-specific motivation. Finally, the JTCI was included to examine the role of temperament and character traits as potential predictors of treatment response, given the evidence linking personality factors with eating disorder course and outcome. The selected subscales (harm avoidance, persistence, self-directedness and cooperativeness) reflect personality features commonly observed in anorexia nervosa and capture domains relevant to MANTRa, including anxiety-related avoidance, rigidity, self-regualtion and interpersonal functioning [32, 33].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics 29.0 and JASP 19.0 and R software. First, a series of univariate linear regression analyses were performed to explore whether different indicators of baseline illness severity (EDE, EDI-2, BDI total scores, BMI-SDS, weight suppression, age of eating disorder onset, illness duration) as well as baseline treatment motivation and personality indicators (ANSOCQ and JTCI scores) can predict eating disorder pathology (EDE total score) at 18-month follow-up. The baseline EDE score was included as covariate in all models.

To reduce the complexity of predictor analyses and to reduce the number of statistical tests and thus to avoid alpha-inflation, we conducted two cluster analyses to identify subgroups based on (1) illness severity indicators and (2) treatment motivation and personality indicators. Personality traits and treatment motivation were considered in one cluster analysis because both dimensions reflect an individual’s internal experience and self-regulatory capacities, which influence treatment engagement and responsiveness to change. For instance, a less self-critical and more flexible personality profile may enhance a patient’s willingness to change, thereby aligning with higher intrinsic motivation. Together, these aspects offer a more comprehensive insight into an individual’s readiness for change and potential for a favorable treatment outcome. A two-step cluster analysis was performed. In step one, cases were pre-clustered using a sequential clustering algorithm based on a cluster feature (CF) tree. In step two, an agglomerative hierarchical clustering method was applied to the pre-clusters. Log-likelihood distance was used to determine similarity between cases. Given the limited overall sample size, the number of clusters was fixed to two. In the first cluster analysis (illness severity), baseline values for the EDE, EDE-2, BDI, BMI-SDS, weight suppression, age of onset and illness duration were included as indicator variables. In the second cluster analysis (motivation/personality), the ANSOCQ total score and JTCI subscale scores (harm avoidance, persistence, self-directedness, cooperativeness) were included as indicator variables. The final cluster solutions were saved into a new variable for further analysis. Cluster quality was assessed using the silhouette coefficient of cohesion and separation. The average silhouette value was 0.4 in both cluster analyses indicating moderate separation between clusters. To determine whether the clusters differentiate between high vs. low illness severity and a favorable vs. unfavorable personality/motivation profile, respectively, t-tests for independent samples were subsequently performed to explore differences in indicator variables between the clusters.

In the next step, we analyzed differences in EDE scores measured at end-of-treatment (12-month follow-up) and the 18-month follow-up between illness severity and motivation/personality clusters using independent t-tests. Additionally, effect sizes for cluster differences were calculated in terms of Cohen’s d. Notably, these initial t-tests did not correct for any covariates or baseline eating disorder pathology.

Finally, we conducted multilevel linear mixed (MLM) models using the lm4-package (version 1.1–35.3; [34]) in R were conducted to address the research question whether illness severity clusters (model 1) and motivation/personality clusters (model 2) moderate trajectories in EDE scores over time. In these models, which were separately fitted for the MANTRa and TAU groups, the EDE total score measured at all four time points were used as the outcome variable, the cluster, measurement time and cluster x time interaction were included as fixed effects. The subject was included as random effect. Additionally, a random slope for measurement time point was added to allow that EDE scores of subjects change differently over time.

As this study represents a secondary analysis of the trial by Wittek et al. [5], no a-priori sample size calculation was performed. Sensitivity analyses based on the achieved sample size indicated that, in the TAU group, the study was sufficiently powered (> 80%) to detect large differences in EDE scores between the illness severity and motivation/personality clusters using t-tests for independent samples, whereas power in the MANTRa group was below 50%. With the available sample size, only large effects Cohen’s d > 1.0) could be detected with adequate statistical power (≥ 80%).

Results

Sample characteristics and main outcome parameters

The study included 92 female participants aged between 13 and 21 years diagnosed with full-syndrome AN, atypical AN, or weight-restored AN who received either MANTRa (n = 45) or TAU (n = 47). At baseline, mean age was 16.56 in the MANTRa and 15.95 years in the TAU group, average BMI was 16.64 (SD 1.46) and 16.9 (SD 1.48) and eating disorder duration was 17.73, respectively 16.32 months. In both groups, 51.1% of patients received inpatient treatment prior to the study inclusion and most patients were diagnosed with AN restrictive type (88.9% in the MANTRa and 86.4% in the TAU group). 35.6% of patients in the MANTRa group and 58.7% in the TAU group reported any current psychiatric comorbidity. Details of the sample have already been reported [5]. Of the 45 patients having started MANTRa therapy, 41, 35 and 35 provided data at 6, 12 and 18-months follow-up (22% dropout at the last follow-up). Of the 47 patients assigned to the TAU group, 40, 36 and 38 provided data at the follow-ups (19% dropout at the last follow-up). More details about the sample are published Wittek et al. [5].

Predictors of the EDE total score at 18-months follow-up from univariate analyses

At the 18-month follow-up neither an indicator for baseline illness severity, treatment motivation nor personality traits significantly predicted the EDE total score in the MANTRa group. In the TAU group, however, higher baseline EDE total scores (t = 3.526, p= .001) and EDI-2 total scores (t = 2.435, p= .020) were significantly associated with higher eating disorder symptomatology after 18 months. Additionally, lower baseline treatment motivation, as measured by the ANSOQ total score (t= – 2.175, p= .037), was linked to higher eating disorder psychopathology at 18-month follow-up for TAU (see Table 1).

Illness severity and motivation/personality clusters

For further analyses, we performed a two-step cluster analyses on (1) baseline illness severity indicators and (2) variables measuring baseline treatment motivation and personality. Regarding illness severity, the two identified clusters reflected patients with rather high baseline severity (N = 54, MANTRa: n = 31, TAU: n = 23) and patients with rather low baseline severity (N = 22, MANTRa: n = 11, TAU: n = 11). Data were missing for 16 participants; thus, they were not assigned to a cluster. Patients from the high severity cluster showed significantly higher EDE (t(74) = 7.413, p < .001), higher EDI-2 (t(74) = 10.246, p < .001) and BDI total scores (t(74) = 8.663, p < .001). In contrast, BMI, weight suppression, age of onset and illness duration did not differ between the clusters.

For treatment motivation and personality traits, the first cluster (subsequently named “unfavorable profile”) comprised 52 participants (MANTRa: n = 27, TAU: n = 25), while the second cluster (subsequently named “favorable profile”) included 30 participants (MANTRa: n = 16, TAU: n = 14). Data were missing for 10 participants. Patients in the unfavorable profile cluster had a significantly lower treatment motivation (t(80)= – 6.986, p < .001), higher harm avoidance (t(80) = 8.016, p < .001), lower self-directedness (t(80)= – 9.236, p < .001) and lower cooperativeness (t(80)= -3.424, p < .001) compared to patients in the favorable profile cluster. No significant difference between the clusters were observed for persistence. Details about cluster characteristics are provided in supplementary Table S1. Moreover, a dropout analysis comparing patients who were included in the cluster analysis vs. patients who were excluded due to missing data yielded no systematic dropout (see Table S2 for details).

Difference between clusters at end-of-treatment

At the end of treatment (12-months follow-up), the two illness severity clusters differed significantly in their EDE total scores in both treatment arms (MANTRa: p= .044, TAU: p= .004). Patients with high baseline illness severity showed poorer treatment outcomes than those with low baseline severity in both conditions. However, the effect of this difference was larger in the TAU group (MANTRa: d = 0.84, TAU: d = 1.29), indicating a more pronounced negative impact of high initial illness severity under TAU. When looking at the treatment motivation and personality clusters, the difference between the groups were even more pronounced. While in the MANTRa group, patients with an unfavorable profile had similar eating disorder symptomatology compared to patients with a favorable profile (p= .665, d = 0.15), in the TAU group the unfavorable cluster had a significant higher EDE total score at end-of-treatment compared to the favorable cluster (p = .004, d = 1.16). This indicates that an initial profile of low treatment motivation, high harm avoidance, low self-directedness and high cooperativeness had a negative impact on treatment outcomes under TAU but not under MANTRa. Difference in outcome trajectories by clusters

Figure 2 illustrates outcome trajectories by cluster assignment. Based on visual inspection only, patients in the high severity cluster appeared to have consistently higher EDE total scores across all time points in both the MANTRa and TAU groups. In the MANTRa group, patients with high illness severity appeared to show a decrease in eating disorder symptomatology over time that was visually comparable to that of the low-severity cluster (see part A1 of Fig. 2). In contrast, in the TAU group, patients with high illness severity appeared to show a visually smaller reduction in symptomatology compared to those with lower illness severity (see part A2 of Fig. 2). A similar descriptive pattern was observed regarding treatment motivation and personality clusters. Based on visual inspection of Fig. 2, in the MANTRa group, patients with an unfavorable profile appeared to show visually comparable over time relative to those with a favorable profile (part B1 in Fig. 2). In the TAU group, however, patients with an unfavorable profile appeared to show smaller improvements in outcome scores (part B2 in Fig. 2).

Difference in outcome trajectories by cluster assignment and group

Results from the MLM models indicated that EDE scores significantly decreased over time in both the MANTRa and TAU groups (fixed effect of time; p < .001 in all models), reflecting overall improvement in ED pathology. In both treatment groups, patients assigned to the high illness severity cluster showed significantly higher EDE scores across all time points compared to those in the low-severity cluster (fixed effect of cluster ED severity; MANTRa: p < .001, TAU: p= .002), indicating a stable difference in in overall symptom level. However, the time x cluster interaction was not statistically significant in either treatment group, suggesting that the rate of symptom change over time did not differ between clusters. In other words, although baseline symptom levels differed, both severity clusters showed comparable trajectories of change.

A similar pattern emerged for the motivation/personality clusters. Patients with an unfavorable profile showed significantly higher EDE scores across time (fixed effect of cluster motivation/personality; MANTRA: p < .001, TAU: p = .004). Again, the time x cluster interaction was not statistically significant, indicating no differential change in EDE trajectories between favorable and unfavorable profiles.

Thus, the visually apparent differences in outcome trajectories shown in Fig. 2 were not statistically supported by the results of the MLM models (Table 2).

Discussion

The present analysis provides new insights into the outcome predictors and moderators of MANTRa therapy in adolescents with AN. Whether analyzed as a whole or divided into clusters, trends towards differences between MANTRa and TAU emerge. Our results provide preliminary evidence that MANTRa may be particularly more effective than TAU for patients with more severe illness and lower initial motivation. These findings, which are partly based on non-significant trends, require replication in future studies with larger sample size and enough power.

Using univariate regression models, in the MANTRa group, no significant baseline predictors emerge that forecast eating disorder psychopathology at the final follow-up, which contrasts with previous literature where a higher BMI and shorter illness duration are typically associated with better outcomes [35]. However, in the TAU group a trend in that direction is observable, but neither BMI nor illness duration reached significance in the TAU group, possibly due to a small sample size.

Additionally, higher treatment motivation and more favorable personality traits generally indicate a better prognosis, which is not the case in this study for the MANTRA group. This may be the case because MANTRa incorporates elements specifically addressing low motivation to change, like motivational interviewing and therapists’ letter to the patients. While the literature indicates no clear effect of motivational interviewing in treatment of AN [11], motivational interviewing is fundamentally a person-centered approach that emphasizes partnership, autonomy and the evocation of patients own motivations for change [36], which may enhance engagement even in initially low-motivated patients. A recent qualitative study with MANTRa therapists further points to the motivational effects of therapist’s letters to the patients which are part of the case conceptualization and which are read to the patients during a session [7]. The focus on enhancing motivation could explain the efficacy of MANTRa especially among patients with lower baseline motivation.

Baseline eating disorder psychopathology does emerge as a significant outcome predictor in the TAU group, while not affecting MANTRa group. This finding aligns well with the mixed results reported in other studies [10, 21], some of which suggest that psychopathology is a predictive factor, while others highlight motivation as more critical. The design and underlying concept of MANTRa, including its integration of motivational interviewing and its foundation in the cognitive-interpersonal maintenance model, may be key factors contributing to its efficacy. MANTRa’s modular approach may particularly excel in addressing and processing the underlying pathology. Evidence from previous research further supports this interpretation, as MANTRA has been shown to be highly acceptable to patients and to demonstrate trends toward better outcomes in individuals with greater illness severity, suggesting that its structure may be particularly suited to addressing core psychopathological features [37].

The subdivision into favorable and unfavorable clusters further underscores the previously described results. Although no significant moderation effects in outcome trajectories were observed, a strong trend is evident. In direct comparison with TAU, MANTRa appears particularly beneficial for patients with higher eating disorder severity and an unfavorable motivation/personality profile. Even though both groups initially show improvement, the trend in the MANTRa group continues until the final follow-up, in contrast to TAU. As mentioned before, these statistically non-significant trends need further validation in future studies. Notably, by end-of-treatment, no significant difference between clusters was observed in the MANTRa group, while in TAU, a difference between the clusters persists until the end. The superior efficacy in the less favorable clusters may explain why the full recovery rate in the main study was significantly higher for MANTRa than for TAU [5]. Apart from low treatment motivation, MANTRa may be particularly effective for patients with notably low self-directedness (a personality trait reflecting difficulties in regulating behavior to pursue personal goals and values), harm avoidance (characterized by heightened fear of uncertainty, shyness, and worry) and low cooperativeness (which includes, among others, social disinterest). The collaborative and patient centered stance of MANTRa, in which therapist and patient jointly determine which treatment elements are most relevant and how they should be sequenced, together with its flexible structure, may facilitate more goal directed and adaptive behavior. Its focus on emotions and interpersonal processes, including work on emotional expression, perspective taking and relationship patterns, may further help patients navigate situations characterized by uncertainty and support the development of more positive interactions with others [8].

These findings provide preliminary evidence that MANTRa is a therapeutic approach capable of assisting a broad spectrum of patients. Its particular strength lies in its apparent potential to engage even those patients who exhibit low motivation or are deeply entrenched in their illness. Pending confirmation in future studies, MANTRa may hold promise as a first-line therapy for adolescents with AN. Future studies should ideally include a randomized controlled trial comparing MANTRa with other evidence-based interventions for adolescents with AN. From the current perspective, however, MANTRa appears to be a realistic alternative for a large number of patients.

The study has several strengths. First, the multi-center design enhances external validity and supports the generalizability of the findings, while the longitudinal nature of the study, with assessments at baseline, 6, 12, and 18 months, allows for a detailed examination of changes over time. The comprehensive assessment strategy, which includes both objective measures (such as BMI and weight suppression) and subjective instruments (such as the EDI, BDI, and ANSOCQ), enables an in-depth analysis of predictors and moderators of the outcome. In addition, focusing on predictors within the context of the adapted MANTRa therapy for adolescents offers a novel contribution to the literature, given the relative paucity of research in this specific area.

However, there are several important limitations to consider when interpreting the findings of this secondary analysis. First, the use of secondary data inherently limits the study, as the original data collection was not explicitly designed to identify or thoroughly investigate predictors or moderators of treatment outcome. Consequently, potentially other relevant variables or important confounding factors, such as coping strategies, social support networks, or additional psychiatric comorbidities, might not have been captured adequately or at all. This could limit the comprehensiveness of the predictor models and potentially obscure the identification of relevant influences on treatment success.

Moreover, the non-randomized allocation of participants into the MANTRa and TAU groups introduces potential selection biases. Systematic differences between the groups at baseline, beyond those measured, cannot be fully ruled out. This limitation is particularly critical given that selection bias could significantly affect the internal validity and thus the interpretation of comparative efficacy between MANTRa and TAU.

In addition, the considerable heterogeneity within the TAU condition, which comprises various therapeutic methods (Systemic Family Therapy, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy, Psychodynamic Therapies and Psychoanalysis or Humanistic Therapies [5]), rather than one standardized approach, potentially weakens the comparability and interpretability of results. As treatment modalities within the TAU condition vary widely, drawing firm conclusions about the specific efficacy of MANTRa relative to a clearly defined control treatment becomes challenging.

The relatively small sample size (n = 92), particularly within the context of a longitudinal study with multiple follow-up assessments, further limits the statistical power of the analyses. Attrition rates over the extended follow-up period could exacerbate this issue, reducing the study’s ability to detect statistically significant predictors or differences between groups, particularly for subtle effects. Consequently, the non-significance of certain predictors could reflect limitations in statistical power rather than a genuine absence of associations.

Lastly, the sample consists exclusively of female adolescents and young adults from clinical centers in Austria, significantly limiting the generalizability of the results. The absence of male participants and the study’s specific cultural and geographic context may restrict the applicability of the findings to other populations or settings. Future research should seek to replicate these findings within more diverse demographic groups, including male participants and individuals from varied cultural backgrounds, to enhance the external validity and applicability of the observed effects.

To conclude, the present study provides preliminary evidence that the MANTRa approach is effective for a broad range of adolescent and young adult patients with AN, including those with high illness severity, low treatment motivation and an unfavorable personality profile. These findings need replication in future studies that compare outcome moderators for the MANTRa approach to specific other evidence-based treatments for AN.
