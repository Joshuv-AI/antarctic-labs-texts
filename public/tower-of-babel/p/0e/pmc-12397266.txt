A novel chromen-based small molecule induces apoptosis and modulates cellular response to triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Bernardine Tuah, Kwadwo Fosu, Diana Ahu Prah, Bright Kwame Yayra Hodogbe, Alberta Serwaa, Jutsum Nii Kotei Amon, Daniel Moscoh Ayine-Tora, Richard Kwamla Amewu, Kwabena Amofa Nketia Sarpong, Anastasia Rosebud Aikins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is challenging to treat due to its aggressive nature. Its lack of hormone receptors renders conventional therapies less effective. This study assessed the efficacy of a novel compound, compound 2, in modulating TNBC cell behaviour. We used in vitro assays with MDA-MB-468 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines. Methods included annexin V apoptosis assay, flow cytometry for cell cycle and qRT-PCR for gene expression. Clonogenic, adhesion and wound healing assays were used for phenotypic characterization. Cytokine and chemokine levels in MDA-MB-468 cells were also measured using a Luminex assay. Compound 2 increased both early and late apoptosis in cancer cells, particularly MDA MB 468 cells. It also upregulated pro-apoptotic genes while downregulating anti-apoptotic genes. Additionally, it induced G1-phase arrest in MDA MB 468 cells with downregulation in Ki67 expression. Compound 2 also reduced cancer stem cell populations, suppressed colony formation, and impaired cell migration at IC50 concentrations. Significant changes in gene expression profiles for EMT-related genes were observed. Compound 2 decreased IL4 and IL8 levels and increased CCL2 and CXCL1. However, it did not significantly affect the levels of IL6, IL10, CXCL2, CCL5, TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-1β, and IL2. Compound 2 thus exhibited a multifaceted anticancer profile, suggesting its potential in preventing cancer relapse and limiting cell proliferation which makes it a promising candidate for TNBC targeted therapy. This study lays the groundwork for further in vivo studies and potential clinical applications to explore full therapeutic potential of compound 2 in aggressive breast cancer types.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is a prevalent malignancy that affects women worldwide1. In 2022, the number of reported BC cases reached 2.3 million, resulting in 670,000 deaths2. Similar to any other disease, these figures represent only reported cases, and the actual number of individuals affected by breast cancer may be higher, as some cases may go undiagnosed or unreported. The disease encompasses various subtypes with distinct molecular characteristics, prognosis, and treatment responses3,4.

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a highly aggressive and heterogeneous subtype of breast cancer, accounts for approximately 10–15% of all breast cancer cases globally and presents significant challenges due to its aggressive behaviour, high relapse rates, and limited treatment options5–7. Although chemotherapy remains the primary approach for TNBC, it faces obstacles such as drug resistance and cytotoxicity to healthy cells8,9. Also, the molecular landscape of TNBC contributes to its varying responses to chemotherapy10. Targeted therapies aimed at specific pathways may enhance sensitivity to chemotherapy but can also suppress the immune system, posing additional complexities in finding effective TNBC treatments10,11. Thus, there is an urgent need to identify novel and effective therapeutic strategies for TNBC management6,12.

Standard chemotherapy for TNBC typically includes anthracyclines, alkylating agents, taxanes, and fluorouracil. This regimen is administered as neoadjuvant therapy, followed by surgery for early-stage cases. However, in cases of relapsed or refractory TNBC, there is no established standard treatment. Responses to existing options such as capecitabine, gemcitabine, eribulin, and platinum-based agents are often short-lived, and metastases remain common13. Current research focuses on discovering new drugs and repurposing existing ones for TNBC treatment. Targeted therapies that block specific molecules and pathways associated with TNBC are gaining prominence.Small-molecule compounds have become crucial in drug discovery, especially in the battle against cancer. They can block cancer-promoting pathways, trigger cell death in cancer cells, and alter the tumour environment to boost the effectiveness of other treatments14. Furthermore, advances in medicinal chemistry and computational biology have enhanced the design and optimisation of small molecules and increased selectivity while reducing adverse effects15. Three small molecules (compounds 1, 2 and 3) previously identified to target Leishmania16. Our previous studies utilized cytotoxicity screening and computer-aided drug discovery method to assess the anticancer potential of three compounds, based on the previously established connection between anticancer and antileishmanial drugs17,18 as well as the core scaffolds of these compounds (chromen-based and triazolopyridazine-based). Initial cytotoxicity and selectivity assays were performed using four cancer cell lines: MDA-MB-468, MDA-MB-231, DLD-1, PC3 and the normal prostate epithelial line PNT2 to evaluate compound 2’s selectivity. Compound 2 which is a chromen-based compound, (PubChem ID: STK610045) emerged as the lead molecule, exhibiting potent and selective cytotoxicity against TNBC cell lines, particularly MDA-MB-468 (IC50 = 9.46 µM) and MDA-MB-231 (IC50 = 18.30 µM), while sparing normal prostate epithelial cells (PNT2), indicating a favourable selectivity index. Network pharmacology, docking, and molecular dynamics simulations identified AKT1, EGFR and VEGFR2 as compound 2’s most likely targets. Compound 2 bound strongly to the ATP-binding site (Lys745) and DFG motif (Asp855) of EGFR, as well as the ATP-binding site (Cys919) of VEGFR2, with both interactions showing favourable binding free energy19. These findings support the hypothesis that compound 2 exerts its anticancer effects via targeted inhibition of EGFR signalling and related oncogenic pathways.

Chromen-based compounds have been found to exhibit anticancer characteristics because they disrupt multiple cellular processes that contribute to cancer growth. They can cause apoptosis, slow cell development, and disrupt the cell cycle of cancer cells. Furthermore, chromen-based compounds have also been shown to influence the tumour microenvironment and improve the efficacy of other therapies20,21. As researchers continue to investigate the various modes of action of chromen-based compounds, these chemicals are promising candidates for developing innovative cancer therapeutics. Systematic in vitro screening of compound 2 was therefore necessary to elucidate its potential anticancer mechanisms and validate its utility as a therapeutic candidate for aggressive breast cancers like TNBC.

In this study, we conducted further in vitro studies built upon previous findings to validate the biological activity of compound 2 in TNBC cells and explore its downstream effects on apoptosis, cell cycle, and EMT. We showed that Compound 2 caused a G1-cell arrest in MDA-MB-468 cells and triggered significant late apoptosis in most cell populations. Furthermore, it affected EMT markers gene expression, cell migration, and cancer stem markers all of which have implications for cancer metastasis. Our findings further show the promise compound 2 holds as a potential therapeutic target for cancer, especially, TNBCs.

Materials and methods

Cancer cell culture and compounds

MDA-MB-468, and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell lines were obtained from ATCC and cultured in DMEM supplemented with fetal bovine serum (10%) and penicillin-streptomycin (1%) (Gibco-life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Compound 2 was purchased from Vitas M lab and doxorubicin (DOX) (D1515-10MG) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA). for most of the assays, MDA-MB-468 cells and MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded at 1 × 106 in 100 mm TC-treated dishes overnight. Subsequently, the cells were treated with the respective IC50 concentrations of compound 2 and DOX (used as the positive control in all assays); 9.46 and 0.8µM (from our initial studies) for 48 h.

Apoptosis

Following the manufacturer’s protocol, apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit (Sigma, APOAF-20TST). Briefly, MDA-MB-468 and MDA-MB-231 cells were overnight and treated for 48 h with the respective IC50 concentrations of compound 2 and DOX. After trypsinisation and centrifugation, 1 × 106 cells were resuspended in 1 mL of 1X binding buffer. Subsequently, 500 µL of each cell suspension was pipetted into labelled FACS tubes. Cells were stained with 5 µL of Annexin V and 10 µL of propidium iodide (PI), followed by incubation in the dark for 10 min. Apoptosis was analysed using a BD FACS LSR FoetessaTM X-20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and the results were analysed using Flowjo v10.9. Cells positive for Annexin V only, were considered early apoptotic, while those positive for both Annexin V and PI were considered late apoptotic. PI staining alone indicated mechanically damaged cells. Duplicate-independent experiments were performed for each cell line.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription real-time PCR

MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 cells were seeded and treated for 48 h. RNA extraction was performed using a Qiagen RNeasy Mini kit according to manufacturer`s instructions. The RNA yield was quantified using a nanodrop. Approximately 10 ng of RNA was used for RT-qPCR assays using the Luna Universal One-Step RT-qPCR kit (New England Biolabs). The mRNA expression levels of the proliferation marker (Ki67), apoptosis-related genes (Bcl-2, Bax, caspases 3 and 8), EMT genes (SNAI1, TWIST1 and Vimentin), and invasion and metastasis-related genes (MMP2, MMP9, VCAM1) were assessed in triplicates. GADPH served as the housekeeping gene. The primer sequences for each gene are listed in Table 1.

Clonogenic assay

We performed a colony formation assay to investigate the effect of compound 2 on breast cancer cell stemness. In a 6-well plate, MDA-MB-468 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 103 cells/well and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h22. Subsequently, cells were treated with either ¼ IC50, ½ IC50, IC50, and 2xIC50 concentrations of compound 2 or DOX for 48 h. After two days, the medium was removed, and the fresh medium was replaced every three days for 14 days. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet dye. Images were captured using an inverted light microscope (OPTIKA® Ponteranica, Italy). The expansion of clonogenic cells was quantified using the ImageJ plugin ColonyArea, and a graph of colony area percentage versus drug concentration was generated.

Cancer stemness immunophenotyping

MDA-MB-468 cells were seeded and treated for 48 h with the IC50 concentrations of compound 2 and DOX respectively. Detection of the cell surface markers CD44+/CD24+ in cancer stem cells (CSC) was performed following the protocol by Guptal et al.23. The results were subsequently analysed using Flowjo v10.9. Duplicate independent experiments were performed.

Cell cycle analysis

MDA-MB-468 cells were seeded and treated with the respective IC50 concentrations of compound 2 and DOX for 24 h. After treatment, the cells were trypsinized and collected as a suspension. Following centrifugation, the cells were fixed using 70% ice-cold ethanol and added dropwise while gently vortexing. The fixed cells were then incubated at -20 °C overnight. The cells were washed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline to remove residual ethanol and treated with 50 µL of 1 mg/mL RNase A. Subsequently, PI staining was performed, and the cells were incubated for 4 h at 4 °C. Flow cytometry analysis was conducted, and the results were analysed using Flowjo v10.9. Duplicate independent experiment was carried out.

Adhesion assay

MDA-MB-468 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate and incubated for 6 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with varying compound 2 and DOX concentrations and incubated for 24 h. After treatment, the cells were collected, and 1000 cells were reseeded in fresh media onto a gelatin-coated 96-well plate. This reseeding step allowed the cells to adhere over 6 h. Following adhesion, the plate was washed to remove any non-adherent cells and adherent cells fixed using 5% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet. Three random images of adherent cells were captured for each treatment from the 96-well plate. Finally, the crystal violet was dissolved in DMSO and absorbance was read at 570 nm.

Wound healing assay

A wound-healing assay was conducted to assess the effect of the compounds on cell migration. MDA-MB-468 cells were used for this study. Specifically, 70 µL of a cell suspension containing 2 × 104 cells were seeded into a 35 mm µ-dish chamber equipped with a culture insert (Ibidi GmbH, Munich, Germany) and placed in a 6-well plate. The cel ls were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with the IC50 and IC25 concentrations of either compound 2 (9.46 and 4.73µM) or DOX (0.8 and 0.4 µM). Cell migration was monitored using an inverted light microscope (OPTIKA® Ponteranica, Italy) at 0, 12-, 24-, 36-, and 48-h post-treatment. The acquired images were analysed using the Wound Healing Size Tool plugin in ImageJ to determine the percentage of wound closure. The experiment was performed in triplicates for two independent experiments.

Luminex assay for determination of cytokines concentration

After treatment of MDA-MB-468 cells for 48 h, the cells were trypsinized and collected as a suspension. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was harvested for cytokine concentration determination using a 12-plex Luminex Discovery Assay, Human Premixed Multi-Analyte Kit (R&D System), following the manufacturer`s protocol. Briefly, all reagents and samples were maintained at room temperature before use. Cytokine standards (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, IFN-δ, IL-2, CCL2, CCL5, CXCL1, CXCL2 were reconstituted, and serial dilutions prepared. Microparticle and biotin-antibody cocktails were diluted. To each well, 50 uL of supernatant from compound 2, DOX-treated cells, the untreated cell, or the standards were aliquoted, followed by incubation. After washing steps, streptavidin-PE was added. Finally, fluorescence data acquisition allowed the determination of cytokine concentrations. The concentration of each cytokine was determined by comparing the sample fluorescence against the generated standard curve. This assay was done in duplicate.

Data analysis

GraphPad Prism 9.1.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, USA) was used to analysis the dataset. A one-way ANOVA was conducted, followed by comparisons among multiple groups. The results were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistically significant group differences were defined as P < .05.

Results

Compound 2 induced apoptosis in breast cancer cell lines

To determine the effect of compound 2 on apoptosis in MDA-MB-468 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines, flow cytometric and gene expression analyses of the selected genes were done. Compound 2 exhibited a more pronounced apoptotic effect, with 40.5% of MDA MB 468 cells in the late apoptosis phase, compared to 11.5% in MDA-MB-231 cells. MDA-MB-468-compound 2- treated cells also had a higher proportion of early and late apoptotic cells than DOX-treated cells (Fig. 1A, Q2, Q3). Gene expression analysis further demonstrated that compound 2 significantly downregulated the expression of Bcl-2 in both breast cancer cell lines. Caspases 8 and 3 were significantly upregulated in MDA-MB-468 (Fig. 1B). The Bcl-2/Bax ratios for compound 2-treated cells were 0.30 in MDA-MB-468 cells and 0.79 in MDA-MB-231 cells (Table 2). Interestingly, a similar trend was observed for DOX, although the Bcl-2/Bax ratio was higher in DOX-treated cells than in compound 2-treated cells. These findings indicated that compound 2-treated cells exhibited reduced cellular resistance to apoptosis stimuli.

Compound 2 induced a downregulation of Ki67 and G1-phase cell arrest in MDA-MB-468

In MDA-MB-468 cells, treatment with compound 2 resulted in the downregulation of Ki67 expression compared to untreated cells (p-value = 0.04) (Fig. 2A). When cells were treated with compound 2 and DOX at their IC50 concentration for 24 h, a typical DNA pattern representing the sub-G1, G1, S, and G2/M phases of the cell cycle was observed. Specifically, compound 2-treated cells exhibited a higher G1 population (49.1%) than untreated control cells (39.45%). Additionally, there was a decrease in the proportion of cells in the G2/M phase from 53.7% in the untreated cells to 44.4% in the compound 2-treated cells (Fig. 2B). Interestingly, DOX-treated cells showed a higher proportion of cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle. Furthermore, compound 2-treated cells showed a higher proportion of cells in the sub-G1 phase (apoptotic cells) at 0.73%. These findings suggest that compound 2 induces G1-phase cell cycle arrest. However, in MDA- MB-231 cells, there was no significant difference (p-value = 0.9) in Ki67 expression between untreated cells and those treated with compound 2 (Fig. 2A). As a result, the cells were not subjected to cell cycle analysis. The MDA-MB-468 cell line was then selected for further studies due to its higher sensitivity to compound 2, as reflected by a lower IC50 and more pronounced apoptosis and gene expression changes in apoptotic genes and Ki67 observed.

Compound 2 suppresses cancer stem-like properties in MDA MB 468 cells

A clonogenic assay was employed to assess the cells’ potential for re-proliferation after exposure to different concentrations of compound 2. The results revealed that compound 2 exhibited a dose-dependent inhibition of cell re-proliferation (Fig. 3A). This indicates that compound 2 may have the potential to reduce breast cancer stemness. In the immunophenotyping study, compound 2 demonstrated a significant reduction (p = .0012) in the number of CSCs in the MDA-MB-468 cell line compared to that in untreated cancer cells. In compound 2-treated cells, the CSCs expressed CD44+/CD24+ markers at a percentage of 43.3, whereas the untreated group had a percentage of 54.2. Notably, DOX induced a more pronounced reduction in the CSC population than compound 2 (Fig. 3).

Compound 2 modulates pro-metastatic and EMT-associated markers in TNBC

Compound 2 affected gene expression differently across cell lines. In MDA MB 468 cells, MMP2, MMP9, and VCAM-1 were downregulated, while MDA-MB-231 cells showed decreased MMP2 and increased MMP9, with unchanged VCAM-1 expression (Fig. 4A). All genes were upregulated in DOX-treated cells, with significant VCAM-1 overexpression (Fig. 4A). EMT-related gene expression varied between the two breast cancer cell lines (Fig. 4B). In MDA- MB- 468 compound 2-treated cells, TWIST1 was upregulated but not significantly, while no difference was observed in MDA-MB-231 cells. SNAI1 showed significant downregulation in MDA-MB-468 treated cells but no change in MDA-MB-231 cells. Vimentin was significantly upregulated in MDA-MB-468 cells but downregulated in MDA-MB-231 treated cells (Fig. 4B). These results suggest compound 2 affects EMT-related processes differently between cell lines and inhibits cell migration in MDA- MB-468 cells.

Compound 2 inhibited cellular adhesion of MDA MB 468 cells

Cells were cultured with two doses (IC50 and IC25, 9.46 µM and 4.73 µM) of compound 2 and DOX (0.8 µM and 0.40 µM) for 24 h, and then the pretreated cells were reseeded for an additional 6 h. Both compound 2 and DOX demonstrated significant dose-dependent inhibition of cellular adhesion in MDA-MB-468 cells compared to the untreated control (Fig. 5A,B). Remarkably, cells treated with the IC50 concentration of compound 2 exhibited the most pronounced inhibition, with only approximately 29.77% of adhered cells remaining post-treatment (Fig. 5A). While this difference was not statistically significant (p-value = 0.93) compared to the percentage of adhered cells (31.5%) in the IC50 DOX-treated group, a statistically significant difference (p-value < 0.001) was evident when comparing compound 2-treated cells with the untreated cells. This observation aligns with the image of cells treated with the IC50 of compound 2 (Fig. 5B), which depicts fewer attached cells than the untreated cells. These data indicate that compound 2 may help prevent cancer growth and spread.

Compound 2 showed differentially an inhibitory effect on wound closure

A wound-healing assay was used to study cell migration. This experiment treated MDA-MB-468 cells with either IC50 or IC25 concentrations of compound 2 and DOX. Initially, the IC50 dose of compound 2 inhibited migration at 12 and 24 h, followed by cell death observed at 36–48 h. Consequently, the wound area also increased. In contrast, the untreated cells exhibited wound closure in a time-dependent manner. Notably, cells treated with the IC25 dose of compound 2 consistently demonstrated inhibition of wound closure (Fig. 6). Specifically, at 48 h, the wound area was 50.54% ± 1.31% for compound 2-treated cells, compared to 26.76% ± 0.9% for untreated cells. These findings suggest that compound 2 inhibits cell migration and wound closure.

Effect of compound 2 and DOX on expression of cytokines

In this study, compound 2 increased the expression of TNF and IL-1B; however, these expressions were not significant when compared to the control. DOX significantly upregulated the expression of IL-6, IL-8, IL-4, and IL-2. Although DOX increased the expression of IL-10 and IFN-γ, its expression was insignificant compared to that in untreated cells. Compound 2 significantly affected IL-8. DOX induced higher cytokine expression than compound 2. Compound 2 upregulated CCL2 and CXCL1 expression; however, there was no significant difference between CXCL2 and CCL5 expression in any of the treated breast cancer cells. DOX significantly increased CCL2 and CXCL1 expression in the breast cancer cells compared with that in untreated cells and compound 2 (Fig. 7).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to discover a new cancer drug candidate by studying the effects of compound 2 on cell behaviour, gene expression, and cancer stemness. Our findings show that Dox induce apoptosis and inhibit proliferation. This is consistent with previous studies on Dox24–26. Our compound also induce apoptosis and inhibited cell proliferation. Compound 2 suppressed the expression of Ki67, a proliferation marker, this correlated with G1 cell cycle arrest in MDA-MB-468 cells. During the G1 phase of the cell cycle, cells encountering arrest face two possible outcomes: either they undergo DNA repair facilitated by the p53 gatekeeper or they proceed toward apoptosis if DNA repair is unfeasible27. G1 arrest can prevent cells from entering the S phase, causing an inhibition of cell proliferation28. The lower Bcl-2/Bax ratio of Compound 2 compared to doxorubicin in our studies indicates that compound 2 effectively promotes apoptosis. A higher Bcl-2/Bax ratio favours cell survival by inhibiting apoptosis, whereas a lower ratio shifts the balance between apoptosis and cell death29. A lower Bcl-2/Bax ratio in cancer cells indicates potential therapeutic benefit. However, further investigation is necessary to validate these findings and explore the precise underlying mechanisms. Gene expression analysis revealed that compound 2 promotes apoptosis by upregulating pro-apoptotic genes Bax and Caspase 3 and downregulating the anti-apoptotic gene, Bcl-2. It induces G1-phase cell cycle arrest by downregulating Ki67 levels, suggesting interference with cell cycle progression.

Aside evading apoptosis, another key mechanism for cancer progression is anchorage-dependent growth, which involves adherence to an extracellular matrix (ECM) and cell migration30,31. Compound 2, like the standard chemotherapeutic agent DOX, suppressed cancer cell adhesion to the ECM component and inhibited cell migration. Cancer cells adhere to the ECM to secrete enzymes like MMP2 and MMP9, which degrade ECM components. This process supports tissue invasion and triggers the release of pro-angiogenic factors32,33. Compound 2 suppressed expression of adhesion marker VCAM-1 and cell migration markers MMP2, MMP9 in MDA-MB-468 but not in MDA-MB-231.This may suggest a distinct mechanism of action for compound 2 in the different breast cancer subtype. Our findings shows that Compound 2 may modulate EMT-related pathways by suppressing SNAI1 and promoting Vimentin. SNAI1 and Vimentin promotes mesenchymal characteristics34. Conversely, the downregulation of Vimentin in MDA-MB-231 cells from this study suggests an opposing effect on EMT. This indicates that Compound 2 modulates EMT differently in distinct breast cancer cell lines. Its differential effect on different breast cancer cell lines highlights the complexity of EMT regulation34,35. Understanding these differential effects can guide personalised treatment strategies. Nevertheless, compound 2 show a clear inhibitory effect on cell migration and adhesion indicated that compound 2 indicating its potential to suppress cancer progression and metastasis.

Reducing the population of CSCs is a promising discovery, as they play crucial roles in tumour initiation, progression, and therapy resistance. In breast cancer, CD44+/CD24−/low is a typical CSC marker, but MDA-MB-468 cells predominantly exhibit CD44+/CD24+ expression36–38. A recent study also supports the concept of interconversion between CD44+/CD24− and CD44+/CD24+ phenotypes in MDA-MB-468 cells39. This aligns with our findings and suggests phenotype interconversion during metastasis. From our studies, DOX significantly reduced CSCs more than compound 2, indicating a potent anti-CSC effect. However, compound 2 showed strong suppression of cancer regrowth near its IC50These findings suggest compound 2 may help prevent relapse and enhance standard treatments, though further studies are needed to clarify underlying mechanisms.

Cytokines are critical mediators of the immune response and play a pivotal role in cancer progression by influencing tumour growth, metastasis, and tumour microenvironment. Our findings provide significant insights into how compound 2 and DOX modulate the inflammatory responses, which can, in turn, impact breast cancer progression. In this study, compound 2 did not show any significant increase in IL-6, IL-1β, IL-8, IL-2 levels suggesting a potentially less aggressive inflammatory response. The absence of an effect on pro-inflammatory cytokines suggests that compound 2 could be a candidate for combination therapy and drugs that specifically reduce these cytokines could be used alongside compound 2 to achieve a more comprehensive anti-tumour effect. There was an increase in IL-6 and IL-8 expressions upon treatment with DOX and this is consistent with findings from other studies and indicates a robust inflammatory reaction40–42. This upregulation is believed to be associated with angiogenesis and EMT, potentially promoting cancer aggressiveness. While DOX reduced IL-1β levels contrary to some studies, indicating complex inflammatory responses43,44. Both DOX and Compound 2 increased TNF-α, a cytokine involved in inflammation and tumour progression, with a milder effect than that observed in the control. Anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 and IL-4 help regulate and suppress excessive immune responses. IL-10 levels were detectable but not significant in either treatment, indicating a minimal anti-inflammatory response. DOX increased IL-4 levels, which may be associated with tumour growth promotion, whereas Compound 2 did not significantly elevate IL-4 levels, suggesting a less tumour-promoting immune profile. IFN-γ levels were decreased in both compound 2 and DOX -treated cells even though this was not statistically significant. However, both treatments significantly elevated the chemokine, CCL2. This might hinder the release of IFN-γ by T cells, which weakens the body’s ability to fight tumours and facilitates tumour growth45,46. CXCL1 levels were highest with DOX, promoting angiogenesis and tumour growth47,48while compound 2 resulted in a moderate increase in CXCL1 levels. Its ability to modulate inflammation and immune responses differently than DOX suggests that it may offer therapeutic benefits with potentially reduced side effects related to inflammation and immune suppression.

In summary, compound 2 shows promise as a targeted cancer therapy by inhibiting proliferation, migration, and EMT-related gene expression, while promoting apoptosis through a reduced Bcl-2/Bax ratio. It significantly lowers cancer stemness, suggesting a role in preventing relapse. Unlike DOX, compound 2 induces a milder inflammatory response with lower levels of tumour-promoting cytokines, indicating fewer side effects. Its immune modulation profile supports its potential as an alternative or complementary therapy for breast cancer. This study corroborates previous in silico findings identifying EGFR, VEGFR2 and AKT1 as plausible targets of compound 219. With EGFR overexpressed in TNBC, particularly basal-like subtypes, these interactions suggest that compound 2 likely impairs EGFR kinase activity by blocking ATP access, thereby inhibiting downstream signalling cascades such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK, which are central to cell proliferation, survival, and migration in TNBC. Notably, VEGFR2 is also frequently co-expressed with EGFR in TNBC and cooperates through autocrine and paracrine mechanisms to enhance tumour growth and angiogenesis. The dual targeting of EGFR and VEGFR2 has shown promise in preclinical models, with bispecific antibodies demonstrating potent inhibition of both receptors and disruption of the autocrine/paracrine loop49. Therefore, compound 2 may exert broader anti-tumour effects if it also interferes with VEGFR2-mediated signalling, either directly or indirectly via EGFR-VEGFR2 crosstalk. Further studies are needed to explore the molecular mechanisms and clinical potential of compound 2 in breast cancer treatment as well as direct biochemical validation of EGFR and VEGFR2 inhibition by compound 2. This study’s limitation is relying on mRNA data without validating protein level changes. Future research should confirm these results using methods like Western blotting, focusing on apoptosis markers and EMT-related proteins.
