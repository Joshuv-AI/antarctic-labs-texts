Layered Pelvic Bowl Model for Profunda Artery Perforator Flap Perineal Reconstruction: A Consecutive Case Series and Technical Framework
Authors: Malka Korenblit, Celia Ernstrom, Arjan Deol, Zain Aryanpour, Jason Yu, Katie Egan
Published: 2026

Background: Perineal reconstruction following oncologic resection presents significant challenges due to complex defects, requiring both pelvic support and perineal resurfacing, particularly in irradiated fields. Traditional options such as vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous (VRAM) flaps provide substantial bulk but incur abdominal wall morbidity. The profunda artery perforator (PAP) flap offers reliable perforator-based vascularity, substantial tissue volume, and minimal donor site morbidity, making it an attractive alternative in patients with low BMI or prior abdominal surgery.1-3 Current reconstructive frameworks classify defects by anatomical subunits but lack systematic approaches to address pelvic support and perineal coverage as distinct yet integrated objectives.4,5 We present a novel “Pelvic Bowl” model-a three-layer anatomical framework for defect analysis and surgical planning applicable to any flap choice-and report outcomes demonstrating this approach.

Methods: All patients who underwent PAP flap perineal reconstruction by a single surgeon (Nov 2023-June 2025) were included. Retrospective chart review extracted demographics, defect characteristics, operative details, and outcomes. The Pelvic Bowl model stratifies reconstruction into three layers: deep pelvic floor/inlet support; middle layer dead space obliteration; and superficial perineal resurfacing. PAP flaps were harvested using perforators selected based on caliber, intramuscular course, and pedicle length to optimize rotation and perfusion. Chimeric gracilis muscle flaps were harvested from the ipsilateral thigh when needed for pelvic floor support. Flaps were tunneled subcutaneously to the perineum. Outcomes included flap survival, complications, donor morbidity, and return to operating room.

Results: Twelve consecutive patients (mean age 68.0 ± 12.1 years, 66.7% male, mean BMI 22.7±2.7 kg/m2) underwent immediate perineal reconstruction. Indications included abdominoperineal resection (APR) (50%), pelvic exenteration (25%), APR with vaginectomy (25%). 83.3% had prior pelvic radiation. Chimeric PAP-gracilis reconstruction was performed in 50% of cases. Perforator A was used in 5 cases and perforator B in 6 cases. Mean operative time 211 minutes (110–297); hospital stay 9 days (6–13.8). Flap survival was 100% with no cases of partial or total flap necrosis. Reconstruction-related complications included wound dehiscence managed conservatively (2/12, 16.7%), seroma requiring IR drainage (1/12, 8.3%), and donor site wound healing managed conservatively (1/12, 8.3%). Two patients (16.7%) experienced disease-related complications (locally advanced tumor with bowel invasion, pelvic abscess) requiring operative interventions; notably, flaps remained viable despite aggressive disease. One patient (8.3%) required return to the OR for exam under anesthesia of delayed healing of vaginal reconstruction. No perineal hernias developed. No donor site deficits were encountered.

Conclusions: In this series, PAP flaps alone or chimeric with gracilis muscle achieved 100% survival and met reconstructive goals despite 83% prior radiation, with minimal donor morbidity. This approach validates framework flexibility, with decisions guided by reconstruction of the pelvic layers. This systematic approach advances defect analysis and surgical planning in complex pelvic reconstruction, providing a reproducible paradigm applicable across reconstructive options.
