Temporal synchrony promotes motor recovery and ipsilesional cortical reorganization in BCI-based stroke rehabilitation: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yu Wu, Qiumeng Dong, Jie Chen, Cong Huang, Ming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe therapeutic mechanisms of brain-computer interface (BCI) rehabilitation remain partially understood. Specifically, it is unclear whether functional gains are driven primarily by active neural engagement or strictly by the temporal contingency between motor intention and sensory feedback. This study aimed to deconstruct the BCI paradigm to isolate the specific contributions of these core components to functional recovery and cortical reorganization in subacute stroke.MethodsSeventy-seven subacute stroke patients were randomized into three intervention arms. Group C (synchronous) received robotic assistance strictly contingent on real-time detection of task engagement during motor imagery; Group B (asynchronous) performed identical motor imagery tasks but received temporally uncoupled robotic assistance; and Group A (sham) received sham motor imagery detection and preset passive robotic assistance. The intervention lasted for 3 weeks. The primary clinical outcome was Fugl-Meyer Assessment-Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) scores. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) was included as an exploratory neuroimaging measure to provide preliminary information on cortical responses associated with the intervention.ResultsSixty-eight participants were included in the final analysis. After adjustment for baseline values, Group C showed significantly higher post-intervention FMA-UE scores than both Group A (adjusted mean difference = 7.47, 95% CI [1.94, 13.00], P = 0.004) and Group B (adjusted mean difference = 9.72, 95% CI [4.14, 15.30], P < 0.001). Group C also showed significantly higher post-intervention MBI scores than both Group A (adjusted mean difference = 21.15, 95% CI [12.49, 29.81], P < 0.001) and Group B (adjusted mean difference = 21.27, 95% CI [12.42, 30.12], P < 0.001). Exploratory neuroimaging analyses showed that only the change in ipsilesional sensorimotor cortex laterality index during motor imagery was significantly correlated with motor improvement (ρ = 0.404, P = 0.002, q = 0.012), whereas no resting-state or task-related fNIRS group comparisons remained significant after false discovery rate correction.ConclusionsSynchronous BCI training was associated with superior clinical improvement compared with asynchronous and sham conditions in subacute stroke. The benefit was observed only when robotic assistance was contingent on real-time task performance, despite all three groups receiving comparable device exposure and motor imagery instruction. The neuroimaging results remain exploratory and should be confirmed in larger studies with prospectively defined imaging endpoints.Trial registration This trial was registered under the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400091197) and was prospectively registered on October 21, 2024. Ethics Approval: Approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Rehabilitation Hospital (XK-LSW-20241017-009). Introduction

After stroke, up to 85% stroke survivors experience impairment of an upper extremity, and between 55 and 75% of survivors continue to experience upper extremity functional limitations associated with diminished health-related quality of life [1, 2]. During the post-stroke rehabilitation process, the recovery of upper limb function often lags behind that of the lower limb [3]. This disparity arises because hand motor control can be primarily attributed to the intricate corticospinal tract projections and the extensive representative area required for hand motor control within the primary motor cortex (M1) [4, 5].

The recovery of these complex motor skills is underpinned by post-stroke neuroplasticity, including the reorganization of perilesional neurons, the redistribution of cortical representations, the enhancement of synaptic plasticity, and interhemispheric functional reorganization [6, 7]. The degree of neural remodelling directly influences the potential for motor recovery during the rehabilitation period [8]. The neural plasticity processes are triggered by several endogenous (genetic expression profiles, hormonal fluctuations, neurotrophic signaling) and exogenous events (injury, environmental enrichment, pharmacological intervention) [9]. Leveraging this dual-trigger mechanism, motor imagery-based brain-computer interfaces (MI-BCI) emerge as a unique paradigm that bridges the gap between internal intention and external feedback. In this framework, MI-BCI systems acquire real-time neural signals from the motor cortex via modalities such as electroencephalography (EEG) or magnetoencephalography during MI tasks. Through signal preprocessing, feature extraction, and classification algorithms, these systems detect motor-related neural patterns and translate them into commands for the real-time control of external devices, such as robotic arms, wheelchairs, and prosthetics, thereby linking decoded motor-related neural activity with peripheral actuation [10, 11].

Over the past two decades, a growing number of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have provided initial evidence for the clinical potential of BCI-based training for upper-limb rehabilitation. However, the reported efficacy of BCI for upper limb rehabilitation remains inconsistent. While multiple studies have demonstrated significant improvements in upper limb function with adjunctive BCI training compared to conventional rehabilitation alone [12, 13], one randomized controlled trial reported that adding BCI based functional electrical stimulation offers no therapeutic advantage over conventional rehabilitation [14]. Furthermore, even when superior efficacy is observed, the precise source of this benefit remains unclear, specifically whether it is derived from the peripheral effector, central MI, or their synchronous coupling. Although studies comparing MI-BCI against pure MI have reported better recovery in the MI-BCI group [15], it remains uncertain whether this advantage is solely attributable to the additional benefit provided by the effector. Conversely, other studies comparing BCI-controlled effectors against the effectors alone found no significant differences in post-treatment upper extremity functional scores [16–18]. Consequently, there is a critical need for a study design capable of disentangling central and peripheral factors to systematically evaluate their individual and combined contributions to neuroplasticity in stroke patients.

To investigate the neural mechanisms underlying the clinical effects of BCI, objective neurophysiological monitoring is essential. Neurovascular coupling (NVC) aligns the delivery of blood and nutrients with the metabolic demands of locally activated neurons. This mechanism not only supports the brain’s energy supply but also plays a crucial role in neural circuit remodeling and functional recovery via its plasticity [19]. Consequently, oxyhemoglobin (HbO) signals can reflect dynamic shifts in cortical neural activity driven by NVC [20]. Based on this principle, functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) quantitatively measures cerebral HbO changes. It operates by analyzing light attenuation caused by the differential absorption of near-infrared light by oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR), correlating these optical properties with chromophore concentrations in the cortex [21]. Due to its portability, motion tolerance, and applicability in naturalistic environments [22], fNIRS is widely used to monitor cortical activity in stroke patients during resting states and tasks [23, 24].

Given that the subacute phase represents the peak window for neuroplasticity [25], this study targets patients within this critical timeframe to deconstruct the BCI neurorehabilitation paradigm and isolate the therapeutic effects of its two core components: active task engagement verified by real-time BCI detection during MI and the temporal contingency between motor intention and peripheral stimulation. To this end, a three-arm randomized controlled trial was designed to compare sham-BCI, asynchronous real-BCI, and synchronous real-BCI. This design allows us to isolate the specific contributions of neural engagement versus synchronous sensory feedback to upper limb functional recovery. A secondary objective is to determine, via fNIRS, whether these components differentially influence cortical reorganization, specifically by examining task-evoked cortical activation and resting-state functional connectivity. We hypothesize that coupling endogenous motor intention with immediate exogenous reinforcement yields greater functional improvement and more pronounced adaptive cortical reorganization than asynchronous BCI or sham control.

Methods

Participants

This study was a three-arm, parallel RCT. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Rehabilitation Hospital (XK-LSW-20241017-009) and prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400091197). All procedures complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the reporting follows the CONSORT guidelines (Supplementary Table S1). A total of 77 stroke patients were recruited from the Department of Rehabilitation Medicine at Xuzhou Rehabilitation Hospital, China, between November 2024 and April 2025. It should be noted that the control intervention differed from the original trial registry entry (ChiCTR2400091197). In the registry, Group A was initially described as receiving conventional rehabilitation alone. Before recruitment began, however, this arm was revised to a sham-BCI + passive soft robotic glove (SRG) condition to better control for nonspecific effects associated with equipment exposure and sensory stimulation. Although this protocol modification had been approved by the local Ethics Committee before implementation, the corresponding change was not reflected in the public trial registry. Accordingly, the present manuscript reports the final implemented protocol.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) right-handed patients aged between 18 and 80 years; (2) first-ever stroke in the subacute phase, defined as a disease course of 1 week to 6 months post-onset, with stable vital signs [26]; (3) unilateral subcortical lesions confirmed by MRI or computed tomography; (4) presence of significant upper limb motor dysfunction, defined as Brunnstrom stages 1–5; (5) no history of neurosurgical intervention; (6) no prior history of other psychiatric or neurological diseases; (7) Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score > 20 [27]; (8) MI ability assessed using the Kinesthetic and Visual Imagery Questionnaire, with a score of > 55 indicating sufficient proficiency [28]; (9) both the participant and their legal guardian(s) provided written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) coexisting neurological disorders (e.g., peripheral nerve injuries, Parkinson’s disease, or multiple sclerosis); (2) skull defects, history of craniectomy, or other factors interfering with EEG/fNIRS signal acquisition; (3) active scalp infection or skin lesions; (4) medical instability or recurrent stroke; (5) severe upper limb spasticity (Modified Ashworth Scale ≥ 3) or joint contractures limiting glove application; (6) concurrent participation in other central nervous system-targeting interventions; (7) receipt of antispasmodic treatment within the past 6 months; or (8) inability to tolerate the experimental procedures.

The withdrawal criteria were as follows: (1) significant deterioration of their condition or occurrence of severe adverse reactions; (2) inability to complete the required follow-up assessments or rehabilitation training; (3) incomplete data that compromised the assessment of efficacy or safety; (4) voluntary withdrawal of informed consent by the participant. Adverse events, procedural intolerance, and other reasons for withdrawal were monitored throughout the study and recorded by group.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) based on a one-way ANOVA model. At the study design stage, the trial was planned as a three-arm parallel-group study. Because prior data were insufficient to support ANCOVA-based sample size estimation, the initial sample size calculation was performed using a one-way ANOVA framework. The effect size (f = 0.493) was derived from a previously published study on Fugl-Meyer Assessment-Upper Extremity (FMA-UE) recovery [29], which was considered the most relevant available reference at the time of protocol development. With a two-sided α of 0.05 and 80% power, the minimum total sample size required was 45 participants. Considering a potential dropout rate of 20%, we planned to recruit beyond this minimum, and 77 participants were ultimately enrolled.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomly assigned to one of the three groups using a computer-generated randomization sequence (1:1:1 ratio) created in SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, sealed opaque envelopes prepared by an independent researcher. No clinical or demographic variables were used for stratification, blocking, or minimization.

Given the distinct nature of the intervention, blinding of participants and therapists was not feasible. Therefore, outcome assessments were performed by an independent evaluator who was blinded to group allocation.

Intervention

All participants received a 3-week comprehensive intervention program, conducted 5 days per week. The daily intervention consisted of two components: a foundational conventional rehabilitation (CR) session attended by all participants, followed immediately by a 20-min group-specific BCI intervention.

Conventional rehabilitation protocol

The CR program was administered 5 days per week, comprising physical therapy (PT), occupational therapy (OT), and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM). The 50-min PT session included 30 min of motor therapy (using Bobath, Brunnstrom, and proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation techniques) to improve upper limb function, followed by 20 min of modalities such as functional electrical stimulation and biofeedback. The 30-min OT session was tailored to Brunnstrom stages: stages I-III focused on inhibiting spasticity and inducing movement via bimanual tasks and sensory facilitation; stage IV targeted dexterity and activities of daily living (ADLs) through task-specific training; and stage V emphasized more selective voluntary movement, fine motor coordination, and functional upper-limb practice in ADL-related tasks. Finally, the 30-min TCM session utilized moxibustion and acupuncture (at LI11 Quchi and TE5 Waiguan) to improve muscle tone.

BCI intervention protocol

BCI system setup and task design

EEG signals were acquired using a ZhenTec-NT1 system (ZhenTec Intelligence Technology Co., Ltd., Xi’an, China) with a 32-channel semi-dry cap (Fp1, Fp2, Fz, F3, F4, F7, F8, FCz, FC3, FC4, FT7, FT8, Cz, C3, C4, T3, T4, CPz, CP3, CP4, TP7, TP8, Pz, P3, P4, P7, P8, Oz, O1, O2, HEOL, and HEOR) positioned according to the international 10–20 system. Signals were sampled at 250 Hz. Electrode impedances were maintained below 50 kΩ. A pneumatic SRG was fitted to the affected hand of each participant (Fig. 1D). During online operation, the continuous EEG signal underwent real-time preprocessing including a 50-Hz notch filter, a 0.5–50 Hz band-pass filter, baseline correction relative to the 30-s pre-training resting-state recording, and automatic artifact rejection of segments with absolute amplitude exceeding 100 μV. Real-time spectral features were computed every 0.1 s from a 2-s sliding-window fast Fourier transform. The control feature was defined as the band-power ratio between the beta (14–30 Hz) and alpha (7–13 Hz) bands (Eβ/Eα). This ratio was linearly normalized to a continuous engagement score on a 0–100 scale using subject-specific calibration constants derived from the 30-s pre-training resting-state recording, and the SRG was triggered only when this engagement score exceeded the preset difficulty threshold.

The 20-min intervention session consisted of two 10-min game-based tasks, Catching Butterflies (grasping-related MI) and Frying Eggs (opening-related MI). Before the formal session, participants completed five practice trials to familiarize themselves with the procedure; data from these practice trials were not included in the analysis. During formal training, each trial began with a 5-s MI preparation/cue period, during which auditory and visual instructions prompted the participant to initiate the required MI task. This was followed by an online control monitoring window of up to 8 s, during which the real-time score was continuously monitored. Difficulty was stratified into three levels according to the threshold required to trigger a successful trial: level 1 (score ≥ 60), level 2 (score ≥ 70), and level 3 (score ≥ 80). All participants began at level 1, and the difficulty level was increased when the success rate reached ≥ 90%. If the score exceeded the preset threshold within the detection window, the system immediately entered a 5-s action/feedback phase that included a success animation. If the score did not exceed the threshold within the time window, the trial ended without triggering the action phase. Each trial then entered a 7-s rest/feedback period before the next trial began. Because the detection window could terminate early when the threshold was exceeded, trial duration varied across trials, ranging from approximately 17 to 25 s for successful trials and 20-s for unsuccessful trials. Consequently, the total number of trials completed within a 20-min session was not fixed (Fig. 1G).

Group-specific control logic

All participants performed these tasks, but the crucial distinction lay in the control logic: ① Group C: when the real-time control score exceeded the preset threshold during the instructed MI task, the system immediately triggered both the successful game action (visual feedback) and synchronous activation of the SRG; ② Group B: the visual success feedback was linked to the online control signal, whereas SRG activation was delivered on a fixed schedule (one full grip-and-open cycle every 20 s); ③ Group A: participants were instructed to perform MI during each trial, but the robotic hand movement was delivered according to a preset passive schedule (one full grip-and-open cycle every 20-s) and was not contingent on the participant’s ongoing task performance. The system played pre-recorded training footage collected during a pilot study, and the SRG was activated at the same fixed schedule (one cycle every 20-s) as Group B. In all groups, auditory and visual feedback for the perceived outcome (Great job!/ Don’t give up!) was provided. All sessions were conducted in a quiet, softly lit room, and participants could pause for rest if fatigued.

Clinical assessments

All clinical assessments were administered at baseline (T0, prior to intervention) and post-intervention (T1, at 3 weeks) by a trained evaluator who was blinded to the group assignments (Fig. 1H).

Primary outcome measure

The primary outcome for this study was the FMA-UE [30], the gold standard for assessing post-stroke motor impairment. The FMA-UE is a 33-item assessment scored on a 3-point ordinal scale (0 = cannot, 1 = partial, 2 = full), yielding a maximum score of 66.

Secondary outcome measures

Secondary outcomes were selected to evaluate changes in functional ability and independence in daily life. These included the Action Research Arm Test (ARAT) [31], a 19-item test scored on a 4-point scale (0–3) that measures upper limb function across its four subscales (grasp, grip, pinch, and gross movement), with a maximum score of 57. Additionally, the Modified Barthel Index (MBI) [32] was used to assess independence in ADLs on a 100-point scale, where higher scores indicate greater independence.

Exploratory fNIRS analysis

FNIRS was included as a prospectively incorporated neuroimaging component to explore potential neuroplastic correlates of motor recovery. Although the acquisition and task paradigms were defined at the protocol stage, the analytic framework was not fully predefined. Therefore, all fNIRS-derived measures were treated as exploratory, hypothesis-generating analyses rather than confirmatory endpoints.

fNIRS data acquisition

Acquisition hardware and probe montage

Cortical hemodynamic responses were recorded using a continuous-wave fNIRS system (NirSmart-Ⅱ-3000A, HuiChuang Medical Equipment Co., Ld., China) operating at 730 and 850 nm (sampling rate: 11 Hz). The montage consisted of 39 channels (16 sources, 16 detectors; 30 mm separation) covering the prefrontal and sensorimotor cortices. 3D coordinates were digitized (Patriot, Polhemus, USA) and probabilistically registered to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI)-152 standard brain template (Supplementary Table S2). Based on this spatial registration, eleven regions of interest (ROIs) were defined: bilateral M1, primary somatosensory cortex (S1), premotor cortex (PMC), ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), and the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). For subsequent analyses, the M1, S1, and PMC within each hemisphere were collectively defined as the sensorimotor cortex (SMC) [33]. Details are shown in Fig. 1F. For statistical analysis, these ROIs were categorized as “ipsilesional” (lesion side) or “contralesional” (non-lesion side) according to the specific stroke laterality of each participant.

Following a 10-min stabilization period, fNIRS data were acquired during resting state and task states (MI and motor execution (ME)). The resting state run involved an 8-min continuous recording where participants remained relaxed and motionless. The MI and ME run utilized a block design consisting of three 15-s task blocks alternating with 15-s rest periods, guided by automated auditory cues. For MI, participants were instructed to kinesthetically imagine repetitive grasping and opening of the affected hand at a self-paced frequency, without overt muscle output. For ME, they performed actual self-paced hand grasping and opening movements. For both MI and ME, each task block lasted 15 s. Details are illustrated in Fig. 1I.

fNIRS data processing

Data processing was performed using MNE-NIRS. For all datasets, raw intensity signals were first converted to optical density and then to HbO/HbR using the modified Beer-Lambert law (PPF = 6.0). Channels with a scalp coupling index (SCI) < 0.5 were excluded as bad channels. Subsequent processing and analysis were conducted separately for resting-state and task-state data because of their distinct signal characteristics.

For the resting-state analysis, the initial 20 s after task onset were excluded to reduce transition-related effects, and the subsequent continuous segment was used for connectivity analysis. To reduce motion-related and systemic physiological artifacts while minimizing data loss, temporal derivative distribution repair was first applied to the optical density data [34], followed by wavelet filtering (interquartile range (IQR) = 1.5) [35]. In the absence of short-separation channels, a principal component analysis-based global signal regression approach was then applied, in which the first principal component of all HbO channels was regressed out from each channel time series. A band-pass filter of 0.01–0.10 Hz was subsequently applied to isolate spontaneous low-frequency oscillations characteristic of resting-state hemodynamic connectivity.

For the task-state (MI/ME) analysis, preprocessing included wavelet-based correction for motion artifacts and a band-pass filter of 0.01–0.30 Hz to preserve task-evoked hemodynamic responses. A general linear model (GLM) incorporating an autoregressive noise model was used to estimate beta values for each condition [36]. The GLM design matrix was constructed using a canonical hemodynamic response model [37, 38]. Beta values were averaged across valid channels within each ROI to generate ROI-level activation estimates. Functional connectivity metrics

For the fNIRS analysis, two classes of exploratory outcomes were derived: resting-state functional connectivity measures and task-related hemodynamic and connectivity measures.

For the resting-state analysis, the preprocessed HbO time series of all valid channels within each ROI were spatially averaged to obtain a single ROI-level time series. Functional connectivity between two ROIs was then quantified as the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) between their mean HbO time series, and correlation coefficients were Fisher z-transformed (z = arctanh(r)) before statistical analysis. Thus, resting-state connectivity estimates were based on ROI-level mean signals rather than averages of pairwise channel-to-channel correlations. To derive system-level indices, composite signals were further constructed by averaging ROI-level time series within predefined cortical systems. Specifically, the SMC signal within each hemisphere was defined as the mean of the M1, S1, and PMC ROI signals, whereas the lateral PFC signal was defined as the mean of the DLPFC and VLPFC ROI signals. The midline mPFC was treated separately and was not included in the hemispheric PFC composite. Based on these system-level signals, sensorimotor-prefrontal coupling was defined as the Fisher-z-transformed connectivity between the SMC and lateral PFC within the same hemisphere, and interhemispheric connectivity was defined as the Fisher-z-transformed connectivity between homologous cortical systems across hemispheres (SMC-to-SMC and lateral PFC-to-lateral PFC). Intrahemispheric connectivity was defined as the mean Fisher-z-transformed connectivity across all ROI pairs within the same cortical system in one hemisphere, that is, among M1, S1, and PMC for the SMC and between DLPFC and VLPFC for the lateral PFC. Contra- and ipsilesional indices were defined according to lesion side. Higher values indicated stronger temporal coupling of spontaneous HbO fluctuations between the corresponding regions.

For the task-state analysis, three classes of indices were derived for both MI and ME conditions: (1) activation, represented by ROI-level HbO beta values; (2) LI, reflecting the relative balance of activation between the contralesional and ipsilesional hemispheres; and (3) task-related inter-regional connectivity measures (ΔFC), reflecting task-related changes in pairwise coupling. These measures were summarized within predefined cortical systems, including the SMC and lateral PFC, and were interpreted as indices of task-evoked cortical recruitment, hemispheric balance, and pairwise inter-regional connectivity.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified. For the exploratory fNIRS analyses, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons, and the corresponding adjusted q-values are reported. Effect sizes were reported for the major analyses, including partial eta squared (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\eta}_{P}^{2}$$\end{document}) for parametric group comparisons, epsilon squared (ε2) for non-parametric group comparisons, and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) for correlation analyses.

Descriptive and baseline comparisons

The distribution of continuous variables was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and IQR. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies. Baseline comparability among the three groups was assessed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square test for categorical variables.

Between-group efficacy analysis

The primary evaluation of treatment efficacy focused on between-group differences in post-intervention outcomes while accounting for baseline values. ANCOVA was performed with the T1 score as the dependent variable, group as the fixed factor, and T0 score as the covariate. Prior to analysis, model assumptions were assessed, including residual normality, homogeneity of variance, and homogeneity of regression slopes. For outcomes that did not satisfy the assumptions for ANCOVA, non-parametric between-group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. When overall group differences were significant, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed. Test statistics (F or H values) and corresponding effect sizes (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\eta}_{p}^{2}$$\end{document} or ε2) were reported. Change scores were also calculated and presented descriptively in the tables. Given the exploratory nature of the fNIRS assessments, the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure was applied to control for the multiple testing burden. The corrections were performed separately for two conceptual families of tests: (1) resting-state functional connectivity changes and (2) task-related activation, laterality, and functional connectivity changes. Both uncorrected P-values and FDR-adjusted q-values were reported.

Within-group analysis

Within-group changes from T0 to T1 were assessed using paired t-tests for normally distributed data or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed data. Cohen’s d (for parametric tests) or matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation (for non-parametric tests) were calculated as effect sizes to aid interpretation. This analysis was considered exploratory and complementary to the primary between-group comparison.

Handling of missing data

The primary analysis was conducted on participants with available post-intervention outcome data. To assess the robustness of the findings to missing outcome data, a sensitivity analysis was performed using multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption. Each imputed dataset was analyzed using the same baseline-adjusted linear model as in the primary analysis, with post-intervention scores as the dependent variables, group as the fixed factor, and corresponding baseline scores as covariates. The resulting estimates were pooled according to Rubin’s rules.

Correlation analysis

To investigate the relationship between neural adaptation and functional improvement, correlation analyses were performed between the change in FMA-UE scores (ΔFMA-UE) and changes in exploratory fNIRS-derived connectivity measures. These measures included: (1) changes in resting-state functional connectivity; and (2) changes in task-related hemodynamic activation and laterality index during MI and ME tasks. Given the modest sample size and the possibility that some imaging-derived variables might not satisfy normality assumptions, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was used as a conservative non-parametric approach for all correlation analyses. To control for type I error arising from multiple comparisons, FDR correction was applied, and the corresponding adjusted q-values were reported.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 77 participants were enrolled and randomized to Group A (n = 26), Group B (n = 25), and Group C (n = 26). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable across the three groups, with no significant between-group differences in age, sex, affected side, stroke type, disease duration, lesion location, or baseline clinical scores (Table 2; all P > 0.05). During the intervention, 9 participants withdrew, resulting in an imbalanced dropout pattern across groups (Group A: n = 7; Group B: n = 1; Group C: n = 1). In Group A, five withdrawals were attributed to a lack of perceived clinical benefit and reduced motivation, one to scheduling conflicts, and one to transfer to another medical facility. The single withdrawal in Group B was also attributed to a lack of perceived clinical benefit. In Group C, one participant withdrew after an unrelated ward fall caused a scalp abrasion that prevented further use of the experimental setup. Thus, 68 participants were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2). Clinical outcome measures

Baseline comparisons showed no significant differences among the three groups, indicating good comparability at study entry (Table 2). After adjustment for baseline values, significant between-group differences were observed in post-intervention FMA-UE, ARAT, and MBI outcomes. For FMA-UE, ANCOVA revealed a significant group effect (F = 10.067, P < 0.001, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\eta}_{p}^{2}$$\end{document} = 0.239). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons showed that Group C had significantly higher post-intervention FMA-UE scores than Group A (adjusted mean difference = 7.47, 95% CI [1.94, 13.00], P = 0.004) and Group B (adjusted mean difference = 9.72, 95% CI [4.14, 15.30], P < 0.001). For ARAT, a significant between-group difference was also found after baseline adjustment (F = 4.267, P = 0.018, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\eta}_{p}^{2}$$\end{document} = 0.118). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons indicated that Group C had significantly higher post-intervention ARAT scores than Group B (adjusted mean difference = 6.64, 95% CI [0.38, 12.90], P = 0.034), whereas the difference between Group C and Group A did not reach statistical significance (adjusted mean difference = 6.18, 95% CI [-0.02, 12.38], P = 0.051). A substantial between-group effect was also observed for MBI (F = 23.625, P < 0.001, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\eta}_{p}^{2} $$\end{document}= 0.425). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons showed that Group C had significantly higher post-intervention MBI scores than Group A (adjusted mean difference = 21.15, 95% CI [12.49, 29.81], P < 0.001) and Group B (adjusted mean difference = 21.27, 95% CI [12.42, 30.12], P < 0.001). Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to examine whether the therapeutic effect of BCI-assisted rehabilitation is more closely associated with active neural engagement, peripheral feedback, or their temporal coupling. To address this question, we used a randomized three-arm design: Group C received robotic assistance contingent on the real-time control signal during instructed MI; Group B performed the same MI tasks but received robotic assistance delivered on a fixed schedule that was not contingent on the real-time control signal; and Group A served as a sham-BCI control receiving passive movement without temporal contingency to the participant’s ongoing task performance. The main clinical finding was that Group C achieved greater improvement in upper-limb motor impairment and activities of daily living than either Group B or Group A.

Group C showed greater clinical improvement than the comparison groups, particularly in upper-limb motor impairment and functional independence. These findings suggest that temporally contingent BCI feedback may provide a therapeutic advantage over temporally uncoupled or non-contingent assistance. Although several fNIRS measures showed nominal differences that were directionally consistent with this behavioral pattern, none remained statistically significant after FDR correction. The neuroimaging results should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as confirmatory evidence of treatment-related cortical reorganization.

The superior improvement observed in Group C relative to Group A may be understood in two ways. First, passive movement alone may be insufficient to produce the same degree of functional benefit, particularly when it is not contingently linked to the participant’s own task engagement during training [9, 40]. Although participants in Group A received repeated robot-assisted grasping, the movement assistance in this group was delivered according to a preset schedule rather than being contingently linked to the participant’s ongoing task performance. Second, this mismatch between internally generated motor intention and externally delivered assistance may have reduced the behavioral relevance of the training experience. A more cautious interpretation is therefore that contingent feedback, rather than passive movement alone, was more effective in supporting motor recovery. Although prior work has linked motor learning to reinforcement-related and plasticity-related neurobiological processes [39, 40], the present study did not directly measure these mechanisms and therefore cannot confirm their specific contribution here. The comparison between Group C and Group B further suggests that temporal congruence may be an important component of therapeutic efficacy. Both groups involved active MI tasks and both received robotic assistance, but only Group C received assistance that was delivered in temporal contingency with the online control signal during training. Therefore, the advantage of Group C cannot be attributed simply to the combined presence of active task engagement and peripheral movement [41]. Instead, it may reflect the added value of temporally aligned intention-feedback coupling in closed-loop rehabilitation, which is broadly consistent with previous work emphasizing the importance of temporally matched sensorimotor feedback for motor learning and plasticity [42, 43]. Although this interpretation is compatible with theoretical accounts of activity-dependent plasticity and temporally specific sensorimotor reinforcement [44, 45], the present data do not directly test these underlying synaptic mechanisms and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Importantly, the superior motor gains in Group C were accompanied by greater improvement in MBI scores, suggesting that the benefits of contingent feedback were not limited to task-specific motor performance but also extended to daily functional ability.

While the clinical outcomes favored the synchronous BCI intervention, the neuroimaging findings should be interpreted cautiously. None of the fNIRS measures remained statistically significant after FDR correction for multiple comparisons. Therefore, these results cannot be considered robust confirmatory evidence of treatment-related cortical reorganization. At most, the nominal findings before correction may provide preliminary, hypothesis-generating clues about possible neural correlates of recovery. In the resting-state analysis, Group A showed a nominal increase in interhemispheric sensorimotor connectivity, yet this increase was not accompanied by correspondingly better clinical outcomes. This dissociation suggests that stronger bilateral coupling does not necessarily correspond to better functional improvement and should be interpreted cautiously [46, 47]. In the task-related analysis, the change in the sensorimotor laterality index during MI was positively correlated with improvement in FMA-UE, and this association remained significant after FDR correction. This finding suggests that better recovery may have been accompanied by greater involvement of the ipsilesional sensorimotor regions and reduced reliance on the contralesional hemisphere [48, 49]. However, this correlation should still be interpreted cautiously, given the exploratory nature of the neuroimaging analyses and the limited sample size available for the fNIRS dataset. Future studies with larger samples, prospectively defined neuroimaging endpoints, and adequate statistical power will be needed to determine whether temporally aligned feedback is reliably associated with cortical reorganization during stroke recovery.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was primarily powered for the main clinical outcome rather than the exploratory neuroimaging analyses, and attrition further reduced the effective sample size available for fNIRS analysis. Accordingly, the neuroimaging findings should be interpreted with caution. Although several nominal differences were observed, none remained statistically significant after FDR correction, and these results should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Second, the fNIRS probe montage was limited to the lateral sensorimotor and prefrontal cortices and did not cover the supplementary motor area; therefore, the neuroimaging findings provide only a partial view of cortical reorganization. Third, no long-term follow-up assessments were conducted. As a result, the present study was limited to evaluating the immediate therapeutic effects after the 3-week intervention, and the durability of the observed clinical benefits beyond the post-intervention period remains uncertain. Finally, the recruitment window covered a relatively broad subacute stage after stroke, which may have introduced heterogeneity in spontaneous recovery despite randomization.

Conclusion

In summary, this study suggests that the therapeutic effect of BCI-assisted rehabilitation may depend in part on temporally aligned intention-feedback coupling rather than on robotic assistance alone. The synchronous intervention was associated with greater improvement in upper-limb motor function and daily functional ability than the asynchronous and sham conditions. The neuroimaging findings were exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously, but the overall pattern warrants further investigation into the role of temporal alignment in closed-loop stroke rehabilitation. Future studies with larger samples, prospectively defined imaging endpoints, and longer follow-up are needed to confirm and extend these findings.
