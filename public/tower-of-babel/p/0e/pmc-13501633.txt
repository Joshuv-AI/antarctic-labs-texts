Strategies of photosynthetic physiology in response to karst habitats and rainfall allocation have maintained the morphology and function of Fraxinus malacophylla seedlings
Authors: Shaojie Zheng, Lehao Li, Qize Zhao, Tao Zhang, Rongxin Gou, Lin Wang, Yuanyou Wang, Shunrou Shi, Yaoyi Chen, Chengmeng Zhang, Qiong Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnder global climate change, prolonged rainfall intervals and intensified single rainfall events in karst rocky desertification areas, together with increased karst fissure thickness, significantly affect plant seedling growth and physiology. This study used a two-factor block experiment to explore the effects of different rainfall intervals and karst habitats on the photosynthetic physiology of two-year-old Fraxinus malacophylla seedlings.ResultsThe results showed that under the same rainfall interval, the carbon (C), nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) content, photosynthetic pigment content, fluorescence parameters, and photosynthetic gas parameters of F. malacophylla seedlings showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing with the increase of karst fissures. Under the I6dS1/2 treatment, the maximum net photosynthetic rate, light saturation point, and light compensation point reached 8.143, 1408.190, and 71.474 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, respectively, with a maximum CO₂ saturation point of 1571.580 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹. Biomass allocation followed the order of stem > leaf > root in the S0 habitat, whereas it showed the order of root > stem > leaf in the S1/2 and S3/4 habitats. At I3d and I6d rainfall intervals, SOD, POD, and SPC activities rose with increasing karst thickness, while an opposite pattern was found at I9d. Specifically, the prolonged rainfall interval (I9d) inhibits the growth of F. malacophylla seedlings, mainly because the long-term water deficit exceeds the osmotic regulation limit of the seedlings and causes an imbalance in the antioxidant system, rather than a single collapse of the antioxidant system.ConclusionsIn summary, the F. malacophylla seedlings grew best under I6dS1/2 treatment, and prolonged rainfall intervals inhibited the growth of F. malacophylla seedlings. This study suggests that shallow-soil karst rocky desertification areas can serve as suitable habitats for F. malacophylla seedlings, thereby providing a theoretical basis and technical support for vegetation restoration, the cultivation of F. malacophylla seedlings, and the maintenance of ecosystem stability in karst rocky desertification regions.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Karst rocky desertification (KRD) areas are the core focus of ecological governance, and this ecological problem poses a serious threat to social sustainable development [1–3]. There are research reports showing that most of the land in the Mediterranean region is facing threats from karst development: karst landform areas in Lebanon account for 70% of the total land area [4], while soil erosion in western Ireland continues to worsen [5]. Other research data shows that the average soil erosion modulus in Cuba is between 12.3 and 13.7 tons per hectare per year, which greatly hinders the local socio-economic development [6]. This problem also exists in China, particularly in the southwestern karst areas. The ecological environment deterioration caused by land degradation and rocky desertification is particularly significant in this region, directly compressing the living space of local people and restricting the regional development process [7–9]. Like the three major karst areas in the world, the karst area in Southwest China is the largest (approximately 550,000 square kilometers) and also the most typical carbonate rock distribution area in the world [10]. Most typical site-type characteristics in the region have been disrupted, such as surface, exposed bedrock, poor soil, and severe decline in land productivity [7, 11]. The fourth monitoring of rocky desertification organized by the National Forestry and Grassland Administration shows that the area of rocky desertification in southwestern China has decreased from 100,700 km2 in 2016 to 72,200 km2 in 2021 [12]. However, the rocky desertification area in Yunnan, China, remains at 22,100 km2, a decrease of only 978.21 km2 compared to 2015 [12]. Amid the context of global climate change, the karst regions in southwestern China tend to have unaltered total precipitation and a marked reduction in annual rainy days [13]. In the case of infrequent intense precipitation, topsoil is readily disaggregated, hydrological erosion is exacerbated, and vertical heterogeneity of edaphic microhabitats is amplified, thereby impeding plant development. Previous studies suggest that karst crevices retain 27% of total rainfall, which is higher than the 21% water supply from surface soil vegetation [14–15]. Accordingly, the infilling or formation of topsoil within superficial karst crevices can partially lower the rate of hydrological erosion; meanwhile, crevice occurrence elevates surface ruggedness and soil consolidation capacity, thus facilitating vegetation development [16]. Yet, root systems serve as pivotal tissues for sustaining vegetation and woodland growth and development, and link subterranean and aerial ecological processes through the circulation of substances and energy [17]. Furthermore, root networks can safeguard topsoil against erosive forces, and key root morphological traits (root length, root diameter, root surface area, root volume) effectively reduce the soil detachment rate, root penetrability, and erodibility of the topsoil [18].

In addition, ecological stoichiometry refers to the balance of various elements in an ecosystem, especially the stable state between carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P), which is crucial. Since the introduction of ecological stoichiometry, it has been used for research on nutrient dynamics [19], biogeochemical cycles [20], and climate change response mechanisms [21]. C, N, and P are essential nutrients for plant growth [22]. C, N, and P are essential nutrients for plant growth, and their stoichiometric ratios serve as core indicators for assessing plant nutrient limitation status and adaptive strategies [22–23]. The ratio of C: N and C: P can reflect the growth rate and nutrient utilization rate of plants; Meanwhile, the N: P value of plant leaves can be used as a threshold for plant nutrient limitation [24–25]. The ecological stoichiometry theory suggests that organisms can maintain a relatively stable composition of chemical elements in their bodies during the process of adapting to environmental changes, and the element content or ratio remains relatively stable within a certain range of environmental changes [26]. However, the contents and proportions of different elements vary among organs [27] and respond accordingly to changes in the external environment [28].

The photosynthetic physiological traits of plants can mirror their environmental adaptability, and investigating the efficiency of plants in light energy conversion constitutes the core of probing into their yield potential [29]. Nevertheless, vegetation in karst rocky desertification regions is subjected to the combined effects of diverse natural and anthropogenic factors, which constrain their photosynthetic performance. Consequently, the photosynthetic yield of most plant species fails to attain an optimal level, impeding the holistic remediation of rocky desertification. Fissures in karst terrain induce severe water and soil loss, leading to a state of water stress in plants. Thus, it remains unclear whether Fraxinus malacophylla enhances its root absorptive capacity for water by promoting root development to expand root surface area, and whether it diminishes stomatal conductance to reduce water transpiration and sustain hydraulic balance. Furthermore, the light response curve and its derived physiological indices are key to elucidating the eco-physiological processes of vegetation, as they can directly or indirectly reflect the photosynthetic adaptation of plants to karst environments [30]. Additionally, soil depth in karst regions modulates the photosynthetic physiology of plants by means of soil water content, nutrient supplies, and the availability of subterranean space [31], which thereby exerts an influence on plant growth and development. To elucidate the adaptive mechanisms of F. malacophylla in karst rocky desertification regions, this study investigated key indicators (root morphology, C: N: P stoichiometry, photosynthetic physiology) to integrate multi-dimensional indices and reveal its comprehensive physiological adaptation strategies under karst stress.

Fraxinus malacophylla is a semi-deciduous tree belonging to the genus Fraxinus of Oleaceae, which is mainly distributed in Alpine mixed forests in karst areas of Guangxi and Yunnan. F. malacophylla has good adaptability to rocky desertification areas, a high survival rate of afforestation, and is an excellent broad-leaved tree companion species in rocky desertification management with high medicinal and ecological value [32]. Mainly used for treating constipation, epilepsy, malaria, and manufacturing furniture [33–34]. As one of the main native species, F. malacophylla is also widely used in the ecological restoration of rocky desertification areas. At present, many scholars have conducted systematic research on the germination characteristics of F. malacophylla seeds [35], fertilization management [36], drought resistance [37], and rainfall patterns [38].

Therefore, in the context of KRD regions characterized by extensively developed karst fissures and seasonal aridity, the adaptive mechanisms of seedlings of the native tree species F. malacophylla in these harsh environments remain poorly understood, particularly about how they modulate their physiological processes to acclimate to local growth conditions. To fill this critical research gap, we developed a clear conceptual framework and put forward the following core hypotheses. (1) Karst habitat types (stone-free soil, soil with moderate rock content, and soil dominated by rocks) and rainfall intervals (3 days, 6 days, 9 days) exerted significant effects on morphological growth, photosynthetic physiology, and nutrient accumulation of F. malacophylla seedlings, and such response patterns differed from those of coniferous species [39]. This karst habitat (S) × rainfall interval (I) interactive effect is the key driver modulating the adaptive response of F. malacophylla seedlings. (2) F. malacophylla seedlings adapt to different karst habitats and rainfall distribution conditions through the regulation of photosynthetic physiological parameters (e.g., Fv/Fm, Pn, ETR), carbohydrate (soluble sugar, starch) accumulation, and antioxidant enzyme activity. This photosynthetic physiological response strategy serves as the core mechanism for maintaining their morphological and functional stability. (3) The combination of half stone and half soil (S1/2) habitat and a suitable rainfall interval (3 or 6 days) is most favorable for the growth and photosynthetic physiological stability of F. malacophylla seedlings; this optimal combination differs from that of coniferous tree species [39], attributed to inherent differences in the biological characteristics of the two tree types. (4) F. malacophylla can utilize the moisture deep in karst fissures by adjusting its root distribution pattern and presents a biomass allocation characteristic of roots > stems > leaves. This is a key adaptive trait of the species to water-limited karst environments. Does this not represent a key adaptive trait of the species to water-limited karst environments? This study not only fills the research gap on the photosynthetic physiological adaptation of F. malacophylla seedlings to karst habitats and precipitation variation, and clarifies the species stress adaptation mechanisms, but also provides scientific support for the cultivation of native tree species and vegetation restoration in karst rocky desertification areas, while offering a new research perspective for revealing interspecific differences in adaptation to karst habitats among tree species.

Materials and methods

Research site and planting materials

The research was conducted in a greenhouse at Southwest Forestry University in China (Kunming, Yunnan, 25°03′ N、102°46′ E). The research area is located in the monsoon climate zone of the subtropical plateau, with an average altitude of 1,954 m, few frost periods, and a warm climate. The annual average temperature is 16.5 ℃, the annual precipitation is 1,035 mm, the relative humidity of the air is 23%~67%, and the atmospheric CO2 concentration is 400–412 ppm. The environmental parameters inside the greenhouse were strictly controlled: the air relative humidity was maintained at 50%~78% (using an automatic temperature and humidity controller, Model: HT-8600, accuracy ± 5%), and the air temperature was stabilized at 24.5 ± 1.5 ℃ (temperature control range ± 0.5 ℃). The vapor pressure is 0.831 ~ 2.119 kPa, and the average annual sunshine hours are 2,250 h. The climate conditions inside the shed are relatively close to the natural growth conditions of the F. malacophylla. The plant material is a 2-year-old F. malacophylla seedling from Jianshui County, Yunnan Province, China (a typical karst area, 23°37′N, 102°50′E). It was sown in March 2022 and transported from Jianshui to the Greenhouse of Southwest Forestry University in October of the same year. Jianshui County is a typical karst region in China. However, the red soil and karst limestone were the test soil and rocks, both of which were taken from Jianshui County, China.

Experimental design

Referring to previous research [39], this study employed a two-factor block experimental design, with karst habitat and rainfall interval as the two experimental variables. Three karst habitat gradients were established, including stone-free full soil (S0), half soil and half rock (S1/2), and rock-dominated with limited soil (S3/4). All treatments were performed in standardized containers (upper diameter: 30 cm, height: 30 cm) with three 2 cm-diameter drainage holes at the bottom (Fig. 1). Specifically, S0 contained full soil without a karst fissure layer; S1/2 was arranged in layers with soil in the upper half and a karst fissure layer in the lower half; S3/4 consisted of soil in the upper one-quarter and a karst fissure layer in the lower three-quarters. Three rainfall-interval levels were set as 3-day (I3d), 6-day (I6d), and 9-day (I9d) intervals according to local meteorological characteristics [40]. The irrigation volume was calculated using a natural rainfall conversion factor (1 mm of rainfall corresponds to 1 L·m−² of irrigation) [41]. The total water supply was maintained at 123.58 mm per experimental cycle across all groups (Table 2). Water was applied to each pot at one time and allowed to drain naturally. The experiment included 9 treatments (3 karst habitats × 3 rainfall intervals), with three replicates per treatment and 10 seedlings per replicate, resulting in a total of 270 seedlings. Seedlings were transplanted into containers in May 2023 with one seedling per pot. Seedling acclimation lasted from May to June 2023, and the formal experiment was conducted from July to December 2023. Regular weeding and pest and disease management were implemented throughout the experimental period.

Sample determination and methods

Plant sampling and biomass

On December 28th, 6 seedlings were randomly selected from each treatment for destructive sampling. After harvesting the plants, rinse the surface of the sample with tap water, rinse it with deionized water, and then use absorbent paper to remove excess water stains on the surface. After that, divide the plants into three parts: leaves, stems, and roots, and mark them properly. To determine root morphology features, the root system scanner EPSON Scan was used. The roots were rinsed and cleaned before the scanning time. The images were afterward processed with WinRHIZO analysis software for extracting values regarding total root length (RL), Root surface area (RS), root diameter (RD), and root volume (RV). Furthermore, the specific root length (cm·g− 1) = root length/root biomass, and specific root area (cm2·g− 1) = root surface area/root biomass. The roots, stems, and leaves were put into an oven at 105 ℃ for sterilization for 30 min, and then adjusted to 80 ℃ for drying to constant weight [38]. The biomass (g) of the roots, stems, and leaves of F. malacophylla seedlings was obtained by weighing. Total plant biomass = root biomass + stem biomass + leaf biomass; allocation of root, stem, and leaf biomass=root, stem, and leaf biomass/total biomass [42].

Nutrient content and enzyme activity

To determine the content of C, N, and P in various organs of plants, the sample was ground into a powder of 0.15 mm using a grinder and then sieved through a 0.15 mm sieve for nutrient content determination in various organs. Weigh 0.02 g of powder sample in a hard test tube for the determination of organic carbon, and weigh 0.1 g of powder sample in a digestion test tube. After digestion and clarification using the H2SO4-H2O2 method, it is used to determine the nitrogen and phosphorus content. Organic carbon was determined using the potassium dichromate sulfuric acid oxidation external heating method, total nitrogen was determined using the Kjeldahl digestion method, and total phosphorus was determined using the NaOH alkali solubilization molybdenum antimony resistance colorimetric method. The specific measurement method is described in detail in [43]. This study defined the non-structural carbohydrate (NSC) content as the sum of soluble sugar and starch content, which was determined using the phenol sulfuric acid method [44]. Take 0.1 g of the ground sample, add a small amount of distilled water, grind it into a homogenate, and dissolve it in an 8 mL centrifuge tube. After soaking in a boiling water bath for 30 min, cool it and centrifuge at 8,000 rpm for 5 min. Take the supernatant and dissolve it in a 50 mL centrifuge tube. Afterward, add 1mL of distilled water, 1 mL of test solution, 1 mL of 9% phenol, and 5 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid in a hard test tube for 30 min to determine the soluble sugar content. Measure the starch content of the centrifuged residue using the same steps. To determine the contents of soluble protein (SPC), peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and proline (Pro) in plants, this study used the kit steps of Suzhou Greasy Biotechnology Co., Ltd. to measure them. SPC, POD, SOD, MDA, and Pro were all measured by weighing 0.1 g of fresh tissue samples.

Photosynthetic pigments and fluorescence parameters

Assay of photosynthetic pigment concentration (mg/g): Weigh 0.2 g of fresh tissue and place it in a grinding bowl. In order to grind the sample better, a small amount of SiO2 is added during the grinding process. In addition, 2–3 mL of 95% ethanol was added, and the homogenate was ground to a volume of 25 mL using 95% ethanol. Finally, the absorbance values of the F. malacophylla seedlings were measured using 665, 649, and 470 nm spectrophotometers to calculate the content of chlorophyll a (Chla), chlorophyll b (Chlb), and carotenoids (Car) in the F. malacophylla seedlings [45]. In addition, the leaves of the F. malacophylla belong to feather-like compound leaves, with petioles measuring 2–4 cm in length, and the leaves are located on the small leaves of the plant.

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameter assay: From 9:00 to 11:30 a.m. Beijing time on sunny days, under the conditions of a light intensity of 1,800 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, a CO₂ concentration of 400 µmol·mol⁻¹, and a temperature of 25 ℃, 2–3 broad leaves at the subapical position of each treated F. malacophylla seedling were selected. Following 30 min of dark acclimation, a Li-6,800 portable photosynthesis analyzer was employed to measure the initial fluorescence (Fₒ), maximum fluorescence (Fₘ), and variable fluorescence (Fv) under dark conditions. The chlorophyll fluorescence parameters assayed included light-adapted maximum fluorescence (Fₘ’), dark-acclimated maximum photochemical quantum yield (Fv/Fₘ) of PSII, PSII potential activity (Fv/Fₒ), excitation energy capture efficiency of the PSII reaction center (Fv’/Fₘ’), non-photochemical quenching coefficient (NPQ), photochemical quenching coefficient (qP), and electron transport rate (ETR).

Photosynthetic gas exchange and response curve

The Li-6,800 portable photosynthesis analyzer (Li COR, USA) was used to measure the photosynthetic gas parameters, including net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), transpiration rate (Tr), and internal cellular CO2 concentration (Ci), of the F. malacophylla seedlings. The light response curve and CO2 response curve of the F. malacophylla seedlings were also measured. To explore how F. malacophylla seedlings optimize energy capture and conversion efficiency, as well as their photosynthetic responses and regulatory mechanisms under changing environments, this study adopted the method described by Ye [46] to measure multiple photosynthetic parameters of the seedlings. Including apparent quantum yield (AQY), maximum net photosynthetic rate under light (LPnmax), light saturation point (LSP), and light compensation point (LCP); Maximum net photosynthetic rate (Pnmax), carboxylation efficiency (α), dark respiration rate (Rd), photorespiration rate (Rp), CO2 saturation point (CSP), and CO2 compensation point (CCP).

Statistical analysis

Utilizing Amos 23.0, construct a structural equation model to explore the degree of influence between physiological growth and photosynthetic characteristics of F. malacophylla seedlings. A structural equation model (SEM) combines path analysis and confirmatory factor analysis to construct, estimate, and test causal relationship models. It can analyze both observed variables and latent variables, quantify direct and indirect effects among variables, and account for measurement errors. This method was applied to verify pre-specified hypotheses on causal relationships between observed indicators and latent variables, including measurement models, confirmatory factor analysis models, and latent variable path analysis models. Model fit was evaluated based on four core indices: x2/df, RMSEA, CFI, TLI (Tucker-Lewis Index), and SRMR (Standardized Root Mean Square Residual). The evaluation criteria were defined as x2/df < 3, RMSEA < 0.10, CFI > 0.90, TLI > 0.95, and SRMR < 0.08. Finally, the data were organized and calculated using Excel 2,010, and multiple comparisons, two-factor analysis of variance, fuzzy comprehensive analysis, principal component analysis (PCA), and correlation analysis were performed using SPSS 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Pictures were created using Origin 2,021 (OriginLab Co., Northampton, MA, USA). Fuzzy comprehensive evaluation is a comprehensive assessment method based on fuzzy mathematics. Following the principle of fuzzy relation synthesis, it quantifies factors with fuzzy boundaries that are difficult to directly measure. In this study, we applied this method to comprehensively evaluate the photosynthetic physiological indicators of F. malacophylla seedlings under different treatments, so as to determine the optimal treatment for seedling growth. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to identify the dominant factors affecting plant growth. All evaluation indicators were processed by range standardization to construct membership functions. The calculation formula was as follows: Membership value = (Xi -Xmax)/(Xmax -Xmin), where Xi is the measured value of a single indicator, Xmax and Xmin are the maximum and minimum values of the corresponding indicator among all samples. All indicators were assigned equal weights. The final comprehensive membership value was calculated by synthesizing the membership values of all individual indicators according to fuzzy synthetic rules.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, USA) and Origin 2,021 (OriginLab, USA). Before parametric tests, the normality of data was verified by the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was examined by Levene’s test. The results showed that all measured indicators conformed to a normal distribution and homogeneous variances (P > 0.05), satisfying the prerequisites for two-way analysis of variance (Two-way ANOVA). Subsequently, Two-way ANOVA was applied to analyze the effects of karst habitat, rainfall interval, and their interaction on seedling growth, photosynthetic physiology, and nutrient indicators. Treatment groups with significant differences were further compared using Duncan’s multiple range test, with the significance level set at P < 0.05.

Results

Biomass

Under different rainfall intervals and karst habitat treatments, the root, stem, leaf, aboveground, and total biomass of F. malacophylla seedlings all showed extremely significant differences (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2). For the interaction effect of the two factors, only root biomass and total biomass exhibited extremely significant differences (P < 0.01), whereas stem, leaf, and aboveground biomass showed no significant differences (P > 0.05). Specifically, under the 3-day rainfall interval, root biomass was the highest (8.980 g) in the S3/4 karst region, while the stems and leaves were highest in the S1/2 karst region, with values of 5.960 g and 5.710 g, respectively. Under the 6-day rainfall interval, root biomass of F. malacophylla seedlings in the S3/4 karst habitat was 2.384 times that in the S0 karst habitat, while the stem and leaf biomass were 0.973 and 0.954 times, respectively. Under a 9-day rainfall interval, the root, stem, and leaf biomass of F. malacophylla seedlings in the S0 karst habitat were 66.039%, 7.800%, and 22.343% lower than that in the S1/2 treatment, respectively. Under the same karst habitat treatment, the biomass of all organs of F. malacophylla seedlings showed a significant trend of first increasing and then decreasing with the extension of the rainfall interval.

The distribution of biomass in various organs of F. malacophylla seedlings varies in different karst habitats and rainfall allocation (Fig. 3). In the S0 karst habitat, the distribution of biomass in various organs is as follows: stem> leaf> root, while in the S1/2 and S3/4 karst habitats, it is as follows: root> stem> leaf. Influenced by the intensification of karst fissures, i.e., the degree of rocky desertification, the F. malacophylla seedlings are more inclined to accumulate root biomass. From Fig. 2, under the same rainfall interval, the root biomass shows a gradually increasing trend with the increase of karst thickness, while the stem and leaves show the opposite significant change trend. Moreover, roots and stems showed significant differences (P < 0.01) under different rainfall intervals and karst habitat treatments, while leaves only showed significant differences in karst habitat.

Root characteristics

From Fig. 4, the root morphological traits of F. malacophylla seedlings, including root length, root surface area, root volume, root diameter, specific root length, and specific root area, exhibited highly significant differences under different karst fissure and rainfall interval treatments (P < 0.01). Specifically, RL, RS, RV, and RD of F. malacophylla seedlings were 108.049%, 54.471%, 73.385%, and 61.2662% higher in the 6-day rainfall interval than in the 9-day rainfall interval, respectively, in the S0 karst habitat. In the S1/2 karst habitat, the RL, RS, RV, and RD of F. malacophylla seedlings treated with a 9-day rainfall interval were the lowest, with values of 566.280 cm, 238.248 cm2, 14.176 cm3, and 0.770 mm, respectively. In the S3/4 karst habitat, RL, RS, RV, and RD of F. malacophylla seedlings were 484.739 cm, 572.944 cm², 13.220 cm³, and 0.798 mm greater under the 6-day rainfall interval than under the 3-day interval, respectively. Under the same karst habitat, the RL, RS, RV, and RD of F. malacophylla seedlings showed a significant trend of first increasing and then decreasing with the increase of rainfall interval. Extending the rainfall interval inhibited the growth of root characteristics of F. malacophylla seedlings.

Nutrients and NSC

Nutrients

From Table 3, under different karst habitats and rainfall intervals, there were significant differences in nutrient content (C, N, P) of various organs of the F. malacophylla seedlings (P < 0.05). For the S×I interaction effect, only the root RN content showed significant differences (P < 0.05). Under the same rainfall interval, the nutrient content of various organs in the F. malacophylla seedlings showed a significant change pattern of first increasing and then decreasing with the increase of karst thickness (P < 0.05). Under the same rainfall interval, the nutrient (C, N, P) content of various organs in F. malacophylla seedlings exhibited a consistent and significant trend of first increasing and then decreasing with the increase in karst thickness (P < 0.05). It is evident from the results that across all treatments, the C content in various organs followed the order of leaves> roots> stems, while the N and P content ranked as leaves> stems> roots. Prolonged rainfall interval (9 days) and severe rocky desertification degree (S3/4) exerted significant inhibitory effects on nutrient accumulation in all organs. Conversely, there was a promoting effect on nutrient content accumulation of F. malacophylla seedlings in the S1/2 karst habitat.

Enzymatic activity

Different karst habitats and rainfall distribution patterns have an impact on the enzyme activity of F. malacophylla seedlings, alleviating the stress of karst fissures on F. malacophylla seedlings, which helps alleviate the stress caused by karst fissures on the seedlings (Fig. 5). Under different rainfall intervals, karst habitats, and their interactions, the SOD, POD, SPC, Pro, and MDA levels of F. malacophylla seedlings showed significant differences (P < 0.01). Under the rainfall intervals of 3d and 6 days, the activities of SOD, POD, and SPC content in the F. malacophylla seedlings gradually increased with the increase in karst thickness, whereas the opposite trend was observed under the 9-day rainfall interval. In contrast, both Pro content and MDA content exhibited a significant increasing trend with the aggravation of karst fissure severity. Notably, the maximum values of Pro (86.588 µg·g⁻¹) and MDA (26.470 nmol·g⁻¹) were detected in the S3/4 karst habitat combined with the 9-day rainfall interval treatment, indicating that this condition induced severe disruption of the biofilm structure and function in F. malacophylla seedlings. Further analysis revealed that in the S1/2 karst habitat, the activities of SOD, POD, and SPC content under the 6-day rainfall interval were 34.907%, 24.561%, and 24.678% higher than those under the 3-day rainfall interval, respectively. Similarly, in the S3/4 karst habitat, the highest SOD (363.834 U·g⁻¹), POD (135.000 U·g⁻¹), and SPC (405.125 U·g⁻¹) activities were observed under the 6-day rainfall interval treatment. In summary, moderate increases in karst thickness (from S0 to S1/2) upregulated the activities of SOD, POD, and SPC content in F. malacophylla seedlings under short-to-medium (3-6-day) rainfall intervals. However, under prolonged rainfall intervals (9-day) and severe rocky desertification conditions (S3/4), the antioxidant defense system was overwhelmed, resulting in enhanced biofilm damage and reduced physiological fitness, which is detrimental to the growth of F. malacophylla seedlings.

Photosynthetic pigment content

The photosynthetic pigments (Chla, Chlb, Car, and Chla + b) of F. malacophylla seedlings showed significant differences (P < 0.01) under different rainfall intervals and karst habitat treatments (Fig. 6). However, under the interaction of the two, except for Chlb, which had no significant difference, the other contents showed significant differences (P < 0.01). Specifically, karst habitats determine the availability of soil nutrients (e.g., N and Mg) essential for photosynthetic pigment synthesis, while rainfall intervals regulate soil moisture status, which in turn modulates root uptake and long-distance transport of these nutrients, as well as the de novo synthesis of photosynthetic pigments in leaves. Under the 6-day rainfall interval combined with the S1/2 karst habitat treatment, the contents of Chla, Chlb, Car, and Chla + b in F. malacophylla seedlings reached their maximum values, which were 0.612 mg·g− 1, 0.340 mg·g− 1, 0.278 mg·g− 1, and 0.952 mg·g− 1, respectively. Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters

From Fig. 7, different karst fissures, rainfall interval, and their interaction have a significant and extreme effect on the fluorescence parameters (Fo, Fm, Fv/Fo, Fv/Fm, Fv’/Fm’, qP, ETR, and NPQ) of F. malacophylla seedlings (P < 0.01). In the S0 karst habitat, compared with other rainfall intervals, the Fm, Fv, Fv/Fo, Fv/Fm, Fv’/Fm’, qP, ETR, and NPQ of F. malacophylla seedlings under the 6-day rainfall interval increased by 190.907, 179.137, 0.797, 0.188, 0.099, 0.045, 3.413, and 0.789, respectively. In conclusion, an appropriate increase in karst thickness can promote the photosynthetic process of F. malacophylla seedlings by optimizing their chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, thereby allowing plants to produce more organic matter to provide energy for their growth and development. In contrast, an excessive increase in karst thickness (S3/4 treatment) significantly inhibits the fluorescence parameters of F. malacophylla seedlings.

This effect arises primarily from the tight coupling of these parameters to photosynthetic carbon assimilation and plant water metabolism, both of which are co-modulated by karst habitat conditions and rainfall intervals. Specifically, karst habitat defines soil water-holding capacity and nutrient supply potential, while rainfall intervals determine the temporal availability of soil moisture. Their interaction further modulates key physiological processes, including stomatal movement, CO₂ uptake, and water transpiration in F. malacophylla seedlings. Under the same rainfall interval, Pn, Tr, and Gs showed a significant trend of first increasing and then decreasing with the increase of karst thickness (P < 0.05), and were highest under the I6dS1/2 treatment, with values of 7.935 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, 2.044 mol·m⁻²·s ⁻¹, and 0.071 mol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, respectively. The Ci value was the lowest under the I6dS1/2 treatment, indicating that the photosynthesis of F. malacophylla seedlings consumed the highest concentration of CO2. Yet, WUE increases with the thickness of karst, and the WUE value is highest under the I9dS1/2 treatment, indicating that plants increase water use to maintain their growth and development under this treatment.

Structural equation modeling

The structural equation model (SEM) (Fig. 10) reveals the direct and indirect relationships between the physiological, growth, and root traits of F. malacophylla seedlings under different treatments. Specifically, the RL, SB, and Pro all exerted a direct positive effect on RB, with path coefficients of 0.90 (P < 0.01), 0.10 (P > 0.05), and 0.31 (P < 0.001), respectively. Although RV had no direct effect on POD activity, it could indirectly affect POD activity by directly regulating SOD activity. RV also had a direct positive effect on both RL and RD, indicating that an increase in the RV of F. malacophylla seedlings can promote the growth of RL and RD. Pro and LB had a direct positive effect on MDA content, with path coefficients of 0.92 (P < 0.01) and 0.05 (P < 0.01), respectively. In addition, SPC had a direct negative effect on POD and MDA on SOD activity, with path coefficients of -1.36 (P < 0.05) and − 0.34 (P < 0.01), respectively. This indicates that an increase in SPC in F. malacophylla seedlings leads to a decrease in POD activity, while a decrease in MDA content enhances SOD activity. These results suggest there are significant correlations between physiological growth, organ biomass, and root traits of F. malacophylla seedlings, which may affect antioxidant enzyme activities (Fig. 10-A). From Fig. 10-B, there were direct or indirect relationships among photosynthetic pigments, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters, and photosynthetic gas exchange parameters of F. malacophylla seedlings. Specifically, Chla, Chlb, and Gs all had a direct positive effect on Tr, with path coefficients of 0.50 (P < 0.01), 0.24 (P < 0.05), and 0.57 (P < 0.01), respectively. Tr had a direct positive effect on Pn with a path coefficient of 1.41 (P < 0.01), indicating that the increase in transpiration rate of F. malacophylla seedlings promotes the synthesis of net photosynthetic rate. Fm indirectly affected Tr through Chlb, thereby exerting an indirect impact on Pn. Fv hurt Car, with a path coefficient of -1.41 (P > 0.05), indicating that an increase in Fv tends to inhibit the Car synthesis.

Fuzzy comprehensive analysis

From Table 9, the average membership function values of F. malacophylla seedlings under different karst fissure and rainfall interval treatments followed the order: I6dS1/2> I3dS1/2> I6dS3/4> I6dS0> I3dS0> I3dS3/4> I9dS1/2> I9dS0> I9dS3/4. This clearly indicates that the I6dS1/2 treatment was the optimal group for seedling growth, followed by the I3dS1/2 treatment. These results suggest that a moderate increase in karst fissure thickness may promote the growth of F. malacophylla seedlings. Combined with previous analysis, it can also be seen that moderate increases in karst thickness facilitate the synthesis of nutrients, photosynthetic pigments, and the optimization of chlorophyll fluorescence parameters in F. malacophylla seedlings. In contrast, under the I9dS3/4 treatment, the average membership function values of various indicators in the F. malacophylla seedlings were the lowest, which fully indicates that severe rocky desertification (S3/4) exerts a significant inhibitory effect on the growth and development of F. malacophylla seedlings. Therefore, in areas with severe rocky desertification, F. malacophylla is not suitable for its growth and development, and can only grow well in areas with moderate or lower levels of rocky desertification, which provides a theoretical basis for the rational planting and application of F. malacophylla in karst rocky desertification remediation.

Under different karst fissures and rainfall interval treatments, significant correlations were observed among the photosynthetic physiological indicators of F. malacophylla seedlings (Fig. 11). Specifically, there was a highly significant positive correlation (P < 0.01) between SB, LB, Gs, E, A, NPQ, ETR, qP, Fv ‘/Fm’, Fv/Fm, Fv, Fm, Car, Chla, and Chlb in the F. malacophylla seedlings. This indicates that F. malacophylla can absorb solar energy through photosynthetic pigments under light conditions, convert water and CO2 into photosynthetic products, and thereby promote plant growth and increase biomass accumulation. SOD and POD activities were significantly positively correlated with Gs, Tr, Pn, NPQ, ETR, qP, Fv/Fm, Fv, Fm, Car, Chla, and Chlb, indicating that an increase in SOD and POD activities is accompanied by an increase in these photosynthetic and fluorescence parameters. This is because enhanced SOD and POD activities help prevent damage to the physiological system of F. malacophylla seedlings, thereby maintaining and promoting plant photosynthetic capacity. In contrast, MDA showed a significant negative correlation with SB, LB, Gs, Tr, Pn, Chla, and Chlb, indicating that an increase in MDA is detrimental to the photosynthetic process of F. malacophylla seedlings, which is consistent with the previous finding that MDA accumulation reflects biofilm damage and inhibits plant growth.

Discussion

Modified and optimized from previous research [39], this study adjusted the habitat gradients established by Zheng et al. [39] by adding the S3/4 gradient for severe rocky desertification. This improvement allowed the treatments to reflect better real habitat conditions across mild, moderate, and severe rocky desertification areas, facilitating exploration of the adaptation threshold of F. malacophylla seedlings under intense habitat stress. Meanwhile, we identified that the I6dS1/2 and I3dS1/2 treatments were optimal for seedling growth, verifying that F. malacophylla can maintain stable morphology and physiological functions via photosynthetic regulation strategies. This finding is consistent with our third core hypothesis, which states that the combination of the half-rock half-soil (S1/2) habitat and moderate rainfall intervals (3 days or 6 days) is the most conducive to the growth and photosynthetic physiological stability of F. malacophylla seedlings. There is a significant difference from the conclusion of Zheng et al. [39] on coniferous trees. This study focuses on broad-leaved trees with F. malacophylla and introduces an extreme habitat with more rocks and less soil (S3/4). The experimental period has been postponed to July to December, which is more in line with the growth rhythm of broad-leaved trees and can reveal the adaptation rules of broad-leaved trees in extreme karst habitats, thereby avoiding the limitations of research on coniferous trees. Zheng et al. [39] found that the optimal habitats for coniferous trees were I6dS1/4 and I3dS0, while this study showed that the optimal habitats for F. malacophylla were I6dS1/2 and I3dS1/2. These results for F. malacophylla suggest that tree species type (coniferous vs. broad-leaved) may influence their suitable habitats: coniferous trees tend to rely on thick soil layers, while this broad-leaved species can effectively acquire and utilize resources within karst fissures. This difference further verifies the plasticity of plant adaptive strategies (Phenotypic Plasticity Theory) and the species-specific patterns of resource utilization (Resource Allocation Theory)—different tree species adjust their adaptive traits and resource utilization strategies according to their own functional characteristics, rather than adopting a uniform adaptation mode. This finding corrects the prevailing misconception that “all tree seedlings in karst areas share consistent suitable habitats”, and provides a theoretical basis for vegetation restoration in karst rocky desertification regions [47–48]. In addition, Zheng et al. [39] focused on the correlation between metal ions (potassium, calcium, sodium, and magnesium) and growth and photosynthesis. This study focused on C, N, P, and carbohydrates, revealing the synergistic adaptation strategy of “photosynthetic physiology carbohydrate” in F. malacophylla. This further supplements empirical support for the resource allocation theory—specifically, optimizing the allocation of nutrients and carbohydrates to sustain photosynthetic function—in broad-leaved trees, enriches the adaptive mechanisms of broad-leaved trees, and deepens the research on the correlation between photosynthesis, nutrient utilization, and seedling growth in karst habitat. In summary, the core differences between this study and Zheng et al.‘s [39] study lie in the research objects, habitat gradients, and research perspectives. This not only fills the gap in broad-leaved tree research, but also corrects relevant knowledge [49], and provides a more targeted theoretical basis for vegetation restoration in karst rocky desertification areas.

Growth and root characteristics

Biomass is an important indicator of plant energy accumulation, and the distribution differences of biomass in various organs can reflect the growth strategy of plants [50]. In line with our established mechanistic framework (karst habitat × rainfall interval - soil water and nutrient dynamics - seedling physiological response and growth), karst habitat defines the intensity of environmental stress (e.g., soil moisture deficit, nutrient scarcity), while rainfall intervals regulate the soil moisture supply regime, and the two coupled factors jointly modulate the antioxidant defense and osmotic adjustment strategies of F. malacophylla seedlings. This interactive regulatory process is a key manifestation of the water-nutrient coupling limitation theory, which posits that plant growth in nutrient-poor and arid environments is jointly limited by water and nutrient availability, and the interaction between these two factors determines the physiological responses and growth status of plants. In this study, the root biomass of F. malacophylla seedlings increased with the deepening of karst fissures, while the stem and leaf biomass first increased and then decreased. The increase of karst fissures promoted the growth of F. malacophylla seedlings, and F. malacophylla seedlings in all-soil habitats were not as effective as moderate and severe rock desertification, which is similar to the research results of Zong and Shi [51]. This phenomenon is closely linked to the interaction between karst habitat properties and soil water-holding capacity, and is further modulated by rainfall intervals. This finding directly supports our first core hypothesis that karst habitat (S) and rainfall interval (I) exert significant interactive effects on the morphological growth of F. malacophylla seedlings. Specifically, under the all-soil treatment (S0), although plants can obtain the most nutrients and living space, the permeability of karst soil is poor, and the soil in this habitat is prone to compaction, resulting in certain deep-water stress [52]; Such stress is more pronounced under long rainfall intervals (I9d). Poor soil permeability in this scenario slows water infiltration and renders the surface soil prone to waterlogging, while the deep soil profile remains deprived of an effective water supply, which further restricts seedling growth. In contrast, habitats with 50% rock outcrop coverage and 50% soil coverage (S1/2) possess favorable soil permeability, which ensures sufficient oxygen availability for plant roots, thereby benefiting root respiration and nutrient uptake. Meanwhile, under short-to-medium rainfall intervals (I3d, I6d), karst fissures exert a strong convergence effect: they collect and channel rainfall infiltration into deep soil layers, creating a stable water supply for seedlings and thus further boosting plant growth. However, the accumulation of biomass in F. malacophylla seedlings was significantly inhibited in severe rocky desertification (S3/4) treatment, consistent with Wu et al. [53] study on the effect of drought stress on Sophora davidii seedlings. This may increase the karst thickness and improve soil permeability to a certain extent, but it also leads to rapid loss of soil moisture, causing drought problems and inhibiting the accumulation of biomass in the stems and leaves of the F. malacophylla seedlings. On the contrary, the biomass of F. malacophylla roots grows better than stems and leaves in severe rocky desertification. This finding directly verifies our fourth core hypothesis, which states that F. malacophylla can utilize moisture in deep karst fissures by modulating its root distribution pattern, thereby exhibiting a biomass allocation pattern of roots> stems> leaves. This pattern represents a key adaptive trait enabling the species to cope with the water-limited karst environment. Specifically, F. malacophylla alleviates karst-related environmental stress through inherent physiological regulatory mechanisms, which ensure its normal growth and development. As the development of karst fissures and the degree of rocky desertification intensify, soil water-holding capacity declines, and the risk of drought stress rises. Accordingly, F. malacophylla seedlings exhibit a stronger tendency to allocate more biomass to roots to enhance their capacity for deep water uptake and resistance to environmental stress. This tendency is more pronounced under long rainfall intervals; by contrast, under short rainfall intervals, the stable water supply reduces the need for excessive root proliferation, resulting in a slight increase in the proportion of biomass allocated to stems and leaves. This is consistent with the research results of Peng et al. [54] and Zheng et al. [38]. The variation trend of organ biomass indicated that prolonged rainfall intervals inhibited biomass accumulation of F. malacophylla seedlings. The increased thickness of karst fissures facilitated the accumulation of root biomass. The expanded karst fissures accelerated water loss and aggravated drought stress. To maintain normal growth, seedlings allocated more assimilates to root tissues.

In this study, the root characteristics of F. malacophylla seedlings increased in thickness with the deepening of karst cracks, and prolonged rainfall intervals (I9d) inhibited root growth, which is similar to previous research results [55]. The limestone and soil on the ground are extremely unstable due to subsurface dissolution and collapse effects, so the plants will adapt to this adverse environment through some special morphology and structure. Therefore, the reason for the thickening of the root system of F. malacophylla seedlings is most likely to ensure their growth and development by increasing the length, root width, root volume, and root surface area of the root system. In addition, drought stress at different growth stages can cause various morphological and physiological changes in plants [56]. During the seedling growth stage, drought stress may lead to an increase in root dry weight, root elongation, or a decrease in stem growth [57]. This study also showed that under karst drought stress, the root biomass allocation of F. malacophylla seedlings was the highest (Fig. 3-A), and indicators such as root length, root volume, and root diameter were the best (Fig. 4). Therefore, under moderate and severe rocky desertification treatments, the distribution of leaf biomass in F. malacophylla seedlings was the lowest (Fig. 3-C), which is a key adaptive response consistent with our mechanistic framework.

The nutrient content of C, N, and P in various organs of plants is one of the important indicators reflecting the nutritional status of plants, which can reflect the growth status of plants in adverse environments [38]. This study found that the C content in various organs of F. malacophylla seedlings showed that leaves> roots> stems, while the N and P content showed that leaves> stems> roots. Notably, the accumulation of C, N, and P in different organs of F. malacophylla seedlings is not only driven by the independent effects of karst habitat and rainfall intervals, but also modulated by the interaction between these two factors. Under moderate karst thickness, it promoted nutrient accumulation in all organs of F. malacophylla seedlings. This effect is primarily driven by two factors: first, the moderately developed karst fissures in the S1/2 habitat balance soil permeability and water-holding capacity; second, under suitable rainfall intervals (I3d, I6d), the fissure convergence effect effectively retains rainfall, thereby providing a stable water supply for nutrient uptake and transport in seedlings. In contrast, prolonged rainfall intervals (I9d) significantly inhibited nutrient accumulation, as long-term water deficit constrains the nutrient uptake activity of roots and the synthesis of nutrient-bearing compounds in leaves. Excessively thick karst fissures would trigger severe soil erosion, thereby inhibiting seedling growth. The nutrient content in leaves was higher than that in stems and roots, which is similar to the study by Luo et al. [58]. This is because leaves are the most active organs for metabolic processes such as photosynthesis, cellular respiration, and transpiration, requiring a large amount of nutrients to maintain these activities, as well as more photosynthetic pigments, proteins, nucleic acids, enzymes, and other compounds containing N and P for plant photosynthesis. However, stems are storage organs for cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin [59]. To prevent excessive storage of N and P in stems and roots, plants allocate more N and P to photosynthetic organs rather than stems and roots [60]. Zhang et al. [61] also reported similar findings, which suggest that the contents of C, N, and P in photosynthetic organs are higher than those in non-photosynthetic organs. C: N and C: P typically affect the efficiency of plant utilization of N and P nutrients [62]. The leaf N: P ratio is commonly used to indicate nutrient limitations during plant growth [63]. Among them, N: P < 14 indicates N limitation, N: P > 16 indicates P limitation, and 14 < N: P < 16 indicates that plants are affected by N and P [64]. This study found that under different desertification treatments, the N: P ratio of the leaves of F. malacophylla seedlings was less than 14 (Table 4), which is different from the results of Han et al. [65]. This N limitation in F. malacophylla seedlings is closely linked to the interaction between karst habitat properties and rainfall intervals. This limitation is driven first by the inherently nutrient-poor soils of karst regions, where low background soil N content directly restricts plant N uptake. In addition, karst ecosystems are inherently prone to severe soil erosion: under long rainfall intervals (I9d), concentrated rainfall events can wash away substantial amounts of soil N; meanwhile, the prolonged soil drying between rainfall events leaves soil N susceptible to degradation via photolysis, oxidative decomposition, and other processes, further driving N loss and exacerbating plant N deficiency. In contrast, under short rainfall intervals (I3d), frequent rainfall events mitigate soil erosion and reduce N loss to a certain extent, thus alleviating N limitation in seedlings. This alleviating effect is more pronounced in the moderate half-rock half-soil karst habitat (S1/2), which can be attributed to its superior water and nutrient retention capacity.

The main components of non-structural carbohydrates (NSC) are soluble sugars and starch; they are not only one of the main components of soil active organic matter [66] but also key products of plant photosynthesis and respiration. The content of NSC can directly determine the carbon supply and demand status in plants [67], which is closely related to plant growth, stress resistance, and adaptive strategies in karst habitats. This study indicates that, with the increase of karst thickness, the contents of soluble sugar, starch, and NSC in various organs of F. malacophylla seedlings showed a significant trend of first increasing and then decreasing. Moreover, the ratio of soluble sugar and starch content in the stems and leaves of F. malacophylla under the S1/2 treatment was significantly higher than that in the S0 treatment (Table 5), which indicates that increasing karst thickness promotes the continuous conversion of starch to soluble sugar in F. malacophylla, which is consistent with previous studies [68]. This study also found that under different treatments, the contents of soluble sugar and NSC in various organs of F. malacophylla seedlings followed the order of leaves> stems> roots. This phenomenon suggests that different karst habitat treatments may slow down the transport of NSC from leaves to roots and stems by increasing the NSC content of leaves and decreasing the NSC ratio of roots and stems. This may also be attributed to the fact that organic matter produced by photosynthesis in leaves is mainly allocated to leaf organs to ensure their growth and development, thereby maintaining efficient plant photosynthetic capacity. Specifically, under the S1/2 karst thickness, the NSC content of F. malacophylla seedlings was significantly increased, which may help alleviate the stress damage of karst habitats to F. malacophylla seedlings and enhance their adaptability to the harsh karst environment.

In addition, plants accumulate beneficial osmoregulatory substances such as proline, soluble proteins, and soluble sugars in their cells when facing adverse stress, to maintain the osmotic equilibrium within the cells and reduce the damage of stress to plants [69]. Antioxidant enzyme activity is a major component of plant antioxidant defense systems [70]. SOD is toxic to H2O2, which must be converted into O2 and H2O through antioxidant enzymes (e.g., POD), and SOD and POD belong to the enzymatic clearance mechanisms [71–72]. However, when plants are subjected to mitigating stress, a large amount of soluble proteins are synthesized to reduce the osmotic potential of plant cells and protect the plant from environmental toxicity [73]. This study shows that under the rainfall intervals of 3-day and 6-day, the SOD, POD, and SPC of F. malacophylla seedlings gradually increase with the increase of karst thickness, and show the opposite trend under the rainfall interval of 9 days. Consistent with previous research findings [74–75]. This distinct difference in antioxidant enzyme activity and osmoregulatory substance content is closely modulated by the interaction between karst habitat (i.e., rock outcrop gradient) and rainfall intervals. This pattern is primarily attributed to the fact that under short-to-medium rainfall intervals (I3d, I6d), with increasing rock outcrop coverage from the full-soil habitat S0 to the half-rock half-soil habitat S1/2, the enhanced soil permeability and fissure convergence effect provide sufficient oxygen and water for seedlings. In addition, Karst habitat defines the intensity of environmental stress (e.g., soil moisture deficit, nutrient scarcity), while rainfall intervals regulate the soil moisture supply regime, with these two factors (S and I) jointly modulating the antioxidant defense and osmotic adjustment strategies of seedlings. This enables F. malacophylla seedlings to actively regulate the activity of antioxidant enzymes (SOD and POD) to mitigate oxidative damage induced by environmental stress in rocky desertification habitats. Meanwhile, the stable water supply under the I3d and I6d intervals allows seedlings to maintain normal physiological metabolism, and thus upregulates SPC to reduce cellular osmotic potential, ultimately supporting normal plant growth. Moderate rocky desertification (S1/2) alleviated the damage to F. malacophylla seedlings from karst cracks under 9 days of rainfall, which is consistent with previous results on nitrogen fertilizer alleviating stress-induced membrane damage [76]. In this study, we also found that MDA and Pro were significantly higher in I9dS3/4 than in other treatments (Fig. 5-D-E), suggesting that prolonged rainfall and S3/4 karst habitats resulted in an increase in the MDA content of F. malacophylla seedlings, and that karst fissure stress has already caused damage to the membrane system, exacerbating membrane lipid peroxidation, which is consistent with the results of Li et al. [77]. The significant increase in MDA content in the I9dS3/4 treatment group is a direct consequence of the interaction between long rainfall intervals and severe stress from highly developed karst fissures in high rock coverage habitats. Long rainfall intervals (I9d) induce persistent water deficit, while the S3/4 habitat with severely developed karst fissures accelerates soil moisture depletion and exacerbates comprehensive environmental stress. These two coupled stress factors inflict severe damage to the cell membrane system of seedlings. However, MDA has a direct positive effect on SPC (Fig. 10-A), indicating that an increase in MDA can lead to physiological disruption in plants, increasing SPC content to resist this damage. An increase in SPC can enhance plant metabolism, promote the synthesis of new tissues, and facilitate the maintenance of normal physiological functions in the body. If lipid peroxidation decreases, SOD increases.

Photosynthetic pigments

Photosynthetic pigments (Chla, Chlb, Car) are one of the most important components of plant photosynthesis, and their content decreases significantly under water scarcity conditions [78]. Increasing chlorophyllase activity under adverse conditions can alter the chlorophyll structure and lipoprotein complexes in the membrane, and reduce the inactivation of PSII-associated light-harvesting pigment proteins and Calvin cycle enzymes [79]. Photosynthetic pigments are important molecules involved in photoprotection during photosynthesis and also serve as oxygen scavengers for antioxidants [78]. In this study, the photosynthetic pigment content of F. malacophylla seedlings showed a trend of first increasing and then decreasing with the increase of karst thickness (Fig. 6), which is consistent with the research results of Ahmadizadeh [79]. It is possible that low-frequency heavy rainfall (I9d) in a habitat with more rocks and less soil (S3/4) can lead to leaching, causing nutrient loss in the soil with the loss of rainfall. It is also possible that the increase in karst fissures causes plants to experience certain drought, resulting in high temperatures that inhibit the formation of chlorophyll esters in the plant body, leading to a decrease in chlorophyll formation. In addition, Fv hurts Car, with a path coefficient of -1.41 (P > 0.05), indicating that the increase in Fv inhibits the synthesis of Car (Fig. 10-A). This is because Chla and Car produce a non-fluorescent exciton complex, decreasing chlorophyll fluorescence parameters. Secondly, Chla has a direct negative effect on Chlb, thereby indirectly producing a direct positive effect on Car. This is because the F. malacophylla in karst fissures will reduce its absorption of light by decreasing chlorophyll content, thereby increasing Car content to protect chlorophyll and dissipate excess light energy. This constitutes an important adaptive response, which is consistent with our core hypothesis regarding photosynthetic physiological regulation.

Fluorescence and gas exchange parameters

Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters are a set of variables or constants used to describe the photosynthetic physiological status and mechanism of plants, reflecting that the greater the electron transfer activity of plant PSII, the greater the light energy utilization efficiency. To a certain extent, ETR is positively correlated with the photochemical efficiency of PSII, and Fv/Fm reflects the potential maximum photosynthetic capacity of plants, which is the most obvious feature of plant photoinhibition. It can provide a relative measure of the maximum quantum yield of primary photochemistry in Photosystem II, and the Fv/Fm value of non-stressed leaves is about 0.83; qP is a decrease in fluorescence caused by photochemical reactions [80]. The level of Fo is related to the state of the reaction center. An increase in Fo indicates severe damage or inactivation of the PSII reaction center, while a decrease indicates increased heat dissipation in the plant body, which is a photoprotective mechanism [81]. Fm reflects the electron-transport ability of PSII, while a decrease in Fm indicates a decrease in the conversion of light energy to chemical energy [82]. This part of the results verifies both the phenotypic plasticity theory and the water-nutrient coupling limitation theory: photosynthetic efficiency parameters (including fluorescence parameters and gas exchange parameters) reflect the adaptive adjustment of the plant’s photosynthetic function in response to water and nutrient conditions. This study indicates that with the thickening of karst fissures, the Fm, Fv, Fv/Fo, Fv/Fm, Fv ‘/Fm’, qP, ETR, and NPQ of F. malacophylla seedlings first increase and then decrease, while Fo shows the opposite trend, which is not completely consistent with the results of Shi et al. [83] and Zhang et al. [84] research. The decrease in fluorescence parameters indicates that the deterioration of rocky desertification habitats and the extension of rainfall intervals can affect the activity of light-harvesting protein complexes and enzymes, leading to an impact on the activity of PSII reaction centers and inhibiting the conversion of captured light energy into chemical energy by photosynthetic pigments [85]. The decrease in Fo indicates that the PSII reaction center has not completely deactivated, and more energy is dissipated in heat [80]. In this study, in the S3/4 karst habitat, the Fv/Fm of the F. malacophylla seedlings was lower than 0.5 (Fig. 7-E), indicating that under this treatment, karst fissures damaged the light and system of the F. malacophylla seedlings to some extent, thereby inhibiting plant photosynthesis. This phenomenon is the net result of the interaction between severe karst fissure stress in the high rock coverage S3/4 habitat and rainfall intervals, with the most pronounced effect observed under long rainfall intervals (I9d). This pattern is primarily driven by two coupled stress processes: first, the severe rocky desertification of the S3/4 habitat drives rapid soil water depletion, which in turn leads to elevated soil temperature; second, the 9-day rain-free period under the I9d interval subjects F. malacophylla seedlings to persistent water deficit. These combined stressors jointly impair the PSII reaction center, thereby reducing the maximum photochemical efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm) in seedlings. In contrast, in the S3/4 habitat paired with the I3d short rainfall interval, frequent rainfall partially replenishes soil moisture and mitigates water deficit stress. In addition, during the experiment, we also found that under this treatment, the number of leaves of the F. malacophylla seedlings was reduced, and the leaves were small, resulting in yellowing and curling, which indicates that the plant’s growth was inhibited. This study found that photosynthetic gas exchange parameters of F. malacophylla seedlings were closely linked to photosynthetic carbon assimilation and plant water metabolism, which were jointly regulated by karst habitat and rainfall interval. Karst habitats determine soil water retention and nutrient supply, while rainfall intervals control soil moisture availability; their combined effects further regulate stomatal movement, CO₂ absorption, and transpiration. The lowest Ci in the I6dS1/2 treatment reflected intense CO₂ consumption during photosynthesis. The elevated WUE under the I9dS1/2 and S3/4 treatments suggested that seedlings regulated water use to sustain normal growth under such conditions (Table 6).

In this study, the F. malacophylla seedlings showed lower LCP and higher LSP under I6dS1/2 treatment (Table 7), which is consistent with previous research results [10]. This study also found that the S3/4 karst habitat inhibited the photosynthesis of F. malacophylla seedlings, which may be attributed to the increase in karst fissure density leading to water deficit in the soil microenvironment. As a result, the insufficient water transported to the leaves induces stomatal closure, resulting in a decrease in leaf CO₂ concentration—both water and CO₂ are essential raw materials for photosynthesis, and their deficiency directly impairs photosynthetic efficiency.

In addition, when the leaves lack water, starch hydrolysis is enhanced [86], leading to continuous sugar accumulation and slowed export of photosynthetic products, sugars, which further inhibits the photosynthetic rate of F. malacophylla. Notably, under I9dS3/4 conditions, seedlings suffered the most severe water shortage, resulting in obvious drought stress. However, the WUE of F. malacophylla seedlings under this treatment was the highest (Table 6), indicating that the plant had the strongest water-saving capacity under extreme stress. Higher drought-tolerant productivity is often associated with stronger dependence of transpiration rate on stomatal regulation. Apart from stomatal limitations, non-stomatal factors also play a significant role in photosynthetic rate [87]. Under drought stress, plants can achieve moderate regulation between prioritizing photosynthesis and prioritizing transpiration to maintain water balance [88]. When water becomes the most critical limiting factor, plants tend to significantly reduce transpiration rate while maintaining the photosynthetic rate at an appropriate level to ensure energy supply, thereby improving WUE. This result is consistent with the relevant research findings of Huang et al. [88]. Furthermore, severe rocky desertification (S3/4) leads to severe soil erosion, resulting in water scarcity in the plant growth environment, which not only induces drought stress but also inhibits the expansion of the photosynthetic area. Meanwhile, the ability of leaves to absorb light energy is also reduced, and these factors collectively contribute to the decline in the photosynthetic rate of F. malacophylla seedlings. In this study, except for the 3-day rainfall interval treatment, the apparent quantum yield of F. malacophylla seedlings under other treatments was relatively low (Table 7), even lower than the general plant AQY range of 0.03 ≤ AQY ≤ 0.05 [89], indicating that the light energy utilization efficiency of F. malacophylla is relatively low under low-light conditions. The CO2 response curve reflects the response characteristics of leaf photosynthetic capacity to changes in ambient CO2 concentration. Generally, the higher the CSP of plants, the stronger their adaptability to high-CO2 environments, while those with low CCP tend to have higher Pn and faster growth [90]. The I6dS1/2 treatment resulted in a higher CSP, lower CCP, and the lowest photorespiration rate in F. malacophylla seedlings, indicating that this optimal treatment significantly enhanced the CO₂ utilization capacity of the seedlings. In addition, as shown in Fig. 10-B, Tr had a direct positive effect on Pn with a path coefficient of 1.41 (P < 0.01), indicating that the increase in Tr promotes Pn synthesis in F. malacophylla seedlings. This mechanism can be explained as follows: when Tr is relatively high, plants keep stomata open to maintain water balance, allowing excess water to dissipate into the atmosphere through the stomatal pores. The opening of stomata not only facilitates water loss but also promotes the absorption of external CO₂—an essential raw material for photosynthesis—thus directly promoting photosynthetic processes and increasing Pn.

Based on the above physiological responses, long-term field monitoring studies are strongly recommended. Although the present controlled experiment clarifies the short-term physiological adaptation strategies of F. malacophylla under varying water regimes and rocky desertification gradients, long-term in situ observations are necessary to capture interannual climate fluctuations, natural soil-water dynamics, and community-level interactions, which will improve the accuracy and applicability of the conclusions in practical afforestation. For policymakers and forest managers, three practical recommendations are proposed: (1) In severely rocky desertification areas (S3/4), appropriate soil and water conservation measures should be implemented to alleviate extreme drought stress, as severe water deficit significantly reduces photosynthetic area and light use efficiency despite high WUE. (2) A rainfall interval of approximately 6 days under mild-to-moderate rocky desertification (S1/2) can be used as a reference for irrigation and tending management during afforestation, as this regime optimizes CO₂ utilization and reduces photorespiration. (3) Targeted soil improvement projects should be carried out in fragile karst areas to reduce soil erosion and maintain stable soil moisture, thereby enhancing the photosynthetic efficiency and survival rate of native afforestation tree species.

Conclusion

This study indicates that under the same rainfall interval, the root traits of F. malacophylla seedlings showed a significant increasing trend (P < 0.05), which reflects the strong adaptability of the species to different growth environments. Their aboveground growth indicators and photosynthetic physiological parameters first increased and then decreased with the gradual increase of karst thickness, among which the growth state was the best under the I6dS1/2 treatment, showing the optimal growth effect. The leaf N: P ratio of F. malacophylla was less than 14, which clearly indicates that the plant growth is limited by nitrogen, so it is necessary to properly add nitrogen fertilizer in the rocky desertification area to meet the growth needs. Fuzzy membership analysis results show that the seedling performance under different treatments is ranked as follows: I6dS1/2> I3dS1/2> I6dS3/4> I6dS0> I3dS0> I3dS3/4> I9dS1/2> I9dS0> I9dS3/4. Core implications: prolonged rainfall interval inhibits growth, while moderate karst thickness (S1/2) promotes photosynthesis. For conservation and management, prioritize F. malacophylla as restoration seedlings, use moderate rocky desert areas for cultivation, apply N fertilizer, and regulate rainfall to enhance restoration efficiency and ecosystem stability. This study provides important technical support for the vegetation restoration and ecological management of karst areas.
