Objective evaluation of platelet-rich plasma mesotherapy on facial skin quality: An interim split-face analysis using an advanced imaging system
Authors: Masashi Umeda, Takahiko Tamura, Kohki Okumura, Reiko Kiuchi, Hiroo Teranishi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAutologous platelet-rich plasma (PRP), which is rich in growth factors, is widely used in aesthetic medicine to improve facial skin quality. However, its efficacy has been largely supported by subjective evaluations and case reports; objective and quantitative evidence remains limited. This study aimed to objectively and quantitatively evaluate the effects of PRP mesotherapy on facial skin quality, using a validated image analysis system.MethodsNine healthy adults (six females and three males; median age, 34 years) employed at the Tokyo Chuo Beauty Clinic were enrolled in this study. A split-face design was used, with PRP injected into the left hemiface and saline injected into the right hemiface under identical conditions (5 mL each). Facial images were obtained at baseline, two weeks, and one month using the NeoVoir system, which automatically identifies ten facial regions and extracts multiple quantitative skin parameters. The percentage change from baseline was compared between the sides.ResultsThe primary endpoint, the gloss area at one month, showed a significantly greater increase on the PRP-treated side than on the saline-treated side (median difference, 16.3 %; p = 0.0039). At two weeks, the gloss also improved more on the PRP side, although the difference was not significant after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Pore scores showed a greater reduction on the PRP side at one month (median difference, −17.6 %; p = 0.0039), but this result did not remain statistically significant after FDR adjustment (q = 0.0663). No significant differences were observed in wrinkle area, melanin content, or pigmentation indices. No serious adverse events occurred; only transient, mild local reactions such as redness and swelling were noted on both sides.ConclusionThis study provides novel objective evidence that PRP mesotherapy significantly improves facial gloss, a key feature of healthy young skin. An exploratory trend toward pore reduction was also observed, although mechanistic interpretations remain hypothesized owing to the absence of biophysical and structural measurements. Moving beyond subjective assessments, these findings support the potential of PRP for enhancing facial brightness, smoothness, and overall skin quality. Further studies with larger cohorts, extended follow-ups, and multimodal evaluations are warranted to validate and expand upon these preliminary results.Keywords: Platelet-rich plasma (PRP), Mesotherapy, Skin rejuvenation, Facial image analysis, Split-face study

Introduction

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has gained widespread use in aesthetic dermatology because of its high concentration of autologous growth factors that promote tissue regeneration and collagen synthesis [1,2]. PRP therapy is frequently used to enhance facial skin quality, particularly skin tone, texture, and elasticity [3]. Despite its popularity, physiological validation of the efficacy of PRP has been limited, as most supporting evidence is derived from subjective assessments, patient questionnaires, or small-scale case reports [4,5]. Only a few studies have objectively and quantitatively evaluated the effects of PRP on facial skin quality using standardized and reproducible methods. Recent advances in facial imaging technology have enabled precise, reproducible, and region-specific evaluation of skin conditions [6]. Among these tools, the NeoVoir imaging system (C-Lab Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) provides high-resolution multispectral analysis of facial skin, automatically identifying multiple anatomical regions and extracting key parameters, such as pore count, wrinkle area, pigmentation, gloss, and sebum content [7]. This technology allows an objective assessment of treatment efficacy and a detailed analysis of time-dependent changes across multiple skin parameters, providing insights into the physiological mechanisms underlying skin rejuvenation. These advances provide a valuable opportunity to move beyond subjective questionnaire-based evaluations and establish empirical evidence for the physiological effects of aesthetic interventions, such as PRP mesotherapy.

This study aimed to objectively and quantitatively evaluate the enhancement of facial skin quality by PRP administered via mesotherapy, using a split-face design. PRP and saline were injected into opposite sides of the face under identical conditions, and serial facial images were obtained at multiple time points using the NeoVoir imaging system [7]. By comprehensively analyzing time-dependent changes in multiple skin parameters, this study aimed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying PRP-induced enhancement of facial skin quality and provide objective evidence of its efficacy under standardized and controlled conditions. Among these parameters, the gloss area serves as an objective optical indicator that reflects the smoothness of the stratum corneum and its light-reflective properties, and is broadly recognized as a representative feature of overall skin quality. Therefore, the primary endpoint of this study was the gloss area at one month, whereas all other parameters were treated as secondary or exploratory outcomes.

Materials and methods

Patients

Nine healthy Japanese volunteers (six females and three males) aged 27–43 years, all of whom were medical staff employed at the Tokyo Chuo Beauty Clinic (Umeda Osaka Ekimae Branch), were enrolled. The inclusion criteria were the desire to undergo facial PRP treatment and the provision of written informed consent. The exclusion criteria were active facial skin disease, current pregnancy or breastfeeding, use of anticoagulant medications, and any other conditions deemed inappropriate by the principal investigator.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Review Board of Tokyo Chuo Beauty Clinic (approval no. UMEDAERB-2024Mar002). This study was registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN-CTR) under the identifier UMIN000058413. Verbal and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions, including the most recent amendment adopted in Fortaleza, Brazil, in October 2013.

This study was conducted as a prespecified interim analysis of a registered clinical trial (UMIN000058413). The registered protocol described a non-randomized, open-label, self-controlled trial with fixed allocation (left side = PRP, right side = saline), a target sample size of 10 participants, and the primary endpoints of photographic assessment and patient satisfaction at 1, 3, and 6 months. Nine healthy volunteer staff participants were included in this interim analysis, each receiving 5 mL of PRP on the left side and 5 mL of saline on the contralateral right side. The discrepancy between the registered target (n = 10) and analyzed sample size (n = 9) was due to the limited feasibility of recruiting volunteer staff. The present report focuses on exploratory secondary endpoints using NeoVoir imaging analysis at 2 weeks and 1 month, while the registered primary endpoints at 3 and 6 months are ongoing and will be reported separately. The UMIN registry entry (UMIN000058413) has been updated to include the fixed side allocation (left, platelet-rich plasma; right, saline) and to clarify that this pre-specified interim analysis focused on early device-based endpoints.

Study design

This was a small-scale, exploratory clinical trial with a fully open-label design. Allocation was fixed with PRP administered to the left hemiface and normal saline to the right hemiface; all procedures were performed by the investigators themselves. Due to the specifications of the NeoVoir analysis system, masking of side labels was technically infeasible; therefore, image data were exported with visible labels, and statistical analyses were conducted with knowledge of the side allocation. However, the regions of interest and quantitative outputs were automatically and objectively determined by the software, thereby minimizing the potential for subjective influences. To confirm baseline symmetry, paired comparisons of each outcome were performed between the two sides, with appropriate adjustments applied to control the effects of multiple testing. The primary endpoint was pre-defined as the gloss area at one month, while all other NeoVoir parameters were regarded as secondary or exploratory outcomes.

Safety assessments were performed through clinical examinations immediately after treatment, at the 2-week and 1-month follow-up visits, and through participant self-reporting during the observation period. Adverse events were evaluated with a focus on mild local reactions, such as erythema, edema, subcutaneous hemorrhage, and tenderness.

Preparation of PRP

PRP was prepared using the MyCells PRP kit (Kay-Light Ltd., Israel; approval number: 30400BZX00169000, approved by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan) with a closed-system single-spin method [8]. This system has been reported in previous studies to yield a platelet concentration approximately 2.5–5 times that of whole blood [8]. However, as this was an exploratory clinical study, platelet counts before and after preparation and white and red blood cell content were not measured. Therefore, PRP was not further classified as leukocyte-rich/-poor.

Peripheral venous blood (30 mL) was collected from each participant and divided into three 10 mL aliquots, each placed in a vacuum-sealed tube containing acid citrate dextrose solution anticoagulant and a gel separator. Each tube was centrifuged individually at 2000×g for 7 min using a tabletop centrifuge (H-19α, Kokusan Co., Japan).

After centrifugation, approximately 4.5 mL of plasma from the upper layer of each tube was carefully aspirated and discarded. The plasma remaining close to the gel interface, which contained concentrated platelets, was gently aspirated and subjected to approximately ten pipetting cycles to ensure a uniform platelet distribution (Fig. 1).

Preparation of PRP Centrifuged peripheral blood prepared using the MyCells PRP kit. Blood samples were collected and processed using the single-spin method, resulting in clear separation into platelet-poor plasma (PPP, upper layer) and platelet-rich plasma (PRP, layer above the gel separator).

The PRP fractions obtained from all three tubes were pooled to yield approximately 5 mL of final PRP per participant. The PRP was transferred into 5 mL syringes fitted with 32-gauge needles for subsequent injection. All PRP injections were administered within 2 h of blood collection to maintain the biological activity.

Injection procedure

All injections were performed using the Bellapro Injector device (Contac Korea Co., Ltd., Seoul, South Korea), a mechanical multi-needle injector designed for uniform and precise intradermal delivery. A total of 5 mL of PRP was injected into the left side of the face and 5 mL of normal saline was injected into the right side under identical conditions.

The injection depth ranged from 0.8 to 1.2 mm and was adjusted according to skin thickness and facial region, with 100 injection points per hemiface. The vacuum was set at Level 2, and the injection mode was configured to “intermittent” with the “Filler + Solution” setting. Injections were administered to the cheeks, infraorbital areas, and lateral canthal regions, which are regions that can be separately analyzed using the NeoVoir imaging system. All procedures were performed by the same practitioner under consistent environmental and technical conditions to ensure standardization.

Imaging equipment

The NeoVoir imaging system is a non-invasive diagnostic platform consisting of two integrated components: a high-resolution facial imaging unit and a proprietary analysis application. The device captures standardized frontal facial images under four illumination conditions: standard visible light, ultraviolet (UV) light, cross-polarized light, and parallel-polarized light (Fig. 2). The software automatically controlled the illumination intensity during image capture to maintain consistency across sessions. The focus was standardized by photographing the calibration chart included in the system to ensure spatial accuracy and reproducibility. According to the manufacturer's documentation (C-Lab Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), the analysis software was developed using data from approximately 3000 Korean individuals, which serve as an internal reference set for system calibration. The system is designed and optimized for use in East Asian populations.

Imaging equipment Representative images for facial skin analysis captured by the NeoVoir imaging system. High-resolution facial images were acquired under various lighting conditions, including standard visible light, cross-polarized light, parallel-polarized light, and ultraviolet light, to visualize and quantify skin parameters. Automated analysis delineates pores, wrinkles, pigmentation, and glossy areas, enabling an objective and region-specific evaluation of skin conditions.

Measurement conditions

Prior to imaging, all participants removed cosmetic or skincare products and rested for 30 min in a climate-controlled room maintained at 20–24 °C and 48–50 % relative humidity. Imaging was performed with the participant seated in a fixed position using chin and forehead supports to ensure consistent facial orientation. The participants were instructed to maintain a neutral expression and gently close their eyes to minimize muscle tension and motion artifacts during capture.

Image analysis parameters

The NeoVoir software uses deep-learning algorithms to automatically identify ten pre-defined anatomical regions of the face: the forehead, nasal dorsum, left and right cheeks, outer corners and lower eyelid areas of both eyes, and bilateral nasolabial folds. Twenty objective skin parameters were measured in each region. These included the number and size of facial pores; total area of wrinkles; skin tone and brightness based on red, green, and blue (RGB) color values; gloss area and intensity; melanin concentration; non-erythematous pigmentation; sebum content; and porphyrin fluorescence.

Measurement principles

Wrinkles and pores were assessed using a shadow-based analysis method that detected topographical depressions by analyzing the variations in shadow density created under oblique lighting. The gloss was evaluated using horizontally polarized light images in which highly reflective surfaces appeared as bright white zones. Pigment-related features such as melanin accumulation and non-red discoloration were identified in the UV light images based on their characteristic light absorption properties, resulting in darkened regions corresponding to pigment-dense areas.

Data output and application

All measured values were automatically converted into numerical data by the NeoVoir software and exported for statistical analysis. These data were used to compare the skin characteristics between the PRP- and saline-treated sides of the face. All imaging procedures, including the light conditions, positioning protocols, and software parameters, were standardized across participants to ensure methodological consistency and reliability.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, and continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs).

Comparisons between the PRP- and saline-treated sides were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The primary endpoint for this interim analysis was pre-specified as the percentage change in the gloss area at 1 month. Because this was a single primary hypothesis, no multiplicity adjustment was applied, and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05.

The percentage changes of other NeoVoir parameters (e.g., pore count, pore size, wrinkle area, melanin content, pigmentation indices) were regarded as secondary or exploratory endpoints. To control for type-I error inflation due to multiple testing, the false discovery rate (FDR = 0.05) was adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Accordingly, both unadjusted p-values and FDR-adjusted q-values are reported, with statistical significance defined as q < 0.05.

Quantitative skin parameters obtained from the NeoVoir imaging system (Fig. 2) were used for analysis. Redness, sebum, porphyrin fluorescence, and skin tone parameters (RGB values) were excluded a priori based on the statistical analysis plan because their baseline values were very low and highly variable, and because they are strongly influenced by inflammatory responses or sebaceous gland activity, which are unlikely to be substantially affected by PRP within the short follow-up period.

The unit of analysis was defined as one hemiface per participant. For each participant, summary values were calculated separately for the PRP- and saline-treated sides, and the paired values were compared. Thus, the ten facial regions identified by the NeoVoir system were not analyzed as independent observations, thereby avoiding pseudoreplication.

Percentage change was calculated as: (post-treatment value − baseline value)/baseline value × 100. A negative percentage change was interpreted as an improvement in pore count, pore size, wrinkle area, melanin content, or pigmentation, whereas a positive percentage change was interpreted as an improvement in gloss area.

Effect sizes were expressed as Hodges–Lehmann median paired differences (PRP minus saline, in percentage points) with 95 % confidence intervals obtained using the Wilcoxon test function in R.

Results

Participant characteristics

Nine healthy adult volunteers (six females and three males) were included in the final analysis. The median patient age was 34 y (IQR: 31–37 y; range: 27–43 y). All the participants were medical staff members of the Tokyo Chuo Beauty Clinic (Umeda Osaka Ekimae Branch) and met the inclusion criteria.

Each participant underwent a single session of split-face mesotherapy in which autologous PRP was injected into the left hemiface and normal saline into the right hemiface under identical conditions. None of the participants had a history of active facial dermatological disorders, systemic illness, recent cosmetic treatments, or concurrent use of topical or systemic medications affecting skin condition.

All the observed adverse events were mild, transient, and self-limiting, and no serious adverse events occurred. The detailed frequencies of each adverse event by side and time point are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

All participants completed follow-up assessments at two weeks and one month after treatment and were analyzed on a per-protocol basis.

At baseline, no significant differences were observed between the PRP-allocated- and saline-treated sides for any skin parameter (Table 1). All FDR-adjusted q values exceeded 0.05, confirming sufficient baseline symmetry between the two sides.

Discussion

PRP is an autologous blood-derived product containing high concentrations of platelets and growth factors that play key roles in tissue repair, angiogenesis, and collagen synthesis [3,9]. Platelets release several growth factors, including platelet-derived growth factor, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), epidermal growth factor, and hepatocyte growth factor [10,11]. These factors promote cellular proliferation and differentiation, tissue regeneration, angiogenesis, connective tissue remodeling, and cell cycle regulation [10,11]. Initially developed for use in fields such as oral surgery and orthopedics [3,9], PRP has recently gained widespread attention in dermatology and aesthetic medicine as a minimally invasive regenerative treatment [5]. In particular, PRP therapy has been employed to improve skin tone, texture, elasticity, and overall skin quality, as it is believed to enhance dermal remodeling and rejuvenation by stimulating fibroblasts and extracellular matrix (ECM) production [10]. Despite its popularity in clinical settings, evidence supporting the efficacy of PRP in improving facial skin quality has largely relied on subjective assessments, anecdotal reports, and small observational studies, lacking robust, objective, and quantitative validation [4,5]. This study adds to the limited evidence available on the objective and quantitative evaluation of PRP mesotherapy for facial skin rejuvenation using a validated image analysis system [7]. In this study, we addressed this gap by using the NeoVoir imaging system, which enables a highly reproducible and region-specific quantitative evaluation, in a standardized split-face design to rigorously assess the effects of PRP [7].

The most notable finding of this study was a significant increase in the facial gloss area on the PRP-treated side. The expansion of the glossy area is presumed to reflect improvements in the optical uniformity of the stratum corneum and surface smoothness of the skin, potentially attributable to enhanced stratum corneum transparency, more homogeneous lipid film distribution, and reduced microtextural irregularities. However, because no direct biophysical assessments (e.g., transepidermal water loss or stratum corneum hydration) were conducted in this study, this interpretation remains hypothesis-generating and should be validated in future research. These changes are associated with enhanced brightness and clarity of the skin, and are generally considered indicative of a healthy appearance [12]. Previous studies have also demonstrated that subjective impressions of skin “radiance” or “glow” are highly correlated with objective parameters derived from optical imaging analysis, such as gloss area and specular light intensity [12]. Our findings align with this relationship and provide objective validation of the effect of PRP on improving skin radiance.

In addition, the pore scores showed a greater reduction on the PRP-treated side at one month, with a nominal p-value below 0.05. However, this did not remain statistically significant after adjusting for multiple testing using the FDR procedure, indicating that the results should be interpreted as an exploratory rather than a definitive effect. From a mechanistic perspective, platelet-derived growth factors in PRP may activate dermal fibroblasts, thereby promoting the synthesis of collagen and other ECM components. This contributes to dermal remodeling and structural reinforcement around the pilosebaceous units, thereby reducing the apparent size of the facial pores [3,13]. Nevertheless, because no structural imaging modalities (e.g., high-frequency ultrasound or optical coherence tomography) were employed in this study, this mechanistic interpretation should also be regarded as hypothesis generating. In particular, TGF-β is known to upregulate the gene expression of type I collagen, fibronectin, and decorin in fibroblasts, playing a pivotal role in ECM reconstruction [1]. Platelet-derived growth factor promotes fibroblast migration and proliferation, while VEGF facilitates angiogenesis, enhancing local oxygenation and nutrient delivery [2,13]. The synergistic action of these bioactive factors is believed to improve dermal elasticity and density, leading to enhanced cutaneous support and a reduction in the visible pore size. Although our study demonstrated only a trend-level improvement after statistical correction, these biological mechanisms remain plausible and warrant further investigation in larger studies.

In contrast, no significant changes were observed in wrinkle area, melanin content, or pigmentation indices during the one-month observation period. These features are typically associated with long-term structural alterations in the dermis or chronic melanocyte activation, which requires extended durations to exhibit measurable improvements [4]. Although TGF-β has been reported to suppress melanocyte activity, the biological effects of PRP on pigmentation are likely not detectable within such a short timeframe.

Additionally, redness, sebum production, porphyrin fluorescence, and skin tone (RGB values) were excluded from the analysis because of their high baseline variability and limited direct association with PRP's primary mechanisms of action of PRP [4]. Porphyrins are metabolic byproducts of Cutibacterium acnes and are more closely associated with sebaceous gland activity and the composition of the skin microbiota than with dermal remodeling. Moreover, in this study, sebum was measured 30 min after facial cleansing, which may not have adequately reflected baseline sebum secretion, potentially contributing to the weak correlations observed.

Future studies incorporating multipoint time-series analyses, such as assessments at 2, 4, 8, and 12 weeks, would allow for a more detailed characterization of the temporal dynamics of PRP efficacy, including the identification of time-dependent treatment response peaks.

In terms of safety, all adverse events observed after PRP and saline administration were mild, transient, and self-limiting and no serious adverse events were identified. Thus, there were no major concerns regarding the short-term safety profile of PRP mesotherapy. However, given the small sample size and short follow-up period, further investigations are warranted to evaluate the long-term safety and detect rare adverse events.

This study has several limitations. The sample size was relatively small, and the follow-up period was limited to one month, which may not fully capture the long-term effects of PRP. Sex was considered a biological variable; however, because of the small sample size, subgroup analyses by sex were not feasible, thereby limiting our ability to evaluate potential sex-specific effects. Furthermore, the study cohort consisted of healthy Japanese adults with relatively favorable baseline skin conditions, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to individuals with more advanced photoaging or different skin types [5]. The registered primary endpoints, including photographic assessment and patient satisfaction at three and six months, are still ongoing and are not included in the present report. Therefore, the findings presented here should be interpreted as preliminary results from a prespecified interim analysis that focuses on exploratory device-based outcomes. This study was conducted with a fully open-label design, and due to the specifications of the NeoVoir analysis system, masking of side labels was not possible during data analysis. Accordingly, statistical analyses were performed with knowledge of side allocation, and the possibility of analytical bias could not be entirely excluded. However, because the NeoVoir system provides automated and objective quantitative outputs, and because no significant baseline differences were observed even after FDR correction, the influence of such bias is considered limited. Although the split-face design and baseline symmetry checks helped minimize interside bias, potential real-world asymmetries—such as habitual sleep position or differences in ultraviolet exposure—cannot be completely excluded. Nevertheless, the inability to implement masking during analysis remains a methodological limitation that should be addressed in future studies. Finally, we did not measure platelet counts or red and white blood cell counts before or after PRP preparation. As a result, we could not confirm the actual enrichment values in our participants or classify the PRP into leukocyte-rich or-poor categories. Although the MyCells system has been reported in previous studies to yield a 2.5–5-fold platelet concentration [8], the absence of direct quantification in this study should be taken into account when considering reproducibility and external comparability across PRP systems.

Nonetheless, this study has several strengths. The split-face design minimizes inter-individual variability, and the use of a high-resolution, validated imaging system ensures objective and reproducible evaluation [7]. Importantly, this study goes beyond questionnaire-based subjective assessments by providing objective quantitative data on the effects of PRP on facial skin quality. Moreover, by comprehensively analyzing time-dependent changes across multiple skin parameters, this study elucidated the physiological basis of PRP's role of PRP in improving skin quality. Standardized injection protocols and measurement conditions further enhanced the reliability of our findings. Importantly, this study highlights the novelty of quantitatively evaluating the temporal dynamics of PRP-induced changes in multiple skin parameters, offering empirical data independent of subjective impressions or anecdotal case observations and underscoring its clinical significance [5].

Conclusions

This study objectively demonstrated that PRP mesotherapy significantly enhanced facial skin gloss, supporting its role in improving skin radiance and overall skin quality. An exploratory trend toward pore score reduction was also observed, although statistical significance was not maintained after correction for multiple tests. Therefore, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating, highlighting the potential of PRP to contribute to a brighter, more refined, and youthful appearance. This study advances the objective evaluation of PRP efficacy by providing quantitative evidence beyond a subjective assessment. Further investigations with larger and diverse cohorts, extended follow-up periods, and complementary biophysical and structural assessments are required to validate and expand upon these results.
