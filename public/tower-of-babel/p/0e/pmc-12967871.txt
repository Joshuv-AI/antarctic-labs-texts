Diabetes Knowledge and Medication Adherence Among High‐Risk Type 2 Diabetic Patients in a Rural Area in Vietnam: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Huong Thi Thu Ho, Ha Thi Vo, Pramote Tragulpiankit, Surakit Nathisuwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is becoming increasingly prevalent worldwide, especially in developing countries such as Vietnam; however, data on diabetes knowledge and medication adherence among Vietnamese high‐risk T2DM patients, particularly those living in the rural areas, remains scarce. This study aimed to evaluate the levels of diabetes knowledge and medication adherence, along with their relationship, among high‐risk T2DM patients in a rural area of Vietnam. A cross‐sectional study was conducted at Quang Thanh General Hospital, a tertiary care hospital in Nghe An Province, Vietnam, between May and July 2024. Diabetes knowledge and adherence were assessed using the Vietnamese versions of the Spoken Knowledge in Low Literacy Patients with Diabetes (SKILLD) and the General Medication Adherence Scale (GMAS), respectively. Binary logistic regression was used to assess the association between diabetes knowledge and medication adherence. A total of 230 high‐risk, T2DM patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the study. The mean age was 67.9 ± 6.6 years, while 43.9% were male. The mean SKILLD score was 36.7 ± 20.03, with 82.6% classified as having low diabetes knowledge. For medication adherence, the mean GMAS score was 30.4 ± 3.07, with 16.1% of patients classified as being non‐adherent. Binary logistic regression showed that better knowledge significantly increased the likelihood of medication adherence (OR = 8.3, 95% CI: 1.1–64.8, p = 0.043). In conclusion, diabetes knowledge was low among Vietnamese high‐risk T2DM patients. A strong relationship existed between high diabetes knowledge and better medication adherence.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a long‐term metabolic condition defined by increased blood glucose levels caused by insulin resistance or reduced insulin secretion [1]. Data from the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) indicates that around 537 million people are living with diabetes, comprising 10.5% of the worldwide population, and this figure is expected to rise to 643 million by 2030 [2]. In Vietnam, diabetes has become a pressing health issue due to rapid industrialization and modernization of the country in the past several decades [3]. Data from a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis including approximately 240,000 individuals suggested that the prevalence of diabetes in Vietnam among adults aged 20–79 in 2021 was 6.0% or about 6 million patients [3].

Despite being an important public health problem, T2DM control in Vietnam remains poor [4]. A recent multi‐center, cross‐sectional study among over 1600 T2DM patients from hospital‐based outpatient care in Vietnam reported that only 36.1% of patients achieved the target hemoglobin A1c of < 7.0% [4]. A recent study using data of over 1.3 million T2DM Vietnamese patients from the national claims database showed that 55% of T2DM patients had diabetes‐related complications, with cardiovascular disease as the most common complication [5]. In addition, 70% of the total direct medical costs were attributed to diabetes‐related complications [5].

Despite increasing urbanization, two thirds of Vietnamese population still resides in the rural area with significant agriculture‐based economy [6]. A recent study conducted among 2580 Vietnamese aged 40–64 years indicated generally poor knowledge toward diabetes, particularly among subjects from the rural areas compared with urban areas. Among Vietnamese T2DM, medication adherence was shown to be poorer in patients from rural compared to urban areas [7]. As a result, improving disease management in Vietnamese T2DM patients living in the rural area represent a major challenge. However, there is a scarcity of data regarding the level of diabetes knowledge and medication adherence among this group of patients. Additional data is therefore needed to guide policy design and implementation to tackle this issue effectively.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study was a prospective, cross‐sectional study conducted among high‐risk T2DM patients at Quang Thanh General Hospital, a private, district‐level healthcare facility located in a rural area of Nghe An province, during May–July 2024. High‐risk T2DM defined as patients who had an established diagnosis of T2DM, were men aged ≥ 55 years or women aged ≥ 60 years with established cardiovascular disease and/or the presence of multiple cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking). Patients were included if they provided consent to participate in the study. Exclusion criteria included a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes or any psychiatric illness that might affect cognitive function and communication. A pilot study was conducted to estimate the medication adherence rate in the target population, revealing a preliminary adherence rate of 0.82. Based on this finding, the minimum required sample size was calculated using Cochrane formula resulting in a sample of 226 patients [8].

Translation, Validation of the Tools

The Spoken Knowledge in Low Literacy Diabetes (SKILLD) questionnaire was originally developed and validated by Rothman et al. to evaluate diabetes knowledge among patients with type 2 diabetes and limited literacy. The original validation study included 217 adults with type 2 diabetes and poor glycemic control in the United States, of whom 38% had a literacy level below the sixth grade. The SKILLD consists of 10 open‐ended questions administered orally to assess diabetes knowledge. In the original study, the questionnaire demonstrated good internal reliability, with a Kuder–Richardson coefficient of 0.72. Construct and criterion‐related validity were supported by significant correlations between SKILLD scores and education level, literacy status, duration of diabetes, and glycemic control [9]. As no validated Vietnamese version of the SKILLD questionnaire was available, the instrument was translated and culturally adapted into Vietnamese following the Beaton et al. [10] guideline. This process included forward translation, synthesis of the translations, back translation, and expert panel review. Minor revisions were made based on expert consensus to ensure cultural appropriateness and linguistic clarity. The pre‐final version of the questionnaire was pilot‐tested on 20 patients at high risk for type 2 diabetes to assess item clarity and comprehensibility. Based on participant feedback, some wording adjustments were made to improve clarity and ensure appropriateness for the target patient group (Table S1). After these revisions, the final version was administered to a sample of 100 high‐risk T2DM patients to assess internal consistency. The resulting Cronbach's alpha value was 0.621, indicating a moderate level of reliability. Knowledge scores represent the percentage of correct answers. Patients with scores above 50% are classified as having good knowledge, whereas those with scores of 50% or below are considered to have low knowledge of diabetes [9].

Data Collection

After selecting patients based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, demographic and clinical data were collected through direct interviews and medical records. Demographic information included age, sex, weight, height, occupation, educational level, family history, comorbidities, and the number of medications being used. Data on comorbidities and prescribed medications were extracted from the patients' medical records.

Data on patients' knowledge was collected using the translated and validated Vietnamese version of the SKILLD questionnaire [9]. Medication adherence was assessed using the General Medication Adherence Scale (GMAS), a self‐reported questionnaire originally developed and validated by Naqvi et al. [11] among outpatients with various chronic illnesses. The GMAS consists of 11 items organized into three categories, including patient behavior, additional disease and pill burden, and cost‐related non‐adherence. The instrument demonstrated good internal consistency with the Cronbach's alpha of 0.82 and construct validity, supported by exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses with satisfactory model fit indices. Evidence of convergent, discriminant, and concurrent validity has also been reported. We chose the GMAS among other tools for several reasons. First, the GMAS questionnaire was developed in a developing country with a similar level of socioeconomic status. Second, the questionnaire assesses multiple dimensions of medication adherence, including patient behavior, comorbidities, and cost‐related factors, thus providing a more comprehensive assessment. In addition, the GMAS had been previously translated and validated into the Vietnamese language by previous investigators. Patients were considered adherent if their GMAS score was 27 or higher, and non‐adherent if their score was below 27 [11, 12].

Data Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 20 software for Windows. Normally distributed continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables and responses to each questionnaire item were summarized as absolute and relative (%) frequencies. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze baseline characteristics, as well as data from the knowledge and medication adherence questionnaires and clinical characteristics. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied to assess data normality. Differences in means were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. The Pearson Chi‐square test was used to examine the association between the level of adherence and other factors. Spearman's rank correlation was employed to assess the relationship between knowledge scores, medication adherence levels, and other variables. Binary logistic regression was also performed to evaluate the relationship between knowledge and adherence. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethical Approval and Informed Consent

This study protocol was approved by Faculty of Dentistry/Faculty of Pharmacy Mahidol University Institutional Review Board (Reference number: 78.0319/EC.0214) and the Scientific Committee at Quang Thanh General Hospital (Reference number: 01/BVĐKQT‐HĐKHKT). This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed about the objectives of the study and provided informed consent prior to participation.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 230 patients were enrolled in the study, with a mean age of 67.9 ± 6.55 years (range: 55–88), and 43.9% were male. The majority of participants (50.4%) were aged between 61 and 70 years. The mean body mass index (BMI) was 22.7 ± 2.66 kg/m2, with 59.6% classified as having normal BMI, 19.1% overweight, and 18.7% at risk of obesity. Regarding educational attainment, 37.4% had completed secondary school, 30.0% primary school, 17.8% high school, 10.9% higher education, and 3.9% were illiterate. Farming was the predominant occupation (35.7%), followed by retirees (27.4%) and dependents (27.0%). The mean duration of diabetes was 6.42 ± 5.17 years, with most patients diagnosed between 2 and 10 years ago (39.1% with 2–5 years, 31.3% with 6–10 years). A family history of diabetes was reported in 42.6% of patients. Comorbid conditions were common, with hypertension present in 66.5% and dyslipidemia in 71.7% of patients. Kidney disease was observed in 17.4%, while cardiovascular disease was rare (1.74%). Regarding medication use, the majority were prescribed combination tablets containing agents from the biguanide and sulfonylurea classes in a prolonged‐release formulation, taken 1–2 times per day. In severe cases, doses could be increased by adding a single‐agent formulation of one of the two active substances. Some patients received premixed insulin twice daily, either in combination with oral medications or as monotherapy. Due to comorbidities, many patients were also prescribed additional medications. Overall, 76.1% of patients were prescribed 3–4 medications, 18.3% used two medications, and 6.1% were on 5–7 medications (Table 1).

Knowledge of Diabetes and Medication Adherence

The mean SKILLD score among participants was 36.7% ± 20.03%, reflecting an overall low level of diabetes‐related knowledge. Applying the standard 50% threshold, 82.6% of patients were categorized as having low knowledge, while only 17.4% demonstrated good knowledge (Table 2). Item‐level analysis indicated that participants performed best on questions addressing the symptoms and management of hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia, with correct response rates of 68.3% and 68.2%, respectively. In contrast, substantial knowledge gaps were observed in questions concerning the normal HbA1C value and foot examination (correct response rate of only 3.9% and 5.2%, respectively). Additionally, low accuracy was noted for items assessing knowledge of the purpose of foot examinations (15.2%) and eye examinations (14.8%). Conversely, participants showed better understanding regarding the recommended frequency of physical activity (53.5%) and awareness of long‐term diabetes complications (49.1%). Detail analysis of each item of SKILLD is provided in (Table S2).

The mean GMAS score among the study participants was 30.4 ± 3.07, with a score range of 20 to 33. A total of 193 patients (83.9%) were classified as adherent (score ≥ 27), while 37 patients (16.1%) were classified as non‐adherent (score < 27) (Table 2). Regarding reasons for non‐adherence, the most commonly reported barrier was the burden of multiple medications and coexisting comorbidities. Correlation of Diabetes Knowledge and Medication Adherence

Gender, education level, duration of illness, hypertension status, and treatment compliance had a statistically significant correlation with the knowledge score, with p‐values of 0.034, 0.022, < 0.0001, 0.03, and 0.004, respectively (Table 3). Age and knowledge score were significantly associated with treatment adherence, with p‐values below 0.05 (Table 4). A binary logistic regression was performed to evaluate the relationship between the level of knowledge and medication adherence. For this analysis, medication adherence was divided into two groups including adherence versus non‐adherence. Variables that were included in the model were age (in years), gender, education level, number of drugs, and duration of diabetes. Results showed that high diabetes knowledge is independently and strongly associated with being adherent to treatment (adjusted OR: 8.33, 95% CI: 1.071–64.787; p = 0.043). In addition, female gender was also significantly associated with higher adherence (adjusted OR: 2.28, 95% CI: 1.074–4.826; p = 0.032) (Tables 3, 4, 5).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that explores the level of diabetes knowledge, medication adherence and their relationship among high‐risk T2DM patients in a rural area of Vietnam. To assess diabetes knowledge, the authors considered various questionnaires that are commonly used in the literature. However, almost all of them are not designed for patients with low literacy, except SKILLD. Strengths of the SKILLD questionnaires lie with its ease of use and its ability to identify knowledge gaps which make it suitable for a tailored made educational intervention in each individual. Through a rigorous translation and validation process employed in our study, we were able to successfully translate, culturally adapt and validate SKILLD questionnaires into the Vietnamese language. This tool will be useful for future research work on this topic in Vietnam for years to come.

Results of our study showed a large knowledge gap in the study population with only 17.4% of patients with good knowledge. While more than half of the study population understood the symptoms of hyperglycemia/hypoglycemia, complications of diabetes and importance of exercise, the proportion of patients who knew about glucose targets and the importance of foot and eye examination were disappointingly low. This rate was lower than that reported in a previous study conducted in Vietnam, where 45.9% of patients using insulin pens were found to have good knowledge [13]. A possible explanation lies with the fact that our study population was generally of substandard education since almost 90% of them had only high‐school education or lower. A higher level of knowledge observed in other studies may be related to a higher proportion of patients living in urban areas with higher levels of health literacy. For example, Nguyen et al. [14] conducted a study among elderly outpatients at the National Geriatric Hospital in Hanoi, where 93.8% of participants were urban residents and had relatively higher knowledge scores in both “diabetes definition and management” (mean = 57.7) and “targets for diabetic control” (mean = 66.1). In contrast, in our study conducted in rural areas, patients had lower SKILLD scores (mean = 36.7) [14]. Attempts to improve diabetes knowledge in the health system can be made in the future by targeting areas of common deficiencies, based on the findings of our study.

Surprisingly, we found that our study population had a high rate of medication adherence, based on high GMAS scores (83.9%). Recent studies in Vietnam showed that the rate of people following treatment for chronic diseases in general and taking their diabetes medicine as prescribed is approximately 60% [15, 16]. One possible explanation for a much higher adherence level in our study was that data were collected in a private hospital setting. Patients presenting to a private hospital may have higher awareness and willingness to take medication, compared to patients going to public hospitals [17]. In addition, patient follow‐up, appointment reminders, and regular adherence support help maintain continuity of care, thereby contributing to improved medication adherence. Among non‐adherent patients, pill burden was the primary reason. Financial constraints had the least impact probably because the expenses were covered by health insurance.

We found that there was a clear and strong association between diabetes knowledge and adherence to treatment. Better diabetes knowledge significantly increased the likelihood of adherence (OR = 8.33, p = 0.043). The finding highlights the importance of diabetes knowledge in improving treatment adherence, which may ultimately improve diabetes management. Health authorities should consider developing interventions using appropriate methods to enhance patients' knowledge, especially among high‐risk individuals living in rural areas. These measures may help slow the occurrence of diabetes complications and improve the quality of life for diabetic patients. Furthermore, female gender is significantly associated with better medication adherence (OR = 2.277, p = 0.032). In rural Vietnam, women often assume primary responsibility for family health care, which may enhance their awareness of disease management and reinforce the importance of regular medication use. Conversely, men in rural areas are more likely to engage in unhealthy behaviors such as smoking or drinking alcohol and may pay less attention to health prevention measures, which can negatively impact medication adherence.

Our study has several notable limitations. First, this study was conducted in a single‐center setting situated in a rural area of Vietnam. As a result, the applicability may be limited to hospitals in the rural area with a similar context. Second, due to the cross‐sectional design, it is not possible to determine a causal relationship between patient knowledge and adherence. Third, both the SKILLD and GMAS questionnaires rely on patient self‐reports, which are susceptible to bias. Patients might over‐ or under‐report their adherence or knowledge in order to meet the expectations of healthcare providers. Fourth, the GMAS questionnaire is a general tool for chronic diseases. Therefore, it may not fully reflect specific medication adherence behaviors in diabetes treatment, such as adherence to injection technique, injection timing, and dosage adjustment, which are crucial parts of treatment adherence. Fifth, the SKILLD questionnaire uses open‐ended questions, which may lead to differences in how patients understand and respond. Lastly, the study only included patients who provided consent to participate, which may have led to selection bias. Patients with poor adherence or insufficient knowledge may be less likely to participate, which could potentially affect the study results.

Conclusion

Diabetes knowledge was low among Vietnamese high‐risk T2DM patients living in the rural area, particularly on glucose targets and the importance of foot and eye examination. A strong relationship existed between diabetes knowledge and medication adherence. Better diabetes knowledge significantly increased the likelihood of better medication adherence. These findings suggest that improving diabetes‐related knowledge could enhance treatment adherence among this high‐risk group of T2DM patients. Policymakers, health workers, and community activists should take practical measures to improve diabetes knowledge and treatment adherence to reduce disease burden on the country's health system.
