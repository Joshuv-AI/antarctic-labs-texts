Advanced accelerated silicosis misdiagnosed as tuberculosis in a 24-year-old gold miner: A case report
Authors: Maria Gabriella Nampiinga, Justine Abaine, Victorious Mangheni, Shaliph Clause Bizumuremyi, Hillary Elgon Wakholi, Nooh Kayiiwa, Anthony Bamwine, Ivaan Pitua

Introduction

Silicosis, an irreversible fibrotic lung disease caused by the inhalation of respirable crystalline silica, remains a neglected epidemic in the developing world [1,2]. While historically associated with lifelong industrial labor, a rapidly progressive phenotype known as “accelerated silicosis” is increasingly documented among young workers in Artisanal and Small-Scale Gold Mining (ASGM) [2]. In this unregulated sector, which employs millions across Sub-Saharan Africa, miners work in confined, poorly ventilated shafts without respiratory protection, inhaling massive loads of silica dust [2,3].

In high-burden settings like Uganda, the diagnosis of silicosis is frequently obscured by its clinical and radiological mimicry of pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) [4]. Both conditions manifest with wasting, fevers, and miliary radiological patterns, creating a diagnostic “blind spot” that often leads to empirical anti-tubercular therapy (ATT) [4,5]. This misdiagnosis is perilous; it exposes patients to hepatotoxic drugs, delays supportive care for pneumoconiosis, and fails to effectively address the “double epidemic” of silicosis and TB.

This case report describes a 24-year-old artisanal miner who presented with a diffuse miliary radiological pattern initially attributed to miliary TB. The subsequent identification of Progressive Massive Fibrosis (PMF) and cor pulmonale highlights the critical necessity of occupational history-taking and high-resolution imaging in differentiating these distinct pulmonary entities.

Case Presentation

A 24-year-old male presented with a five-month history of debilitating pleuritic chest pain, non-productive cough, and progressive dyspnea. The respiratory symptoms were associated with constitutional decline, including intermittent fevers, drenching night sweats, and profound cachexia. Prior to admission, he had received multiple empiric courses of antibiotics and antimalarials at local clinics with no symptomatic relief.

Crucially, the patient's social history revealed seven years of employment in an artisanal gold mine. He described laboring in deep, confined shafts with poor ventilation and consistently without personal protective equipment (PPE), exposing him to high concentrations of silica dust.

On physical examination, the patient appeared chronologically older than his stated age, was severely cachectic, and in evident respiratory distress. Resting oxygen saturation was 82% on room air, with a tachycardia of 135 beats per minute and a blood pressure of 117/77 mmHg. Respiratory examination revealed rightward tracheal deviation and reduced chest expansion, with diffuse fine crackles auscultated bilaterally.

Initial investigations

The initial chest radiograph demonstrated a diffuse, bilateral miliary pattern indistinguishable from the disseminated micronodular appearance of hematogenous tuberculosis. Coalescent homogenous opacities were noted in the right upper and mid-zones. Fig. 1.

A chest X-ray of a 24-year-old gold miner demonstrating a diffuse, bilateral miliary pattern (profuse micronodules) initially suggestive of pulmonary tuberculosis. Prominent coalescent opacities in the upper lung zones indicate progressive massive fibrosis with rightward tracheal deviation, characteristic of advanced silicosis.

A subsequent contrast-enhanced CT scan revealed a cavitary lesion in the right upper lobe (RUL), extensive bilateral consolidations, and a “tree-in-bud” nodular pattern. Despite a negative HIV test, the radiological impression strongly favored pulmonary tuberculosis. However, repeated sputum microscopy and molecular tests (GeneXpert) were negative for Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Given the endemicity of TB and the radiological features, a presumptive diagnosis of “silico-tuberculosis” was made. The patient was commenced on a standard six-month anti-tubercular regimen (rifampicin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol).

Diagnostic re-evaluation and outcome

The patient's clinical course was marked by a lack of response to ATT. He suffered progressive hypoxic failure, necessitating repeated hospitalizations and continuous high-flow oxygen support. Upon completion of the six-month regimen, with sputum cultures remaining negative and respiratory function deteriorating, a non-contrast High-Resolution CT (HRCT) was performed to interrogate the pathology.

The HRCT (Fig. 2) provided definitive clarity, revealing the hallmarks of advanced pneumoconiosis. It demonstrated extensive, symmetrical upper-lobe consolidations with internal calcifications, characteristic of Progressive Massive Fibrosis (PMF). The previously noted RUL cavity persisted, displaying irregular margins and bronchial communication, consistent with ischemic necrosis of a fibrotic mass rather than infectious cavitation.

Coronal reformatted CT images showing extensive, bilateral, symmetrical upper-lobe consolidations (stars) consistent with progressive massive fibrosis and multifocal calcifications. Findings include a thick-walled (20 mm) apical cavity in the right upper lobe (blue arrow), diffuse centrilobular nodules, and an enlarged main pulmonary artery (Orange arrow, 3.8 cm) indicative of pulmonary hypertension secondary to advanced silicosis.

Furthermore, the background lung parenchyma showed diffuse centrilobular nodules and ground-glass opacities. A ominous finding was the enlargement of the main pulmonary artery to 3.8 cm, indicating severe pulmonary hypertension (cor pulmonale).

The diagnosis was revised to advanced accelerated silicosis with PMF. Management was pivoted to palliative support, including high-dose corticosteroids for inflammation and nutritional rehabilitation. The patient passed away after ten months of hospitalization, being oxygen-dependent, and suffering from the severe psychosocial sequelae of this terminal occupational illness.

Discussion

This case exemplifies the devastating trajectory of accelerated silicosis, a form of the disease defined by its rapid onset (<10 years) following intense dust exposure [6]. Unlike chronic silicosis, which develops over decades, accelerated silicosis is a hallmark of the extreme exposure conditions found in ASGM, where dust levels can exceed safety limits by orders of magnitude [7,8].

The primary clinical lesson here is the radiological overlap between silicosis and tuberculosis. The presence of diffuse, profuse micronodules is classic for acute/accelerated silicosis but is frequently misidentified as miliary TB in resource-limited settings [7,9,10]. The HRCT findings in this case offered specific distinguishing features. The “angel wing” appearance of symmetrical upper-lobe conglomerate masses (PMF) and the presence of high-density calcifications are specific to silicosis [11]. Furthermore, while cavitation is a cardinal sign of TB, this case illustrates that large silicotic masses can cavitate due to ischemia, independent of mycobacterial infection [12].

The patient's progression to cor pulmonale, evidenced by the dilated pulmonary artery, signals a poor prognosis. Pulmonary hypertension in silicosis results from the obliteration of the pulmonary vascular bed by fibrosis and hypoxic vasoconstriction [13]. Once established, it is a strong predictor of mortality, often rendering the patient oxygen-dependent for life.

Finally, this case shows the multifaceted burdens miners in Africa face including poverty, occupational hazards, and infectious disease risk. The patient's profound psychosocial distress reflects the reality of young men who, seeking livelihood in mines, face “social death” and physical disability.

Implications for clinical care

Clinicians in resource-limited settings must maintain a high index of suspicion for occupational lung diseases in young patients presenting with respiratory failure, particularly in regions where artisanal mining is prevalent. A detailed occupational history is paramount; the radiological finding of a diffuse miliary pattern should not trigger automatic empirical tuberculosis treatment in the absence of bacteriological confirmation. In patients with known silica exposure who are smear-negative or non-responsive to anti-tubercular therapy, early referral for HRCT is essential to differentiate advanced silicosis from tuberculosis, thereby preventing unnecessary medication toxicity and facilitating timely supportive management.

Conclusion

Accelerated silicosis is a preventable but incurable tragedy that frequently masquerades as pulmonary tuberculosis. In young males with a history of mining, a miliary pattern on chest radiography should prompt immediate consideration of pneumoconiosis, particularly when bacteriological tests for TB are negative. Early utilization of HRCT to identify PMF is essential to prevent unnecessary medication toxicity and to initiate appropriate palliative and supportive care.
