Targeting HASPIN-mediated H3T3 phosphorylation disrupts an epigenetic-kinesin axis to suppress colorectal cancer mitotic progression
Authors: Tong Wu, Yaguang Zhang, Sicheng Liu, Chensong Zhang, Yang Zhang, Qiuye Luo, Dian Gao, Yang Meng, Yixin Jiang, Junhong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chemoresistance remains a major barrier in colorectal cancer (CRC) therapy. Through epigenetic compound screening in patient-derived organoids (PDOs), we identified CX6258.HCl as a potent growth inhibitor. Treatment with CX6258.HCl significantly inhibited cell mitosis and induced apoptosis in CRC cell lines. Mechanistically, CX6258.HCl binds the D687 residue within HASPIN’s kinase domain, suppressing H3T3 phosphorylation (H3T3ph). This triggers an epigenetic cascade: loss of H3T3ph upregulates demethylase KDM5B pre-mRNA, depleting H3K4me3 at promoters of Kinesin family member (KIFC1/KIF10/KIF14). Consequently, microtubule dynamics are disrupted, leading to mitotic arrest. Target specificity was validated genetically via HASPIN-D687A mutation. In vivo, CX6258.HCl suppressed CRC xenograft growth and further enhanced 5-FU-mediated tumor suppression without obvious histological injury in major organs. Clinically, elevated H3T3ph levels in human CRC tissues were associated with Ki67-positive proliferative tumor regions, suggesting that H3T3ph may represent a proliferation-associated marker in CRC. Together, our findings identify the HASPIN/H3T3ph-KDM5B-H3K4me3-KIF axis as a targetable antimitotic pathway and support therapeutic inhibition of HASPIN/H3T3ph as a potential strategy for CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12943-026-02723-5.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) accounts for approximately 10% of all cancer cases and remains the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally [1]. While advances in surgical techniques, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, targeted therapies, and multimodal treatment approaches have improved outcomes, many patients still experience relapse and poor prognosis [2, 3]. These clinical challenges stem from late-stage diagnosis [4], tumor heterogeneity, and acquired drug resistance [5, 6]. Emerging patient-derived organoid (PDO) technology now offers unprecedented opportunities for drug discovery and personalized medicine. These 3D culture systems largely recapitulate key epithelial tumor features, including heterogeneity and drug response profiles [7, 8], thus making them ideal platforms for developing targeted CRC therapies.

Epigenetic dysregulation is increasingly recognized as a hallmark of cancer pathogenesis. Aberrant DNA methylation and histone modifications can silence tumor suppressors while activating oncogenic pathways, driving malignant proliferation and metastasis [9, 10]. The clinical success of epigenetic drugs like DNMT inhibitors (azacitidine) and HDAC inhibitors (vorinostat) in hematologic malignancies [11, 12] has spurred interest in epigenetic therapies for solid tumors. Such approaches may not only restore normal gene expression patterns but also overcome chemoresistance by resensitizing tumor cells to conventional agents.

The serine/threonine kinase HASPIN phosphorylates histone H3 at threonine 3 (H3T3ph) [13], a critical mitotic mark that recruits the chromosomal passenger complex (CPC) to regulate chromosome alignment and segregation [14]. Beyond its catalytic role, HASPIN maintains cohesion stability through interactions with Pds5B [15, 16]. Notably, H3T3ph has emerged as a proliferation marker in mantle cell lymphoma and meningiomas due to its mitosis-specific expression pattern [17, 18]. While HASPIN overexpression has been reported in CRC [19], its functional consequences and therapeutic potential remain unexplored.

Using PDO-based screening of epigenetic compounds, we identified CX6258.HCl as a potent CRC growth inhibitor. Mechanistically, CX6258.HCl targets HASPIN to suppress H3T3 phosphorylation, triggering a novel epigenetic cascade: H3T3ph depletion upregulates KDM5B, which erases H3K4me3 marks at kinesin gene promoters (KIFC1/10/14), ultimately disrupting mitosis. In vivo, CX6258.HCl enhanced 5-FU-mediated tumor suppression and showed no obvious histological injury in major organs under the tested conditions. Clinically, H3T3ph was increased in CRC tissues and positively associated with Ki67 expression, linking this mitotic histone mark to proliferative CRC regions. Together, these findings highlight the HASPIN/H3T3ph axis as a druggable therapeutic target in colorectal cancer.

Results

Organoid-based epigenetic drug screening platform for colorectal cancer

We established a patient-derived organoid (PDO) platform using freshly resected colorectal cancer (CRC) tissues to identify novel therapeutic candidates (Fig. 1A; Figure S1A). Screening a library of 100 epigenetic compounds revealed that 56% represented investigational agents without current clinical applications (Fig. 1B). The collection targeted diverse epigenetic regulators including: Kinases (AMPK, Aurora, Pim, PKC), Chromatin modifiers (DNA methyltransferase, HDACs, Sirtuins), Signaling pathways (HIF, JAK, PARP) (Fig. 1C). Compound efficacy was quantitatively assessed through organoid viability measurements (pre- versus post-treatment cell survival) and morphological analysis of growth inhibition (Fig. 1D).

Identification of CX6258.HCl as a novel CRC growth inhibitor

Through screening of six patient-derived CRC organoid lines, we identified Belinostat, Panobinostat, and CX6258.HCl as the top three compounds with the highest average inhibition rates (Fig. 1E). While Belinostat and Panobinostat have entered clinical trials, CX6258.HCl represents a previously unexplored therapeutic candidate. At 10 µM concentration, CX6258.HCl treatment significantly inhibited organoid growth and induced fragmented morphology, while DMSO-treated controls showed minimal effects (Fig. 1F). Notably, CX6258.HCl achieved 90% growth inhibition in 4/6 organoid lines and 50% inhibition in 1/6 organoid line (Fig. 1G; Figure S1B). Mechanistic characterization revealed half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 0.46 µM in HCT116 cells (Fig. 1H), and dose-dependent proliferation inhibition in colony formation assay and EdU incorporation assay (Fig. 1I, J; Fig. S1C-H).

CX6258.HCl induces mitotic arrest and apoptosis in CRC

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of CX6258.HCl in vivo, we established patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models by implanting CRC tissues into NCG mice. CX6258.HCl treatment (25 mg/kg) significantly reduced tumor growth compared to vehicle controls (Fig. 2A). RNA sequencing of PDX tumors revealed that CX6258.HCl treatment predominantly downregulated genes involved in nuclear division, microtubule binding, and motor activity pathways (Fig. 2B). Notably, kinesin family members (KIFC1, KIF10, and KIF14) were particularly suppressed (Fig. 2C). Based on the pathway analysis and existing literature, we identified HASPIN (rather than PIM1) as the likely molecular target (Figure S2A). This was confirmed by significant reduction in H3T3 phosphorylation (Fig. 2D; Fig. S2B), and concentration-dependent inhibition of mitosis in H2B-mCherry-labeled HCT116 cells (Fig. 2E, F; Figure S2C, D). Consistently, the independent HASPIN inhibitor CHR-6494.HCl also impaired mitotic progression in H2B-mCherry-labeled HCT116 cells. Live-cell imaging showed reduced cell division events, and quantification confirmed a dose-dependent decrease in mitosis rate (Figure S2E, F). While DMSO-treated controls maintained normal perinuclear microtubule bundles, CX6258.HCl treatment induced severe microtubule disorganization, manifesting as multibranched, disordered fibers and impaired spindle polarization during mitosis (Fig. 2G).

In addition, cell cycle and apoptosis analyses in CRC cell lines revealed that CX6258.HCl treatment induced profound G2/M phase arrest (increasing from 14.4% to 69.8%) while modestly decreasing G0/G1 populations, with no significant effect on S phase progression (Fig. 2H, I; Figure S3A). Complementary RNA-seq and GSEA demonstrated significant enrichment of apoptotic pathways in treated HCT116 cells (Fig. 2J), corroborated by flow cytometry showing a 3.6-fold increase in Annexin V-positive apoptotic cells (9.07% to 32.4%; Fig. 2K, L; Figure S3B, C). Mechanistically, CX6258.HCl triggered dose-dependent activation of executioner caspases-3/9 (Figure S3D) and elevated ROS levels (Figure S3E), collectively demonstrating that CX6258.HCl simultaneously arrests mitosis at G2/M and induces caspase-mediated apoptosis through suppression of H3T3 phosphorylation.

CX6258.HCl binds the D687 residue within HASPIN’s kinase domain

To investigate the CX6258.HCl-binding sites of HASPIN, we conducted simulated docking using AutoDock. Molecular docking simulations using CX6258.HCl (PubChem CID: 72201040) and the HASPIN crystal structure (PDB: 2WB8) revealed specific binding at the D687 residue within the kinase domain, forming π and van der Waals interactions with a binding energy of -11.5 kcal/mol (Fig. 3A, B). Given D687’s proximity to known functional residues (K659/K759) that regulate H3T3 phosphorylation, we hypothesized CX6258.HCl disrupts HASPIN-nucleosome interactions. To validate the functional importance of the predicted D687 binding site, we generated a HASPIN-D687A mutant and confirmed the mutation by sequencing before assessing its impact on CX6258.HCl-mediated H3T3ph suppression (Fig. 3C, D). Validation studies with a HASPIN-D687A mutant demonstrated that while wild-type transfectants showed CX6258.HCl-mediated reduction in H3T3ph (Fig. 3E) and mitotic inhibition (Fig. 3F, G), these effects were significantly attenuated in mutant cells. Similarly, in PDOs, CX6258.HCl selectively inhibited growth in wild-type but not D687A-overexpressing lines (Fig. 3H, I). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed CX6258.HCl treatment in wild-type cells caused prophase-specific H3T3ph depletion and KIF10 chromatin exclusion - effects absent in mutants (Fig. 3J, K). Consistent with RNA-seq data, wild-type transfectants showed CX6258.HCl-mediated suppression of kinesins (KIFC1/10/14) at both transcriptional and translational levels, while D687A mutation rescued this inhibition (Fig. 3L, M). These findings establish D687 as the critical binding site through which CX6258.HCl modulates the HASPIN/H3T3ph-KIF axis in CRC cells.

Depleting HASPIN in CRC cells suppresses mitotic progression

Next, we knocked down HASPIN using siRNAs (Fig. 4A). Live-cell fluorescence imaging of siHASPIN-treated cells showed that HASPIN depletion impaired mitotic progression (Fig. 4B, C). Flow cytometry analysis further revealed that HASPIN knockdown induced a G2/M phase arrest (Fig. 4D, E). RT-qPCR and immunoblotting analyses showed that HASPIN knockdown significantly reduced the expression of KIFC1, KIF10, and KIF14 (Fig. 4F, G). Additionally, immunoblotting and immunofluorescence assays demonstrated a decrease in H3T3 phosphorylation upon HASPIN depletion (Fig. 4A, H). Immunofluorescence imaging of H3T3ph and KIF10 revealed that HASPIN knockdown reduced H3T3 phosphorylation during prophase, accompanied by a decline in KIF10 levels. This led to aberrant spindle assembly and chromosomal misalignment. The loss of KIF10 disrupted proper chromosomal segregation, resulting in mitotic defects and ultimately preventing cells from completing division (Fig. 4H).

CX6258.HCl decreases H3K4me3 levels by upregulating KDM5B

To evaluate potential alterations in other histone modifications following CX6258.HCl treatment, we analyzed RNA-seq data from HCT116 cells. The results revealed a significant upregulation of KDM5B mRNA levels after CX6258.HCl treatment (Fig. 5A). RT-qPCR further showed that KDM5B pre-mRNA was upregulated in a dose-dependent manner, indicating that CX6258.HCl promotes KDM5B expression at the transcriptional level (Fig. 5B). Moreover, this induction was markedly attenuated in cells expressing the HASPIN-D687A mutant, suggesting that the effect depends on the intact CX6258.HCl-responsive residue in HASPIN (Fig. 5C). Immunoblotting confirmed increased KDM5B protein expression alongside reduced H3K4me3 levels (Fig. 5D, E, Figure S4A, B), suggesting that CX6258.HCl downregulates H3K4me3 by inducing KDM5B expression.

To explore the potential transcriptional basis of CX6258.HCl-induced KDM5B pre-mRNA upregulation, we analyzed RNA-seq data from CX6258.HCl-treated HCT116 cells. CX6258.HCl increased several cell-cycle-associated transcriptional regulators, including E2F2, MYBL2 and MYC, as well as RNA Pol II activation and elongation-related genes such as POLR2A, AFF4 and SUPT6H. These changes suggest that CX6258.HCl-induced G2/M arrest and abnormal mitosis are accompanied by adaptive transcriptional remodeling. By integrating predicted KDM5B promoter-binding transcription factors with CX6258.HCl-induced DEGs, we further identified POU5F1, HOXB2, KLF2, KLF7 and EN2 as candidate regulators, suggesting that this transcriptional response may contribute to KDM5B transcriptional induction (Figure S4C).

We next performed H3K4me3 ChIP-seq in CX6258.HCl-treated HCT116 cells, which showed a decreased genome-wide H3K4me3 enrichment (Fig. 5F). Consistent with the RNA-seq data, transcriptional downregulation of KIFC1, KIF10, and KIF14 was observed, with reduced H3K4me3 occupancy at their loci (Fig. 5G; Figure S4D, E). Immunoblotting further demonstrated diminished protein levels of KIFC1, KIF10, and KIF14 in HCT116 and HT29 cells after CX6258.HCl treatment (Fig. 5H; Figure S4F). CHR-6494.HCl treatment similarly increased KDM5B expression, reduced H3T3ph and H3K4me3 levels, and decreased KIFC1, KIF10 and KIF14 expression at both mRNA and protein levels (Figure S4G-I). These changes paralleled those induced by CX6258.HCl, further supporting the involvement of HASPIN/H3T3ph suppression in regulating the KDM5B-H3K4me3-KIF axis. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that CX6258.HCl reduced KIF10 and H3T3ph levels. Notably, KIF10 was largely excluded from chromatin, with only a minor fraction remaining chromatin-bound in treated cells. This disruption impaired spindle function, preventing proper chromosome segregation and mitotic completion (Fig. 5I).

To investigate the relationship between HASPIN and KDM5B, we knocked down HASPIN (Figure S4J) and analyzed KDM5B expression by RT-qPCR. HASPIN depletion upregulated KDM5B pre-mRNA and protein levels, while reducing H3K4me3 (Figure S4K-M), implying that HASPIN-mediated H3T3ph modulation may regulate KDM5B expression and H3K4me3 dynamics. These findings suggest crosstalk between H3T3ph and H3K4me3 pathways.

Crosstalk between H3K4me3 and H3T3ph regulates Kinesin family member expression

To further investigate the crosstalk between H3T3ph and H3K4me3, we performed ChIP-qPCR to assess the level of H3T3ph, KDM5B, and H3K4me3 at the promoters of KIFC1, KIF10 and KIF14. Following CX6258.HCl treatment, enrichment of both H3T3ph and H3K4me3 was reduced at these promoters (Figs. 6A, B). Conversely, the demethylase KDM5B was significantly more enriched (Fig. 6C). These findings suggest that H3T3ph regulates not only KDM5B expression but also its recruitment to kinesin promoters, thereby influencing H3K4me3 modification and gene expression. This model was supported by experiments in HASPIN-knockdown HCT116 cells, which showed similarly reduced promoter enrichment of H3T3ph and H3K4me3 (Figure S5A, B), and a concomitant increase in KDM5B binding (Figure S5C). Thus, the loss of H3T3ph following HASPIN knockdown promotes KDM5B recruitment, reduces H3K4me3 levels, and represses the transcription of KIFC1, KIF10 and KIF14. Collectively, our data demonstrate that H3T3ph and H3K4me3 are crucial regulators of kinesin gene expression and function cooperatively to ensure proper mitotic progression.

KDM5B contributes to CX6258.HCl-induced kinesin repression and mitotic impairment

To further determine whether KDM5B contributes to CX6258.HCl-induced mitotic defects, we knocked down KDM5B in CX6258.HCl-treated HCT116 cells. Live-cell imaging showed that KDM5B depletion partially restored the reduced mitosis rate caused by CX6258.HCl treatment (Fig. 6D, E). RT-qPCR and western blot analyses showed that KDM5B knockdown partially rescued CX6258.HCl-induced downregulation of KIFC1, KIF10 and KIF14, and attenuated the reduction of H3K4me3 (Fig. 6F, G).

We next performed kinesin overexpression experiments to assess whether restoration of kinesin expression could counteract KDM5B-induced mitotic defects. RT-qPCR and western blot analyses confirmed the overexpression of KDM5B, KIFC1 and KIF14 in HCT116 cells (Figure S5D, E). Live-cell imaging showed that KDM5B overexpression impaired mitotic progression, whereas co-expression of KIFC1 and KIF14 partially restored the mitosis rate in KDM5B-overexpressing cells (Figure S5F, G). Together, these results suggest that KDM5B induction contributes to CX6258.HCl-induced H3K4me3 reduction, kinesin repression and mitotic impairment.

Combined treatment with CX6258.HCl and 5-FU suppresses subcutaneous colorectal tumor growth

To evaluate the effects of CX6258.HCl on CRC tumor growth in vivo, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model by injecting HCT116 cells into nude mice. After one week, the mice were administered intraperitoneal injections of DMSO, CX6258.HCl (25 mg/kg), 5-FU (25 mg/kg), or a combination of CX6258.HCl and 5-FU (12.5 mg/kg each) for seven consecutive days (Fig. 7A). Tumor size was significantly reduced in CX6258.HCl and 5-FU monotherapy groups compared to DMSO control. The combination treatment group showed further tumor growth suppression compared with either monotherapy (Fig. 7B-D). Immunohistochemistry analysis revealed a reduction of Ki67 in the 5-FU group, and reductions of Ki67, H3T3ph and H3K4me3 in CX6258.HCl group. These indicators were most significantly reduced in the combination-treated tumors (Fig. 7E-G). Furthermore, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed an apparent reduction in tumor cell density after CX6258.HCl, 5-FU, or combination treatment, with the most evident decrease observed in the combination group (Fig. 7H). Together, these findings demonstrated that CX6258.HCl significantly inhibits tumor growth in vivo. Histological examination of major organs revealed no obvious tissue damage after treatment with either CX6258.HCl alone or in combination with 5-FU (Figure S6A). The anti-tumor efficacy of a higher dose of CX6258.HCl (50 mg/kg) was also evaluated and was found to suppress tumor growth significantly (Figure S6B-D). Body weight remained stable, and histological examination of major organs did not reveal obvious tissue damage under the tested conditions (Figure S6E-F). Consistent with the mechanism of action, immunohistochemical analysis confirmed significant inhibition of H3T3ph (Figure S6G), and H&E staining showed reduced tumor cell density after high-dose CX6258.HCl treatment (Figure S6H). Together, the results demonstrate that CX6258.HCl suppresses CRC xenograft growth in vivo and shows a favorable short-term safety profile, as supported by stable body weight and the absence of obvious histological injury in major organs under the tested conditions. These findings highlight the HASPIN/H3T3ph axis as a targetable mitotic pathway and support its therapeutic potential in colorectal cancer.

H3T3ph is elevated in CRC tissues and is associated with proliferative tumor regions

To investigate the potential role of H3T3ph in CRC progression, we collected 12 paired samples of human CRC tissue and adjacent normal mucosal tissue from West China Hospital, Sichuan University. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis revealed that H3T3ph expression was significantly elevated in CRC specimens compared to matched normal tissues (Fig. 8A, B). Furthermore, IHC demonstrated that H3T3ph expression was positively correlated with the proliferation marker Ki67 within tumor regions (Fig. 8C). This positive association suggests that H3T3ph is linked to proliferative CRC tumor regions.

Discussion

Patient-derived tumor organoids (PDTOs) provide a useful ex vivo platform for anticancer drug screening because they retain many histopathological features of the original tumors and may better reflect interpatient heterogeneity than traditional two-dimensional cell culture models [20, 21]. Using this platform, we screened a library of 100 epigenetic compounds across six CRC PDTOs and identified CX6258.HCl as a potent inhibitor of CRC organoid growth. Further validation in CRC cell lines, PDOs, PDX tumors, and xenograft models showed that CX6258.HCl suppresses CRC cell proliferation, disrupts mitotic progression, and inhibits tumor growth in vivo. These findings support the utility of PDO-based screening for identifying candidate epigenetic and mitotic vulnerabilities in CRC.

CX6258.HCl was initially developed as an orally active pan-PIM kinase inhibitor that suppresses PIM kinase-dependent phosphorylation events, including phosphorylation of Bad and 4E-BP1 [22]. A previous study by Melms et al. used kinase profiling and functional validation to show that CX-6258 binds not only PIM1–3 but also HASPIN and MYLK4, and that its anti-tumor activity in melanoma cells is more closely associated with HASPIN inhibition than with PIM inhibition [23]. In that study, depletion of PIM1, PIM2, or MYLK4 did not alter cellular sensitivity to CX6258, and CX6258 did not measurably reduce BAD phosphorylation. In contrast, CX6258 dose-dependently reduced HASPIN-dependent H3T3 phosphorylation, and HASPIN knockdown phenocopied CX6258-induced growth inhibition [23]. Consistent with this finding, our CRC data showed that CX6258.HCl significantly reduced HASPIN-mediated H3T3ph, whereas BAD-pS112 was not markedly changed under the tested conditions. HASPIN knockdown or treatment with CHR-6494.HCl, an independent HASPIN inhibitor, also reproduced key mitotic phenotypes, including H3T3ph reduction, kinesin downregulation, and impaired mitotic progression. In addition, the HASPIN-D687A mutant attenuated CX6258.HCl-induced H3T3ph loss and downstream mitotic defects. These results support the view that inhibition of the HASPIN/H3T3ph axis is a major functional mechanism underlying the anti-mitotic activity of CX6258.HCl.

HASPIN is a serine/threonine kinase that localizes to mitotic chromosomes and phosphorylates histone H3 at threonine 3 (H3T3ph). H3T3ph is required for centromeric recruitment of the CPC and proper chromosome alignment and segregation [14]. HASPIN is overexpressed in several tumor types, including pancreatic cancer [24], gallbladder cancer [25], esophageal cancer [26], bladder cancer [27], prostate cancer [28], and colorectal cancer [29]. Previous studies mainly linked HASPIN expression or inhibition to proliferation, apoptosis and mitotic defects. Our study extends these observations by connecting HASPIN/H3T3ph inhibition to an epigenetic-kinesin regulatory axis in CRC. Specifically, genetic depletion or pharmacological inhibition of HASPIN reduced H3T3ph, increased KDM5B expression, decreased H3K4me3 enrichment at kinesin gene promoters, and suppressed the expression of KIFC1, KIF10 and KIF14. Because these kinesin family members are involved in spindle organization, chromosome congression, and mitotic progression, their downregulation provides a plausible mechanism for the mitotic arrest and spindle abnormalities observed after CX6258.HCl treatment (Fig. 8D). Our rescue experiments further support the functional relevance of the proposed H3T3ph-KDM5B-H3K4me3-KIF axis.

The regulation of KDM5B after H3T3ph suppression is an important mechanistic issue. Our results showed that CX6258.HCl increased KDM5B pre-mRNA, suggesting regulation at the transcriptional level. This induction was attenuated by HASPIN-D687A, and HASPIN knockdown similarly increased KDM5B expression, supporting a link between HASPIN/H3T3ph inhibition and KDM5B upregulation. To further explore this connection, we analyzed transcriptional changes after CX6258.HCl treatment. Several cell-cycle-associated transcriptional regulators, including E2F2, MYBL2, and MYC [30–32], were increased, suggesting that CX6258.HCl-induced G2/M perturbation may trigger adaptive remodeling of cell-cycle transcriptional programs. In addition, genes associated with RNA polymerase II transcriptional activation and elongation, such as POLR2A, AFF4, and SUPT6H [33, 34], were also increased. These changes indicate that KDM5B induction may occur as part of a broader compensatory transcriptional response to mitotic stress. Promoter motif analysis of the KDM5B promoter further identified several candidate transcription factors, including POU5F1, HOXB2, KLF2, KLF7, and EN2 [35–37]. Among them, POU5F1 and HOXB2 were upregulated after CX6258.HCl treatment and may contribute to KDM5B transcriptional activation, whereas the downregulation of KLF2 and KLF7 may reflect reduced context-dependent inhibitory regulation. Although these analyses provide possible mechanistic clues, further ChIP-based validation will be required to determine whether these candidate transcription factors directly regulate KDM5B after HASPIN/H3T3ph inhibition.

Several HASPIN inhibitors have been developed, including CHR-6494, 5-ITu, LJ5242 [38–40]. However, the specificity of many HASPIN inhibitors remains imperfect. For instance, 5-ITu can also inhibit adenosine kinase, CDC-like kinases (CLKs), and dual-specific tyrosine phosphorylation-regulated kinases (Dyrks) [41]. In this study, molecular docking predicted that CX6258.HCl may interact with the D687 residue within the HASPIN kinase domain. Functional experiments using HASPIN-D687A showed that this mutation attenuated CX6258.HCl-induced H3T3ph loss and mitotic defects, supporting the functional relevance of this predicted interaction. Importantly, we do not interpret these data as definitive atomic-level proof of direct binding to D687. Instead, they suggest that the D687-containing region may contribute to the cellular response to CX6258.HCl. To further strengthen pharmacological validation, we used the independent HASPIN inhibitor CHR-6494.HCl. CHR-6494.HCl reproduced key effects of CX6258.HCl, including H3T3ph suppression, KDM5B upregulation, H3K4me3 reduction, kinesin downregulation, and impaired mitotic progression. These findings support the conclusion that inhibition of HASPIN/H3T3ph is sufficient to reproduce the major anti-mitotic phenotypes observed with CX6258.HCl.

As H3T3 is currently the only known substrate of HASPIN, its inhibition is anticipated to have a more favorable side effect profile compared with that of broader-spectrum anticancer agents [42], making it a promising therapeutic target. The therapeutic potential of HASPIN inhibition may be particularly relevant in combination regimens. Previous studies have shown that HASPIN inhibition cooperates with other mitotic regulators to enhance anti-tumor effects. For example, CHR-6494 combined with the Aurora A inhibitor MLN8237 reduced breast cancer cell viability and inhibited tumor growth in preclinical models [43], and related findings have also been reported in CRC models [19]. In our study, CX6258.HCl combined with 5-FU produced stronger tumor suppression than either monotherapy in vivo. This effect may reflect the complementary actions of the two agents. 5-FU mainly suppresses tumor growth by inhibiting thymidylate synthase and incorporating fluorinated metabolites into RNA and DNA, thereby disrupting nucleotide metabolism and DNA/RNA synthesis [44]. In contrast, HASPIN inhibition suppresses H3T3ph, disrupts mitotic chromosome regulation [45], increases KDM5B, reduces H3K4me3 enrichment at kinesin promoters, and downregulates mitosis-related kinesin genes. Thus, nucleotide metabolic stress induced by 5-FU and epigenetic-mitotic disruption induced by CX6258.HCl may jointly contribute to the enhanced tumor suppression observed in vivo. Future dose-matrix studies and formal combination-index analyses will be needed to determine whether this interaction represents formal pharmacological synergy.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although our results support HASPIN/H3T3ph inhibition as a major functional mechanism of CX6258.HCl in CRC cells, the complete target spectrum of CX6258.HCl in CRC remains to be determined. Future kinase profiling, chemical proteomics and additional genetic rescue experiments will be important to further clarify target specificity and potential off-target contributions. Second, although our data suggest that KDM5B upregulation may be linked to adaptive transcriptional remodeling after HASPIN/H3T3ph inhibition, the direct transcription factors responsible for KDM5B induction remain to be experimentally validated. Third, the in vivo safety evaluation was based on short-term body weight monitoring and histological examination of major organs. Although no obvious histological injury was observed under the tested conditions, more extensive pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic and toxicological studies are needed before considering translational development. Fourth, the in vivo combination study supports enhanced anti-tumor activity but does not establish formal drug synergy. Finally, although the increased expression of H3T3ph was further validated in a CRC tissue microarray, its association with patient prognosis remains to be determined in larger, independent, outcome-annotated CRC cohorts.

In conclusion, our findings support HASPIN/H3T3ph inhibition as a functionally relevant antimitotic strategy in CRC. CX6258.HCl disrupts a KDM5B-H3K4me3-kinesin regulatory axis, suppresses CRC xenograft growth, and enhances 5-FU-mediated tumor suppression in vivo. These results provide preclinical rationale for further evaluating HASPIN/H3T3ph-targeted therapeutic strategies in CRC, while future studies are needed to define compound selectivity, combination synergy, long-term safety and clinical biomarker potential.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

All cell lines were purchased from the Shanghai Cell Bank. HCT116, HT29, and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (HyClone, AG29629575) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (ExCell Bio, FSP500) and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime, C0222) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 5% CO2. All cell lines were confirmed using short tandem repeat (STR) analysis before use.

Patient-derived organoids culture and viability assay

Colorectal cancer tumor tissues were obtained from patients at the West China Hospital of Sichuan University. Tumor tissues were minced and digested with collagenase I (17018029, Gibco) and collagenase IV(17104019, Gibco) for 50 min, and the supernatant was collected by filtration through a 100 μm cell sieve, centrifuged at 400 g for 10 min, and the cell precipitate was collected. Organoids were maintained in Matrigel (356231, CORNING) in 384-well plates and treated with the indicated compounds (10 µM) for 3 days. The inhibition scores of each compound were calculated by normalizing to the control group. PDOs used for drug screening were established from freshly resected colorectal adenocarcinoma tissues from patients who had not received preoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy and had sufficient viable tumor tissue for organoid establishment. Six independent patient-derived PDO lines were included. Organoid identity and epithelial tumor features were confirmed by H&E staining and IHC staining for Ki67, CK20 and CK7.

Drug treatment

Unless otherwise indicated, HCT116 and HT29 cells were treated with 1.5 µM CX6258.HCl or an equivalent volume of DMSO. For cell-based endpoint assays, including cell-cycle analysis, apoptosis assay, ROS detection, immunofluorescence staining, microtubule staining and RT-qPCR analysis, cells were treated for 24 h. RNA-seq analysis was also performed after 24 h of treatment. Western blot, ChIP-seq and ChIP-qPCR assays were performed after 48 h of treatment. For organoid assays, organoids were treated with 10 µM CX6258.HCl for 72 h unless otherwise indicated. Experiments using other concentrations or treatment durations are specified in the corresponding figure legends. Unless otherwise indicated, CHR-6494.HCl was also used at 1.5 µM.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells per well and incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C.The cells were treated with CX6258.HCl (S7041, Selleck). After 1 week, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min, stained with crystal violet solution for 20 min, and colonies were counted.

EdU incorporation Assay

EdU was detected using the BeyoClick™ EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (C0071S, Beyotime) containing Alexa Fluor 488 fluorochrome. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3000 cells per well. After 48 h of drug treatment, cells were incubated with EdU solution (final concentration of 10 µM) for 2 h and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. The cells were washed with PBS and incubated with 0.5% Triton X100-PBS for 10 min to permeabilize cells. Click reaction solution was incubated for 30 min at room temperature and protected from light and then washed three times with PBS for 3 min each time. Finally, nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342. Images were acquired with a fluorescence microscope (Nikon).

Live cell tracking

H2B-mCherry-labeled HCT116 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 3,000 cells per well. 24 h later, the drug was added (siHASPIN HCT116 cells were seeded on 96-well plates 24 h after transfection) and placed into real-time dynamic live cell monitoring and function analysis system (Incucyte S3). Selected wells were photographed every 24 h, and the cell division rate was counted by the number of cell divisions, and the data were collected for a total of 48 h.

Microtubule staining

Cellular microtubules were visualized using the Microtubule Green Fluorescence Staining Kit (C2213S, Beyotime). Tubulin-Tracker Green staining working solution pre-warmed at 37 °C and co-incubated with cells at 37 °C for 60 min. Remove the Tubulin-Tracker Green staining solution and wash the cells 2 times with a dilution of Tubulin-Tracker Green containing a staining enhancer (1X). Finally, nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342 for 10 min. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Spin IxPloreI).

Cell cycle and Cell apoptosis assays

A total of 1 × 106 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate. After overnight attachment, cells were treated with indicated drugs for 24 h. Cell cycle and apoptosis were measured with Cell Cycle Analysis Kit (FXP0211, 4 A BIOTECH) and Annexin V-FITC/PI Kit (FXP018, 4 A BIOTECH), respectively according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Treated cells were analyzed using flow cytometry.

ROS assay

HCT116 and HT29 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate and treated with CX6258.HCl at a density of 80% per well. All the cells were harvested in DMEM containing DCFH-DA (10 µM, S0033, Beyotime Biotechnology). After incubation at 37 °C for 20 min, the cells were washed with PBS and subjected to flow cytometry (FACSCanto II System, BD).

RT-qPCR

RNA was prepared using Total RNA Kit I (R6834-01-E.Z.N.A. Omega Bio-tek) and cDNA PrimeScript™ IV 1st strand cDNA was prepared using Synthesis Mix (6215 A, Takara) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. PCR was performed using Bio-Rad CFX96™ (San Diego, Bio-rad) in the presence of SYBR (Q321, ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix). A melting-curve analysis was performed after the fluorescence values were collected. The sequences of primers for the RT-qPCR analysis are listed in Table S1. All the above primers were synthesized by Youkang Biotech (Chengdu, China).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

ChIP assay was performed according to the protocol published previously [46]. Briefly, cells were cross-linked with 1% PFA followed by quenching the reaction with 125 mM glycine. After being washed twice with PBS buffer, cell pellets were transferred to a microfuge tube. Cells were lysed with lysis buffer plus protease inhibitors and sonicated to about 500-bp fragments. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 10,800 rpm for 15 min and diluted 10-fold with ChIP dilution buffer plus protease inhibitors. The chromatin supernatant was incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. The protein G agarose beads were washed with ChIP dilution buffer for several times and incubated with the resulting supernatant for 3 h. The beads were sequentially washed for 5 min at 4 °C with low-salt buffer, high-salt buffer, LiCl buffer, and Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer. DNA fragments were enriched with 10% chelex (Bio-Rad). Immunoprecipitated DNA was analyzed by qPCR with specific primers for target genes (Table S1).

ChIP-seq analyses

ChIP-seq library was constructed using protocol published previously and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with 150 bp paired-end reads. The following antibodies were used: H3K4me3 (A22146, Abclonal); H3T3ph (ab78351, Abcam); KDM5B (15327 S, CST). Raw reads were trimmed for adaptors and low-quality reads were filtered out. Clean reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) by bowtie2 (2.4.2). Duplicate reads were marked and removed by MarkDuplicates implemented in GATK (4.1.9). Peaks were called using MACS2 with a q-value cutoff of 0.05. The bigWig format files were generated by bamCoverage implemented in deepTools (3.5.0) using parameters-binSize 25 –normalizeUsing BPM. Visualization of the average profile at TSS was performed with computeMatrix and plotHeatmap implemented in deeptools. IGV was used for visualizing the enrichment of histone modifications at specific genomic loci.

RNA-seq analyses

RNA-seq libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were trimmed to remove adaptors and low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome hg38 using STAR v2.7.9a. Gene-level read counts were generated using featureCounts v2.0.3. Three biological replicates were included for each group. Differentially expressed genes were identified using DESeq2 v1.30.1. Genes with |log2(fold change)| > 1 and adjusted p value < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed.

Hematoxylin-eosin staining (H&E) assay

Treated tissue or cell sections were stained with hematoxylin staining solution for 5 min, immersed in tap water and rinsed with excess staining solution for 5 min. According to the staining results and requirements, the sections were stained with eosin solution for 5 min. If direct observation is required, the samples can be washed twice with 70% ethanol. For long-term storage, the sections are subsequently dehydrated, cleared, and mounted.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis was performed as previously reported [47]. Briefly, the tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and the sections were embedded in paraffin. The sections were baked at 95 °C for 30 min, then rehydrated using a gradient ethanol series, and after antigen repair with EDTA antigen repair solution(ZLI-9071 ,ZSGB-BIO), 0.3% Triton X100 was added for 20 min, The sections were treated with endogenous peroxidase blocker for 10 min and rinsed three times with PBS. After blocking with 2% goat serum in PBS for 30 min, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies H3T3ph (ab78351, Abcam), H3K4me3 (A22146, Abclonal), and Ki67 (A20018, Abclonal) at 4 °C overnight. Excess primary antibody was washed away with PBS and then incubated for 15 min with the corresponding secondary antibody: biotinylated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ZB-2050, ZSGB-BIO) for 15 min followed by streptavidin-biotin peroxidase complex (ZB-2050, ZSGB-BIO) for 15 min. After development with nickel-enhanced 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB, ZLI-9017, ZSGB-BIO) and followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin for 5 min. The sections were dehydrated, mounted, sealed with neutral resin, and scanned using a scanner for visualization.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

For immunofluorescence assays, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well on a coverslip in 24-well plates. After transfection, the coverslips were fixed in cold PBS buffer and washed 3 times. Then, the coverslips were fixed with 4% formaldehyde, and the cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min and washed thoroughly with PBST buffer. Lastly, cells were blocked with goat serum for 40 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The cells were washed 5 times with PBST buffer and then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488–tagged or Alexa Fluor 594–tagged secondary antibodies. DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology) was used to stain DNA. Primary antibodies used for immunofluorescence analysis were as follows: KIF10 (ab5093, Abcam), H3T3ph (ab78351, Abcam).

Western blotting assay

Cells were lysed to prepare protein samples. Equal amounts of proteins were loaded onto 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gels for electrophoresis and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF, EMD Millipore, ISEQ00010, Kenilworth, NJ, USA) membranes. The membrane was then washed with TBST and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZB-2306, ZB-2305, ZSGB-BIO) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were then washed with TBST and incubated with Immobilon Western HRP substrate (WBKLS0500, EMD Millipore), and images were acquired using a ChemiScope 6000 Touch (Clinx, Shanghai, China). Primary antibodies used for immunoblot analysis were as follows: β-Tubulin (TA347064, ZSGB-BIO), GAPDH (TA-08 ZSGB-BIO), KIFC1 (A0077, Abclonal), KIF10 (A15263, Abclonal), KIF14 (A10275, Abclonal), H3T3ph (ab78351, Abcam); H3K4me3 (A22146, Abclonal); H3 (17168-1-AP, Proteintech); KDM5B (15327 S, CST); PIM1 (A9695, Abclonal); BAD (A19595, Abclonal); BAD S112ph (AP1010, Abclonal); Caspase3 (A11319, Abclonal); Cleaved Caspase3 (A27145, Abclonal); Caspase9 (Bs0049R, Bioss); Cleaved Caspase9 (9509, CST); MYC (AE070, Abclonal);

Tumor xenograft assay

All animal experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals at institutional guidelines and were approved by the local Animal Care and Use Committee. HCT116 cells were implanted into the right dorsum of 5-week-old nude mice at 1 × 107 cells/ mouse, and tumors were monitored once a day using digital calipers. Mice were treated with intraperitoneal injections of DMSO or 25 mg/kg of CX6258.HCl or 25 mg/kg of 5-FU or 12.5 mg/kg CX6258.HCl + 12.5 mg/kg 5-FU for 7 consecutive days. All mice were housed and provided water and food. Tumor volume (mm3) was calculated according to the following formula: volume = 0.5 × length × width2. Mice were randomly divided with no blinding according to different experimental requirements.

Human tumor samples and PDX model generation

All patients underwent pathologically confirmed diagnosis of colorectal cancer and resection surgery at West China Hospital of Sichuan University. Tumor tissues were collected immediately after surgery, minced, injected into NCG mice (male, 6 to 8 weeks old) by subcutaneous injection. One week later, when the tumors were palpable, the mice were treated with intraperitoneal injections of DMSO or 25 mg/kg of CX6258.HCl once daily for 7 consecutive days. Tumor size was measured once a day using digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula volume = 0.5 × length × width2.

Tissue microarray and immunohistochemical evaluation

The tissue microarray was purchased from Shanghai Zhuoli Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Zhuoli Biotechnology Co, Shanghai, China; ZL-ColRecA96). H3T3ph expression in the CRC tissue microarray was evaluated by immunohistochemical staining. The immunohistochemical staining score was calculated based on staining intensity and the proportion of positive cells. Staining intensity was classified into four categories: 0, no staining; 1, weak staining; 2, moderate staining; and 3, strong staining. The proportion of positive cells was scored as follows: 0, 0%-5%; 1, 6%-25%; 2, 26%-50%; and 3, > 50%. The final IHC score for each tissue core was calculated by multiplying the staining intensity score by the positive proportion score.

Statistical analysis

All data were obtained from at least three independent experiments, unless otherwise stated. All statistical analyses and graphics were performed using GraphPad software (v10.0). Data are presented as mean ± SD. For comparisons between two unpaired groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. For paired comparisons between CRC tissues and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues, paired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used. Correlation analysis between H3T3ph and Ki67 staining was performed using Pearson correlation analysis. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.
