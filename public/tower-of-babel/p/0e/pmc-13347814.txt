Secondary peripheral T-cell lymphoma in a patient with DLBCL harboring BLM mutation following CD19/CD22 bispecific CAR-T cell therapy
Authors: Xindi Liu, Jia Cong, Xiayu Xia, Henan Wang, Xinjian Yu, Qinlong Zheng, Liang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe describe a case of secondary peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) arising from infused CD19/CD22-4-1BB-CD3ζ-lenti bispecific chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells in a patient with relapsed or refractory B-cell lymphoma who carried a pathogenic germline BLM p.L107Ffs*36 variant. Integrated genomic and molecular analyses confirmed that the PTCL was clonally derived from the infused CAR-T product and revealed a multistep oncogenic process involving pre-existing driver mutations (BLM), somatic TET2 mutations (p.R1261H and p.E1755*), and genomic instability. Clonal CAR vector integration events in cancer-associated genes (NF1, CBX5, RNF213, etc) were identified as markers of clonal expansion, although no functional evidence supports a direct oncogenic role. We acknowledge that this case lies on a spectrum between clonal lymphoproliferative disorder and overt PTCL, and terminology continues to evolve. This case suggests a need for re-biopsy of suspected relapses after CAR-T therapy and highlights the limitations of conventional product assessment in detecting premalignant clones.Keywords: Chimeric antigen receptor - CAR, Lymphoma, Secondary malignancy

Background

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has transformed the treatment landscape for patients with relapsed or refractory B-cell malignancies, achieving high rates of durable remission. However, rare but serious late complications have emerged, notably secondary hematologic malignancies. In November 2023, the US Food and Drug Administration issued a safety communication reporting 22 cases of T-cell malignancies among CAR-T cell recipients, prompting heightened scrutiny of secondary T-cell lymphomas as a potential adverse event.1 Concurrently, several case reports have described peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) following CD19-targeted CAR-T therapy.2,4 Here, we describe a patient who developed PTCL 9 months after autologous CD19/CD22 bispecific CAR-T cell infusion and delineate the molecular features of this CAR-T-derived secondary PTCL relative to de novo PTCL.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old woman with a history of bilateral breast cancer (2016) and cervical cancer (2021) presented in March 2023 with a right cervical mass. Positron emission tomography-CT (PET-CT) demonstrated hypermetabolic lymphadenopathy. Histopathologic examination revealed diffuse infiltration by large atypical lymphocytes expressing CD20, MUM1, and focal CD3 positivity and negativity for CD10 and BCL6, consistent with non-germinal center B-cell-type diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). Bone marrow biopsy showed no involvement.

The patient received two cycles of R-CHOP chemotherapy (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) starting in March 2023, achieving a partial response (PR) of the cervical mass. She subsequently declined further treatment due to adverse reactions and personal reasons. In April 2024, she developed pulmonary symptoms including dyspnea and cough. Chest CT revealed a pulmonary space-occupying lesion, and lung tissue biopsy confirmed DLBCL infiltration. Accordingly, she received six additional cycles of R-CHOP beginning in May 2024, with imaging demonstrating a PR. In October 2024, she presented with left-sided craniofacial sensory deficits. Brain MRI revealed multiple intracranial space-occupying lesions, and cerebellar biopsy confirmed central nervous system relapse of activated B-cell-like DLBCL.

Given the refractory disease course, the patient underwent leukapheresis in November 2024 and received oral zanubrutinib as bridging therapy. A preinfusion PET-CT scan performed before CAR T-cell therapy showed no evidence of metabolically active disease. Lymphodepleting conditioning with fludarabine (25 mg/m2/day) and cyclophosphamide (250 mg/m2/day) was administered from days −5 to −3 relative to CAR T-cell infusion, followed by infusion of autologous CD19/CD22-directed CAR T cells (2×106/kg) on day 0.5 The vector structure and transduction process are detailed in the online supplemental materials and online supplemental figure S1. Brain MRI 1 month after CAR-T cell infusion showed no residual intracranial disease. The patient subsequently completed four cycles of PD-1 monoclonal antibody therapy and continued maintenance zanubrutinib for 1 year (October 2024 to October 2025).

In July 2025, a lower lip mass was noted during routine follow-up. Biopsy revealed diffuse infiltration by atypical lymphocytes positive for CD2, CD3, CD4, CD5, and CD8, and negative for CD7, CD20, CD21, PAX5, ALK, and EBER, leading to a diagnosis of PTCL, not otherwise specified (PTCL-NOS) (figure 1A). Notably, the malignant cells exhibited a CD4+/CD8+ double-positive immunophenotype, which is unusual for PTCL-NOS. This double-positive status may suggest a thymic or early T-cell precursor origin, representing a distinctive feature of this CAR-T-derived secondary lymphoma. Post-resection PET-CT showed only mild metabolic activity in the mandibular soft tissues (figure 1B). Given the absence of systemic symptoms and limited disease, active surveillance was elected. The patient remains alive without PTCL-directed therapy as of this report.

Genetic analyses were conducted on the PTCL tissue to characterize the underlying genetics and confirm the malignancy. T-cell receptor (TCR) clonality assessment of the PTCL tissue was initially performed, and four clonal rearrangements including TRBV12-3*01/TRBD1*01/TRBJ1-1*01, TRBD1*01/TRBJ2-7*01, TRGV3*01/TRGJ1*01 and TRGV8*01/TRGJ1*01 were identified. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) analysis further revealed pathogenic chromosomal aberrations like del(11)(q23.2q25) with a cell fraction of about 58.0% and loss of chromosome X with a cell fraction of about 72.3%, somatic mutations of TET2 p.R1261H with variant allele frequency (VAF) of 45.5% and p.E1755* with a VAF of 52.8%, and several CAR vector integration sites within the CBX5, NF1, RNF213, PPP2R5D, ARHGAP45, and DACH2 gene intronic regions, which served as clonal markers (figure 2A). Moreover, the analysis also detected a heterozygous germline mutation of BLM p.L107Ffs*36 (frameshift insertion), which was further confirmed by Sanger sequencing of the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) sample. The mutation was classified as Pathogenic/Likely pathogenic according to the ClinVar database (Variation ID: 405281) with a genomeAD frequency of 1.3E-5, and has been reported as a disease-causing mutation in both Chinese and Japanese patients with breast cancer or hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome.6 7

To track the cellular origin, we performed comparative analyses on the preinfusion CAR-T product and concurrent PBMCs sample, separately. Flow cytometry, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), and WGS were performed on the CAR-T product, while ultra-high-depth sequencing of TET2 mutations was performed on both the CAR-T product and PBMCs samples. While no overt malignant population was detected in the CAR-T product by flow cytometry and scRNA-seq (figure 2B), identical TCR rearrangements—specifically TRBD1*01/TRBJ2-7*01, TRGV3*01/TRGJ1*01 and TRGV8*01/TRGJ1*01—were identified by WGS or scRNA-seq (figure 2C), confirming the clonal relationship. The detailed information of integration sites was illustrated in online supplemental table S1 for the PTCL tissue, and S2 for the CAR-T product. However, the number of cells carrying these premalignant TCR clonotypes was extremely limited in the scRNA-seq dataset, which precluded robust differential gene expression analysis and transcriptomic characterization of the precursor clone. This aspect will be further explored in future studies with larger sample sizes. TET2 mutations with much lower VAF were also present in both the CAR-T product (TET2 p.R1261H: 1.15% and TET2 p.E1755*: 0.20%) and PBMCs (TET2 p.R1261H: 2.16% and TET2 p.E1755*: 0.56%) samples’ ultra-high-depth sequencing data, indicating that these mutations were likely present in a small subset of T cells prior to CAR-T cell manufacturing and expansion after the infusion (figure 2D). Notably, allele frequencies of TET2 mutations and the shared TCR clones were markedly higher in the PTCL tissue than in the infused product, reflecting clonal expansion and evolution. Additionally, much more sporadic CAR vector integration events were discovered in the CAR-T product, while fewer but clonal ones with higher frequency (eg, CBX5, RNF213, and NF1 intronic integration) were discovered in the PTCL tissue (figure 2A and E).

To further confirm the expression of CAR-derived tEGFR protein in the PTCL cells, immunohistochemistry was performed on the same PTCL tissue using antibodies against tEGFR and CD2. Localization of tEGFR and CD2 was observed, providing direct protein-level evidence that the malignant T cells originated from the infused CAR-T product (figure 1C–E).

Discussion

This case provides multimodal evidence that CD19/CD22 bispecific CAR-T cells can transform into secondary PTCL. Clonal identity was confirmed through shared TCR rearrangements and TET2 mutations in both the therapeutic product and the resulting lymphoma. We describe a multistep oncogenic process driven by recurrent PTCL-associated aberrations, including somatic TET2 loss-of-function mutations, and augmented by patient-specific factors, including a germline BLM mutation. These findings are contextualized within the emerging literature on post-CAR-T lymphoproliferative disorders (LPDs) and the molecular biology of PTCL.

TET2 is a tumor suppressor, and its loss promotes clonal expansion of CAR-T cells.8 TET2 loss-of-function mutations are common in PTCL and promote clonal expansion.9,11 In T cells, pre-existing TET2 mutations confer a growth advantage that CAR-T manufacturing may amplify.3 12 13 In our patient, low-VAF TET2 mutations in the preinfusion product clonally expanded in the PTCL tissue, consistent with reports linking TET2-mutant clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) to post-CAR-T T-cell malignancies.14

Distinguishing this lesion from benign post-CAR-T LPDs is critical. The patient had oligoclonal TCR rearrangements, an indolent course, and atypical biology—features shared with recently described TET2-mutant LPDs after CAR-T therapy.15 However, several findings favor a diagnosis of PTCL. Pre-existing TET2 mutations, a germline BLM variant, CAR vector integration, and genomic instability (including del(11)(q23.2q25) and X loss) all drove expansion of multiple modified clones with partial oncogenic potential. The result was an oligoclonal proliferation with features between clonal LPD and overt PTCL. Its precise classification remains uncertain, and the terminology is still evolving.16

The patient’s germline BLM mutation likely contributed. BLM encodes a RecQ helicase required for DNA repair, and its loss promotes chromosomal breaks.17 18 The specific variant p.L107Ffs*36 produces a truncated protein lacking functional domains, leading to loss of function. During CAR-T expansion, this defect likely drove secondary hits, including the 11q deletion and X loss. Unlike typical de novo PTCL, which often has TET2 and ATM deletions,19 20 our patient carried TET2 mutations but no ATM deletion. Other alterations (NF1 intronic integration, X loss, CBX5 or RNF213 integrations) are not typical of de novo PTCL. Also, CAR vector events are specific to gene therapy.

No functional evidence confirms that NF1, CBX5, or RNF213 integrations cause gene inactivation. Unlike a prior report of TP53 intronic integration with functional loss in a BCMA CAR-T lymphoma,21 our case lacks such evidence. CAR vector integration site enrichment is most likely a passenger event from TET2-driven clone expansion, not a driver of transformation. Integration is a clonal marker, with no functional evidence of a direct oncogenic role. Similarly, X loss22 23 is not a PTCL hallmark and likely reflects BLM-defect instability.

After CAR-T therapy, the patient received zanubrutinib (no direct T-cell effects)24,26 and a PD-1 inhibitor. PD-1 limits T-cell expansion, and blocking it enhances proliferation.27 28 In our patient, PD-1 blockade was administered after TET2-mutant clones were already present. It may have promoted their outgrowth but was not the initiating event.

A notable finding is that conventional testing missed the malignant precursor clone, which was identifiable only retrospectively by deep sequencing of TET2 and TCR clonotype matching. This suggests standard assays may not detect low-abundance CHIP-mutant clones (TET2, DNMT3A). Given CHIP as a risk factor for post-CAR-T T-cell malignancies, deep sequencing of final products may be worth exploring.20 However, this recommendation stems from a single case, and prospective studies are needed.

Secondary T-cell malignancies after CAR-T therapy remain rare. Most are PTCL-NOS occurring within 2 years. Notably, these often involve skin and mucosal tissues. Our patient had an isolated mass of the lower lip. A prior tisa-cel case presented as an isolated oral ulcer.29 And a BCMA CAR-T PTCL study found skin involvement common.30 This may serve as a reminder that even in complete remission, new skin nodules, ulcers, or oral lesions should prompt biopsy and CAR gene testing.

Conclusions

We report a secondary PTCL originating from CD19/CD22 bispecific CAR-T cells in a DLBCL patient carrying a pathogenic BLM germline mutation. Pre-existing drivers (TET2, germline BLM) and genomic instability are key oncogenic risks. CAR vector integration is a passenger event, not a proven driver. PD-1 inhibition may be permissive. Enhanced screening (cancer susceptibility genes, deep sequencing of CAR-T products) and long-term monitoring.
