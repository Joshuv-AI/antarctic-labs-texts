Assessing the impact of farmers’ willingness to pay on different cultivated land attribute levels in China: an empirical analysis using the choice experiment method
Authors: Hao Li, Mi Xu, Wei-Yew Chang, Linhan Feng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractExploring farmers’ willingness to pay (WTP) for cultivated land protection and its influencing factors is key to promoting their enthusiasm for participating in cultivated land pollution governance. However, existing studies often overlook the differences in farmers’ preference for different levels of cultivated land attributes and the issue of intertemporal choice in farmers’ WTP, thus lowering the effectiveness of cultivated land protection policies. Using survey data from 646 small farmers in Shandong, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang provinces of China, this study empirically analyzes the impact of time preference on farmers’ WTP for cultivated land attribute levels by utilizing the choice experiment method, measures the relative importance of different cultivated land attributes and attribute levels to farmers, and calculates farmers’ WTP for different cultivated land attribute levels. The results revealed that farmers are generally reluctant to pay for cultivated land protection. Time preference affects farmers’ WTP for different levels of cultivated land attributes, with future-biased preference increasing farmers’ WTP and present-biased preference inhibiting it. Furthermore, for farmers, the relative importance of cultivated land attributes is in the order of cultivated land quality, cultivated land surrounding landscape and ecological environment, cultivated land social security function, and cultivated land area. The most crucial cultivated land attribute level is the improved cultivated land quality, with an average WTP of 3290.625 CNY per hectare (approximately 459.117 USD). In contrast, the least important cultivated land attribute level is the unchanged cultivated land area, with an average WTP of 1001.250 CNY per hectare (approximately 139.697 USD). The economic benefits of cultivated land protection in the sampled provinces can reach 353.892 million CNY per year (approximately 49.376 million USD) annually, which is calculated based on vegetable planting area. Our results suggest that the government should diversify farmers’ sources of income while increasing the amount of income, thereby reducing barriers to farmers’ payment. Simultaneously, farmers’ time preferences should be considered to guide them toward expecting long-term benefits and encouraging their active participation in cultivated land pollution governance. Moreover, cultivated land protection policies should match the preference of farmers for cultivated land attributes and attribute levels, and a limited fiscal fund should be allocated to the cultivated land attributes (i.e., cultivated land quality) and attribute levels (i.e., improved cultivated land quality) preferred by farmers to improve policy efficiency. This study highlights the importance of encouraging farmers to actively participate in cultivated land pollution governance and improving the allocation efficiency of the government’s cultivated land protection fund.Keywords: Farmers’ willingness to pay, Time preference, Cultivated land attributes and attribute levels, Choice experiment methodSubject terms: Agroecology, Environmental economics, Environmental impact, Environmental impact, Sustainability

Introduction

Cultivated lands are foundational to human survival, playing a crucial role in sustaining livelihoods, maintaining social stability, and preserving ecological balance1. As the primary productive land type, cultivated lands not only provide essential food for humans but also play a vital role in ecological regulation, such as nurturing water sources, promoting nutrient cycling, and maintaining biodiversity2. However, population expansion and urbanization have resulted in cultivated land gradually becoming a focal point for the conflict between the natural environment and economic development3. Additionally, the problem of cultivated land pollution is worsening4. Simultaneously, the irrational use of cultivated land and excessive emphasis by farmers on agricultural production have further degraded the ecological environments of cultivated land5. According to data from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, soil erosion has become the main factor behind the ecological environment deterioration of cultivated land globally. The proportion of soil eroded areas in cultivated lands is as high as 31%6, and how to sustainably use cultivated land has become a critical problem7. To this end, governments around the world have introduced a series of cultivated land protection policies, such as the European Union’s Common Agricultural Policy, which encourages farmers to change their agricultural production methods through numerous financial subsidies, thus fostering the sustainable development of cultivated land8. The United States has implemented a fallow conservation program to promote the efficient and sustainable use of cultivated land resources through agricultural subsidies and technical support9. In the Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Soil Pollution, the Chinese government proposes protecting and improving cultivated land quality by promoting environmentally friendly agricultural production technologies. However, this series of policy arrangements has not yielded the expected results10,11.

Some studies posit that the cultivated land protection policy has not achieved the expected effect because policy formulation only focuses on the leading role played by the government and ignores the importance of farmers’ independent participation in their WTP12. Additionally, the limited effect of the policy has been attributed to the excessively high payment standard of the cultivated land protection policy, which excludes low-income farmers due to financial constraints under the premise that farmers are willing to pay, resulting in a decrease in participation in cultivated land protection, thus lowering the effectiveness of the policy13. Farmers, as the main body of agricultural operation, are the most direct and important participants in cultivated land protection, and they are not only the polluters of cultivated land but also the beneficiaries of its protection5. Whether the cultivated land protection policy can be maximized depends to a large extent on the participation14 and willingness of farmers to pay15. Therefore, exploring farmers’ WTP for cultivated land protection is a crucial consideration in the formulation of cultivated land protection policies16.

Researchers have conducted extensive empirical studies on farmers’ WTP for cultivated land protection, gradually forming an analytical paradigm that focuses on exploring farmers’ WTP and its influencing factors. Trenholm et al.17 studied farmers’ WTP for cultivated land protection in two watersheds in southern Ontario, Canada, and found that only 18.28% of farmers were willing to pay. Moreover, only 37.59% of farmers were willing to pay for returning straw in southwest China18. However, a study on farmers’ WTP for land-use attributes in northwestern Ethiopia found that 93.20% of farmers were willing to pay for cultivated land protection19. Additionally, Geussens et al.20 revealed that farmers in the Elgon region of Uganda had a high WTP for cultivated land protection. Regarding the influencing factors of farmers’ WTP for cultivated land protection, some studies have revealed that male farmers with higher income have a higher WTP21, as have farmers with a higher level of education22, family income23, and awareness of the harm caused by the environmental degradation of cultivated land24. However, other scholars argue that gender25 and family income18 do not affect farmers’ WTP. Additionally, studies indicate that whether farmers are willing to pay for cultivated land protection depends on their perception of the value of cultivated land governance, with greater perceived value leading to a higher WTP26. Existing studies provide a strong foundation for promoting farmers’ participation in cultivated land pollution governance and offer a reference for the application of the “polluter pays” principle at the farmers’ level. However, differences among various study results have led to a policy-making dilemma, revealing scope for further explorations of farmers’ WTP for cultivated land protection.

Studies on farmers’ WTP for cultivated land protection typically treat cultivated land holistically. However, according to Lancaster’s theory of value characteristics27, the same item has different attribute characteristics, and individuals have different preferences for different attribute characteristics. This suggests that the relative importance of different cultivated land attributes and attribute levels differ among farmers. Exploring farmers’ WTP for different levels of cultivated land attributes and analyzing the relative importance of different cultivated land attributes and attribute levels to farmers can help maximize the effectiveness and value of fiscal expenditures on cultivated land protection. This is particularly important for developing countries with limited fiscal revenue and a large population of small farmers28. Moreover, unlike general commodities, the effectiveness of cultivated land pollution governance has marked lag characteristics, which makes farmers’ payments for cultivated land protection uncertain in the short term. This payment has conspicuous intertemporal choice characteristics, and ignoring this characteristic will result in an inaccurate representation of the logic behind farmers’ payments for cultivated land protection.

The aforementioned issues may be primarily attributable to the differences in the results in the literature. Therefore, based on survey data from 646 small farmers in Shandong, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang provinces in China, we utilized the choice experiment method to explore farmers’ WTP for different levels of cultivated land attributes by introducing time preference. Our marginal contributions are as follows: (1) Exploring the impact of time preference on farmers’ WTP for cultivated land attributes can broaden the theoretical perspective of cultivated land protection, thus elucidating the intertemporal choice characteristics of farmers’ WTP for cultivated land protection. (2) At the policy level, we assess farmer’s WTP for different cultivated land attribute and attribute levels, to some extent, to reflect farmers’ preferences of different cultivated land attribute and attribute levels, thereby identifying the relative importance of different cultivated land attributes and attribute levels to farmers. This can provide precise focus points for formulating government policies for cultivated land protection, thereby maximizing the efficiency of utilizing fiscal funds. (3) At the empirical level, we calculate farmers’ WTP for different levels of cultivated land attributes, providing a realistic basis for related studies on farmers’ WTP for cultivated land protection and the formulation of cultivated land ecological compensation policies. Our study results provide a novel and realistic perspective for studying farmers’ cultivated land protection while also offering a feasible solution for the participation of small farmers in cultivated land pollution governance in developing countries with limited income and large populations.

To meet the objectives of this study, we discuss the relationship between time preference and farmers’ WTP for different levels of cultivated land attributes in Sect. 2. We present the study design, including the principles of the choice experiment method; determination basis for cultivated land attributes, cultivated land attribute levels, and the choice set; setting of the econometric model; selection of the study area; and choice of data sources in Section "Methods and materials". The empirical analysis is presented in Section "Empirical results". We also provide the experimental economics measurement plan for farmers’ time preferences. In Section "Discussion", we discuss farmers’ overall WTP, the impact of time preference on farmers’ WTP for different levels of cultivated land attributes, the relative importance of different cultivated land attributes and attribute levels to farmers, and the payment amount for farmers’ cultivated land attribute levels. Finally, we present the conclusions and policy implications in Sect. 6.

Time preference and farmers’ WTP for cultivated land attribute levels

Time preference refers to an individual’s inclination toward either short-term or long-term benefits, reflecting the psychological characteristics of how behavioral subjects make intertemporal choices over time-varying outcomes29,30. It is generally divided into present-biased preference, time-neutral, and future-biased preferences. Time preference plays a crucial role in individual intertemporal choices, and individuals exhibit different preferences for time selection. Future-biased preference makes individuals more patient in anticipating long-term benefits, resulting in lower discount rates. In contrast, present-biased preference inclines individuals toward short-term benefits, resulting in higher discount rates31.

The governance of cultivated land pollution often involves a time lag, requiring present time or money investments in cultivated land for expected future benefits32. This process may take several months or even years, giving rise to the intertemporal choice characteristics of farmers’ WTP for cultivated land protection33. Faced with intertemporal decisions involving long- and short-term benefits, farmers need to balance present investments and future benefits34. Farmers with present-biased preferences typically have low patience levels, becoming increasingly impatient with the extension of time between investments and benefits35. In the context of cultivated land protection with delayed benefits, the focus is on the immediate benefits for farmers with present-biased preference, who are less willing to pay for cultivated land attribute levels. In contrast, farmers with future-biased preferences are more patient36, tending to invest presently for future cultivated land protection benefits. This results in farmers with future-biased preferences having a higher WTP for cultivated land attribute levels. Therefore, we hypothesize the following:

Hypothesis:

The more farmers’ time preferences lean toward the long term, the higher their WTP for cultivated land attribute levels.

Methods and materials

Choice experiment method

The choice experiment (CE) method utilizes a nonmarket valuation approach and facilitates the measurement of individual preference and the estimation of WTP37. This method assesses individual preference by constructing hypothetical scenarios, typically involving two alternative options, with each comprising multiple attributes and attribute levels. Respondents were tasked with selecting their preferred option, with each alternative including a monetary attribute for gauging WTP38. Consistent with this experimental design, our study, which investigated farmers’ WTP for varying levels of cultivated land attributes, was structured with two alternative options. To enhance the authenticity of the choice experiment results, a “status quo” option was included. This option allows respondents to abstain from a forced choice between two alternative options if they are dissatisfied with both39. Two alternative options and a “status quo” option constitute a choice set, and farmers make decisions across multiple choice sets, revealing their preference and allowing the WTP to be calculated based on their choices.

Determination of attributes and attribute levels

Identifying meaningful and pertinent attributes for surveyed farmers and defining the levels of these attributes is pivotal for evaluating farmers’ WTP for cultivated land protection40. We utilized a literature review, focus group discussions (FGDs), and a presurvey to determine cultivated land attributes and attribute levels. Initially, we summarized the important cultivated land attributes mentioned in most of the studies through a literature review. Subsequently, through semi-structured interviews in FGDs, we engaged with local village leaders, agricultural technicians, and villagers to discuss and derive the essential attributes of cultivated land. Finally, based on the results from the literature review and FGDs, we initially designed the choice experiment and conducted a pre-survey. Afterward, we adjusted the choice experiment design based on the pre-survey results. Ultimately, we determined four cultivated land attributes, namely cultivated land area, cultivated land quality, cultivated land social security function, and cultivated land surrounding landscape and ecological environment, with the payment amount as a monetary attribute (Table 1).

By determining the cultivated land attributes, we set different levels of cultivated land attributes based on the following considerations.

(1) Cultivated land area

Since the late 1980s, China’s economic development has undergone structural changes, with increased opportunities for off-farm work in cities and rising wages encouraging some farmers to exit agricultural production41. Simultaneously, urbanization and industrialization have accelerated, leading to the conversion of cultivated land for nonagricultural uses, such as industrial, residential, and infrastructural42, resulting in a continual decrease in cultivated land area. According to the annual report by the Chinese Ministry of Land and Resources, China’s cultivated land area decreased by a total of 393,700 hectares from 2009 to 2018. China has implemented stringent land-use control policies to slow the decline in cultivated land area. Therefore, we determined the levels of this attribute as “decrease” and “unchanged.”

(2) Cultivated land quality

Unreasonable application of pesticides and fertilizers has led to severe cultivated land pollution, which is a significant cause of deteriorating cultivated land quality43,44. According to the “2019 National Cultivated Land Quality Grade Report” by the Chinese Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs, the excessive use of pesticides and fertilizers has degraded more than 40% of the total cultivated land area. To this end, the Chinese government has formulated a series of policies to benefit farmers, such as subsidies for the rational use of pesticides and fertilizers to encourage farmers to engage in green production to maintain, restore, and improve the quality of cultivated land45. Therefore, we categorized the levels of this attribute as “deteriorate,” “unchanged,” and “improved.”

(3) Cultivated land social security function

For small farmers, cultivated land serves both as a means of production and a source of social security, ensuring basic livelihoods for rural farmers46. However, due to land fragmentation, non-agricultural income exceeding agricultural income, and changes in rural labor structure, among other factors, some cultivated lands have been abandoned, limiting and weakening the social security function of cultivated land. Thus, enhancing this function has great practical significance for social stability and economic development47. Therefore, we determined the levels of this attribute as “weaken,” “unchanged,” and “enhanced.”

(4) Cultivated land surrounding landscape and ecological environment

The conversion of cultivated land utilization forms has led to improvements or deterioration in the landscape and ecological environment of cultivated land48. Activities such as reclamation and intensive farming have resulted in the degradation of China’s cultivated land ecosystems49. To address this, the Ministry of Finance of the People’s Republic of China has set up a transfer payment for national key ecological function zones to protect and improve the ecological environment of cultivated land. Therefore, we set the levels of this attribute as “deteriorate,” “unchanged,” and “improved.”

(5) Payment amount

Based on FGDs and pre-survey, we finalized the monetary attribute levels for this study as 0 CNY, 50 CNY, 100 CNY, 150 CNY, and 200 CNY per mu per year. These levels were chosen to measure farmers’ WTP for different levels of cultivated land attributes. Among them, the payment level of “0 CNY per mu per year” serves as a critical reference point in the experimental design, indicating that farmers are unwilling to pay for any combination of cultivated land attribute levels. The inclusion of this monetary attribute level allows respondents not to have to choose a positive amount, thereby enhancing the authenticity of the experimental results and facilitating a more comprehensive understanding of farmers’ WTP for different levels of cultivated land attributes50.

Determination of choice sets

This study utilized the method of Gunasekara et al.51 to determine the cultivated land attributes and attribute levels, which involves using the factor design method to obtain the selection set formed by the combination of different attribute levels. As listed in Table 1, the study considered five attributes (including the monetary attribute) with 2, 3, 3, 3, and 5 levels, resulting in 2 × 3^3 × 5 = 270 possible combinations and yielding (270 × 269) / 2 = 36,315 choice sets. To avoid the decrease in data quality due to surveyed farmers being fatigued by excessive choice sets, this study refers to the processing method of Owuor et al.52, adopts the factor orthogonal design, excluding some unrealistic options, and only retains the orthogonal items. Eventually, 14 choice sets were determined as the combinations for surveyed farmers to choose from, with each set comprising two alternative options (option A and option B) and a “status quo” option (option C). We created tabs for each set to intuitively display the choice sets for farmers (Fig. 1).

Example of tabs.

It is important to clarify that the factorial orthogonal design does not presuppose any specific outcome or trend; rather, it is a method of experimental arrangement to reduce respondent burden and improve data quality and statistical efficiency. Its purpose is to ensure that each level of each attribute is examined evenly and to minimize experimental error. Moreover, this method, to a certain extent, guarantees the independence of different cultivated land attribute levels53, allowing for the separate analysis of the impact of each attribute on the experimental results54. Therefore, if a scenario arises where the combination of attribute levels leads to improved “Cultivated land surrounding landscape and ecological environment” but deteriorated “Cultivated land quality”, it does not affect the validity of the experimental design and research findings. Additionally, the factorial orthogonal design distributes the different levels of each attribute evenly within the experiment, balancing the frequency of occurrence of each attribute level and ensuring the balance of the experiment. This, in turn, mitigates potential biases in the results that could stem from the difference in the number of cultivated land attribute levels and monetary attribute levels52.

Study area and data source

The data for this study were collected through a microsurvey of vegetable growers in Shandong, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang provinces in China, conducted by our study team from July to September 2021. Vegetable growers were selected as the target group due to the characteristics of vegetable crops, which have significant profit margins, short growth cycles, and susceptibility to pests and diseases. The improper use of pesticides and fertilizers by farmers in vegetable production is commonplace, leading to severe cultivated land pollution60. To ensure the representativeness of the sample, we selected Shandong, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang as the sample provinces for the following reasons: According to the 2020 China Statistical Yearbook, in terms of economic development, Shandong, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang, as economically developed provinces in the eastern coastal region of China, have higher income levels of farmers, and stronger payment capabilities for cultivated land protection, which makes it practically meaningful to study farmers’ WTP for cultivated land attributes and attribute levels. In terms of vegetable sowing area, the three provinces all have relatively large sowing areas, namely 1464.20, 1424.50, and 645.80 thousand hectares respectively, which are representative to some extent.

Before the formal survey, we conducted a presurvey of 10 vegetable growers in each sample province and revised the questionnaire based on the presurvey results to ensure the rationality of the questionnaire design. The survey was conducted through one-on-one household interviews, and professionally trained investigators were responsible for filling out the questionnaire, ensuring there was no missing values problem. To ensure awareness regarding the different cultivated land attributes and attribute levels among the surveyed farmers, we selected the actual decision-makers in agricultural production as the sampling objects. The choice set and the time preference core independent variable involved in the survey were first explained by the investigators to ensure that the farmers could properly understand the content of the questionnaire before data collection.

The questionnaire was divided into three parts: the first part covered basic characteristics of the farmers, the second part measured the farmers’ time preference, and the third part involved the design of the choice experiment options. To avoid the potential influence of the order of choice sets on the results, the 14 choice sets presented to farmers were randomly ordered. The formal survey utilized a stratified random sampling design. First, 2–3 sampling cities (counties) were randomly selected based on the differences in economic development levels in different regions of the sample provinces. Subsequently, towns and villages were selected in the same way. In each village, 10–15 questionnaires were randomly distributed based on the number of farmers. After completing the data collection, we first examined each questionnaire and removed those with contradictory answers. Based on this, we further conducted descriptive statistical analysis of the data and removed extreme values based on the criterion that the absolute value of the z-score exceeds three times the standard deviation61. In total, 700 questionnaires were distributed. After excluding incomplete or contradictory information, we obtained 646 valid questionnaires, with an effective rate of 92.29%.

This study was performed in accordance with the research ethics guidelines of the School of Economics, Lanzhou University. Accordingly, the present study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the School of Economics, Lanzhou University. Informed consent was obtained from all respondents.

Variable selection

Dependent variable

The dependent variable in this study was the choice of farmers for each option in the choice set. The dependent variable was created using the dummy variable assignment method. In a given choice set with three options A, B, and C, if a farmer chooses option A, then option A is assigned a value of 1, whereas options B and C are assigned values of 0.

Core independent variable

The core independent variable in this study was the time preference, and we adopted experimental economics methods62 to measure farmers’ time preference (i.e., stated time preference) based on a hyperbolic discounting model with two time dimensions. The design of payoff amounts was determined based on the results of our presurvey (Table 2).

The decisions are listed in Table 2. To maintain consistency in the experiments, the time interval for both experiments was set to one month. In both experiments, short-term earnings started relatively high (950 CNY) and gradually decreased by the same magnitude, whereas long-term earnings (1000 CNY) remained constant. Discount rates can be calculated for each experiment based on the decisions made by the farmers within the same time frame (one month) in both experiments.

Farmers made choices (A or B) for each of the 12 decisions in experiment 1 and 2, and a change in the farmer’s choice from A to B represented a turning point (no preference reversal phenomenon was observed in our experiment). For example, in experiment 1, if a farmer chooses A from decision 1 to decision 8 but switches to B from decision 9, then X = 600 CNY is the farmer’s turning point for decision-making. At this point, the farmer chooses A in decision 8 (X = 600 CNY) and does not choose B (Y = 1000 CNY). In decision 9, the farmer chooses B (Y = 1000 CNY) but not A (X = 550 CNY). According to the discount rate calculation formula r = X/Y, the discount rate range for this farmer is 0.55–0.60, taking the average 0.58 as the discount rate r1. When all decisions are A for a farmer, to ensure the measurability of the discount rate range, we take X = 400 CNY as the turning point. Further, we decrease the earnings associated with choice A in decision 12 (400 CNY) by the same magnitude of 50 CNY while keeping the earnings associated with choice B fixed at 1000 CNY. The discount rate range for this farmer was calculated as 0.35–0.40, and the average of 0.38 was taken as the discount rate r1. When all decisions for a farmer are B, we take X = 1000 CNY as the turning point. Further, we increase the earnings of choice A in decision 1 (950 CNY) by the same magnitude of 50 CNY while keeping the earnings of choosing B fixed at 1000 CNY. The discount rate range for this farmer was calculated as 0.95–1.00, and the average of 0.98 was taken as the discount rate r1. Similarly, in experiment 2, r2 was obtained using the same method.

The value of r1 – r2 was used to determine the farmer’s time preference. A smaller r1 – r2 indicates a lack of patience and inclination toward a present-biased preference. Conversely, a larger r1 – r2 suggests greater patience and inclination toward future-biased preference. If the value of r1 – r2 is equal to 0, it indicates a time-neutral preference. Accordingly, this study defines r1 – r2 < 0 as present-biased preference, r1 – r2 > 0 as future-biased preference, and r1 – r2 = 0 as time-neutral preference63. Control variables

Following the general paradigm of studies on farmers’ WTP, we selected demographic, socioeconomic, and cognitive characteristics of farmers as control variables. The demographic characteristics include age and education level64,socioeconomic characteristics include household size, agricultural income ratio, planting area and number of plots23,and cognitive characteristics include perception of the impact of cultivated land environmental degradation and perception of the multifunctionality of cultivated land24. Moreover, it is important to note that integrating control variables into the Mixed logit model requires the inclusion of the interaction terms with ASC in the regression analysis. Therefore, we included interaction terms between the ASC and the demographic, socioeconomic, and cognitive characteristics mentioned above to investigate how the baseline utility of an alternative might change under different conditions or for different subgroups of respondents19.

The education level was relatively low, mainly primary school (32.20%) and junior high school (44.70%), which is consistent with the current characteristics of “agriculture for the elderly” and the generally low education level of farmers in China67. In terms of socioeconomic characteristics, the average household size of the surveyed farmers was 3.90, and the ratio of agricultural income to the annual household income was relatively low, with an average of 19%, indicating that agricultural income was no longer the main source of household income, which is related to the increase in nonagricultural income due to the large-scale migration of rural households to urban areas to work. The surveyed farmers had a planting area of 0.28 ha and an average of 4.49 plots, which is consistent with the characteristics of fragmented cultivated land and limited operating cultivated land area for farmers in rural China68. Regarding cognitive characteristics, farmers’ perception of the impact of cultivated land environmental degradation and perception of the multifunctionality of cultivated land had mean values of 3.21 and 2.52, respectively, indicating a moderate level of awareness. Overall, the basic characteristics of the surveyed farmers suggest a certain level of representativeness for the selected sample.

Endogeneity treatment

Given the potential issues of endogeneity in the model, such as omitted variable bias, bidirectional causality, or sample selection bias, which could compromise the accuracy and reliability of the research findings, we employed the Hausman test to analyze the endogeneity of time preference71. The results indicated the presence of endogeneity in time preference (F = 24.703, p = 0.000), necessitating endogeneity treatment. Adhering to the academic principles of selecting instrumental variable (IV) that must satisfy both relevance and exogeneity, we chose the “number of outbound travel trips” as the IV and applied Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) for endogeneity treatment.

In terms of relevance, an individual’s time preference is often closely linked to their travel decisions. As the number of outbound travel trips increases, it not only broadens farmers’ horizons but also stimulates their aspirations and pursuit of future life72, prompting them to adopt a more forward-thinking approach in investment decisions. This, in turn, may cause farmers more willing to invest for long-term benefits73, leading to a trend of longer-term time preference. Regarding exogeneity, the number of farmers’ outbound travel trips is unlikely to directly influence their choices within the choice sets. Instead, it indirectly affects farmers’ time preference and, subsequently, their WTP for cultivated land attributes and attribute levels by broadening their horizons and inspiring aspirations for future life. In summary, the IV selected in this study satisfies both the relevance and exogeneity conditions, preliminarily justifying its validity.

The results of endogeneity treatment are presented in Table 9. Drawing upon the approach of Mogstad et al.74, we tested the correlation between the IV and the endogenous core independent variable (see Model 6 of Table 9) and conducted endogeneity treatment for time preference (in Model 7).

Discussion

Our study result indicates that, overall, farmers tend to maintain the status quo and are reluctant to pay for cultivated land protection. This finding is similar to that of He et al.18, who found that only 37.69% of farmers in Sichuan Province, China, were willing to pay for returning straw to the fields. On one hand, the WTP is closely related to farmers’ income, and the likelihood of farmers investing in cultivated land protection increases with income77. While the sampled provinces in China are relatively economically developed, small farmers still have lower incomes than those in developed countries78. Small farmers primarily use their incomes for livelihood maintenance, which lowers their willingness and ability to invest in cultivated land protection79. On the other hand, inadequately promoting the importance of cultivated land protection can lead to insufficient awareness among farmers about current land pollution governance, thus lowering their WTP80. This suggests that the government should fully consider the impact of farmers’ income on their WTP for cultivated land protection. Meanwhile, cultivated land protection should be publicized to increase farmers’ enthusiasm for participating in cultivated land pollution governance.

Farmers’ WTP for cultivated land attribute levels is higher when their time preference leans toward the long term. Future-biased preference farmers have a higher WTP than present-biased preference farmers. Different time preferences among farmers lead to differences in WTP36. When farmers participate in cultivated land pollution governance, they consider not only expected benefits but also the attainability of benefits over multiple periods in the future81. Future-biased preference farmers, driven by the logic of achieving high future economic benefits, focus more on long-term benefits, resulting in a higher WTP36. In contrast, present-biased preference farmers lack patience, and delayed benefits increase their impatience. Relatively low agricultural returns and pressure to maintain family livelihoods, among other factors, incline present-biased preference farmers toward short-term benefits35, which leads to a lag in cultivated land protection benefits and prevents farmers from present payments and future benefits. In contrast to our findings, Ihli et al.82 found that present-biased preference farmers were more willing to pay for forest environmental protection. The difference could be attributable to the study area in Ihli’s study being Uganda, where forest coverage is low, and the planted trees have fast growth characteristics. This satisfies the short-term benefit demands of present-biased preference farmers, leading to a higher WTP. Farmers generally prioritize short-term benefits65, especially in developing countries, where pursuing short-term benefits may prevent them from investing in cultivated land protection66 while triggering survival strategies involving the predatory use of natural resources, such as cultivated land83. This suggests that government policies on cultivated land protection should focus on the impact of time preference on farmers’ WTP.

Furthermore, we found that, for farmers, cultivated land quality is the most preferred land attribute, followed by the cultivated land surrounding landscape and ecological environment, then the cultivated land social security function, and finally, the cultivated land area. Cultivated land quality critically affects farmers’ income, as it relates to high and stable agricultural production and the safety of agricultural products84. The result implies that farmers are more concerned with cultivated land quality. In recent years, as the ecological environment of cultivated land has entered the social consciousness, people’s demand for cultivated land has gone beyond providing agricultural products to include ecological requirements for the environmental landscape85. With rapid industrialization and urbanization, rural labor is gradually shifting to nonagricultural sectors, and farmers’ incomes increasingly depend on nonagricultural sectors. Agricultural income is no longer the main source of income for farmers, which weakens the cultivated land social security function to some extent86. The land tenures (ownership, user rights, and other rights) of China’s rural areas are complete, and farmers’ cultivated land areas and locations are fixed87. Therefore, farmers may not expect additional cultivated land area, which may explain in large part why the cultivated land area is of relatively low importance to farmers. The most important cultivated land attribute level to farmers is the improved cultivated land quality, which corroborates the results of Jin et al.22. Improving cultivated land quality not only enhances soil fertility and crop yield but also effectively lowers agricultural production costs, thus increasing farmers’ income88. The least preferred cultivated land attribute level to farmers is the unchanged cultivated land area, which has a relative importance of only 6.181%. Consistent with earlier discussions, the institutional arrangement of the cultivated land area remaining unchanged with population growth leads to stable expectations among farmers, lowering their interest in this attribute level87. This suggests that the government, in formulating cultivated land protection policies, should consider the relative importance of different cultivated land attributes and attribute levels to farmers.

Farmers were most willing to pay for improved cultivated land quality, with an average annual willingness of 3290.625 CNY per hectare (approximately 459.117 USD). They were least willing to pay for unchanged cultivated land area, which had an average annual of 1001.250 CNY per hectare (approximately 139.697 USD). Applying the method of Ali et al.89, which involves summing the four cultivated land attributes and calculating the WTP from the status quo to the optimal level (e.g., the cultivated land area from decrease to unchanged), the total WTP was estimated as 9185.625 CNY hectare/year (approximately 1281.606 USD). This exceeds the value obtained by Yang et al.90 for the WTP for cultivated land protection in the Jianghan Plain of China (2076.590 CNY hectare/year, approximately 287.190 USD hectare/year), which is similar to those we measured for the individual cultivated land attribute. The difference may be attributed to Yang et al.90 broadly calculating the WTP for cultivated land protection without distinguishing between different cultivated land levels, leading to some ambiguity in farmers’ WTP91. Considering different levels of cultivated land attributes allows for a detailed examination of farmers’ WTP, making their goals and expected outcomes in cultivated land protection more explicit. This can enhance farmers’ WTP for cultivated land protection, contributing to both increased farmer participation in cultivated land protection and a more precise formulation of cultivated land protection policies. This finding may also suggest that the government, in formulating cultivated land protection policies, should consider farmers’ WTP for different levels of cultivated land attributes and develop more detailed cultivated land protection policies based on the relative importance of cultivated land attributes and attribute levels to farmers.

This study also has some limitations. Firstly, farmers’ time preferences were measured using the hyperbolic discounting model, which only allows us to assess the range of farmers’ discount rates and does not provide precise numerical values. Moreover, in measuring time preference, we did not make actual payments for farmers’ choices but only assessed their stated time preferences, thereby potentially affecting the precision of our research results to a certain extent. Secondly, when constructing the choice sets, we employed an orthogonal factorial design and retained only the orthogonal terms, instead of adopting a randomized conjoint experiment approach. This method somewhat diminished the randomness in how cultivated land attributes and attribute levels were presented to farmers92, which may have limited our ability to gain a deeper understanding and accurate assessment of how cultivated land attributes and attribute levels influence farmers’ preferences. Therefore, caution must be taken when interpreting our results. Utilizing randomized conjoint experiment method to further validate and deepen our research findings will be the direction of our future research endeavors. Additionally, given that our study focused on farmers’ WTP for cultivated land attribute levels, we did not consider farmers’ willingness to accept compensation, which may affect the formulation of a comprehensive cultivated land protection policy. Finally, we did not apply a hybrid choice model to address potential endogeneity issues between time preference and choice behavior. Those topics can be studied further in the future.

Conclusion and policy implications

By first considering the intertemporal choice of farmers’ WTP for cultivated land protection, we further divided the cultivated land into different attribute levels to explore the impact of time preference on farmers’ WTP for cultivated land attribute levels. This has marked implications for guiding farmers to actively participate in cultivated land pollution governance and formulating more efficient cultivated land protection policies. We constructed a theoretical framework for the impact of time preference on farmers’ WTP for cultivated land attribute levels. Using survey data from 646 small farmers in Shandong, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang provinces in China, we employed the choice experiment method to empirically analyze the influence of time preference on farmers’ WTP for cultivated land attribute levels. We measured the relative importance of different cultivated land attributes and attribute levels to farmers, and estimated farmers’ WTP for different cultivated land attribute levels.

Overall, farmers are unwilling to pay for cultivated land protection. This implies that the government should have related strategies to broaden farmers’ income sources and increase their income levels, thereby reducing the barriers to farmers’ WTP. For instance, the government can enhance farmers’ employment opportunities and income through the integrated development model of rural primary, secondary, and tertiary industries, thus motivating farmers to pay for cultivated land protection.

Furthermore, compared to present-biased preference farmers, future-biased preference farmers are more willing to pay for cultivated land protection. Therefore, the government should fully consider the effect of time preference on farmers’ WTP for cultivated land attribute levels when formulating cultivated land protection policies. For example, the government can collaborate with research institutions or universities to simulate, through modern simulation technologies, the potential economic, ecological, and social benefits of cultivated land protection over different future time periods. By utilizing intuitive charts, animations, or virtual reality technologies, the government can demonstrate to farmers the long-term value of cultivated land protection, helping them recognize the close connection between current investments and future returns. This approach can weaken the inhibitory effect of present-bias preference on farmers’ enthusiasm for cultivated land protection and gradually shift their time preference towards the long term.

Moreover, we found differences in the relative importance of different cultivated land attributes and attribute levels to farmers. For farmers, the order of relative importance of cultivated land attributes is as follows: cultivated land quality, cultivated land surrounding landscape and ecological environment, cultivated land social security function, and cultivated land area. Improved cultivated land quality is the most important attribute level to farmers, with an average annual WTP of 3290.625 CNY per hectare (approximately 459.117 USD). In contrast, farmers are least interested in unchanged cultivated land area, with an average annual WTP of 1001.250 CNY per hectare (approximately 139.697 USD). Our conservative estimate of the economic benefits of cultivated land protection in the sampled provinces is 353.892 million CNY per year (approximately 49.376 million USD). This indicates that in promoting cultivated land protection, the government should not only broadly consider cultivated land but also account for farmers’ preference for different cultivated land attributes and attribute levels. The allocation of the limited fiscal fund should be based on the relative importance of cultivated land attributes and attribute levels to farmers, thereby improving the efficiency of fund utilization.
