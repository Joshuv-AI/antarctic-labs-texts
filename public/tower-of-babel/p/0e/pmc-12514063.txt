Evaluation of biomarkers and immune microenvironment of gestational diabetes mellitus evidence from omics data and machine learning
Authors: Ming Chen, Xueyan Cao, Sijia Huang, Jiaqi Yang, Junze Bao, Min Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to identify core genes of Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and explore its immune microenvironment. Using the limma package, we were able to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between GDM and normal placental tissue. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and various machine-learning algorithms were subsequently employed to identify core genes that may influence the occurrence of GDM. Analysis was used to evaluate the diagnostic usefulness of the core genes by using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis method. In gene enrichment analysis, we utilized the CIBERSORT algorithm to assess the immune cell composition in various samples, followed by the application of the Wilcoxon test to evaluate the immune cell content in diabetes samples during pregnancy. Conversely, analysis was done on the relationship between immune cells and core genes. Finally, we used RUN PCA to integrate different data sets and cluster cells with different functions. 527 up-regulated genes were found, and 690 down-regulated were found. Combining the results of the algorithms and ROC analysis, we identified CCL3/FAM3B/IL1RL1 as potential diagnostic biomarkers for GDM, and validated their diagnosibility using an external dataset. The results of the functional enrichment analysis indicated that core genes are associated with immune cells. When compared to pregnant women who were having diabetes, there was a considerable rise in the percentage of macrophages in immunological cells. The expression of three core genes in different cells of different samples showed that the expression of CCL3 was increased in macrophages of GDM. Cell communication analysis showed that macrophage communication was significantly active in GDM, and CCL signal was significantly increased, which mainly played a significant role through CCL3-CCR1 pathway. The findings suggest that CCL3 closely related to GDM occurrence and progression, represent new GDM marker, and that the modification of immune microenvironment plays a significant role in the occurrence of GDM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19645-9.Keywords: Gestational diabetes mellitus, Immune microenviroment, Core genes, MacrophagesSubject terms: Biological techniques, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

Gestational Diabetes mellitus is one of the common complications of pregnancy; this is the first of these complications because of abnormal glucose metabolism in the mother after getting a pregnancy. According to statistics, the prevalence of diabetes in pregnancy has increased year by year in recent decades1. Therefore, how to prevent and treat diabetes during pregnancy has become the focus of our research. Diabetes in pregnancy has many complications for pregnant women and newborns2, such as macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia, polycythemia, shoulder dystocia, hyperbilirubinemia, neonatal respiratory distress syndrome and so on. Besides, pregnant women with gestational diabetes have an increased risk of getting type 2 diabetes3. At present, the pathogenesis of GDM is not clear, which we widely recognized are inflammatory immune response and insulin resistance (IR)4. Previous studies have shown abnormal changes in the number and frequency of immune cells in GDM group compared to non-GDM group, with a linear increase in white blood cell count and significantly higher proportions of CD4 + T cells and B cells5,6. Studies have shown that GDM insulin sensitivity is reduced, and there is inflammation in adipose tissue, and macrophages in adipose tissue are more polarized to M1 type7.

Bioinformatics is the use of computer technology and mathematical thinking, combined with biological tools to explore and clarify the potential biological significance hidden in various databases. With the development of science and technology, bioinformatics can provide new ideas for the research of GDM. Considerable attention has been given to identifying biomarkers for disease diagnosis through biographical analysis8. Relevant studies have shown that some immune-related genes such as DKK1, FABP4 and CXCL10 are increased in the placenta of GDM and can be used as effective biomarkers for the diagnosis of GDM4.

In this study, we firstly obtained differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in GDM and normal placental tissue by mining one GEO dataset (GSE70493). Next, we ran a number of enrichment analyses of the pathways and functions for these DEGs. To evaluate the key module and to screen out hub biomarkers highly correlated with GDM, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was conducted alongside the application of three machine learning algorithms least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) and logistic regression. We validated the selected hub genes using GEO datasets (GSE128381, GSE92772), and verified their diagnostic value with receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Gene expression profiles of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and normal placental tissues were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/)9. For selection eligibility, the profiles should be generated through Homo sapiens expression profile analysis using arrays and be of GDM or normal placental tissues. The datasets must contain more than five samples with complete sample information. Additionally, each subject should have only one biopsy sample analyzed without duplication. As part of the test set, GSE70493 were chosen, which included 31 normal samples and 32 GDM samples. We downloaded the original count data of GSE128381 as a validation set.

Data processing and identification

The datasets were merged, and the sva package10 was employed to normalize the original data. The DEGs were screened in the batch calibrated test set using the limma package11. We selected |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05 as the truncation criteria.

WGCNA network analysis and key module identification

Using the weighted gene co-expression network analysis software (WGCNA, version 1.73, https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/WGCNA/index.html)12, a co-expression network was built that targeted DEGs. In WGCNA analysis, all DEGs with an adjusted p value < 0.05 and log2 fold changeFC > 1 in the GDM and normal samples were taken as inputs for topology calculation, with soft threshold values ranging from 1 to 25. The β value was determined from the lowest value near the scale-free network. After the relationship matrix was changed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) after the optimal soft threshold was reached, and the relation matrix was transformed into an adjacency matrix. Average link hierarchy clustering was carried out, and relevant modules were classified according to the TOM, with the requirement that the number of genes in each module be no less than 50. Similar modules were then merged. The Pearson method was employed to calculate the correlation between the combined module and GDM, and hub modules and potential hub genes related to clinical traits were identified.

Functional enrichment analyses

Gene ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway, and disease ontology (DO) analysis in order to visualize them using the GOplot package package13. Terms and pathways with a p-value less than 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant. The clusterprofiler R package (v.4.0.1)14 was employed to conduct gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using sequencing data. The GSVA R program15 was used to identify pathways most closely associated with DEGs, and p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The h.all.v7.4.symbols gene set was downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB)16, and GSEA analysis was performed on the gene set and gene expression matrix to explore possible regulatory pathways involved.

Machine learning-based hub biomarker screening

In the process of classifying features related to diseases, machine learning classification methods are being used more and more to predict them. The LASSO17 regression analysis approach can be used for gene classification and gene selection. To avert collinearity arising from high-dimensional data, redundant genes were eliminated through LASSO’s 10-fold cross-validation (using the GLMNET package) on the genes screened by WGCNA. Support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE)18,19 is a machine-learning method based on the support vector machine (SVM). It locates the optimal variable by subtracting the feature vector generated by SVM. Subsequently, the SVM-RFE method was applied to the genes processed by LASSO for further screening to determine the optimal number of genes, while minimizing classification errors and overfitting. After that, we screened the genes using univariate logistic regression analysis, with p < 0.001 serving as the cutoff point. Finally, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs), genes screened by SVM-RFE, and genes screened by logistic regression were overlapped to identify hub biomarkers.

Validation of hub biomarkers

Expression analysis of the hub biomarkers was performed on the test set. Is computed separately to assess the predictive value of discovered hub genes, and the ROC curves were plotted using the pROC R package20. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated separately to assess the predictive utility of the identified hub genes. An AUC value greater than 0.7 and a p-value less than 0.05 indicated that the genes were highly predictive for gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) diagnosis. The validation set GSE128381 and GSE92772 based on the GPL11154 platform was utilized to verify the analysis results.

Investigation of immune cell infiltration and the relationships between hub genes and immune cell infiltration

Considering the significant role of immune-related pathway in the occurrence of GDM in gene enrichment analysis, we used CIBERSORT algorithm to examine the content of immune cells in different samples.

Identification of hub immune cells

The Wilcoxon test was employed to analyze variations in immune cell content across different tissues. The random forest program package was adopted to build a random forest tree for the 22 kinds of immune cells so as to determine the point with the least error. After that, the immune cells were ranked in terms of their significance. The identified immune cells were overlapped to sift out hub immune cells that might potentially influence the occurrence of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM).

Single cell analysis

We used RunPCA to reduce dimensions and find anchor points to integrate different data sets. Next, the cells are grouped by FindNeighborsand FindClustersthese two functions (Resolution = 0.5). After obtaining the cell subgroup, we annotate each cell subgroup through the original document. At the same time, we made statistics on the proportion of each cell in the sample of diabetes during pregnancy. In addition, we compare the expression of related genes in different cells of different samples, and display the expression of these genes in the cell evolution track. In order to clarify the possible influence of secreted proteins of these related genes on the occurrence of gestational diabetes, we analyzed the secreted signals in placental samples by cell communication.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

We collected peripheral blood samples from 30 normal pregnant women and 48 gestational diabetes pregnant women in the mid-trimester (24–28 weeks) and late trimester. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were utilized to measure the levels of CCL3, FAM3B, and IL1RL1, following the manufacturer’s instructions21.

Western blot analysis and antibodies

We first extracted tissue proteins from normal placentas and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) placental tissues. RIPA buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan) was employed for total cellular protein lysis. Electrophoresis was performed with 2.5 µl protein marker in the first lane, followed by thirty micrograms of protein were separated via 10% SDS–PAGE, which was transferred to a PVDF membrane (Servicebio, Wuhan)22. Following the incubation of antibodies, a chemiluminescence system (CLINX, Shanghai) was utilized for the detection of signals. After membrane transfer and blocking, diluted primary antibody (rabbit anti-) overnight at 4 °C and then incubated with fluorescent anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:30000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies employed included CCL3(1:1000, 2624398, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), FAM3B (1:1000, 27131-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), IL1RL1(1:1000, 11920-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). The results were standardized against either GADPH (1:3000 dilution, M20024, Abmart, China). Finally, Western blotting was performed and analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.8.0, https://imagej.net/downloads) for quantitative analysis through the area under the molecular band method.

Immunohistochemistry and hematoxylin–eosin staining

Staining were used to verify the hub genes experimentally in the field of immunohistochemistry. Thirty samples of normal placental tissues and thirty GDM placental tissue samples were retrieved from Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University. Prior to the retrieval of tissue samples, none of the individuals underwent drug therapy. An informed consent form was signed by each patient, and the hospital’s ethics committee granted its approval. After the tissues were fixed and embedded in paraffin, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was conducted. The slides were cut to a width of 5 μm, dewaxed, and then rehydrated. H2O2(3%) was used for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase after incubation for 1 hat room temperature with a 3%blocking solution of bovine serum albumin (BSA). To inhibit endogenous peroxidases, hydrogen peroxide was administered to the cells for 10 min. The relevant protein antibodies were used to incubate the sections at 4 ° C overnight. The antibodies used were as follows: CCL3 (1:100, 2624398, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), FAM3B (1:500, 27131-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and IL1RL1 (1:1000, 11920–1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) antibodies were obtained from Proteintech (Wuhan, China); Subsequently, the incubation of the portions was done with a biotinylated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody for a half-hour at 37 °C (Cat. No. 5151 S,1:300, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). A freshly synthesized 3,3 ′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) reagent was used for color development (Boster, Wuhan, China). All tissues were examined by two pathologists for independent IHC staining. Under the microscope, the presence of brownish-yellow substance in the cytoplasm was positive expression. Five positive cells were selected from each section at 200 times field for statistical analysis, and the proportion of positive cells in this field was calculated by Image J software (version 1.8.0, https://imagej.net/downloads).

Results

Differentially expressed genes between GDM and normal tissues

We analyzed the DEGs of 32 GDM and 31 normal placenta samples in the test set (GSE70493 datasets). Subsequent differential gene expression analysis, using the limma package, led to the identification of 1217 DEGs, including 527 upregulated genes and 690 downregulated genes (Fig. 1A, B). We show the top twenty differentially expressed genes in GDM, the up-regulated (ERVV1, DEPDC1B, USP27X, PLCE1-AS1, ALAS2, FABP4, FABP5P1, FABP5P7, RPS6KA6 and SNORD18A) and down-regulated (IGFBP1, DKX1, CXCL9, CXCL10, IGLC7, IGHV3-30, IGKV1D-27, IGHV3-23, IGKV2-28 and IGKV2D-28) genes are indicated. To further correlate clinical information with important genes, we performed WGCNA analysis. Topological calculations were executed with a soft-thresholding power ranging from 1 to 25, and the optimal soft-thresholding power of 19 was determined (Fig. 1C). Using this soft threshold processing capability, the correlation matrix is converted to an adjacency matrix and then to a Topological Overlap Matrix (TOM). Related modules were classified according to the TOM, with the number of genes in each module being no less than 30 (Fig. 1D). Similar gene modules were combined, leading to the formation of seventeen modules (Fig. 1E). Among them, the core midnightblue module most related to the occurrence of diabetes in pregnancy contains 136 genes. We intersected the two and identified 77 potential core genes related to GDM (Fig. 1F).

Enrichment analysis of DEGs

In order to explore the potential biological mechanisms of GDM, KEGG elaborated specific pathways, such as Complement activation and other signaling pathways (Fig. 2A). Further GO analysis showed that cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions were significantly enriched (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, we conducted GSEA analysis on the expression matrix, and the results showed that chemokine signaling pathway, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway and other pathways played an important role (Fig. 2C). The above results suggest that immune-related factors affect the occurrence of GDM. In order to further identify the core genes of placental changes in GDM, we used two machine learning algorithms, LASSO and SVM, to screen 77 genes again, and finally identified 7 overlapping genes (Fig. 2D–G).

Exploration of core gene

By logstic analysis to clarify the OR value of each core gene and determine its contribution to GDM, we found that most of the core genes had potential protective effects, while FAM3B and PAGE2B were risk factors for GDM (Fig. 3A). Subsequently, we conducted a detailed exploration of the differential expression of core genes in combination with relevant biological backgrounds. Among them, CCL3/FAM3B/IL1RL1 encodes secretory proteins that can be secreted into peripheral blood, while MEDAG/P2RY14/PAGE2B/ID4 are intracellular membrane proteins or intracellular proteins. Considering that we often conduct OGTT tests on peripheral blood during the second trimester, we take CCL3/FAM3B/IL1RL1 as potential diagnostic genes. The changes of three proteins in plasma may represent the dynamic changes of GDM patients and the changes of immune microenvironment in the placenta (Fig. 3B).

Validation of diagnostic gene

Differential expression analysis was performed on CCL3, FAM3B and IL1RL. Considering that in the second trimester of pregnancy, we often carry out OGTT test on peripheral blood, so we take CCL3/FAM3B/IL1RL1 as our potential diagnostic genes, and the changes of three proteins in plasma may represent the dynamic changes of GDM patients’ condition and the changes of immune microenvironment in the placenta. In a similar manner, we conducted a differential analysis of three key genes using the placental tissue microarray chip GSE128381 and the peripheral blood sequencing chip GSE92772 during the second trimester of pregnancy, and observed that the expression levels aligned with those in our validation cohort (Fig. 4A, B).

ROC curve analysis

The ROC curve analysis showed the potential diagnostic role of each core gene in each data set (Fig. 5A–C). It is noteworthy that the AUC value of CCL3 reached 0.766 in the peripheral blood sequencing set GSE92772 (Fig. 5C). These results indicated that expressions of CCL3 were highly correlated with GDM progression, and that these genes may act as biomarkers to diagnose and verify effective biomarkers of GDM.

Potential diagnostic role of the core genes in the ROC curve in each dataset (A–C).

Analysis of differences in immune microenvironment

Considering the significant role of immune-related pathways in the occurrence of GDM during gene enrichment analysis, we employed the CIBERSORT algorithm to analyze the immune cell composition across different samples. The bar chart demonstrates the overall landscape of immune cell distribution (Fig. 6A). The Wilcoxon test showed that the GDM samples contained more T cells CD8, T cells CD4 native, NK cells resting, NK cells activated and Macrophages M1 cells (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, our analysis identified three core genes associated with immune cells. Specifically, CCL3 shows positive correlation with mast cells and negative correlation with CD8 T cells (Fig. 6C); FAM3B demonstrates positive association with CD8 T cells while showing negative correlation with CD4 + T cells (Fig. 6D); IL1RL1 exhibits positive correlation with CD4 + T cells but negative correlations with both CD8 T cells and macrophages (Fig. 6E).

Single cell analysis validation

Dimensionality reduction via PCA was performed using RunPCA to identify anchor points for integrating different datasets. Cell clustering was conducted through the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions (Resolution = 0.5), ultimately yielding a total of 17 subpopulations (Fig. 7A–D).

We found that in the GDM sample, the proportion of granulocytes was significantly reduced, while the proportion of macrophages and NK/T cells was significantly increased, and the proportion of extravilli trophoblast cells and villous trophoblast cells was significantly increased (Fig. 8A). The differences in immune cells were highly consistent with the results of bulk RNA-seq in our previous study. We compared the expression of three core genes across different cell types in various samples. The results showed that macrophages in GDM samples exhibited higher CCL3 levels. Additionally, FAM3B demonstrated consistent expression patterns between the two samples in VCT cells, while its expression was notably elevated in GDM within SCT cells. Notably, IL1RL1 showed no significant variation in expression levels across mononuclear cells from different samples (Fig. 8B). In addition, we demonstrated the expression of CCL3/FAM3B/IL1RL1 in the evolutionary trajectory of cells, but the expression of these core genes did not affect the development and typing of cells in placental tissue (Fig. 8C–F).

Ultimately, to clarify the potential impact of secretory proteins from the aforementioned three core genes on the development of GDM, we conducted cell communication analysis of the secretory signals in placental samples.

The results revealed that macrophage cell communication became significantly more active following the onset of GDM (Fig. 9A, B), and the CCL signal was significantly increased in GDM (Fig. 9C). In normal samples, the CCL signal dominated by NK/T cells and supplemented by macrophages was transformed into a strong autocrine signal of macrophages after GDM occurred (Fig. 9D), and played a significant role mainly through the CCL3-CCR1 pathway (Fig. 9E).

Clinical trial verification

According to the ELISA analysis results, the expression levels of CCL3 and FAM3B were elevated in the peripheral blood of patients with GDM during mid-pregnancy, while the expression level of IL1RL1 was decreased, which had predictive value (Fig. 10A). The peripheral blood test results in late pregnancy were consistent with those in mid-pregnancy and showed statistical significance (P < 0.001) (Fig. 10B). The ROC curve analysis demonstrated a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) area (AUC) of 0.808 with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.700-0.916. When the cutoff value was set at 80.64pg/ml, the model showed a sensitivity of 96.67%, specificity of 56.67%, and a concordance index (CI) of 0.533. These results indicate that peripheral blood CCL3 levels during mid-pregnancy possess predictive value for gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Additionally, FAM3B and IL-1RL1 levels in the same blood sample also showed predictive potential, with AUC values of 0.781 and 0.750 respectively (Fig. 10C). Notably, the predictive value of peripheral blood CCL3 expression levels for GDM was significantly higher than that of these other biomarkers.

The respective expression levels of CCL3 and FAM3B proteins were remarkably elevated in GDM compared to the relatively healthy placental tissues, as per a Western blot analysis of 30 normal placental tissues and 48 GDM placental tissues. Conversely, there was a considerable decrease in the expression level of IL1RL1 in GDM placental tissue when compared to normal placental tissue. The expression levels of FAM3B and IL1RL1 are consistent with the results of dataset based bioinformatics analysis, while the expression levels of CCL3 are opposite (Fig. 10D, E).

In the subsequent steps, we collected 30 cases of normal placental tissues and 48 cases of GDM placental tissues for immunohistochemical detection. The proportion of positive cells was quantified by ImageJ software for immunohistochemistry. CCL3, FAM3B and IL1RL1 were expressed in the cytoplasm. Immunohistochemistry analysis showed that CCL3 and FAM3B were increased in GDM placental tissues, while IL1RL1 were reduced in GDM placental tissues (Fig. 10F, G). This aligns with the findings from our protein imprinting experiment.

Discussion

GDM is a typical pregnancy-related comorbidities that result in significant problems for the mother and fetus. The pathophysiological mechanism of GDM is unknown, however, because it is difficult to diagnose and treat GDM because of the lack of effective biomarkers. GDM usually results from the imbalance of insulin resistance and the ability to secrete insulin during pregnancy23. This article combines WGCNA and machine learning algorithms to discover new biomarkers associated with GDM, and uses CIBERSORT and Wilcoxon tools to study the role of immune cell infiltration in GDM.

We retrieved the expression profiles of GSE70493 genes from the GEO database for this work, and we found that there were 1217 DEGs in total, of which 527 were up-regulated and 690 were down-regulated in the GDM sample as compared to the normal sample. Then, we investigated the biological functions of these common DEGs and KEGG analysis revealed these genes to be enriched in the natural killer cell mediation cytotoxicity pathway, which is inflammatory. Our GO enrichment analysis of DEGs suggested that immune responses were stronger in GDM samples than in normal tissues. GDM is closely related to immune cell infiltration of placenta, and immune cell infiltration of GDM placenta tissue correlates with GDM disease progression24. These results confirm that immune-related factors may affect the progression of GDM, so continued efforts to identify GDM-related immune cell infiltration may be of value for treatment of this disease.

To better understand the progression of GDM, hub modules and core gene of GDM were identified using WGCNA. According to WGCNA analysis, there was a strong correlation found between the midnightblue modules and the clinical features of GDM. Using three different machinelearning algorithms (LASSO, SVM-RFE and logistic regression), CCL3/FAM3B/IL1RL1 were identified as hub genes, which were statistically significantly over-expressed in the GDM samples (p < 0.05). The results of the ROC curve analysis demonstrated that in both the test set and the validation set, CCL3 had a high sensitivity and specificity in the diagnosis of GDM. Because there is no amino acid difference in the cysteine residues, CCL3 is part of the C-C motif family of chemokines. Depending on whether the amino acid separates the first two NH2 terminal cysteine25,26, the members of the chemokine family are separated into two primary subfamilies, namely CXC and CC chemokines. CCL3 is also known as macrophage inflammatory protein-1α(MIP-1α) because to its function in inflammation, which was first established in preclinical studies involving stimulated macrophages that released CCL327. CCLL3 cells come from many cell types, such as immune cells (macrophages, monocytes, lymphocytes, NK cells, neutrophils), epithelial cells, fibroblasts, osteoblasts, and glial cells28–30. CCL3 is a major regulator of monocyte and myeloid precursor cell trafficking252627. CCL3 binds to three distinct receptors and, thus, it exerts divergent actions in multiple immune cell subtypes. CCL3 is an important chemokine implicated in both immune surveillance and tolerance31–33. The four members make up the FAM3 gene family, and one of them is called FAM3B. The increasing body of evidence indicates that FAM3B is a significant regulator of the metabolism of glucose and lipids34. IL1RL1(also known as ST2) is a member of the IL-1 superfamily, which has been found for the first time to act as a mediator for type 2 inflammatory response35,36. GDM is caused by multiple factors. In our study, compared with healthy patients, we found that the expression level of CCL3 increases in GDM placenta tissues. The ROC curve for CCL3 indicated that it has good predictive performance in GDM (AUC = 0.766), suggesting that CCL3 plays a significant role in the progression of GDM. Inflammatory cytokines are mainly expressed in GDM, but under pathological conditions, the body still has some anti-inflammatory gene expression for selfprotection and repair. When the ultimate balance between anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory is broken, anti-inflammatory genes can’t work as effectively37. CCL3 may play this role, and the expression of CCL3 may be the body’s self-protection regulation. The above results remind us CCL3 may be a promising core gene for diagnosis of GDM. Using the GSE128381 and GSE92772 dataset, these results were verified. All validations are consistent with the microarray hybridization, indicating that CCL3/FAM3B/IL1RL1 may be involved in the pathogenesis of GDM, and thus we consider CCL3/FAM3B/IL1RL1 to be very effective biomarkers for GDM diagnosis. However, during the experimental verification process, we found that the expression level of CCL3 was opposite to the results screened by our bioinformatics analysis. This may be due to the increased secretion of CCL3 in other cells, which leads to an overall increase in the expression level of CCL3, but the specific reason is still unknown.

Our study has limitations. The sample sizes, while typical for discovery, warrant validation in larger, multi-center cohorts. Furthermore, the statistically significant differences observed for some biomarkers, though robust, were subtle in magnitude. This presents a challenge for clinical translation, necessitating highly sensitive detection platforms. However, CCL3, FAM3B, and IL1RL1 offer potential advantages over the standard OGTT: (1) Mechanistic Insight: They reflect specific dysregulated immune pathways in GDM (e.g., CCL3-CCR1 mediated macrophage activation). (2) Potential for Earlier Prediction: Circulating levels might identify risk before the OGTT window. (3) Simpler Testing: A blood-based assay could be less cumbersome than the OGTT. (4) Complementary Role: They could augment OGTT by providing biological context. The translational roadmap includes: (1) Large-scale validation in diverse cohorts. (2) Development of sensitive, standardized assays. (3) Prospective studies to confirm predictive value. (4) Assessment of clinical utility and cost-effectiveness. (5) Further investigation into their pathological roles. Addressing these points is crucial for translating these findings into improved GDM diagnosis and management strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we investigated the potential correlation between placenta inflammation and the occurrence of GDM by machine learning and found their tight relationships. Some immune-related genes, like CCL3/FAM3B/IL1RL1, are highly expressed in GDM placenta. And in comparison to the control group, the M1 macrophage ratio in the GDM placenta is higher. Thus placenta inflammation may play a vital role in GDM.
