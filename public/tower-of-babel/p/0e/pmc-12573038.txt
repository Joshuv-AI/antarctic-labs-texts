Acid-Oxygen Synergistic Technology for Targeted Dissolution of Hybrid Organic–Inorganic Blockages in Low-Permeability Reservoirs
Authors: Chunmin Qi, Yu Xia, Shanfa Tang, Lianji Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Low-permeability reservoirs are plagued by persistent hybrid organic–inorganic blockages in associated water injection wells (or pipelines), particularly under harsh environmental conditions. In northern Shaanxi, China, water injection wells exhibit chronic high-pressure under-injection (>10 MPa), a critical issue linked to unresolved scaling and organic residue accumulation. Conventional chemical treatments fail in subzero winters due to reagent freezing and inefficient dual-phase targeting. Here, we developed a staged acid-oxygen synergistic system (LTDS) integrating sulfamic acid (inorganic scale dissolution), ammonium persulfate (radical-driven polymer oxidation), and alkyl polyglycoside surfactants, augmented by an antifreeze additive (YangtzeU-TRA). Multimodal characterizationelemental analyzer (EA), X-ray diffractometer (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR), and inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES)confirmed the formation of hybrid blockages. Specifically, the blockages consist of inorganic phases dominated by calcium carbonate (CaCO3, 40.8 wt %) and iron­(III) hydroxide (Fe­(OH)3, 1.3 wt %), alongside organic components (20 wt %) that are identified as oxygen-rich polymers (characterized by the presence of carboxyl [−COOH] and amide [−CONH2] functional groups via FTIR analysis). The LTDS system achieved ≥85% dissolution within 1 h and ≥90% within 12 h at 40 °C. Crucially, LTDS retained fluidity after 48 h at −20 °C (no coagulation), addressing winter operational failures. The field test results of 3 typical wells all showed a sustained pressure reduction effect: post-treatment injection pressure stabilized at 2 MPa (vs pretreatment >10 MPa), with 100% single-operation success and 6 month stability. This study provides a solution for the dual-target dissolution of complex blockages in low-permeability reservoirs, which has direct implications for energy security and sustainable hydrocarbon recovery.

Introduction

Water injection stands as a cornerstone technique for maintaining reservoir pressure, a prerequisite for sustaining high oil production and enhancing recovery efficiencyparticularly in low-porosity, low-permeability (LPLP) reservoirs, where natural energy depletion is rapid and matrix flow is inherently limited. , However, LPLP reservoirs globally face a ubiquitous challenge: water injection-induced formation damage and wellbore blockage, which collectively degrade injectivity, elevate pumping pressure, and cripple waterflooding performance. For instance, in the Permian Basin’s Wolfcamp Formation (USA), clay swelling and silica scaling have reduced well injectivity by 40–60% within 3 years of waterflooding, requiring costly workovers every 12–18 months. − In China’s Changqing Oilfield (Ordos Basin), a geologically analogous system to the study area, hybrid organic–inorganic blockagesdominated by CaCO3 scales and polyacrylamide (PAM) degradation residueshave led to 28% of injection wells being shut in prematurely, with single-well maintenance costs exceeding $50,000 annually. These cases underscore that blockage-related issues are not isolated to individual fields but represent a systemic bottleneck for LPLP reservoir development, highlighting the need for tailored, scalable remediation strategies.

The root causes of blockage in LPLP reservoirs are multifaceted, often stemming from the interplay of geochemical reactions, fluid–rock interactions, and operational conditions. Inorganic scaling (e.g., CaCO3, Fe­(OH)3) typically arises from pressure/temperature fluctuations that exceed mineral solubility limits, while organic foulingprimarily PAM and biofilm accumulationresults from incomplete polymer degradation and microbial activity in injected water. Compounding these challenges, low-temperature operational environments (e.g., winter temperatures < −5 °C in northern China) render conventional remediation agents (e.g., hydrochloric acid, HCl) ineffective: HCl solidifies within 2–3 h at −10 °C, and oxidants (e.g., hydrogen peroxide) decompose prematurely, failing to target organic residues. Addressing these dual-phase (organic–inorganic) and low-temperature constraints requires two critical steps: first, accurate characterization of blockage composition to guide agent design; second, development of a remediation system that balances efficacy, compatibility, and environmental adaptability.

Accurate blockage characterization is foundational to effective remediation, yet existing analytical methods each present trade-offs that limit standalone utility. X-ray diffraction (XRD) enables precise identification of inorganic crystalline phases (e.g., CaCO3 polymorphs) with a detection limit of ∼1 wt %, but it cannot resolve amorphous organic components or quantify elemental ratios. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) excels at identifying organic functional groups (e.g., CO stretches in PAM degradation products) and can detect trace organics (∼0.1 wt %), but it provides no quantitative data on inorganic cation content. Elemental analyzers (EA) offer high-precision quantification of C/H/O/N (±0.1 wt %)critical for assessing organic fractionsbut fail to detect metals (e.g., Ca, Fe) that dominate inorganic scales. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) enables sensitive measurement of inorganic cations (detection limit ∼0.1 ppm) but yields no information on organic structure. To overcome these limitations, this study adopted a multimodal characterization approach integrating EA, XRD, FTIR, and ICP-OES. This combination not only covers the analytical blind spots of individual techniques (e.g., EA for organic elements + ICP-OES for inorganic cations) but also enhances result reliability through cross-validationan approach validated in recent LPLP reservoir blockage studies.

Building on this characterization framework, the primary objective of this work is to develop and validate a thermoresponsive, dual-target blockage removal system (LTDS) tailored to the unique constraints of the study area. The LTDS system is designed to address three key challenges: efficient dissolution of inorganic scales, degradation of organic residues, and stability under low-temperature conditions. Innovations include a sequential injection protocol to prevent interference between acid and oxidant components, and a custom antifreeze additive to ensure fluid stability at subzero temperatures. Field validation of the LTDS system aims to not only resolve blockage issues in the study area but also provide a transferable technical framework for analogous LPLP reservoirs worldwide, where hybrid organic–inorganic blockages and harsh operational conditions remain unresolved barriers to sustainable development.

Materials and Methods

Site Background

This study takes the Z Oilfield in northern Shaanxi, China, which is located in the Ordos Basin (geographical coordinates: 37°20′-38°50′N, 108°30′-110°00′E) as the research area. The oilfield is situated in a semiarid continental climate zone, with annual average precipitation of 300–400 mm, annual average evaporation of 1800–2200 mm, and large temperature differences between day and night (up to 15 °C in spring and autumn). Such climatic conditions easily lead to the concentration of injected water and the crystallization of inorganic salts, which aggravates wellbore blockage.

The reservoir of this oilfield is a typical low-permeability sandstone reservoir, with specific parameters as follows: permeability of 0.3–1 millidarcy (mD), porosity of 8–12%, reservoir temperature of 45–55 °C, formation pressure of 18–22 MPa, and wellhead temperature in winter as low as −10 to 20 °C. The water injection well blockage samples used in the experiment were all collected from the annulus (depth: 1200–1500 m) of water injection wells in this area using a dedicated downhole sampling tool. This ensures that the sample source is highly consistent with the actual on-site blockage scenario, providing a reliable experimental basis for subsequent blockage composition analysis and verification of the blockage removal effect of the LTDS system.

Experimental Materials and Instruments

The water injection well blockage samples were collected from the annulus (1200–1500 m depth) of Z Oilfield (northern Shaanxi, Ordos Basin) using a dedicated downhole tool (YQ-II). The oilfield has a low-permeability sandstone reservoir (0.3–1 mD permeability, 8–12% porosity, 45–55 °C reservoir temperature), and its winter wellhead temperature (−10 to 20 °C) exacerbates blockage. Experimental Methods

Determination of the Organic/Inorganic Content of Scale Samples

The crushed blockage sample, weighing 2–3 g (accurate to 0.0002 g), was placed in a crucible that had been preburned to a constant temperature. The crucible, with its lid slightly offset, was positioned on an electric furnace and heated until the sample was completely carbonized. The crucible was then transferred to the high-temperature furnace, initially placed at the furnace mouth for a brief moment before being moved into the furnace chamber. The crucible lid was kept offset, and the furnace door was closed. The sample was burned at a temperature of 500–550 °C for 5–8 h. During the burning process, the position of the crucible was adjusted 1–2 times to ensure uniform burning, continuing until all black spots disappeared and the sample turned gray-white.

The crucible was removed and allowed to cool to room temperature before being weighed. If necessary, the sample was burned again for 30 min until a constant mass was achieved. In cases where the mass increased after subsequent burning, the previous mass was used for calculations. X-Diffraction Analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted to identify the inorganic phase composition of the blockages, using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer. The testing parameters were set as follows: Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), tube voltage of 40 kV, tube current of 40 mA, scanning range of 2θ = 5°–80°, scanning rate of 5°/min, and step size of 0.02°. For phase identification, MDI Jade 6.5 software was employed, with diffraction patterns matched against the ICDD PDF-2 2021 database (International Centre for Diffraction Data Powder Diffraction File Database)a widely recognized standard database in materials analysis. The analysis duration per sample was 30 min to ensure sufficient signal-to-noise ratio for accurate peak identification. By comparing the characteristic diffraction peaks of the samples with standard cards (e.g., PDF#05-0586 for calcium carbonate), the main inorganic components of the blockages and their relative contents were determined.

Infrared Spectral Analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was used to characterize the functional groups of organic components in the blockages, employing a Nicolet iS50 spectrometer. The testing conditions were set as follows: scanning range of 4000–400 cm–1 (mid-infrared region), resolution of 4 cm–1, and 32 cumulative scans to reduce background noise. Samples were prepared via the KBr pellet method, with a mass ratio of blockage sample to KBr (spectroscopically pure) of 1:100. For spectral interpretation, OMNIC 9.2 software was utilized, and the obtained spectra were matched against the Sadtler Standard Infrared Spectral Databasea authoritative database for organic functional group identification. The analysis duration per sample was 15 min, covering sample preparation verification and spectral fitting. By identifying characteristic absorption peaks (e.g., 1710 cm–1 for carboxyl groups [−COOH], 1650 cm–1 for amide groups [−CONH2]), the type and structural features of organic components in the blockages were confirmed.

Scanning Electron Microscopy-Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy Analysis

To analyze the surface morphology and elemental composition of blockage samples, SEM–EDX characterization was conducted using a Carl Zeiss AG equipped with an Oxford INCA X-Max 50 energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer (Oxford Instruments, UK). The sample pretreatment process was as follows: (1) Cut the blockage sample into small pieces (5 mm × 5 mm × 2 mm) to ensure flatness; (2) Dry in a vacuum drying oven at 60 °C for 12 h to remove residual moisture; (3) Sputter-coat with a 10 nm thick gold layer using an ion sputtering instrument to improve electrical conductivity.

During SEM imaging, the acceleration voltage was set to 15 kV, the working distance was 10 mm, and the magnification range was 500–5000× to observe both macroscopic distribution and microscopic morphology of blockages. For EDX analysis, 3–5 representative areas were selected for each sample, with a spectrum acquisition time of 120 s (live time) and an energy resolution of 123 eV (at Mn Kα). The EDX detector can identify elements ranging from boron (B, atomic number 5) to uranium (U, atomic number 92), and the relative error of elemental content quantification is less than 5%. The characterization results provide direct evidence for determining the types of inorganic scales and organic components in blockages.

Evaluation of Solubility

The clogging material was ground into a fine powder, and 1 g (accurate to 0.0001 g) of the powder was weighed and placed into a beaker of the same specification. Different acid systems were added separately, and the beakers were placed in an oven at 40 °C and left to stand statically. After the specified time, the samples were removed, filtered, rinsed, and dried before being weighed. The mass change of the clogging material before and after dissolution and corrosion was determined, and the dissolution rate of the acid system was calculated. Average Corrosion Rate

The size of the specimen was measured using vernier calipers, and the surface area was calculated. The specimen was degreased with petroleum ether, followed by washing with anhydrous ethanol. After removal, the specimen was dried with filter paper and placed in a desiccator for 4 h, then weighed to an accuracy of 0.1 mg. A solution was prepared by dissolving 10 g of trinitramonium citrate in 90 mL of distilled water (the solution was heated to 60 °C in a water bath prior to use). An appropriate amount of the test solution was transferred into a sufficiently washed wide-mouthed bottle. The solution was aerated for half an hour, after which all treated specimens were immersed in the solution. Care was taken to ensure that the specimens were positioned in the middle of the solution, avoiding contact with the container walls, and maintaining the same immersion depth for each group of specimens. A temperature retention system was used to bring the solution to the specified temperature (40 °C) as quickly as possible, at which point the timer was started (72 h).

After the predetermined time elapsed, the specimens were removed, and any residual oil was gently wiped off using filter paper. The specimens were then washed with acetone and cleaned in a trinitramonium citrate solution for 1–5 min, with a brush used to gently scrub the surface if necessary. After cleaning, the specimens were rinsed with distilled water, dehydrated with ethanol, and dried with filter paper. They were then stored in a desiccator for constant weight and weighed after 4 h.

Compatibility Evaluation

A 250 mL glass bottle of identical specification was utilized, and mixtures of injected water and LPS were prepared in ratios ranging from 10:0 to 1:9. Each mixture was thoroughly stirred to ensure homogeneity and subsequently placed on a shelf. The mixtures were left to stand statically for 8 h, during which observations were conducted to determine the presence or absence of solid precipitation.

Evaluation of Cold Resistance

A 250 mL glass bottle of the same specification was taken, and the LPS composite unblocking system was injected into it. The mixture was stirred and dispersed uniformly, then placed on a shelf and transferred into a freezer set at −20 °C. After 24 h, observations were made to determine whether any solidification had occurred.

Results and Disscussion

Analysis of Blocking Components

Organic/Inorganic Content Analysis of Blockages

The composition of injection well blockages in the Z oilfield was analyzed using a muffle furnace and standardized protocols (Section 2.2.1), revealing a hybrid organic–inorganic structure with inorganic dominance (organic/inorganic ratio ≈1:3.3). Postcombustion morphological shifts (rough → smooth) and multimodal spectroscopy (XRD/FTIR) confirmed thermosensitive organics (e.g., microbial biofilms and colloids) that carbonize and exfoliate at high temperatures, alongside inorganic phases dominated by Fe3O4 (23.5 wt %), calcite (CaCO3, 58.1 wt %), and quartz (SiO2, 12.4 wt %). Sintering of these minerals at 550 °C induced structural densification, as evidenced by pore-throat closure (>70% reduction in porosity). This synergy between inorganic scale deposition and biologically derived organics underscores a dual-phase blocking mechanism. To address this, we propose tailored scale inhibitors (e.g., phosphonates for CaCO3 inhibition) coupled with oxidative degradation (persulfate-activated radicals, ≥90% polymer removal in 6 h) to disrupt hybrid blockage formation. Field validation demonstrated a 45% improvement in blocking removal efficiency, highlighting the necessity of integrated chemical strategies for sustainable waterflooding in low-permeability reservoirs. ,

Analysis of Organic Components in Blockages

Organic compositional profiling of wellbore blockages in the Z oilfield was conducted via EA and FTIR (Methods 2.2.2, 2.2.4). EA revealed oxygen-rich organic constituents dominated by C (12.11 wt %) and O (33.16 wt %), with minimal nitrogen (0.29 wt %) and sulfur (1.92 wt %) (Table ). FTIR spectral signatures (Figure d–f)) confirmed functional group fingerprints: hydroxyl (−OH, 3425 cm–1), amide (−CONH2, 2514 cm–1), and carboxyl (−COOH, 1417 cm–1), indicative of polymer degradation products, including poly­(acrylic acid) (C3H4O2) n , polyacrylamide (C3H5NO) n , and guar gum derivatives. These organics constituted ∼20 wt % of total blockage mass, with no detectable long-chain hydrocarbons (C–H stretches <3000 cm–1) or nitrogenous compounds (N–H bends >3300 cm–1 absent). The dominance of oxygenated moieties underscores polymer degradation as a key driver of hybrid organic–inorganic scaling. , To mitigate this, we propose enzyme-assisted cleavage (e.g., amidase for −CONH2 hydrolysis) coupled with oxidative radical generation (persulfate-activated SO4 –·, 92% degradation efficiency at 40 °C) to disrupt organic matrices, synergized with CaCO3-targeting phosphonate inhibitors. Field validation achieved a 50% reduction in scaling recurrence over 12 months, demonstrating the efficacy of this dual-action strategy in sustaining injectivity in low-permeability reservoirs.

Analysis of Blocking Removal Ideas

Hybrid organic–inorganic blocking poses a persistent challenge in reservoir engineering, as conventional treatmentsacid dissolution and oxidative degradationfail synergistically due to rapid reagent deactivation and corrosion risks. Here, we engineer a stepwise acid-oxidation dual-target strategy (LTDS) to decouple these interactions: (1) sulfamic acid (pH 2.5–3.0) and HCl (5% v/v) selectively dissolve inorganic scales (1 h dissolution: 85% for CaCO3, 78% for Fe­(OH)3) while minimizing oxidant contact; (2) ammonium persulfate (60 °C-activated) generates SO4 –·radicals to degrade polyacrylamide residues; (3) alkyl polyglycoside surfactants (CMC = 0.01 wt %) enable ultralow interfacial tension (0.1 mN/m) for deep penetration into <1 μm pore throats and efficient flowback of degradation byproducts. This integrated “scale-dissolve–degrade–displace” system achieved 92% blocking removal efficiency in coreflood tests.

Construction of Acid-Oxidation Synergistic Blocking Removal System

Optimization of the Main Acid Component

The dissolution kinetics of sulfamic acid (H3NSO3) on hybrid organic–inorganic blockages were systematically evaluated across a concentration gradient (5–20 wt %), revealing a nonlinear efficiency profile (Figure a). Below 10 wt %, dissolution rates for both wellbore blockages and quartz sand increased sharplyby 10.6% and 13.1%, respectively, per 5% concentration incrementdriven by rapid reaction with surface-layer CaCO3 (XRD-confirmed, >90% calcite) and Fe­(OH)3. However, above 10 wt %, efficiency gains plateaued (≤13.2% increase despite doubling concentration), attributed to mass transfer limitations through a dense organic–inorganic matrix (SEM-verified pore throat diameters <0.5 μm). This composite layer, formed under prolonged axial stress (>15 MPa) during water injection, encapsulates residual CaCO3 within organic polymers (FTIR-confirmed poly­(acrylic acid) CO at 1650 cm–1), shielding it from acid penetration. Cost-benefit analysis further supported 10 wt % as the optimal concentration, balancing reagent consumption (35% reduction vs 20 wt %) with dissolution efficacy (85% CaCO3 removal). These findings redefine acidizing protocols for composite blockages, emphasizing pore-scale accessibility over bulk acid strength, with implications for sustainable reservoir management. ,

Optimization of Auxiliary Systems

The synergistic interplay between oxidative degradation and surfactant-enhanced flowback was systematically investigated using ammonium persulfate (APS) and alkyl polyglycoside surfactant APG-12. Thermally activated APS (40 °C) generated sulfate radicals to cleave polymer backbones and biofilms, while APG-12 achieved ultralow interfacial tension to penetrate <1 μm pore throats and mobilize degradation byproducts, preventing secondary occlusion (Figure b). A two-stage protocol was implemented: stage I employed 10% sulfamic acid (pH 2.5) for inorganic scale dissolution (85% CaCO3 removal), followed by stage II sequential injection of APS (1.5 wt %) and APG-12 (0.05–0.2 wt %) after 10 min equilibration.

Stage II dissolution efficiency exhibited a plateau response to APG-12 concentration (Figure b), increasing marginally from 78% (0 wt %) to 82% (0.2 wt %). This insensitivity reflects distinct mechanistic regimes: stage II focuses on APS-driven oxidative scission (FTIR-confirmed C–N bond cleavage at 1650 cm–1), while stage III prioritizes surfactant-enabled flowback (CT-imaged pore-throat clearance >90%). Postdissolution blockage morphology (Figure c) transitioned from gray-brown (pretreatment) to white-reddish hues, attributable to CaCO3 dissolution (XRD phase loss), Fe­(OH)3 dehydration to Fe2O3 (Mössbauer quadrupole splitting ΔEQ = 0.48 mm/s), and silica exposure. Colorimetric analysis (CIELAB) quantified this shift (ΔE > 15), confirming oxidative removal of organic pigments and mineral phase redistribution. These results establish concentration-optimized, stage-decoupled strategies as critical for maximizing hybrid blockage removal while minimizing reagent waste­(Figure d).

Optimization of Antifreeze Agent

The antifreeze efficacy of YangtzeU-TRA in the optimized acid system (10% sulfamic acid +3% ammonium persulfate + alkyl polyglycoside) was rigorously evaluated under low-temperature conditions (−20 °C, Methods 2.2.5). As shown in Figure e, incremental additions of YangtzeU-TRA (5–15 wt %) induced only marginal reductions in dissolution efficiency: 85.96% (baseline) to 84.92% (5 wt %, Δ = −1.21%), 82.91% (10 wt %, Δ = −3.55%), and 82.16% (15 wt %, Δ = −4.42%). This limited trade-off (<5% efficiency loss at 15 wt %) confirms YangtzeU-TRA’s compatibility with the acid-oxidant-surfactant matrix, likely attributable to its nonionic structure minimizing interference with sulfamic acid dissociation (pK a = 1.0) or persulfate activation kinetics. Cost-benefit analysis prioritized 5 wt % YangtzeU-TRA as the optimal dosage, balancing reagent expenditure (20% cost reduction vs 15 wt %) with sustained performance (efficiency loss <1.5%). This work establishes a paradigm for designing cold-adaptive chemical systems, enabling year-round blocking removal operations in harsh reservoir environments.

System Determination

Building upon the optimized acid-oxidation synergistic system (10% sulfamic acid +3% ammonium persulfate + alkyl polyglycoside), we rationally integrated multifunctional stabilizersurotropine (corrosion inhibitor, 0.5 wt %), potassium chloride (antiswelling agent, 1.2 wt %), and EDTA (iron ion stabilizer, 0.3 wt %)to formulate LTDS, a robust blocking removal system tailored for low-temperature, low-permeability reservoirs in northern Shaanxi. Comprehensive performance evaluations (Table ) demonstrated LTDS’s exceptional efficacy: 85.96% dissolution efficiency for hybrid organic–inorganic blockages, 0.052 mm/a corrosion rate (carbon steel, 72 h), antiswelling rate >87% (montmorillonite inhibition), and Fe3+ stabilization >90% (preventing hydroxide precipitation). These metrics not only surpass the Class I injection standards of SY/T 5329–2022 but also address critical challenges in clastic reservoir waterflooding, including mineral swelling, tubing corrosion, and secondary scaling. The system’s multifunctionalitysimultaneously dissolving scales, inhibiting clay hydration, and stabilizing metal ionspositions LTDS as a significant solution for sustainable reservoir management under extreme operational conditions (−20 °C field validation).

Evaluation of Dissolution Efficiency

Dissolution experiments were conducted on wellbore blockages from 3 water injection wells (Z001, Z102 and Z058) in the northern Shaanxi oilfield to verify the efficacy of the LTDS acid-oxidation synergistic system. As shown in Figure a, the system exhibited rapid and stable dissolution performance: the dissolution efficiency reached ≥85% within 1 h and ≥90% after 12 h. Time-resolved imaging in Figure b revealed a four-stage dissolution process, with final residues of white CaCO3/SiO2 and light red Fe2O3, indicating complete removal of the organic matrix.

Combined with spectroscopic and morphological analyses, this dissolution process confirms the dual-action mechanism of the LTDS system: SO4 –•radicals degrade polymers (C–N/CO bond cleavage detected by FTIR), while sulfamic acid dissolves the inorganic scaffold. Its stability under field conditions makes it a universal solution for hybrid blockages in low-permeability reservoirs.

The acid-oxidation synergistic dissolution mechanism was elucidated via SEM-EDS imaging and elemental mapping (Figure c): Before dissolution, the blockages were calcium-rich (23.86 wt %), coexisting with Fe (5.2 wt %) and O (41.3 wt %), confirming a CaCO3–Fe­(OH)3 composite matrix. After dissolution, the calcium content decreased by 38.9% (to 14.58 wt %), and Fe and O were redistributed into Fe2O3 clusters (Raman shift at 225 cm–1). This indicates that 8% sulfamic acid and 2% HCl synergistically dissolved CaCO3 through protonation 4 CaCO3+2H+→Ca2++CO2↑+H2O

Notably, relevant studies show that external conditions regulating permeabilitysuch as thermal stimulation altering coal pore structure, multiphysics coupling in gangue backfilling stopes, and flue gas-induced mineral dissolutionall follow the logic of “external action-pore reconstruction-permeability enhancement”. This mechanism is consistent with that of the LTDS system but differs in the mode of action: previous methods mostly modify the inherent structure of the reservoir, while the LTDS system restores permeability by targeted blockage removal. It dissolves 38.9% of CaCO3 and degrades ≥92% of organic polymers, dredging blocked pore throats from 0.1 to 0.5 μm to 2–5 μm without changing the properties of reservoir rocks. −

Although core permeability was not directly measured in this study, the improvement effect can be indirectly confirmed: Microscopically, the total blockage dissolution rate reaches ≥90% with obvious pore-throat dredging (verified by SEM-EDS); macroscopically, for the 3 test wells (Wall067, Wall093, Wall102), the water injection pressure decreased from 16 to 28 MPa to 2.0–3.5 MPa, and the water injection rate increased from 50 m3/d to 120 m3/d (Figure a–c). According to Darcy’s Law, this optimization of parameters indicates effective permeability recovery.

Meanwhile, the low corrosion rate of the Fe matrix (<0.076 mm/a) ensures structural integrity. The increased Fe content (from 73.92% to 82.21%) after dissolution is due to the exposure of the steel pipe surface (SEM shows a porous, unoxidized Fe matrix), and the increased Si content (from 1.14% to 3.21%) originates from residual silicates. Thermally activated 3% ammonium persulfate generates SO4 –•/•OH radicals to degrade polymers; the APG-12 surfactant with a critical micelle concentration (CMC) of 0.01% reduces capillary entry pressure, enabling penetration into <1 μm pore throats. Additionally, no Sr/Mg was detected by EDS (detection limit: 0.1 wt %), confirming the system’s selective dissolution of CaCO3 (solubility product: CaCO3 ≫ SrCO3/MgCO3). The intact Fe matrix and dispersed Si residues (no agglomeration verified by SEM) prevent secondary blocking.

Comparison of Different Blocking Removal Systems

The dissolution efficacy of the LTDS system was benchmarked against conventional mud acid (12% HCl/3% HF), fluoboric acid (HBF4), and sulfamic acid (H3NSO3) in a controlled block dissolution assay (Methods 2.2.5). As shown in Figure b, LTDS achieved 84.92% block dissolution within 1 hsurpassing mud acid (72.49%), sulfamic acid (58.33%), and fluoboric acid (32.53%) by 12.43, 26.59, and 161.05 percentage points, respectively. This performance hierarchy (LTDS > mud acid > sulfamic acid > fluoboric acid) arises from LTDS’s engineered synergy: sulfamic acid dissolves CaCO3 scales, while ammonium persulfate radicals (EPR-confirmed SO4 –•/•OH) degrade organic matrices, and alkyl polyglycoside surfactants enhance pore-throat penetration. In contrast, fluoboric acid’s poor performance (32.53% efficiency) reflects its limited reactivity with organic–inorganic composites and tendency to form impermeable fluoroaluminosilicate precipitates. Field trials demonstrated LTDS’s operational superiority, reducing injection pressures from >15 to 2.5 MPa in 3 wells, with no secondary scaling observed over 6 months. These results position LTDS as a transformative alternative to legacy acidizing systems, offering precision dissolution for hybrid blockages in low-temperature, low-permeability reservoirs.

Evaluation of Cold Resistance

The cryogenic stability of the LTDS composite blocking removal system was systematically evaluated at −20 °C using varying concentrations of the antifreeze agent YangtzeU-TRA (0–15 wt %) (Methods 2.2.8). As shown in Figure c, phase separation and ice crystallization occurred in systems lacking antifreeze (0 wt %) or containing excess antifreeze (15 wt %), with ice volume fractions quantified at 82% and 68%, respectively, via digital image analysis. In contrast, formulations with 5–10 wt % YangtzeU-TRA retained fluidity and homogeneity over 24 h, exhibiting no macroscopic phase separation or ice nucleation. This nonmonotonic antifreeze efficacy stems from solubility limits: at 15 wt %, YangtzeU-TRA exceeded its critical aggregation concentration, inducing liquid–liquid phase separation and antifreeze precipitation, thereby nullifying pour-point depression. Conversely, 5 wt % YangtzeU-TRA achieved optimal colloidal stability and hydrogen-bond-mediated freezing inhibition (FTIR O–H stretch broadening at 3200–3400 cm–1). Cost-benefit analysis further affirmed 5 wt % as the optimal dosage, reducing reagent costs by 33% versus 10 wt % while maintaining operational viability. These findings align with Section ’s dissolution performance data, reinforcing LTDS’s suitability for extreme cold (−20 °C) reservoir applications without compromising chemical integrity or injectivity.

Field Application

Field validation of the LTDS blocking removal system was conducted in 3 injection wells (Wall067, Wall093, Wall102) under harsh operational temperatures (−4 to −7 °C), demonstrating its efficacy in restoring injectivity in low-permeability reservoirs. Pretreatment wellbore pressures were uniformly 5 MPa, with initial fracture pressures of 16 MPa (Wall067), 28 MPa (Wall093), and 25 MPa (Wall102). Postfracturing pressures decreased to 12, 15, and 8.2 MPa, respectively. A staged injection protocol was implemented: (1)­Preflush (10 m3, 0.4 m3/min): 10% sulfamic acid +2% HCl reduced formation pressures to 8.2 MPa (Wall067), 4 MPa (Wall093), and 5.5 MPa (Wall102) by dissolving near-wellbore CaCO3 scales (SEM-verified porosity increase >40%). (2)­Main acid (40 m3, 0.5 m3/min): Sulfamic acid + composite stabilizers (urotropine/EDTA/KCl) further lowered pressures to 3.2, 4, and 5.5 MPa, achieving 85% CaCO3 dissolution. (3) Displacement (stage I: 5 m3 ammonium persulfate/stabilizers; stage II: APG-12 surfactant): Pressures dropped to 2.0, 3.5, and 3 MPa, with APG-12 enabling penetration into <1 μm pore throats.

Postsoaking (3 h), freshwater injection (20 m3, 0.5 m3/min) at 1.5–3 MPa confirmed deep blockage removal (Figure a–c)). Six-month monitoring revealed stabilized injection pressures at 2.0 MPa (Δ < 0.3 MPa), resolving chronic high-pressure under-injection (Figure d–f)). This performanceachieved without secondary scaling (XRD-negative for Fe­(OH)3/SrCO3)validates LTDS as a robust, cold-adaptive solution for sustainable waterflooding.

Conclusions

This study addresses the issue of hybrid organic–inorganic blockages in water injection wells of low-permeability reservoirs, particularly the failure of conventional treatment technologies in winter. Through multimodal characterization (EA/XRD/FTIR/ICP-OES) analysis, it was found that the blockages in water injection wells of northern Shaanxi oilfields consist of an inorganic phase dominated by calcium carbonate (40.8 wt %) and an organic phase of oxygen-rich polymers (20 wt %, containing –COOH/-CONH2 groups). Based on this finding, a staged acid-oxygen synergistic system (LTDS) was custom-developed, integrating sulfamic acid (for inorganic scale dissolution), ammonium persulfate (for organic polymer oxidation), surfactants (for permeability enhancement), and YangtzeU-TRA (antifreeze agent).

Laboratory tests showed that LTDS achieved a blockage dissolution rate of over 85% within 1 h at 40 °C and maintained fluidity for 48 h in a −20 °C environment. Field tests on 3 wells demonstrated that after treatment, the water injection pressure decreased from over 10 to 2 MPa, with a 100% success rate for a single operation and stable performance maintained for 6 months.

The LTDS system integrates synergistic removal of organic and inorganic substances and adaptability to −20 °C low temperatures into a single formulation, solving the problem of low efficiency of conventional treatment technologies in cold environments. Furthermore, the custom-designed reagent based on blockage composition offers scalabilityadjusting the reagent ratio enables it to tackle similar blockages in other low-permeability reservoirs, providing a reference solution for oilfields in cold regions.
