Association of Clinical Factors, IL4, and IL17 Levels with the Development of Postherpetic Neuralgia: A Prospective Study Among Patients with Herpes Zoster from India
Authors: Basavaraju Shreelakshmi, Subramaniam PS Ranugha, Akila Prashant, Garehatty R. Kanthraj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The incidence of postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) is highly variable due to the lack of a uniform definition of PHN and differing views on its severity. Most of the studies on risk factors for PHN are retrospective, and pain severity has not been uniformly graded. There is also a paucity of literature on cytokine levels as markers for the development of PHN. A few studies done so far have shown conflicting results. The objective of this study was to assess the association of clinical factors, interleukin 4 (IL4), and IL17 levels during acute zoster with the development of PHN, as well as to grade PHN using the Zoster Brief Pain Inventory (ZBPI).Patients and Methods:Eighty clinically diagnosed herpes zoster cases within 1 week of the onset of the rash were recruited. The severity of prodromal pain and acute zoster pain was analyzed based on the visual analog scale and ZBPI, respectively. IL4 and IL17 estimation was done using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits. Patients were followed up in the first and third months for the presence of PHN using ZBPI.Results:PHN was present in 54 (67.5%) cases during the first month and 38 (47.5%) during the third month. Age (40–60 years; P = 0.03), trigeminal distribution (P = 0.05), lesion number between 25 and 50 (P = 0.008), presence of prodromal pain (P = 0.01), zoster-associated pain (P = 0.04), higher IL4 (P = 0.02) and IL17 (P = 0.01) levels had a significant association with PHN in the third month. Using logistic regression, PHN risk increased with increasing age. Moderate to severe PHN was present in 8 (10%) patients in the third month. No significant difference in clinical characteristics and cytokine levels was found between patients with mild and moderate to severe PHN.Limitations:Relatively small sample size.Conclusion:IL4 and IL17 may be potential markers for the risk of PHN, and the role of these markers needs to be studied further.

Introduction

Herpes zoster (HZ) presents as a vesicular rash in dermatomal distribution associated with pain. It occurs due to the reactivation of the latent varicella-zoster virus (VZV) in sensory ganglia, probably from the waning of cell-mediated immunity below a critical level.[1] Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), a common complication of HZ, is defined as pain persisting for more than 30 or 90 days after rash onset.[1] It is a potentially debilitating neuropathic pain syndrome commonly seen in clinical practice.

PHN has a wide range of incidence varying from 5% to 30%.[2] There is no uniform definition of PHN, and there are differing views on its severity. Some consider it as pain persisting after 1 month of rash, while others consider pain persisting for more than 3 months as PHN. There are differing views on the definition of PHN based on severity grading; while few studies consider >3 as PHN, others consider 1 or more as PHN based on the validated Zoster Brief Pain Inventory (ZBPI) scale.[34] Hence, the incidence is highly variable. PHN is refractory to treatment and affects the quality of life, thus contributing to a substantial burden on the healthcare system and society.

A detailed knowledge of the various risk factors for PHN could contribute to research on prevention. Early intervention in patients with high risk might prevent or reduce the severity and duration of PHN.

Several studies have identified multiple demographic and clinical risk factors for PHN.[1234] However, the study population, definition of PHN, and severity grading of PHN have been highly heterogeneous. While some have studied risk factors for the occurrence of PHN, others have assessed the risk for severe PHN, and some have studied only the elderly population. Most of these studies are retrospective. To the best of our knowledge, we could not find any Indian data on the same. Hence, we intended to study the demographic and clinical risk factors in a prospective manner, both in the first and third months after HZ, with grading of pain severity using the validated ZBPI scoring.

Currently, effective biomarkers to predict PHN remain unavailable. Studies on cytokines in zoster and PHN are few. Different cytokines have been studied by different researchers, and their results are inconclusive. Although broad activation of the immune system, with an increase in interferon-γ (IFNγ), interleukin 6 (IL6), and IL8, has been observed in zoster patients compared to controls, these differences only verged on significance. No difference in any of these cytokines was observed between those individuals who developed PHN and those who did not.[5] Zajkowska et al.[6] found an increase in IL17 and IL4 in zoster patients compared to the control group, which was statistically significant. Interestingly, there was a simultaneous increase in expression of IL4 and IL17, which is unusual because these cytokines normally negatively regulate one another. Yu et al.[7] suggested that the augmented Th17 cell differentiation and IL17 signaling might underlie the increased risk of HZ among patients with COVID-19. Hence, we decided to study IL4 and IL17 levels in our patients with zoster and follow them up for the development of PHN to obtain some preliminary data on the association of these cytokines with PHN.

Methodology

Ethical clearance was obtained for the study from Instituitional Ethical Committee, JSS Medical College, Mysore (JSS/MC/PG/5156/2020-21) on 22.01.2021.

We recruited 80 clinically diagnosed HZ patients of any age and both sexes presenting within 1 week of onset of rash (day 1 is the day of rash onset) to our dermatology outpatient department. If there was a clinical suspicion of secondary infection, Gram stain was done from the blister contents, and if found positive, they were excluded from the study. We documented the patient’s demographics, treatment history, immune status, and underlying diseases at recruitment. Patients were clinically examined for specific characteristics of herpes lesions (severity, dermatome involved). The severity was graded by counting the number of vesicles and bullae as <25, 26–50, and >50 lesions.

Patients were enquired about prodromal pain before the onset of the rash. The severity of prodromal pain was assessed using a visual analog scale (VAS).[8] VAS intensity rating is a 100 mm line representing no pain to the worst pain. Patients were asked to mark the line that represents their prodromal pain intensity. It is graded as follows: No pain: <5 mm, mild pain: 5–44 mm, moderate pain: 45–74 mm, and severe pain: >75 mm.[8]

Acute zoster pain (pain at the time of zoster rash) and post-herpetic neuralgia (persistence of pain at 1 and 3 months after rash) were graded using ZBPI. It is a modified version of the Brief Pain Inventory used in HZ patients.[9] Using an 11-point Likert scale (0–10), ZBPI measures the severity of current, least, average, and worst pain within the last 24 hours. It also measures the interference of HZ pain with seven activities of daily living: working ability, sleep, relations with others, mood, enjoyment of life, general activity, and work. ZBPI is graded as follows: (0) no pain, (1–2) mild, (3–6) moderate, and (>7) severe.[9]

Following clinical evaluation, 5 mL of venous blood was drawn from all patients to measure serum cytokine levels (IL4, IL17) using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Immunoconcept India Pvt. Ltd: CK BIO). Zoster treatment was given for all as per standard guidelines. The study subjects were re-examined to evaluate for PHN in the first month (PHN1) and third month (PHN3) using ZBPI.

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

Eighty patients were recruited. The mean age of study subjects was 48.7 ± 3.5 years. Thirty (37.5%) subjects were >60 years old, while 26 (32.5%) were in the age group 41–60 years, and 24 (30.1%) were less than 40 years old. Men accounted for 52.53% of the patients, and women 47.47%. Forty-four (55%) presented to us within 3 days of rash onset. The most common dermatomes involved were thoracic 26 (32.5%), followed by trigeminal 21 (26.3%), and cervical 17 (21.3%). Lumbar and sacral dermatomes were affected in 6 (7.5%) and 10 (12.5%) patients, respectively. Fifty-six patients had a rash with >25 vesicles and bullae. Thirty-seven patients (46.3 %) had comorbidities; diabetes in 21.3%, hypertension in 13.75%, malignancy in 2.5% and connective tissue disorder in 1.25% with 3.75% cases having liver or renal failure and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, respectively. Prodromal pain was present in 43 (53.8%), with 90.6% of cases having moderate to severe pain on VAS. Most of them had fever, malaise, nausea, or vomiting for 1 to 2 days before the rash onset. The most common type of prodromal pain was burning, followed by itching.

Zoster brief pain inventory

According to ZBPI, zoster-associated pain (ZAP) was present in 61 (76.25%) patients. PHN was present in 53 (67.25%) patients in the first month and 38 (47.5%) patients in the third month. The pain was continuous and intermittent in the first month and intermittent only in the third month.

Discussion

Nineteen studies found a highly variable incidence of PHN ranging from 2.6% to 67.3%. This difference might be due to the different definitions used for PHN and the varying age groups of the study population. While few have considered ZBPI score >3 as PHN, others have taken a score of 1 or more as PHN.[1011] In our study, we followed up the patients for 3 months to look for the incidence of PHN (ZBPI ≥ 1) as well as its severity (ZBPI > 3) both at 1 month and 3 months after rash onset. We found the incidence of PHN to be 67.5% in the first month and 47.5% in the third month. However, Gauthier et al.[12] found a much lower incidence of 19.5% (PHN at 1 month, PHN1) and 13.7% (PHN at 3 month, PHN3) in the United Kingdom. The incidence of PHN among all age groups across the world ranges from 9.3% in the United States to 20% in Singapore and up to 21% in Northern Cyprus.[113] According to a recent Chinese study with 2840 HZ patients, 15.6% developed PHN.[14] However, when we included patients with ZBPI > 3 alone, 28.75% and 10% of patients had PHN in the first and third months, respectively. In a recent Spanish study, 11.79% above 50 years of age developed moderate to severe PHN (ZBPI > 3).[15]

Age has been identified as a consistent factor in predicting the development of PHN.[4] Most studies have confirmed age as an independent risk factor for PHN.[1014] The incidence of PHN increases with age. Gauthier et al.[12] observed PHN1 risk to range from 10.3% (95% CI, 9.3–11.4) for ages 50–54 to 28.9% (95% CI, 27.2–30.7) for the 80–84 age group. A similar finding was observed in our study, where patients older than 60 years had a higher risk (OR: 19.45; CI: 2.49–151.78) compared to those between 40 and 60 years of age (OR: 15.04; CI: 2.27–99.56). The waning of cell-mediated immunity with increasing age could explain this. However, we could not find a significant association between sex and the risk of developing PHN. There is conflicting evidence for gender as a risk factor for PHN. While some have found males to be at higher risk, others have shown a higher female predisposition.[34]

Among the clinical characteristics of HZ, the severity of ZAP, severe rash at presentation, prodromal pain, and ophthalmic distribution of eruption are commonly cited in many studies as risk factors for PHN.[341011] In a Chinese study on 2840 HZ patients, lesions on upper limbs and shoulders were observed be independent risk factors for PHN.[14] In our study, in subjects with 25–50 vesicles and bullae, the presence of ZAP (ZBPI > 1) and the presence of prodromal pain had a significant association with PHN. However, regression analysis did not reveal any significance. This could be because of a relatively small sample size.

Meta-analysis and systematic review by Forbes et al.[4] and Zhou et al.,[3] respectively, showed that acute severe pain in the HZ stage was one of the independent risk factors of PHN. Some studies have pointed out that the degree of pain in the acute phase correlates positively with the severity of nerve injury, and the risk of PHN increases accordingly. Though we found PHN risk to be associated with the presence of ZAP, we did not find a significant association with moderate to severe ZAP. Neuropathic pain questionnaire scores of ≥4 or VAS ≥5 have been considered as severe pain in the studies included in the meta-analysis,[4] while we used ZBPI to grade the pain associated with zoster.

A greater number of vesicles and a more extensive rash may underlie more significant nerve damage, resulting in an increased risk of PHN. This could explain the significant association between PHN and the eruption of 25–50 vesicles, as observed in our study. Opstelten et al.[16] found vesicles ≥47 to be an independent risk factor for PHN. Their study population, however, included adults above 50 years of age. Some have graded the severity of the rash based on the area involved by lesions in the affected dermatome and found an association between severity of the rash and the development of PHN.[17]

Prodromal symptoms are also an independent risk factor for PHN. Prodromal symptoms indicate early replication of the HZ virus in the affected ganglion, resulting in more significant nerve damage. Also, when prodromal pain without skin lesions occurs, patients may delay treatment due to an unknown diagnosis.[2] We also found that patients with a history of prodromal pain had a higher incidence of PHN. Surprisingly, itching type of prodromal pain and pain duration of 1 to 2 days had a significant association with PHN in the third month of our study.

Several studies and systematic reviews have observed that patients with ophthalmic zoster were at greater risk of PHN.[13418] In our study, lumbar and trigeminal distribution were associated with a greater risk of PHN in the first and third months, respectively. Hu et al.[14] found involvement of upper limbs and shoulders in HZ patients to be an independent risk factor for PHN.

Immunosuppression is a risk factor for PHN because of reduced cell-mediated immunity. It is known to increase the incidence of HZ complications and disease severity.[19] In a study, patients taking immunosuppressants and those with hypertension were at an increased risk of developing PHN.[14] In a case-based study conducted in the United States, immunosuppressed individuals were found to have a 10-fold increase in the risk of PHN.[20] However, we did not observe this in our study. This might be because immunocompromised patients accounted for only 11.25% of our study subjects. Three of our patients were HIV positive on anti- retroviral treatment (ART). We did not find a severe presentation or severe PHN in these cases, as they were already on ART. Jih et al.[21] found a 50-fold decreased risk of PHN in HIV. Further large-scale studies are required to assess the role of immunosuppression in PHN.

The patho-mechanism governing the transition of VZV from latency to reactivation is not yet clear. However, it is believed that T cells play a crucial role in the interplay between the immune system and latent VZV infection.[6] Cytokines are important mediators of immune responses, and they play an essential role in the expression of cell-mediated immunity. Zhang et al.[22] have found that cells with a Th1-like cytokine profile are affected mainly in HZ. However, zoster does not occur in all individuals with decreased T cell immunity; thus, this factor is necessary but insufficient for reactivation. Decreasing antibody titers to VZV do not predispose to reactivation.[23]

Studies on cytokines in Zoster

Different cytokines have been investigated in HZ. In a recent meta-analysis[24] on cytokines in zoster, 13 studies were identified, of which eight were on inflammatory markers in HZ, three were in PHN, and two were on markers in both zoster and PHN. Among the studies in HZ, five were on IL levels in zoster cases and controls, three on expression of lymphocytes, and two on other inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP), ferritin, tuftsin phosphorylcholine, homocysteine, uric acid, mitochondrially encoded ATP synthase 8, mitochondrially encoded NADH dehydrogenase 4, etc., They concluded that there is compelling evidence that CRP, homocysteine, IL-1β, IL4, IL6, IL8, and IL10 are associated with the genesis and development of HZ and PHN. However, there was a significant degree of heterogeneity in some of the included studies, and there was a possibility of publication bias in this meta-analysis, as mentioned in their limitations.

Zak-Prelich et al.[5] studied the levels of IFNγ and IL2, IL4, IL6, IL8, IL10, IL12, IL13, and IL18 in the serum of patients with zoster and compared them with controls. Although the levels of IFNγ, IL6 and IL8 were higher in zoster patients than in controls, there was only a slightly significant difference in IL6. On the other hand, Zhang et al.,[25] found significantly lower levels of TH1 cytokines like IL2 and TNF and higher levels of TH2 cytokines like IL4 and IL10 in blister fluid of HZ cases when compared to suction blister fluid in volunteers. Fukuyasu et al.[26] found higher serum levels of IL10 in patients with severe HZ than those with mild-moderate HZ. Khazan et al.[27] found significantly high levels of Indole oxygenase, IL18, IL6, ferritin, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, and homocysteine, and low levels of melatonin in HZ patients compared with controls. Using logistic regression, they concluded that only elevated homocysteine levels and reduced melatonin levels, and no cytokines, could be associated with the manifestation of HZ.

Zajkowska et al.[6] compared the serum cytokine profile (IL17, IL23, IL21, IL4, IL12), representing both cellular and humoral immunity in HZ patients with healthy controls, and found an increase in IL17 and IL4 in zoster patients than controls. Hence, we tried to estimate the levels of IL4 and IL17 at the time of acute zoster and study whether the difference in these levels had a role in the development of PHN later. In our study, the mean values of IL4 and IL17 in HZ patients were 122 ± 257 pg/dL and 420 ± 255 pg/dL, respectively. There was a lot of variation in the values among our patients. Others have made similar observations of highly varying cytokine levels.[5] Zajkowska et al.[6] found the mean levels of IL4 to be 5.01 ± 0.48 pg/dL and IL17 to be 24.00 ± 12.49 pg/dL in patients with HZ. This difference in interleukin levels between our study and theirs could be because of the different ELISA kits used and the varying calibrations.

Studies on cytokines in PHN

Zak-Prelich et al.[5] did not find a significant difference in any of the cytokines (IFNγ, IL2, IL4, IL6, IL8, IL10, IL12, IL13) between those who developed PHN and those who did not. On the other hand, serum IL10 and serum IL6 levels significantly correlated with the development of PHN in two different studies.[2628] We observed a significant difference in the levels of IL4 and IL17 between PHN and non-PHN patients. ROC curves supported the same. Zajkowska et al.[6] found significantly higher levels of these cytokines (IL4 and IL17) between HZ cases and controls; however, they did not compare the cytokine levels between those with and without PHN. We are not aware of any other similar studies on these cytokines to compare our results. Raised IL4 and IL17 levels during acute zoster in those who developed PHN imply that broad activation of the immune system (both cellular and humoral) during presentation could increase the risk for PHN.

Recently, Cao et al.[29] assessed the expression of inflammatory cytokines in the affected and normal skin of PHN patients. They found that the expression of inflammatory cytokines in PHN skin was not significantly different from that in normal skin. This suggests that cytokine levels during the acute phase and the ensuing nerve damage may have a role in PHN, rather than cytokine expression after zoster resolution. The observations by Prelich et al.[5] that patients who developed PHN had lower infiltration by CD3, CD8, and CD56-positive cells into their skin lesions at the time of the acute HZ support this.

Lin et al.[30] observed higher IL6 levels in PHN patients compared to volunteers, with a decrease in levels after treatment. Patients in the severe PHN group had a higher serum IL6 level than those in the mild PHN group. However, serum cytokine analysis was done after the resolution of zoster, not during the acute phase, as in our study. Zhao et al.[31] found low levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor, nerve growth factor, neurotrophin 3, and neurotrophin 5 in PHN cases when their cerebrospinal fluid was studied. We tested for cytokines in serum and not in cerebrospinal fluid.

The levels of IL17 were much higher than IL4 in patients who developed PHN in our study. Yu et al.[7] demonstrated the protein–protein interaction in COVID-19 patients with zoster and found that augmented Th17 cell differentiation and IL17 signaling might underlie the increased risk of HZ among patients with COVID-19. It is, therefore, worthwhile to investigate in the future whether modulation of IL17 signaling could have any prophylactic and therapeutic value in HZ and its complications.

Limitations

The relatively small size and measurement of a few cytokines are some of the limitations of our study. The levels of cytokines may not be reliable in immunocompromised patients. Genetic polymorphisms in pro- and anti-inflammatory responses, and target cell reactivity to cytokine signaling, may also influence variations in cytokine levels among individuals.[32]

Conclusion

With regression analysis, increasing age was the only independent risk factor associated with the development of PHN in both the first and third months. IL4 and IL17 levels had a significant association with the development of PHN in both the first and third months. Further large-scale studies on cytokine levels in serum and skin, with measurements both during the acute phase and during PHN, are necessary to understand the role of these as serological markers for PHN.
