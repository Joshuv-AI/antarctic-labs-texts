Development and validation of a green dispersive liquid-liquid microextraction-LC-MS/MS method for sotalol quantification in human plasma using central composite design optimization and pharmacokinetic application
Authors: Ahmed Serag, Maram H Abduljabbar, Manal E Alosaimi, Alaa O Baryyan, Reem M Alnemari, Fotoon F Redwan, Atiah H Almalki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn LC-MS/MS method coupled with central composite design (CCD)-optimized dispersive liquid-liquid microextraction (DLLME) was developed and validated for sotalol quantification in human plasma according to ICH M10 guidelines. CCD with response surface methodology systematically evaluated four DLLME parameters — disperser solvent volume, extraction solvent volume, sample pH, and centrifugation time — identifying extraction solvent volume, pH, and disperser volume as dominant factors governing extraction efficiency. Optimal conditions of disperser volume 1200 µL, extraction solvent 300 µL, pH 10.0, and centrifugation time 3 min yielded predicted recovery of 95.29%, experimentally confirmed at 99–103%, with organic solvent consumption of 1.5 mL per sample representing a reduction compared to conventional SPE and LLE approaches. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 2.7 μm) with 4-min isocratic elution, and quantification employed MRM transitions m/z 273.1→255.1 for sotalol and m/z 267.2→145.1 for atenolol internal standard in positive ESI mode. The method demonstrated excellent linearity (1–1200 ng/mL, r² = 0.9994), sensitivity (LLOQ 1 ng/mL), acceptable accuracy and precision, and comprehensive stability under multiple storage conditions, in full compliance with ICH M10 acceptance criteria. Preliminary pharmacokinetic application in healthy volunteers following single oral 80 mg sotalol administration yielded Cmax 701 ± 53.8 ng/mL, t½ 10.1 ± 1.0 h, and AUC0→∞ 11,713 ± 1,345 ng·h/mL, broadly consistent with published literature values, supporting method applicability for therapeutic drug monitoring. Multi-metric sustainability assessment encompassing AGSA-prep (64/100), AGSA (65.28/100), CaFRI (69/100), BAGI (75.0/100), CACI (69/100), AMRI (71/100), and WECA (81%) demonstrated improved environmental performance relative to conventional extraction approaches, providing comprehensive sustainability characterization of the developed method.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-59711-4.Keywords: Sotalol, Dispersive liquid-liquid microextraction, LC-MS/MS, Central composite design, Green analytical chemistrySubject terms: Chemistry, Drug discovery

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually1. Among various manifestations, cardiac arrhythmias affect over 59 million individuals globally2,3. Sotalol hydrochloride, a class III antiarrhythmic agent with combined β-adrenergic blocking and potassium channel blocking properties, is widely used for managing ventricular arrhythmias and atrial fibrillation4,5. Its dual mechanism prolongs action potential duration and effective refractory period through IKr inhibition6. The drug’s narrow therapeutic window, predominantly renal elimination (80–90% unchanged), and risk of fatal proarrhythmic effects including torsades de pointes render therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) essential, particularly in patients with renal impairment7,8. Despite its clinical importance, existing analytical methodologies often employ large plasma volumes, hazardous organic solvents, and time-consuming sample preparation procedures, highlighting the need for miniaturized and environmentally sustainable bioanalytical approaches9,10.

Several analytical techniques have been reported for sotalol determination in biological matrices. HPLC-FLD methods utilizing sotalol’s native fluorescence represent the most widely employed approach, with sample preparation based on either SPE or protein precipitation11,12. While SPE provides effective matrix cleanup, it is time-consuming and consumes substantial organic solvent volumes13,14, whereas protein precipitation, though simpler, suffers from limited selectivity and significant matrix interference from endogenous plasma components11,15. HPLC-UV methods have also been reported but demonstrate inferior sensitivity, restricting their application to higher therapeutic concentration ranges16–19. LC-MS/MS has emerged as the gold standard, offering superior sensitivity and selectivity through electrospray ionization with multiple reaction monitoring9,20–22; however, the commonly employed protein precipitation sample preparation provides insufficient cleanup of phospholipids and endogenous matrix components, resulting in ion suppression effects that may compromise quantitation accuracy and instrument longevity23. Alternative techniques including capillary electrophoresis24 and electrochemical sensors25,26 remain limited to pharmaceutical formulations due to insufficient sensitivity for complex biological matrices. These limitations collectively highlight the need for simple, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable sample preparation approaches for routine sotalol bioanalysis in human plasma.

Dispersive liquid-liquid microextraction (DLLME), introduced by Rezaee et al. in 200627, represents a promising green alternative to conventional sample preparation techniques in bioanalytical chemistry. The technique involves rapid injection of a mixture of extraction and disperser solvents into an aqueous sample, generating a fine-droplet emulsion with large interfacial surface area that facilitates rapid analyte mass transfer28,29. Key advantages include minimal organic solvent consumption, operational simplicity, rapidity, and high extraction efficiency30,31. DLLME has been successfully applied to pharmaceutical bioanalysis, with reported recoveries of 81.7–95.6% for CDK4/6 inhibitors32, enrichment factors of 19–50 for gliflozins33, and recoveries exceeding 96% for tricyclic antidepressants in plasma34. Air-assisted and ultrasound-assisted variants have further expanded its applicability to polar compounds35. Nevertheless, DLLME application to plasma samples requires careful optimization to overcome challenges including protein interference and emulsion formation.

Design of Experiments (DoE), particularly response surface methodology (RSM) based on central composite design (CCD), provides a powerful framework for systematic optimization of analytical methods by evaluating multiple parameters simultaneously while minimizing experimental effort36,37. CCD efficiently estimates main effects, interaction effects, and quadratic terms through second-order polynomial models, enabling simultaneous optimization of multiple responses with excellent predictability38. CCD-based optimization has been successfully applied to DLLME of pharmaceutical compounds39 in biological matrices, including ionic liquid-based DLLME for antichagasic drugs40, chlordiazepoxide determination in plasma41, and effervescent tablet-assisted DLLME of pharmaceutical drugs39, demonstrating robust and efficient method development with minimal experimental runs.

Given the limitations of existing sotalol bioanalytical methods and the growing emphasis on green analytical chemistry, the present study aimed to develop and validate an LC-MS/MS method coupled with CCD-optimized DLLME for sotalol quantification in human plasma. Four critical DLLME parameters — extraction solvent volume, disperser solvent volume, sample pH, and centrifugation time — were systematically optimized using CCD with RSM to maximize extraction efficiency. The method was comprehensively validated according to ICH M10 guidelines, encompassing selectivity, sensitivity, linearity, accuracy, precision, recovery, matrix effect, and stability under multiple storage conditions. Clinical applicability was demonstrated through a preliminary pharmacokinetic study in healthy volunteers following single oral administration of 80 mg sotalol. Furthermore, a comprehensive multi-metric sustainability assessment was conducted to evaluate the environmental performance of the developed method relative to conventional extraction approaches.

Experimental

Chemicals and reagents

Pure reference materials of sotalol hydrochloride (purity 99.12%) and atenolol (internal standard, purity99.42%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Organic solvents for sample preparation including ethyl acetate, acetonitrile, ethanol, methanol, and acetone were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Additional extraction solvents evaluated during preliminary screening experiments, including chloroform, tetrachloromethane, and chlorobenzene, were also procured from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). LC-MS grade acetonitrile used for mobile phase preparation was supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Formic acid of analytical reagent grade (purity ≥ 98%) was procured from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Ammonium hydroxide solution (25% aqueous NH₃) utilized for pH adjustment was acquired from El-Nasr Pharmaceutical Chemicals Co. (Cairo, Egypt). High-purity deionized water employed throughout all experimental procedures was produced using a Milli-Q water purification system manufactured by Millipore (Bedford, MA, USA). Drug-free human plasma samples, collected from healthy volunteers following informed consent procedures under institutional ethics committee approval, were stored at -70 °C until required for method development and validation experiments.

Instrumentation

Chromatographic separation and mass spectrometric detection were accomplished using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography system coupled to an Agilent 6470 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionization interface (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The LC system comprised a quaternary solvent delivery module, temperature-controlled autosampler with sample cooling capability, thermostated column compartment, and online vacuum degassing unit. Sample processing utilized a high-speed vortex mixer (VWR International, Radnor, PA, USA) and a temperature-controlled microcentrifuge (Eppendorf 5424R, Hamburg, Germany). pH measurements were conducted using a calibrated Jenway 3510 benchtop pH meter fitted with a combination glass electrode (Cole-Parmer, Stone, UK).

Experimental design for DLLME optimization

Central composite design (CCD) was utilized to systematically optimize critical DLLME parameters and evaluate their interactive effects on extraction efficiency. Four independent variables were selected for optimization based on preliminary screening experiments and literature precedents for extraction of basic drugs from biological matrices. These factors included disperser solvent volume (A), extraction solvent volume (B), sample pH (C), and centrifugation time (D). The experimental design consisted of 16 factorial points, 8 axial (star) points at α = ±1.2, and 3 center point replicates, yielding a total of 27 experimental runs executed in randomized order to minimize systematic errors. The actual levels for each factor were established as follows: disperser volume ranged from 400 to 1200 µL with center point at 800 µL, extraction solvent volume from 100 to 300 µL with center point at 200 µL, pH from 3.0 to 10.0 with center point at 6.5, and centrifugation time from 3 to 10 min with center point at 6.5 min. For each experimental run, drug-free human plasma samples (400 µL) were spiked with sotalol at a concentration of 600 ng/mL and internal standard (atenolol) at 100 ng/mL, then subjected to DLLME according to the design matrix specifications.

Two response variables were monitored to evaluate extraction performance: extraction recovery (%) calculated as the ratio of peak area obtained from extracted samples to that from post-extraction spiked samples, and coefficient of variation (CV %) as an indicator of method precision and reproducibility. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and mean values were used for statistical modeling. Design-Expert® software (version 11) was employed for experimental design generation, statistical analysis, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and response surface visualization. Second-order polynomial regression models were fitted to the experimental data, and the adequacy of models was evaluated using coefficient of determination (R²), adjusted R², predicted R², adequate precision ratio, and lack-of-fit tests. Three-dimensional response surface plots were constructed to visualize the relationships between factors and responses, facilitating identification of optimal extraction conditions.

Stock solutions, standard solutions, and sample preparation

Stock solutions of sotalol hydrochloride and atenolol (internal standard) were independently prepared at a concentration of 100 µg/mL in ethanol, selected as a greener alternative to methanol due to its lower toxicity and reduced environmental impact. Stock solutions were prepared by accurately weighing appropriate amounts of each compound and dissolving in ethanol in amber volumetric flasks. The stock solutions were stored at -20 °C in amber glass vials and were stable for at least 3 months under these conditions. Working standard solutions of sotalol were prepared by serial dilution of the stock solution with ethanol to obtain intermediate concentrations. These working solutions were used to prepare calibration standards and quality control (QC) samples by spiking appropriate volumes into drug-free human plasma. Eight calibration standards were prepared at concentrations of 1, 10, 25, 50, 200, 400, 800, and 1200 ng/mL to establish the analytical range. Quality control samples were independently prepared at four concentration levels: lower limit of quantification quality control (LLOQ QC, 1 ng/mL), low quality control (LQC, 3 ng/mL), medium quality control (MQC, 600 ng/mL), and high quality control (HQC, 1000 ng/mL).

Optimized DLLME procedure

Based on the CCD optimization results, the following optimized DLLME procedure was employed for extraction of sotalol from human plasma samples. Plasma samples (400 µL aliquots) were transferred into 2 mL polypropylene microcentrifuge tubes, followed by addition of internal standard working solution to achieve a final concentration of 100 ng/mL atenolol in the total plasma volume. The pH of plasma samples was adjusted to 10.0 by adding ammonium hydroxide solution (25% NH3), followed by brief vortex mixing for 15 s to ensure complete pH adjustment and homogeneous distribution.

A mixture of extraction solvent (ethyl acetate, 300 µL) and disperser solvent (acetonitrile, 1200 µL) was rapidly injected into the alkalinized plasma sample using a microsyringe in a single swift motion. Upon injection, acetonitrile simultaneously precipitated plasma proteins while generating a fine-droplet ethyl acetate emulsion throughout the aqueous phase, achieving concurrent deproteinization and analyte extraction in a single step. The cloudy mixture was gently mixed by inversion for 10 s to enhance extraction efficiency.

The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 5000 rpm (2,427 × g) for 3 min at room temperature to achieve complete phase separation. Following centrifugation, the organic phase (ethyl acetate layer) containing extracted analytes formed as a distinct upper layer and was carefully collected using a micropipette and transferred to clean glass autosampler vials. The organic extract was evaporated to dryness under a gentle stream of nitrogen at 40 °C. The dried residue was reconstituted with 400 µL of mobile phase (water: acetonitrile, 70:30, v/v, containing 0.1% formic acid) by vortex mixing for 30 s, followed by brief sonication for 1 min to ensure complete dissolution. The reconstituted samples were transferred to autosampler vials with glass inserts, and 5 µL aliquots were injected into the LC-MS/MS system for analysis.

LC-MS/MS analytical conditions

Chromatographic separation was performed on a reversed-phase Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 2.7 μm; Agilent Technologies) maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase A) and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (mobile phase B). Isocratic elution was performed using 30% mobile phase B at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, with a total run time of 4.0 min and injection volume of 5 µL.

Mass spectrometric detection employed an Agilent Jet Stream electrospray ionization (AJS ESI) source operating in positive ionization mode with the following optimized parameters: gas temperature 350 °C, drying gas flow rate 10 L/min, nebulizer pressure 45 psi, sheath gas temperature 400 °C, sheath gas flow 12 L/min, capillary voltage 4000 V, and nozzle voltage 500 V. Nitrogen was used as both the collision and drying gas. Quantification was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode: sotalol precursor ion m/z 273.1 [M + H]⁺ with product ion transition m/z 273.1→255.1 (fragmentor voltage 135 V, collision energy 13 eV), and atenolol precursor ion m/z 267.2 [M + H]⁺ with product ion transition m/z 267.2→145.1 (fragmentor voltage 130 V, collision energy 24 eV). The dwell time was set at 200 ms for each transition. Data acquisition and quantitative analysis were performed using Agilent MassHunter Workstation software (version 10.1).

Pharmacokinetic study application

To demonstrate real-world clinical utility, the validated DLLME-LC-MS/MS method was applied to a pharmacokinetic investigation following single oral sotalol administration in healthy human subjects. The research protocol obtained ethical clearance from the Faculty of Medicine, Al-Azhar University, Damietta branch (approval no. DFM-IRB00012367-26-01-077). Five healthy adult participants were recruited following comprehensive eligibility assessment and provision of written informed consent. All experimental procedures complied with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for medical research involving human participants and adhered to International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Following an overnight fast of at least 10 h, each participant received an 80 mg oral sotalol hydrochloride tablet administered with 200 mL of water. Venous blood samples (3 mL) were withdrawn into vacutainer tubes containing K₂EDTA anticoagulant at the following time intervals: immediately before dosing (zero time) and at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 12, 18, 24, 36, and 48 h following drug intake. Each blood specimen was promptly centrifuged at 5000 rpm (2,427 × g) for 10 min at 4 °C to separate plasma. The supernatant plasma was carefully transferred into clean polypropylene cryovials and immediately frozen at -70 °C pending analytical determination. All samples were analyzed using the validated DLLME-LC-MS/MS method.

Pharmacokinetic analysis utilized non-compartmental methodology with R statistical software (version 4.3.0) and the PKNCA package for parameter determination. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation accompanied by coefficient of variation percentage, while tmax is expressed as median with range reflecting its non-parametric distribution. The extended 48-hour sampling timeframe captured complete absorption and elimination phases, facilitating comprehensive characterization of sotalol pharmacokinetic behavior in healthy participants.

Results and discussion

DLLME optimization using central composite design

Central composite design and statistical modeling

Prior to the formal CCD optimization, preliminary one-factor-at-a-time solvent screening was conducted to identify suitable extraction and disperser solvents. Various extraction solvents commonly employed in DLLME applications, including chloroform, tetrachloromethane, chlorobenzene, and ethyl acetate, were evaluated at fixed disperser volume (1000 µL acetonitrile) and extraction volume (200 µL). Ethyl acetate demonstrated superior extraction recovery (> 85%) while offering reduced toxicity and lower environmental impact compared to halogenated alternatives. Among disperser solvents screened (methanol, acetonitrile, and acetone), acetonitrile was selected based on its superior miscibility with both the aqueous plasma phase and ethyl acetate, facilitating stable emulsion formation and simultaneous plasma protein precipitation. This preliminary selection established acetonitrile and ethyl acetate as the solvent system for subsequent CCD optimization.

Systematic optimization of DLLME parameters was accomplished through CCD coupled with RSM to maximize extraction recovery while minimizing method variability. Four independent variables were investigated: disperser solvent volume (Factor A, 400–1200 µL), extraction solvent volume (Factor B, 100–300 µL), sample pH (Factor C, 3.0–10.0), and centrifugation time (Factor D, 3–10 min). The CCD matrix comprised 16 factorial points, 8 axial points at α = ±1.2, and 3 center point replicates, yielding 27 randomized experimental runs (Table S1). Extraction recovery and CV% were monitored as response variables to comprehensively evaluate extraction performance.

Effect of individual DLLME parameters

The influence of individual DLLME parameters on extraction performance was systematically evaluated through main effects analysis to elucidate their mechanistic contributions to sotalol recovery and method precision (Fig. 1). Extraction solvent volume exerted the most pronounced influence on both responses, with recovery increasing continuously across the 100–300 µL range while CV% decreased correspondingly (Fig. 1A and B). This behavior is consistent with fundamental liquid-liquid partitioning principles, where larger ethyl acetate volumes are expected to provide greater organic phase capacity and likely generate more numerous fine droplets, potentially increasing interfacial contact area and facilitating mass transfer — effects quantitatively reflected in the largest regression coefficient magnitudes among all investigated factors (+ 5.15 for recovery, − 3.50 for CV%).

Main effects plots showing the individual influence of DLLME parameters on (A) extraction recovery and (B) coefficient of variation. Each plot displays the effect of disperser volume, extraction solvent volume, pH, and centrifugation time across their investigated ranges with 95% confidence intervals.

Sample pH demonstrated the second most significant influence, with recovery exhibiting a marked upward trend at alkaline values (Fig. 1A). This pH-dependence is governed by the acid-base equilibrium of sotalol (pKa ≈ 9.8). At acidic pH, sotalol exists predominantly as the protonated cation with minimal affinity for the non-polar ethyl acetate phase, whereas at pH 10.0 the neutral species predominates, maximizing partitioning into the organic phase. The substantial positive regression coefficient (+ 4.36) quantitatively reflects this relationship, while the relatively flat CV% curve (coefficient − 0.9785) indicates that pH primarily affects recovery rather than precision.

Disperser solvent volume demonstrated moderate positive effects on both responses, with recovery increasing gradually and CV% decreasing with increasing acetonitrile volume from 400 to 1200 µL (Fig. 1A and B). Higher disperser volumes are expected to facilitate more complete emulsification and finer dispersion of extraction solvent droplets throughout the plasma matrix. The positive regression coefficient (+ 3.57) confirms this enhancement, though the negative quadratic term (A² = −2.38) suggests diminishing returns at excessive volumes, likely attributable to increased analyte solubility in the acetonitrile-enriched aqueous phase. Centrifugation time exhibited negligible influence on both responses across the 3–10 min range (Fig. 1A and B), consistent with the favorable density differential between ethyl acetate (approximately 0.90 g/mL) and the aqueous plasma matrix (approximately 1.03 g/mL), which promotes rapid spontaneous coalescence of dispersed organic droplets into a distinct upper layer.

Interactive effects and response surface optimization

Beyond individual factor effects, significant two-factor interactions were identified, revealing synergistic relationships that substantially influenced extraction performance (Fig. 2). The AB interaction (disperser × extraction volume) demonstrated the highest significance among all interactions (F = 87.66 for recovery, 116.74 for CV%), with a positive regression coefficient (+ 2.95) for recovery and negative coefficient (− 2.59) for CV%, indicating simultaneous enhancement of extraction efficiency and precision. The response surfaces (Fig. 2A for recovery, Fig. 2D for CV%) exhibited characteristic curvature with optimal performance achieved at simultaneously high levels of both factors. This synergistic behavior suggests that adequate disperser volume is likely essential for effective emulsification of larger extraction solvent volumes — at low disperser volumes, increasing extraction solvent may fail to proportionally enhance recovery due to potentially insufficient droplet formation, whereas at high disperser volumes the benefit of additional extraction solvent is more fully realized.

Three-dimensional response surface plots depicting two-factor interactions on extraction recovery (A-C) and coefficient of variation (D-E): (A) disperser volume × extraction solvent volume, (B) disperser volume × pH, (C) extraction solvent volume × pH for recovery; (D) disperser volume × extraction solvent volume, and (E) disperser volume × pH for coefficient of variation.

The AC interaction (disperser × pH, F = 46.47 for recovery, 7.55 for CV%) indicated that pH effects become increasingly pronounced at higher disperser volumes, suggesting a cooperative relationship between ionization state control and emulsification efficiency (Fig. 2B and E). The positive coefficient (+ 2.15) for recovery and negative coefficient (− 0.6578) for CV% confirm simultaneous improvement in both responses when these factors are optimized together. The BC interaction (extraction volume × pH, F = 68.96) demonstrated that maximum recovery requires simultaneous optimization of both factors (Fig. 2C), reflecting the combined influence of organic phase capacity and ionization state control — at alkaline pH where the neutral sotalol species predominates, larger extraction volumes more effectively capture the lipophilic analyte, whereas at acidic pH where the protonated form dominates, increasing extraction volume provides comparatively limited benefit.

Numerical optimization using the desirability function approach simultaneously maximized recovery while minimizing CV% (Fig. S2), identifying optimal conditions of disperser volume 1200 µL, extraction solvent volume 300 µL, pH 10.0, and centrifugation time 3 min. At these settings, the predicted extraction recovery was 95.29% with CV% of 4.16%, representing an excellent balance between extraction efficiency and method precision. Experimental verification under these conditions confirmed close agreement with predicted values, demonstrating the reliability of the CCD-RSM approach for DLLME method development and optimization.

Method development and optimization

Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 2.7 μm), whose embedded polar group chemistry minimized secondary interactions with protonated amines, reducing peak tailing commonly observed for basic compounds on conventional C18 columns. Isocratic elution with water: acetonitrile (70:30, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid delivered baseline separation within a 4-minute runtime, with the acidic modifier simultaneously enhancing electrospray ionization efficiency and maintaining adequate chromatographic retention for both analytes. Atenolol was selected as the internal standard based on its close structural similarity to sotalol — both compounds share an identical para-substituted phenoxy core and aminopropanol side chain, differing only in their para-substituents, and exhibit comparable hydrophilicity and pH-dependent extraction behaviour, ensuring consistent compensation for matrix-induced variability throughout the analytical procedure. Mass spectrometric detection employed positive ESI-MRM, with sotalol monitored via the structurally specific m/z 273.1→255.1 transition corresponding to neutral loss of water, and atenolol via the fully resolved m/z 267.2→145.1 transition, eliminating any risk of cross-interference between analyte and internal standard channels.

Representative chromatograms demonstrated excellent sensitivity and peak shape (Fig. 3). Sotalol at the LLOQ (1 ng/mL) exhibited a well-defined peak at 2.91 min with minimal baseline noise (Fig. 3A), while atenolol at 100 ng/mL displayed a sharp, symmetric peak at 2.07 min (Fig. 3B), both with signal-to-noise ratios exceeding 10:1, confirming adequate sensitivity for pharmacokinetic applications. The earlier elution of atenolol relative to sotalol is consistent with its lower lipophilicity, attributable to the more polar para-aminophenylacetamide side chain compared to the methanesulfonamide group of sotalol, resulting in reduced retention on the EC-C18 stationary phase under the employed reversed-phase isocratic conditions.

Method validation

The developed DLLME-LC-MS/MS method was comprehensively validated according to ICH M10 bioanalytical method validation guidelines to ensure reliability and suitability for quantitative determination of sotalol in human plasma42. Validation parameters evaluated included selectivity, linearity, sensitivity (lower limit of quantification), accuracy, precision (intraday and interday), extraction recovery, matrix effect, and stability under various storage and handling conditions relevant to clinical sample analysis.

Selectivity

Method selectivity was evaluated by analyzing blank human plasma from six different sources to assess potential interference from endogenous compounds at the retention times and MRM transitions of sotalol and atenolol. Representative chromatograms of blank plasma demonstrated absence of interfering peaks in both the sotalol quantifier transition (273.1 → 255.1, Fig. S3A) and atenolol internal standard transition (267.2 → 145.1, Fig. S3B). The chromatograms exhibited only baseline noise without discernible peaks at the respective retention times of 2.91 min for sotalol and 2.07 min for atenolol, confirming that endogenous plasma components did not compromise analyte detection or quantification.

Linearity and sensitivity

Calibration curves were constructed using eight concentration levels ranging from 1 to 1200 ng/mL, encompassing the expected therapeutic range of sotalol. Linear regression with 1/x² weighting yielded excellent linearity with coefficient of determination (r²) of 0.9994 (Table 2). The regression equation demonstrated slope of 0.0048 ± 0.00005 and intercept of 0.00004 ± 0.0001, indicating minimal systematic bias. The lower limit of quantification was established at 1 ng/mL with precision of 7.4% CV and signal-to-noise ratio exceeding 10:1, meeting ICH M10 guideline requirements. This sensitivity is adequate for pharmacokinetic studies and therapeutic monitoring applications where sotalol plasma concentrations typically range from 0.5 to 4.0 µg/mL.

Accuracy and precision

Intraday and interday accuracy and precision were assessed by analyzing quality control samples at four concentration levels: LLOQ QC (1 ng/mL), LQC (3 ng/mL), MQC (600 ng/mL), and HQC (1000 ng/mL) in six replicates across three independent analytical runs (Table 3). Intraday accuracy ranged from − 10.92% at LLOQ to + 3.35% at HQC, while intraday precision ranged from 5.72% at HQC to 10.51% at LLOQ, with all values meeting the ± 15% (± 20% at LLOQ) acceptance criteria established by ICH M10 guidelines. Interday accuracy ranged from − 1.33% to + 6.00%, while interday precision ranged from 6.36% to 15.39%, demonstrating excellent method robustness and reproducibility. The slightly higher variability at LLOQ reflects expected increased variability at the lower quantification limit but remains within acceptance criteria. These results confirm reliable quantification of sotalol across the entire calibration range under both within-run and between-run conditions, establishing suitability for routine pharmacokinetic analysis and therapeutic drug monitoring.

Recovery and matrix effect

Extraction recovery and matrix effect were evaluated at three QC levels to assess the efficiency of the DLLME procedure and potential ion suppression or enhancement from plasma matrix components (Table S3). Mean extraction recovery values were 99.54 ± 4.91% at LQC, 102.92 ± 5.36% at MQC, and 99.05 ± 6.37% at HQC, demonstrating consistently high and reproducible extraction efficiency across the concentration range. The recovery values approaching 100% with precision (CV%) below 7% confirm the effectiveness of optimized DLLME parameters, particularly alkaline pH adjustment to convert sotalol to its neutral, lipophilic form that partitions favorably into ethyl acetate. Matrix effect was assessed using six independent plasma lots at LQC and HQC levels in three replicates each, in full compliance with ICH M10 requirements42. Matrix effect values were 97.47 ± 9.61% at LQC, 91.57 ± 3.75% at MQC, and 97.22 ± 9.47% at HQC, all falling within the 85–115% acceptance range, indicating minimal ion suppression or enhancement. This is attributed to efficient removal of phospholipids and proteins by DLLME coupled with use of a structurally similar internal standard. Evaluation of hemolyzed and lipemic plasma matrices was not performed within the current validation scope, as ICH M10 guidelines specify the use of non-haemolysed and non-lipaemic sources for matrix assessment42. Future investigations are recommended to evaluate method performance under these conditions to further establish robustness for routine clinical implementation.

Stability

Comprehensive stability assessment was conducted under various storage and handling conditions relevant to clinical sample analysis (Table S4). Short-term stability at room temperature for 24 h yielded recoveries of 93.62% (CV 3.05%) at LQC and 92.21% (CV 1.45%) at HQC. Refrigerated storage stability at 2–8 °C for 24 h showed recoveries of 98.53% (CV 6.76%) at LQC and 101.25% (CV 0.47%) at HQC. Post-preparative stability in the autosampler at 10 °C for 48 h demonstrated recoveries of 96.25% (CV 1.99%) at LQC and 95.53% (CV 2.38%) at HQC. Freeze-thaw stability over three cycles at -70 °C yielded recoveries of 93.70% (CV 1.85%) at LQC and 92.16% (CV 7.05%) at HQC. Long-term storage stability at -70 °C for 40 days showed recoveries of 92.23% (CV 8.08%) at LQC and 92.18% (CV 5.06%) at HQC. All stability conditions met the acceptance criteria of 85–115% recovery with CV% ≤ 15%, confirming method robustness for routine clinical sample analysis.

Pharmacokinetic Study Application

The validated DLLME-LC-MS/MS method was successfully applied to a pharmacokinetic study following single oral administration of 80 mg sotalol hydrochloride in five healthy volunteers. Plasma concentration-time profiles demonstrated characteristic absorption and elimination phases (Fig. 4), with individual subject variability typical of single-dose pharmacokinetic investigations. The mean plasma concentration-time curve exhibited rapid absorption with peak concentrations achieved within the first few hours post-dosing, followed by gradual elimination over the 48-hour sampling period. Individual concentration-time profiles showed some inter-subject variability in both absorption and elimination phases, likely attributable to physiological differences in gastrointestinal absorption, distribution, and renal clearance among participants.

Individual and mean plasma concentration-time profiles following single oral administration of 80 mg sotalol in five healthy volunteers over 48 h. Pharmacokinetic parameters calculated using non-compartmental analysis are summarized in Table 4. The mean maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) was 701 ± 53 ng/mL (CV 7.68%), achieved at median tmax of 3 h (range 2–5 h). These values demonstrate excellent agreement with literature reports for 80 mg oral sotalol, where Deneer et al. documented Cmax of 580 ± 150 ng/mL with tmax of 2.79 h43, and Dutch bioequivalence studies reported Cmax ranging from 781 to 852 ng/mL with tmax of 1.5–5.0 h44. The median tmax of 3 h falls within the expected 2–4 h absorption window characteristic of sotalol’s near-complete oral bioavailability (90–100%)45.

The elimination half-life (t1/2) was 10.1 ± 1.0 h (CV 9.86%), consistent with published values of 7–15 h in subjects with normal renal function45. Kimura et al. reported stereoselective half-lives of 7.2–8.5 h for (-)-sotalol and 9.1–11.4 h for (+)-sotalol following racemic administration46, with our observed value falling within the expected range for total racemic measurement. Apparent oral clearance (CL/F) was 6.9 ± 0.88 L/h (CV 12.78%) and apparent volume of distribution (Vz/F) was 101.9 ± 21.9 L (CV 21.49%), corresponding to 1.46 L/kg for a 70 kg individual. These values are consistent with sotalol’s pharmacokinetic characteristics, with Vz/F falling in the middle of the reported 1.2–2.4 L/kg range45 and CL/F matching stereoselective enantiomer clearances of 6.1–7.6 L/h46. The elimination rate constant (λz) was 0.07 ± 0.01 h⁻¹ (CV 9.56%), and mean residence time (MRT) was 14.2 ± 0.76 h (CV 5.37%). These parameters are in line with sotalol’s negligible plasma protein binding, minimal hepatic metabolism, and predominantly renal elimination pathway with 80–90% excreted unchanged in urine7,8. The successful application of the validated method to this preliminary pharmacokinetic investigation supports its potential suitability for therapeutic drug monitoring and clinical pharmacokinetic applications. It should be noted, however, that the present pharmacokinetic dataset was derived from a small cohort of five healthy volunteers, which is appropriate for preliminary demonstration of method applicability but limits the generalizability of the reported parameters. Future investigations employing larger and more clinically diverse populations, including patients with varying degrees of renal impairment for whom sotalol therapeutic drug monitoring is most critically indicated, are warranted to more comprehensively characterize inter-individual pharmacokinetic variability and to fully establish the method’s clinical utility.

Greenness and sustainability assessment

The environmental impact, sustainability, and reliability profiles of the developed DLLME-LC-MS/MS method were comprehensively evaluated using seven complementary assessment tools: Analytical Green Star Area for Sample Preparation (AGSA-prep)47, Analytical Green Star Area (AGSA)48, Carbon Footprint Reduction Index (CaFRI)49, Blue Applicability Grade Index (BAGI)50, Click Analytical Chemistry Index (CACI)51, Analytical Method Reliability Index (AMRI)52, and Whiteness Evaluation for Chemical Analysis (WECA)53(Fig. 5).

Comprehensive greenness, sustainability, and reliability assessment of the developed DLLME-LC-MS/MS method: (A) Analytical Green Star Area for Sample Preparation (AGSA-prep, 64/100) evaluating DLLME procedure greenness; (B) Analytical Green Star Area (AGSA, 65.28/100) assessing overall method greenness across all 12 principles of Green Analytical Chemistry; (C) Carbon Footprint Reduction Index (CaFRI, 69/100) reflecting good carbon footprint performance; (D) Blue Applicability Grade Index (BAGI, 75.0/100) confirming high practical applicability; (E) Click Analytical Chemistry Index (CACI, 69/100) with notable advantages for sample size and analytical sensitivity; (F) Analytical Method Reliability Index (AMRI, 71/100) confirming good overall method reliability; and (G) Whiteness Evaluation for Chemical Analysis (WECA, 81%) with domain scores of 92% for analytical performance (Red), 80% for environmental impact (Green), and 71% for practical efficiency (Blue).

Since sample preparation constitutes the most environmentally burdensome step in bioanalytical workflows, AGSA-prep was applied to specifically evaluate the greenness of the DLLME procedure, yielding a score of 64/100 (Fig. 5A). This moderate-to-good score reflects the favorable environmental characteristics of the optimized DLLME procedure, particularly the use of low-toxicity ethyl acetate, minimal plasma volume (400 µL), and reduced waste generation compared to conventional SPE or LLE approaches. Areas identified for improvement included multi-step manual operation, waste generation, and energy consumption associated with LC-MS/MS instrumentation. To complement this sample preparation-focused evaluation, overall method greenness was assessed using AGSA, which evaluates adherence to all 12 principles of GAC through a star-area diagram with built-in scoring, yielding a score of 65.28/100 (Fig. 5B). Favorable performance was demonstrated for avoidance of derivatization, operator safety, and sample miniaturization, while moderate performance was observed for multi-step sample treatment and use of petroleum-derived reagents. Collectively, both tools highlight the environmental advantages of DLLME over conventional extraction techniques while consistently identifying energy consumption and multi-step operation as primary areas for future methodological improvement.

Carbon footprint evaluation using CaFRI, selected to address the energy consumption dimension not fully captured by greenness pictograms, yielded a score of 69/100 (Fig. 5C), reflecting good overall sustainability performance. Excellent performance was demonstrated for waste management, low organic solvent consumption, and single analyst operation, while the inherent energy demands of LC-MS/MS instrumentation and transportation-related emissions represented the primary areas for improvement. Solvent recycling and adoption of renewable energy sources are recommended as future mitigation strategies.

Practical applicability was assessed through complementary application of BAGI and CACI, since these tools address distinct but complementary dimensions of practicality. BAGI yielded a score of 75.0/100 (Fig. 5D), substantially exceeding the practicality threshold of 60, with high compliance demonstrated for quantitative confirmatory analysis, commercially available reagents, miniaturized sample preparation, and optimal bioanalytical sample volume. Limitations were identified for single-analyte scope and specialized instrumentation requirements. Since BAGI does not explicitly address affordability and method accessibility, CACI was additionally applied, yielding a score of 69/100 (Fig. 5E). CACI confirmed that the method demonstrates notable advantages in terms of small sample size requirements and high analytical sensitivity, while moderate performance was observed for automation, portability, and preparation complexity. The combined BAGI-CACI assessment confirms strong overall practical applicability of the method, with instrumentation cost and single-analyte focus representing the primary practical constraints.

To provide a standardized quantitative evaluation of method reliability beyond conventional validation reporting, AMRI was applied, yielding a score of 71/100 (Fig. 5F). This score reflects good overall method reliability, with excellent performance demonstrated for linearity, accuracy, and real sample application, and moderate performance observed for robustness and stability parameters. This confirms that the method demonstrates reliability exceeding minimum ICH M10 acceptance criteria, supporting its suitability for routine clinical implementation.

Finally, holistic whiteness assessment integrating analytical performance, environmental impact, and practical efficiency simultaneously was performed using WECA, yielding an overall whiteness score of 81% (Fig. 5G). Excellent analytical performance (Red domain: 92%) was consistent with the robust validation outcomes confirmed by AMRI. Strong environmental performance (Green domain: 80%) corroborated the environmental benefits identified by AGSA and CaFRI. Moderate practical efficiency (Blue domain: 71%) reflected the instrumentation demands inherent to LC-MS/MS bioanalysis, consistent with CACI findings, demonstrating excellent concordance across all applied assessment frameworks. Future comprehensive sustainability evaluation using additional emerging tools, including the Sustainability of Analytical Methods Index (SAMI)54, Environmental Performance and Practicality Index (EPPI)55, Red Analytical Performance Index (RAPI)56, Modified Green Analytical Procedure Index (MoGAPI)57, and Green Environmental Assessment and Rating for Solvents (GEARS58, is recommended to further characterize the broader sustainability dimensions of the developed method, particularly with respect to SDG alignment, solvent safety, and analytical performance benchmarking.

Comparison with reported literature methods

The analytical performance of the developed DLLME-LC-MS/MS method was systematically compared with previously reported sotalol determination methods to evaluate its competitiveness and practical advantages (Table S5). Bartek et al. developed an HPLC-FLD method employing SPE on C8 cartridges with elution using ethyl acetate-acetonitrile mixtures, demonstrating linearity over 10–6000 ng/mL with an absolute extraction recovery of 76.0% from 1000 µL plasma and LLOQ of 10 ng/mL, though the method required prolonged analysis times of approximately 18 min and consumed substantial volumes of organic solvents during the multi-step SPE procedure12. Boutagy and Shenfield described a simplified HPLC-FLD procedure employing single-step protein precipitation with perchloric acid from 300 µL plasma, achieving near-quantitative recovery (> 98%) with a linearity range of 160–10,000 ng/mL and LLOQ of 80 ng/mL, offering rapid sample preparation but limited sensitivity that restricts application primarily to therapeutic drug monitoring at higher plasma concentrations11. Chiap et al. developed a fully automated HPLC-UV method employing on-line dialysis coupled with cation-exchange trace enrichment pre-column, demonstrating LLOQ of 25 ng/mL from 370 µL plasma with a mean recovery of 60.4%, though the method required specialized ASTED automated dialysis instrumentation and prolonged analysis times of 19 min per sample17. Rbeida et al. subsequently improved upon this approach using restricted access material with cation exchange properties for direct plasma injection, achieving substantially improved LLOQ of 5 ng/mL from only 100 µL plasma with near-complete recovery (~ 96%) within a comparable 19 min runtime, though requiring specialized RAM-based column switching infrastructure15.

LC-MS/MS approaches have emerged as the current gold standard for sotalol quantification. Li et al. reported simultaneous determination of ten antiarrhythmic drugs including sotalol using simple protein precipitation with acetonitrile from 100 µL plasma, achieving a linearity range of 20–4000 ng/mL with LLOQ of 20 ng/mL and recovery of 85–99.7% within 7.5 min10. While protein precipitation offers procedural simplicity, it provides insufficient cleanup of phospholipids and endogenous matrix components, resulting in potential ion suppression effects that may compromise quantitation accuracy and instrument longevity23. Zarghi et al. employed ion-pair HPLC with monolithic column and fluorescence detection following protein precipitation from 500 µL plasma, achieving linearity over 20–1500 ng/mL with recovery of 96.5% and LLOQ of 10 ng/mL within a rapid 6 min runtime, though fluorescence detection demonstrates inferior selectivity compared to mass spectrometry and the ion-pair approach introduces additional mobile phase complexity19. Goryński et al. developed an innovative SPME-LC-MS/MS method for eight beta-blockers including sotalol using thin-film SPME with HLB-coated extraction phases in 96-well format from 1200 µL plasma, achieving linearity over 1–1000 ng/mL with LLOQ of 1.15 ng/mL, though absolute recovery for sotalol was limited to approximately 50% and the method required specialized thin-film SPME devices and extended extraction times of 75 min22.

The present DLLME-LC-MS/MS method demonstrates several distinct advantages over existing approaches, as summarized in Table S5. Utilizing a reduced plasma volume of 400 µL, the method achieves high extraction recovery of 99–103% — substantially superior to SPE (76%), dialysis-based (60.4%), and SPME-based (~ 50%) approaches — with minimal organic solvent consumption of 1.5 mL per sample, excellent sensitivity (LLOQ 1 ng/mL), wide linearity range (1–1200 ng/mL), and rapid analysis time of 4 min. The systematic CCD-RSM optimization of DLLME parameters ensures maximum extraction efficiency while employing ethyl acetate as a greener alternative to halogenated solvents. Furthermore, the comprehensive multi-metric sustainability assessment conducted in the present study, encompassing AGSA-prep (64/100), AGSA (65.28/100), CaFRI (69/100), BAGI (75.0/100), CACI (69/100), AMRI (71/100), and WECA (81%), provides a more comprehensive environmental characterization than previously reported sotalol bioanalytical methods, none of which incorporated greenness or sustainability evaluation.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated an LC-MS/MS method coupled with CCD-optimized DLLME for sotalol quantification in human plasma. Systematic optimization using central composite design established optimal conditions of disperser volume 1200 µL, extraction solvent 300 µL, pH 10.0, and centrifugation time 3 min, achieving extraction recovery of 99–103% with reduced organic solvent consumption of 1.5 mL per sample compared to conventional SPE and LLE approaches. The method demonstrated excellent analytical performance meeting all ICH M10 acceptance criteria for linearity (1–1200 ng/mL, r² = 0.9994), sensitivity (LLOQ 1 ng/mL), accuracy (− 1.33% to + 6.00%), precision, and stability under multiple clinically relevant storage conditions.

Preliminary pharmacokinetic application in five healthy volunteers following single oral 80 mg sotalol administration yielded parameters broadly consistent with published literature values (Cmax 701 ± 53.8 ng/mL, t½ 10.1 ± 1.0 h, AUC0→∞ 11,713 ± 1,345 ng·h/mL), supporting the method’s potential suitability for therapeutic drug monitoring, though larger-scale investigations in clinically diverse populations are recommended to fully establish its utility. Comprehensive sustainability evaluation using seven complementary metrics (AGSA-prep 64/100, AGSA 65.28/100, CaFRI 69/100, BAGI 75.0/100, CACI 69/100, AMRI 71/100, WECA 81%) demonstrated moderate-to-good environmental performance relative to conventional extraction approaches, with reduced solvent consumption and elimination of halogenated solvents. The developed method offers an environmentally improved approach for sotalol bioanalysis, with potential applicability to other β-adrenergic blocking drugs sharing similar physicochemical properties, and future work should explore multi-analyte extension and microsampling techniques for point-of-care therapeutic drug monitoring applications.
