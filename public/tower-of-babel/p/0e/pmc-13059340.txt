The effectiveness of robotic therapy in reducing upper limb spasticity in stroke survivors: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Salvatore Facciorusso, Alberto Esquenazi, Franco Molteni, Stefania Spina, Andrea Santamato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRobot-assisted therapy (RAT) represents an evolving approach to post-stroke upper limb rehabilitation; however, its impact on spasticity modification remains inadequately characterized. Critical knowledge gaps persist due to the diversity of robotic technologies, heterogeneous treatment protocols, and methodological limitations in outcome reporting, particularly the conflation of proximal and distal spasticity measures. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the comparative effectiveness of robotic versus conventional interventions for upper limb spasticity management, examining the influence of robotic design, stroke stage, and treatment dosage on spasticity outcomes.MethodsWe searched six databases and two trial registries up to November 2024 for RCTs of RAT versus non-robotic comparators in adults with post-stroke upper limb spasticity. Secondary outcomes included upper-limb motor function, activities of daily living, and health-related quality of life. We used random (I2 ≥ 50%) or fixed effects meta-analysis to synthesize results, with subgroup analyses for stroke phase, device type, and anatomical region. The certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE.ResultsTwenty-nine RCTs (n = 1,577) were included. Overall, RAT produced a small but statistically significant reduction in elbow flexor spasticity on the Modified Ashworth Scale (MD − 0.25; 95% CI [− 0.40 to − 0.10]). This reduction was observed primarily in trials conducted in the chronic phase. Wrist flexor spasticity was also reduced (MD = − 0.22; 95% CI −0.37 to − 0.08). Reduction was also significant in studies using multi-joint devices (SMD − 0.26; 95% CI [− 0.43 to − 0.08]). In contrast, no significant reduction was found for shoulder adductor spasticity or for hand-only devices. RAT also improved motor function with Fugl-Meyer Assessment MD 1.97; 95% CI [0.83 to 3.12] but did not consistently improve ADL and quality of life. Importantly, there was no significant difference in the odds of treatment-related adverse events (OR 1.18; 95% CI [0.53 to 2.66]).ConclusionsThis systematic review provides evidence that RAT is a safe modality that can modestly reduce elbow and wrist flexor spasticity in post-stroke patients. The benefit is specific, appearing more robust with multi-joint devices and in the chronic phase of recovery. The primary value of RAT seems to be in safely delivering high-intensity motor practice rather than acting as a first-line anti-spasticity treatment.Registration PROSPERO CRD 42024615294.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-01925-8.

Introduction

Stroke remains the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability world-wide. The 2024 Global Burden of Disease update estimated 12.2 million first-ever strokes and 6.55 million deaths in 2023, with an age-standardized incidence of 153 per 100 000 person-years [1, 2]. Upper limb impairment affects approximately 50–80% of acute stroke survivors and persists in nearly half of patients six months post-stroke, during the chronic phase. Furthermore, between one-fifth and two-fifths of survivors (20–43%) dvelop clinically significant upper-limb spasticity within the first year [3, 4].

Spasticity is a component of the upper motor neuron syndrome and is classically defined as a velocity-dependent increase in tonic stretch reflexes with exaggerated tendon jerks, reflecting stretch-reflex hyperexcitability [5]. Clinically significant abnormal tone contributes to reduced active range of motion, pain, and limitations in activities of daily living, including dressing, eating, and personal care [6, 7].

Upper limb rehabilitation after stroke is particularly challenging, as spasticity, weakness, and loss of dexterity converge to limit functional independence. Robotic devices have been introduced as an adjunct to conventional therapy to address upper-limb rehabilitation [8]. These devices deliver computer-controlled, repetitive, goal-oriented arm movements with precisely adjustable speed, torque and trajectory [9]. Two main categories of robots dominate clinical practice: end-effector, which apply forces at a distal interface, and exoskeletons, which align with anatomical joints to guide multi-segment motion [8]. The primary advantages of robotic devices over conventional therapy lie in their ability to deliver thousands of task-specific repetitions per session—an order of magnitude higher than what is typically feasible—while logging kinematic and kinetic data in real-time to track and guide objective progression [10, 11].

The integration of robotic therapy into stroke rehabilitation services is accelerating, supported by its potential to provide high-intensity, consistent training [12]. However, evidence regarding its specific effects on spasticity remains inconclusive [13]. A focused systematic review is needed for several reasons: first, prior meta-analyses of upper-limb robotics have centered on general motor impairment (e.g., using the Fugl-Meyer Assessment) rather than spasticity [13, 14] and none has treated spasticity as a primary endpoint; second, although spasticity has occasionally been reported as a secondary outcome, it has been analyzed in a pooled and unspecific manner, without distinguishing between upper-limb segments or considering whether the robotic intervention directly targeted the assessed muscles [15]; third, the influence of protocol-related variables (e.g., device characteristics, timing post-stroke) on spasticity outcomes remains unexplored. Given the rapid diversification of robotic platforms and their high capital cost, a rigorous synthesis of the evidence is essential to guide clinical decision-making, justify health-service investment, and direct future research in post-stroke spasticity management. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess (1) the efficacy of upper limb robotic rehabilitation on upper limb spasticity compared to conventional rehabilitation, usual care or other technologies; (2) the influence of robotic device characteristics, intervention delivery features and time since stroke on rehabilitation outcomes when these were sufficiently and consistently reported; (3) the safety profile of robotic rehabilitation by documenting adverse events in this population; and (4) the impact on upper limb motor function, activities, and quality of life in stroke survivors with spasticity.

Methods

This systematic review was registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD 42024615294) and carried out according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) [16] ​​guidelines. Any deviations from the published protocol will be transparently reported in the manuscript.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was executed in MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE, Scopus, Web of Science, CINAHL and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from inception to 17 November 2024, in English language. Search strategies combined Medical Subject Headings and free-text terms for stroke, spasticity, and robotic rehabilitation; the full strategies are provided in the Additional file 1. Trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP) additionally device-manufacturer websites were searched to identify unpublished or ongoing studies.

Eligibility criteria

We included randomized controlled trials (parallel-group or cross-over) that enrolled adults (≥ 18 years) with clinically or instrumentally confirmed ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke and upper-limb spasticity documented via validated scales (e.g., Modified Ashworth Scale, Tardieu Scale). Trials in mixed neurological populations were eligible if stroke-specific outcomes could be obtained directly from study authors. The intervention had to consist of robotic-assisted upper-limb therapy—either end-effector or exoskeleton devices—providing active, active-assistive, or resistive movements. We considered as eligible not only powered robots but also instrumented, non-motorized exoskeleton systems providing configurable assistance through gravity compensation (e.g., spring-based arm-weight support). Comparators included conventional physiotherapy or occupational therapy, alternative rehabilitation technologies, or usual care without robotic input. The primary outcome was the change in spasticity at treatment end (within one week of the final session); secondary outcomes included motor function, activities of daily living, pain, adverse events, and health-related quality of life.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers (S.S., S.F.) independently screened titles and abstracts using Rayyan [17] and then assessed full texts against eligibility criteria; discrepancies were resolved by discussion or third-party adjudication (A.S.). Full-text articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded, and the specific reasons for exclusion were recorded and categorized (Supplementary Table S2). Data were extracted in duplicate into a piloted Excel form. Extracted variables were selected a priori to support planned subgroup/sensitivity analyses and transparent reporting. Specifically, Table 1 summarizes study setting and population descriptors (sample size, time since stroke, age, setting, country) and device descriptors (robot name, robot architecture, robot target anatomical joints). Table 2 summarizes intervention delivery (experimental and comparator protocol; session dose/frequency), and outcome domains (MAS by joint; composite/unspecified tone scores; motor/ADL/QoL measures; adverse events). The severity of baseline spasticity, categorized by study group and joint muscle, is detailed in Supplementary Table S3. Intervention protocols were extracted when reported and summarized in Supplementary Table S4 (procedures, who provided, when and how much, tailoring); however, due to incomplete and non-standardized reporting across trials, these variables were generally not suitable for quantitative meta-analysis and were primarily synthesized descriptively.

Risk-of-bias assessment

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane ‘Risk of bias’ tool as described in the Cochrane Handbook (version 5.2.0, Chap. 8) [20]. The seven standard domains evaluated were: (1) random sequence generation; (2) allocation concealment; (3) blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias); (4) blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias); (5) incomplete outcome data (attrition bias); (6) selective outcome reporting (reporting bias); and (7) other sources of bias. For each domain, we provided support for judgement based on the study report (and protocols/registrations when available) and assigned a judgement of low, high, or unclear risk of bias according to Cochrane guidance [20, 21]; ‘unclear’ was used when reporting was insufficient to permit a judgement or when there was uncertainty regarding the potential for bias. Consistent with Cochrane recommendations, domains related to blinding and incomplete outcome data were considered at the level of the relevant outcome (or outcome class) where appropriate. Two reviewers performed assessments independently, with discrepancies resolved by consensus and judgments were visualized in RevMan 5.4.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted in RevMan 5.4, calculating mean difference (MD) for outcomes measured on the same scale and Hedges’ g–corrected standardized mean difference (SMD) when different instruments were pooled. Study weights were assigned using the inverse-variance method in RevMan. A DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model was chosen a priori to accommodate anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity [22], with fixed-effect inverse-variance models used only when trials were judged sufficiently homogeneous. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed by chi-square tests, Tau-square estimates, and the I² statistic (with I² ≥ 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity).

In accordance with the principles outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [23], to investigate sources of heterogeneity and explore clinically relevant differences, we first performed separate meta-analyses for spasticity at specific anatomical locations, including elbow flexors, wrist flexors, and shoulder adductors, as measured by MAS. For studies that reported either a composite MAS score (calculated sum or average of MAS values across multiple joints) or a non–joint-specific MAS outcome (without joint-specific data), a separate analysis of “unspecified” spasticity was conducted. Because MAS is inherently joint- and muscle-group–specific, trials were excluded from target-specific syntheses if MAS was not reported for the relevant joint or if spasticity was reported only using non–joint-specific or composite/summed metrics, as these measures do not allow attribution of tone changes to the joint(s) targeted by the intervention.

Primary analyses were stratified by anatomical device class and by delivery mode. Anatomical device class analyses compared multi-joint shoulder–elbow–wrist devices with hand-only devices. Shoulder–elbow–wrist devices were defined as systems enabling upper-limb practice that includedshoulder, elbow and wrist movements, whereas hand-only devices primarily targeted finger and hand training. Delivery mode analyses distinguished replacement designs, in which robot-assisted therapy (RAT) was compared directly with conventional therapy (CT), from adjunct designs, in which RAT was provided in addition to CT (CT + RAT vs. CT).

To explore clinically plausible sources of residual heterogeneity, we pre-specified subgroup analyses, which were undertaken only when each subgroup included at least two studies. Subgroup analyses were performed according to stroke phase (subacute: 1 week to 5 months; chronic: ≥6 months post-stroke) [24]. Within each anatomical device class, robot architecture subgroups (exoskeleton vs. end-effector) were explored.

Furthermore, to isolate the specific effect of the robotic technology from the effect of increased therapy time, a distinct meta-analysis was conducted including only dose-matched trials, where the total duration and frequency of therapy were equivalent between the robotic and control groups. Publication bias was examined with funnel plots when at least ten studies contributed to an outcome [25]. Sensitivity analyses were planned to assess the influence of high-risk-of-bias studies on the overall effect estimate.

Certainty of evidence

The GRADE approach [26] was used to appraise the certainty of evidence for each outcome across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Evidence was initially rated as high (randomized trials) and downgraded as appropriate. When rating certainty using GRADE, we considered clinical heterogeneity in intervention delivery (protocol content and dose, stand-alone vs. add-on implementation, and co-interventions) as a potential contributor to inconsistency/indirectness and documented downgrading decisions accordingly. Summary-of-findings tables were prepared using GRADEpro GDT.

Results

Study selection

This systematic review identified 1,304 potentially relevant records. Following deduplication, which eliminated 520 records, 784 citations underwent initial screening. Subsequently, 680 records were excluded based on title and abstract screening. Full-text assessment was conducted for 104 articles, of which 80 were excluded. Full-text reports were excluded for predefined eligibility reasons, most commonly because the comparison was not eligible (NC, n = 26; e.g., device-based therapy delivered in both arms or an uninformative comparator), the study design did not meet inclusion criteria (ND, n = 24; including pilot studies; e.g., non-randomized, uncontrolled, or otherwise ineligible designs), or the intervention was not eligible (NI, n = 17; e.g., non-robotic interventions and/or lower-limb or gait-focused robotics outside upper-limb rehabilitation). Additional exclusions occurred because spasticity outcomes were not reported (NO, n = 9; e.g., no Modified Ashworth Scale or Tardieu-derived measure), because the report was protocol-stage (PR, n = 3), or because the study aim was not aligned with the review question (NA, n = 1). A complete list of excluded full-text reports and primary reasons is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Finally, 29 studies met all inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the final review. The flow of studies through the review process is presented in the PRISMA diagram (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of studies

A total of 29 RCTs, involving 1,577 randomized participants, were included in this review. These studies were published between 2005 and 2024, with sample sizes ranging from 18 to 215 participants. Geographically, Italy contributed the largest proportion of studies (20.69%), followed by Korea (17.24%) and the United States (13.79%). Among the included studies, 34.48% involved inpatients, 13.79% involved outpatients, and the setting was not reported in 51.72% of the studies.

All studies included adult participants, with 15 studies focusing on individuals in the subacute stage [27–41], 13 on those in the chronic stage [42–54], and 1 study involving a mixed population (both subacute and chronic) [55].

A wide array of robotic devices was utilized, ranging from proximal shoulder-elbow end-effector robots [39, 52] and complex 3D exoskeletons [46] to distal devices focusing on the hand and wrist [44, 48, 56].

In 16 studies [27, 29, 31–33, 35, 40, 42, 44–49, 52, 53], RAT was directly compared against a non-robotic, therapist-led conventional therapy (replacement protocols). In the remaining 13 studies [28, 30, 34, 36, 37, 39, 41, 43, 50, 51, 54–56], RAT was provided in addition to a baseline of conventional therapy that was also received by the control group (adjunct protocols).

Further details on the clinical characteristics of the included populations are available in Table 1 and detailed descriptions of the interventions and outcomes are provided in Table 2 and supplementary table S4.

Risk of bias in included studies

Risk of bias across the 29 included trials is summarized in Figs. 2 and 3. For random sequence generation, 79.3% of studies were judged at low risk and 20.7% at unclear risk. For allocation concealment, 44.8% were judged at low risk and 55.2% at unclear risk due to insufficient reporting of concealment procedures, leaving uncertainty regarding selection bias. Performance bias was judged at high risk in 100% of trials because blinding of participants and therapists is generally not feasible in rehabilitation interventions. For blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias), 72.4% of studies were judged at low risk, 20.7% at high risk, and 6.9% at unclear risk. For incomplete outcome data (attrition bias), 89.7% were judged at low risk and 10.3% at high risk. For selective reporting, 93.1% were judged at low risk and 6.9% at unclear risk. For other bias, 58.6% were judged at low risk, 24.1% at unclear risk, and 17.2% at high risk, mainly due to baseline imbalances, small sample sizes, and/or insufficient methodological detail.

Effects of interventions

Twenty-eight of the studies included provided data for quantitative analysis. In one study [55] outcome data for MAS were not reported and authors did not provide missing data. The main results of MAS are detailed in the text, while all the other outcome forest plots, sensitivity analyses, grade evaluation and funnel plots can be found in Additional files 5–8.

Effectiveness of RAT on wrist flexors spasticity

Seven RCTs (221 participants, 110 experimental and 111 control) evaluated the effect of robot-assisted therapy on wrist flexor spasticity (Fig. 4). The pooled analysis indicated a statistically significant reduction in spasticity in favor of robotic therapy compared to conventional treatment (MD = -0.22 points, 95% CI [-0.37 to -0.08]; p = 0.002). The statistical heterogeneity among the included studies was moderate (I² = 33%), suggesting consistency in the direction and magnitude of the effect. The limited number of subacute studies precluded phase-specific conclusions. GRADE assessment rated the certainty of this evidence as low.

Effectiveness of RAT on elbow flexors spasticity

Ten RCTs (402 participants, 196 experimental and 206 control) evaluated the efficacy of upper limb RAT on MAS elbow flexor (Fig. 5). Overall, the pooled MD was − 0.25 (95% CI: -0.40 to -0.10; p = 0.001), indicating a statistically significant reduction in MAS scores favoring the robotic group. Subgroup analysis revealed distinct patterns: in the subacute phase (5 studies, 111 experimental/117 control), the MD was − 0.12 (95% CI: -0.30 to 0.06; p = 0.20), with negligible heterogeneity (Tau² = 0.00, I² = 0%), suggesting no significant treatment effect. In contrast, the chronic phase (5 studies, 85 experimental/89 control) demonstrated a stronger effect (MD: -0.36; 95% CI: -0.57 to -0.15; p = 0.0007), with low-to-moderate heterogeneity (Tau² = 0.01, I² = 19%). Subgroup differences were non-significant (Chi² = 3.05, p = 0.08; I² = 67.2%). The certainty of evidence was rated as moderate. There were no serious concerns for inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision or publication bias.

Adverse events

Of the twenty-nine included trials, 23 provided sufficient data for the meta-analysis of treatment-related adverse events (Fig. 12). The analysis included a total of 1413 participants (719 experimental; 694 control). The pooled analysis showed no statistically significant difference in the odds of experiencing an adverse event between the robot-assisted therapy and control groups (Odds Ratio [OR] 1.18; 95% CI 0.53 to 2.66; p = 0.68). This corresponds to an absolute effect of 16 more adverse events per 1000 participants (from 99 per 1000 in the control group to 115 per 1000 in the experimental group), but the confidence interval is very wide, ranging from 44 fewer to 128 more events. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I²=45%). According to the GRADE framework, the certainty of this evidence was rated as very low.

Other outcomes

Fugl-Meyer upper extremity assessment

Fifteen randomized controlled trials (726 participants; 362 experimental, 364 control) assessed motor impairment using the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (FMA-UE). The overall pooled effect, with no statistical heterogeneity (I² = 0%), showed that robot-assisted therapy was significantly more effective than conventional therapy, yielding a mean improvement of 1.97 points (95% CI 0.83 to 3.12; p = 0.0008). Subgroup analysis by stroke phase revealed a consistent benefit in both populations. In subacute stroke patients (8 trials, N = 532), RAT produced a MD of 1.89 points on the FMA-UE (95% CI 0.54 to 3.24; p = 0.006), with low heterogeneity (I² = 11%). In chronic patients (7 trials, N = 194), the MD was 2.20 points (95% CI -0.01 to 4.40), an effect that was borderline significant (p = 0.05), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). A test for subgroup differences showed no significant interaction between the timing of the intervention (subacute versus chronic) and the treatment effect (p = 0.82; I² = 0%). According to GRADE, the certainty of this evidence is low.

Box and block test

The overall pooled analysis of eleven RCTs (274 participants) assessing the Box and Block Test demonstrated a statistically significant benefit in favor of robot-assisted therapy (MD = 0.89; 95% CI = 0.05 to 1.74; p = 0.04), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). While subgroup analysis revealed a marginally non-significant effect in the subacute phase (MD = 0.88; p = 0.05) and a non-significant effect in the chronic phase, the test for subgroup differences was not significant (p = 0.91), suggesting comparable benefits regardless of recovery stage.

Barthel index

A total of six studies, (330 participants) assessed the impact of the intervention on BI. The pooled analysis revealed no statistically significant effect of the experimental intervention compared to control (MD = 1.35; 95% CI -1.24 to 3.93; p = 0.31). Heterogeneity was negligible (Chi² = 0.28, df = 5; p = 1.00; I² = 0%). According to GRADE, the evidence is of low certainty.

Motricity index

The pooled analysis of seven studies (289 participants) evaluating the Motricity Index with no statistically significant between-group difference (MD = 3.23; 95% CI: −0.10 to 6.57; p = 0.06). Subgroup analyses for both subacute and chronic patients yielded similar non-significant positive trends. The analysis showed no statistical heterogeneity (I²=0%), but due to the wide confidence interval crossing the line of no effect, the certainty of this evidence was rated as low.

Quality of life

Finally, for quality of life, the available evidence, summarized narratively, indicated no consistent benefit of robotic-assisted therapy over conventional rehabilitation.

The effect of RAT on quality of life (QoL) was inconsistent across the nine studies that assessed this outcome. The majority of trials [37, 46, 51, 52, 54, 55], found no significant difference in QoL between their robotic and active control groups. Lo et al. [49] found that RAT improved scores on the Stroke Impact Scale (SIS) more than usual care at 12 weeks. Highlighting the importance of the therapeutic context, Bhattacharjee et al. [28] demonstrated that a hybrid protocol combining RAT with ADL training led to superior improvements on both SIS functional domains and the WHOQOL-BREF. Finally, Taravati et al. [50] observed a significant reduction in depressive symptoms, even when overall QoL scores were not different.

Sensitivity analysis

Considering that all included studies had one or more domains rated at high risk, we performed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. The sensitivity analysis for elbow flexor spasticity identified the Bhattacharjee et al. trial as the primary source of the heterogeneity (I²=24%), as its removal reduced the I² to 0%. Similarly, the significant reduction in spasticity for shoulder-elbow-wrist devices was also highly robust. The sensitivity analysis on delivery mode found the Kim et al. (2023) trial as the main contributor to the heterogeneity (I²=24%), as excluding it brought the I² = 0%. Conversely, the exclusion of the large trial by Rémy-Néris et al. (2021) resulted in the largest increase in effect size (SMD − 0.29), indicating that this study exerted a conservative influence on the overall estimate. The non-significant trend observed for the unspecified MAS score was found to be stable after leave one out analysis For FMA-UE, the overall finding of a statistically significant benefit was extremely robust.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted to synthesize the current evidence on the effectiveness of RAT for managing upper limb spasticity in adults after stroke. Further aims were to assess how outcomes vary by device type, delivery mode and stroke chronicity, and evaluates broader effects on motor function, daily activities, and quality of life.

A primary finding of this review is that RAT produces a small but statistically significant reduction in upper limb spasticity, as measured by the MAS. This effect was particularly evident in the elbow and wrist flexor muscles and appeared to be more pronounced in the chronic phase of stroke recovery compared to the subacute phase. By contrast, no significant effect was observed for shoulder adductor spasticity, and interventions using hand-only robotic devices did not yield significant improvements. Beyond spasticity, RAT demonstrated consistent benefits in motor outcomes. The most prominent finding was a statistically significant improvement on the FMA-UE favoring RAT, with an overall MD of 1.97 points. Several studies reporting average improvements in experimental [32, 34, 39, 41, 44, 53] and control [32, 37, 41] groups that exceeded the established Minimally Clinically Important Difference for the FMA-UE, which ranges from approximately 4.25 to 10 points depending on the patient’s stage of recovery and impairment level [57, 58]. The pooled effect on dexterity, measured by the Box and Block Test, was a small, statistically significant improvement of 0.89 blocks. In contrast, no significant benefit was found for activities of daily living via the Barthel Index or for motor activity via the Motricity Index.

When interpreting these results, the extensive heterogeneity across the 29 included RCTs must be acknowledged. The robotic devices, treatment dosages (from 10 to 40 sessions with different devices and settings), and control interventions (from dose-matched therapy to alternative technologies) were all highly variable complicating direct comparisons. The certainty of the evidence, evaluated using the GRADE framework, was assessed as very low to moderate across the various outcomes due to methodological limitations. All studies had performance bias, many lacked allocation concealment, and spasticity outcomes were downgraded for imprecision and indirectness.

The meta-analysis of 23 trials demonstrated that RAT is well-tolerated: the events reported were minor and transient, such as brief pain, mild skin irritation, or hand swelling [31, 46, 49]. They are reported as occurrences, but without a causal link being established by the investigators [37]. It is important to note that a large number of the included trials reported zero adverse events in both arms, and no serious events were directly attributed to the robotic interventions. However, the certainty of this evidence is very low, meaning the true effect on the risk of minor side effects remains uncertain. Additionally, adjunct protocols (CT + RAT vs. CT) may differ in total exposure and monitoring time, which can influence both AE occurrence and detection.

These findings offer a balanced perspective for clinicians. The primary implication for practice is that RAT is a safe modality for patients with post-stroke spasticity. This addresses a significant clinical barrier and supports the use of robotics to solve the “dose problem” in rehabilitation, where conventional therapy often involves an insufficient number of movement repetitions [59]. Consequently, RAT should be positioned as a tool for improving motor function and control, with spasticity reduction being a modest, secondary benefit. Evidence from Boardsworth et al. further emphasizes that robotic assisted rehabilitation is not a uniform intervention but a heterogeneous category whose efficacy depends on specific device features, application, and protocol characteristics [60]. Their meta-analysis highlighted greater benefits for devices targeting distal upper limb segments, those providing partial assistance to promote active patient engagement, unilateral application, limited degrees of freedom, and individual delivery [60]. In line with this, RAT appears particularly valuable for patients in the chronic stroke phase, who may have plateaued with conventional therapy but still benefit from higher intensity training [49]. To maximize functional carry-over, RAT should not be used in isolation. The success of a hybrid protocol combining RAT with explicit ADL training, as demonstrated by Bhattacharjee et al., suggests that the most effective clinical approach is to integrate robotic training into a comprehensive, task-oriented rehabilitation program [28].

Our findings represent an update to the evidence base on RAT and spasticity. The systematic review by Veerbeek et al., which included 44 RCTs published up to 2015 and analyzed MAS data from 13 RCTs (429 participants), reported a pooled effect favoring control interventions [13]. In contrast, by incorporating 15 more recent additional RCTs, our review demonstrates a modest but statistically significant reduction in spasticity associated with RAT. Similarly, Park et al. in their umbrella review of RAT for upper limb including 25 RCTs, found no significant effect on spasticity (SMD − 0.25; 95% CI − 0.55 to 0.06) with extreme heterogeneity (I² = 98.7%), a result that they attributed to the pooling of “general muscle spasticity regardless of the specific treatment area” and to the limited sensitivity of the MAS as an ordinal scale [15]. This methodological choice, collapsing outcomes across heterogeneous joints, devices and training sites, likely diluted anatomically confined effects, whereas our region-specific approach—analyzing MAS scores at the targeted joint and stratifying by device class and training site—allowed detection of significant reductions in selected segments. This shift aligns partially with two recent meta-analyses that evaluated spasticity as a secondary outcome in a limited number of trials: one reported a significant reduction in spasticity with distal robotic training, while the other identified a subgroup-specific reduction among individuals with chronic stroke [14, 61].

Beyond statistical differences, the divergence between our findings and prior reviews likely reflects the substantial evolution of RAT hardware and protocols over the last decade. Earlier trials more commonly employed relatively rigid, kinematically constrained platforms and highly standardized repetitive paradigms, often with substantial guidance of the paretic limb [31, 36, 52]. In contrast, the recent trials included here leverage a broader spectrum of designs including soft robotic gloves [48, 53] and compliant exoskeletons [44] together with increasingly adaptive, patient-cooperative assistance strategies. From a mechanistic standpoint, control strategies that modulate assistance and increase compliance are intended to better accommodate physiological trajectories and interaction forces, potentially reducing abrupt stretch-related inputs while enabling higher volumes of active, task-relevant practice tailored to residual ability [9]. This interpretation is consistent with recent evidence indicating that device and program features that promote active engagement and appropriate assistance are associated with differential clinical effects across trials [60] aligning with frameworks that prioritize motor learning principles over passive guidance alone [62]. Although a meta-analysis cannot directly test such mechanistic pathways, this technological and protocol shift provides a plausible explanation for heterogeneity in spasticity outcomes across studies.

A further reason why results vary across trials and across prior reviews is that ‘robot-assisted therapy’ encompasses substantial heterogeneity in how devices are used, and this heterogeneity has evolved alongside device development. In rehabilitation robotics, assistance strategies include counterbalancing/gravity compensation, impedance-based assistance, and patient-cooperative (‘assist-as-needed’) approaches, each of which can modify movement kinematics, effort demands, and the dose of active practice delivered during training [9]. From a spasticity-management perspective, several protocol elements are plausibly relevant to abnormal tone: gravity compensation (reducing antigravity demands and facilitating selective voluntary activation), controlled excursions toward end-range with attention to velocity and comfort, and high-repetition task practice that can reduce maladaptive co-contraction and enable more efficient reciprocal activation patterns [63]. However, reporting of spasticity-specific protocol parameters (e.g., stretch duration, velocity, rest intervals, positioning, and whether training explicitly targets spastic muscles) is heterogeneous, limiting confident conclusions regarding the optimal robotic protocol for spasticity. Conversely, between-study differences in whether RAT replaces conventional therapy or is delivered as an add-on, the degree of dose matching, baseline spasticity severity, concomitant spasticity treatments, and limitations of the Modified Ashworth Scale as a clinical measure of resistance to passive movement may dilute or obscure protocol-specific effects.

Proposed mechanisms for spasticity reduction, such as reciprocal inhibition [43] and high-intensity, controlled stretching to improve soft tissue rheologic properties [42] likely contribute to the results but appear secondary to the primary effect on motor control. The quality of the movement appears critical, as one study demonstrated that the constrained, supported trajectories of an exoskeleton were superior to less-controlled virtual reality movements, which were observed to potentially increase tone [27]. Other proposed mechanisms include the ability of robots to provide pain-free mobilization within an engaging environment to break the pain-spasticity cycle [40] and the potential for high-repetition training, including bilateral paradigms in selected devices/trials (e.g., Bi-Manu-Track bilateral forearm/wrist training [32], MIME bilateral arm training [36]), in which movements of the non-paretic limb can guide or be coupled to the paretic limb. In these specific contexts, bilateral coupling may contribute to improvements in motor control and abnormal tone regulation.

When stratified by delivery mode, both replacement (RAT vs. CT) and adjunct (CT + RAT vs. CT) protocols were associated with reduced spasticity, without significant difference between delivery modes. Therefore, delivery mode should not be interpreted as a confirmed effect modifier for spasticity outcomes. Future trials should report standardized feasibility metrics (e.g., therapist time, setup time, adherence, and resource use) alongside clinical outcomes.

This systematic review has several limitations. Our analysis is constrained by the quality and reporting of the primary studies. The reliance on the MAS as the predominant spasticity outcome measure limited our ability to draw conclusions about the underlying physiological changes [64]. Heterogeneity in robotic devices, intervention protocols, and patient populations, though explored via subgroup analyses, complicates the interpretation of the pooled estimates. Intervention protocol is likely a major determinant of outcomes in neurorehabilitation thus, future trials should provide complete protocol descriptions in line with TIDieR recommendations [65]. Adverse event comparisons may be biased in dose-unmatched add-on trials, where increased exposure can increase both true event risk and event ascertainment. While we conducted a comprehensive search, the potential for publication bias cannot be entirely excluded, and our search was restricted to English-language publications.

To advance the field, future research should prioritize several areas. First, there is a pressing need for large-scale RCTs powered to detect clinically meaningful changes in spasticity. Second, these trials must incorporate objective, instrumented measures of spasticity to dissect the effects of RAT on different components of the spastic movement disorder​ and also integrate complementary approaches, such as muscle synergy analysis, to better capture the mechanisms underlying recovery [66].

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that RAT is a safe modality that does not exacerbate existing spasticity and can produce a small, statistically significant reduction in elbow and wrist flexor tone, particularly in chronic stroke. Adverse events were generally minor and transient; however, given very low-certainty evidence and potential confounding from dose-unmatched add-on protocols, adverse event reporting should be interpreted cautiously and patients should be monitored during implementation. Therefore, based on the current evidence, which is of low to moderate certainty, RAT should be considered a tool to increase the intensity of motor practice rather than a primary anti-spasticity intervention. Substantial, high-quality research is still required to identify the specific protocols and patient populations for which RAT offers a clear and meaningful advantage over other rehabilitation approaches for spasticity management.
