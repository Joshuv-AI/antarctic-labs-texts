From trial site to research emerging research hub: mapping Ghana’s vaccine research ecosystem, 1977–2025
Authors: Sabastine Eugene Arthur, Gilbert Turkson, Joseph Ahia Quarcoo, Maame Ama Pentsiwaa Cudjoe, Caroline Trotter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGhana’s vaccine research ecosystem has evolved substantially alongside national and global immunisation efforts. However, the extent and distribution of research activity across the full vaccine development continuum have not been systematically synthesised. This review maps peer-reviewed publications and institutional reports across a nine-stage vaccine development framework from 1977 to 2025, comparing patterns between the pre-pandemic (2000–2020) and post-pandemic (2021–2025) periods.Main bodyBefore 2020, vaccine-related research in Ghana was concentrated predominantly in downstream domains, including disease surveillance, programme implementation, and Phase II–III clinical trials, with notable contributions to malaria and pneumococcal vaccine development. Following the COVID-19 pandemic, publication output increased more than fivefold and diversified into emerging areas such as genomic surveillance, regulatory science, behavioural research, and digital health systems, accompanied by stronger leadership from domestic institutions. Despite this expansion, engagement in upstream domains, including discovery science, preclinical development, Phase I clinical trials, and local vaccine manufacturing, remains limited, reflecting persistent structural and infrastructure constraints.ConclusionsGhana has transitioned from a primarily implementation-focused setting to an increasingly active contributor to multidisciplinary vaccine evidence. Sustained investment in upstream research infrastructure, early-phase clinical trial capacity, sustainable financing mechanisms and behavioural intervention research will be essential to strengthen national vaccine sovereignty. The Ghanaian experience offers transferable lessons for other low- and middle-income countries seeking to build resilient and locally driven vaccine research ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12961-026-01516-y.

Introduction

Over the past four decades, vaccine research and development (R&D) has evolved into a complex, multi-stage ecosystem spanning discovery science, preclinical development, phased clinical evaluation, regulatory review, programme implementation, and uptake—conceptualised here as a nine-stage continuum (Fig. 1) that links laboratory innovation to population-level impact.Fig. 1Conceptual framework of the vaccine development continuum used to map the Ghana vaccine research ecosystem (1977–2025). The COVID-19 pandemic marked a major inflection point, catalysing unprecedented scientific collaboration, accelerating mRNA and viral-vector platforms and exposing critical vulnerabilities in manufacturing capacity, supply chains and equitable access, reshaping vaccine research trajectories in low- and middle-income countries.

Ghana’s vaccine research landscape reflects this global evolution within specific national contexts. Following EPI introduction in 1978, Ghana progressively expanded its immunisation schedule to include BCG, DTP, polio, hepatitis B, Hib, measles-rubella, pneumococcal conjugate, rotavirus, yellow fever, meningococcal conjugate and the RTS,S malaria vaccine, achieving notable reductions in vaccine-preventable diseases, though coverage inequalities persist.

This expansion was substantially enabled by Ghana’s partnership with Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, beginning in 2001, which co-financed the introduction of pentavalent, pneumococcal conjugate, rotavirus and measles-rubella vaccines into the national immunisation schedule.

Early Ghanaian research was largely descriptive, documenting disease burden and immunisation coverage. From the early 2000s, Ghana increasingly participated in multicentre trials, notably for malaria (RTS,S, R21) and pneumococcal vaccines, while the portfolio broadened over time to include implementation science, health economics and behavioural research. Engagement in discovery science, preclinical development and platform innovation has nonetheless remained limited.

A phase-based comparison of Ghana’s vaccine research is timely for three reasons. COVID-19 functioned as a disruptive turning point, markedly increasing research volume and diversifying thematic focus into operational, behavioural and regulatory domains. The pandemic also transformed financing and procurement environments and elevated vaccine hesitancy as a dominant research theme, making a pre/post comparison across the nine-stage continuum both analytically tractable and policy-relevant.

Against this backdrop, this narrative review has three objectives: to map Ghana’s vaccine-related research output from 1977 to 2025 across the nine stages of vaccine development; to compare thematic patterns between the pre-pandemic (2000–2020) and post-pandemic (2021–2025) periods; and to identify structural and policy-relevant gaps to guide future investment and institutional strengthening. This continuum is adapted from internationally established vaccine development frameworks and is not specific to Ghana.

Methods

This narrative synthesis mapped Ghana’s vaccine-related research from 1977 to 2025 using a structured narrative synthesis approach. A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed using the search terms ‘vaccine’ AND ‘Ghana’, supplemented by targeted manual searches of reports from WHO, Gavi, UNICEF, Ghana Health Service, and Africa CDC.. Eligible studies were peer-reviewed, English-language publications or institutional reports addressing any aspect of vaccines or immunisation in Ghana. Each study was assigned to the single most relevant stage of an adapted nine-stage vaccine development continuum (discovery science through uptake, access, and demand), with findings synthesised descriptively across two comparative periods: pre-pandemic (2000–2020) and post-pandemic (2021–2025). The search was last conducted between 15 August 2025 and 30 September 2025, with the final search updated on 4 January 2026. PubMed was selected as the primary database given its comprehensive indexing of biomedical literature relevant to vaccine research in LMICs. Although additional databases such as Scopus, Web of Science, African Journals Online and Google Scholar may contain relevant publications, PubMed was considered sufficient for the broad evidence-mapping objectives of this narrative review because of its extensive coverage of biomedical, clinical and public health research. Studies were screened by title and abstract, with full-text review conducted for all potentially eligible articles. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers. Potentially eligible studies underwent full-text review, and disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. No language or date restrictions were applied beyond the study period (1977–2025). For this review, ‘Ghana-exclusive’ publications are defined as studies in which all authors are affiliated with Ghanaian institutions. ‘Ghana-led’ publications are defined as studies in which the first or corresponding author is affiliated with a Ghanaian institution, regardless of co-author affiliations. This review is conducted as a scoping evidence-mapping exercise, employing a structured search strategy consistent with scoping review methodology, though it does not follow a formal registered protocol. The full PubMed search string used was: (("vaccine*" OR vaccination OR immunisation OR immunisation) AND (Ghana OR Ghanaian)) AND (clinical trial OR immunisation program* OR immunisation program* OR policy OR financing OR uptake OR coverage OR hesitancy OR surveillance OR effectiveness OR implementation)]. Institutional reports were identified through targeted searches of the websites of WHO, Gavi, UNICEF, the Ghana Health Service, Africa CDC, and the Ghana FDA, supplemented by reference list screening of included publications. Grey literature searches were conducted systematically between June and July 2025 using targeted searches of the websites of the World Health Organization (WHO), Gavi, UNICEF, Africa CDC, Ghana Health Service (GHS), Ministry of Health (MoH), and the Ghana Food and Drugs Authority (FDA). Searches covered documents published between 1977 and 2025. The search terms used included combinations of ‘vaccine’, ‘vaccination’, ‘immunisation’, ‘clinical trial’, ‘vaccine policy’, ‘vaccine financing’, ‘vaccine regulation’, ‘vaccine manufacturing’, and ‘Ghana’. Reports were included if they contained information relevant to vaccine research, vaccine development, immunisation programmes, vaccine financing, vaccine policy, vaccine regulation, clinical trials or vaccine implementation activities in Ghana. Reference lists of eligible reports and publications were additionally screened to identify further relevant sources. A detailed summary of the grey literature sources searched, search terms used and eligibility criteria applied is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Formal risk-of-bias assessment was not undertaken because this review was designed as a scoping evidence map rather than an effectiveness review, consistent with accepted scoping review methodology [1, 2]. Although this review employs a structured search strategy, it has not been registered prospectively and does not adhere to a formal scoping review protocol; it is therefore reported as a narrative synthesis.

Historical foundations (1977–1999)

Before 2000, vaccine-related research activity in Ghana was limited but foundational. Following the introduction of the Expanded Programme on Immunisation (EPI) in 1978, early research focused primarily on disease surveillance, immunisation coverage monitoring and documentation of vaccine-preventable disease burden. Key institutions established during this period, including the Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research (NMIMR) and the Kintampo Health Research Centre (KHRC), provided the institutional infrastructure upon which subsequent vaccine research capacity was built. Although no Ghana-led clinical trials of novel vaccine candidates were conducted during this period, participation in regional surveillance networks and early epidemiological studies laid the groundwork for Ghana’s later emergence as a clinical trial site. Only a limited number of vaccine-related publications were identified during this period, reflecting the relatively early stage of research infrastructure development and lower publication output compared with subsequent decades. The period also saw the establishment of the Ghana Food and Drugs Authority (FDA) and early regulatory frameworks that would later underpin the country’s clinical trial oversight capacity.

PHASE 1: pre-pandemic era (2000–2020)

Overall distribution and temporal evolution

Funding for vaccine research in Ghana during this period was predominantly external, with major contributions from the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation, Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, the World Health Organization, and the Wellcome Trust.

The pre-COVID period (2000–2020) was marked by modest but consistent growth in Ghana’s vaccine-related research, concentrated in operational and implementation science—primarily immunisation programme monitoring, coverage assessments, and evaluations of childhood vaccines. Discovery and preclinical research were minimal throughout, reflecting inadequate funding, absent high-containment facilities, and a historical prioritisation of downstream public health research. Discovery and preclinical science (Stages 1–2)

Ghana’s engagement in discovery and preclinical vaccine science remained extremely limited throughout the pre-pandemic period. Structural constraints, inadequate funding, absence of BSL-3 facilities and insufficient laboratory infrastructure meant that Ghana’s contribution to global vaccine pipelines typically commenced at the clinical trial phase. Institutions such as Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research (NMIMR) and Kumasi Centre for Collaborative Research in Tropical Medicine (KCCR), while strong in diagnostics and surveillance, did not function as centres for antigen discovery or animal-model experimentation.

Overall, the persistent lack of discovery-level capacity positioned Ghana primarily as a trial site rather than a co-developer of novel vaccines. This limitation had important implications for national scientific sovereignty, knowledge generation and innovation potential [3, 4].

Proof-of-concept and efficacy trials (Stages 3–4)

In contrast to its limited upstream activity, Ghana played an important and highly visible role in proof-of-concept and large-scale efficacy trials across multiple disease areas, establishing itself as a trusted environment for human clinical research. This prominence was underpinned by the country’s epidemiological relevance, relatively robust clinical infrastructure and the presence of experienced research centres, including Kintampo, Navrongo, Agogo and NMIMR, all of which consistently met Good Clinical Practice standards [5, 19].

Malaria vaccine development represents Ghana’s most substantial contribution. The country participated actively in Phase II and pivotal Phase III trials of the RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine, generating crucial data that later informed the World Health Organization’s 2021 recommendation for its widespread use [5, 19]. Ghana also supported later studies of the R21/Matrix-M vaccine, including trials demonstrating efficacy exceeding 70% in seasonal transmission settings [20]. These studies were characterised by rigorous study designs, detailed immunological assessments, long-term follow-up and comprehensive safety monitoring.

Beyond malaria, Ghana contributed to proof-of-concept and efficacy trials of tuberculosis vaccine candidates [13, 21], also supported large-scale pneumococcal conjugate vaccine immunogenicity and serotype-specific response studies as part of broader multicountry Phase III evaluations, and played a key role in regional rotavirus vaccine trials that clarified vaccine performance in high-burden African settings [22, 23]. Across these studies, methodologies included randomised controlled or cluster trial designs, active disease surveillance, sophisticated laboratory analyses and sustained community engagement [24]. Ghanaian scientific leadership in these multinational studies gradually increased, as reflected in authorship, coordination and local implementation roles, even as upstream decision-making and funding remained predominantly external [19]. Collectively, Ghana’s contributions within Stages 3–4 of the vaccine continuum represent one of the most mature and internationally recognised aspects of its vaccine research profile.

Regulatory evaluation, registration and WHO prequalification (Stages 5–6)

Ghana’s regulatory role evolved in parallel with the strengthening of the Food and Drugs Authority (FDA). The institution progressively built capacity in regulating vaccines, biological products, and clinical trials, while continuing to rely on WHO prequalification and approvals from stringent regulatory authorities for marketing authorisation of imported vaccines. The FDA’s primary functions are centred on reliance-based authorisation, cold-chain oversight and post-marketing surveillance. A key milestone in strengthening the regulatory framework for vaccine evaluation and registration was the adoption of the Guidelines for the Registration of Human Vaccines (FDA/VBP/GDL-05/03) by the Technical Advisory Committee in 2015; the guidelines subsequently came into effect in 2024 [25].

The application of WHO’s Global Benchmarking Tool led to Ghana’s designation as a Maturity Level 3 regulatory authority in 2020, as a non-producing country, an important acknowledgement of progress in regulatory assessment, inspection, vigilance and laboratory oversight [26, 27]. While this did not yet position Ghana as a global reference authority, it did establish the country as one of the more advanced national regulatory bodies in the region [28].

Implementation and effectiveness evidence (Stage 7)

Ghana’s most notable and sustained strength in vaccine research during the pre-pandemic period lay in implementation science and real-world effectiveness studies. A substantial body of work assessed vaccine coverage, programme performance and linkages with primary healthcare delivery systems, particularly through the Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) strategy [15, 29]. In many studies, immunisation outcomes were also used as proxies for broader health system performance.

Seminal research on measles vaccination in northern Ghana demonstrated substantial reductions in all-cause childhood mortality, shaping national policy and reinforcing the importance of early and complete immunisation [9]. Subsequent analyses identified coverage gaps and drop-out patterns for the second dose of measles-rubella vaccine (MR2), influencing the design of defaulter tracing systems and district-level planning [17, 30].

The introduction of the pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13) in 2012 generated extensive research activity. Surveillance data demonstrated significant declines in invasive pneumococcal disease among children under five, while other studies investigated serotype replacement, carriage patterns and emerging antimicrobial resistance [7, 31].

Similarly, Ghana emerged as an early contributor of post-introduction evidence for rotavirus vaccination. Sentinel surveillance documented sharp reductions in severe diarrhoeal hospitalisations shortly after vaccine introduction, with sustained impact over time [32, 33]. Complementary cost-effectiveness analyses further strengthened the policy case for continued investment in the vaccine [34].

Nationally representative datasets, including the DHS and MICS, as well as multilevel analytical studies, consistently identified socioeconomic and geographic inequities in vaccine coverage. Maternal education, facility-based delivery, and access to CHPS services emerged as key predictors of full immunisation [35, 36]. These findings were frequently translated into targeted outreach, workforce training and local planning strategies [37].

Financing and procurement dynamics (Stage 8)

Ghana’s vaccine financing during this period was heavily shaped by its partnership with Gavi, beginning in 2001. This relationship enabled the introduction of relatively high-cost vaccines, including pentavalent, pneumococcal conjugate, rotavirus and measles-rubella vaccines [38]. As Ghana’s income classification changed over time, it transitioned into higher co-financing categories, placing increasing fiscal pressure on the national budget allocated to immunisation [39].

Procurement mechanisms largely relied on pooled purchasing through the UNICEF Supply Division, granting Ghana access to vaccines at reduced global prices [40]. Although this system enhanced affordability, it also constrained national autonomy and bargaining power, as procurement remained linked to global tenders and donor-supported mechanisms [41]. With Ghana approaching transition from Gavi support, concerns regarding price stability, supply security and long-term affordability have intensified.

Health economic modelling played a critical role in policy decisions. Cost-effectiveness analyses supported the introduction of PCV and rotavirus vaccines [42, 43] and demonstrated that sustained coverage of measles vaccination remains among the most cost-effective interventions in Ghana’s health system [44]. More recent modelling related to HPV and malaria vaccines further underscored the importance of sustainable domestic financing strategies [45, 46].

System-level research also revealed vulnerabilities, including delays in budget disbursement, dependence on external donors and exposure to currency volatility. These challenges continue to pose risks to immunisation programme stability and continuity [47, 48].

Uptake, access and demand (Stage 9)

Despite relatively high national immunisation coverage, Ghana exhibits persistent geographic, socioeconomic and service-delivery inequalities in vaccine uptake. Analyses of DHS and MICS data revealed disparities by wealth, location and maternal education, with higher drop-out rates consistently observed in northern and coastal savannah regions [11, 36]. One recurring challenge was attrition between the first and second doses of measles-rubella vaccine, often linked to weak second-year-of-life service delivery and low caregiver awareness [11, 17].

Studies conducted at facility and community levels identified stock-outs, travel distance, inadequate defaulter tracing and inconsistent outreach as continuing bottlenecks [37]. While CHPS has significantly improved geographic access in rural areas, variations in staffing levels, infrastructure and supervision have resulted in uneven service quality [15]. Delayed immunisation, even among fully vaccinated children, further undermines the achievement of optimal herd immunity [35].

Although vaccine hesitancy emerged more prominently during the COVID-19 pandemic, earlier studies had already highlighted misconceptions related to newer vaccines such as HPV [49]. Pandemic-era research later revealed concerns related to vaccine safety, political distrust and misinformation [50]. Acceptance was generally higher among educated, urban populations and among individuals who expressed trust in health authorities. It should be noted, however, that many hesitancy studies identified in this review relied on cross-sectional survey designs and convenience sampling, which limits the generalisability of findings and preclude causal inference about the determinants of vaccine acceptance. Furthermore, relatively little evidence was identified regarding whether findings from hesitancy studies were translated into measurable policy interventions or evaluated communication strategies.

Gender norms, maternal autonomy, religious affiliation and population mobility were also found to influence uptake patterns [11]. Strong evidence indicates that community engagement and improved communication by health workers can significantly increase vaccine demand and reduce dropout rates [11, 12].

Cross-cutting pre-pandemic evidence gaps

Before COVID-19, Ghana’s vaccine ecosystem was characterised by strong downstream implementation capacity alongside significant deficits in preclinical research, advanced regulatory science, domestic manufacturing and systematic behavioural inquiry. Phase I trial activity was virtually absent, and social and behavioural science relating to vaccination remained largely descriptive, leaving Ghana with limited baseline data on vaccine confidence and hesitancy dynamics.

The COVID-19 pandemic coincided with a rapid change in Ghana’s vaccine research landscape, accelerating behavioural science, strengthening regulatory capacity and expanding the thematic scope of national scholarship, including the Ghana FDA’s attainment of WHO Maturity Level 3 status and feasibility discussions around local vaccine manufacturing.

PHASE 2: pandemic and post-pandemic era (2021–2025)

Overall distribution and temporal evolution

Across the pre-pandemic period (2000–2020), a total of 146 vaccine-related publications were identified, compared with 261 publications between 2021 and 2025. This corresponds to an increase in annualised publication output from approximately 7.0 publications per year before the pandemic to 52.2 publications per year during the post-pandemic period. The period from 2021 to 2025 represents the most significant expansion in Ghana’s vaccine-related research. Annual publications increased more than fivefold compared with the pre-pandemic period, coinciding with the COVID-19 emergency, increased research investment, expansion of scientific infrastructure, growth in the national research workforce and broader global interest in vaccine-related research. Institutional participation expanded markedly, with increased contributions from Ghanaian public universities (University of Ghana, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology and the University of Cape Coast), the Ghana Health Service, the FDA, and the newly established National Vaccine Institute, alongside long-standing centres of excellence (NMIMR, Kintampo, Navrongo, KCCR). This broader institutional base reflects enhanced domestic ownership and deeper interdisciplinary engagement, marking a decisive shift from vaccine deployment and monitoring to the production of multidimensional evidence. A map of the geographic distribution of major vaccine research institutions and clinical trial sites in Ghana is provided as Supplementary Figure S1.

Taken together, this phase marks a decisive structural shift: Ghana’s role moved beyond vaccine deployment and monitoring to become an increasingly prominent producer of vaccine evidence across clinical research, behavioural science, regulatory systems and health system performance.

Discovery and preclinical science (Stages 1–2)

Despite the overall expansion of research, Ghana’s direct engagement in early-stage vaccine discovery and preclinical development remained limited. No locally led studies on antigen discovery, vector design or animal-model experimentation were identified. However, significant strengthening of SARS-CoV-2 genomic surveillance, enabling routine variant sequencing and contributions to regional databases, alongside studies of immune correlates (antibody persistence, neutralisation capacity, T cell responses), represents an upstream-adjacent capacity that could underpin future discovery engagement.

Thus, while Ghana did not fully enter Stages 1–2 of vaccine development during this phase, the expansion of genomic, immunological and molecular analytic capacity represents a foundational platform on which future upstream engagement could be built, contingent upon sustained infrastructure development and targeted investment.

Proof-of-concept and clinical evidence (Stages 3–4)

Ghana significantly deepened its contribution to clinical and translational vaccine research during the post-pandemic period. Building on long-standing experience in malaria vaccine trials, the country participated in continued implementation and evaluation studies of the R21/Matrix-M vaccine, generating additional evidence on safety, immunogenicity and real-world effectiveness [51, 52].

The COVID-19 pandemic further expanded Ghana’s clinical research portfolio. Local institutions conducted studies examining post-vaccination immune responses, breakthrough infection patterns, booster-dose effectiveness and hybrid immunity, thereby contributing essential African population data to the global COVID-19 evidence base [53, 54]. Although large-scale Phase I trials of COVID-19 vaccines were limited, Ghana made substantial contributions through post-licensure monitoring, pharmacovigilance and real-world effectiveness research.

Growing attention was also directed toward HPV vaccination. Studies explored acceptability, dose-sparing strategies and the feasibility of school-based and community-delivered models, closely aligning Ghana’s research agenda with global cervical cancer elimination strategies [11, 46].

Importantly, this period was marked by increasing methodological sophistication. Researchers made expanded use of real-world datasets, longitudinal cohort designs, advanced serological assays and mixed-method approaches, indicating a maturing research environment and enhanced readiness for participation in complex, multi-vaccine clinical evaluation programmes.

Regulatory evaluation, registration and WHO alignment (Stages 5–6)

The post-pandemic era represents a critical milestone in Ghana’s regulatory development. Attainment of WHO Maturity Level 3 (ML3) status significantly strengthened the FDA’s capacity to oversee vaccine trials, regulate emergency-use products, conduct pharmacovigilance and participate in regional harmonisation initiatives, including joint dossier reviews.

WHO [10] although Ghana still lacks WHO-prequalified vaccine manufacturing facilities, improvements in Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) inspection capacity, laboratory testing functions and regulatory frameworks have moved the country closer to potential engagement in prequalification pathways. The establishment of the National Vaccine Institute represents a policy commitment to future local production and technology transfer, rather than demonstrated manufacturing capacity and will require sustained political and financial support to translate into operational capability.

Implementation and effectiveness evidence (Stage 7)

COVID-19 vaccination campaigns constituted the largest adult immunisation initiative in Ghana’s history and generated a substantial body of implementation and real-world effectiveness evidence. Studies assessed a range of delivery strategies, including mass vaccination centres, mobile outreach services, workplace campaigns, school-based programmes and faith-based partnerships [55, 56].

Digital innovation became central to vaccine delivery and monitoring. The introduction of electronic vaccination registries, QR-coded vaccination certificates and real-time monitoring dashboards significantly strengthened surveillance systems, enabled improved tracking of coverage patterns, and facilitated the identification of zero-dose and underserved populations [55]. This marked a clear shift away from paper-based and fragmented reporting systems that dominated the pre-pandemic period.

Although national coverage levels improved over time, persistent inequities linked to geography, socio-economic status, gender and digital exclusion were consistently identified [50]. Importantly, these findings are now informing strategic adjustments within the EPI and shaping the rollout of newer vaccines, including malaria vaccines.

Overall, this body of research not only generated operational insights but also piloted delivery models that are increasingly being adapted and integrated into routine immunisation systems in Ghana.

Financing and procurement dynamics (Stage 8)

The global competition for COVID-19 vaccines profoundly disrupted Ghana’s traditional procurement pathways. In contrast to its historical reliance on Gavi- and UNICEF-supported supply systems, Ghana was compelled to navigate a fragmented and highly competitive landscape that included COVID-19 Vaccines Global Access (COVAX) allocations, African Vaccine Acquisition Trust (AVAT) deliveries, bilateral donations and direct market purchases [39, 40].

This disruption coincided with new streams of health financing and policy research focused on supply security, price volatility, cold-chain expansion costs and vulnerabilities related to currency fluctuations and macroeconomic instability. Preliminary economic analyses also explored the feasibility of domestic vaccine manufacturing and local fill-and-finish capacity as long-term strategies to enhance supply resilience.

At the same time, Ghana’s impending transition from Gavi support intensified debate around domestic resource mobilisation, innovative financing mechanisms and participation in regional pooled procurement initiatives under the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS) and Africa CDC frameworks [38, 57]. Despite this growing attention, comprehensive long-term sustainability modelling remains limited, highlighting a critical evidence gap as Ghana’s vaccine portfolio continues to expand.

Uptake, access and demand (Stage 9)

A substantial proportion of post-pandemic vaccine research in Ghana centred on behavioural determinants of vaccine uptake, with particular attention to hesitancy and public trust. Studies consistently identified misinformation, perceived safety risks, political distrust and religious beliefs as major drivers of early COVID-19 vaccine resistance [11, 50].

Social media platforms played a central role in shaping public perceptions, facilitating the spread of conspiracy narratives related to infertility, population control and technological surveillance [58, 59]. However, vaccine acceptance gradually increased as trusted community leaders, healthcare professionals and institutions strengthened engagement strategies and expanded targeted communication campaigns.

Parallel research on HPV vaccination revealed similar patterns of fear, misconception and parental concern, reinforcing the need for adolescent-focused education, school-based outreach and culturally appropriate communication approaches [11, 46]. Increasingly, studies in this period incorporated behavioural theory, social-listening approaches and mixed-method designs to deepen understanding of vaccination decision-making processes.

Despite early resistance, Ghana’s experience demonstrates that vaccine confidence can be rebuilt through transparency, community engagement and integration of vaccination services within established maternal, child and adolescent health platforms.

Discussion

This review reveals a fundamental paradox in Ghana’s vaccine research ecosystem: while the country has become one of the most active sites for vaccine implementation and evaluation in sub-Saharan Africa, it remains largely absent from the upstream stages of vaccine development, antigen discovery, preclinical research and Phase I trials, which determine which vaccines are developed and for whom. This paradox reflects broader structural inequities in the global vaccine research enterprise, in which LMICs contribute substantially to the generation of clinical evidence while retaining limited agency over the scientific agenda.

A sharp quantitative and qualitative expansion characterised the post-2021 period: publications increased more than fivefold and the thematic landscape broadened to include genomic surveillance, regulatory science, immunology, behavioural analysis and digital health systems, with strengthened institutional leadership from NMIMR, KCCR, and the Ghana FDA.

The COVID-19 pandemic functioned as a critical inflection point. Where pre-pandemic research focused on monitoring routine immunisation coverage and evaluating established vaccines, the post-pandemic period accelerated engagement with digital health technologies, viral genomics, variant surveillance and behavioural determinants of vaccine acceptance. The Ghana FDA’s attainment of the WHO Maturity Level 3 status enabled oversight of emergency-use authorisations and participation in regional harmonisation. Despite these advances, persistent upstream deficits remain: Ghana has yet to develop locally led antigen discovery, preclinical pipelines or Phase I trial infrastructure, mirroring broader patterns across LMICs where engagement in the global vaccine pipeline typically begins at the efficacy-testing stage.

Within the regional African context, Ghana demonstrates a distinctive profile. Ghana has demonstrated notable strengths in clinical trial participation, implementation research, and regulatory development relative to many sub-Saharan African settings, though direct comparisons are constrained by differences in reporting systems and research infrastructure across countries [26, 50, 57]. Formal quantitative comparison with other African countries was beyond the scope of this review, and available comparisons should therefore be interpreted cautiously. The progression presented in Tables 1 and 2 highlights this pattern clearly: while the pre-pandemic era was dominated by externally led malaria and pneumococcal trials, the post-pandemic period saw the emergence of domestically anchored studies on COVID-19 immunogenicity, HPV vaccine delivery, and regulatory systems strengthening domestically anchored studies on COVID-19 immunogenicity led by NMIMR and WACCBIP (West African Centre for Cell Biology of Infectious Pathogens), HPV vaccine delivery research coordinated by the Ghana Health Service and University of Ghana, and regulatory systems strengthening led by the Ghana FDA. Compared with continental initiatives such as the African Vaccine Manufacturing Accelerator, Ghana’s establishment of a National Vaccine Institute represents an important strategic commitment to future local production and technology transfer, although sustained political and financial support will be essential to mitigate vulnerabilities associated with Gavi transition [38, 39]. These findings align with broader WHO calls for the creation of more equitable and geographically distributed vaccine research and manufacturing ecosystems, positioning Ghana as a potentially instructive model for lower-middle-income countries seeking to build post-pandemic resilience [10].

This review is strengthened by the application of a nine-stage analytical framework, which enabled granular mapping of research activity across the full vaccine life-cycle. Triangulation of peer-reviewed literature with institutional reports further enhanced the robustness of temporal and thematic interpretations [63]. Nevertheless, methodological limitations are inherent to narrative synthesis. The restriction to English-language sources may have resulted in the underrepresentation of locally published work. The assignment of individual studies to single stages inevitably simplified some multi-domain research contributions, and the absence of formal risk-of-bias appraisal precludes weighted assessment of evidence quality. Despite these constraints, the scale of change documented, including an approximate fivefold increase in output following COVID-19, strongly supports the central hypothesis that the pandemic functioned as a significant inflection point for Ghana’s vaccine research ecosystem. The evidence in this review more strongly supports access, availability, and logistics as the dominant barriers to vaccination in Ghana, with hesitancy being a prominent but secondary theme that emerged specifically in the COVID-19 context. Additionally, the nine-stage framework does not distinguish between research evaluating vaccine programmes and the programmes themselves; some studies classified under Stage 7 (implementation and effectiveness) describe programme activities rather than independent research. A comprehensive audit of all citations has been conducted to ensure that references accurately support the claims to which they are attached.

The contrasting profiles presented in Tables 1 and 2 illustrate this transition clearly, with the pre-pandemic period dominated by externally funded clinical trials and the post-pandemic period characterised by domestically anchored, thematically diversified research. The findings point to three strategic priorities. First, targeted investment in upstream infrastructure, BSL-3 laboratories, and Phase I clinical trial capabilities is essential for strengthening scientific sovereignty. Second, the large body of research documenting vaccine hesitancy must evolve into intervention-focused trials that test and scale evidence-based communication strategies. Third, as Ghana moves towards full Gavi transition, comprehensive health-economic modelling of an expanding multi-vaccine portfolio, including sustained deployment of RTS,S/R21 and HPV vaccines, is urgently needed. Addressing these upstream deficits will require coordinated action from Ghanaian universities, the Ghana FDA, the National Vaccine Institute and international funders, including sustained investment in BSL-3 infrastructure, Phase I trial capacity, and translational research platforms. Among the more unexpected findings of this review is the near-complete absence of Ghana-led antigen discovery or preclinical research despite two decades of sustained clinical trial participation, a pattern that highlights the structural barriers separating trial-site engagement from upstream scientific sovereignty. Addressing these upstream deficits will require coordinated action from Ghanaian universities, the Ghana FDA, the National Vaccine Institute and international funders, including sustained investment in BSL-3 infrastructure, Phase I trial capacity and translational research platforms.

Conclusions

This review provides the first comprehensive mapping of Ghana’s vaccine-related research across the nine-stage development continuum from 1977 to 2025, demonstrating a clear transformation in both scale and scope. Before 2020, Ghana’s contributions were concentrated predominantly in downstream domains, including programme implementation, financing and uptake, alongside participation in major multicentre malaria and pneumococcal trials. However, engagement in discovery, preclinical development, and early-phase clinical research remained minimal.

This trajectory parallels experiences in other African countries such as Kenya and Senegal, where the pandemic similarly accelerated diversification of vaccine research portfolios, though Ghana’s regulatory milestone (WHO ML3 status) and establishment of a National Vaccine Institute represent distinctive institutional achievements.

Despite these advances, critical gaps persist, particularly in antigen discovery, preclinical research, Phase I trials, domestic manufacturing and long-term financing sustainability in the context of the Gavi transition. Addressing these gaps will be essential to move Ghana from downstream excellence to upstream participation.

Specifically, government investment in BSL-3 laboratory infrastructure and Phase I trial capacity is needed to enable upstream research participation. International partners and funders should prioritise support for translational research platforms and technology transfer initiatives. Research institutions should expand training in vaccine science, regulatory affairs and health economics to build the human capital required for a self-sustaining vaccine research ecosystem.

Priority actions include: investment in BSL-3 and translational research infrastructure; establishment of Phase I clinical trial capacity at existing research centres; strengthening of domestic vaccine manufacturing initiatives through the National Vaccine Institute; expansion of behavioural intervention research beyond descriptive surveys to test and scale evidence-based communication strategies; and development of sustainable financing mechanisms to support immunisation programmes following Ghana’s transition from Gavi support.

Ghana’s trajectory offers an instructive example for other low- and middle-income countries seeking to strengthen vaccine sovereignty and research resilience in the post-pandemic era.
