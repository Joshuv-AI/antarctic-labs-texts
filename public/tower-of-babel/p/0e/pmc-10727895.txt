Pemphigus Foliaceus Possibly Associated with Excessive Intake of Spirulina
Authors: Ji Min Seo, Jin Young Choi, Jae Yeon Ryu, Dong Soo Yu, Young Bok Lee
Published: 2023

Dear Editor:

Dietary supplements have increased in popularity in Korea, but their effects on skin diseases have not been well studied1. Only few studies have reported the precipitation or exacerbation of autoimmune bullous disease associated with immuneenhancing dietary supplement intake2. Herein, we report a case of pemphigus foliaceus that developed in a patient who consumed a large amount of a food supplement containing spirulina.

A 79-year-old man presented with multiple, pruritic, variable-sized, erythematous, crusted patches with ruptured flaccid blisters on the whole body for several weeks (Fig. 1). On physical exam, the Nikolsky sign was positive. The patient had a history of hypertension and diabetes taking olmesartan, aspirin, cilostazol, metformin, empagliflozin, gliclazide, and r-thioctic acid tromethamine for decades. He also reported consuming Earth Spirulina (ES Biotech Inc.) for more than 10 years. He had consumed 8 grams a day in the past, but he increased the amount of daily intake to 20 grams a day for the last seven months, despite the recommended dose of 4 grams per day for adults.

Multiple, pruritic, variable-sized, erythematous, erosive, crusted patches with ruptured flaccid blisters were present on the patient’s whole body. We received the patient’s consent form about publishing all photographic materials.

A biopsy performed on the trunk showed subcorneal blisters with acantholytic cells. Eosinophils were observed in the blister cavity (Fig. 2A). A direct immunofluorescence study demonstrated intercellular deposition of immunoglobulin G in the epidermis (Fig. 2B). The histopathologic findings were consistent with those of pemphigus foliaceus.

(A) Histopathologic feature from a skin biopsy revealed subcorneal blister with acantholytic cells and eosinophils as well as dermal infiltration of lymphocytes (H&E, original magnification ×40). (B) Direct immunofluorescence on perilesional tissue showed immunoglobulin G deposition in the intercellular space of the epidermis.

The patient’s symptoms waxed and waned along the course of the treatment with prednisolone and antihistamines. After nine months, his condition rapidly exacerbated, resulting in admission to the hospital. He reported continued use of spirulina and was strongly advised to stop taking the supplement. After treatment with steroids and an immunosuppressive drug (azathioprine) for two weeks, his condition improved.

Spirulina is a type of blue-green algae that is commercially produced as a nutritional supplement. A few recent studies have reported the relationship between spirulina and autoimmune skin disorders1,2,3,4. Kraigher et al.2 reported a case of an immunoblistering disorder which showed mixed features of bullous pemphigoid and pemphigus foliaceus after one year spirulina intake. The skin lesions disappeared with prednisolone treatment and avoidance of spirulina. Lee and Werth3 also reported two cases involving spirulina. One patient experienced a flare of pemphigus vulgaris within 7 to 10 days following consumption of a spirulina-containing supplement. The other patient developed dermatomyositis 1 to 2 days after spirulina intake. It must be noted, however, that in the above three cases, the amount of the supplement intake was not provided in detail, and the time intervals between the supplement intake and the presentation of symptoms varied.

In the present case, the patient took spirulina in quantities of over five times more than the recommended dose and the patient’s disease improved after avoidance of the supplement and treatment with immunosuppressive drugs. Therefore, it is crucial to consider a patient’s medication history, including health supplements, when diagnosing a patient with an autoimmune skin-blistering disease. Further investigations are needed to provide rational evidence about the correlation between spirulina and pemphigus.
