Optimum feature selection for classification of PD signals produced by multiple insulation defects in electric motors
Authors: Waqar Hassan, G. Amjad Hussain, Abdul Wahid, Madia Safdar, Haris M. Khalid, Mohamad Kamarol Mohd Jamil
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Partial discharges (PD) are initiated in electrical equipment during various points of the equipment’s lifecycle. The intensity of PD defects rises continuously with time, which can lead to insulation degradation and reduced operational life of the electrical equipment. The optimum feature selection of PD signals captured, from different insulation defects, can enhance the classification accuracy of PD defects and facilitate better visualization of PD parameters for electric motor (EM) insulation monitoring and diagnostics. This paper presents a hybrid approach, based on Maximize Relevancy and Minimize Redundancy (mRMR) and random forest (RF), for the optimum feature selection and classification of PD signals in EMs containing multiple defects. For this purpose, four PD defects are developed in the EMs insulation under laboratory conditions, and 800 PD signals are acquired using a conventional IEC-60,270 experimental platform. The severity of these defects is determined and investigated based on PD characteristic parameters. Several features of both PD sweep signals and conventional PD pulses are extracted. Consequently, the mRMR feature selection technique is implemented to select the significant features of the detected PD signals. To establish the plausibility of this technique, several other feature selection algorithms, including RefliefF, Gini Index (GI), and Information Gain (IG), are introduced for the same datasets. The performance of all these feature selection algorithms is validated using three commonly used classification techniques such as RF, support vector machines (SVM), and k-nearest neighbors (k-NN). In summary, the results show that the combination of mRMR and RF proves to be the most effective feature selection algorithm for the classification of insulation defects in EMs, achieving an accuracy of 99.875%. This accuracy is significantly better than other feature selection and classification techniques and indicates its potential for application to other power system components.

Introduction

The dielectric insulation in electrical equipment, particularly electric motors (EMs), can deteriorate over time due to thermal, electrical, ambient, and mechanical (TEAM) stresses experienced during the operation1,2. To prevent potential issues with electrical equipment, it is necessary to assess the dielectric insulation condition at an initial stage. Partial discharge (PD) measurement is a widely recognized diagnostic tool used to assess the dielectric insulation condition of electrical equipment in both industrial applications and academic research3,4. The operating state of the electric equipment can be monitored by identifying the type and estimating the severity of PD signals captured from insulation defects. The dataset obtained from each PD source has a distinct phase-resolved PD (PRPD) pattern which can be recognized by extracting several features5,6. However, for the identification of PD signals, the recognition accuracy is significantly improved by the optimum selection of features7,8.

Several researchers have studied different types of PD features, such as statistical features, texture features, fractal features, spectral features of PD signals, and waveform features of conventional PD pulses9–13. In addition to these traditional features, different mathematical functions can be employed to generate a large number of combination features. Consequently, a PD pattern can yield thousands of features. When all these features are used as input parameters for training the recognition algorithms, the process will be complex and the recognition efficiency will be reduced. Investigating the relationship between the number of input features and the recognition accuracy is possible when features are ranked by their importance. Therefore, optimum feature selection can lead to improved recognition efficiency.

Recently, significant importance has been given to different feature selection techniques, such as Relief, RefliefF, Maximize Relevancy and Minimize Redundancy (mRMR), Gini Index (GI), Rough Set (RS), Information Gain (IG), Genetic Algorithm (GA), Random Forest (RF)14–17, discrete short-time Fourier transform (STFT)18, V-φ ultrasonic and q-φ discharge pulse signal scatter plots19, wavelet transform analysis20, etc. These techniques aim to identify a group of features from the original data that can enhance prediction or classification accuracy, reduce computation time and complexity by eliminating redundant features, and improve understanding of the results. For instance, Karimi et al.21 presents four methods of feature selection from raw PD data. The first approach is based on deep belief networks (DBN). The other three approaches employ statistical and vector-norm-based operators to select discriminatory features, which are then used to train a DBN classifier. Dai et al. in22 employed a S-transform and singular value decomposition technique for feature selection. Also, an improved support vector machines (SVM) using particle swarm algorithm is proposed for the classification of PD defects. In23, Duan et al. proposed a deep learning (DL) model for identifying four kinds of PD defects from the current waveforms. The DL framework is composed of a sparse autoencoder (SAE) layer and a softmax layer for feature extraction and classification, respectively. Peng et al. proposed RF based feature selection algorithm to identify the significant features from both PD signals and interference signals for data processing24.

In summary, the existing algorithms excel in distinguishing PD signals by selecting significant features from PD data. However, the performance of these algorithms is affected by the nature of PD data collected from various insulation defects. Therefore, it is difficult to accurately characterize closely related PD signals with limited information obtained from either pulse sequence or pulse shape analysis. To address this issue, a hybrid approach is proposed that includes both PD sweep signal and conventional PD pulse. This comprehensive approach considers the whole shape of PD signal, improving the classification efficiency. To attain optimum classification accuracy (above 99.5%), authors are motivated to perform extensive experimental comparisons of commonly used feature selection and classification techniques using laboratory data.

This paper aims to propose a hybrid optimum feature selection and classification technique for PD signals captured from multiple PD defects in EMs insulation using laboratory data. For this purpose, PD signals are captured from four different PD defects artificially created in the EMs insulation to obtain the raw PD data, and a set of 100 PD sweep signals and a set of 100 conventional PD pulses are extracted from each defect for further processing. Several features of the captured PD signals are generated and the severity of PD defects is investigated based on PD characteristic parameters. To select the optimum features of detected PD signals, different feature selection processes including mRMR, ReliefF, GI, and IG are introduced. An extensive experimental comparison of these methods is performed using three different classifiers (RF, SVM, and k-nearest neighbors (k-NN)), and four different PD defects. Based on the experimental results, it can be seen that the proposed mRMR with RF classifier leads to promising improvement in feature selection and classification accuracy (99.875%) than the previous techniques.

B. Experimental Platform.

According to IEC-60,270, Fig. 3 presents the standard PD measurement system for data acquisition of PD defects. The schematic diagram of PD experimental setup is presented in Fig. 3(a) and the laboratory physical arrangements in Fig. 3(b). The experimental setup consists of power source and PD detection mechanisms. The AC power source includes a single-phase AC supply connected to a voltage regulator (T1), and a high voltage step-up transformer (T2) connected in series to provide the regulated voltage at 50 Hz. With negligible PD (< 5 pC) detected in T2, it is considered PD free. In PD detection system, multiple stators of the induction motor with artificial PD defects were selected as a discharge source, represented as a test object (M1).

Fig. 3(a) Schematic diagram and (b) physical images of the PD circuit arranged in the laboratory.

(a) Schematic diagram and (b) physical images of the PD circuit arranged in the laboratory.

One phase of M1 is energized and the remaining two phases are grounded. A protecting resistor (2.5 MΩ) protects the test setup from possible over-currents due to any potential flashover occurring in M1. A coupling capacitor (Ck) is attached in parallel with M1, and a quadrupole (Qp) is connected in series with Ck. When PD occurs in M1, Ck draws the charges and transmits the PD current pulses to the Qp, which translates the PD current pulses to voltage signals. For conventional PD measurements, pulse transformer is connected in series with the Ck. A directly triggered oscilloscope (Tektronix TDS2001C) with a bandwidth of 50 MHz and sampling rate of 500 MS/s is used to acquire the output PD signals to digital samples. The complete PD envelope for 1.5 cycles (having reference time (Tref) equal to 30 ms) of applied voltage (Ua) and damped PD pulses are collected by the oscilloscope, with the attenuation of the measured PD signals limited by tuning the measurement system at 1 MHz frequency26. The maximum linear error and basic noise level allowed are 0.2 pC and 0.1 dB, respectively. The electric connection of M1 with the PD measurement system is made using a shielded cable of less than 1 m length, and the PD detection system is calibrated according to IEC standard guidelines at the beginning of each experiment to measure PD precisely27.

C. Measurement Procedure.

A typical PD sweep signal (30 ms) detected for case-1 at an applied voltage waveform represented by Ua (ti) and a typical damped single PD pulse (i) are presented in Fig. 4. The sweep signal captures all the PD signals present during a 1.5 power cycle of 30 ms whereas a damped single PD pulse corresponds to an individual PD activity and hence shows only one PD signal. Electronic damping circuit (RLC circuit) is used to dampen the oscillatory PD signal and acquire a unipolar PD pulse as shown in Fig. 4 (right). The single PD pulse is captured using Tektronix TDS2001C, denoted by Ui(t) and its amplitude is calibrated in mV as per IEC standard (IEC 60270). In the calibration process, a known amount of charge is injected to the test object using pulse calibrator and PD pulse amplitude is measured. The corresponding amplitude of the measured PD pulse represents the injected PD apparent charge. The process is repeated to determine a conversion scale between the PD pulse amplitude in mV and apparent charge in nC or pC.

Fig. 4Typical PD signal detected for case-1 and corresponding damped PD pulse with its amplitude represented in mV.

Typical PD signal detected for case-1 and corresponding damped PD pulse with its amplitude represented in mV.

PD measurements in different insulation defects are carried out using voltage rise test28. For this purpose, the test voltage was gradually increased to estimate the PDIV until the PD activity was observed and then, the test voltage was decreased steadily. This experiment is repeated 25 times and the PDIV (average value) for each PD defect is calculated. A prestress voltage equal to 1.5 × PDIV is applied for 5 min before the PD measurements. Therefore, by raising the Ua level in small step up to 1.5 × PDIV, several PD characteristic parameters are evaluated by investigating the PD signals obtained from different insulation defects. These parameters such as average discharge current (I), pulse repetition rate (m), accumulated apparent charge (qa), quadratic rate (D) and discharge power (P) are used to access the insulation condition and severity of various PD defects29.

Optimum features selection of PD signals

In this section, four different feature selection methods such as mRMR, ReliefF, GI, and IG are used for the selection of significant features of PD signals captured from four different insulation defects. To establish the plausibility of mRMR method, the comparison of these four feature selection techniques was carried out.

A. Minimum Redundancy and Maximum Relevance (mRMR).

To overcome the major drawback of IG, that the redundancy between features is not considered, the mRMR is introduced32. The mRMR is the most powerful and widely used filter feature selection technique for the analysis of large-scale datasets. where S represents the selected subset of features. B. ReliefF.

ReliefF33, supplanted the original Relief algorithm34, is a multivariate variable selection technique that is able to handle large-scale multiclass datasets with highly redundant features.

Let xi is randomly selected samples from class C. ReliefF finds k nearest neighbors from C, called nearest hits h. Then k-nearest neighbors from each distinct class is computed, called nearest misses m. Based on the values of xi, hits hj, and misses mj, ReliefF updates the better estimation R(f) for all features f. The updated mean of the contribution of all of the hits and misses can be computed as follows,10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:R\left(f\right)=R\left(f\right)-\frac{\sum\:_{j=1}^{k}diff\left(f,{x}_{i},\:{h}_{j}\right)}{m.k}+\sum\:_{c\ne\:class\left(P\right)}\left(\frac{P\left(C\right)\sum\:_{j=1}^{k}diff\left(f,{x}_{i},{m}_{j}\right)}{1-P\left(class\left({x}_{i}\right)\right)}\right)\times\:{\left(m.k\right)}^{-1}$$\end{document}

where diff(.) finds the difference between the feature, f, values of two sample observations. The contribution of each class of misses is weighted with prior probability class P(C) in data. The factor, 1-P(class(xi)) indicates the sum of the probabilities for each miss class.

D. Information Gain (IG).

Information Gain (IG) is a widely used correlation-based supervised univariate feature selection approach that estimate the dependence between the feature and the class label17. Depending on the information-theoretical idea of entropy, IG uses the correlation approach to evaluate the uncertainty of random features. IG is a powerful feature selection technique that has the advantages of easy computation and simple interpretation. IG of a feature f and the class labels C is defined by (12),12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:IG\left(f,C\right)=H\left(f\right)-H\left(\frac{f}{C}\right)$$\end{document}

where H(.) denotes entropy of a variable. Therefore, the entropy of feature f, H(f), is given by (13).13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H\left(f\right)=-\sum\:_{i}p\left({f}_{i}\right){\text{l}\text{o}\text{g}}_{2}\left[p\left({f}_{i}\right)\right]$$\end{document}

The maximum value of IG is 1. For each feature, IG is independently evaluated and the features with high values of IG are considered as relevant. However, the redundant features are not eliminated in IG algorithm. The entropy of feature f after observing the values of class variable C is given by (14).14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H\left(f/C\right)=-\sum\:_{j}p\left({c}_{j}\right)\sum\:_{i}p\left(\frac{{f}_{i}}{{c}_{j}}\right){\text{l}\text{o}\text{g}}_{2}\left[p\left(\frac{{f}_{i}}{{c}_{j}}\right)\right]$$\end{document}

Classification accuracy

To investigate the best combination of feature selection and classification techniques, the performance of feature selection algorithms is validated using three commonly used classification techniques such as RF, SVM, and k-NN. For this purpose, 10-fold cross-validation is performed to train the classifiers so that each class is represented in each fold. RF feature selection algorithm is employed using the R package (randomForest) with its preset parameters, i.e., ntree, mtry, and nodesize are fixed at 400, 1 to 13, and 1, respectively. For each combination of dataset, fifty repetitions of training and testing are performed, and the average accuracy is determined for all the repetitions. The SVM and K-NN classifiers are implemented using R packages ‘class’ and ‘e1071’, respectively. In the SVM algorithm, a linear kernel and a grid of values between 0.01 and 10 of the cost parameter are used. A. Random Forest.

Random Forest (RF) is a specific tree-based model that helps to reduce overfitting in decision tree (DT)24. It is an ensemble ML algorithm that is capable of performing both classification and regression tasks. The RF uses the bagging technique, which involves combining multiple models to create a single model, more effective than DT regression and bootstrap aggregating DT models. It also utilizes dimensional reduction to reduce prediction variance, handles outlier values, takes care of missing values, and completes other important steps of data analysis. The RF is composed of many DTs that each output a class, which is then used to generate the final output.

Conclusion

In this paper, a hybrid approach was presented for optimum feature selection and classification of PD signals captured from multiple PD defects in EM insulation using laboratory data. For this purpose, PD signals were captured from four different insulation defects to acquire the raw PD data, and a set of 400 PD sweep signals and a set of 400 conventional PD pulses were extracted for further processing. Multiple features are extracted from the captured PD signals, and the severity of these PD defects is estimated based on several characteristic parameters. To investigate the optimum features in the detected PD signals, different feature selection techniques are employed. An extensive experimental comparison of these techniques is carried out using different classifiers. The study concluded that the combination of mRMR and RF proves to be the most effective feature selection algorithm for the classification of insulation defects in EMs, with an accuracy of 99.875%. Additionally, the following conclusions can be derived from the experimental results:

The severity of insulation defects is evaluated by determining different PD characteristic parameters. Based on the experimental results, it is concluded that case-3 has the highest severity level, whereas case-4 has the lowest severity level among the four insulation defects.

As demonstrated by RF, SVM, and k-NN results, the mRMR-based feature selection algorithm was the most effective technique for analyzing PD signals from EMs insulation and distinguishing between different types of defects. The accuracy of mRMR with RF, SVM, and k-NN is 99.987%, 99.962%, and 99.246%, respectively. Furthermore, the most significant feature in mRMR, IG, and ReliefF is qi (F7). However, the least significant features include ΔUn (F2), Et (F14), and ΔUn/Δtn (F5).

Initially, the classification accuracy increases rapidly when the number of features applied to the classifier increases but levels off and tends to decrease slightly after reaching around 5–7 features. For example, the highest accuracy of mRMR with RF, SVM, and k-NN is at 5, 6, and 2 features, respectively.

The PD signals investigated in this paper were collected from four artificial defects under laboratory conditions. Future research will focus on selecting the optimal features to be included in PD data captured using an online monitoring system on industrial applications. Using a wide range of PD signals, our conclusions can be further estimated.
