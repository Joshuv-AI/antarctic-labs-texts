Guardrails for GenAI drafted replies in patient portal messaging
Authors: Johnny Sakr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenAI is increasingly used to draft replies to patient portal messages to reduce clinician workload. Evidence shows modest utilization and measurable workflow effects, alongside safety risks when clinicians miss errors or accept drafts with minimal editing. As deployments scale with deeper EHR integration and expanding transparency mandates, this Comment proposes governance that preserves efficiency without normalizing unsafe delegation: scoped use, risk tiering, accountable human authorship, auditability, and patient-facing transparency.Subject terms: Health care, Mathematics and computing

Draft replies are becoming a care pathway, not a clerical aid

Patient portal messaging has shifted from “extra communication” into a routine channel for clinical care, triage, and longitudinal management. When generative AI (GenAI) drafts replies inside the electronic health record (EHR), it does more than speed up text entry: it can influence what clinicians communicate, what patients do next, and how risk is framed. As a result, AI-drafted messaging should be treated as a clinical workflow intervention—and governed accordingly.

From a governance perspective, this mirrors a familiar problem in other socio-technical domains: responsibility diffuses precisely where functional influence increases. Automation does not eliminate accountability; it redistributes it across interfaces, institutions, and workflows. Treating AI drafts as merely clerical obscures this redistribution and risks normalizing what is, in effect, silent delegation of clinical judgment. Accountability, therefore, turns less on whether AI is “used,” and more on whether responsibility remains clearly anchored despite its use1. Importantly, this redistribution extends to technology developers: if the GenAI tool itself produces consistently low-quality or contextually inappropriate drafts, the resulting burden on clinicians may exceed their capacity to identify and correct errors effectively, rendering downstream guardrails insufficient.

Two recent strands of evidence are especially salient.

First, real-world data show that GenAI drafts can yield efficiency gains, including reductions in perceived cognitive burden and message turnaround time, but also introduce new forms of workflow burden2,3. Mandal et al. analyzed EHR audit logs from a large health system (Oct 2023–Aug 2024) and found overall utilization was low, but improved after prompt refinements; drafts were also generated for 80% of messages that received no clinician response—many of which were informational updates or closing messages in ongoing threads that did not clinically require a direct reply—creating added review load. Even so, turnaround time fell by ~6–7%, suggesting that targeted, better-scoped deployment can deliver modest operational benefit4. Critically, these efficiency figures do not reveal how carefully clinicians reviewed drafts before sending; because reduced turnaround time is equally consistent with diligent editing and with superficial acceptance, reported gains cannot be disentangled from the automation complacency risk described below.

Second, controlled testing shows a safety gap: clinicians may fail to detect and correct clinically meaningful errors in AI drafts. In a simulation study, Biro et al. embedded objective inaccuracies and potentially harmful omissions into AI-generated draft responses. Each error was insufficiently addressed by 13–15 of 20 participants, and 35–45% of erroneous drafts were submitted entirely unedited. Notably, errors varied in severity: while some represented low-risk inaccuracies, others involved potentially harmful omissions, such as failing to address symptoms requiring urgent medical evaluation, underscoring the clinical stakes of unchecked automation. The authors also report high clinician trust in the tool—precisely the combination that makes automation complacency plausible at scale5.

The point is not that AI drafting is inherently unsafe. The point is that “drafting” is already downstream of risk: patient messages often contain subtle red flags, incomplete context, or urgent ambiguity. If GenAI becomes a default first pass, the system’s benefits will be real—but so will the probability that rare but severe failures leak through review. Governance should therefore aim at an equilibrium: reduce inbox burden without quietly reassigning clinical judgment to an unaccountable text generator.

Why existing consent and disclosure norms do not cleanly fit

Clinical disclosure norms were not designed for ubiquitous embedded automation. Patients are not typically told that spellcheck ran, templates were used, or a clinical decision support rule nudged a workup. Yet GenAI is different in kind. It introduces content that is neither purely templated nor transparently rule-based, nor equivalent to deterministic machine learning models used in clinical decision support; its influence is exercised upstream of clinician judgment rather than downstream of it. It can introduce novel content, elide uncertainty, and produce plausible but incorrect recommendations.

Recent ethical work argues that patient expectations and trust can be undermined if AI meaningfully shapes communication while remaining invisible, and it offers a framework for deciding when disclosure is ethically required6. The core practical insight is that disclosure should be risk- and materiality-sensitive: not every assistive function merits patient notification, but patients should not be misled about who is speaking, how recommendations are produced, or what accountability exists.

In patient portal messaging, the communication itself is often the intervention. A reply might reassure, escalate, advise self-care, recommend emergency evaluation, change medication adherence, or interpret test results. That makes disclosure and accountability harder to treat as optional “nice to haves.”

Five guardrails for safe scalable use

1) Scope GenAI drafting to message types where failure is low consequence

Mandal et al. show that drafts were generated even for messages that ultimately received no response—suggesting poor targeting4. Systems should constrain drafting to message classes where (a) needed context is usually present, and (b) harm from a subtle mistake is limited. Examples might include routine administrative clarifications, follow-up scheduling guidance, or standardized education tied to a clinician-entered plan—ideally integrated with existing EHR message classifiers or natural language processing (NLP) flags for low-risk content. Emerging work demonstrates the feasibility of such classification: recent studies have used NLP and large language model-based approaches to triage patient portal messages by urgency, including detection of emergency content with promising accuracy, suggesting that automated risk stratification of incoming messages is increasingly practicable7.

Conversely, GenAI should be restricted or disabled for message categories that are inherently high-risk: chest pain equivalents, neurologic symptoms, pregnancy-related bleeding, mental health crisis language, anticoagulant complications, pediatrics with limited history, or situations where missing one sentence can change urgency.

A related concern is that risk classification may itself create a false sense of security: if a message is labeled “low-risk” and a draft is generated, clinicians may anchor on the system’s categorization and assume the draft is safe, reducing the very scrutiny the classification was intended to preserve. Scoping must therefore be accompanied by interface cues that maintain clinician vigilance regardless of assigned tier.

This is not merely a clinical preference; it is a governance principle: the easier it is for an error to become harm, the higher the evidentiary threshold for deploying automation.

2) Tier messages by risk and require “active attestation” for higher tiers

Biro et al. show that physicians sometimes accept flawed drafts with limited editing, consistent with well-known human factors risks5. A governance response is to require different levels of human action depending on risk.

Tier 2 (moderate risk): clinician must edit at least one clinically meaningful element (e.g., assessment/plan or red-flag instructions) before send.

Any tiering model will introduce friction. The justification is proportionality: for low-risk messages, clinicians experience no additional steps; moderate-risk messages add a single mandatory interaction; only high-risk messages trigger substantive workflow changes. Whether these incremental costs are outweighed by prevented errors is ultimately an empirical question, but the default alternative—uniform low-friction processing across all risk levels—is precisely what enables the most dangerous failures to pass undetected.

The goal is not bureaucracy. It is to prevent the most dangerous mode of failure: illusory safety created by superficial review.

I acknowledge that mandatory editing at Tier 2 carries a risk of ritual compliance—clinicians may make a single tokenistic change without conducting a genuine review. This limitation is well recognized in human factors research, where forced interaction does not guarantee cognitive engagement8. The Tier 2 requirement should therefore be treated as one layer within a defense-in-depth strategy rather than a standalone safeguard. Complementary measures—particularly reframing the AI draft from “ready to send” to a “starting point requiring verification,” combined with structured prompts that surface potential gaps—may be more effective at inducing meaningful scrutiny. Empirical studies examining error detection rates across tiers will be essential to validate and refine this model.

Tiering is not about mistrusting clinicians; it is about recognizing that responsibility cannot be meaningfully exercised where review becomes perfunctory. Where systems are designed in ways that predictably encourage automation complacency, governance, not individual vigilance, must carry the corrective burden5.

3) Preserve accountable human authorship in both fact and interface

Accountable authorship is the linchpin of safe GenAI deployment in clinical communication. While drafting may be assistive, responsibility for clinical meaning, prioritization, and instruction remains non-delegable—even where the proximate text originates elsewhere9. In patient messaging, the clinician should remain the author in a substantive sense: accountable for clinical judgment, tone, and instruction.

Consistent with legal and governance scholarship that treats accountability as functionally allocable rather than metaphysically grounded1,10, this approach distributes specific responsibility elements—such as documentation integrity, audit obligations, and liability triggers—across clinicians, institutions, and systems, without implying machine agency or legal personhood.

Interface design matters. If the EHR presents the AI draft as “ready to send,” it primes acceptance; if it presents it as a “starting point requiring verification,” it primes scrutiny. Building on human factors principles, health systems should:

4) Make drafts auditable and monitor failure modes continuously

The strongest safety argument for GenAI drafting is that it can be monitored, improved, and constrained faster than human-only workflows—if systems are instrumented.

This matters because Mandal et al. show workflow effects are measurable and not uniformly positive, making “monitor and recalibrate” essential rather than optional4.

Auditability is also a fairness issue: if some patient groups receive more “templated” or less empathetic drafts due to differential editing patterns, inequities may be silently amplified. Health equity stakeholders are increasingly calling for governance that includes bias audits and transparency reporting in healthcare AI, with audit logs ideally including stratified analyses by patient demographics to detect and mitigate such disparities11.

Without such traceability, post-hoc accountability collapses into speculation, and systems cannot distinguish between individual lapse and structural risk, an outcome that undermines both safety learning and fairness.

5) Adopt patient-facing transparency that is truthful but non-disruptive

Transparency should not mean dumping technical disclaimers into clinical messages. Recent patient surveys show a mild preference for AI-drafted messages (often perceived as more detailed and empathetic), yet slightly lower satisfaction when AI involvement is disclosed, underscoring the ethical need for transparency to preserve autonomy despite potential minor impacts on trust6. A practical approach is a stable, institution-level disclosure that:

This can be implemented via persistent portal banners or footers, aligning with emerging ethical arguments that disclosure should protect trust without impeding care6. It also reduces the risk of a “therapeutic illusion” where patients attribute empathy or expertise to the machine rather than the clinician relationship.

Where this sits in broader regulation

Internationally, governance is moving toward risk-based obligations for AI, emphasizing documentation, transparency, and accountability for higher-risk uses. The European Union’s (EU) AI Act is a clear example of this direction even if health systems elsewhere differ in implementation details12. In the United States, FDA work on AI/ML software as a medical device reflects similar themes: lifecycle monitoring, transparency, and governance processes13. And general risk management frameworks stress organizational controls, measurement, and incident handling9.

As of 2026, state-level mandates reinforce these concerns for patient-facing tools. California’s Health & Safety Code §1339.75 (AB 3030) requires disclosures for GenAI-generated patient communications concerning clinical information unless reviewed by a licensed clinician14. Texas has adopted parallel transparency requirements: SB 1188 mandates that practitioners disclose the use of AI for diagnostic purposes to patients15, while HB 149 (the Texas Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act) requires disclosure when consumers interact with AI systems, including in healthcare service contexts16. Together, these developments underscore the expectation that patients remain informed when automated systems shape clinical communication.

This regulatory trajectory aligns with jurisprudential frameworks that conceptualize AI accountability in functional terms—distributing responsibility across human and institutional actors without invoking metaphysical claims about machine agency1,10. Although portal-messaging GenAI may not always fall within formal medical-device regulation, it can function as one when it shapes triage and care decisions. Health systems should therefore not wait for perfect category alignment; the governance package outlined above is implementable now and adaptable across regulatory environments.

Conclusion

The empirical picture is now clear enough to justify a shift from pilot enthusiasm to governance discipline. Real-world deployments can yield modest efficiency gains but also generate drafts in contexts that add burden rather than reduce it. Simulation evidence shows that clinically significant errors may slip through review, especially when trust is high.

The governance challenge posed by GenAI drafting is not novel in kind but familiar in structure: responsibility tends to diffuse precisely where automation exerts the greatest influence. Treating draft replies as clerical assistance mischaracterizes their role in care delivery. The guardrails proposed here do not reject automation; they insist that its benefits be realized without dissolving accountability. This discipline is increasingly urgent as deployments mature amid deeper EHR integration and expanding transparency requirements. In patient messaging, as elsewhere in medicine, the central ethical commitment is not to efficiency alone, but to ensuring that responsibility remains intelligible, traceable, and owned.
