Reevaluation of ECGs of a German non coronary sudden cardiac arrest cohort
Authors: Fabienne Kreimer, Pia Thiesing, Ibrahim Akin, Nazha Hamdani, Michael Gotzmann, Andreas Mügge, Assem Aweimer, Ibrahim El-Battrawy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile primarily a disease of older adults due to coronary artery disease, sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) also affects younger individuals, particularly those with genetic predispositions or congenital heart defects. This study aimed to systematically investigate SCA in younger patients (aged ≤ 65 years), focusing on non-coronary artery-related causes. Patients admitted due to SCA or acute resuscitation scenarios at the University Hospital Bergmannsheil in Bochum, Germany, between 2010 and 2021, were comprehensively investigated. Patients were categorized into groups based on their diagnosed etiology, with non-coronary causes being the primary focus of this analysis. 232 patients with SCA were included. The median age was 52 years, 66.7% were male. The causes were idiopathic (54.2%), cardiomyopathies (29.2%), myocarditis (8.3%), valve diseases (2.1%) and channelopathies (6.3%). Two independent cardiologists reviewed all ECGs, at least two from each case. Post-resuscitation ECG analysis identified Short- and Long-QT-syndrome in two patients previously classified as idiopathic SCA. SCA was witnessed by bystanders in 87.5% of cases, with layperson cardiopulmonary resuscitation in 45.8%. SCA occurred predominantly at home (37.5%) and in public places (29.2%). In-hospital mortality was 29.2%. During a mean follow-up of 53.2 ± 7.3 months, three patients died. ICDs documented ventricular tachyarrhythmias in nine patients. This study comprehensively analyzed 48 non-coronary SCA patients from a cohort of 232 SCA cases. Introduction

Sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) is a major public health challenge and contributes to a significant proportion of cardiovascular-related mortality worldwide1,2. Each year, SCA affects approximately 4–5 million people worldwide, with approximately 350,000 to 450,000 cases in the United States alone1.

The incidence of SCA varies significantly between different populations and age groups1,2. It is predominantly a disease of older adults, with the highest incidence rates observed in people over the age of 65 years1. However, it can also affect younger people, particularly those with a genetic predisposition or congenital heart defects1–3. The causes of SCA vary and are often age-dependent1,2. In older adults, coronary artery disease is the predominant underlying disease and accounts for 70–80% of SCA cases1,2. Younger people, on the other hand, are more likely to have SCA due to inherited cardiac arrhythmias, cardiomyopathies, and congenital heart defects3. Specific conditions such as hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, brugada syndrome (BrS), long QT syndrome (LQTS), and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC) are more common in younger populations and athletes1,2,4,5.

Idiopathic SCA refers to cardiac arrest for which no specific cause can be identified despite extensive clinical investigation6. This condition is of particular concern as it affects individuals who otherwise appear healthy and present without obvious risk factors or underlying cardiac disease6. The CASPER study included 63 patients (age 43.0 ± 13.4 years, 29 women) from nine centers who suffered an unexplained cardiac arrest without obvious cardiac disease (normal cardiac function on echocardiogram, no evidence of coronary artery disease, and a standard electrocardiogram (ECG))6. These patients underwent a comprehensive workup that included magnetic resonance imaging of the heart, signal-averaged ECG, exercise testing, drug testing, and selective electrophysiologic testing6. Diagnoses were based on established criteria or provocation of characteristic clinical features for conditions such as LQTS, catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT), BrS, early repolarization, ARVC, coronary spasm, and myocarditis6. A specific diagnosis was established in 35 patients (56%)6. Targeted genetic testing revealed causative mutations in 9 (47%) of 19 patients6. Screening of 64 family members of these patients identified 15 affected individuals (24%)6. The remaining 28 patients (44%) were classified as idiopathic ventricular fibrillation6. Hence, systematic clinical testing identified the cause of unexplained cardiac arrest in more than 50% of patients6. These results emphasize the importance of careful investigation of patients with unexplained cardiac arrest to uncover the underlying conditions and thus enable appropriate treatment and preventive measures for patients and their families.

The present study aimed to systematically investigate a young SCA cohort (age ≤ 65 years) focusing on patients with non-coronary artery-related causes of a referral center in Germany.

Methods

Study design, data collection, inclusion, and exclusion criteria

This study investigated all patients admitted due to SCA, acute resuscitation scenarios, or those presenting at the central emergency department at the University Hospital Bergmannsheil in Bochum, Germany. We used the hospital’s internal registry to identify records with ICD codes I47.2, I49.0, I46.-, and R57.0, representing ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, and flutter, survived sudden cardiac death, and cardiogenic shock. This comprehensive search covered the period from 2010 to 2021 and included 4,728 patients. The study was conducted according to the.

Guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the local ethics committee.

The study focused on individuals 65 years and younger at admission. Patient data were collected, including medical history, medication, laboratory results, ECG, echocardiography, and coronary angiography at the time of SCA. We excluded duplicate records and cases with incomplete data sets. Additionally, patients whose resuscitation was due to non-cardiac causes (e.g., trauma, intoxication, or other underlying conditions) and those involved in peri- and post-interventional resuscitation scenarios were excluded. The final study cohort consisted of 232 patients (Fig. 1).

Schematic flow chart of the study cohort.

Patients were categorized into three groups based on their diagnosed etiology. The first group included cases with coronary causes (139 patients), such as coronary artery disease, vasospasm, and coronary vascular dissection. The second group (48 patients) consisted of patients with non-coronary causes, including cardiomyopathies, cardiac channelopathies, myocarditis, and idiopathic cases. The third group (45 patients) encompassed patients whose rapid death precluded definitive diagnostic classification into the previous groups. This analysis focused on the second group. Idiopathic case is defined if resting ECG, holter ECG, stress test, cardiac MRI/echocardiography and/or cardiac biopsies were normal.

ECG re-analysis

ECGs were analysed and re-analysed by two independent cardiologists. The documentation of the ECGs was at least 48 h after the resuscitation and/or hypothermia treatment. Furthermore, ECGs were written during the time of termination drugs related to ECGs changes. Even more, electrolyte imbalance was excluded. Reviewers were blinded to initial diagnoses and clinical outcomes. The criteria for the diagnosis of SCA cause was considered according to current ESC guidelines and statement papers33. The QT interval was measured using the tangent method in the precordial lead presenting the highest T-wave amplitude in V2 or V3.

Follow-up data

In addition to the retrospective evaluation, the surviving patients were included in a prospective follow-up after the SCA event. This follow-up was extended by contacting cardiologists, general practitioners, patients, or their families. The follow-up was completed at the end of March 2023. Patients received routine check-ups and device interrogations at our outpatient clinic six weeks after implantation. These were followed by regular evaluations every six months, either at our university outpatient clinic or at a cardiologist’s practice. The interrogations of the patients’ implantable cardioverter defibrillators (ICDs) were analyzed to assess ICD shocks and anti-tachycardia pacing. The primary aim was to collect data on the frequency of implantations, recurrence of ventricular tachyarrhythmias and subsequent ICD shocks or anti-tachycardia pacing, and ICD-related complications.

Statistics

The statistical software SPSS 29 was used to perform the statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables with a normal distribution, as median (interquartile range) for continuous variables with a non-normal distribution, and as frequency (%) for categorical variables. For comparing continuous variables between groups, an unpaired t-test was used for normally distributed variables, while the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test was used for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using either χ2 analysis or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All probability values demonstrated are two-sided.

Results

From 2010 to 2021, we enrolled 232 consecutive patients with SCA (Fig. 1). 187 patients were divided into groups with coronary (n = 139) (first group) and non-coronary cause of death (n = 48) (second group). In 45 patients, the etiology of SCA could not be identified, e.g., because no coronary angiography or further diagnostic investigations were performed due to death (third group). In this analysis, we focused on the patients with non-coronary related SCA (second group).

Patient characteristics of the cohort with non-coronary associated SCA

The study analyzed 48 patients with non-coronary related SCA. In 26 of 48 patients (54.2%), the cause of SCA was idiopathic. Cardiomyopathies were responsible for SCA in 14 cases (29.2%), myocarditis in four cases (8.3%), and valvular disease in one case (2.1%). Channelopathies were identified in three cases (6.3%), including LQTS (n = 2, 4.2%; congenital, n = 1, 2.1%) and Short-QT syndrome (SQTS) (n = 1, 2.1%) (Fig. 2). The SQTS and LQTS cases were only identified through a profound re-evaluation of the cases; see below.

Causes of sudden cardiac death after diagnostic work-up.

The median age of the study cohort was 52, ranging from 16 to 65. Thirty-two patients (66.7%) were male. The median duration of total hospitalization was 13 days, ranging from one to 144 days, and the median duration of intensive care unit (ICU) hospitalization was five days, ranging from zero to 29 days. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation data

The first monitored rhythms were ventricular fibrillation in 33 patients (68.8%), ventricular tachycardia in six patients (12.5%), asystole in eight patients (16.7%), and pulseless electrical activity in one patients (2.1%). Defibrillators were used in 37 patients (77.1%), with a median of 1.5 shocks delivered (range 1–10). In none of the cases mechanical resuscitation assistance was used.

SCA occurred at home (n = 18, 37.5%), at work (n = 8, 16.7%), in public places (n = 14, 29.2%), in ambulances (n = 1, 2.1%), in hospitals (n = 8, 16.7%) and in unknown locations (n = 1, 2.1%).

Cardiac arrest was observed during resting (10.4%), sports (12.5%), non-strenuous daily activities (31.3%), working (12.5%), and unknown activities (31.3%).

In 77.1% of cases (n = 37), cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was initiated within 10 min, with the total duration of CPR between 11 and 20 min in most cases. Bystanders witnessed cardiac arrests in 87.5% of cases (n = 42), and laypersons performed CPR in 45.8% of cases (n = 22) (Table 2).

Demographics of clinical presentation and treatment approaches during hospitalization

The median left ventricular ejection fraction after successful recovery of circulation was 54.5% (range 10–70%), and this increased to 60% (range 35–71%) during six months of follow-up. The median initial body temperature was 36.6 °C (36.2–37.0 °C).

Intrahospital diagnostics comprised echocardiography (n = 42, 87.5%), magnetic resonance imaging (n = 20, 41.7%), ajmaline testing (n = 6, 12.5%), electrophysiologic testing (n = 4, 8.3%), and exercise ECG (n = 1, 2.1%).

During hospitalization, 20 patients (41.7%) underwent hypothermia treatment. 24 patients (50%) underwent ICD implantation, four patients (8.3%) had implantation recommended or planned, and five patients (10.4%) already had an ICD. Circulatory assist devices were used in 10.4% of cases (n = 5) (Table 3).

Follow-up data

In-hospital findings for short-term outcomes demonstrated that 14 patients (29.2%) died during hospitalization, with a median time to death of two days (range 1–13).

Of the 34 of 48 patients who were followed up for an average of 53.2 months (± 7.3), three patients died, of whom one died within the first year after the SCA event, and two died after at least five years. The causes of death were cardiovascular-related in one patient and unknown in two patients.

ICDs were implanted in 33 patients (68.8%), including nine subcutaneous ICDs (18.8%) and 24 transvenous ICDs (81.2%). Documented ventricular tachyarrhythmias occurred in nine patients, including sustained ventricular tachycardia in two patients, ventricular fibrillation in seven patients, and non-sustained ventricular tachycardia in two patients, with a mean time to first ventricular tachyarrhythmia of 573 ± 553 days. Two patients had more than one episode of ventricular tachyarrhythmias. Treatment of ventricular tachyarrhythmias included appropriate ICD shock delivery in six patients, inappropriate shock delivery in four patients, and successful anti-tachycardia pacing in two patients (Table 4; Fig. 6).

Short- and Long-QT syndrome patients

Two independent cardiologists analyzed the ECGs at the time of admission and in the following days after resuscitation to determine the underlying cause. The ECGs included in this second-look analysis were recorded at least 48 h after any possible therapeutic hypothermia treatment, ensuring that the observed changes were detectable in serial ECG recordings. In two patients with an unexplained cause of SCA, the reason was subsequently related to SQTS and congenital LQTS (Table 5).

Patient with congenital Long-QT syndrome

The patient was a 46-year-old man with congenital LQTS who suffered cardiac arrest during a non-strenuous and non-stressful activity in public. His left ventricular ejection fraction was 65%. The cardiac arrest was witnessed, but unfortunately, no layman-performed resuscitation was initiated. Initial rhythm analysis revealed ventricular fibrillation. Sinus rhythm was successfully restored with a single shock from an external defibrillator. No hypothermia treatment was performed. Serum potassium levels were in the normal range on admission.

The patient had no other pre-existing medical conditions and did not take any medication. There was a family history of sudden cardiac death; both his father and grandfather had suffered cardiac arrest. The 12-lead resting ECG revealed a prolonged QTc time of 624 ms (Fig. 3).

Three days after the event, the patient received a transvenous ICD and was subsequently transferred to a rehabilitation facility. His neurological outcome, as assessed by the Glasgow Outcome Scale, indicated a good recovery.

The patient was followed up for 94 months. He suffered another ventricular fibrillation episode 144 days after ICD implantation, which the ICD adequately terminated. The patient did not suffer any ICD-related complications.

Patient with Short-QT syndrome

The SQTS patient was a 30-year-old male who suffered SCA at work as a police officer during an assignment in which he was running. Cardiac arrest was bystander-witnessed, and layman-performed CPR was initiated. The first documented rhythm was ventricular fibrillation. An external defibrillator was used, and one shock was delivered to restore sinus rhythm.

The patient was of Caucasian ethnicity, had no pre-existing medical conditions, and did not take any medications. He had no symptoms before the SCA event, neither syncope, palpitations, or otherwise documented supraventricular and ventricular tachycardias. Potassium values were within the normal range. No hypothermia treatment was performed.

A further ECG was obtained three years and two months later, demonstrating a QTc time of 355 ms and a J-point to T-wave peak of 140 ms (Figs. 5 and 6).

The left ventricular ejection fraction was 61% at admission. Assessment of his neurological outcome using the Glasgow Outcome Scale revealed an excellent recovery. No electrophysiologic study was performed on this patient. After the SCA event, he received a wearable cardioverter defibrillator for three months and, after that, a subcutaneous ICD. During the follow-up of 44 months, no ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, adequate or inadequate shocks, or anti-tachycardia pacing could be documented. Furthermore, the patient did not suffer any ICD-related complications. Genetic testing has been performed, and the results are negative for HERG, KCNQ1 and KCNj2 genes. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of 48 patients with non-coronary related SCA from a cohort of 232 patients with cardiac arrest enrolled between 2010 and 2021. The main findings were: (1) Cardiac arrests were most common at home and in public places and occurred most frequently during non-strenuous daily activities. (2) Idiopathic causes were the most common, accounting for 54.2% of cases, and during the follow-up, seven patients suffered from appropriate ICD shocks. (3) ECG re-analysis identified two patients with SQTS and congenital LQTS previously classified as idiopathic SCA.

Non-coronary related SCA

Particularly in younger patients, as in our study, coronary artery disease is less prominent as a major cause of SCA than in older patients. Previously, a study examined adults aged 20 to 45 years who had ventricular tachyarrhythmias, including patients who suffered an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest7. Among the age group of 20 to 34 years, 27% of cases were classified as idiopathic, while 18% of cases were due to channelopathies and 13% to primary cardiomyopathies7. In contrast, acute myocardial infarction was the cause of ventricular arrhythmia in only 11% of cases7. Other causes were toxic cardiomyopathy in 9%, secondary cardiomyopathy in 7%, and myocarditis in 8% of cases7.

In the age group of 35 to 45 years, there is a clear shift towards acute myocardial infarction, which was the cause in 28% of cases7. However, 20% of cases were still considered idiopathic7. On the one hand, these data emphasize the significance of inherited arrhythmias, such as channelopathies and cardiomyopathies, which are frequently responsible for SCA in the absence of coronary artery disease in younger patients7,8, and on the other hand, these data indicate that a large proportion of SCA at a younger age are classified as idiopathic. Our cohort, which additionally included patients aged 46 to 65 years, demonstrated an even more pronounced shift towards coronary causes as triggers of SCA, with a total number of 139 out of 232 cases (59.9%). However, our cohort included not only patients with coronary artery disease but also those with other coronary issues, such as coronary spasms.

Data from the national National Cardiac Inherited Diseases Registry in New Zealand indicated that 51% of resuscitated SCA cases had underlying congenital heart disease, including LQTS (42%), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (24%), Brugada syndrome (14%), catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (8%), arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (8%), and dilated cardiomyopathy (4%)9. Like our and other studies, most cases (62%) were male9. Most SCA events (40%) occurred during normal daily activities9. Notably, patients under 24 years old had more frequent LQTS and catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, while those over 24 years had more commonly cardiomyopathies and Brugada syndrome9. For those over 40 years of age, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy was the most common cause9.

The term idiopathic ventricular fibrillation describes survivors of unexplained cardiac arrest without a specific diagnosis after a diagnostic workup10. In our overall cohort, 11.2% of SCA cases were classified as idiopathic. Taking only the non-coronary associated SCA cases into account, the proportion increases to 54.2%. The underlying causes of SCA can often remain hidden and undiagnosed, particularly in younger populations8. Comprehensive diagnostic approaches, including genetic testing, are needed to uncover latent causes and improve patient outcomes8. However, a study by Waldmann et al. revealed that a deficient proportion of unexplained SCA cases receive thorough investigation11. While cardiac magnetic resonance imaging identified the cause in 3.5% of diagnoses, other investigations accounted for an additional 2.4%11. Nevertheless, 55.7% were diagnosed with idiopathic ventricular fibrillation, primarily middle-aged (48.7 ± 15 years) and predominantly male (69.4%)11. Only 16.3% of idiopathic cases underwent a complete diagnostic workup11. Moreover, younger patients and those admitted to university centers received more comprehensive evaluations11. Other studies have also demonstrated that a systematic diagnostic evaluation can lead to a diagnosis in many patients with unexplained SCA6,12. However, approximately half of these cases remain classified as idiopathic, similar to our findings6.

Regarding the outcomes of patients with unexplained and idiopathic SCA, there are reports indicating recurrence rates of ventricular arrhythmias up to 31%, while mortality rates are relatively low (3–5%)13–15. In our study, the recurrence rate of 34.6% was comparable, but the mortality rate was proportionally high. This could be explained by the fact that other studies only included patients who survived the acute phase after SCA.

Studies investigating the benefit of ICD therapy in patients with idiopathic ventricular fibrillation have demonstrated that approximately one-third of patients benefited from ICD therapy and received appropriate ICD shocks13,16. However, there are also reports of inappropriate ICD shocks occurring in up to 18% of cases and device-related complications in 15% of cases13,16. This underscores the importance of a careful diagnostic work-up to identify potentially treatable causes and conditions, avoid inappropriate ICD shocks, and reduce the overall recurrence of ventricular arrhythmias. Subcutaneous ICDs (S-ICDs) may represent a more effective alternative to transvenous ICDs to reduce the rate of inappropriate ICD shocks and lead-associated complications17–20. Recently, a multicentre registry examined the efficacy and safety of S-ICDs in patients with cardiomyopathies and channelopathies21. From January 2016 to December 2020, 628 patients with cardiomyopathies or channelopathies underwent S-ICD implantation and were followed up until December 2022. After 12 months, the rate of inappropriate shocks was 4.6% in patients with cardiomyopathies and 1.1% in patients with channelopathies. At a median follow-up of 43 months, there were no significant differences in the rate of inappropriate shocks. The rate of appropriate shocks at 12 months was 2.3% in patients with cardiomyopathy and 2.1% in patients with channelopathy, also with no significant differences. Device-related complications occurred in 0.9% of cardiomyopathy patients and in 3.2% of channelopathy patients. The need for pacemakers was low (0.8%). The results suggest that S-ICDs are an effective and safe alternative to transvenous ICDs in patients with cardiomyopathies and channelopathies, with a low rate of inappropriate shocks21.

ECG Re-evaluation

As mentioned above, we performed a second-look evaluation of the ECGs of all patients, focusing particularly on cases described as idiopathic SCA. LQTS and SQTS were identified in two out of 28 (7.1%) cases initially classified as idiopathic.

After cardiac arrest, a QT interval prolongation is frequently observed, which is often multifactorial and caused, for example, by external triggers such as hypothermia therapy and the use of antiarrhythmic drugs and sedation22. Recently, it was demonstrated that QTc and JTc intervals are significantly and independently prolonged up to six days after SCA, irrespective of known QT-prolonging triggers22. Salinas et al. conducted a study showing that the QTc interval was prolonged by a mean of 58 ms (P < 0.005) because of therapeutic hypothermia treatment23. The authors stressed that such prolongation may favor ventricular tachyarrhythmias in patients with (undiagnosed or diagnosed) LQTS23.

Thus, a prolonged QTc value on admission is not necessarily indicative of LQTS and usually resolves before discharge24. On the other hand, an initially normal QTc value after an SCA does not rule out LQTS, which underlines the importance of thorough diagnostic tests and multiple ECG analyses in these patients24.

Long-QT syndrome and SCA

Congenital LQTS is the most common ion channel disorder and refers to a group of inherited cardiac disorders that impair ventricular repolarization9,25. The prevalence is around one in 2,000 individuals, with a slight predominance among women25. Clinical examination, repetitive ECGs, and genetic testing can establish diagnosis25. As described above, QTc prolongation is frequently observed in cardiac arrest survivors’ ECGs24. In an observational study, the QTc trends in 60 out-of-hospital cardiac arrest patients were examined over 20 months24. QTc prolongation was noted in 55% of patients on admission, which resolved in 79% by discharge, with no significant differences in the etiology of cardiac arrest24. In this cohort, there was one genetically confirmed diagnosis of LQTS. Still, the patient initially presented with an average QTc time, and QTc prolongation only became apparent during the course, emphasizing the importance of repetitive ECG examinations24.

Short-QT syndrome and SCA

SQTS is a rare condition whose true prevalence remains unknown8. A recent systematic review identified only 145 published cases of SQTS8,26. In cohort studies involving patients with resuscitated cardiac arrest or SCA, no instances of SQTS have been reported8. Therefore, our study identified a SQTS patient in a SCA cohort for the first time. One out of 48 SCA patients in our cohort had SQTS, suggesting that the prevalence could be higher than assumed and that there may be a higher number of unreported cases. In contrast, reports from other SCA cohorts indicate a prevalence of < 0.07% for patients with a QTc interval of < 330 ms27. Also, a systematic study in a pediatric clinic with 99,380 patients could only detect a QTc interval < 340 ms prevalence of 0.05%28.

The risk of ventricular arrhythmias and cardiac arrest for SQTS patients is up to 41%3,20,29–34. Mazzanti et al. examined 73 patients and observed that cardiac arrest is a common initial symptom, particularly in the first year of life34. At the age of 40 years, the probability of cardiac arrest was 41%34. Although males predominated in the cohort, the risk profile of female patients was similar to that of males34. Genetic screening had a low yield (14%), although 44% had a family history of the disease34. A history of cardiac arrest was the strongest predictor of recurrence, and cardiac arrhythmias occurred mainly at rest34.

Given the high risk for ventricular tachyarrhythmias in SQTS patients and the significant recurrence rate of cardiac arrest in survivors, ICD implantation is recommended for these patients8,34. Previous studies investigating ICD therapy for SQTS have reported variable appropriate shock rates ranging from 2% to 19%30,32,35. In contrast, inappropriate shock rates of up to 64% and other complications of up to 21% have been described30,35. In our patient, a subcutaneous ICD was implanted. No rates of inappropriate ICD shocks were documented over follow-up.

ICD therapy is an effective therapy for SQTS patients due to the high rates of ventricular tachyarrhythmias and SCA. However, it is also associated with a significant risk of device-related complications.

Limitations

The study was based on single-center observations, and the cohort size was relatively small. Nevertheless, in this relatively small SCA cohort, we identified one patient with SQTS. Furthermore, the lack of systematic genetic testing is a notable limitation in this cohort, especially in patients with idiopathic SCA. The definition of “idiopathic SCA” was based on the time of the index event and a subsequent, systematic review of all idiopathic cases with magnetic resonance imaging, electrophysiological studies and genetic testing was not possible. In our study, we used only ECG re-analysis. Adding genetic analyses may increase the rate of channelopathies or cardiomyopathies diagnoses in idiopathic cases, however genetic testing was not done systematically in all our cases. In addition, bias in the patient selection cannot be excluded.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of 48 patients with non-coronary SCA from a cohort of 232 patients with cardiac arrest. Cardiac arrests occurred most frequently at home and in public places and most often during non-strenuous daily activities. 54.2% of cases were classified as idiopathic after diagnostic evaluation. However, ECG-reevaluation identified two instances of SQTS and LQTS initially classified as idiopathic SCA. These results emphasize the importance of repeated ECG analyses in patients with cardiac arrest, for example because QT times may be altered after hypothermia treatment or due to cardiopulmonary resuscitation itself. In addition, it is essential to identify patients with SQTS, either after surviving SCA or before a ventricular tachyarrhythmia event, to enable risk stratification and appropriate therapy.
