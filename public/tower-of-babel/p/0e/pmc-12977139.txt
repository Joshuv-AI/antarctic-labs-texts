Oral Health in Hospitalized Children: A Cross Sectional Study
Authors: Claudia Capurro, Virginia Casali, Giulia Romanelli, Giulia Telini, Stefano Parodi, Nicola Laffi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsChildren with chronic or acute conditions requiring hospitalization, often exposed to pharmacological treatments, altered nutrition, and disrupted hygiene, may experience oral health deterioration as an underappreciated complication. This study aimed to assess the oral health, dietary habits, and dental history of pediatric inpatients in relation to systemic conditions and previous hospitalizations.Methods and Results559 children, hospitalized between November 2023 and April 2025, were enrolled. Caregivers completed structured interviews regarding medical history, diet, and dental care. Clinical examinations assessed the Decayed Missing Filled Index (dmft/DMFT), Modified Gingival Index (MGI), Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN), Plaque Index (PI), and salivary pH. 86.9% had systemic diseases, 82.3% had been previously hospitalized, and 65.5% were under pharmacological therapies. Nutritional and oral health education was poor. Oral deterioration, caries experience, and cariogenic diet were observed in 20%, 63%, and 93% of the participants, respectively. Children with prior hospitalizations showed poorer oral health (p = 0.019), higher PI (median 40% vs. 30%, p = 0.023), MGI (p = 0.007), and IOTN (≥ 4:28% vs. 13%, p = 0.006).ConclusionsHospitalization represents a critical period for pediatric oral health, providing opportunities for detection, education, and prevention. However, this potential remains underexploited, underscoring the need to integrate oral health promotion into hospital care.Keywords: adolescent, child, dental care, dentistry, hospitalization, oral health

Introduction

Oral health is closely associated with overall health and plays a critical role in determining the quality of life [1]. Management of oral health is an essential component of inpatient care, particularly in medically compromised or frequently hospitalized individuals, where it is often overlooked. Neglecting it may be associated with adverse outcomes, including worsening nutritional status and increased risk of hospital‐acquired infections [2]. Nevertheless, maintaining thorough oral hygiene during hospitalization can be challenging, especially in pediatric hospital settings, due to multiple intersecting factors, including children's limited cooperation, dependence on caregivers, medication‐related effects, inconsistent ward protocols, and lack of nurses’ training. Recent surveys and reviews have highlighted persistent knowledge gaps, unclear role ownership, and variability in practice across pediatric units, emphasizing the need for standardized, evidence‐based oral care pathways and staff education [3, 4, 5]. Although the relationship between systemic and oral health is well established, few studies have examined oral health indicators in hospitalized children outside specialized units. Preventive and management protocols for the pediatric population are currently implemented only in hematology‐oncology [6, 7], bone marrow transplantation [8], cardiology [9], and intensive care units [10, 11]. This is related to a greater awareness in critically ill children [9, 10, 12]. Within the framework of special care dentistry, hospitalized children should be regarded as a group that requires targeted preventive strategies and interdisciplinary management. Integrating oral health assessments into hospital routines could mitigate the long‐term burden of untreated dental diseases and promote overall well‐being.

This study aimed to characterize the oral health status of hospitalized children considering their medical conditions, dental history, and nutritional intake. Furthermore, it investigates whether previous hospitalizations are associated with differences in oral health outcomes, hygiene behaviors, and dietary patterns.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross sectional observational study was conducted at the IRCCS Giannina Gaslini Children's Hospital in Genoa, Italy, between November 6, 2023, and April 30, 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Regional Ethics Committee prior to the initiation of participant recruitment (N. CET ‐ Liguria: 294/2023 ‐ DB id 13233). This research was supported by dedicated funding from the Italian Ministry of Health, 5×1000‐2020 (Ministero della Salute, 5M‐2020‐23682532).

Study Participants

The study population consisted of children who were consecutively admitted to the IRCCS Giannina Gaslini Children's Hospital during the recruitment period, irrespective of the reason for hospitalization.

Children aged 0–17 years who permitted oral examination were included. Patients admitted for emergency care, day hospital, day surgery, or dental reasons were excluded.

Written informed consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian prior to enrollment.

Variables and Data Collection

Data regarding hospitalization and medical history were obtained from the electronic medical records of the children. Further details, including previous hospitalizations and surgeries in our hospital or other institutions, along with data on dietary habits, dental background, oral hygiene practices, and prior dental care, were collected via a structured verbal interview with caregivers and, when available, through a review of medical documentation. Dietary habits were evaluated by recording the number of daily meals and types of beverages and snacks consumed, with a particular focus on cariogenic items. The dental history section focused on previous dental visits, treatments received, and oral hygiene behaviors, including the frequency of tooth brushing and the tools used. To ensure the clarity of the questionnaire and consistency of the clinical assessments, the research team conducted preliminary training sessions. Before recruitment, the three examiners jointly reviewed the evaluation criteria and standardized the procedures to promote consistency among the examiners.

Subsequently, clinical oral bedside examinations were performed using dental mirrors, probes, and professional lighting. The clinical assessment employed validated indices: Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth Index (dmft/DMFT), Modified Gingival Index (MGI), Index of Orthodontic Treatment Need (IOTN), Plaque Index (PI), and salivary pH.

The dmft/DMFT Index quantifies caries experience by recording the number of decayed, missing, and filled teeth in primary (dmft) and permanent (DMFT) dentition. In mixed dentition, the scores are combined. Participants were classified as caries‐free (dmft/DMFT = 0) or having caries experience (dmft/DMFT ≥ 1) [13, 14].

The MGI is a noninvasive tool for assessing gingival inflammation. It uses a 0–4 scale, from healthy gingiva (0) to severe inflammation [4, 15].

The IOTN assesses the necessity of orthodontic intervention, with scores ranging from 1 (minimal malocclusion) to 5 (very severe malocclusion) [16]. In children under 6 years of age, it is used as a screening tool to detect potential malocclusions.

The PI, developed by O'Leary, quantitatively assesses plaque accumulation and is expressed as the percentage of plaque‐covered surfaces relative to the total examined [17].

Salivary pH was measured using calibrated colorimetric indicator strips. The assessment was performed exclusively in patients who had fasted for at least one hour prior to the clinical examination [18]. Salivary pH values were additionally categorized into clinically meaningful ranges as follows: acidic (<6.25), physiological (6.25–7.5), and alkaline (>7.5) [19].

At the end of the examination, the examiners assigned a global oral health score based on the abovementioned indices, integrating oral hygiene, gingival status, malocclusion, dental anomalies, and caries presence, and classified it into five categories: poor, sufficient, fair, good, or very good.

In addition to the previous data, sociodemographic information was collected, including caregivers’ nationality, family composition, and educational background.

Statistical Methods

Qualitative variables were described using absolute and relative frequencies. Quantitative variables were described as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR) in the presence of non‐normal distributions. Normality was assessed by visual inspection of the corresponding histograms.

To assess the influence of hospitalization on oral health preventive measures and education, the study population was stratified into two groups based on hospitalization history: Group A comprised children experiencing their first hospitalization, and Group B included those with previous hospitalizations. Comparative analysis between these groups focused on oral health parameters and oral hygiene education, including whether participants had already undergone their first dental examination. Statistical comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, when appropriate, for categorical nominal variables. The chi‐square test for trend was employed for ordinal variables. To assess the potential confounding effects of age at hospitalization, sex, and underlying systemic disease, unconditional multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed using the standard binary model for binary variables and the cumulative logit model for polychotomous variables. Finally, the randomness of missing values was assessed using the Little's test [21]. All analyses were performed using the STATA for Windows statistical package (release 18.0 MP, Stata Corporation, College Station, Texas, USA).

Clinical Oral Examination

The patients’ characteristics inherent to the clinical oral and dental examination are presented in Table 4. A poor general oral health assessment score was recorded in almost 20% of the patients. Only 36.6% had a low PI (< 25%). Only 18.4% of the patients showed no gingival inflammation (MGI = 0), whereas 11.4% presented high and 23.3% very high inflammation. Oral manifestations of underlying systemic diseases were observed in 95 patients (19.5%). The IOTN revealed that 42.9% (n = 180) showed no potential or effective malocclusion requiring orthodontic treatment. The dmft/DMFT Index increased with age, reaching its highest values between 6 and 11 years, when 47.1% of patients had experienced caries (median 4.0; IQR: 2.0–7.5). Salivary pH measurements were available for a subset of patients (n = 299). Of these, 66.6% were within the physiological range, 30.8% were classified as acidic, and only 2.6% as alkaline.

Age at study enrollment was equally distributed among the groups (mean 8.66 years in Group A, SD: 5.11 years vs. 8.68 years in Group B, SD: 4.93 years, p = 0.974).

Table 5 shows a comparison of the oral health status between the groups. Patients with previous hospitalization (Group B) showed, on average, poorer oral health status (p = 0.019), higher PI (p = 0.015), and a trend toward higher MGI values (p = 0.007). No significant differences were observed in the consumption of cariogenic foods/beverages or caries experience. Furthermore, the dmft/DMFT scores did not differ between the groups. Among the other evaluated variables, only the IOTN showed a statistically significant difference, with higher scores in Group B than in Group A (p = 0.006, Table 5). No significant differences were observed between the groups regarding previous dental visits or the age at the first dental visit.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive description of the oral health status of hospitalized children, highlighting a substantial gap in preventive care and parental education. Our results showed a generally high prevalence of oral health issues and limited knowledge of good dietary habits, oral health practices, prevention, and care.

More than 80% of our cohort had experienced at least one previous hospitalization, reflecting the tertiary referral nature of our institution. National Hospital Discharge Record (HDR) data show relative stability in hospitalizations, with a decline during the 2020–2021 pandemic period, followed by recovery in 2022–2023 [21, 22]. This trend also reflects the phenomenon of interregional health mobility, as families of children, especially those with complex medical conditions (CMC), frequently seek care outside their home regions [23]. As previously outlined in other reports, children with CMC are particularly vulnerable, as chronic conditions and frequent hospitalizations are associated with higher healthcare needs, reliance on technology, and special care coordination [24, 25, 26]. Despite these factors, our results suggest that a history of hospitalization does not appear to be associated with improved oral health, as children with previous hospitalizations showed worse scores compared to those at first admission, even if poor oral health is widespread across both medically complex and otherwise healthy children.

Several emerging patterns emphasize the urgency of incorporating oral health as an essential component of pediatric hospital care. In our analysis, the PI, MGI, and IOTN reached a statistically significant difference, being higher in patients with previous hospitalizations (Group B) than in patients at their first admission (Group A). First, the overall oral hygiene status was suboptimal; most children presented with elevated plaque scores and gingival inflammation, regardless of prior hospitalizations. These findings align with previous studies that have consistently reported inadequate oral hygiene among hospitalized children and limited integration of oral health into inpatient care plans [25, 26]. A recent study on nursing practices showed that oral health is often under‐assessed: most nurses do not perform oral assessments at admission, include oral care in treatment plans, or document it properly [4].

Regarding the IOTN, previous studies have similarly reported associations between higher scores and systemic or syndromic conditions [27, 28, 29]. Our study population included a substantial proportion of children with underlying systemic diseases and genetic syndromes. The increased IOTN values observed in Group B may partly reflect the impact of these conditions on craniofacial and occlusal development. However, the association between IOTN and previous hospitalization remained almost unchanged after adjusting for the effect of potential confounders, including preexisting systemic diseases. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Further multicenter studies with larger and more heterogeneous samples are needed to confirm whether this finding reflects a true association or a sampling artifact. Furthermore, detrimental oral habits, such as snoring, mouth breathing, and pacifier use, were highly prevalent, affecting more than half of the patients with previous hospitalizations. This may be explained by the use of such habits as a form of self‐consolation and the absence of interdisciplinary assessment and counseling during hospital stays. If not addressed early, these behaviors may contribute to functional and developmental complications, including malocclusion and speech difficulties [30, 31].

General preventive dental care was consistently delayed. Among children who had attended a dental visit, the first consultation occurred at a mean age of 5.5 years, well beyond the timeframe recommended by major pediatric dental authorities, including the American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD), the World Health Organization (WHO), and national guidelines, all of which endorse the first visit at 18–24 months of age [1, 12]. Consistent with the findings of previous studies, regular dental follow‐ups were uncommon, professional hygiene was rarely reported, and parental awareness of the protective role of fluoride was limited. These factors were compounded by the widespread consumption of cariogenic foods and beverages, as reported by most participants [4, 32]. These results reinforce the importance of caregiver education and early preventive engagement, which remain underemphasized in pediatric care.

Overall, our findings highlight missed opportunities for early intervention. Hospitalization represents a potential and unique window of contact with children and their families and can serve as a strategic opportunity for oral health screening, education, and preventive care. The lack of improvement in oral health among repeatedly hospitalized children likely reflects structural and organizational gaps, such as the absence of screening protocols, insufficient staff training, and limited collaboration between disciplines. Previous evidence in selected wards, such as pediatric oncology or cardiology, has shown that structured oral health protocols during hospitalization can reduce complications and improve clinical outcomes [9, 10]. Our findings extend these insights to a broader population, suggesting that systematic oral health integration into hospital routines may benefit all hospitalized children. Future efforts should prioritize actionable hospital‐based strategies, such as the development of standardized oral health checklists at admission, training modules for nursing and medical staff, and family counseling sessions to reinforce daily hygiene and dietary recommendations. These measures could transform hospital encounters into effective opportunities for prevention and health promotion.

The strengths of this study include its large sample size and multidimensional approach, which combines clinical examinations with structured caregiver interviews. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the single‐center design may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings. Second, information obtained from caregiver questionnaires on children's dietary and hygiene habits is subject to recall and social desirability biases, which could have influenced the accuracy of self‐reported data.

While statistical calibration was not formally quantified, examiner consistency and reliability were supported through the use of objective and easily interpretable indices, standardized assessment procedures, and preliminary pilot training. Valid data were not available for all patients for the considered variables. The proportion of missing data was less than 5% in most cases, except for the PI (8.9%), IOTN (24.2%) and pH (46.2%) with a nonrandom pattern of missing distribution. However, in our opinion, the association with previous hospitalization and poor health status, measured by PI and IOTN, seemed to be scarcely influenced by such a bias, because it was consistent with the pattern observed for the global oral health assessment and MGI that were both free of missing data. The cross sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships between the history of hospitalization and oral health outcomes.

Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal associations and explore whether interventions implemented during hospitalization can lead to measurable improvements in oral health over time.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study highlights a consistent association between suboptimal oral health and repeated hospital visits, without evidence of improvement attributable to hospitalization experiences. The persistence of poor oral conditions among both healthy and medically complex children suggests that oral health remains marginalized within pediatric inpatient care. Bridging this gap requires the integration of oral assessments into routine hospital workflows, enhancement of interdisciplinary training, and fostering of parent engagement through structured education programs. Incorporating these strategies into routine pediatric care could improve oral health outcomes and reduce long‐term disparities.
