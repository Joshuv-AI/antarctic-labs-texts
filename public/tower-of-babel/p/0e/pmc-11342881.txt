Genome-wide characterization and expression profiling of FARL (FHY3/FAR1) family genes in Zea mays
Authors: Sharah Jabeen Mou, Prodipto Bishnu Angon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The expression patterns and characteristics of the FARL gene family in Zea mays were systematically investigated.•Through comparison with other species like Arabidopsis, grape, rice, and sorghum, the FARL gene family in maize was examined to determine its functions and relationships.•Gene mapping, motif analysis, phylogenetic relationships, and synteny analysis were used to methodically explore the insights related to localization, orthologous and paralogous pairs, and ancestry of the FARL gene family.•Gene expression of the FARL gene family, specifically in ear, shoot, tassel and leaf tip, etc, were revealed.

Introduction

Light is an essential environmental factor for plants. Without it, plants can’t fulfill different biological processes like photomorphogenesis, chlorophyll biosynthesis, and chloroplast development.20, 29 Additionally, it works in tandem with the circadian clock to control several plant growth and developmental processes, such as the timing of flowering.24, 36 Light control of plant development in Arabidopsis largely depends on the FARL gene family. In Arabidopsis thaliana, phytochrome A regulates far-red responses through two homologous proteins: FAR1 (far-red-impaired response) and FHY3 (far-red elongated hypocotyls 3).36 These proteins are engaged in the perception and reaction to light signals. The nucleus is the target of all FARL proteins (Lin et al., 2004). According to a study, the FARL family is descended from transposases, which are introduced as transcription factors specifically for plants. FARLs were discovered to be significantly expressed in different tissues, specifically leaves, and the greatest number of these genes were discovered to be unquestionably articulated under saline conditions (NaCl) and adversely represented under cold conditions (4 °C).40 As FARL is a transposase-derived transcription factor, previously it was unknown that it is one of the ‘controlling elements’ that contribute greatly to the angiosperms on the planet. FARL has a complicated role in physiological and developmental processes such as ABA triggering,59 branching and plant architecture,57 flowering,1, 33, 36 chlorophyll biosynthesis,66 programmed cell inactivity,57 etc. Interestingly, FARL is only found in diversified angiosperms but not in other organisms.64, 65 In rice, the development of panicles and spikelets at high ambient temperatures is regulated by the THERMOSENSITIVE BARREN PANICLE (TAP) gene, which encodes a FARL (FAR1-RELATED SEQUENCE) protein derived from transposase.73 By combining ethylene and light signaling with developmental aging, FARL (FHY3 and FAR1) work together in an age-gating mechanism to stop premature leaf senescence.67

FARL transcription factor triggers ELF4 to control flowering time, and in this process, far-red elongated hypocotyls help as partners.33 In addition, FARL strongly controls the fabrication of myo- inositol, which prevents the buildup of ROS and oxidative stress from leading to cell damage.45 Through the direct or indirect regulation of genes associated with the homeostasis of ROS, FARL reduces the levels of ROS.22 ABA and abiotic stress can increase FARL transcript levels. These results show that far-red elongated hypocotyls and far-red impaired responses are beneficial ABA pathway controllers and a focal point for integrating ABA and light signaling.64, 65

FARL is one of the members of seven subgroups of the FRS family. The majority of FRS subgroup members have three domain regions, one is the putative transpose domain, and another two are C terminal and N terminal.34, 36 The N-terminal, which binds to DNA, unites to particular cis-elements in the promoter regions of different ranges.34, 43 In a yellow-striped 3 mutant allele, a putative maize FRS5-LIKE gene's port reversed restarts were discovered to be an effective transposon placed in the ZmTOM1 coding sequence (ys3). Therefore, it can be inferred that the central transposase activity of the FRS family proteins may have persisted in maize. Fortunately, FARL is one of them.7

According to a research on tea, CsFHY3/FAR1s (FAR1/FHY3 = FARL) may be required for maintaining responses to abiotic stresses, growth and development, and photomorphogenesis.40 The spread of FARL/FRS=FARL-type genes and their pattern of expression may be related to the regulation of the behavior of shoot expansion in BT (one of the genotypes of rose).75 The research on Arabidopsis discovered that FARL’s specific downstream indicators, MIPS1 and MIPS2, significantly influence the feedback to photo-induced inositol and peroxidation.45

Maize, along with rice and wheat, provide over 30 % of food calories to more than 4.5 billion people in 94 developing countries. It is a global cereal crop that is an important source of phytochemicals that are beneficial to our health.28 The consumption of phytochemicals is inversely related to the development of chronic diseases.51 The inbred line B73 was used to completely sequence the maize genome (2n = 20) in 2009.53 Centromeres in maize are approximately 2 MB to 200 MB in size and are distinguished by the presence of two repeating components: CRM is an energetic retrotransposon that appears to prefer centromeric chromatin as an insertion site. A 156 bp satellite called CentC can be noticed in extremely repetitive arrays.17 The maize sequenced data opens a new door for upcoming researchers to analyze previously unknown facts about maize as well as compare and contrast with other Poaceae family crops such as rice, sugarcane, wheat, barley, and so on. The FARL family of genes has been shown to perform critical roles in plant growth and development. However, no information is available on the FARL family genes in maize.

Maize contributes to food and nutrition security as well as global agri-food systems in a variety of dynamic ways.50, 19 This paper highlighted the functions and analysis of FARL family genes in maize. 15 ZmFARL family genes have been recognized in the maize plant genome through a genomic study and examined using bioinformatics, along with their basic traits, phylogenetic relationships, gene infrastructure, conserved motifs, exon–intron distribution and expression analysis of various plant tissues, which provides a theoretical foundation for future investigation of ZmFARL’s biological function. Since FARL family genes are involved in light signaling pathways, they regulate various aspects of plant growth and development, including seed germination, phototropism, flowering and circadian rhythms, hence, for maize genomic research, this gene family may make an excellent candidate. Consequently, to understand the function of the FARL transcription factor family genes in light signaling in maize, genome-wide characterization and analysis of these genes would be essential.

Methods and materials

Sequence retracement and identification of FARL gene family

To retrieve the FARL genes in Z. mays, the itak database (https://itak.feilab.net/) was performed using Arabidopsis, Sorghum, Grape, and Rice FARL proteins as a reservation. From the Grassius database (https://grassius.org/), FARL coding DNA sequences (CDS), protein sequences, and genomic sequences of Z. mays were collected (supplementary files). Their physiochemical properties, resemble length, molecular weight, isoelectric point, and grand average of hypopathicity of ZmFARL genes62 were determined by the Protparam tool (http://www.expasy.org/protparam/). ORF of ZmFARL genes was identified by the Ensemble Plants database (https://plants.ensembl.org/). For the identified proteins, their sub-cellular localization was presumed using cell ploc2 (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell-PLoc-2/).8 In fifteen ZmFARL genes, the location of the FARL domain was predicted with the help of Pfam (http://pfam.xfam.org/).14 The sequences of protein of Arabidopsis (17), Grape (19), Rice (17), and Sorghum (37) were collected from the iTAK database (https://itak.feilab.net/).74 These sequences were then deduced with the help of TAIR databases (https://www.arabidopsis.org/),6 a database of transcription factors based on plant PlnTFDB (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/)48, compliance information services for grass Grassius database,70 respectively.

Using phylogenetic tree to analyze the evolution of the FARL proteins and synteny analysis

A phylogenetic tree is constructed to represent the relationship between entities seized by the topology and the amount of evolutionary change. With the help of MEGA 7,30 the total length protein sequences of grapes, arabidopsis, rice, and sorghum were used in the analysis. The Neighbour Joining (NJ) method76 was chosen to show the value of bootstrapping with 1000 replicates. Syntenic analysis was performed through Circoletto (https://bat.infspire.org/circoletto/).

Exon-Intron representation and assessment of conserved motif

The GSDS web tool was used to study their exon–intron representation, and for that, the CDSs of ZmFARL genes were aligned as instructed (https://gsds.gao-lab.org/).21 MEME suite v.5.1 was recruited to study the FARL protein motifs.4 The predetermined analysis parameters included a limit of 10 motifs, a minimum width of 6, and a maximum width of 50.

Localization and duplication of chromosomes

To reveal the locations of chromosomes (i.e., number of chromosomes, start–end point, and size) of the FARL genes, the Ensemble Plant Database26 used their arrangement in the ten Z. mays chromosomes were arranged with the help of MapGene 2 chrome web V2 web tool (http://mg2c.iask.in/mg2c_v2.0/).25 An NCBL Blast2 search was done to determine the gene duplication, and the proportion of queries covered was then utilized to determine where the ZmFARL genes were duplicated.

Cis-acting components and cohesive prediction and GO annotation

The cis-acting regulatory (about 5–10 base pairs) of 15 ZmFARL genes were predicted by the use of the tool which is PlantCare-based (http://mg2c.iask.in/mg2c_v2.0/).31 The Blast 2GO functional observation and genome-based software (http://cucurbitgenomics.org/blast)11 was conducted to evaluate the molecular activity, physiological action, and cellular positioning of the 15 ZmFARL proteins. GO annotation was performed through ShinyGO v0.741.

RNA sequencing and data investigation for Z. Mays

We collected sequenced RNA data from the Expression Atlas database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gxa/home/),49 to profile the expression of ZmFARL genes, which were previously examined by Walley et al.63 Expression data consists of 10 kinds of tissues, such as the anther, ear, embryo, endosperm, leaf base, leaf middle, leaf tip, root, shoot, and tassel. The scores of the exon model Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) were converted to the FPKM+1 mode. A heat map was constructed using Microsoft Excel with these (FPKM+1) values.

Using a phylogenetic tree to analyze the evolution of the FARL proteins and synteny analysis

A phylogenetic analysis of FARL proteins from different species of plants was carried out to find out how the FARL gene family developed. The tree was constructed from the sequences of amino acids of maize (15), Arabidopsis (17), Grape (19), Rice (17), and Sorghum (37), (Fig. 1). Some nodes' bootstrap values were low due to species identity. However, the majority of nodes had more trustworthy bootstrap values. All the sequences of protein of the FARL gene family were sorted into 5 groups (A-E). Among all of them, group C constituted the larger members which are 32 representing 30 % of total FARL proteins, followed by group A (26) representing one-fourth of total FARL genes, group B (15), group D (16), and group E (16). Regions of FARLs from both monocot and dicot plants were found in all groups. The 15 ZmFARL proteins are divided into five groups. Exon-Intron representation and assessment of conserved motifs

The evolution of multigene families is familiar to be driven by structural differences among genes. We conducted exon–intron distribution of individual ZmFARL genes to obtain insights into the in-sights structural diversification of FARL genes in Z. mays (Fig. 3). We found exon–intron coding sequences in all ZmFARL genes, which varied from four to sixteen. One exception was found out in the ZmFARL4 gene, which contains 40 exons and 20 introns. Among them, four genes constituted four introns, one gene constituted six introns, and one contained the highest number of introns, which is twenty; another three genes contained only one intron and seven introns are also found in one gene. The nearly equal group shared the same type of exon–intron representation in length or number (Fig. 3). In group B, the intron number varied from four to six in the ZmFARL genes. Cis-acting components and cohesive prediction and GO annotation

Cis-acting elements are crucial for figuring out how genes are expressed in response to specific tissues, stresses, or hormones in specific external circumstances.71 To identify possible stress, growth, and development as well as hormone-responsive cis-elements of ZmFARL genes Plantcare database was used. There have been five abiotic stress-responsive cis-elements: Myb-binding site (MBS) available in 14 different ZmFARL genes (Fig. 6) were the cause of drought, Low Temperature Radiators (LTR) in 9 genes (Fig. 6), TC-rich repeats in 6 genes were sensitive to defense and stress (Fig. 6). ARE present in 13 genes and GC-motif present in 11 genes all were responsive to defense and stress (Fig. 6). We also detected 11 hormones responsive cis-acting methyl jasmonate (MeJA) and abscisic acid found in all genes and salicylic acid in 8 genes (Fig. 6), auxin in 9 genes. Furthermore, the rest of the cis-acting components that were linked to the specific expression of tissues (meristem, endosperm, and mesophyll) regulation of circadian, metabolism of zein, and responsiveness to light were detected in ZmFARL genes (Fig. 6). In the phylogenic tree, genes that are connected have remarkably similar cis-acting components. Moreover, by studying GO (gene ontology) annotation (Fig. 7, Fig. 8,9,10) (Table 3), all genes in ZmFARL have more or less similar molecular and biological functions. They are mainly located in the nuclear region, are regulated in the nuclear region, and regulate other transcription factors like HY5. Mays. The output showed that seven genes ZmFARL1, ZmFARL3, ZmFARL4, ZmFARL6, ZmFARL8, ZmFARL10, ZmFARL15 were expressed in varying degrees across all tissues (Fig. 11). Discussion

The sequence of the entire genome is only the beginning of the biological journey. Genome sequencing projects must be followed up with thorough genetic investigations to determine the role of each gene in the organism's overall biology.46 In comparative genomics, molecular evolution, and systematics, gene families are commonly used.32 There are many studies on FARL genes in tea,40 rose,75 Arabidopsis,44, 36 and also some nematodes. However, there are no considerable studies on FARL genes in Z. mays. Maize is a major cereal crop used in many parts of the world and an important livestock feed component. Because of its nutritional value, maize has global significance, it is not only used for food, but its byproduct has special importance in industries and for animals. Therefore, based on such advantages, it is endorsed that it may be included in daily diets and that more research is conducted on it to discover the latest advantages.23

In this investigation, Z. mays presented 15 ZmFARL genes that were observed and explored. Some crops have a higher number of FARL genes like rice (74), cabbage (27), pea (61), and Arabidopsis (17) but some have a lower number of FARL genes like potato (3), carrot (7), and ambarella (9). Though the genome size is larger in some crops such as potato (726 Mb),69 maize (2300 Mb),53 rice(441 Mb) (Eckardt, 2000), carrot (480 Mb),60 cabbage (530 Mb),41 pea (4.45 Gb),16 Arabidopsis (135 Mb) this shows there is no relation between FARL gene number and genome size in different crops. Two genes are acidic (ZmFARL3, ZmFARL5) in nature as the pI value < 7 (Table 1), and rest of the genes are basic. As is observed for transcription factor-related phenomena, the ZmFARL proteins were detected in the nuclear territory.38

A transformative analysis was conducted to detect the association between phylogenetic similarity and functional similarity among ZmFARL proteins (Fig. 1). Two important biological mechanisms in this investigation were C3 vs. C4 and monocot vs. dicot. The grass family contains about 10,000 species, nearly 45 % of which are C4 plants.52 The arrangement of C4 grasses in unique taxonomic groups, combined with the significant variance of their C4 syndrome, led to the conclusion that the C4 pathway has numerous origins in the FARL gene family.56 The Panicoideae subfamily's phylogenetic analysis also revealed that C4 photosynthesis seemed on many occasions to be on its own.3, 13, 61 Additionally, the speed of transition from C3 to C4 could alter over time, for example, as an activity of natural CO2 levels or after acquiring earlier adaptations to C4 photosynthesis.52, 56 The involvement of a Ser at location 780 of PEPC, which is counted based on sequences of maize, is necessary for the activity of C458 and was associated with numerous other repeated adaptive amino acid alterations 10 that left trustworthy genetic signatures for C4. The subfamily Panicoideae, which was divided into three significant groups, included Z. mays, S. bicolor, and Panicum vargitum.3 Maize (ZmFARL protein) and sorghum (SbFARL protein) were closer than other FARL proteins. Since they both belong to the Panicum/Urochloa/Setaria clade, this was anticipated.9, 61 In our study, the phylogenic tree contains both monocots and dicots. For example, groups A, C and D contain both proteins of monocot (rice, sorghum, and maize) and dicot (grape, Arabidopsis). On the other hand, groups B and E contain proteins of only monocots (maize, sorghum, and rice) (Fig. 1). This phenomenon suggests that they may have evolved from a common ancestor before monocots and dicots split. Moreover, orthologous and paralogous are both present in the tree. Identical genes that have diverged across the same organisms and were created through the duplication of genes are referred to as “paralogs”. These new genes have new functions, though they frequently share characteristics with their ancestral genes15. Inversely, after a speciation condition, orthologs have differed greatly and continue to operate in variety, to the aboriginal gene15.

One of the remaining unanswered questions regarding gene evolution is the origin and significance of exon–intron architecture. Around 40 years ago, the primary role of exons in presenting mRNA and coding for proteins was discovered.18 Because alternative splicing, which introns participate in, greatly increases the protein repertoire or variety, that's why introns are significant. The structural variation that exists in the gene family may be indicated by the divergence of exon–intron distribution. Modifications in coding regions, especially those that can change the activity of the gene, are caused by exon/intron changes in value, exonization or pseudo-exonization, exonization of amino acids, and the addition or removal of amino acids.68 Similarities in exon–intron arrangements indicate that they may be tremendously conserved during evolution. In this study, low introns in FARL genes indicate that it may happen through its evolution. No duplication was found in our study, but some showed the same number of exon-introns. Combining several peptides results in Motif, an analytical sequence pattern depicting specific protein segments' conservatism.5 The motifs are displayed as regional multiple alignments of the input sequences (subsets), in addition to several other formats, in the MEME output, which is HTML. The different locations of the motifs are displayed in “block diagrams”.5 Due to their short length and decadence, TFBS motifs following a whole genome statistically become “invisible”.5, 77 Variability may specify the functional divergence among FARL genes, and similar motifs are seen, mainly generated from the same group of FARL proteins. All living creatures are strictly controlled by a class of proteins called transcription factors (TFs), which lead to activation. To unlock or suppress the transcription of numerous target genes, they interact with specific DNA sequences in the promoter arrays of the genes.65, 64 Gene duplication has long been regarded as a crucial factor in evolutionary biology and a precursor to it.65, 64 We looked into FARL family gene duplication activities in maize to discover more about how the FARL genes descended. An essential method by which organisms acquire new genes and produce genetic novelty is gene duplication. Uneven crossing over, retroposition, and chromosomal (or genome) duplication are all possible causes of gene duplication.42 Segmental duplication is the term for the duplication of genes across different chromosomes, whereas tandem duplication is the duplication of two or more genes on a particular chromosome.39 There was no evidence of tandem duplication in this study, which proves that segmental duplication is the reason for the expansion of FARL genes in Z. mays rather than tandem duplication.

Exploiting the cis-regulatory elements is most probably the reason for target gene transcription, which is a reasonable cause for expected gene discovery. Such cis-elements allow the identification of putative particular genes whose promoters consist of the components.72, 78 These cis-regulatory elements are tissue-specific to the genes that respond to plant growth and, hormone and stress responses. In this study, the promoter territories of the majority of ZmFARL genes contained a large number of stress-responsive cis-elements, indicating that they play a role in biotic, abiotic, and environmental stress adaptation. Except for ZmFARL6, ZmFARL7, ZmFARL12, ZmFARL14, all FARL genes showed abscisic acid-responsive elements (Fig. 5). The promoter territories of every ZmFARL gene displayed methyl jasmonate responsive motifs, indicating a strong association between those genes and methyl jasmonate responses (Fig. 5). Several physiological processes, including the germination of seeds and the development of seedlings, are controlled by the phytohormone ABA and light. Early research demonstrated that phytochromes control the metabolism of ABA in seeds.54 However, uncertainty exists regarding the specifics of how ABA and light signaling interact. ABA and abiotic stress can both increase the expression of FARL transcripts. These findings show that the ABA pathway is strongly controlled by FARL, which also serves as a focal point for synthesizing ABA and light signaling.64, 65 Remarkable biotic and abiotic stress-responsive cis-elements and phytohormones help them to adapt or fight against unfavorable conditions. The domain structure of FARL is comparable and consists of a C-terminal, N-terminal, and a putative central transposase domain. Additionally, previous research indicates that the divergent promoter operations and subfunctionalization of the protein of FARL are responsible for their interrelated functions. FARL gene family has three conserved regions in which the middle and the C terminal are important for the function of transposase regulation and the N terminal plays a remarkable role in binding with the DNA.35, 34 A significant amount of sequence homology exists between FAR-RED ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL3 (FHY3) and FAR-RED IMPAIRED RESPONSE1 (FAR1), the expected transposases are expressed by the maize Mutator component, which, namely Jittery and MURA.22, 36, 79 These two transposons are under the superfamily of elements that resemble Mutator elements (MULEs).37 Database exploitation and phylogenetic results demonstrated that MULE transposases are mixed in with several phylogenetic clusters that contain FARL-type sequences, which are found in different angiosperms.34

The functional diversity of genes comes out of the connection with the differential expression of tissue present in plants. This variation in the expression of tissues may be responsible for the growth and development and other things, of FARL genes in Z. mays during evolution. The expression of leaf tip, leaf middle, shoot, and root are mostly expressed through ZmFARL1, ZmFARL2, ZmFARL3, ZmFARL4, ZmFARL5 genes rather than ZmFARL8, ZmFARL9, ZmFARL10, ZmFARL13 genes, and so on. Ultimately, in the current study, we may say that ZmFARL (1–5) is of prime importance for the expression of those tissues. In the expression of the ear, the ZmFARL1 gene is extremely low, indicating that this gene has a minor contribution rather than the rest of the genes. Overall, the expression of various tissues was at variable levels in different genes. FARL homologous protein is needed for the responses of phytochrome (A regulatory in Arabidopsis).80 In A. thaliana, FARL controls processes related to light activation, shade avoidance, and time of flower initiation.75 Therefore, diversified gene expression in roses is caused by the distribution and duplication of FARL genes in roses, they also play a role in the timing of flower initiation and shoot growth patterns.75 Two transcription factors associated with MYB are important parts of the central oscillator in Arabidopsis. While at first light, CCA1 and LHY intermittently inhibit the expression of ELF4, inversely activation of the expression of ELF4 occurs remaining the daylight by far-red impaired response, HY5 and far-red elongated hypocotyl.64, 65

Conclusion and future work

The FARL gene family in maize was clarified in detail and examined in this study. We evaluated phylogenetic analysis, exon–intron distribution, chromosomal localization, motif analysis and so on. We also tried to tabulate the molecular properties of 15 genes in the FARL gene family. Then, we also investigated if any duplication had taken place. Lastly, validation of ZmFARL gene intervention in maize growth and development using RNA-sequencing-based expression profiling was done. The findings of this study lay the foundation for further investigation of the biological roles played by the FARL protein in maize.

A few future works that we highlighted are listed below:•More details on the precise functions of each FARL gene in the development, growth, and stress responses of maize needs to be examined. One way to do this is to create transgenic lines with modified FARL genes expression levels and examine their phenotypic characteristics in various environmental settings.•The signaling pathways that interact with the regulatory networks that govern the expression of the FARL genes can be investigated. The regulation of FARL genes may entail the identification of transcription factors and cis-regulatory regions, as well as the investigation of epigenetic alterations that affect the expression of these genes.•It is possible to look at the natural genetic variation in FARL genes between several wild relatives and maize types. Comprehending the role of genetic variation in FARL genes in phenotypic variety and environmental adaptation might help guide breeding initiatives that enhance crop resilience and production.•Multi-omics data (genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics) can be integrated to comprehensively understand FARL genes function and regulation. Systems biology techniques can uncover intricate molecular networks and processes impacted by maize FARLgenes.•FARL genes may have biotechnological uses, such as tuning light signaling pathways to increase photosynthetic efficiency or altering plant morphology to increase biomass output.
