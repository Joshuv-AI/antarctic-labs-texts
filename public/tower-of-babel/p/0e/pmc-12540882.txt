Chromosome-level genome assembly of the dung beetle Catharsius molossus (Coleoptera: Scarabaeidae)
Authors: Xiaobo Xu, Xinyuan Fan, Yijie Tong, Rongrong Shen, Zhehao Tian, Shaolong Wu, Ming Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dung beetles (Scarabaeinae, Scarabaeidae, Scarabaeoidea, Coleoptera) contribute to ecosystem processes by fragmenting dung, aerating soil, and facilitating nutrient cycling. Among them, Catharsius molossus is a large-bodied tunneler species known for its high dung removal efficiency and ecological significance. It not only exerts an indirect contribution to livestock productivity through dung decomposition, but also holds medicinal value due to its traditional and empirical pharmacological applications. Despite its ecological and economic significance, no genomic data for C. molossus or any species within Catharsius were previously available. In this study, a chromosome-level genome assembly of C. molossus was generated using PacBio HiFi, Illumina, and Hi-C sequencing. The assembled genome is 1.54 Gb in size, with an N50 of 164.06 Mb, 35.36% GC content, and 10 chromosomes. BUSCO completeness reached 94.40%, with 10,009 predicted protein-coding genes, 98.23% functionally annotated. This genomic resource provides a foundation for studying stress responses, functional genomics, and species conservation, supporting further research in ecological and evolutionary studies.

Background & Summary

Dung beetles of Scarabaeinae (Scarabaeidae, Scarabaeoidea, Coleoptera) constitute a diverse insect group distributed across all terrestrial ecosystems except Antarctica and marine environments1. Adult body length ranges from 2 mm to 68 mm, with approximately 245 genera and 6,231 species recorded globally2. By fragmenting dung pats, aerating soil, and facilitating nutrient cycling, dung beetles play a crucial role in ecosystem processes3. In addition to these functions, contributions to vegetation growth, carbon cycling, and trophic network stability are well established4. These ecological functions also underpin economic benefits, such as improving pasture quality and indirectly supporting livestock productivity4. However, the global decline of dung beetle biodiversity, particularly pronounced in Scarabaeinae species including Catharsius molossus, has been irrefutably linked to anthropogenic stressors such as climate change, unsustainable grazing regimes, and excessive agrochemical use (herbicides and insecticides)5,6. Dung beetle population collapse negatively impacts grassland ecosystem health by impairing dung decomposition services. Given the high sensitivity of dung beetles to environmental changes, deciphering the molecular mechanisms underlying their stress responses is imperative. Species exhibiting exceptional dung removal capacity and environmental resilience are key targets for conservation and ecological restoration. Constructing high-quality reference genomes for ecologically critical dung beetles will advance multi-omics research, providing mechanistic insights for population recovery and habitat rehabilitation in degraded ecosystems7.

The species selected for sequencing in this study, C. molossus, is a large-bodied tunneler dung beetle. It was prioritized due to its exceptional dung removal efficiency, broad distribution across Asia, and ecological importance in grassland ecosystems. Its large body size, functional dominance, and conservation relevance make it a representative and tractable model for dung beetle genomics. As a tunneler species8–10, C. molossus exhibits superior dung burial capacity compared to other functional groups but is also more susceptible to extinction. A single adult can bury an average of 18.4 ± 0.687 g of dung within 24 hours at an altitude of 1,206 m11, with burrowing depths reaching 60 cm12. Beyond its ecological role, C. molossus holds significance in traditional Chinese medicine, as reflected in its Chinese name prefix, “Shennong” (神农), referencing the legendary figure regarded as the father of Chinese herbal medicine. Pharmacological studies have reported its efficacy in suppressing experimental prostatic hyperplasia13 and its potential anti-tumor properties14. Additionally, as a sexually dimorphic species, C. molossus has been used to investigate the origins and evolution of postcopulatory reproductive traits15.

Despite the ecological and medicinal significance of C. molossus, no genomic data for this species or any other species within the genus Catharsius is currently available in the NCBI database. This absence of genomic resources presents challenges for the prediction of resistance-related functional genes and epigenetic analyses. In this study, a chromosome-level genome assembly for C. molossus was successfully generated by integrating PacBio HiFi long reads, Illumina short reads, and Hi-C data, followed by comprehensive assembly, scaffolding, and annotation, providing a valuable resource for further genetic, ecological, and evolutionary research.

Methods

Sample preparation

Adult specimens of C. molossus were collected from Longhui County, Shaoyang City, Hunan Province, China, during August 2024. A sexually mature, medium-sized, and highly active adult male C. molossus was selected for dissection. The prothoracic muscle and testes were carefully extracted, ensuring no contamination from intestinal contents, parasitic mites, or other impurities. The isolated tissues were immediately placed into a sterile 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube (Axygen), snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen to prevent nucleic acid degradation, and stored at −80 °C until sequencing. During transportation to the sequencing facility, samples were shipped on dry ice to maintain ultra-low temperature conditions.

Genomic DNA sequencing

All sequencing was conducted by Grandomics Biosciences Company (Wuhan, China). Illumina libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Nano DNA HT Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina). Hi-C libraries were prepared using Kit A. PacBio HiFi libraries were prepared using the SMRTbell® Prep Kit 3.0 (PacBio). Illumina and Hi-C sequencing were performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform. PacBio HiFi sequencing was performed on the PacBio Revio platform.

Adapter sequences and low-quality reads were filtered out using Fastp (version 0.23.4)16. Illumina sequencing generated 104.91 Gb of clean data (68 × coverage). PacBio HiFi sequencing generated 119.59 Gb of clean data (78 × coverage). Genome assembly

Genome assembly of C. molossus was performed using HiFiasm (version 0.19.8‐r603)20 with default settings for Hi-C-assisted assembly. To improve assembly continuity, purge_dups (version 1.2.6)21 was used to remove duplicated regions. The resulting draft genome had a total size of 1.54 Gb, comprising 79 contigs with an N50 length of 60.13 Mb (Table S1).

Hi-C clean data were mapped to the draft genome using BWA (version 0.7.18)22, and Juicer (version 1.6, https://github.com/aidenlab/juicer) was used to identify valid paired-end sequences. Final genome scaffolding and refinement were performed using 3D-DNA23, ALLHIC (version 0.9.13)24, and Juicebox (https://github.com/aidenlab/juicebox), which enabled visualization and manual correction of the assembly.

The final chromosome-level genome measured 1.54 Gb, with a scaffold N50 of 164.06 Mb and a GC content of 35.36% (Table 2). The final assembly consists of 10 chromosomes, which are clearly distinguishable based on the chromatin interaction heatmap (Fig. 2a). Genome completeness was assessed using BUSCO (version 5.3.2)25 with the endopterygota_odb10 database, identifying 94.40% of universally conserved genes, including 93.00% single-copy genes and 1.40% duplicated genes (Table 2). Repeat elements prediction

Repetitive elements were identified using de novo prediction methods. RepeatModeler (version 2.0.5)26 was used to construct a de novo repeat library. Long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons were detected using LTR_retriever (version 2.9.4)27. Tandem repeats were identified using Tandem Repeats Finder (version 4.09)28. Finally, RepeatMasker (version 4.0.9)29, based on the RepBase database30, was used for genome-wide identification and annotation of repetitive elements. Gene prediction

Gene prediction in the assembled C. molossus genome was performed using a combination of homology-based, transcriptome-based, and ab initio approaches. Protein-coding gene sequences of Tribolium castaneum (GCA_031307605.1), Cetonia aurata (GCA_949128085.1), Popillia japonica (GCA_042919845.1), Onthophagus taurus (GCA_000648695.2), and Holotrichia oblita (GCA_023690525.1) were downloaded from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and used as homology references. For transcriptome-based prediction, RNA-seq data from Mrinalini et al.15 were utilized, which included sequencing reads from the testes and accessory glands of two male C. molossus specimens (SRR1392178131, SRR1392178232, SRR1392189933, SRR1392190034).

RNA-seq reads were first mapped to the assembled genome using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1)35. Gene annotation was conducted using BRAKER (version 3.0.8)36–39, which integrates RNA-seq data, protein homology, and ab initio gene predictions. GeneMark-ETP (version 1.02)39 was first used to incorporate RNA-seq and protein alignment data for gene structure identification. These data were then used to train AUGUSTUS (version 3.5.0)39 specifically for C. molossus, refining the recognition of coding regions. Finally, AUGUSTUS performed genome-wide gene prediction, incorporating both extrinsic evidence and ab initio modeling, leading to the identification of 10,009 high-confidence genes (Table 2). The average mRNA length was 7741.7 bp, and the average CDS length was 1,555.4 bp.

To further assess gene features, comparisons were made between C. molossus and five species with high-quality genome assemblies: Drosophila melanogaster, Nicrophorus vespilloides, Onthophagus taurus, Oryctes borbonicus and Tribolium castaneum. Gene annotation data for these species were retrieved from InsectBase2.0 (http://v2.insect-genome.com/) (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Distribution statistics of gene features among the six species. Gene function annotation

Protein-coding genes were annotated by aligning them against multiple databases. Functional annotation was performed using DIAMOND (version 2.0.8.146)38 to search the UniProt UniRef9040 and NCBI Non-Redundant Protein Sequence (NR) database. Pathway and ontology annotations were assigned using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)41, Gene Ontology (GO)42,43, and evolutionary genealogy of genes: non-supervised orthologous groups (eggNOG)44 via eggNOG-mapper (version 2.0.1)45.

Protein family classification was conducted using HMMER (version 3.4)46 to annotate genes based on the Pfam database47. Protein domain annotation was performed using InterProScan (version 5.70–102.0)48 with the InterPro database. A Circos0 (version 0.69-8)49 diagram was generated to visualize C. molossus genomic features, including LTR, LINE, gene distribution, GC content, and chromosome structure (Fig. 2b).

Non-coding RNA annotation

Non-coding RNA (ncRNA) annotation was performed based on structural and sequence characteristics. Transfer RNA (tRNA) genes were identified using tRNAscan-SE (version 2.0.12)50. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes were predicted with RNAmmer (version 1.2)51. Small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) and microRNA (miRNA) genes were detected using Infernal (version 1.1.5)52.

Data Records

The sequencing datasets generated in this study, including Illumina, PacBio, Hi-C, and transcriptome data, have been submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) under the accession numbers SRR3363267453, SRR3363267554, SRR3363267655, respectively. The assembled genome has been archived in the NCBI Assembly database under accession number JBOCED00000000056. The corresponding genome annotation file is available in the Figshare repository57. In addition, all raw sequencing data, as well as the assembled genome and its annotation, have been deposited in the Science Data Bank58.

Technical Validation

The quality and completeness of the chromosome-level genome assembly were assessed using three independent approaches. First, consensus quality values (QV) were estimated using Merqury (version 1.3)59 by comparing k-mers in the assembly to those in the Illumina sequencing reads. The results showed that the QV for the chromosome assembly was 72.13, indicating a high level of assembly accuracy.

Second, mapping rates were calculated to evaluate assembly accuracy. The mapping rates for PacBio and Illumina reads were 99.96% and 99.65%, respectively, as determined using Bedtools (version 3.31.1)60. The mapping rate for RNA-seq reads was 62.13%, calculated using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1)35, which is likely attributable to inter-population divergence and the restricted tissue origin of the reference transcriptome.

Third, BUSCO (version 5.3.2)27 was used to assess genome completeness and annotation quality based on the endopterygota_odb10 database. The final genome assembly contained 2,005 (94.40%) complete BUSCO genes, including 1,975 (93.00%) single-copy genes, 30 (1.40%) duplicated genes, and 114 (5.40%) missing genes (Table 2). Additionally, 88.70% of conserved orthologous genes were present in the predicted protein-coding gene set, comprising 70.70% single-copy genes and 18.00% duplicated genes (Table 6).
