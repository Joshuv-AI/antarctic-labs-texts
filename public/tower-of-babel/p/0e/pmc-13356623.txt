Incidental nondysraphic cervical intradural extramedullary lipoma at C1-C2: CT and MRI findings
Authors: Asmae Kasimi, Mohammed Leknani, Nadia Mouna, Hajar Errahal, Hamid Ziani, Siham Nasri, Imane Skiker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntradural lipomas are rare benign tumors, accounting for less than 1% of all spinal tumors. Their exact physiopathology is not fully understood. There are many theories that share some basic aspects. Cervical intradural lipomas not associated with dysraphism are even rarer. We report the case of an incidental finding of a nondysraphic cervical intradural lipoma in a 17-year-old male patient initially admitted for polytrauma management. Whole-body trauma CT revealed a well-circumscribed intradural extramedullary fat-density lesion within the left dorsolateral spinal canal at the C1-C2 level, demonstrating attenuation values consistent with fat and mild mass effect on the adjacent cervical cord without significant canal compromise. MRI confirmed a homogeneous lesion hyperintense on T1-weighted images, intermediate-to-hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with complete signal suppression on fat-saturated sequences and no enhancement or cord signal abnormality. As the patient was asymptomatic, a conservative management approach was implemented with annual MRI follow-up and clinical evaluation.Keywords: Spinal tumor, Intradural lipoma, Nondysraphic lipoma, Cervical lipoma, MRI

Introduction

Intradural lipomas are rare, benign tumors that account for less than 1% of all spinal tumors [1]. They are mainly located in the lower thoracic and lumbosacral regions [1]. Intradural lipomas in the cervical spine are even less common, representing only 12% of spinal lipomas [2]. Vertebral dysraphism may or may not be associated [3]. Neurological deficits associated with intradural lipomas generally exhibit a slow progression and are usually present at the time of diagnosis [4,5]. Asymptomatic intradural lipomas are exceptional with only few cases reported in the literature [[5], [6], [7]]. Diagnosis typically involves MRI, which provides high-resolution details of both the lipoma and the surrounding spinal structures. We report the case of an incidental finding of a cervical nondysraphic intradural lipoma in an asymptomatic 17-year-old patient. The present case illustrates several uncommon features with important radiological teaching value, including incidental detection during trauma imaging, high cervical localization at the C1-C2 level, absence of associated spinal dysraphism and lack of neurological symptoms despite mild cord displacement. In addition, this case highlights the role of MRI in confidently establishing the diagnosis of intradural lipoma and guiding conservative management in asymptomatic patients.

Case report

We report the case of a 17-year-old male patient admitted to the emergency department for the management of polytrauma following a road traffic accident. The patient was stable. Clinical examination revealed nasal bleeding with bruising around the nose and pelvic pain. An initial whole-body CT scan showed a nasal fracture and multiple pelvic fractures. There were no other post-traumatic findings. Whole-body multidetector CT was performed using thin-section axial acquisition with multiplanar reformations following intravenous contrast administration for trauma evaluation. CT demonstrated a well-defined intradural extramedullary hypodense lesion in the left dorsolateral aspect of the spinal canal at the C1-C2 level, measuring approximately 22 × 9 × 29 mm (W × AP × H), with attenuation values consistent with macroscopic fat. The lesion produced mild mass effect on the adjacent cervical cord without significant canal stenosis (Fig. 1).

Axial soft-tissue window CT image (A) and sagittal reformatted CT image (B) demonstrating a well-defined fat-density intradural extramedullary lesion (red circle) within the left dorsolateral spinal canal at the C1-C2 level, causing mild mass effect on the adjacent cervical cord without significant canal stenosis.

Further investigations were carried out. The patient's medical history was unremarkable. He denied any neck pain, weakness, bladder or sexual dysfunction. Neurological examination revealed normal muscle bulk, tone and strength in all 4 extremities, as well as intact perineal sensation and rectal sphincter tone. Deep tendon reflexes were present, symmetric and not pathologically brisk. No long-tract signs were identified. Local examinations of the cervical spine did not reveal any dysraphic lesions. MRI of the cervical spine demonstrated a well-circumscribed intradural extramedullary dorsolateral lesion located on the left at the C1-C2 level, measuring 22 × 9 × 29 mm (W × AP × H). The lesion was markedly hyperintense on T1-weighted images and intermediate-to-hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with signal characteristics identical to subcutaneous fat. Complete signal suppression was observed on fat-saturated sequences. No internal septations, mural nodules, hemorrhagic components, or diffusion restriction were identified. Postcontrast images showed no enhancement. The lesion caused mild anterior displacement of the cervical cord without associated cord edema or myelopathic T2 signal abnormality. A preserved surrounding CSF cleft supported the intradural extramedullary location. No associated vertebral anomaly or spinal dysraphism was identified (Fig. 2).

Sagittal T2-weighted MRI (A), sagittal T1-weighted MRI (B), and sagittal fat-suppressed T1-weighted MRI (C) demonstrating a well-circumscribed intradural extramedullary dorsolateral lesion at the C1-C2 level (red circle). The lesion demonstrates signal characteristics identical to fat, including marked T1 hyperintensity and complete signal suppression on fat-saturated imaging. Axial T1-weighted image (D) demonstrates mild displacement of the cervical cord without intramedullary signal abnormality.

Given the absence of neurological symptoms, lack of cord signal abnormality and the potential surgical morbidity associated with high cervical intradural lesions, conservative management was favored after multidisciplinary discussion. Follow-up consists of neurological examination and cervical spine MRI at 6-12 months after diagnosis, followed by annual surveillance if stability is confirmed. MRI surveillance includes sagittal and axial T1-weighted, T2-weighted and fat-suppressed sequences, with postcontrast imaging if interval change is suspected. Neurosurgical reevaluation would be indicated in case of lesion growth, progressive cord compression, development of cord signal abnormality or onset of neurological symptoms.

Discussion

Spinal lipomas are categorized as intramedullary, intradural extramedullary, extradural, radicular, filum terminale and cauda equina lipomas [8]. Intradural lipomas are considered a rare entity, accounting for less than 1% of all spinal tumors [3,6]. The lumbosacral region is the most commonly affected area, often associated with spinal dysraphic conditions such as spina bifida and myelomeningocele [1,6]. In fact, intradural lipomas are generally considered to be congenital. They are thought to result from migration of mesenchymal tissue during embryonic development, leading to fat deposition within the spinal canal [2,9]. This is known as the “developmental error theory” and is the most widely accepted [2,10]. Cervical intradural lipomas are particularly rare and the absence of associated dysraphism, as observed in our patient, is even rarer [[2], [3], [4],6,11]. The incidence of intradural lipomas without associated dysraphism is reported to be around 0.45%-0.6% of all spinal tumors [12,13]. These lesions are common in children and young adults, with no significant gender predilection [4,14].

The location of the tumor determines the onset of symptoms. For example, bowel and bladder dysfunction often presents early in lumbosacral lipomas, but typically as a late complication in cervicothoracic lipomas [15]. In addition, due to the very slow growth of these lesions, symptoms tend to progress gradually, leading to variability in clinical presentation [16,17]. While some patients present with multiple symptoms, others may remain asymptomatic for long periods of time.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the imaging modality of choice. The short T1 relaxation time of fat tissue results in a distinctly high signal intensity for lipomas in short repetition time sequences. Typically, these hyperintense signals are suppressed in fat-inverted sequences, further supporting the diagnosis [18]. Benign lipomas have relaxation parameters similar to subcutaneous fat [2]. Liposarcomas and other adipose tissue tumors have longer T1 relaxation times, resulting in lower intensity on T1-weighted images compared to subcutaneous fat [19,20]. MRI allows accurate assessment of the extent of the lipoma and its anatomical relationship to the spinal cord and nerve roots [16].

The imaging differential diagnosis for a fat-containing intradural extramedullary lesion at the craniovertebral junction includes dermoid cyst, epidermoid cyst with proteinaceous content, hemorrhagic lesions and lipomatous neoplasms such as liposarcoma [18,19]. In the present case, the homogeneous fat attenuation on CT, signal identical to subcutaneous fat on all MRI sequences, complete suppression on fat-saturated images, absence of enhancement, absence of soft-tissue nodularity and lack of diffusion restriction strongly favored benign lipoma. Dermoid cysts are typically heterogeneous and may contain calcifications or mixed signal intensities. Epidermoid cysts usually demonstrate diffusion restriction and lack complete fat suppression. Liposarcoma was considered unlikely due to the absence of thick septations, enhancing soft-tissue components, or aggressive features.

The management of intradural lipomas remains controversial [8]. Surgery is reserved for patients with progressive neurological symptoms [7]. The aim of surgery is to achieve decompression with preservation of neural structures. Therefore, complete surgical resection and aggressive debulking are not recommended because of the lack of a clear dissection plane between the tumor and the spinal cord, which increases the risk of postoperative complications [4,5,16]. The preferred approach is usually surgical decompression and/or subtotal excision followed by regular surveillance [6,7]. Partial removal of the lipoma still carries the risk of leptomeningeal inflammation, postoperative scarring between the pia mater, arachnoid and dura mater, and recurrence in 46%-52% of cases [7,12]. Therefore, many authors argue that decompression alone is sufficient to achieve substantial neurological improvement [2,7,21]. For asymptomatic patients, conservative management with regular follow-up is recommended, as was done in our patient [6,7,22].

Conclusion

Intradural lipomas are slow growing tumors [23]. Progressive neurological presentation is the most common clinical picture. MRI is the diagnostic modality of choice, providing essential information about the extent of the lesion and its anatomical relationship to the spinal cord. The diagnosis of an intradural lipoma is relatively straightforward on imaging. However, the real challenge is to reach a consensus on the optimal management approach. Surgery is indicated in patients with progressive neurological symptoms, whereas conservative management is recommended for asymptomatic patients [7].
