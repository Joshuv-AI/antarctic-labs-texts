Association of serum essential and non-essential elements with dyslipidemia in patients with cardiovascular disease in Western Iran: an application of Bayesian kernel machine regression
Authors: Samaneh Nakhaee, Zohreh Manoochehri, Maryam Mahjoubian, Mehdi Khodamoradi, Borhan Mansouri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDyslipidemia poses a significant challenge to public health worldwide. Many researchers have suggested that nutritional and environmental factors contribute to dyslipidemia. This study aimed to examine the relationship between serum levels of selected essential and non-essential elements, calcium (Ca), cobalt (Co), magnesium (Mg), potassium (K), lithium (Li), boron (B), and aluminum (Al) and dyslipidemia. Additionally, we aimed to explore the interactions between these elements in various mixtures using the Bayesian Kernel Machine Regression (BKMR) model.MethodsThis cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among the Kurdish population in western Iran, using data from the Ravansar non-communicable diseases (RaNCD) study. A total of 224 participants aged between 35 and 65 years were included. Data collection included demographic information and blood samples, which were analyzed for selected elements using Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS). We applied logistic regression and BKMR models to analyze the effects of the studied serum elements on dyslipidemia.ResultsAmong 224 participants, dyslipidemia prevalence was 54.9%, higher in cardiovascular disease (CVD) patients (61.8%) than in non-CVDs (49.2%). Individuals with dyslipidemia had significantly higher age, body mass index (BMI), and blood pressure, while no significant differences were found in sex, residence, marital status, or education level. The logistic regression analysis, after adjusting for confounding factors in the CVD subgroup, indicated that higher serum Ca concentrations were associated with higher odds of dyslipidemia compared with lower concentrations. In contrast, higher serum Co and Mg concentrations were associated with lower odds of dyslipidemia. The BKMR model revealed that the serum levels of B in all subjects, and K, Co, and Ca in the CVD group, as well as B and Co in the non-CVD group, exhibited the highest posterior inclusion probability (PIP) values.ConclusionThis study suggests a potential association between serum levels of Ca, Co, Mg, and dyslipidemia in a representative sample of patients with CVD.

Introduction

Dyslipidemia which is defined as abnormalities in the metabolism of lipoproteins is common and epidemic in modern society [1, 2]. It has been emerged globally as a threat to public health, and most of the populations suffering from this disorder are ignorant about their lipid profile, leading to poor treatment and control rates of lipid abnormalities [3]. According to the Global Burden of Disease, study conducted in 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2019, elevated levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were reported as one of the leading risk factors for disability-adjusted life years globally among older populations [4]. Dyslipidemia is among the most common factors contributing to CVD and mortality [5, 6]. In addition, it is implicated in the etiology of other diseases such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), metabolic syndrome, obesity, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease [2, 3, 7].

Various researchers have found nutritional and environmental factors as major contributors of dyslipidemia and CVD [2, 5, 8–10]. Lipid metabolism disorders may lead to life-threatening diseases that have already become an epidemic and are associated with imbalanced levels of elements [6]. The risk factors with dyslipidemia and a full understanding of disturbances in the ranks of micronutrients in patients suffering from dyslipidemia, therefore, need to be identified, and appropriate early prevention and treatment measures given first priority [2]. Various studies have suggested the association of dyslipidemia with environmental metal exposure, including both nonessential and essential elements [5, 11–13].

Although the content of trace elements in a human organism is small, they perform a powerful biological function. They are included in enzymes, hormones, vitamins, nucleic acids, metabolic processes, and their physiological functions support the delivery of macro elements [2, 14, 15]. It should be noted that while essential trace elements are necessary in various cellular processes, they may be potentially toxic in excess amounts [16]. According to recent studies Ca, zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), selenium (Se), copper (Cu), chromium (Cr), and Mg are significantly related to lipid metabolism [17, 18]. The reduction of Zn, Cu, and Ca interferes with normal lipid metabolism, leading to an increase in serum total cholesterol (TC) and LDL. However, findings regarding disturbances in lipid metabolism and Fe are still inconclusive and contradictory. Moreover, research on disorders of lipid metabolism and Se levels remains indefinite [2]. The relationship between elements and dyslipidemia has high heterogeneity among various minerals with different patterns of protective and adverse effects on lipid disorders. There is an indication by scientific studies that essential minerals, including Ca and Mg, which are classified as macro-elements, show some protective effects against dyslipidemia. Specifically, Ca decreases intestinal fat absorption by forming insoluble fatty acid complexes with fatty acids as well as chelating bile acids to support cholesterol excretion. Also, Mg serves as a cofactor for enzymatic reactions involved with lipid metabolism and cardiovascular function [2, 19]. Most studies are carried out based on exposure to one kind of metal, whereas the effect in reality would come from a mixed exposure, and a single type of metal cannot fully explain the occurrence and development of the disease. Few studies, on the other hand, have estimated the relationship between essential and non-essential element, element exposure and dyslipidemia among specific groups of CVD patients. Therefore, this study investigated the relationship between the serum levels of selected element mixtures (Ca, Co, Mg, K, Li, B, and Al) and dyslipidemia. In addition, this study investigated the effect of the interaction between the studied elements on dyslipidemia to examine the interaction between elements using a BKMR model.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The research was carried out as a cross-sectional analytical study, utilizing fundamental data from the RaNCD cohort, which is part of the larger PERSIAN cohort study in Iran. The study was conducted in Ravansar County, located in Kermanshah Province, Western Iran, as part of the RaNCD cohort. The study population was selected from Kurdish individuals residing in Ravansar County, as defined by the RaNCD cohort design, and participants of other ethnicities were not included. For our study, a total of 224 participants aged 35 to 65 years were enrolled. individuals with CVD and non-CVD individuals were selected from within this study to ensure demographic and environmental comparability. Although participants were categorized according to CVD status for stratified analyses, the overall study design was cross-sectional and no sampling was based on case or control selection. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to maintain sample integrity, and all participants provided informed consent. Further information about the RaNCD cohort has been documented in earlier publications [20, 21]. The study included individuals with CVDs and those who had lived in the region for a minimum of two years. People with mental disorders, chronic kidney and liver disorders, occupational exposure to heavy metals, non-native individuals, and those who had been living in the region for less than two years were excluded from the study. CVDs were diagnosed based on clinical tests and expert examination within the RaNCD cohort framework. This study employs standardized diagnostic protocols, including medical history evaluation, physical examination, and laboratory assessments, to confirm CVD status. Only individuals with verified diagnoses were included in the case group. The non-CVD group was selected from the same demographic group as the cohort, with a similar lifestyle and no history of CVD. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants to enter the study. Moreover, the research protocol was approved by the Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences (ethic number: IR.KUMS.REC.1403.505). We confirm that all experiments were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

After entering the study, the demographic characteristics of the participants, including age, gender, residential type, level of education, and BMI, were investigated using questionnaires and direct interviews. Blood pressure was measured using a digital sphygmomanometer after five minutes of rest in a seated position. Height and weight were recorded with a stadiometer and calibrated scale, and BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Subsequently, each participant provided ten ml of blood samples after fasting for at least 8 h, and serum samples were obtained using centrifugation. The measured parameters included triglyceride (TG), TC, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and LDL-C. Dyslipidemia was defined as the presence of at least one of the following: total cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, LDL-cholesterol ≥ 130 mg/dL, or HDL-cholesterol < 40 mg/dL in men or < 50 mg/dL in women.

Element analysis

To evaluate the concentration of selected essential and non-essential elements in blood serum samples, the mixture method of nitric acid and perchloric acid with a ratio of 2:1 was used. Thus, one milliliter of serum was initially mixed with four times the volume of nitric acid (HNO3, 65%, Suprapore, Merck, Germany). This mixture remained undisturbed under the chemical hood for 24 h to gradually facilitate the digestion process of the samples. The next day, two ml of perchloric acid (HClO4, 70%, Merck, Germany) was added to the mixture and heated under controlled heat in a Bain-Marie (TW12, Julabo GmbH, Germany) at 98 °C for six hours until the samples reached a white solution. After cooling in the laboratory environment, the samples were filtered through a cellulosic filter, and then the volume was brought to 25 ml with the help of deionized water (18.2 MΩ-cm at 25 °C, Fistreem, WSC044, UK). The levels of Ca, Co, Mg, K, Li, B, and Al were assessed using ICP-MS (Perkin Elmer Elan 6000). The LOD/LOQ values (in µg L− 1) were as follows: Ca (0.25/0.80), Co (0.02/0.06), Al (0.1/0.3), B (0.10/0.15), K (0.2/0.60), Li (0.10/0.30), and Mg (0.15/0.50). The recovery rate for all investigated essential and non-essential elements ranged between 92 and 105%. Five analyses were performed for both spiked fractions and control samples, and were adjusted to fit the midpoint of the working range of the methods. To prevent secondary contamination, all labware was acid-washed (0.5%) and rinsed with ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ·cm). External calibration with certified standards (R² ≥ 0.99), indium and germanium as internal standards, and batch-wise analysis with mid-batch recalibration and linear interpolation drift correction. The corresponding results are presented as µg/L of serum.

Statistical analysis

To assess the normality of the quantitative variables studied, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was utilized. If the data showed normal distribution, parametric tests such as the Student’s t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA) were applied. Conversely, if the data did not meet the normality assumption, non-parametric tests such as the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test were used to compare quantitative variables across groups. Qualitative variables were described using frequencies and percentage frequencies, and the chi-square test was employed to compare these percentages across different groups. Additionally, the correlation between trace elements was examined using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. The response variable was dichotomous, categorizing participants as either dyslipidemia or non-dyslipidemia. Factors influencing dyslipidemia were analyzed separately in patients with CVD, in a non-CVD group, and across the total population (total = CVD + non-CVD) using the logistic regression model.

In addition, we examined the exposure-response relationship between selected essential and non-essential element concentrations and dyslipidemia by fitting a BKMR model. In this framework, age, BMI, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), gender, residence, married status, and level of education were incorporated as covariates. By including these variables directly in the BKMR model, we were able to adjust for their potential confounding influence while evaluating the mixture effects of selected essential and non-essential elements on the outcomes. One of the features of the BKMR model, which is in the field of machine learning models, is that it is a flexible model that, in addition to linear relationships, detects and displays nonlinear and non-additive relationships between a large number of predictor variables (serum element concentrations) and outcome (dyslipidemia / non-dyslipidemia) [22].

To fit the BKMR model, a Gaussian kernel and probit link function were used. The model was run with 50,000 MCMC iterations using default prior settings. No thinning was applied. The BKMR model yields PIPs for each element, which indicate the likelihood of each element contributing to the prediction of the outcome. In addition to the PIP values, the model provides univariate exposure-response function curves for each element, bivariable exposure–response functions, the cumulative effect of overall exposure, and the interactive effects of chemical exposure. In addition to the heavy metals, variables with statistically significant associations in the univariate analyses were also included in the models. It is important to note that the BKMR model was fit using the “bkmr” package in the R environment, version 4.0.3 [23].

Results

Characteristics of participants

Descriptive statistics related to the characteristics of 224 samples by non-dyslipidemia (n = 101) and dyslipidemia (n = 123) groups are presented in Table 1. According to the results of this table, the mean age of total participants was 48.83 ± 8.75 years. The mean age of people with dyslipidemia (50.78 ± 8.94 year) was higher than the mean age of people in the non-dyslipidemia group (46.46 ± 7.92 year) (P-value < 0.001). In addition, patients with dyslipidemia had a higher BMI (27.79 ± 4.15 (kg/m2)) than non-diseased people (26.45 ± 4.58 (kg/m2)) (P-value = 0.023). Also, the two groups had significant differences in terms of mean SBP and DBP (P-value < 0.05), so that people in the dyslipidemia group had higher mean SBP and DBP (114.35 ± 21.14 (mmHg), 73.18 ± 12.18 (mmHg)) respectively than the people in non-dyslipidemia group (106.18 ± 18.53 (mmHg), 69.05 ± 10.25 (mmHg)). Among the 224 participants, 111 (49.6%) persons were female and 113 (50.4%) persons were male, and there was no statistically significant difference between men and women in terms of having dyslipidemia (P-value = 0.893). There was no significance between the two groups regarding the variables of residence, marital status and education level (P-value > 0.05). Based on the results, the prevalence rate of dyslipidemia in total population was (54.9%), in CVD patients (61.8%) and in the non-CVD group (49.2%).

Serum element levels

The results indicated the concentrations of serum elements in all participants (n = 224) as follows: Al = 3.7 µg L− 1, B = 0.90 µg L− 1, Ca = 95.12 µg mL− 1, Co = 0.086 µg L− 1, K = 145.00 µg mL− 1, Li = 0.416 µg L− 1, and Mg = 12.70 µg mL− 1. The distributions of selected essential and non-essential element concentration by dyslipidemia and non-dyslipidemia groups according to median and interquartile range (IQR) are presented in Table 2. According to the results of this table, the concentration of element B in the dyslipidemia group was higher than in the non-dyslipidemia group, and this difference was statistically significant (P-value = 0.017). However, in none of the subgroups (CVD, non-CVD), there was no statistically significant difference in the concentration of serum elements between the dyslipidemia and non-dyslipidemia groups (P-value > 0.05).

Logistic regression

The concentration of the selected essential and non-essential elements was divided into quartiles, and these quartiles were entered into the logistic regression model as an explanatory variable. The results of logistic regression after adjusting for the significant variables identified in the univariate analysis (age, BMI, SBP and DBP) are shown in Table 3. As shown in Table 3, Al and B concentration levels were related to dyslipidemia in the total studied population. Odd ratio (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the highest concentration level of Al (Q4) and B in Q3 group are: (OR = 3.37; 95% CI: 0.820, 14.9) and (OR = 3.02; 95% CI: 1.22, 7.71) respectively. In other words, people with the highest level of Al element concentration are 3.37 times more likely to suffer from dyslipidemia than people with the lowest level of this element. Participants in the third quartile (Q3) of serum B concentration had approximately threefold higher odds of dyslipidemia compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q1).

Discussion

According to the results of the present study in the CVD group, higher serum Ca levels were associated with higher odds of dyslipidemia compared with lower Ca levels. Ca constitutes the most prevalent cation in the human organism and is involved in numerous processes of metabolism. It is responsible for maintaining nervous and muscular function, and for supporting the activity of certain enzymes in the organism, such as lipase and adenosine triphosphatase [24]. The relationship between Ca intake and lipid metabolism was documented in both animal and epidemiological studies. A study conducted on subjects who underwent health checkups at the health examination center of the First Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University in 2019 showed that corrected serum ionized Ca was positively associated with an increased risk of dyslipidemia and increased TC, LDL-C, and Non-HDL-C, but inversely associated with a decrease in HDL-C [25]. In a cross-sectional, population-based study that recruited 640 subjects from eight provinces of South Korea, it was indicated that serum Ca showed a positive relationship with serum TC and TG; the chance of having hypercholesterolemia also increased significantly according to serum Ca ion quartiles after adjustment for age, gender, BMI, and body weight [26]. Another study showed that high intake of Ca increases serum levels of TG, TC, LDL-C, and HDLC, and eventually results in Ca ion deposition in the carotid artery [27].

These findings were reported by similar studies in Italy [28] and Iran [29] on the association of serum Ca levels with dyslipidemia. A study in a Chinese pediatric population reported a nonlinear L-shaped dose-response relationship between corrected Ca and the incidence of dyslipidemia [30]. A systematic review and meta-analysis study suggested that Ca, Zn, and Cu levels in the human body may be related to lipid metabolism. Although studies have shown that Ca signaling disorders affect lipid metabolism, the nature of the relationship between lipid metabolism and Ca levels remains unclear [2]. In contrast to our results, a case-control study found no correlation between Ca concentration and lipid profile [31]. In addition, Liu et al. (2020) studied the relationship between serum concentrations of six mineral elements and the risk of dyslipidemia in Chinese adults attending a health examination center and reported that serum Ca ion levels were not significantly associated with dyslipidemia in a confounder-adjusted model [32]. The different populations studied may be the cause of this difference. The mechanisms by which Ca ions may exert its effects on dyslipidemia were hypothesized but remain unclear. Firstly, elevated serum Ca ion levels led to reduced levels of serum pyrophosphates and enhanced tissue calcification [30, 33]. Secondly, the dynamics of mitochondrial and free Ca established a homeostatic circuit that compartmentalized and synchronized energy metabolism, reactive oxygen species generation, signaling pathways, cellular proliferation, and apoptosis in response to both internal and external stimuli [30, 34]. The presence of Ca may reduce cholesterol catabolism and promote lipid synthesis. Specifically, elevated Ca concentrations can reduce the activity of 7a-hydroxylase (CYP7A), an enzyme critical to cholesterol catabolism, while also enhancing the expression of sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP)-1c, a transcription factor in de novo lipid synthesis [30]. An elevated dietary Ca level restricts Ca in fat cells by inhibiting Ca-regulated hormone levels. This action lowers fatty acid synthase activity, modulates energy metabolism, and ultimately reduces lipid synthesis and body fat deposition [35, 36]. The relationship found between serum element levels and dyslipidemia may also represent some of the underlying clinical characteristics that have not been adequately captured in the present study. For example, renal function affects the metabolism and circulating levels of many elements. Additionally, the ways that lipid-lowering medication or other co-morbidities influence lipid levels and may also impact homeostasis of the different elements. Even though we adjusted for several demographic and cardio metabolic factors, but residual confounding may still persist. In addition, we found that higher serum Co levels tended to be associated with lower odds of dyslipidemia in the CVD patients. Co is an essential trace element, a component of vitamin B12, that can promote the production of erythropoietin, improve the quality of red blood cells, moderate the response ability of the cardiovascular system, maintain the vitality of tissues, induce vascular endothelial growth factor, and promote blood circulation [37, 38]. It was previously reported that the lack of this trace element can lead to lipid metabolic disorder and also promote atherosclerosis and cerebrovascular diseases [38, 39]. In the research of 6866 US adults in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey during the period 2015–2018, after full adjustment for demographic characteristics, higher blood Co concentrations were associated with reduced ORs of dyslipidemia, including elevated TC, elevated LDL-C, and low HDL-C [40]. A separate study involving 1,013 elderly individuals from China found that after adjusting for multiple variables, the OR (95% confidence interval) for dyslipidemia in the highest quartile of Co exposure was 0.76 (0.53–1.11) compared to the lowest quartile, indicating a negative association between Co exposure and the risk of dyslipidemia [38]. However, some animal experiments revealed that Co exposure may lead to elevated levels of lipids and lipoproteins in the blood and liver, as well as alterations in lipoprotein composition [38, 41]. In an experimental study, serum TG and LDL-C levels were significantly higher in mice exposed to Co, with no effect on TC levels [42]. Animal studies demonstrate conflicting results when compared to our study. Further research is recommended to clarify the association between Co and lipid profiles.

We also found that in the CVD group, individuals with higher serum Mg levels had a lower risk of dyslipidemia than those in the lower quartiles. Mg is recognized as an essential mineral vital for the body’s functioning, playing a role in about 80% of known metabolic processes [43]. Previous research exploring the relationship between this serum element and lipid profile levels, as well as the incidence of dyslipidemia, has produced inconsistent findings. Some studies indicated a statistically significant inverse relationship between plasma Mg and total or LDL-cholesterol, while others reported no significant association [36, 44]. Furthermore, some studies have reported statistically significant positive correlations between plasma Mg and total and/or LDL-cholesterol levels. In a retrospective analysis of 12,284 individuals from the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2001 to 2013, Jin and Nikodemus-Johnson (2018) noted that in women, Mg intake was positively related to HDL-C levels, while negatively correlating with the TC/HDL-C ratio. However, TG levels showed a negative correlation with Mg intake in both genders [45].

Barragán et al. (2020) studied the interactions among Mg concentrations, hypercholesterolemia, T2DM, and other cardiovascular risk factors in a cohort of 492 subjects from Spain. Plasma Mg concentrations were higher in hypercholesterolemic individuals (p = 0.001) compared to those without hypercholesterolemia. Additionally, patients in the fourth quartile of Mg concentrations had a greater likelihood of being diagnosed with hypercholesterolemia compared to those in the first quartile (OR: 3.12; 95% CI: 1.66–5.85, p < 0.001) [43]. In a cross-sectional study, Liu et al. (2020) examined the association between Mg levels and dyslipidemia. Patients identified with dyslipidemia exhibited elevated Mg levels (p = 0.002) when compared to individuals with normal serum lipid profiles. Individuals in the fourth quartile of serum Mg had a heightened risk of dyslipidemia compared to those in the first quartile (OR = 1.4, 95% CI 1. 0–1.9, p = 0.023) in the unadjusted model; however, this observation was not corroborated in the adjusted model [36]. In a recent systematic review and meta-analysis examining the relationship between Mg concentrations and dyslipidemia, Găman et al. (2021) demonstrated a link between Mg levels in the body and serum lipids in dyslipidemia and associated disorders [3]. Conflicting results underscore the need for further research to clarify the link between Mg levels and lipid profile and the lipid-lowering action of Mg intake.

There is initial evidence to indicate that B, as a trace element, may have lipid-protective effects by inflammation and ROS pathway modulation, but available clinical data are limited [18, 46, 47]. In contrast to such effects, exposure to Al has been associated with increased risk of dyslipidemia and CVD by inducing oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions, with population-based investigations suggesting positive correlations between levels of Al with TC/LDL-C [48]. Cobalt’s effects on dyslipidemia are less defined, with an influence primarily mediated via vitamin B12-dependent pathways; potassium’s effects reveal mostly indirect cardiovascular benefits; Lithium’s has complex interplays and condition-dependent metabolic effects that vary between therapeutic and environmental exposure contexts [49].

The BKMR analysis supplemented the logistic regression model by evaluating the joint, nonlinear and interactive effects of multiple elements. Boron had the highest PIP in the overall population and was the only element exhibiting a non-linear exposure–response relationship, which is consistent with its importance in the logistic regression analysis. Furthermore, the positive overall mixture effect indicates that exposure to multiple elements combined may have a greater impact on the risk of dyslipidemia than exposure to individual elements alone. The interaction observed between boron and cobalt further highlights the potential importance of interactions between elements that may not be captured by conventional regression models. While these results should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size, they emphasis the importance of mixture-based approaches in the study of dyslipidemia.

The strengths of this study include its statistical design, which utilizes multivariate logistic regression models to control for confounding variables. The use of advanced statistical techniques improves the reliability and interpretability of the findings. Furthermore, the study examines both essential and non-essential elements, providing a comprehensive biochemical perspective. High-precision elemental analysis using ICP-MS ensures the accuracy of the data. Additionally, the regional focus on Western Iran offers valuable insights into an underrepresented population in the existing literature.

Limitations

This research faced several limitations. The study design made it challenging to establish a causal link between serum element concentrations and dyslipidemia. Therefore, future research should explore causality through longitudinal studies. A significant limitation of this study was a lack of information on a number of potentially important clinical factors lipid-lowering medications, physical activity, dietary assessments, supplementation with minerals/vitamins, detailed indicators of kidney function, certain comorbid conditions, alcohol intake and other related hormones such as Vitamin D, Calcitonin, and Parathyroid Hormone. These variables can impact both serum element concentrations and lipid metabolism; therefore, these would be residual confounders. Therefore, caution should be taken when interpreting our results, they should be considered as associations not evidence of causality. Further epidemiological or clinical studies considering these important variables are needed for validation. Additionally, the fundamental mechanisms remain poorly understood, and future longitudinal studies are essential to confirm the relationship between blood element concentrations and dyslipidemia. It is also important to note that our study focused on a specific cohort of patients, which means that applying these findings to other patient groups should be done with caution. Furthermore, one limitation of the present study is its relatively small sample size. This may have reduced the precision of some effect estimates, resulting in wide confidence intervals for several odds ratios. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and confirmed by future studies with larger sample sizes. Also, exposure variables were categorized into quartiles, which may lead to some loss of information and reduced statistical power. Future studies are recommended to use continuous exposure measures [50].

Conclusion

Our investigation demonstrated a potential correlation between serum concentrations of Ca, Co, and Mg and dyslipidemia in a representative group of patients with CVDs. In summary, we found that higher levels of Ca were positively correlated with an increased likelihood of dyslipidemia. Conversely, Co and Mg levels demonstrated an inverse relationship with the likelihood of dyslipidemia among patients with CVD.
