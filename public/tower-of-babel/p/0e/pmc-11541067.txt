Passive Anti-amyloid Beta Monoclonal Antibodies: Lessons Learned over Past 20 Years
Authors: Alexandra Wicker, Jahnavi Shriram, Boris Decourt, Marwan Noel Sabbagh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder that significantly impairs cognitive and functional abilities, placing a substantial burden on both patients and caregivers. Current symptomatic treatments fail to halt the progression of AD, highlighting the urgent need for more effective disease-modifying therapies (DMTs). DMTs under development are classified as either passive or active on the basis of their mechanisms of eliciting an immune response. While this review will touch on active immunotherapies, we primarily focus on anti-amyloid beta monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), a form of passive immunotherapy, discussing their multifaceted role in AD treatment and the critical factors influencing their therapeutic efficacy. With two mAbs now approved and prescribed in the clinical setting, it is crucial to reflect on the lessons learned from trials of earlier mAbs that have shaped their development and contributed to their current success. These insights can then guide the creation of even more effective mAbs, ultimately enhancing therapeutic outcomes for patients with AD while minimizing adverse events.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Amyloid beta plaques, Immunotherapy, Anti-amyloid beta monoclonal antibodies, Clinical trials dementia, Tau, Vaccine

Key Summary Points

The development and clinical use of anti-amyloid beta monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) for the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) has been informed by many lessons.Targeting monomeric species of amyloid with mAbs has not been successful; conversely, mAbs that target oligomers, protofibrils, fibrils, and plaques have shown efficacy signals.Amyloid needs to be rapidly reduced to below 25 centiloids, the proposed threshold for amyloid-negativity.The signal of clinical efficacy might be due to the lowering of tau rather than directly related to the removal of amyloid.Appropriate patient selection is crucial; factors to consider are amyloid presence, stage of AD, baseline microhemorrhages, ability to undergo an MRI to monitor amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), and genetic risk of ARIA.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a pervasive neurodegenerative disorder associated with progressive cognitive and functional impairment, ultimately resulting in dementia [1]. Although its etiology is still unclear, AD is believed to result from a combination of biological, genetic, and environmental factors [2]. Pathologically, it is characterized by the accumulation of extracellular plaques made up of amyloid beta (Aβ) peptides and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFT) comprised of hyperphosphorylated tau proteins in the brain. The aggregation of both plaques and tangles as the core pathological features of AD leads to synaptic loss, inflammation, and cell death primarily in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus, effectively altering the affected individual’s memory and behavior [2].

Aβ is critical to many theories regarding the development of AD. The amyloid cascade hypothesis proposes that Aβ production in the brain initiates a series of events culminating in the clinical manifestation of AD. The cascade begins with the formation of Aβ oligomers, which are primarily responsible for neurotoxicity; local inflammation, oxidation, excitotoxicity, and tau hyperphosphorylation soon follow [3]. In alignment with the amyloid cascade hypothesis, Jack et al. [4] presented the hypothetical biomarker model of AD in 2010, which proposes that the levels of the Aβ biomarker Aβ42 become abnormal prior to others associated with NFT-caused degeneration, such as phosphorylated tau (pTau). As more is learned about AD, this model, too, has been revised and built off several times in the last decade. Long and Holtzman [5] noted that early AD neuroinflammatory changes correlate with Aβ deposition and that both occur prior to the onset of mild cognitive impairment (MCI); MCI then correlates with rising NFT levels and a corresponding loss of synaptic function and density. Leng and Edison [6] then developed the inflammatory model of AD, which once again stresses the significance of neuroinflammation in Aβ and tau pathologies as well as its effects on the disease’s ultimate trajectory; in this model, the microglial activation acts as a bridge between the accumulation of Aβ and tau. These models hold clinical significance as they suggest that the accumulation of Aβ is the initial discernible event in AD pathogenesis and that the later appearance of other biomarkers through the exacerbation of neuroinflammation is instead more closely correlated with symptom severity. Thus, the presence of Aβ is crucial for defining AD biologically, and it has quickly become a pharmacological target for the treatment and prevention of AD. As a result, both active and passive immunotherapies that specifically target Aβ have been developed and tested over the last 20 years in the hope of discovering a disease-modifying therapy (DMT) with clinical efficacy and limited adverse events. While tau and other targets are also being explored, they are not explicitly considered here.

Immunization against Aβ via vaccination is one of the earliest proposed active immunotherapies for AD. Active immunotherapies were thought to offer benefits such as sustained activation with less frequent administration of therapeutics to patients [7]. AN-1792, the first vaccine introduced for the treatment of AD, was a synthetic form of Aβ42 that promoted the clearance of Aβ peptides through the production of anti-Aβ antibodies within the host. In preclinical trials, virtually all mice treated with the AN-1792 vaccine outperformed untreated Tg-2576 APP/PS1 transgenic mice on cognitive tasks and showed no detectable Aβ deposits in their brains 13 months following immunization initiation [8]. The initial mouse immunotherapy studies changed the field and subsequent development was also guided (or sometimes misguided) by these animal models.

Initial phase I findings suggested that the vaccine was safe and well tolerated in patients with mild to moderate AD [9]. However, a subsequent phase IIa trial was quickly discontinued when 18 of the 300 patients in the vaccine arm developed encephalitis and five died during the study’s follow-up period. With limited precedent for such treatments, these severe adverse events highlighted the challenge of ensuring patient safety while targeting the immune system and made regulatory bodies cautious about approving a therapy with unknown long-term effects and safety profile. Despite these adverse events, 59 of the surviving patients from the phase IIa trial of AN-1792 did develop anti-Aβ antibodies. However, their cognitive and memory abilities showed no significant differences. Lower Aβ deposition was also observed as determined by MRI during the follow-up period, but survival rates and dementia progression were unaffected [10–12]. Upon autopsy of one of the phase IIa patients who suffered terminal events related to meningoencephalitis, Nicoll et al. [13] noted that Aβ was either missing or sparse throughout much of the brain besides the medial prefrontal gyrus, suggesting that target engagement had occurred. Still, the patient had exhibited no change in cognitive scores prior to developing meningoencephalitis. At autopsy, the postmortem analysis conducted 11 months after the clinical episode in one of the participants revealed striking neuropathological findings with abundant leptomeningeal T lymphocytes, relatively scanty lymphocytes in the cerebral cortex, cortical microvascular lesions related to severe cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), and edema of the white matter. The autopsy finding suggests a process separate from amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA). Overall, the immune response prompted by AN-1792 was inconsistent among patients and varied drastically from the original murine models. This inconsistency made it difficult to predict and standardize the vaccine’s efficacy across a broader population, complicating regulatory approval processes that require consistent and reproducible results. Ultimately, the development of AN-1792 was discontinued because of concerns for patients’ safety.

The development of passive immunotherapies for AD was initiated in acknowledgment of safety concerns about AN-1792. One such immunotherapy was monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), which have already been used to treat numerous other pathologies. Their conceptualization dates back to the early 1930s, and muromonab-CD3 (Orthoclone) became the first mAb approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1985 to prevent kidney rejection in transplant patients. Since then, mAbs have been approved to treat diverse conditions, including cancer, rheumatoid arthritis, and psoriasis [14]. As a treatment for AD, anti-Aβ mAbs are designed to target different forms of Aβ to encourage their natural removal by the body’s immune system. mAbs are more easily controlled than active immunotherapies owing to their relatively short half-lives, which ameliorates their risk profile while also potentially limiting their effect duration.

Early investigations with one of the first mAB, bapineuzumab, which targeted the mature plaque was an important drug insofar as many lessons were learned including managing fears about ARIA and selecting patients who had the target pathology (25% of the sample did not have amyloid). Its failure derived from the concerns regarding ARIA leading to inadequate exposure to the antibody (3% the amount of aducanumab and less than 1% of high dose solanezumab due to administration every 90 days). These kinds of lessons paved the way for more informed trial designs.

Three mAbs are currently FDA approved for the treatment of AD. Importantly, these mAbs are not first-generation; rather, they come from a long line of previous iterations that, when paired with vaccines, have repeatedly cast doubt on the amyloid cascade hypothesis. Many have found it difficult to accept evidence of clinical benefit of immunotherapy when there has been many, many studies finding immunotherapy ineffective. Some argue that a lack of an overall and immediately obvious benefit is reason enough to avoid the continued testing and prescribing of mAbs. However, despite encountering setbacks and ambiguous outcomes during the testing of anti-Aβ mAbs, the knowledge gleaned from these trials has yielded crucial insights for improving AD treatments that target disease mechanisms rather than only symptoms.

Ultimately, the goal of anti-Aβ mAbs is to demonstrate clinical efficacy within patients, which can be defined as the statistically significant superiority in cognitive and biomarker outcomes compared to a matching placebo. The development and clinical use of anti-Aβ mAbs has been informed by many lessons: what species of amyloid to target, specifics of amyloid removal, amyloid’s relationship to tau, and the importance of selecting the appropriate patient profile. Sufficient context will be needed to understand why the findings over 20 years of investigative effort are consistent and supportive. This review aims to summarize these lessons to demonstrate the usefulness of anti-Aβ mAbs to date and support their further development based on what we currently know about their efficacy in symptomatic therapeutic trials. This article relies on previously conducted research and does not include any new studies involving human or animal subjects carried out by the authors. Further, this review does not focus on presymptomatic or prevention trials.

Selection of Amyloid Species Matters

Although the ultimate goal is for mAbs to remove Aβ from the brain, the exact cellular and molecular mechanisms engaged in the process remain unclear. Nonetheless, there are two main theories on how this may be accomplished. One theory, the central nervous system clearance hypothesis, suggests that the antibodies enter the brain, where they can then bind to Aβ and facilitate the phagocytosis of the resulting complex by local microglia [15]. However, antibodies are rather large molecules (150 kDa), making them ill-equipped to pass through the blood–brain barrier (BBB); when the BBB is not altered, it is most likely that only a very small amount of antibodies would actually be able to reach the brain parenchyma each time mAbs are administered [16, 17]. Acknowledging this issue, the peripheral sink hypothesis instead proposes that peripheral antibodies sequester Aβ in the blood, reducing the levels of free Aβ and prompting the brain to release its store of the peptide [15]. To note, the latter theory is unlikely to occur with mAbs like donanemab that targets modified forms of Aβ only present in senile plaques.

Crucially, mAbs target different forms of Aβ. The Aβ peptides, cleaved from the amyloid-beta precursor protein (AβPP), have variable lengths and conformations that result in different Aβ species. Anti-Aβ antibodies, predominantly of the immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) isotype, bind to distinct epitopes on these Aβ species, targeting monomers, oligomers, protofibrils, fibrils, and plaques. The heterogeneity in binding sites, coupled with each compound’s unique binding affinity, half-life, and BBB penetrance, enables each mAb to preferentially target specific Aβ species and conformations. This selectivity is a valuable characteristic, especially in light of emerging information regarding the clinical implications of these variations. For example, the 42-residue Aβ peptide is more likely to aggregate and thus cause neurotoxicity due to the addition of two hydrophobic amino acids [18]. Aβ oligomers are considered the most pathogenic conformation, with fibrils also thought to cause neurotoxicity. By contrast, Aβ42 monomers may exert a neuroprotective effect by preventing excitotoxicity as well as mediating neuronal glucose uptake [19]. However, they are also highly likely to aggregate into more harmful species. Aβ neurotoxicity exerted through oligomers and fibrils continues to be debated, but it is likely they do not kill neurons. Humans tolerate considerable amounts of Aβ for some period before neurodegeneration is apparent (post tauopathy) and mouse models have little neuron loss in the vast majority of models studied. Aβ can interfere with synaptic activity and processing but it may not be a sufficient driver for the cognitive impairment of AD dementia.

Understanding these pathogenic mechanisms sheds light onto why some mAbs have shown greater efficacy than others. Clinical trial data suggests that mAbs that target monomeric species may be ineffective, whereas drugs targeting oligomers, fibrils, and plaques constitute those that have yielded more promising results. This point is well illustrated when comparing the discontinued drugs solanezumab and crenezumab with those that have been FDA approved or are nearing approval.

Solanezumab

Solanezumab (LY2062430; Eli Lilly) is a humanized IgG1 version of a murine mAb (m266.2) designed to bind to the central region of soluble monomeric forms of Aβ. By binding to free Aβ monomers, solanezumab was intended to prevent the formation of neurotoxic oligomers and fibrils, thus delaying AD progression [20]. Phase I (NCT02614131) and II (NCT00329082 and NCT04623242) trials in the development of solanezumab demonstrated that the drug had high tolerability and showed no signs of inducing adverse events (i.e., ARIAs).

Two large phase III randomized control trials (RCTs) followed, EXPEDITION1 (NCT00905372) and EXPEDITION2 (NCT00904683), that enrolled a combined total of 2052 patients with mild-to-moderate AD. Both placebo and experimental groups had similar increases in total CSF Aβ1-42 levels and showed either a decrease or stability in mean free Aβ1-42. Primary outcomes were changes from baseline to week 80 on the 11- or 14-item Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale (ADAS-cog11; ADAS-cog14) and Alzheimer’s Disease Cooperative Study: Activities of Daily Living (ADCS-ADL) subscale, yet neither study met its primary cognitive and functional endpoints. Aβ-PET signals also did not appear to decrease in either trial, suggesting a lack of target engagement. Interestingly, an additional subgroup analysis did indicate that patients with mild AD showed a 34% decrease in cognitive decline, which was statistically significant [21]. On the basis of these findings, solanezumab was considered potentially efficacious in patients with mild AD and brain Aβ deposition; a follow-up RCT, EXPEDITION3 (NCT01900665), was then conducted utilizing 2129 patients from this population. EXPEDITION3 utilized ADAS-cog14 as a single primary endpoint, changing ADCS-ADL to a secondary outcome. At the conclusion of the study, ADAS-cog14 scores were not improved, but ADCS-ADL scores were slightly significantly in favor of the experimental group [22]. While the trial was once again unsuccessful, solanezumab was still selected for two more secondary prevention studies, the DIAN-TU and A4.

The DIAN-TU (NCT01760005) was a 5- to 7-year phase II/phase III RCT that compared the effects of solanezumab and another mAb, gantenerumab, in 490 asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic patients who possessed mutations in one of the three AD-associated genes (i.e., AβPP, presenilin-1, and presenilin-2). Ultimately, the data compiled from the DIAN-TU suggested that treatment showed no statistically significant changes on the DIAN Multivariate Cognitive Endpoint (DIAN-MCE), a composite scale of cognitive performance developed for the study. Still, the DIAN-TU was successful in the sense that it provided some insight into why solanezumab, in particular, was ineffective. Compared to solanezumab, gantenerumab had a high affinity for fibrillar Aβ species; it itself was not clinically efficacious and had a higher incidence of ARIA, but solanezumab-treated subjects demonstrated notably greater cognitive decline. Additionally, no benefits were observed for downstream biomarkers. These subjects had higher levels of neurofilament light chain (NfL) and a mean value of total CSF Aβ1-42 more than double that seen in any of the EXPEDITION trials, indicating significantly more monomer sequestration. All of these factors are consistent with increased cognitive decline and suggest that a wrong biological target was potentially the main reason for solanezumab’s clinical failures [20, 23].

Finally, the A4 study (NCT02008357) was a 3-year phase III RCT that tested solanezumab in 1169 Aβ-PET-positive yet asymptomatic or very mild symptomatic patients with AD. Its primary outcome was the ADCS-Preclinical Alzheimer Cognitive Composite, a cognitive battery designed to be sensitive to AD at early clinical stages. There were also biomarker and imaging outcomes that included various MRIs, Aβ-PET scans, and, in a subsample of subjects, tau-PET scans, CSF Aβ1-42, tau, and pTau. Solanezumab did appear to slow Aβ deposition over the course of the trial, but it did not prevent it. In addition to this drawback, solanezumab once again did not demonstrate clinical efficacy within the A4 study. Near the end of the trial, the dose of solanezumab was increased from 400 to 1600 mg every 4 weeks; this increase severely depleted monomeric Aβ and led to cognitive worsening in subjects [24]. In light of receiving mixed results regarding efficacy, Eli Lilly has discontinued their efforts to obtain FDA approval for solanezumab.

Crenezumab

Highly homologous to solanezumab is crenezumab (MABT5102A and RG7412; Genentech and Hoffman-La Roche), an IgG4 mAb targeting multiple conformations of Aβ with varying affinity. Specifically, crenezumab recognizes oligomeric and fibrillar species of Aβ with high affinity and monomeric species of Aβ with low affinity. Crenezumab was intended to target aggregated Aβ, clear plaques, and diminish the activity of chronically reactive microglia by reducing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., tumor necrosis factor alpha, TNFα) [25, 26]. It was hoped that this would reduce the risk of subjects developing vasogenic edema (ARIA-E) while still encouraging the phagocytic removal of Aβ.

Two phase I trials (NCT02353598 and NCT02670083) indicated that crenezumab had a safe profile with no evidence of ARIA-E. However, two phase II trials of crenezumab termed ABBY (NCT01998841) and the Alzheimer’s Prevention Initiative Autosomal-Dominant AD (API-ADAD) Colombia Trial (NCT01998841) collectively failed to demonstrate benefit to cognitive performance—in patients with mild-to-moderate AD and autosomal dominant, unimpaired individuals, respectively—as measured by the ADAS-cog12, the API-ADAD, the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR-SOB), and the Episodic Memory Measure: Free and Cued Selective Reminding Task (FCSRT) scales [27, 28]. A third phase II trial, BLAZE (NCT01397578), looked for a change in brain Aβ-PET standard uptake value ratio (SUVR) with various doses of crenezumab. Still, no significant differences were found, though a secondary outcomes analysis did suggest that target engagement occurred [29]. Finally, the effect on CDR-SOB scores was further investigated in the CREAD (NCT02670083) and CREAD2 (NCT03114657) phase III trials, but these were discontinued early because of an interim analysis showing poor likelihood of finding a statistically significant treatment benefit [30]. Hoffmann-La Roche is no longer developing crenezumab, and its FDA status is officially discontinued.

Targeting Monomeric Amyloid-Beta is Not Effective

The results summarized above, while disappointing, were also crucial for furthering the development of more effective mAbs. As crenezumab is an IgG4 antibody, it lacks effector function. Thus, even though it may bind with some affinity to oligomers and fibrils in addition to monomers, it does not activate microglial phagocytosis through FC-gamma receptors. Consequently, its selectivity for oligomers likely has no biological relevance in humans, and crenezumab will ultimately bind primarily to monomers just as solanezumab did [20]. As neither solanezumab nor crenezumab showed clinical significance with this target, it is likely that any drug targeting a monomeric species of Aβ has a low probability of significantly altering cognitive decline. Conversely, drugs that have demonstrated efficacy signals are aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab, which target fibrils, protofibrils, and plaques, respectively [31, 32].

As mentioned above, Aβ monomers are generally considered less toxic than their aggregated forms. In comparison, oligomers and fibrils are more closely associated with neurotoxicity and synaptic dysfunction, which are critical aspects of AD pathology. Although targeting the monomeric Aβ species does not directly address the primary drivers of disease progression, it should theoretically decrease the likelihood of oligomerization and increase the probability of removing aggregated Aβ in the brain. However, one problem with binding monomeric Aβ is that it acts as a decoy for antibodies that bind both monomers and aggregates, which then in turn reduces the effective antibody concentration in the brain available to bind to the more neurotoxic species already present. As a very small percentage of circulating IgG antibodies are able to cross the BBB in the first place, antibody affinity for monomers then further reduces the amount available for the more beneficial aggregate reduction. Finally, Aβ performs important neuroprotective functions outside of AD pathology, such as the promotion of synaptic plasticity and integrity; these functions may partly explain why the removal of monomeric Aβ can be detrimental in patients with AD, as shown by the greater cognitive decline exhibited by patients who took solanezumab [20].

Importantly, we have learned that passive immunotherapies will likely be more effective if they produce positive cognitive outcomes without disrupting the physiological functions of monomeric Aβ. Accordingly, targeting the species known for being neurotoxic has become the next standard for the development of mAbs. As oligomers, fibrils, and plaques are more heavily implicated in the disease’s pathology, they have been shown to be much more effective in the management of AD. By developing drugs that target Aβ aggregates, not only can Aβ be removed but mAbs can also significantly curb clinical decline and AD progression, which is attributed to a mirrored reduction in neurotoxicity.

Quick Removal of Amyloid to Below 25 Centiloids is Crucial

Beyond identifying the preferred species of amyloid to target, the development of mAbs has also allowed the scientific community to be better informed of what constitutes effective Aβ removal, as well as its ideal timeframe. The centiloid scale is a 100-point scale that standardizes the measurement of Aβ plaque levels in PET imaging. On this scale, 0 represents a definitively Aβ-negative brain, while 100 represents the average signal observed in patients with mild-to-moderate AD. The centiloid scale is preferred over SUVR because it provides more consistent and comparable results across clinics and research studies, though it is possible to convert between the two [33]. On the basis of this scale, phase III clinical trials for aducanumab, lecanemab, donanemab, and gantenerumab have suggested that amyloid reductions to below 25 centiloids on Aβ-PET correlate to a statistically significant slowing of cognitive decline [34]. Additionally, for the most noticeable improvement, removal of amyloid should also be rapid, and titration periods should not be unnecessarily long [35].

Gantenerumab

Gantenerumab (RG1450 and RO4909832; Chugai Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., and Hoffman-La Roche) is a humanized IgG1 mAb with a high affinity for aggregated Aβ fibrils, more precisely targeting the N-terminal and central amino acids; it binds preferentially to Aβ localized in the brain parenchyma and vasculature. Gantenerumab facilitates the disassembly of Aβ plaques through effector cell-mediated clearance within the CNS, removing Aβ deposits mainly via microglial phagocytosis. Additionally, it was found to reduce new plaque formation without increasing soluble plasma Aβ levels in murine models [36]. Ultimately, gantenerumab was an early example of how quantity of Aβ reduction showed initial promise in affecting the rate of cognitive decline.

A phase I trial (NCT01656525) of 28 healthy volunteers receiving subcutaneously administered gantenerumab determined that the drug was safe and could later be considered for at-home injections [37]. Two phase III studies (SCarlet RoAD and Marguerite RoAD; NCT01224106 and NCT02051608) further explored the efficacy of subcutaneously administered gantenerumab at low doses of 105 or 225 mg, and interim results indicated that a higher dose would most likely be required to achieve clinical efficacy. Accordingly, a PET substudy was conducted to evaluate the efficacy of gantenerumab uptitrated to 1200 mg every 4 weeks. This exploratory interim analysis found that this dosing regimen resulted in significant Aβ removal after 104 weeks. Specifically, mean Aβ levels were reduced by 39 centiloids by the first year and 59 centiloids in the second, a change that was more than three times greater than what was observed with 225 mg of gantenerumab. These reductions culminated in all included trial groups having a mean centiloid value below 20 after 2 years. On a participant level, 52% at 24 months fell below the trial’s Aβ positivity threshold of 20 centiloids [38]. A 36-month update further supported these results, finding that after the third year, 80% of participants had negative Aβ-PET scans [39].

Two more efficacy and safety phase III trials were initiated, termed GRADUATE I and II (NCT03444870 and NCT03443973); combined, these trials recruited just under 2000 participants with prodromal to mild AD and Aβ pathology confirmed via imaging. As a result of concern for potential ARIA, the dose of gantenerumab administered was increased over a period of 36 weeks up to a target level of 510 mg every 2 weeks, regardless of the participant’s APOE ε4 genotype. The primary endpoint for the GRADUATE trials was a change in CDR-SOB scores from baseline to week 116, yet no significant differences were found between the experimental and placebo groups. At week 116, the difference in Aβ-PET signals between the gantenerumab and placebo groups was − 66.44 centiloids in the GRADUATE I trial and − 56.46 centiloids in the GRADUATE II trial. However, only 28.0% of participants in GRADUATE I and 26.8% in GRADUATE II receiving gantenerumab registered Aβ values less than 25 centiloids at week 116 (i.e., the proposed threshold for amyloid-negativity). While a dose-dependent reduction of plaque burden was achieved in some individuals, the primary endpoint was not met. Accordingly, gantenerumab did not reliably demonstrate the ability to reach amyloid levels thought to be necessary for clinical efficacy (i.e., a slowed rate of cognitive decline) [40].

GRADUATE I and II yielded a much lower magnitude of plaque removal than had been predicted by previous trials of gantenerumab. Mean Aβ-PET signals remained high even after treatment, and only slightly more than a quarter of participants achieved amyloid-negative status. Bateman et al. [40] suggested that these results support he hypothesis that rapid plaque reduction to a level below the threshold of detection may be necessary to show clinical efficacy. The 36 weeks the participants had to wait until the target dose was administered may have contributed to gantenerumab’s inability to fulfill this requirement. The lower doses given during this period would have been very minimally effective at crossing the BBB and, thus, engaging their target.

While the sponsors are no longer conducting trials using gantenerumab, they did begin developing a reformulation of the drug called trontinemab. As a bispecific, shuttle mAb, trontinemab is specifically designed to cross the BBB more efficiently than its predecessor by binding to the endothelial cell transferrin receptors lining the brain’s blood vessels. Gantenerumab was most effective at clearing Aβ when administered at doses higher than 1000 mg every 4 weeks, but this excessively high amount is associated with an increased rate of ARIA; by being better able to cross the BBB, much lower doses of trontinemab could not only be more effective but safer [41], though this remains to be determined.

Aducanumab

Aducanumab (Aduhelm and BIIB037; Biogen) is just one of the drugs whose development marked the beginning of a new generation of mAbs. It is an IgG1 antibody that targets both oligomeric and fibrillar forms of Aβ by preferentially binding to aggregated forms of Aβ in the N-terminal region (residues 3–7). In addition to promoting the clearance of Aβ aggregates through the activation of microglial phagocytosis, it is the only mAb that disrupts the aggregation process before it begins by inhibiting the secondary nucleation (formation of oligomers on the fibril surface); these characteristics pointed to aducanumab being a very promising drug [42].

Preclinical studies of aducanumab in transgenic mice showed both satisfactory target engagement and a significant reduction of Aβ plaques. Interestingly, a dose-dependent decrease in plaque size was observed in young (9-month-old) but not old (22-month-old) Tg2576 mice, which indicated that aducanumab was better able to prevent Aβ aggregation as opposed to the elimination of preexisting plaques [43]. Following the overall positive results of preclinical studies, the initial phase I study (NCT01397539) was conducted to evaluate the safety and pharmacokinetics of an ascending dose of aducanumab (0.3–30 mg/kg) given to subjects with mild-to-moderate AD dementia over the course of 24 weeks. After only mild-to-moderate adverse events were observed in participants, the highest dose was increased to 60 mg/kg. However, in addition to ARIA, participants receiving this high dose began to exhibit elevated plasma levels of Aβ40 and Aβ42, suggesting that aducanumab may begin to bind to monomeric species when given in high quantities. Additionally, there were no observable differences in cognitive scores compared to the placebo group upon administration of the ADAS-cog13; while this demonstrated a lack of patient improvement, it did, at a minimum, suggest that aducanumab was not accelerating cognitive decline. Overall, aducanumab was shown to be acceptably safe and tolerable at doses of 30 mg/kg or less [44].

Following the overall mixed outcomes of the initial phase I trial, a phase Ib study, PRIME (NCT01677572), yielded highly promising results. In this study, participants with prodromal or mild AD received monthly infusions of aducanumab at doses ranging from 1 to 10 mg/kg. After 1 year, Aβ-PET confirmed an overall reduction of Aβ plaques among study participants [45]. Biogen also conducted an exploratory clinical evaluation in PRIME, which found a significant time- and dose-dependent reduction of Aβ plaques. The mean PET SUVR composite score of the 10 mg/kg group was reduced significantly after 54 weeks of treatment, with the end value very near the score that separates a positive scan from a negative one. At 26 weeks, the 10 mg/kg treatment group produced results similar to the dose group below (6 mg/kg) at 54 weeks [46]. These Aβ reductions were accompanied by a dose-dependent slowing of cognitive decline, as measured by the CDR-SOB and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). Data showed that 10 mg/kg had the greatest impact on each scale, with 3 mg/kg producing similar results on the MMSE [46]. Consequently, the PRIME study was highly significant, marking the first trial to show evidence of a possible correlation between the reduction of brain Aβ levels and clinical efficacy.

In light of these promising results, two phase III clinical trials were designed to assess aducanemab’s continued efficacy at both doses that had demonstrated a slowing of cognitive decline in PRIME. ENGAGE (NCT02477800; N = 1638 subjects) and EMERGE (NCT02484547; N = 1647) utilized a sample of symptomatic patients who were 50–85 years old and had Aβ pathology confirmed via imaging. In each trial, aducanumab was administered monthly at doses of 3 or 10 mg/kg. The primary outcome measure was a change in CDR-SOB score from baseline to 78 weeks, with MMSE, ADAS-cog13, and ADCS-ADL scores as secondary endpoints. Both trials ended prematurely when an interim futility analysis indicated neither trial would meet their primary endpoints [47]. However, interest in aducanumab was rekindled when a 2019 follow-up report suggested that this prediction was incorrect. While ENGAGE did not meet its primary or secondary endpoints, EMERGE met them all. By analyzing each trial’s data independently, it became clear that the high dose of 10 mg/kg caused a significant change in CDR-SOB compared to placebo controls. All secondary endpoints also reflected a trend of lessened cognitive decline; the low-dose group also appeared to slow AD progression, but these differences were not significant [47].

Many critics come back to the point that aducanumab was studied in two identically designed trials yet was only shown to be effective in one. In the report’s Aβ-PET sub-studies, SUVR scores were analyzed for 488 patients from the EMERGE study and 585 patients from the ENGAGE study. Similar to PRIME, these sub-studies showed that aducanumab had a dose- and time-dependent reduction in Aβ-PET SUVR. In the EMERGE high-dose group, baseline SUVR was equivalent to 85.3 centiloids. At 78 weeks, that score had been reduced to just less than 25 centiloids (a 71% reduction). The low-dose group also showed a reduction in SUVR, but it was not as significant—88 centiloids at baseline to 46.7 after 78 weeks. As for ENGAGE, SUVR was only reduced to around 37.3 centiloids in the high-dose aducanumab group (a 59% reduction), potentially explaining this trial’s poor clinical outcomes. Overall, the reduction in adjusted mean change from baseline in Aβ-PET SUVR for the high-dose aducanumab arm in ENGAGE was 16.5% less than that in EMERGE at week 78 [47, 48]. Thus, the discrepancies between the clinical efficacy of these two trials can most likely be attributed to their respective abilities to effectively reduce Aβ-PET signals to below 25 centiloids. This observation reaffirms the conclusions drawn from GRADUATE I and II: rapid and sufficient amyloid reduction is likely critical for slowing cognitive decline.

A reason offered as to why ENGAGE was unable to produce a similar decrease in Aβ is that there was a lower participant exposure to the 10 mg/kg dose in this trial than there was in EMERGE. First, trials that involve mAbs must carefully monitor their participants for ARIA. After ARIA occurs, it is common for these trials to require patients to either stop treatment or go down to a lower dose. Acknowledging this limitation, EMERGE and ENGAGE both incorporated a protocol amendment that would allow participants with asymptomatic ARIA to continue at the same dose or titrate up to the target dose upon its resolution. The incidence of ARIA is also higher in individuals who possess the APOE ε4 allele, and as a safety measure, their original target dose was only 6 mg/kg. However, another amendment was introduced, and carriers of the allele could consent to the higher dose (10 mg/kg). Although these amendments were useful, they were introduced after enrollment had already begun; this discrepancy impacted ENGAGE more heavily as it had already recruited around 200 more participants than EMERGE. Thus, fewer ENGAGE participants had an opportunity to receive the higher dose as a result of having to take a notably longer time to titrate up. Ultimately, at the studies’ halfway point, 29% of participants in EMERGE and only 22% of those in ENGAGE had achieved the target dose, which most likely contributed to the differences observed in efficacy. To further support this, patients randomized to groups with the opportunity of receiving the full dose in ENGAGE did show similar results to their counterparts in EMERGE [48]. Additionally, the placebo groups between the two trials had different rates of decline; in ENGAGE, the placebo group showed a slower rate of decline than that of EMERGE, which most likely affected statistical comparisons between placebo and experimental groups [48]. Thus, dosing inconsistencies and discrepancies in placebo group outcomes between trials likely inhibited ENGAGE’s ability to reduce Aβ levels below the proposed threshold for a negative scan, which translated into clinical inefficacy.

Owing to the overall positive nature of EMERGE’s results, aducanumab became the first Aβ immunotherapy to be granted accelerated approval status by the FDA in 2021. However, aducanumab still faced another issue. Post-FDA approval, safety data from EMERGE and ENGAGE showed that more than one-third of the participants taking aducanumab experienced ARIA, with 25% of those participants being symptomatic [49]. Accordingly, a phase IIIb/IV (ENVISION; NCT05310071) RCT was designed to assess real-world effectiveness and safety; ENVISION was expected to be complete in 2026 [50]. Regardless, in an unexpected turn of events Biogen announced at the beginning of 2024 that it would discontinue aducanumab to reallocate resources to other AD treatments; presently, no other sponsor has taken its place.

Lecanemab

Lecanemab (Leqembi and BAN2401; BioArctic AB, Biogen, and Eisai Co.) is a humanized IgG1 mAb version of the mouse mAb158. Preclinical findings were favorable, with several groups observing that the murine version binds primarily to soluble Aβ protofibrils derived from the AβPP isoform 695, which contains both the Arctic (E693G) and Swedish (KM670/671NL) mutations. This binding results in a decrease in pathogenic Aβ levels, reduced Aβ deposition, and selective reduction of Aβ protofibrils in Tg-ArcSwe transgenic mice [51, 52]. Phase I (NCT01230853 and NCT02094729) safety and proof-of-concept trials also yielded positive results, gathering significant interest in lecanemab as a treatment for AD from the beginning of its development [53, 54].

In 2018, a phase II (NCT01767311) study with a Bayesian design attempted to find the simplest dose of lecanemab that achieved at least 90% of the maximum treatment effect. This model showed that biweekly administration of lecanemab demonstrated dose-dependent reductions in Aβ-PET signals, and the highest dose (10 mg/kg) significantly slowed the progression of cognitive decline as measured via the Alzheimer’s Disease Composite Score (ADCOMS) and ADAS-cog14 scales. In the high-dose group, Aβ brain accumulation was reduced by up to 93%, and cognitive decline was slowed by 47% on the ADAS-cog14 and 30% on the ADCOMS. Thus, 10 mg/kg of lecanemab biweekly was determined to be the most effective dosing regimen [55]. The FDA then designated lecanemab as a breakthrough therapy in 2021, which expedited the regulatory review process and resulted in the drug receiving a fast-track designation. In January of 2023, lecanemab was placed under the Accelerated Approval Pathway on the basis of the surrogate endpoint of brain Aβ removal, as demonstrated in the phase IIb trial, and its ability to reasonably predict clinical efficacy [56].

Lecanemab’s efficacy was later assessed using CLARITY AD (NCT03887455), a phase III RCT that enrolled 1795 participants with MCI thought to be attributed to AD. On the basis of the findings of the Bayesian study, subjects were given either 10 mg/kg of lecanemab or a placebo biweekly, and the primary outcome measure was a change in CDR-SOB score from baseline to 18 months. Notably, participants receiving lecanemab did not have a titration period, instead starting immediately at the highest dose. On the CDR-SOB as well as the ADAS-cog14, ADCOMS, and activities of daily living for mild cognitive impairment (ADCS-MCI-ADL) scales, patients receiving lecanemab showed a significant slowing of cognitive decline compared to those in the placebo group [57]. A substudy was also conducted that analyzed yet another secondary endpoint (i.e., the change in Aβ-PET signals from baseline to 18 months). Among a sample of 698 participants, the mean Aβ level at baseline was 77.92 centiloids in the treatment group and 75.03 centiloids in the placebo group. The adjusted mean change from baseline to 18 months was − 55.48 centiloids for those taking lecanemab (a 74% reduction) and + 3.64 centiloids for those assigned to placebo. In other words, the mean Aβ level in the lecanemab group fell below 22.99 centiloids, once again supporting the theory that 25 centiloids is both the threshold for amyloid-negativity and significant clinical efficacy [57].

In response to lecanemab demonstrating a significant reduction in the decline of clinical measures, the FDA granted the mAb full approval in July 2023. Lecanemab’s long-term effects continue to be investigated in a phase IV process as it is prescribed to the general public more frequently in the USA. While representing an important milestone in the history of anti-Aβ mAbs, it is important to note that lecanemab still takes a considerable amount of time to noticeably slow cognitive decline (around 18 months). Accordingly, competing mAbs such as donanemab have been developed in the hope of achieving clinical efficacy much sooner.

Donanemab

Donanemab (Kisunla, LY3002813, and N3pG; Eli Lilly) is a humanized IgG1 mAb derived from mouse mE8-IgG2a, which targets a pyroglutamate form of Aβ that is commonly found in senile plaques. Consequently, in contrast to many other mAbs, donanemab preferentially targets Aβ already deposited in plaques as opposed to inhibiting their formation.

A phase I clinical trial (NCT01837641) conducted between 2013 and 2016 found that donanemab was generally safe and well tolerated up to 10 mg/kg and was shown to reduce brain Aβ levels by 40–50% compared to placebo after just 28 weeks [58]. Accordingly, the FDA designated donanemab a breakthrough therapy in 2021, allowing Eli Lilly the opportunity to submit an application for accelerated approval. However, the application was later rejected on the basis of insufficient safety data produced by the phase II trial, TRAILBLAZER-ALZ1 (NCT03367403).

TRAILBLAZER-ALZ1 was an 18-month study that enrolled 257 participants with early AD and Aβ and tau pathologies confirmed via imaging. Participants were assigned randomly to receive intravenous doses of donanemab or placebo every 4 weeks for up to 72 weeks; they received 700 mg for only the first three doses and 1400 mg thereafter, resulting in a titration period of only 3 months. Aβ-PET scans were performed at 24 and 52 months; if the Aβ level was 11 to 25 centiloids, the dosage was lowered to 700 mg, and if any one scan was less than 11 centiloids or less than 25 centiloids on two consecutive scans, the participant was switched to placebo. The primary endpoint was a change in Integrated Alzheimer’s Disease Rating Scale (iADRS) score from baseline to 76 weeks (i.e., end of dosing plus 4 weeks of washout), and secondary outcomes included changes in CDR-SOB, MMSE, and brain Aβ and tau burden. It was hoped that disease progression would be halved, and the power calculation for these scales was based on that assumption [32].

In the donanemab group, the percentage of participants who achieved amyloid-negative status (< 24.10 centiloids) was 40.0% at 24 weeks, 59.8% at 52 weeks, and 67.8% at 76 weeks. The total removal averaged 86 centiloids (an 86% reduction). Additionally, about 27.4% and 54.7% of patients had a sufficient reduction in Aβ levels that they were able to switch to placebo at 28 and 56 weeks, respectively. Ultimately, TRAILBLAZER-ALZ1 showed that donanemab led to a modest reduction in cognitive and functional decline, but the study’s ultimate goal of slowing disease progression by half was not achieved [32]. Additionally, only 94 people had remained on donanemab for the entire length of the trial, which the FDA had mentioned in its reasoning for not granting donanemab accelerated approval. Regardless, Eli Lilly decided to continue the drug’s development and pursue traditional approval.

Following TRAILBLAZER-ALZ1 was TRAILBLAZER-ALZ2 (NCT04437511), a phase III trial modeled closely after its predecessor. All endpoints were essentially the same, but TRAILBLAZER-ALZ2 had enrolled nearly 1800 participants with early symptomatic AD. Participants were stratified according to PET quantification of Aβ and tau pathologies, with each strata showing clinically significant slowing of disease progression, meeting the study’s primary and secondary endpoints [59]. Owing to the significant increase in participant numbers from TRAILBLAZER-ALZ1 to this trial, the positive results of TRAILBLAZER-ALZ2 were much better received by regulatory groups. In June 2024, an FDA advisory committee unanimously voted that donanemab has demonstrated efficacy in treating patients with early AD, and the drug was fully approved in July 2024.

Overall, donanemab’s development was significant not just as a result of its association with clinical efficacy but also because of how quickly the slowing of cognitive decline was observed. While complete clearance was most prevalent at 18 months, a considerable portion of participants reached amyloid-negativity just after the 1-year mark; no other mAb had previously achieved this performance, partially because of long titration durations due to safety concerns. In fact, in TRAILBLAZER-ALZ4 (NCT05108922)—a study that directly compared donanemab with aducanumab—37.9% of donanemab-treated patients versus only 1.6% of aducanumab-treated patients achieved brain Aβ clearance within 6 months; aducanumab’s titration period lasted 4 weeks longer than donanemab’s and was much more gradual [60]. Thus, donanemab shows that it may be possible to intervene much earlier in a patient’s progression by more rapidly reducing brain Aβ levels to below 25 centiloids.

However, the rate of clearance argument is not universally held. There is preclinical experimental evidence and clinical evidence that faster removal leads to a greater risk of ARIA. ARIA can be associated with pre-existing CAA, but the removal of amyloid plaques also can induce the development of CAA and associated microhemorrhage as amyloid filters out of the brain through the periarterial drainage pathways. This is evidenced in part by the CAA containing both Aβ40 and Aβ42 after immunotherapy, but almost exclusively Aβ40 normally. Boche and Nicoll [61] found this in the phase 1 vaccine autopsies they have collected. The titration data for aducanumab suggests that the titration does reduce ARIA incidence.

Emerging Monoclonal Antibodies

At any given time, there are dozens of other anti-Aβ mAbs in various phases of development that are formulated to optimize the rate and quantity of amyloid reduction that appears to be crucial to meaningfully slow cognitive decline. Four of the most promising, yet less well-known biologics, are trontinemab, remternetug, PRX012, and ACU193.

Trontinemab One already mentioned is trontinemab (see end of Sect. “Gantenerumab”), which has shown promising preliminary results in a phase Ib/IIa trial (Brainshuttle AD; NCT04639050). This data indicates a dose-dependent reduction in brain Aβ levels, with the highest dose (3.6 mg/kg) reducing Aβ-PET signals to below the threshold of negativity in five out of eight participants within just 3 months. Trontinemab thus appears to remove brain Aβ much more rapidly than gantenerumab, which failed to achieve amyloid-negativity in 116 weeks. Overall, trontinemab’s effect is estimated to be 40 to 50 times more potent than that of gantenerumab [62].

Remternetug (LY3372993; Eli Lilly): This mAb is similar to donanemab in that it binds to N-terminal pyroglutamated Aβ. An ongoing phase III trial to evaluate the safety and efficacy of remternetug began in 2022 and aims to enroll 600 participants with early symptomatic AD. Termed TRAILRUNNER-ALZ1 (NCT05463731), its primary outcome is the percentage of patients whose brain Aβ plaques are cleared by 52 weeks compared to baseline and placebo. It is expected to run until early 2025. If its primary endpoint is met, remternetug will follow in donanemab’s footsteps in terms of rate of Aβ removal. Indeed, preliminary data from a phase I trial (NCT04451408) suggests that, when assessed via Aβ-PET, a monthly intravenous dose of 2800 mg can convert Aβ-positive participants to Aβ-negative in just 12 weeks [63].

PRX012 (Prothena Corp.): This is a humanized IgG1 antibody that recognizes an epitope in the N-terminal region of Aβ protofibrils with an affinity greater than any other comparable mAb. This drug actually received fast-track designation from the FDA in 2022 on the basis of favorable data presented by the manufacturer. According to preclinical data reported at the 2023 Alzheimer’s Association International Conference (AAIC), PRX012 bound to protofibrils with a 20 times greater affinity than lecanemab and cleared pyroglutamate-modified Aβ with three to eight times the potency of donanemab despite not binding to it. A phase I trial began in 2022 to test the safety, tolerability, immunogenicity, and pharmacokinetics of PRX012 in 50 healthy individuals and patients with AD following a single-ascending-dose regimen of subcutaneous injections of up to six dose levels. The sponsor also indicated a partnership with Walgreens in 2023 to improve enrollment for a multiple-ascending-dose regimen testing doses of 400 mg administered monthly. Preliminary results suggest that a reduction of brain Aβ occurs after 6 months of treatment with a dose of 70 mg; the trial is expected to be completed in 2024, and its results are highly anticipated [41].

ACU193 (Sabirnetug; Acumen Pharmaceuticals): Also promising is ACU193, a humanized IgG2m4 mAb that binds with high affinity to oligomeric Aβ species. In mouse models, its murine parent (ACU3B3) lessened Aβ plaque burden and improved various behavioral deficits [64]. Owing to the drug’s promising pharmacokinetics, it is predicted that ACU193 will be less toxic and more effective than other mAbs in human subjects. To support this, INTERCEPT-AD (NCT04931459) was a phase I trial completed in early 2023 that showed promising results. Specifically, higher doses of intravenously administered ACU193 (60 and 25 mg/kg) demonstrated significant reductions in brain Aβ plaque load after just 6–12 weeks. Aβ-PET levels were not quite below 25 centiloids (mean baseline SUVR in the higher dose group was approximately 72 centiloids with a 25% reduction at day 63), but this relatively rapid rate of reduction is a step in the right direction [64]. As of the time of writing this, Acumen is currently enrolling subjects for ALTITUDE-AD (NCT06335173), a phase II trial designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of ACU193.

These emerging mAbs all have one goal in common—remove brain amyloid aggregates fast—and for good reason. The clinical failures of trials such as GRADUATE I, GRADUATE II, and ENGAGE have demonstrated that higher residual brain Aβ levels fail to translate into clinical efficacy. All three of these trials resulted in participants’ Aβ levels being in the 34–51 centiloid range, well above the threshold for amyloid-negativity. In contrast, the trials EMERGE, CLARITY AD, and TRAILBLAZER-ALZ all demonstrated statistically significant and clinically relevant slowing of decline in activities of daily living, cognitive abilities, and behavioral functions in addition to depleting Aβ levels to below 25 centiloids. CLARITY AD and TRAILBLAZER-ALZ also achieved this negative status even faster than EMERGE, primarily as a result of purposeful reductions in time between the initial and highest dose. Notably, the GRADUATE and ENGAGE trials also took a significantly long time to titrate up to the highest dose because of fear of ARIA, another factor that may have contributed to these studies failing to slow disease progression. However, trials have continuously demonstrated that most cases of ARIA occur within the first eight infusions, regardless of dose titration [46, 49, 57]. Thus, as long as patients are appropriately monitored, drugs that allow for a quicker administration of the target dose can also begin to act much faster. Conversely, when titration is substantially slowed in fear of ARIA, efficacy becomes further out of reach. Accordingly, trials involving anti-Aβ mAbs have consistently established that clinical efficacy may correlate with achieving amyloid-negative status through rapid titration coupled with proper monitoring for ARIA.

TAU as a Signal of Clinical Efficacy

The biomarker model of AD proposes that changes in Aβ42/Aβ40 CSF levels mark the onset of the disease; subsequent alterations in other biomarkers occur sometime after, leading to the emergence of AD symptoms. Although the removal of Aβ generally correlates with a slower rate of disease progression, it has been suggested that because it does not play a primary role in AD severity, Aβ itself may not be directly responsible for the resulting signals of clinical efficacy. Instead, improvements may be more accurately attributed to the reduction of other AD pathologies that occur secondary to decreases in Aβ levels, such as tau. Generally, anti-Aβ drugs are able to address both Aβ and tau pathologies simultaneously by directly decreasing Aβ levels, consequently mitigating the associated neuroinflammation that leads to increased tau and disrupting the interactions between both pathologies that often result in the exacerbation of their toxic effects [65, 66]. As tau is believed to be more heavily implicated in the neurodegeneration and cognitive decline associated with AD, the effect of lowering tau levels likely translates to more significant clinical improvements. Thus, effective mAbs directly and indirectly address both Aβ and tau pathologies, delivering a comprehensive approach to AD treatment.

Various forms of pTau, a CSF- and blood-based biomarker for AD, are relevant to this discussion. Similar to Aβ, plasma pTau levels are elevated in patients who have been clinically and pathologically diagnosed with AD but not necessarily in those with other neurodegenerative dementias, barring the existence of certain comorbidities (i.e., chronic kidney disease). When AD-induced biomarker changes are chronologically ordered, it appears that pTau levels begin to rise following Aβ but before total tau (tTau) [4]. Accordingly, it is hypothesized that this biomarker may more precisely explain the relationship between brain Aβ and tau aggregates by playing the role of a mediator. If Aβ-induced tau aggregation—and, therefore, cognitive decline—is driven by increases in pTau levels [65, 66], then removing Aβ should also reduce pTau and start a critical chain reaction that culminates in the reduced accumulation of NFT and slowed clinical progression.

Acknowledging the correlation between pTau, tTau, and clinical efficacy, Hansson et al. [67] analyzed the dose- and time-dependent changes in one specific form of plasma pTau (pTau181) in patients treated with aducanumab in the EMERGE and ENGAGE trials. To reiterate, EMERGE resulted in clinical efficacy, while ENGAGE did not; this discrepancy is often attributed to the former’s ability to drop brain Aβ levels below the threshold for amyloid-negativity. Interestingly, significantly reduced levels of CSF and plasma pTau181 and tTau were also observed in EMERGE but not ENGAGE. Moreover, the treatment effect of aducanumab on plasma pTau181 correlated with the lowering of Aβ-PET SUVR. It also correlated with reduced cognitive and functional decline. These results are consistent with the hypothesized relationships between the underlying pathologies of AD.

To continue to assess the correlation between anti-Aβ treatments and plasma levels of various biomarkers, Pontecorvo et al. [68] conducted an exploratory, post hoc analysis of the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ1 trial. This secondary analysis focused not only on brain Aβ but also on plasma levels of pTau217 (a stronger indicator of Aβ-positivity than pTau181), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), and NfL. GFAP indicates glial activation in central inflammatory processes, with elevated levels observed in individuals with AD and those who are cognitively normal but Aβ-positive [69, 70]. Plasma NfL levels correspond to those in the CSF and the severity of NFT pathology observed postmortem in patients with AD [71, 72]. While no significant changes were observed in plasma levels of NfL, the study found that donanemab was associated with reductions in plasma levels of pTau217 and GFAP compared with placebo. These changes also correlated with the percent change in brain Aβ levels as determined by Aβ-PET imaging. The key difference observed was that pTau217 also correlated with baseline brain Aβ and tau PET endpoints, whereas GFAP did not, consistent with previous data suggesting that GFAP only reflects the activation of astrocytes in response to Aβ accumulation.

These findings further support the hypothesis that brain Aβ reduction can have downstream biomarker effects on disease modification. Plasma pTau217 levels dropped rapidly within 12 weeks of starting donanemab treatment, and the rate at which these proteins are removed has already been demonstrated to be critical. Although the study did not find an overall association between iADRS scores and changes in plasma pTau217 and GFAP, modeling the relationship between plasma pTau217 and a minimal clinically important difference in the iADRS score showed a modest but statistically significant association at 26 weeks. This preliminary finding suggests that pTau217 may be a proxy for the clinical efficacy of mAbs, but Pontecorvo et al. [68] acknowledge the need for a more extensive follow-up study to specifically focus on the relationship between changes in plasma biomarkers and the rate of cognitive decline. Similar analyses also need to be conducted with other anti-Aβ mAbs to validate the use of biomarkers as proxy markers of biological and clinical efficacy.

The results of the TRAILBLAZER-ALZ2 trial also highlighted that donanemab showed differing levels of efficacy in participants depending on the amount of tau they possessed. Subjects were separated into different groups based on Multi-block Barycentric Discriminant Analysis (MUBADA) SUVR thresholds: low tau (< 33rd percentile), medium tau (33rd–67th percentiles), and high tau (> 67th percentile). Among those with low/medium tau burden, 80.1% achieved Aβ clearance by week 76, compared to none in the placebo group. When combined with high tau patients, 76.4% of donanemab participants achieved Aβ clearance at 76 weeks versus none in the placebo group [59]. Plasma pTau217 concentration was also significantly reduced from baseline after donanemab treatment compared with placebo in the low/medium tau and combined populations; the least-squares mean change in tau PET SUVR was − 0.25 in the low/medium tau population and − 0.22 in the combined population at 76 weeks. In the low/medium tau group, disease progression slowed by 35.1% using iADRS and 36% using CDR-SOB. In the high tau group, disease progression slowed by 22.3% and 28.9%, respectively [59]. Thus, participants with a lower baseline level of tau showed a more significant reduction in disease progression; perhaps with tau, just as with Aβ, efficacy is more likely to occur at lower levels of accumulation because mAbs are able to begin the process required for the protein’s removal before critical damage is endured.

While anti-Aβ treatments primarily aim to reduce Aβ plaques, their efficacy signals might also stem from their secondary effects on tau pathology. By reducing Aβ levels, these treatments might decrease tau hyperphosphorylation and aggregation, leading to indirect cognitive benefits and highlighting the interconnected nature of Aβ and tau pathologies in AD. Consequently, we anticipate that starting treatment of the Aβ pathology at early stages of aggregation will likely be the most effective strategy in slowing, or event preventing, cognitive decline, in agreement with the current models of archetypical sequence of biomarker changes in AD [73, 74].

The Importance of Careful Selection of Appropriate Patient Profiles

While mAbs like donanemab and lecanemab have demonstrated clinical efficacy for patients with AD, trials have confirmed that this form of treatment is unfortunately not suitable for all patient profiles. Just as with any other medication, patient history, genetics, and disease progression are all important factors to consider when deciding if an anti-Aβ mAb is appropriate. If this step is overlooked, the patient will most likely experience little clinical benefit and may even develop unwanted adverse effects.

Early-Stage Alzheimer’s

In clinical trials for approved drugs such as aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab, mAbs have shown promise, primarily in patients with prodromal or early-stage AD. The EXPEDITION trials further underscored this point. Although solanezumab was ultimately deemed ineffective, the trials revealed differences in cognitive measures in patients with mild AD but not in those with moderate AD, suggesting that the cutoff for efficacy is very early on in disease progression [24]. This distinction could be because patients in the early stages of AD have lower levels of Aβ plaques built up in their brains, making it easier for the mAbs to clear Aβ deposits to the threshold of amyloid-negativity. This early intervention is crucial because it allows the drug to reduce Aβ burden before it can fully begin to cause significant and irreversible damage, which would otherwise contribute to the disease’s progression. Indeed, triple-transgenic mouse models suggest that anti-Aβ therapies are unlikely to be successful once neurodegeneration is well established [75]. As explained in the previous section, it has been suggested that the later stages of AD become increasingly independent of Aβ toxicity and that other mechanisms—such as tau protein pathology—predominate [76]. Thus, once AD advances beyond the moderate stage, mAbs have limited influence over the behavioral and cognitive changes that characterize the later stages of AD dementia.

Confirm Amyloid Positivity

The first mAb developed for the treatment of AD was bapineuzumab (AAB-001; Pfizer Inc. and Janssen) in the early 2000s. It was intended to specifically target the N-terminal of both insoluble and soluble forms of Aβ, with a higher affinity for the former. Four phase III trials were conducted using bapineuzumab: Studies 301 (NCT00574132), 302 (NCT00575055), 3000 (NCT00667810), and 3001 (NCT00676143). None demonstrated any significant differences in clinical or functional endpoints [77, 78]. In their report on the 3000/3001 studies, Vandenberghe et al. [78] suggested that one of the primary reasons for bapineuzumab’s inefficacy lies within the decisions made regarding participant enrollment, pointing out a similar issue that occurred with solanezumab. Although brain imaging was conducted at baseline and week 80, confirmed amyloid-positivity was not required of participants for enrollment in the EXPEDITION I and II trials. In fact, only 169 patients in EXPEDITION I (17%) and 97 patients in EXPEDITION II (9%) underwent both baseline and follow-up Aβ-PET scans; even fewer had measurements of Aβ in the CSF conducted [24]. Thus, it is highly likely that participants without elevated levels of baseline Aβ were included in these trials. For bapineuzumab, additional analysis of the 301/302 and 3000/3001 trials confirmed that patients with an insufficient amount of Aβ were enrolled. Aβ-PET scans conducted in a subgroup analysis of the 3000 and 3001 studies revealed that 25% of participants who met the clinical criteria for AD dementia did not meet the threshold for Aβ positivity at baseline. Similarly, in the 301 and 302 studies, 42.5% of participants were included despite subthreshold Aβ levels [78]. As a result of their “suspected non-amyloid pathology,” it is likely that these patients had some other form of dementia. mAbs are specifically designed for patients with AD, not conditions such as Lewy body dementia, frontotemporal dementia, or vascular cognitive impairment. These drugs target the accumulation of Aβ in the brain because Aβ is distinctly characteristic of Alzheimer’s; if there are no brain Aβ aggregates, then the drug is treating a population without the target pathology. Thus, it is crucial to confirm potential participants’ Aβ pathology using imaging techniques or blood/CSF analysis prior to their enrollment.

Selection of Patients with Few Microhemorrhages

Microhemorrhages are small ruptures that occur within blood vessels in the brain; they become more prevalent with age and can occur even in neurologically healthy individuals. While often asymptomatic, these cerebral microbleeds can become worrisome, sometimes linked to consequences such as cognitive decline, stroke, and increased mortality [79]. In the context of mAbs, having many microhemorrhages at baseline is a serious risk factor that is predictive of future ARIA events. In support of this, preliminary analyses of a phase II bapineuzumab trial noted that patients with baseline microhemorrhages were more likely to later develop ARIA-E; 35% of individuals with microhemorrhages at baseline later developed ARIA-E compared to only 6% of those with no preexisting microhemorrhages [79]. This same result was mirrored in the target dose group in ENGAGE and EMERGE [49]. In order to mitigate the risk of ARIA, candidates for anti-Aβ mAbs must possess little to no microhemorrhages prior to beginning treatment. Specifically, the Appropriate Use Recommendations (AUR) group suggests that patients with more than four microhemorrhages or any other evidence of cerebrovascular disease at baseline should be excluded from treatment [80]. It is also recommended that patients be treated at the earliest stage of detection of abnormal Aβ aggregates possible because these patients are younger, hence have less risk of experiencing pre-existing cerebrovascular damage than older patients with more advanced Aβ burden.

Safety Monitoring of ARIA Through MRIs

While identifying existing microhemorrhages can help minimize the incidence of ARIA, it is still a common adverse event associated with anti-Aβ mAbs. The frequency of ARIA with the approved mABs is listed in Table 1. As AD progresses, vascular integrity and Aβ clearance become impaired as cerebral vessels begin to accumulate significant Aβ deposits in their wall. Following the initiation of anti-Aβ therapy, vessels in the brain with preexisting Aβ pathology (referred to as cerebral amyloid angiopathy, or CAA) become more susceptible to vascular extravasation events because of this; accordingly, vasogenic edema and microhemorrhages are more likely to occur [81]. ARIA becomes particularly problematic when it causes the patient to begin experiencing confusion, headache, or dizziness; the appearance of these symptoms necessitates close monitoring and potential cessation of treatment. Thus, in addition to a pre-treatment scan to identify any existing microhemorrhages, routine MRI scans are essential for identifying ARIA early and ensuring patient safety if it is developed. For lecanemab in particular, AUR suggests that MRIs should be obtained at baseline, after the 5th, 7th, and 14th infusions, after 1 year of treatment, and if the patients display symptoms suggestive of ARIA [80]. Although an MRI is an extremely safe procedure, its use of a strong magnet excludes most patients with implanted pacemakers, certain prosthetics, and any other iron-based metal implant. Therefore, the existence of these medical devices must be identified before a mAb can be prescribed to ensure safety monitoring is possible.

Assess APOE Genetic Profile Risks

Whereas APOE ε2 may actually demonstrate a protective effect against AD, the APOE ε4 allele is associated with an increased risk of AD and cognitive decline [85]. The APOE ε4 genotype is present in approximately 15–25% of the total population, but female carriers are at a higher risk of AD at younger ages than their male counterparts [85]. In patients who have already been diagnosed with AD and begin a treatment regimen involving an anti-Aβ mAb, the presence of APOE ε4 also correlates to a higher risk of ARIA, symptomatic ARIA, and recurrent ARIA due to CAA. While the risk of ARIA remains relatively low for APOE ε4 heterozygotes, the risk of developing ARIA in individuals with two copies is significantly more worrisome. In CLARITY AD, APOE ε4 noncarriers only had a 5.4% incidence of ARIA-E, which doubled in heterozygotes (10.9%). However, in APOE ε4 homozygotes, the incidence of ARIA-E jumped to 32.6% [80]. The risk becomes even higher with donanemab, with clinical trials reporting ARIA-E in 15.7% of APOE ε4 noncarriers, 22.8% in heterozygous APOE ε4 carriers, and 40.6% in homozygous participants [59]. Even more concerning is aducanumab; in the combined high-dose groups from the EMERGE and ENGAGE trials, the incidence of ARIA-E was 20.3% in noncarriers, 35% in heterozygotes, and a staggering 65% in homozygous carriers [47]. Accordingly, the AUR deems it necessary to carefully examine a patient’s genetic profile and discuss with them the serious adverse events they could experience on the basis of the evidence provided by these trials, especially if they are APOE ε4 homozygotes [80].

Special Considerations for Diverse Patients

Patients with AD come from diverse ethnoracial, socioeconomic, and regional backgrounds, necessitating tailored considerations before starting treatment with anti-Aβ mAbs. While a patient’s background does not necessarily determine their treatment eligibility, it should still be acknowledged with regards to access and efficacy.

Although AD dementia rates are higher among older Hispanic and Black adults, safety and efficacy studies for mAbs primarily utilize White participants [1]. In the trials for lecanemab and donanemab, only 20% of participants in CLARITY AD and 7% in TRAILBLAZER-ALZ2 were people of color [57, 59]. This underrepresentation is largely because people of color tend to have lower baseline levels of Aβ than White patients. A secondary analysis of the Imaging Dementia-Evidence for Amyloid Scanning (IDEAS) cohort study showed a higher proportion of Aβ-positive PET scans among White participants compared to Black and Asian participants. After controlling for social and demographic factors, the higher proportion of Aβ-positive scans persisted for White participants compared to Hispanic and Asian (but not Black) participants [86]. As clinical trials require subjects to possess sufficient Aβ-PET signals for enrollment, low Aβ levels at baseline make people of color two to four times less likely to qualify for a trial involving an anti-Aβ mAb [87]. This lack of racial diversity in anti-Aβ mAb trials, combined with the disease pathology differences observed in the IDEAS study, raises concerns about the generalizability of these biologics across ethnoracial groups. Specifically, as a result of these diverse patients possessing lower levels of Aβ at baseline, anti-Aβ mAbs may be an inappropriate treatment; more diversity in trials could help confirm that this is indeed the case.

Residing in a rural area also poses substantial barriers to accessing anti-Aβ mAbs. Firstly, obtaining an AD diagnosis and receiving appropriate care is challenging in regions with limited availability of medical specialists, although the advent of telemedicine has mediated this disparity somewhat. In a study by Liu et al. [88], 59.4% of dementia patients in the USA resided in areas with fewer than 45 dementia specialists per 100,000 population aged 65 and older; a larger share of rural patients resided in these shortfall areas than urban patients despite the rural population being much smaller. Even when a specialist is accessible, rural areas often lack the necessary MRI and Aβ-PET technologies required for mAb prescription and monitoring. This necessitates frequent travel to urban centers, which is not feasible for many patients because of time, financial constraints, and physical limitations. The current standard administration route of mAbs (i.e., intravenous infusion) contributes to these obstacles. For example, if a patient has to travel over 2 h to an infusion center every 2 weeks for an infusion of lecanemab, this can impose a significant burden on the patient and affect their ability to adhere to their treatment regimen. In the future, the availability of mAbs in the form of a subcutaneous injection could significantly enhance accessibility for a more diverse patient population. While this approach would not resolve all treatment barriers faced by rural patients, it could reduce the need for frequent travel to specialized infusion centers, thereby alleviating some of the logistical and financial burdens. Crucially, subcutaneous injections could be administered at home, making treatment more manageable and less disruptive to patients’ lives.

Finally, the substantial costs associated with anti-Aβ mAbs deter many low-income patients. For example, despite Medicare coverage, the annual out-of-pocket cost of lecanemab exceeds $5000 [89]. Given that the median income for individuals on Medicare is approximately $30,000 per year, this expense represents a considerable financial burden that may render such treatments inaccessible to a large segment of the population [89]. Addressing responsible pharmaceutical pricing and ensuring sufficient insurance coverage are essential to mitigating healthcare disparities in the long term, but affordability remains a crucial deciding factor for patients considering anti-Aβ mAb treatment.

Anti-Aβ mAbs can be very beneficial DMTs, but only for those who are appropriate candidates. If AD has progressed too far, Aβ is no longer a useful target; anti-tau medications may instead be more beneficial. Similarly, if the patient has no significant accumulation of Aβ in their brain, the mAb will have little to nothing to target. Furthermore, MRIs are a necessity to ensure that the mAb is not causing unwanted effects, like ARIA. While ARIA is not usually life-threatening, it can be extremely harmful if not caught and treated quickly. For this reason, genetic profiling is also crucial as it allows patients and providers to determine if the risk of complications is too high. Finally, diverse patient profiles hold implications for anti-Aβ mAb treatments and highlight areas future research should consider to help ameliorate health disparities and broaden access to these DMTs.

Discussion

With the advent of DMTs, the approach to treatment for AD is evolving. Until recently, symptomatic treatments were the standard of care for AD and include acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, such as donepezil, galantamine, and rivastigmine, and the N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist memantine. While these options have a modest therapeutic effect over time relating to the reduction of memory loss and aid in symptom management, they are unable to reverse, halt, or even slow the progression of AD.

As one of the top ten leading causes of death worldwide [1], it is imperative that effective DMTs are developed for the treatment of AD. Accordingly, both active and passive immunotherapies have been investigated in an attempt to address this unmet medical need. The AD vaccine, AN-1792, was the first active immunotherapy proposed, intended to have long-lasting effects with the convenience of fewer administrations; however, it came with severe adverse effects and an inability to show clinical efficacy. While other vaccines such as Affitope AD02, CAD106, ACC-001, and UB-311 followed AN-1792, few have been able to proceed to phase III trials because of a lack of clear benefits in cognitive measures and an extensive array of associated adverse events such as microhemorrhages, vasogenic edema, brain atrophy, and even death [10]. Notably, none have received FDA approval to date. In contrast, passive immunotherapies—specifically, mAbs—have already seen success. Aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab have all been approved by the FDA, and the last two are available for clinical use. Early mABs (bapineuzumab, solanezumab, crenezumab, gantenerumab) might have failed because the dose was too low (bapi), the species of amyloid was not the toxic species (sola, cren), or the titration was too slow (gant).

Despite some criticisms, it is generally believed that lecanemab and donanemab represent the first steps taken in the development of a more hopeful future for patients with AD [90]. However, these advancements are the culmination of over 20 years of foundational research. Accordingly, lecanemab and donanemab owe their efficacy to insights gained from earlier therapeutic attempts. Bapineuzumab underscored the necessity of targeting patients with Aβ pathology, while solanezumab and crenezumab revealed the critical importance of targeting specific Aβ species—demonstrating that monomeric Aβ is likely an unsuitable target. Still, merely targeting oligomers, fibrils, and plaques proved insufficient to generate strong clinical benefits. Belonging to a trial that valued safety over timeliness, gantenerumab was pivotal in demonstrating that speed of titration and, in turn, rate of Aβ removal is critical. Careful monitoring for ARIA enabled by the selection of appropriate patient profiles ensures that quick administration is possible. Aducanumab was similarly instrumental, but it additionally illustrated how critical reducing brain Aβ loads to below the threshold of 25 centiloids is through its unique trial outcomes. Lecanemab and donanemab do not signify the end either, as their own unique findings and implications have been taken into consideration to develop more potent emerging mAbs. Donanemab specifically highlighted tau’s potential role in contributing to signals of clinical efficacy as it seemed to simultaneously address both tau and Aβ much more rapidly than its predecessors. By following donanemab’s lead, drugs such as remternetug, PRX012, and ACU193 have the potential to remove Aβ more quickly and efficiently, opening new avenues to efficacy.

Conclusion

Future research will likely identify combination therapies, indication and maintenance dosing, and multi-drug target approaches. Additionally, forthcoming drugs are expected to have better safety profiles, potentially reducing the risk of ARIA and enabling more accommodating safety protocols. Given the unknown etiology of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), it remains unclear how long patients will need to take anti-Aβ mAbs. As mAbs like lecanemab and donanemab continue to be prescribed and monitored, the required treatment duration will hopefully become clearer. Regardless, current administration regimens can be difficult to deploy for diverse individuals, so innovations such as effective mAbs that can be administered via subcutaneous injections should be prioritized to help minimize healthcare disparities. Addressing issues of cost, accessibility, and lack of diverse patient representation in clinical trials is also crucial. Prioritizing early diagnosis and prognostication, especially for diverse individuals, will help these patients achieve the best possible health outcomes. Ultimately, considering all of the above factors will help create more effective, equitable treatment options and improve prospects for a broader patient population.
