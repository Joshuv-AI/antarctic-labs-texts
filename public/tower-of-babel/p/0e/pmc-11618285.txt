Role of social media in the presentation of disorders of gut–brain interaction: Review and recommendations
Authors: Michael R Salzberg, Hannah Kim, Chamara Basnayake, Darcy Holt, Michael A Kamm
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs clinicians involved in the care of patients with disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBIs), we—and many colleagues—have the impression that social media are adversely shaping the nature, presentation, and ability to manage these disorders, especially at the severe end of the DGBI clinical spectrum. We turned to the research literature to see if these clinical impressions were corroborated but found it virtually nonexistent. Social media have rapidly become a ubiquitous, pervasive part of the lives of most people on the planet. Although they bring many benefits, they are also replete with health misinformation, reinforcement of abnormal sick‐role behavior, and undermining of the legitimacy of psychological care. We first set out four reasons for concern about social media and DGBIs, particularly severe DGBIs. These reasons stem from phenomena described in medical fields outside DGBIs, but there is no reason to think DGBIs should be exempt from such phenomena. We then present the results of a literature search, which yielded only eight disparate recent empirical studies. We review these studies, which, although not uninformative, reveal a field in its infancy. We set out implications, most urgently multidisciplinary research directly addressing the role of social media and evaluation of interventions to mitigate its ill effects. Gastroenterological clinicians involved in DGBI care and research need to collaborate with experts in social media research, which is a very rapidly evolving, specialized field. Although knowledge is at an early stage, there are implications for specialist practice, education and training, and DGBI service delivery.Keywords: delivery of health care, functional gastrointestinal disorders, patient care team, social media, somatoform disorders

Background and aim

The advent of social media has brought many benefits for patients, especially in regard to information seeking, social connection and support, and interaction with clinicians and health care, 1 as well as creating new possibilities for medical research. 2 The extraordinary speed of penetration of social media around the world means that their uses in the health sector are constantly and rapidly evolving and undoubtedly will continue to evolve in the coming years, as will research into their benefits and costs. This is true for most types of illness, including gastroenterological disorders. Among clinicians treating disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBIs), concerns have been raised that social media have become a vehicle for disseminating misinformation, reinforcing abnormal sick‐role behavior, and undermining the relevance of psychological care. Similar concerns have been raised in other medical specialties for a range of other disorders. The concern is especially acute in regard to children and young people, and particularly so at the more severe end of the DGBI clinical spectrum.

There are four reasons—from outside the DGBI field—to suspect that these impressions may be correct and that social media may indeed also be influencing DGBI presentations: (i) evidence of adverse effects of social media on other dimensions of the mental and physical health of young people, for example, body image and eating disorders; (ii) potent effects of social media on the social worlds of young people during crucial developmental stages; (iii) effects of social media on health information‐ and support‐seeking and illness‐ and sick‐role behavior; and (iv) the example from neurology of epidemics of Tourette's‐like behavior indubitably spread by social media.

It is unlikely that DGBIs are exempt from the kinds of social media phenomena occurring in other sectors. Thus, it is timely to examine the research literature to see whether or to what extent these concerns are justified. We conducted a literature search seeking empirical studies of social media and DGBIs. We found only eight disparate studies, the earliest published in 2017, and as yet no replication.

After an introductory discussion of relevant aspects of social media, we enlarge on the four indirect reasons for concern. We then describe and discuss the eight empirical studies, which, although not uninformative, reveal a field in its infancy, needing urgent attention. We conclude with implications, including research priorities. Although the state of knowledge is early‐stage, enough is already known to see implications for clinical training and practice and for DGBI service models.

Social media

In the last 10–15 years, especially since the advent of smartphones, social media have become an integral part of the lives of most of the world's population. 3 , 4 , 5 Defining social media is challenging given their fluid, heterogeneous nature. Bayer et al. 6 emphasize five features: internet‐based channels of communication that are interactive and perceived as social in nature; that support asynchronous social interaction; that derive value mainly from user‐generated content; and that allow “broadcasting” by individuals to large audiences (p. 473) (as well as one‐to‐one and one‐to‐several communication). In addition to well‐known platforms such as Facebook, YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, Twitter (aka X), and so forth, this definition encompasses online social games, virtual social worlds, and blogs. Similarly, the purposes of social media use are manifold, including entertainment, social networking, dating, information seeking, not least regarding health topics, marketing, and political activity.

Web 1.0, characterized by largely passive consumption of mostly textual material, was revolutionized by the advent of Web 2.0 and social media, which offer a radically more interactive and communal experience and a dramatically more effective vehicle for information—and misinformation 7 —exchange.

On Web 2.0, everyone can be a content creator through the ability to easily and quickly post content—images, videos, or text—and receive immediate responses, for example, via comments, likes/dislikes, and so forth. Importantly, posts can also be rapidly “shared” or “forwarded” to large numbers of people, in a manner analogized to viral amplification.

Social media have by no means eliminated traditional media dealing with DGBIs and “gut health” such as books, magazines, documentaries on TV, cinema, online, public lectures, and so forth. On the contrary, material on DGBIs in all these media is abundant, and patients are undoubtedly consuming a mixture of online and traditional content. Individual communicators—both expert and less‐than‐expert—often are active across several traditional and social media: writing books, producing documentaries, lecturing, running seminars and health retreats, as well as having a social media presence and creating a range of social media content (blogs, apps, etc.), sometimes also marketing products relevant to gut health and DGBIs. Traditional and social media constitute an interconnected ecosystem that needs to be considered in toto.

Reasons for concern arising outside the disorder of gut–brain interaction field

Apart from our own clinical impressions and those of many colleagues, there are four sets of reasons to prompt concern that social media might be adversely shaping DGBIs. We focus our discussion on children and young people principally because they represent critical developmental periods, including the development of ideas and behaviors around health and illness.

iImpacts of social media on mental health

Impacts of social media on mental health

Extensive research addresses the causes of the youth mental health crisis seen in some societies in the last 10–15 years, marked especially by steeply rising rates of anxiety, depression, and suicidality. Some publications attribute a crucial role to the advent of both smartphones and the evolution of social media in this time period, while others dispute this. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Regrettably, with a few exceptions (e.g. body image and eating disorders and Tourette's‐like syndromes; see below), somatoform and functional disorders, including DGBIs, have largely been neglected. Certainly, there are controversies about the causal role of social media, controversies that stem from the diverse theoretical and methodological commitments of the researchers as well as the complex nature of social media themselves as agents—simultaneously—of both benefits and harms. 14 , 15 , 16

The causation of mental illness in young people (as for the causation of DGBIs 17 , 18 ) is currently understood within a biopsychosocial framework that assumes a complex interplay of genetics, early life experience, temperament, personality, cognitive and other individual differences, family dynamics, and socioeconomic and cultural circumstances. The complex impacts of social media now need to be factored in. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22

Some of the toxic aspects of online life are well recognized and account for some of the harms: social media/smartphone addiction, bullying, doxing, catfishing, and stalking; sexual harms, including exposure to pornography, online grooming and sexual abuse, and “revenge porn” 23 ; and compromised privacy and permanence of posted material. However, other harms are likely generated in more complex ways by the effects of social media on facets and stages of identity development in childhood and adolescence.

iiThe new technologically mediated social worlds of children and adolescents

The new technologically mediated social worlds of children and adolescents

Although most platforms require the user to be ≥13 years old, there is widespread use by younger children, a majority of whom have used a personal smartphone in late childhood. For those born in the last 10–15 years, “social media natives,” social media are a pervasive part of growing up. Many young people live in a “hybrid reality,” a mix of online and offline reality. 19 The complex consequences for psychological development of these technologically driven changes in social life are intensely debated and researched. Many benefits have flowed from the arrival of social media, such as the mitigation of social isolation, but also many harms.

For reasons to do with both psychological 19 , 20 and neural 24 development, adolescents seem particularly susceptible to “sensationalist and fake news,” 25 to emotionally gripping content blurring the boundaries between fact and fiction. Part of the success of social media platforms is attributable to algorithms that, typically driven by commercial motives, intensify users' engagement by privileging content that is dramatic, emotive, and/or conflictual.

For most young people and their families, social media—for good or ill—are now an inescapable fact of life, such that withdrawing from them—voluntarily or through parental control—might be as consequential as using them.

iiiSocial media and health information‐seeking and illness‐ and sick‐role behavior

Social media and health information‐seeking and illness‐ and sick‐role behavior

The forms of information and support available online are very diverse, as are the creators of online content and experiences. These span websites, blogs, and other social networking sites dealing with a huge array of health conditions and their diagnosis, causation, prevention, and treatment. Some are created and maintained by individuals, sufferers of particular illnesses, or “experts,” qualified or not; others by organizations, which may be nonprofit, such as government health authorities, or commercial entities marketing products or health care.

Spurred by political developments and the COVID‐19 pandemic, there has been burgeoning research on the identification and mitigation of misinformation generally 7 , 26 and health information in particular. 1 , 27 , 28 Although a range of illnesses and behaviors have been considered, misinformation about DGBIs has not been directly studied, although a recent publication on the gut microbiome discusses dietary fads and unproven treatments found on social media, 29 and one of the studies reviewed below did directly explore this. 30

Given the dominance of social media as the source of health information and their potent capacity to facilitate online social connection, they may act as a “spread vector” for illness 31 and facilitate sick‐role subcultures. 32 , 33 Compared with “traditional” mass psychogenic illnesses—conversion disorder spreading through face‐to‐face social networks—spread is via digitally connected networks. 34 Indeed, the terms mass social media‐induced illness, 35 social media‐associated abnormal illness behavior, 36 and even Munchausen's by the internet 35 have been proposed. “Echo chambers,” extensively discussed regarding politics, undoubtedly apply to health content as well. There exists a “Darwinism of the algorithm” where the most extreme symptoms or presentations are rewarded with views. 36

Social media have also seen the rise of so‐called influencers with thousands, even millions, of followers and views. Individual influencers may deal with one or more mental or physical health topics. Some are—or claim to be—sufferers from the illness they post about. They may be emotionally supportive, provide social connection, act as positive role models, advocate for services or research, and help destigmatize various conditions. Others cause harm by disseminating misinformation, selling useless or harmful products, propagating unachievable or unrealistic goals and aspirations, reinforcing abnormal sick‐role behavior, and making accurate content and healthy support more difficult to find in the immense, complex information landscape. Abnormal illness‐ and sick‐role behavior are essential to consider, as these are often evident in patients with more severe forms of DGBIs entrenched in a sick role that has become part of their identity.

As mentioned, these kinds of processes have been implicated in recent changes in the presentation of a range of disorders, notably eating and body image disorders. 32 , 37 Again, there is no reason to think that the DGBI world is immune from such phenomena.

There is, however, a dearth of research into the impact of social media (positive or negative) on DGBI presentations. In the absence of this, however, for prima facie evidence, one need only view TikTok posts, typically by teenage girls, from their hospital bed with a nasogastric tube in view (sometimes decorated), narrating their illness journey and explaining their potential diagnoses. The video posts can include hashtags such as #POTS, #Ehlers‐Danlos, #gastroparesis, #smasawareness, #vascularcompressionsyndrome, and #tubie. Online social networking may help people cope with the isolation and disempowerment that can come from experiencing such illnesses, but at the same time raise concerns about misinformation and reinforcement of sick‐role behavior, diversion from appropriate care (for the functional and/or psychological drivers of DGBIs), and the pursuit of physical diagnoses and interventions that incur the risk of iatrogenic harm.

A core challenge in the management of some patients with more severe DGBIs is their insistence on further investigation and physical intervention, linked to the belief that their symptoms are due to an organic syndrome such as superior mesenteric artery syndrome (SMAS), nutcracker syndrome, median arcuate ligament syndrome (MALS), gastroparesis, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), hypermobility syndromes (notably Ehlers Danlos), chronic fatigue syndrome/myalgic encephalomyelitis (CFS/ME), dysautonomia, and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO). Whatever the general validity of these disorders, in severe DGBI syndromes, the diagnosis is highly questionable, ignoring clear evidence of psychological disturbance and somatization.

Physical interventions requested and/or considered include parenteral feeding (nasogastric and nasojejunal tubes and total parenteral nutrition) and bowel and vascular surgery. The evidence for such interventions in these patients is typically minimal or nonexistent or, when present, contraindicates them. In our experience, relief—when it does occur—is partial and/or temporary, and complications are common, with a significant risk of iatrogenic harm.

This is exemplified by the recent death in Australia of a 20‐year‐old woman after surgery for MALS and nutcracker syndrome. The regulatory tribunal noted “a background of a severe eating disorder,” placing restrictions on the surgeon's practice. 38

Consistent with the above trends, in the last decade, intestinal failure units in Australia have seen a radical transformation in the clinical profile of patients referred for artificial nutrition support, including home enteral and parenteral nutrition. The classical causes of intestinal failure, such as short bowel syndrome, are now outnumbered by patients who could be broadly classified as severe forms of DGBIs. Research prompted by this strong clinical impression is only just beginning (Dr Darcy Holt, Monash Health, personal communication).

ivSocial media and another functional disorder: epidemics of Tourette's‐like behavior

Social media and another functional disorder: epidemics of Tourette's‐like behavior

It is vital to appreciate that analogous phenomena are playing out with other non‐gut disorders, notably a striking increase in epidemics of functional tic‐like behaviors (FTLBs) in young people, which was identified around 2010, 34 and again with COVID‐19, 39 undoubtedly propagated via social media. 40 FTLBs often mirror those of social media influencers, including specific expressions, coprophenomena, and involvement of body parts not usually seen in typical tics.

In sum, these four lines of evidence compel us to consider whether social media are affecting DGBIs, as there is no reason to suppose that DGBIs are immune from such phenomena. But what at present is known about the types, scale, and seriousness of the effects, if any?

Methods

We conducted searches in both PubMed and Web of Science, covering any year up to October 2023. To maximize yield, we allowed searches on “any field.” The terms employed were “social media” AND each of “functional gastrointestinal disorders,” “disorders of gut‐brain interaction,” and “functional gut disorders.” This was repeated for a range of individual, major social media platforms (TikTok, Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, etc.) and for several common DGBIs individually (irritable bowel syndrome [IBS], functional dyspepsia, etc.). We excluded publications in which social media were employed solely to recruit subjects for a study of a topic other than social media themselves. We excluded one study that appeared only as an abstract. 41

Results

Our search yielded eight empirical studies 30 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 (one reported in two publications 43 , 44 ) (Table 1), the earliest as recent as 2017. One study addressed DGBIs collectively, 43 , 44 one “gastrointestinal discomfort” (in effect covering many forms of DGBIs), 46 one gastroparesis, 48 three IBS, 30 , 42 , 49 one functional abdominal pain, 45 and one cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (CHS). 47 Four studies were of sufferers, either of specific DGBIs or DGBIs generally; four were studies of online content concerning one or more DGBIs.

Studies of social media use in disorder of gut–brain interaction sufferers

Alateeq et al. 42 investigated whether “the number of hours spent daily on social media” was correlated with IBS symptoms in adults. A total of 810 Saudi adults responded to an online, self‐administered questionnaire. This sample was young (~58% aged 20–29 years), ~60% female, mostly single, with ~67% educated to bachelor's degree, with an overall “IBS diagnosis” (presumably self‐attested) prevalence of 41.2%. Although a univariate association was found between “IBS diagnosis” and a greater number of hours per day spent using a mobile phone, this was not supported by multivariate regression; that is, their (implied) hypothesis was not supported. Unfortunately, it is not clear from this report how the diagnosis of IBS was made.

Cinquetti et al. 43 , 44 conducted a cross‐sectional survey of ~1600 schoolchildren aged 11–14 years. A pen‐and‐paper, self‐completed questionnaire assessed smartphone use, diet, physical activity, and Rome IV‐defined DGBIs. A Rome IV disorder was found in 30.6%, with a greater prevalence in girls than boys. A total of 89.5% of children owned a smartphone, with the average age of receiving a first smartphone being ~10 years. In multivariate logistic regression, the presence of functional gastrointestinal disorder was significantly associated with smartphone addiction (odds ratio ~2) and some other variables. This study's strengths include the high response rate and use of validated scales. However, “smartphone addiction” provides no direct insights into social media use.

A large body of literature links functional abdominal pain to anxiety, depression, stress, and trauma; other studies report associations between social media use and psychological ill‐health.

Prompted by these findings, Samuel et al. 45 conducted a case–control study exploring associations between social media use and functional abdominal pain in adolescents. Cases were 26 patients aged 13–18 years with either IBS or functional abdominal pain alone; 33 age‐matched controls were from a “well‐child clinic.” Inclusion required possession and use of an iPhone, as this automatically records social media data: screen time per day, which platforms were used, and screen time for various purposes (social networking, entertainment, “reading and reference,” and “productivity”). Subjects were interviewed in clinic or by telephone, with the questionnaire including a pediatric DGBI scale and questions eliciting social media platforms utilized in the past year.

No association was found between case status and screen time per day or number of platforms utilized, nor with iPhone use for social networking, although cases used their iPhone significantly more often for “reading and reference,” “entertainment,” and “productivity.” This study exhibits a loose concept of “social media,” for example, including the use of Kindle and iBooks and not distinguishing between social and solitary games.

Yu et al. 48 aimed primarily to characterize “burdens, concerns, and quality of life of patients with gastroparesis” by a cross‐sectional survey via the internet, also obtaining some data about social media usage. They recruited ~1400 adults with a self‐attested diagnosis of gastroparesis. Two findings are noteworthy here: first, high levels of comorbidity, including with other DGBIs (IBS 30.8% and constipation 26.4%) and with other arguably functional disorders (fibromyalgia 18.8% and CFS 12.9%); second, many patients reported receiving “good information” regarding treatment options from the internet (46%) and various social media platforms, for example, Facebook (34%) (p. 887). For comparison, only 50% of patients reported receiving “good information” from physicians (p. 887).

Studies of online content concerning disorders of gut–brain interaction

Schäfer et al. 46 used a web crawler to extract ~200 000 text posts on 14 French‐language health discussion forums from 2003 to 2018, employing gastrointestinal symptom keywords and a “dictionary” of causal terms. Using a correlated topic model based on latent Dirichlet allocation, they characterized “topics” of discussion. Statistical clustering based on users' symptoms, “topics,” and online behavior identified 16 classes of users. A final analysis identified categories of perceived causal factors.

Topics included diet, stress, consultations with health professionals, treatments, impact on social life, microbiota, gluten, and so forth. A range of misconceptions were observed. A key limitation of this study is that in recent years, much online interaction, particularly among adolescents and young adults, has rapidly shifted to image and video‐based platforms, although textual interactions remain important. Also, the study did not aim to tackle social interaction on the online forums; for example, were some “posters” more influential than others?

Zhong and Liu 49 also used latent Dirichlet allocation topic modeling of text to analyze posts on an online IBS forum from 2010 to 2019 by teenagers with IBS and by parents. They characterized the main topics of discussion among the two groups, exploring differences in parental concerns depending on whether their child was male or female, and exploring the social network structure of forum users. They analyzed interaction data, for example, users' replies or comments, finding that most posts were by a small number of users. The top contributors “functioned like opinion leaders” (p. 11), with more interactions among themselves than with other users. However, the authors did not do the kinds of analyses needed to characterize users' influence on each other; for example, someone who posts relatively little may have a disproportionate influence because of qualitative aspects of how they communicate, how authoritative they seem, or how persuasive they are. This study, like that of Schäfer et al., is best viewed as an early proof‐of‐concept study. The findings, only very partially summarized here, are interesting and deserve follow‐up and integration with findings from other kinds of social research.

Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome was included in the Rome IV nosology, although its “functional” status is unclear (see discussion by Drossman 17 ) (p. 1271). Wightman et al. 47 analyzed ~2700 Reddit posts containing discussion of CHS from five subreddits over a ~4‐year period. Users of Reddit can “upvote” or “downvote” a post; these “votes” were utilized to identify the top‐scoring posts (n = 69), which were then manually examined to identify discussion themes. The five main identified themes were titled “science” (discussion of theories about the causation and mechanisms of CHS), “timing,” “treatment/prevention,” “diagnosis/learning,” and “health impacts.” Theme data were then utilized to train three machine learning models whose performance was compared, and the best chosen for final analysis of the ~2700 posts. Particular attention was paid to the perceived triggers of bouts of CHS and to the range of therapies discussed.

Several interesting substantive findings are reported, most relevant here being the authors' assertion that many topics discussed online by sufferers are largely ignored in CHS medical research and that participation in CHS subreddits is in part compensation for limitations in access to medical care 18 , 50 and for an ongoing lack of medical consensus on the causation of CHS and even its validity as a disorder. They urge CHS researchers to study web‐based patient discourse to inform more probing, comprehensive (especially longitudinal) studies. Due to the lack of demographic data about individuals who posted, the generalizability of this study to all CHS sufferers is unknown.

Jafri et al. 30 noted that “the TikTok hashtag ‘#IBS’ had garnered over 1.2 billion views” (p. 2) and that “TikTok's user demographics aligned with the distribution of IBS, primarily consisting of females aged 18–24 …” (p. 2). From a search on the hashtag #IBS, they selected the first 100 English‐language videos of duration ≥ 2 min that discussed IBS symptoms, diagnosis, management, or treatment (p. 2). These 100 videos had collectively attracted ~285 million views (p. 3). Each was categorized on several dimensions, notably content (“comedy/humor, lifestyle, marketing, health advice”), whether “educational or non‐educational” and whether “factual or non‐factual,” “overall sentiment” (“positive, negative, or neutral”), and the source or entity posting the video (influencer, lay individual, organization, medical professional, etc.).

Influencers were defined as posters with “verified status or >250k following” (p. 3). Also recorded were the number of “views,” “likes,” and “shares.” A formal tool was employed to rate the understandability and actionability of each video.

A key finding was that “[a]lmost half … were posted by influencers and had poor educational content” (p. 1). “HCPs [healthcare providers] had significantly more factual content than non‐HCPs (75% vs. 36.3%, P < 0.001). Educational content created by HCPs was more understandable and actionable, while non‐HCP videos were more popular as measured by views, likes, and shares, though these results were not statistically significant” (p. 4). This study provides valuable preliminary insights regarding IBS, which has very rapidly become a dominant social media platform. The high proportion of poor‐quality or misleading information deserves further examination.

Conclusions

Many clinicians suspect that social media are affecting the presentation of DGBIs generally, particularly in regard to more severely affected DGBI patients. Is this impression correct?

Given what we know about social media and the immense clinical and public health burden of DGBIs, one might expect a commensurate research literature. However, our search yielded only eight empirical studies of variable informativeness and quality, with no studies as yet replicated.

Several observations can be made. First, clearly, this field is in its infancy. Although diverse methods are displayed by these studies, they do not yet avail themselves of the more powerful research designs and methodologies available in the social media research field. For DGBI clinicians seeking edification from research, the literature is forbidding. Even recent studies can be rendered obsolete by rapid changes in the social media world. Social media and the sciences investigating their social and psychological consequences are a “moving target,” evolving extremely rapidly. 6 , 12

Second, five of the eight studies can be characterized as descriptive, using cross‐sectional surveys of DGBI sufferers or characterizing online content and/or users of online platforms. Only three addressed implicit or explicit hypotheses, two via one or other measure of “screen time” or “time spent on social media,” supplemented by various other measures (e.g. number of platforms used).

One articulated a more comprehensive model, asserting a potential causal connection based on stress. 3 , 43 , 44 , 51 The authors argued that social media use can be stressful and that stressed mental states (anxiety, depression, etc.) are implicated in the causation of DGBIs; thus, it is likely that social media use can lead to DGBIs. They tested this via the concept of “smartphone addiction” (implicitly assuming that “smartphone addiction” is inherently stressful), finding significant associations between this variable and the presence of DGBIs.

This is undoubtedly a worthwhile contribution that can be built upon. The stress paradigm is valuable but of limited explanatory value. It needs integration with the wealth of ongoing research in other areas (such as eating disorders, gender dysphoria, and dissociative identity disorder, among others) examining connections between social media and aspects of health, research based on well‐grounded theory of child and adolescent development, and diverse, up‐to‐date social media research strategies. “Screen time” studies have been superseded by more sophisticated research addressing the richness and complexity of young people's engagement with social media. 19 Although the DGBI studies we have reviewed cite this work to some extent, they have not adequately engaged with it. On the other hand, the social media/mental health literature displays the reverse sin of largely neglecting functional disorders and somatization, including DGBIs.

Implications and recommendations

The most urgent implication is for research examining connections between social media use and DGBIs, including more severe DGBI presentations. However, even at the present stage of imperfect knowledge, enough is known to see implications for clinical practice, education and training, and service delivery. The following comments are mainly directed to gastroenterologists but are relevant to all clinical specialties involved in the care of DGBI patients.

The most urgent research task is examining the possible, indeed likely, role of social media in the lives of DGBI sufferers, especially more severely affected DGBI patients, using diverse research strategies, both qualitative and quantitative, cross‐sectional and longitudinal. Social media research is an advanced, specialized field, so DGBI clinicians and researchers need to partner with expert researchers from that field. What online sites and content do sufferers encounter? What use do they make of those sites? How do they evaluate sites and come to trust one site over another? How does this online experience shape their symptoms, self‐management, and interaction with medical care? These are just some research questions that need answering.

In formulating research questions and conducting research, the benefits of social media must be taken into account, namely, their role in providing information, social and emotional support, and facilitating advocacy for services and research. Beneficial and harmful aspects of social media may be complexly intertwined; even sites or influencers disseminating misinformation may be providing much needed social connections for many patients; indeed, this is undoubtedly a key reason they flourish.

Research is also needed to characterize the DGBI social media “information landscape,” assess information quality, and trial interventions to identify and mitigate misinformation. 7 , 26 , 27 , 28 There is an urgent need for the design and evaluation of high‐quality social media content on DGBIs, providing authoritative information and support, and aiming to mitigate the effects of harmful sites and misinformation. Examples already exist, but much more is needed, auspiced, or provided by professional bodies, patient support organizations, public health bodies, or all these collaboratively, possibly collaborating with established online influencers. 1 , 52

We make a special plea for more research attention to the severe, indeed extreme, end of the DGBI spectrum. Although such patients are uncommon, their needs are great, their health‐care utilization is disproportionately large, and their impact on clinicians and wards can be immensely challenging. Indeed, we suggest that a “severe DGBI scenario” can be delineated, a scenario that long predates social media but which we suspect is now being reshaped and intensified by social media. Apart from severity and chronicity, key features include strong evidence of psychological disturbance and abnormal sick‐role behavior; disavowal by the patient of psychological issues; strenuous adherence to plausible but unlikely physical diagnoses and pursuit of physical interventions, including parenteral feeding and surgery; repeated investigations and procedures, often by a succession of medical specialists and services; fraught hospital admissions, stressful for both patient and staff, often with very little benefit or even deterioration and/or iatrogenic harm; and frequently, the presence of comorbid somatic symptoms in other bodily symptoms. Often, an eating disorder is the core problem, although it is frequently unrecognized. 53 , 54 As can be seen, this scenario may be associated with a variety of DGBIs and psychiatric diagnoses, with different combinations in different patients.

Management, already very difficult, is doomed to failure unless the psychological aspects are recognized and competently dealt with.

The epidemiology of DGBI disorders has advanced greatly in recent years, revealing robust interrelationships among symptom severity, number of symptoms and of DGBI diagnoses (“overlapping”), extra‐gastrointestinal somatic symptoms, psychological comorbidity, and impaired quality of life. 55 , 56 , 57 We advocate here a complementary epidemiology of this low‐frequency, high severity, high health‐care impact scenario: its conceptual validity, its prevalence and course, current approaches to and quality of care, its economic costs, its impacts on clinicians and teams, studies of interventions to identify and manage it, and, most pertinent to this article, studies of the role of social media in these patients' lives and clinical presentation.

Patients' social media use is not routinely assessed in the clinical interview. The excellent Rome Foundation review of interventions to improve clinicians' communication skills does not mention this topic. 58 Like all competent interviewing, enquiring about a patient's social media use requires time, respect, empathy, and skill. Relevant skills have to be incorporated into undergraduate and specialist clinical training. Clinicians need frequently updated sources of authoritative information about what patients are engaging with online.

The real and perceived deficiencies in DGBI medical care 18 , 58 , 59 , 60 , 61 provide the context for a heightened role for social media in the lives of sufferers and their carers, who, dissatisfied with medical care or frustrated by limited access, turn to social media for information and support. They are then confronted by a bewildering array of sites and content, some genuinely constructive, supportive, and informative, some disseminating misinformation, and some reinforcing abnormal sick‐role behavior. Discriminating among these can be very difficult.

The present situation makes more urgent the need to reform the predominant “gastroenterologist alone” model of DGBI care, shown to result in poor outcomes and patient dissatisfaction. 62 , 63 , 64 , 65 Despite the immense clinical, public health, and economic burden of DGBIs and recurrent proposals for integrated, multidisciplinary care, it was only very recently that formal trials of such models appeared, 66 , 67 notably the MANTRA Study, which was both effective and cost‐effective. 66 , 68 These limitations in the delivery of DGBI care are even more problematic in the care of more severely affected DGBI patients. The rapid advent of social media has made more painfully apparent longstanding problems in DGBI care, especially the care of severe DGBIs. These problems stem from lack of training; failure to deploy forms of treatment and service models for DGBIs that are known to be effective, notably psychotherapies and integrated, multidisciplinary care; and funding models with perverse incentives that, for example, reward repeated investigations. Our literature review on the topic of social media and DGBIs has found a field in its early infancy, needing urgent development.
