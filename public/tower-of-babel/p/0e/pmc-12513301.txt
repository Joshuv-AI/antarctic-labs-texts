Multimodal imaging of cutaneous solitary plasmacytoma: Line-field confocal optical coherence tomography, in vivo and ex vivo confocal microscopy
Authors: Kenny Vélia, Cyril Habougit, Jean-Luc Perrot
Published: 2025

A 75-year-old patient previously treated by radiotherapy for a left-maxillary sinus plasmacytoma, presented with a red papular lesion, in the left inguinal region, which appeared 3 months prior to the consultation (Fig 1).Fig 1Clinical (macroscopical) aspect of the lesion, presenting as a red nodule in the left inguinal region.

Clinical (macroscopical) aspect of the lesion, presenting as a red nodule in the left inguinal region.

We hypothesized that this lesion could be a Merkel carcinoma, an achromic melanoma, or another plasmacytoma given the patient history.

We first performed line-field confocal optical coherence tomography (LC-OCT) colocalized with dermatoscopy (DeepLive, Damae).

In dermatoscopy, we described a nonpigmented lesion, a pinkish homogenous pattern, with polymorphic atypical blood vessels and yellow globules (Fig 2).Fig 2Dermatoscopic appearance of the lesion: structureless pink background with polymorphous vessels and yellowish globules in the lower right quadrant.

Dermatoscopic appearance of the lesion: structureless pink background with polymorphous vessels and yellowish globules in the lower right quadrant.

LC-OCT revealed a multitude of deeper epidermal microcysts (Fig 3). Thanks to the colocalization, these were identified as the yellow globules that we described in dermatoscopy. We also noticed dermal nest of low-reflectance cells.Fig 3Line-field confocal optical coherence tomography colocalized with dermatoscopy. Yellow stars indicate microcysts; red arrowhead shows dermal nests of low-reflectance cells.

Line-field confocal optical coherence tomography colocalized with dermatoscopy. Yellow stars indicate microcysts; red arrowhead shows dermal nests of low-reflectance cells.

Secondly, we analyzed the same lesion with in vivo confocal microscopy (Vivascope 3000 Caliber) (Fig 4). There was the same aspect as with LC-OCT, but it showed more, regular dendritic cells, which corresponded to Langerhans cells (as confirmed with CD1a marking in immunohistochemistry).Fig 4Ex vivo reflectance confocal microscopy showing dermal lobules of mononuclear ovoid cells.

Ex vivo reflectance confocal microscopy showing dermal lobules of mononuclear ovoid cells.

With such aspect, we could rule out melanoma, but definitive diagnosis could not be obtained. So, we opted for invasive imaging, with ex vivo confocal imaging after biopsy, (Vivascope 2500 Caliber) a quick way to obtain imaging close to histologic analysis.

It showed that the lesion was composed of a dense infiltrate of mononuclear ovoid cell, organized in grouped lobules, and invading the totality of the derma. These cells had a small round nucleus, with a rarefied cytoplasm, and are highly compatible with plasma cells.

This architecture, combined with the cytology, allowed us to rule out a Merkel carcinoma and a melanoma, therefore strengthening our diagnostic hypothesis of extramedullary plasmacytoma.

Histopathologic examination and immunochemistry marking confirmed the diagnostic of plasmacytoma, with positivity of MUM-1, monotypic expression of lambda light chain and negativity of synaptophysin, CK20, CD20, and Pax5 marking.

The microcysts seen in dermatoscopy, LC-OCT and in vivo confocal microscopy, are seen in the ex vivo confocal microscopy, but not within our histologic sample, probably because the biopsy was performed on the outer limit of the lesion. We theorized that these microcysts are simply retention-related structures although we cannot prove it.

To our knowledge, this is the first time a solitary plasmacytoma has been studied with LC-OCT and confocal microscopy (in vivo and ex vivo).

Our case illustrates the power of ex vivo confocal microscopy, allowing the dermatologist to approach quickly and efficiently an anatomopathologic diagnosis.

The rapid diagnosis given by the confocal ex vivo study, allowed us to quickly refer the patient to a hematologist, quickening his access to proper care.
