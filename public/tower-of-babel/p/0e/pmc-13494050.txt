Visualizing excitonic states in constructed cyclic molecular architectures by tip-enhanced photoluminescence
Authors: Shi-Hao Jing, Fan-Fang Kong, Xian-Ke Wang, Yan-Zhong Li, Xiao-Jun Tian, Yang Luo, Gong Chen, Yao Zhang, Zhen-Chao Dong, Yang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural photosynthesis inspires the design of artificial cyclic light-harvesting architectures, yet probing their excitonic behaviors—particularly optically dark states—remains challenging for far-field optics due to diffraction limits and dipole selection rules. Here, combining scanning tunneling microscope manipulation with tip-enhanced photoluminescence, we visualize discrete excitonic states in constructed cyclic zinc-phthalocyanine architectures with sub-nanometer resolution. By controlling intermolecular distances, we map excitonic evolution across weak, intermediate, and strong coupling regimes. We uncover a transition dipole rotation phenomenon arising from the molecules’ orthogonal, degenerate dipoles, which facilitates coherent intermolecular coupling. Furthermore, by comparing hollow and solid architectures, we demonstrate that the molecular architectures determine the nature of the lowest-lying excitonic state: a hollow ring possesses an optically dark lowest-lying state, whereas a solid architecture features a bright one, highlighting a possible structural physical advantage of hollow cyclic architectures for suppressing radiative losses. These findings provide guidelines for designing organic architectures with high energy transfer efficiencies.Subject terms: Light harvesting, Fluorescence spectroscopy, Scanning probe microscopy

Introduction

Driven by the remarkable energy transfer (ET) efficiencies in photosynthesis, researchers have long sought to duplicate the fundamental excitonic features of natural light-harvesting complexes in simplified systems1–4. The discovery of the highly symmetric cyclic structure in the light-harvesting (LH) complex of purple bacteria5,6 provides a blueprint for such designs2,7–10. To mimic these natural architectures, it is essential to employ chromophores that share the optoelectronic properties of natural chlorophylls. In this regard, porphyrin-type molecules have stood out as privileged building blocks of choice, owing to their structural resemblance to chlorophylls, outstanding photostability, rigid planar geometries, and tunable optoelectronic properties. As a result, cyclic porphyrin-type architectures have emerged as a paramount model system for exploring this structure-function relationship2,7–10. Nevertheless, traditional spectroscopic studies mainly rely on far-field optical measurements, which adopt an electromagnetic field that is uniform over the relevant chromophore size of light-harvesting architectures and thus fail to directly access individual chromophore units, not to mention optically dark excitonic states. Therefore, a real-space microscopic picture of excitonic phenomena in these cyclic systems—particularly concerning dipole-dipole coupling and coherence—still remains elusive.

Scanning tunneling microscopy (STM)-based tip-enhanced optical spectroscopy overcomes the limitations of far-field optics11,12. By combining sub-nanometer spectroscopic imaging with the ability to manipulate individual molecules, it offers a unique platform for investigating single-molecule optoelectronics in real space13–20. This technique has also been used to investigate intermolecular energy transfer processes21–26, but so far focuses mainly on linear porphyrin-type molecular architectures, while cyclic porphyrin-type architectures—a crucial model for understanding natural LH structures—remain barely explored.

In this work, we investigate the excitonic states of constructed cyclic zinc phthalocyanine (ZnPc) molecular architectures with sub-molecular resolution via tip-enhanced photoluminescence (TEPL). The highly localized and inhomogeneous plasmonic field of the TEPL enables us to access not only bright excitonic states but also optically dark states in the far field. Our investigation starts with a simple 2 × 2 cyclic tetramer. By precisely controlling intermolecular distances and arrangements, we study in real space the excitonic coupling features across three representative coupling regimes: weak, intermediate, and strong, respectively. We show how the presence of two orthogonal, degenerate transition dipoles in phthalocyanines enables coherent excitonic coupling through dipole rotation. In addition, by further extending to larger eight-membered cyclic architectures, we also explore why hollow cyclic architectures are preferred over solid architectures in terms of suppressing radiative losses, linking this preference to the formation of a lowest-lying excitonic dark state.

Results

Cyclic tetramers with different intermolecular distances

Figure 1a shows a schematic of the TEPL experiments for probing the excitonic properties of constructed cyclic ZnPc architectures such as tetramers. Zinc phthalocyanine molecules are chosen because of their structural similarities to the porphyrin core of chlorophyll molecules in natural light-harvesting complexes and their broad applications in artificial light-harvesting architectures. To preserve their intrinsic excitonic properties, the ZnPc molecules are deposited on a three-monolayer (3 ML) NaCl film, which electronically decouples them from the underlying Ag(100) substrate and suppresses energy dissipation27–29. The coherent coupling and associated excitation energy delocalization among different chromophore units are important for efficient ET processes3,26,30,31. To explore how molecular arrangements affect the excitonic coupling feature, we use STM manipulations to construct three representative cyclic ZnPc tetramers with varying intermolecular distances21,32 and then monitor their spectral evolution using the TEPL technique (Fig. 1b−1d). In the present work, the intermolecular excitonic coupling can be identified through two experimentally observable signatures: the emergence of peak splitting relative to the monomer emission that indicates the formation of coupled excitonic eigenstates, and the occurrence of state-specific photon-imaging patterns extending over multiple monomers that reflect the underlying transition dipole configurations21,25.

When each ZnPc unit in a tetramer is well separated from each other, as exemplified in Fig. 1b for a tetramer with a center-to-center intermolecular distance (d) of ~3.2 nm, the TEPL spectrum from the ZnPc unit shows a dominant spectral peak at ~653 nm with a linewidth of ~10 meV. Both the peak energy and linewidth are almost the same as those obtained from an isolated ZnPc molecule (detailed in Supplementary Note 1). Furthermore, the corresponding TEPL image shows four similar patterns (detailed in Supplementary Note 2), each replicating the characteristic four-lobe patterns of an isolated ZnPc molecule in TEPL measurements15. The spectral and imaging signatures suggest that the cyclic tetramer with d = 3.2 nm is in the weak coupling regime and each ZnPc unit in the tetramer acts as an independent individual unit (i.e., the excited state of each unit decays independently from its neighbors).

Excitonic features of cyclic ZnPc tetramer in the strong coupling regime

Interestingly, as the intermolecular distance decreases, the TEPL spectra acquired over the ZnPc units (Fig. 1c, 1d) evolve from a single peak to a complex multi-peak structure. This signifies the emergence of new, collective excitonic states arising from the coherent coupling of individual ZnPc excited states21,23,25,26. According to our previous study21, the closest center-to-center intermolecular distances d that we can achieve on the NaCl surface is about 1.4 nm and a ZnPc dimer with d = 1.4 nm can exhibit pronounced coherent dipole−dipole coupling with well-defined peak splitting. Thus, we begin by analyzing the excitonic features of the cyclic tetramer with d = 1.4 nm.

The TEPL spectra of this cyclic tetramer configuration exhibit many excitonic and vibronic emission peaks (illustrated in Fig. 2a), far more complex than those of a simple linear dimer21. Theoretical calculations (detailed in Supplementary Note 3) predict six excitonic eigenstates for this architecture (illustrated in Fig. 2b, top panel). It has been known that the spectral profile of vibronic emissions, if plotted with respect to the 0–0 band origin, resembles that of the Raman spectra33–35, since both reflect the energies of vibrational modes of the molecule. Therefore, we can identify the band origin for the lowest-lying excitonic peak through this comparison of spectral matching (detailed in Supplementary Note 3), unambiguously assigning the peak at ~1.879 eV (~660 nm) as the lowest-lying excitonic state (labeled as State 1). The remaining five excitonic states (labeled as States 2−6) are then assigned sequentially to the higher-energy peaks at ~1.885 eV, ~1.892 eV, ~1.905 eV, ~1.912 eV, and ~1.919 eV, respectively. Note that the energy spacing between States 1 and 6 for the cyclic tetramer with d = 1.4 nm is about 40 meV, which can reflect the strength of coherent coupling.

With the energy levels assigned, we further resolve the spatial feature of each excitonic state as well as corresponding dipole coupling configuration using TEPL imaging in combination with theoretical simulations (Fig. 2b). The nice agreement between the experimental images and simulated patterns substantiates our assignments. Because the plasmonic field is highly localized, the relative intensities of excitonic emission peaks can vary with the tip position, which helps to resolve the corresponding dipole coupling configurations. Based on the dipole coupling configurations among ZnPc units in the cyclic tetramer, the excitonic states can be classified into optically dark states (i.e., States 1 and 6 with Ag symmetry as well as States 2 and 5 with Bg symmetry, with no net dipole) and optically bright states (i.e., States 3 and 4 with Eu symmetry, with a net dipole). Interestingly, we observe no significant difference in emission intensity between these bright and dark states. This observation demonstrates a key merit of near-field TEPL measurements: the presence of the plasmonic tip and associated highly inhomogeneous plasmonic field breaks the far-field dipole selection rules, allowing for the direct visualization of optically forbidden states21,36. (Note that there are some deviations between the simulated pattern and TEPL image for State 3, which probably originated from the pronounced internal conversion from State 4 to State 3 due to the vibronic resonance effect37, as detailed in Supplementary Note 3).

Remarkably, when analyzing the real-space imaging patterns in detail, the transition dipole of each constituent unit within the coupled architecture is found to be substantially reoriented. Such a finding is illustrated by taking the case of the excitonic state 1 as an example (Fig. 3). For an isolated ZnPc molecule, the two degenerate transition dipoles align with the orthogonal molecular axes (Fig. 3a), resulting in a characteristic four-lobed emission pattern (Fig. 3b)15. However, a simulation of the tetramer assuming these dipoles remain fixed (Fig. 3c) yields a pattern (Fig. 3d) that evidently disagrees with the experimental image (Fig. 2b), which indicates that such a hypothetical fixed dipole configuration is not the eigensolution of the tetramer Hamiltonian. In contrast, a simulation, which allows the dipoles to reorient to take into account the Coulomb interactions among all the orthogonal dipole components (Fig. 3e, 3f, also see Supplementary Note 3), nicely reproduces the experimental features. In other words, the excitonic coupling is accompanied by a rotation of the transition dipole orientations relative to their original directions, reflecting the change of eigensolutions of the tetramer Hamiltonian.

This pronounced dipole rotation is rooted in the electronic structure of the ZnPc monomer, which possesses two orthogonal and degenerate transition dipoles15, conventionally labeled as Qx and Qy. When these monomers form a coupled architecture, the new excitonic states are no longer composed of pure Qx or Qy states of the ZnPc units, but are rather superpositions of them. In other words, the effective transition dipole of each ZnPc unit in a given excitonic state is a specific linear combination of its original Qx and Qy basis dipoles: μi=cixμx+ciyμy, where μx and μy are the basis transition dipoles, and cix and ciy are the superposition coefficients for that specific eigenstate (detailed in the Supplementary Note 3). The dipole rotation directly reflects the mixing of the Qx and Qy basis states, which is determined by the eigenstates of the tetramer Hamiltonian.

Excitonic features of a cyclic ZnPc tetramer in the intermediate coupling regime

With the increase in intermolecular distances d (e.g., a cyclic tetramer with d = 2.0 nm), the coupling enters the intermediate regime where the energy delocalization between the chromophore units becomes somewhat comparable with the dissipation at each unit (as illustrated in Supplementary Note 3). Unlike the spectral feature in either the monomer-like emission in the weak coupling case (d = 3.2 nm) or the well-resolved excitonic manifold in the strong coupling case (d = 1.4 nm), the site-dependent TEPL spectra here (Fig. 4a) display broad, overlapping peaks. The presence of multiple peaks across the architecture and their site dependency indicate the occurrence of a coherent coupling behavior. However, it is very difficult to clearly identify all six excitonic peaks in this intermediate regime due to their significant broadening and resultant overlapping of excitonic states, pointing to weaker excitonic coupling strengths. Note that the term intermediate regime is used only as a phenomenological description of the observed spectral evolution for convenience, rather than as a strictly defined regime.

The congestion of excitonic peaks makes it particularly challenging to resolve the energies of the excitonic states. To tackle this complexity, the assignment of excitonic states is based on the comprehensive consideration of the agreement between experiment and theory regarding the energy of excitonic states and the spatial emission patterns, together with the spectral matching of vibronic features (also detailed in Supplementary Note 3). Specifically, the excitonic state 6’ is predicted to be the highest-lying excitonic state and thus shows the strongest emission in the tetramer’s center, as expected for its dipole arrangement (Fig. 4b). This allows us to assign the peak at ~1.911 eV in the turquoise curve (Fig. 4a) to State 6’. In addition, similar to the spectral matching comparison used for the tetramer with d = 1.4 nm, we can identify the peak at ~1.896 eV as the lowest-lying excitonic state (namely, State 1’) through the comparison of the vibronic feature with the Raman spectrum. (Note that such a spectral analysis also supports our assignment of State 6’, as illustrated in Suppl. Fig. 9 in Supplementary Note 3). After identifying the energies of both the lowest-lying and highest-lying excitonic states (States 1’ and 6’), we can scale the calculated energies for these two excitonic states, and then the remaining four excitonic peaks can be assigned by referring to the calculated energies rescaled for these states. Namely, the positions of the other four excitonic states (States 2’–5’) are at about 1.898 eV, 1.900 eV, 1.904 eV, and 1.906 eV, respectively (indicated by the dashed lines in Fig. 4a, detailed in Supplementary Note 3). Note that the energy spacing between State 1’ and State 6’ here is about 15 meV, which is much smaller than that between State 1 and State 6 for the cyclic tetramer with d = 1.4 nm.

While the simulated patterns based on the dipole coupling configurations of the calculated excitonic states capture the general distribution symmetry of the TEPL images, they fail to fully reproduce the detailed features (comparing second and third rows in Fig. 4b). Specifically, the experimental images for the low-lying excitonic states (States 1’−3’) appear blurred and all exhibit strong emission intensities around the periphery areas of the tetramer. We attribute this discrepancy to excitonic state mixing. In this intermediate coupling regime, the energy spacing between adjacent excitonic states becomes comparable to or smaller than their spectral linewidths (Fig. 4a), leading to the mixing effect among the contributions from adjacent excitonic states. As detailed in Supplementary Note 3, the simulated patterns that take into account the state-mixing effect (bottom row of Fig. 4b) can well reproduce the experimental observations, suggesting that such an effect plays a key role in governing the real-space distribution of excitonic states in the intermediate coupling regime.

We would like to point out that there is also a dipole rotation for the intermediate case of the tetramer with d = 2.0 nm shown in Fig. 4b, evidently ~45 degrees for States 1’, 2’, 5’, 6’ and slightly ~9 degrees for States 3’ and 4’, with respect to the Qx/Qy orientation of the isolated monomer. The dipole rotation phenomenon always shows up as long as coherent excitonic coupling takes place, regardless of whether the molecules are rotated with respect to the vector connecting neighboring molecules. In other words, the dipole rotation is a direct manifestation of the mixing between Qx and Qy basis states, which is an intrinsic excitonic property of the coupled molecular system and is associated with the symmetry and strength of the intermolecular coupling.

Relation between lowest-lying excitonic dark state and hollow cyclic architecture

Dark excitonic states are critical for efficient energy transfer processes, as their inability to decay radiatively allows them to act as energy reservoirs38,39. In natural light-harvesting complexes of purple bacteria, the lowest-lying excitonic state is often an optically dark state in the far field, a feature thought to be essential for reducing radiative loss and improving transfer efficiency. Having demonstrated our ability to resolve the full manifold of bright and dark states in cyclic tetramers, we are now uniquely positioned to directly investigate one of the central questions in photosynthesis: how does a complex’s molecular architecture determine the nature of its lowest-lying excitonic state?

To address this question, we construct and compare two distinct larger architectures: a hollow “3 × 3–1” octamer and a solid “3 × 3” nonamer with a molecule occupying the center, as shown in the inset STM images of Figs. 5a and 5b, respectively. The TEPL spectra acquired from similar positions (marked by red and blue “×” in the inset STM images) of these two architectures are evidently different, reflecting differences in their excitonic states and associated dipole coupling schemes. But because of the involvement of many more molecules in the architectures, the spectral analyses of their excitonic states (12 for the octamer and 13 for the nonamer) are much more complicated than those for the cyclic tetramers. Nevertheless, based on relatively well-defined spectral features of several low-lying states and their photon imaging patterns, as well as by combining with theoretical calculations, we can assign the energies of these excitonic states and determine their associated dipole coupling configurations (see details in Supplementary Note 3). Of particular interest are the lowest-lying excitonic states shown in Figs. 5c and 5d. For the hollow octamer, the lowest-lying excitonic state (State 1) is an optically dark state with Ag symmetry (Fig. 5c). In contrast, for the solid nonamer, the lowest-lying state (State 1’) is an optically bright state with Eu symmetry (Fig. 5d). This direct observation demonstrates that the architecture dictates the quantum nature of the lowest-lying excitonic state in terms of its optical darkness and brightness. By possessing a dark lowest-lying excitonic state that is protected from radiative loss, the hollow architecture is intrinsically better suited for efficient energy storage and transfer. We note that the photon imaging patterns for the lowest-lying excitonic states, as shown for the cyclic (“3 × 3–1” hollow octamer) and filled (“3 × 3” solid nonamer) structures in Fig. 5c and 5d respectively, are evidently different between these two architectures. In this sense, it is possible to make the crucial distinction between cyclic and filled structures on the basis of the experimental data. Nevertheless, theoretical calculations can help us to gain deep insights into the microscopic mechanism of such evident differences in both the exact dipole-coupling configurations and resultant photon imaging patterns. Our findings shed light on one of the possible factors that may have led to the preference for hollow cyclic structures over solid structures in some natural photosynthetic systems.

In summary, by combining single‑molecule manipulation with sub‑nanometer‑resolved TEPL spectroscopy, we have visualized the excitonic landscape of artificially constructed cyclic molecular architectures with different intermolecular separations. Our results indicate that (1) the presence of two orthogonal, degenerate transition dipoles in porphyrin-type ZnPc molecules facilitates intermolecular coherent coupling via dipole rotation, which is helpful for efficient energy transfer among cyclic architectures; and (2) a hollow cyclic architecture is intrinsically favorable for energy storage and transfer because its lowest‑lying excitonic state is optically dark in the far field and capable of reducing radiative loss. These findings provide insights into the future design of artificial light-harvesting nanostructures with optimized efficiencies.

Methods

Descriptions of experimental details

All experiments were conducted using a custom-built low-temperature (LT) ultrahigh-vacuum (UHV) scanning tunneling microscope (STM) (Unisoku) integrated with the optical detection system. The system was operated at ~7 K with a base pressure below 1×10−10 Torr. The Ag(100) substrate (MaTeck) was cleaned by repeated cycles of argon-ion sputtering and annealing. A three-monolayer (3 ML) NaCl film was then grown on the Ag(100) substrate via thermal evaporation to serve as a decoupling layer. Subsequently, ZnPc molecules (Tokyo Chemical Industry, 95%) were thermally evaporated onto the 3 ML NaCl/Ag(100) substrate, with the substrate held at ~7 K. The electrochemically etched Ag tips, used for STM and TEPL measurements, were cleaned via electron bombardment and argon-ion sputtering, followed by controlled gentle tip indentations into the Ag(100) substrate to create an atomistic protrusion at the tip apex15,40.

For optical measurements (TEPL/TERS), the STM junction was illuminated with a linearly polarized 532 nm continuous-wave single-longitudinal-mode laser (Cobolt 08-DPL 532 nm), following a setup similar to our previous work15,40,41. The photoluminescence (PL) signal from the junction was collected, filtered by a 532 nm long-pass edge filter (Semrock, LP03-532RE-25) to remove scattered laser light, and then fiber-coupled to a spectrometer equipped with a liquid-nitrogen-cooled charge-coupled device (CCD). All spectra were acquired using the spectral setting of a 100 µm slit size and a 600 grooves/mm grating, providing an energy resolution of ~2 meV.

TEPL spectroscopic and imaging measurements were conducted using a 532 nm laser at a power of 0.2 mW. Imaging measurements were performed in constant-height mode. The tip height was set as follows: the tip was first positioned on the 3 ML NaCl surface with a setpoint of –1.0 V and 2 pA, then the feedback is switched off, accompanied by a tip retraction of 70 pm before scanning over the molecular area. This height setting can provide a balance between luminescence signal intensity and molecular stability during TEPL mapping experiments while ensuring stable control over the desired tip height (detailed below). Spectroscopic images of different excitonic states in main-text figures were generated by integrating the signal over specific energy windows corresponding to each excitonic state. The integration windows (in eV) used were listed as follows.

For the TEPL images shown in Fig. 2 for the tetramer with d = 1.4 nm: State 1, 1.8775–1.8805 eV; State 2, 1.8835–1.8865 eV; State 3, 1.8905–1.8935 eV; State 4, 1.9030–1.9060 eV; State 5, 1.9107–1.9137 eV; State 6, 1.9175–1.9205 eV.

For the TEPL images shown in Fig. 4 for the tetramer with d = 2.0 nm: State 1’, 1.8953–1.8967 eV; State 2’, 1.8973–1.8987 eV; State 3’, 1.8993–1.9007 eV; State 4’, 1.9033–1.9047 eV; State 5’, 1.9057–1.9067 eV; State 6’, 1.9103–1.9117 eV.

For the TEPL images shown in Fig. 5 for the hollow “3 × 3–1” octamer: State 1, 1.8742–1.8772 eV; State 2, 1.8770–1.8800 eV; State 3, 1.8793–1.8823 eV.

For the TEPL images shown in Fig. 5 for the solid “3 × 3” nonamer: State 1’, 1.8708–1.8738 eV; State 2’, 1.8739–1.8769 eV; State 3’, 1.8793–1.8823 eV.

To minimize the influence caused by the thermal drift and piezo creep during the long-time measurements for spectral mapping, three strategies are used in our experiments: (1) We scan over the target molecular architectures for about one hour to reduce thermal drift and piezo creep; (2) We use the piezo calibration module of the controller to further compensate the thermal drift; (3) We implement a periodic height recalibration process to tackle the drift issue in the vertical direction. During the data acquisition (approximately every 3 minutes), when the tip is positioned over the bare NaCl substrate (a chemically uniform reference region), we instantly re-engage the feedback loop to reset the absolute vertical position to the initial setpoint, and then switch off the feedback to continue the mapping experiment.

Descriptions of theoretical simulations

Theoretical calculations were performed to investigate the excitonic states of different cyclic architectures and simulate their corresponding imaging patterns. To reveal the intermolecular geometry, the STM images were acquired at –1 V, a scanning voltage that can highlight the molecular skeleton structures26. The relative orientations and intermolecular distances can then be obtained and used for theoretical calculations. Since the intermolecular distance in our system is comparable to the size of ZnPc molecules, point dipole approximation becomes inadequate15,42. Therefore, a more precise distributed atomic-dipole model is employed in this work, which projects the transition density to each individual atom: First, the geometry of a single ZnPc molecule was optimized, and its electronic transitions were computed via time-dependent density functional theory (TDDFT) at the ωB97XD/6-311 G(d,p) level using Gaussian 16 RevC.0143. Subsequently, the spatial distribution of the transition density for each excited state was represented as a set of atomic dipoles, derived using transition current theory44,45 in conjunction with Hirshfeld population analysis46,47.

Using this distributed atomic-dipole representation, the interaction matrix element Hijαβ between the α transition of molecule i and the β transition of molecule j (where α and β denote the Qx or Qy transitions) was obtained by summing the dipole-dipole interactions over all atom pairs between the two molecules.

where μi,m (μj,n) and ri,m (rj,n) represents the projected transition dipole moment and position of the m-th (n-th) atom of the ith (jth) ZnPc molecule, the superscripts α(β)=xory represents the two degenerated Qx and Qy transition dipoles in a single ZnPc, respectively. In this way, each element of the full interaction Hamiltonian matrix (HI) can be calculated. Diagonalization of this Hamiltonian matrix provides the eigenvalues (excitonic energy levels) and eigenvectors (the composition of the excitonic states).

The TEPL imaging patterns were simulated using a well-established theoretical framework15. For a given tip position R, the TEPL intensity ITEPL(R) is proportional to the product of the local excitation rate γexc(R) and the quantum efficiency η(R). Because the spatial variation of η(R) is primarily determined by the radiative emission rate γem(R)15,23,48, the spatial features of the TEPL pattern can be approximated by the product of γexc(R) and γem(R), in line with previously adopted approaches23,48. Both the excitation and emission rates are governed by the near-field coupling between the molecular transition dipole μ and the highly localized plasmonic field E(r∣R). Consequently, rather than calculating absolute emission intensities, the spatial distribution of the TEPL pattern is approximately simulated by evaluating this near-field coupling at each tip position. The local plasmonic field E(r∣R) was computed via classical electromagnetic simulations using COMSOL Multiphysics 6.1. Following our previous work49, the Ag tip was modeled as a truncated cone with a 30 nm shaft radius, terminating in an atomistic protrusion of 0.4 nm radius at its apex. The simulation was performed by using a plane-wave excitation with an incident photon energy of 2.33 eV (532 nm), together with the tip−substrate distance set to 1.4 nm.
