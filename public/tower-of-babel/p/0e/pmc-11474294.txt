Routine hemostasis profile in steady state sickle cell disease adult patients compared to laboratory values, between phenotypes and during pregnancy: A retrospective, descriptive study
Authors: Comlan Affo, Carine Schmidt, Antoine Bosquet, Bénédicte Dumont, Isabelle Mahé
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSickle cell disease is a rare genetic disease resulting from an abnormality in hemoglobin. Hemostasis in the steady state, defined as ≥2 months without vaso‐occlusive crises, is poorly described in the literature. We report the routine hemostasis profile in steady state patients with sickle cell disease (SCD), including during pregnancy and according to phenotype. This retrospective study collected data over the period 2010 to 2021. Data on routine hemostasis parameters (prothrombin time [PT] activated partial thromboplastin time [aPTT], and platelets) were collected from medical records and were compared with laboratory norms including during pregnancy; the HbSS phenotype was compared with the HbSC, HbSB°thalassemia, and HbSB+thalassemia phenotypes. we included 119 adults (representing 190 day‐hospitals) with SCD who had attended at least one checkup in the steady state. Seven patients (15 day‐hospitals) on anticoagulants were excluded. Eleven (17 day‐hospitals) were pregnant. Mean routine hemostasis parameters were within normal values regardless of pregnancy. Mean PT was lower during pregnancy (12.3 ± 0.6 s vs. 13.2 ± 1.0 s; P = .01). PT and platelet counts were higher (P = .01) and aPTT was lower (P = .03) in men and nonpregnant women in the HbSS group compared with those in the HbSC group. routinely collected hemostasis parameters in steady state patients were within normal laboratory values, including in pregnant women. PT values differed significantly between pregnant women and nonpregnant women, and PT, aPTT, and platelet counts differed between HbSS, HbSC, and HbSB+thalassemia phenotypes.Keywords: hemostasis parameters, phenotypes, pregnancy, sickle cell disease, steady state

INTRODUCTION

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is caused by an autosomal recessive hemoglobinopathy. The most common forms are Hb SS, Hb SBeta+ Thalassemia, Hb SBeta Thalassemia, and Hb SC.

The pathophysiology of the clinical manifestations is based on inflammatory phenomena, endothelial dysfunction, and coagulation changes. Patients with the disease are at increased risk of thromboembolism and in situ thrombi [1, 2]. Experimental data in animals [3] and exploratory data in humans have identified an increased expression of tissue factor and a reduction in natural anticoagulants [4, 5], including proteins S and C [6, 7] leading to a prothrombotic condition in the steady state. These phenomena would be largely at the origin of the clinical manifestations of the disease [8, 9]. Conversely, the inflammatory condition during acute complications increases this prothrombotic state.

Hemostasis is a physiological phenomenon that contributes to the prevention and stopping of bleeding. The coagulation profile of patients with SCD in the steady state is poorly described. A steady state in SCD is a state of the patient without vaso‐occlusive crises (VOC) [10], for at least 2 months. It defines the baseline condition of the patient.

SCD is rare (1 person/2000 inhabitants [10]) and only a few small studies of coagulation parameters have been published, the findings of which can be regarded as hypothesis‐generating (Table 1 [6, 7, 11–18]). Wright et al. [13] reported that coagulation‐factor levels were lower in steady state patients than in subjects with normal hemoglobin (AA). In contrast, Ajuwon et al. [16] reported a state of hypercoagulability in steady state patients with hemoglobin SS (HbSS) or SC (HbSC) disease versus AA subjects, with higher platelet and fibrinogen levels and a shorter activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) but an identical prothrombin time (PT). This lack of concordance and limited evidence highlight the need to carry out large prospective studies to evaluate common coagulation factors in SCD. Understanding physiological changes is necessary to improve patient care, first to identify the threshold at which the diagnosis of thromboembolic pathologies can be excluded, and then to determine the optimal anticoagulant management in the case of a thromboembolic event.

The primary endpoint of the study is to describe the hemostasis parameters (PT, APTT, and platelets count) in a steady state in patients with SCD. The secondary endpoints are to describe the values of PT, APTT, and platelet count according to the different phenotypes and during pregnancy in a steady state in patients with SCD.

This retrospective study of routine hemostasis values in steady state patients with SCD will provide the basis for a larger prospective study, extended to thrombophilia parameters.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This single‐center, retrospective, descriptive study collected data covering an 11‐year period (January 2010 to February 2021) in steady state sickle cell patients who took a blood test at Louis Mourier Hospital (AP‐HP, Paris Cité University). The data were extracted from archived medical files, day‐hospitalization reports, and biology software from ORBIS (computer software used for managing medical reports at the AP‐HP: Assistance Publique – Hôpitaux de Paris). The data included demographics, temperature, prevention treatment of VOC, and hemostasis parameters (PT, aPTT, and platelet count).

Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years) with HbSS disease, HbSB thalassemia, HbSB+thalassemia, HbSC, or HbSE disease who attended at least one checkup during the study period. Patients on anticoagulant therapy were excluded.

Blood samples were taken on an ethylene diamine tetra‐acetic acid tube (full blood count) and on a 0.109 M citrate tube (PT, aPTT) with preanalytical treatment in accordance with French Society of Hematology recommendations. Platelets and white blood cells were measured on Sysmex analyzers (XE2100, XN1000). Coagulation tests (PT and aPTT) were carried out on ACL TOP750 and 550 systems (IL/Werfen, Bedford, MA, USA) by chronometric assay on an optical analyzer. The results of both tests were measured in seconds. PT is expressed as a percentage compared with the control time defined for each reagent batch (normal value ranges from 80% to 120%) and aPTT as a ratio (patient/control aPTT, normal value <1.20 in adults). Normal platelet counts for the laboratory range from 150 to 400 × 109/L (150–400 g/L).

Statistical analysis

The results are expressed as mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR). Student's t‐test was used to test the significance of the differences between group means. P < 0.5 was considered significant. The data were compared with the normal values of the hemostasis laboratory of the Louis Mourier Hospital.

RESULTS

Between January 2010 and February 2021, 119 patients attended ≥1 checkup for sickle cell disease in 190 day‐hospitals. Seven patients treated with anticoagulants during the checkup (four pregnant and one patient with protein S and protein C deficiency) representing 15 day‐hospitals were excluded from the analysis. The study population therefore comprised 112 patients (175 day‐hospitals), comprising 87 nonpregnant women, 71 men (158 day‐hospitals), and 11 pregnant women (17 day‐hospitals; Figure 1).

The mean platelet count was 353.9 ± 147.5 g/L (Table 3). Only patients with the HbSB°thalassemia phenotype had a mean value above the normal range (410.6 ± 286.0). The mean platelet count in the HbSS phenotype (385.5 ± 124.7) was higher than in the HbSC (P < .01) and HbSB+thalassemia (P < .01) phenotypes. Pregnant women

Baseline demographic and hematological characteristics of pregnant women are shown in Table 2. The median age was 26 years (IQR 21–33) and mean hemoglobin value was 9.2 ± 1.3 g/dL. Over half (58.8%) had HbSS, 35.3% had HbSC, and 5.9% had HbSB+thalassemia. Ten women (62.5% of 16 with available data) had ≥1 VOC in the year preceding the checkup. Mean PT, aPTT, and platelet values were within normal ranges.

Comparison of nonpregnant women and pregnant women

All hemostasis parameters were within normal ranges in both men and nonpregnant women, nonpregnant women, and pregnant women (Tables 5 and 6). Mean PT in men and nonpregnant women was higher than in pregnant women (13.2 ± 1.0 s vs. 12.3 ± 0.6 s; P = .01). Mean PT in nonpregnant women was higher than in pregnant women (13.3 ± 1.0 s vs. 12.3 ± 0.6 s; P = .01). There was no significant difference between mean platelet counts and aPTT values.

DISCUSSION

We report coagulation parameters in the largest reported series since the series of Garrido et al. in 2012 (Table 1) of steady state adults with sickle cell disease, including 17 pregnant women, and compare our results across different phenotypes with those in the literature. We found that routinely collected hemostasis parameters in steady state patients were within normal laboratory values, including in pregnant women. PT values were significantly different in pregnant women compared with nonpregnant women. We also found a difference in PT, aPTT, and platelet counts between HbSS, HbSC, and HbSB+thalassemia phenotypes.

Studies in patients with sickle cell disease are small except the series of Garrido, and heterogeneous, involving different populations, comparator groups, and hemostasis parameters (Table 1). We used normal laboratory values as the comparator in our study, whereas previous studies made comparisons to patients with normal hemoglobin [11‐13, 15‐19]. The studies by Bayazit et al. [12], Chinawa et al. [15], and Awoda et al. [18] were restricted to children. Our population had a similar median age to adults in the studies by Buseri et al. [13] and Ajuwon et al. [16], but were younger than those in the studies by Colella et al. [17] and Garrido et al. [14].

Our findings for platelet counts are similar to those reported by Ajuwon et al. [16] and Colella et al. [17] (in Ghana and Brazil, respectively), with a significant difference between HbSS and HbSC phenotypes (445.7 ± 128.3 vs. 330.0 ± 97.7 g/L, respectively). Ajuwon et al. [16] reported a higher aPTT in HbSS versus HbSC patients, whereas aPTT was lower in HbSS patients in the present study, and the authors did not find any difference in PT between HbSS and HbSC patients, which contrasts with our findings.

Inflammation parameters were not available in all our patients. These elements could influence hemostasis parameters. The absence of fever in all our patients is not enough to compensate for this lack of data.

Sickle cell disease is a proinflammatory pathology leading to the modification of various biological parameters, including coagulation factors. These changes are more important during acute complications such as VOC or acute chest syndromes because of the increased inflammatory state. The description of steady state values is fundamental for the correct interpretation of the variations observed during acute complications. Thus, patients are at increased risk of venous thromboembolic events [20], and suggestive signs such as chest pain and dyspnea are common in acute complications. The suspicion of pulmonary embolism is therefore frequent in patients with sickle cell disease, and the dosage of d‐dimers is vital in ruling out the diagnosis in the case of a low or moderate probability. In the case of a positive diagnosis, it is uncertain how to monitor anticoagulant treatment. The published literature has studied isolated hemostasis parameters in a steady state. Chinawa et al.’s [15] series (in Nigeria) is the only one to assess these parameters in the steady state and during an acute complication in children and found a significant difference between aPTT values in HbSS children in a steady state and those in crisis. We did not find any similar studies involving adults in the literature.

Our study is a state‐of‐play of routine hemostasis values in steady state sickle‐cell patients. A prospective study will be undertaken to collect common hemostasis parameters (which are more extensive than routine parameters) and thrombophilia parameters. The intraindividual stability of the parameters will be explored by describing these parameters in the same patient over time in the steady state and during periods of acute complications. Knowledge of these parameters will help define pathological thresholds in sickle‐cell disease, such as d‐dimers in thromboembolic events.

Our study has several strengths and limitations. Despite its small size, our sample is one of the largest in the literature and the first to include pregnant women with sickle cell disease. The fact that we included several day‐hospitals for the same patient may allow us to more accurately measure the mean values during the steady state, even though most patients attended only 1 day‐hospital in this study. This hypothesis needs to be confirmed in a prospective study involving several checkups to make it possible to calculate a mean value for each parameter. This study is retrospective, with missing data, and we were unable to collect the different factors that could have influenced hemostasis parameters in all patients. Some parameters were available for only a few patients, making it difficult to analyze them. The results from this single‐center study require confirmation in other centers. Our patients were not compared with an AA group but with normal laboratory values. This strategy was intentionally chosen because a comparable non‐sickle cell control group is impossible to constitute.

In summary, routine hemostasis parameters in steady state patients with sickle cell disease were within normal laboratory values, including during pregnancy. PT values were significantly different in pregnant and nonpregnant groups. We also found a difference in PT, aPTT, and platelet counts between HbSS, HbSC, and HbSB+thalassemia phenotypes. These results require confirmation in larger multicenter prospective studies.
