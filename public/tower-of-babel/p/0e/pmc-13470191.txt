Comparative Efficacy of Herbal Products for Improving Liver Enzymes and Inflammation in Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-Analysis
Authors: Alejandro Chen Liang, Yeison Cruz Castillo, Mariela Denise Fermin Madera, Revathi Thirumushi Sairaj, Jose Medina, Tatiana Alexandra Moreno Díaz, Supritha Chintamaneni, Gabriela Flores Monar, Camila Sanchez Cruz, Rashmi Advani, Sushovan Guha, Ernesto Calderón-Martinez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsMetabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) affect adults worldwide and are causes of morbidity. While lifestyle modification remains in first-line therapy, interest has emerged in herbal agents with potential hepatometabolic effects. Evidence synthesis has been limited by heterogeneity in formulations and study quality. We conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) to compare the efficacy of herbal therapies in MASLD/MASH.MethodsFollowing Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews registration (CRD420251157401), PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, and Cochrane were searched through September 2025 for randomized controlled trials evaluating herbal therapies or derivatives in adults with MASLD/MASH. The primary outcomes included alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase, and liver stiffness. Random-effects NMAs estimated mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals, with treatment ranking using surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA). Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2.0, and certainty of evidence using Confidence in NMA.ResultsAcross 131 randomized trials (n = 9191), several herbal interventions significantly improved liver enzymes. In the NMA for alanine aminotransferase, the greatest reductions were seen with artichoke leaf (MD -28.32; P < .01), cinnamon (MD -25.10; P = .01), and piperine (MD -25.39; P = .01), with artichoke leaf achieving the highest SUCRA (0.93). For aspartate aminotransferase, significant reductions were observed for cinnamon (MD -23.50; P < .01), berberine (MD -19.63; P < .01), and bergamot + CyC (MD -17.83; P < .01), with cinnamon being the most effective (SUCRA 0.96). For gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase, significant reductions were observed with cinnamon (MD -12.44; P = .01), garlic + silymarin (MD -10.92; P = .01), and berberine (MD -9.74; P = .01). For liver stiffness, only curcumin (MD -0.76; P = .03) and pomegranate (MD -0.86; P = .04) were statistically significant, despite higher SUCRA rankings favoring silymarin (0.81) and hesperidin + flaxseed (0.79). The secondary outcomes showed variable effects on steatosis and fibrosis indices without consistent metabolic significance.ConclusionThis analysis found that several herbal preparations may improve biochemical markers in MASLD and MASH, though results varied and require cautious interpretation given formulation and regulatory inconsistencies. Future work should focus on the most consistently performing agents: cinnamon, curcumin, silymarin, berberine, artichoke leaf, and Nigella sativa. This should involve standardized production, dose-finding studies, purity verification, and mainly long-term safety evaluation before any clinical integration.Keywords: MASLD, MASH, Herbal Medicine, Liver Enzymes

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), previously termed nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), encompasses a spectrum of disorders characterized by excessive hepatic fat accumulation and is closely linked to obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome.1 Its progressive subtype, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), or metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), is defined by steatosis, hepatocellular injury, and inflammation and affects approximately 5.27% of the global population, with MASLD overall affecting 30.05%.1 Liver biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard, with hepatocellular ballooning serving as a hallmark histological feature.1,2 The clinical burden is substantial, particularly among patients with MASH and clinically significant fibrosis, since clinically significant fibrosis is a strong predictor of liver-related complications and the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma increases with the development of fibrosis and cirrhosis.3 The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases and American Diabetes Association guidelines emphasize the use of non-invasive tools, such as the fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index, for initial fibrosis risk stratification in individuals with metabolic risk factors.4,5 Although pharmacologic options, including thyroid hormone receptor-β agonists and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, have expanded therapeutic options for selected patients, lifestyle modification, particularly weight reduction, dietary optimization, and physical activity, remains the cornerstone of management across the MASLD-MASH spectrum.3,5, 6, 7

Alongside pharmacological development, interest has grown in the use of herbal and plant-derived compounds in MASLD and MASH. Despite skepticism related to variable formulations, dosing, and product quality, several agents have investigated for hepatoprotective, metabolic, and anti-inflammatory properties.8 Evidence suggests that some of them, such as silymarin, may exert antifibrotic or fibrosis-modifying effects, while curcumin and turmeric improve insulin resistance and reduce liver enzyme levels.8,9 Garlic supplementation has been linked with favorable changes in aminotransferases, steatosis, and lipid metabolism, and compounds such as ginger and berberine possess antioxidant, insulin-sensitizing, and lipid-lowering effects.10,11 Collectively, these agents have been proposed to modulate liver biochemistry and metabolic-inflammatory mechanisms relevant to disease progression. However, evidence remains heterogeneous; head-to-head comparisons are scarce, and no consensus exists regarding the relative efficacy of individual interventions. Moreover, safety concerns persist, as some herbal and dietary supplements have been associated with drug-induced liver injury, and issues related to product identification, purity, standardization, contamination, and undeclared ingredients may affect safety and reproducibility of outcomes.12

Given these uncertainties, a robust comparative synthesis capable of integrating direct and indirect evidence is needed. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review (SR) and network meta-analysis (NMA) of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to evaluate the efficacy of herbal therapies in adults with MASLD/MASH across key biochemical and noninvasive hepatic outcomes.

Methodology

This SR followed the recommendations and criteria established by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses reporting guidelines. The protocol was preregistered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251157401).13, 14, 15

Eligibility Criteria

This SR included RCTs published from database inception through September 2025, enrolling participants with MASLD/NAFLD diagnosed by imaging, biopsy, or with a clinical profile suggestive of NAFLD characterized by elevated liver enzyme levels in the absence of other liver diseases, consistent with American Gastroenterological Association guidance.16 Studies were excluded if they involved animals, participants with significant alcohol intake, acute liver failure, other chronic liver diseases, major nonmetabolic comorbidities, nonoriginal research, duplicate data, insufficient detail, or missing information. Throughout this manuscript, the term MASLD is used to encompass the disease spectrum previously described as NAFLD and non-NASH, acknowledging that it included trials used in legacy terminology. Interventions of interest were single-compound herbal therapies or any herbal derivatives, compared with placebo, no treatment, lifestyle modifications, or standard treatment. Studies of approved traditional Chinese medicines or multiple herbal formulations (>4 active ingredients) were excluded to ensure consistency and isolate individual herbal effects. When overlapping reports from the same population were identified, both were included if they provided unique variables; however, data from the less comprehensive report were excluded to prevent double-counting and retain the most informative dataset. The primary outcomes included liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT)) fibrosis severity assessed histologically or using noninvasive measures, including FIB-4 and liver stiffness. The secondary outcomes included inflammatory markers (Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha [TNF-α], interleukin 6 [IL-6], interleukin 8) and hepatic steatosis indices (HSI) (fatty liver index [FLI]). Only studies reporting at least 1 predefined primary outcome were eligible for inclusion.

Searching Methods

A systematic search was initially conducted in September 2025 on PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, and Cochrane, using the following terms, but not limited to: “NAFLD,” “Fatty Liver,” “Steatohepatitis,” “MAFLD,” “NAFLD,” “NASH,” “Silymarin,” “Milk,” “Curcumin,” “Turmeric,” “Herbal,” “Phytomedicine,” “medicinal plant,” among others. All detailed search strategies can be found in the supplementary material (Supplementary Tables 1-4).

Selection of Studies

All references were exported to Rayyan (Rayyan Systems Inc., Cambridge, MA, and duplicates were removed.17 Two authors (R.T.S.) and (Y.C.C.) independently completed the eligibility assessment, first by title and abstract analysis, followed by full-text assessment. In cases of disagreement between reviewers, a third reviewer (E.C-M.) helped reach a consensus.

Data Extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted the data (T.A.M.D.) and (A.C.L.), and disagreements were resolved by consensus. Extracted data included sample sizes, intervention type, comparator, duration, and measured outcomes. Conventional methods were used for data extraction, complemented by specialized tools such as WebPlotDigitizer (Automeris, Austin, TX) for digitizing data from graphs, the Cochrane Calculator for statistical conversions, and StatsToDo for advanced calculations.18, 19, 20 Extracted outcomes comprised ALT (U/L), AST (U/L), GGT (U/L), FIB-4 score, FLI, liver stiffness (kPa), HSI, TNF-α (pg/mL), and IL-6 (pg/mL). Additional data extracted included subgroup characteristics, such as country of study, sample size, intervention duration, and risk-of-bias levels. Adverse events were also extracted and categorized by intervention and system-specific outcomes (eg, gastrointestinal, hepatic, neurological, and other disorders), when available, and are summarized in Supplementary Table S61.

Assessment of Risk of Bias in Included Studies

To assess the quality of the studies included in the SR, according to the Cochrane guidelines, we applied the Cochrane RoB 2.0 tool for RCTs.21,22 Two independent reviewers (A.C.L.) and (J.M.) evaluated the risk of bias in each study, considering the specific criteria and guidelines provided by the respective tools. Any reviewer's discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third, blinded reviewer (Y.C.C.).

Statistical Analysis

A meta-analysis was performed using R version 4.0 or later.23 The pooled effect of the outcomes was examined using a random-effects meta-analysis (DerSimonian-Laird approach).24 Whenever the number of studies reporting an outcome of interest was insufficient, only a qualitative analysis of the results was performed. Effect sizes were expressed as change from baseline mean differences (MDs), since all variables were measured using the same units (eg, ALT, AST, GGT, FIB-4 score, FLI, liver stiffness, HSI, TNF-α, IL-6). All estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The I2 statistic assessed heterogeneity, and the following cutoff values used for interpretation: <25, 25 to 50, and >50% were considered small, medium, and large heterogeneity, respectively and in the context of low heterogeneity, extensive sensitivity analyses are generally not recommended.25 Network geometry and rankings were visualized using network plots, ranking probabilities expressed as P-scores or surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), and forest plots showing multiple outcomes. For all outcomes, sensitivity analyses for low risk of bias studies and studies performing the intervention for at least 3 months were carried out to determine the influence of these variables on the overall effect.26 Egger's regression test examined publication bias when 10 or more reports with the same outcome were available.27 Results were presented using consistent analytical approaches and tabular formats across outcomes wherever possible, including random-effects NMAs with MDs, league tables, and SUCRA rankings assessments. Node-splitting analyses were conducted when both direct and indirect evidence were available within the network to assess inconsistency. When comparisons were informed exclusively by direct or indirect evidence, inconsistency assessment was not feasible and results were interpreted accordingly. Adverse events reporting across included studies was limited and heterogeneous, with many interventions lacking detailed safety data or reporting no events. Where reported, adverse events (AEs) were predominantly mild and most involved gastrointestinal symptoms, with few serious adverse events observed and no consistent signal of hepatotoxicity or mortality across interventions (Supplementary Table S61). Due to sparse reporting, lack of standardized AE outcomes, and insufficient direct or indirect comparisons between interventions, a NMA for safety outcomes was not feasible. Further statistical details are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis Assessment of Outcomes

Two reviewers (R.T.S.) and (M.D.F.M.) independently assessed confidence in the evidence for each outcome using the CINeMA (Confidence in NMA) framework. CINeMA evaluates 6 domains: within-study bias, across-study bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. Assessments were conducted according to established methodological standards using the CINeMA web application. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.28, 29, 30

Results

Study Selection

In the initial phase of our research, we identified 14,014 potential articles across 4 databases. After 3664 articles were removed, we conducted screening based on title and abstract, leading to the exclusion of 10,166 articles. Fifty-two of the 184 articles sought for retrieval were not retrieved, leaving 132 articles for eligibility assessment. After eligibility review, one article was excluded due to the wrong study design. Finally, 131 studies were included in this review. The selection process is summarized in our Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses flow chart (Figure 1).

Characteristics of Included Studies

The 131 included studies encompassed a total of 9191 participants, representing a diverse geographic distribution. Most studies were conducted in Iran (66.4%), followed by China (9.2%), Italy (6.9%), and Pakistan (3.1%). Other countries represented included India (2.3%), South Korea (1.5%), Malaysia (1.5%), and Japan (1.5%). Additional studies originated from Romania, the United States, Korea, Spain, Poland, Denmark, Germany, Egypt, and Thailand, each representing 0.8% of the total. All 131 included studies were RCTs and aimed to evaluate the efficacy of herbal therapies in MASLD/MASH. The interventions assessed across the included studies were diverse, with silymarin (19 studies) being the most frequently evaluated treatment, followed by curcumin (15 studies), resveratrol (6 studies), turmeric (5 studies), Nigella sativa (5 studies), green tea (5 studies), and pomegranate (5 studies). Less common therapies included sumac, green coffee, ginger, berberine, caper fruit, chicory, garlic, saffron, quercetin, and other combination regimens. Risk of Bias

Risk of bias was evaluated using the RoB tool for all 131 included RCTs.21 Of those, 104 studies (79%) were considered low risk, 23 (18%) moderate risk, and 4 (3%) high risk. Additional information on the risk-of-bias assessment process is available in the Supplementary Tables 5-14.

Meta-Analysis

Primary Outcomes

The NMAs for the liver-related primary outcomes included broad evidence base across multiple interventions. The ALT and AST networks each comprised 96 studies, 113 pairwise comparisons, 60 interventions, and 56 treatment designs, and were analyzed using a random-effects MD model using placebo as the reference. The GGT network included 45 studies, 62 pairwise comparisons, 37 interventions, and 32 designs under the same modeling approach. The liver stiffness network consisted of 18 studies, 23 pairwise comparisons, 17 interventions, and 15 designs, also evaluated with a random-effects MD model using placebo as the reference treatment (Figure 2A–J). Certainty of evidence for all network estimates was formally evaluated using the CINeMA framework and is presented separately below (Section 4.4).

Alanine aminotransferase

Multiple herbal interventions significantly reduced ALT levels compared with placebo (Figure 2A). The largest reductions were observed with artichoke leaf (MD −28.32, 95% CI −41.37 to −15.28, P < .01), piperine (MD −25.39, 95% CI −43.89 to −6.89, P < .01), cinnamon (MD −25.10, 95% CI −41.75 to −8.45, P < .01), chicory (MD −22.69, 95% CI −34.10 to −11.28, P < .01), and caper fruit (MD −21.80, 95% CI −37.27 to −6.33, P = .01). Additional reductions were observed with garlic + silymarin, berberine, green tea, turmeric, N sativa, silymarin, and curcumin, whereas remaining interventions did not differ from placebo. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 85.3%), with the global Q statistic indicating variability (Q = 300.12, df = 44; P < .0001). Decomposition analyses showed heterogeneity arose mainly from within-design variability, particularly placebo comparisons involving N sativa, sumac, curcumin + piperine, silymarin, and curcumin. A smaller but significant between-design inconsistency component involved placebo comparisons with silymarin, hesperidin, turmeric, and chicory (Supplementary Table 15); this remained significant under the design-by-treatment interaction model (P = .02). Node-split analyses showed agreement between direct and indirect estimates, with localized inconsistencies confined to turmeric and turmeric + chicory contrasts (Figure 3A; Supplementary Table 16), supporting network coherence. SUCRA rankings showed the highest probabilities of benefit for artichoke leaf (0.94), cinnamon (0.91), piperine (0.88), chicory (0.85), and caper fruit (0.82), whereas the lowest rankings were observed for placebo (0.23), silymarin + curcumin + sunflower oil (0.15), and Melissa officinalis + N sativa (0.07) (Figure 4A; Table 2). The league table confirmed that the most pronounced benefits clustered among top-ranked interventions, while most active-active comparisons showed overlapping CIs (Supplementary Table 17). Funnel plot inspection showed no asymmetry, and Egger’s test was not significant (P = .98), indicating no small-study effects (Supplementary Figure 1A).

Aspartate aminotransferase

For AST, several interventions significantly reduced levels compared with placebo (Figure 2B). The largest decreases were observed with cinnamon (MD −23.50, 95% CI −37.59 to −9.41, P < .01), berberine (MD −19.63, 95% CI −30.21 to −9.04, P < .01), bergamot + CyC (MD −17.83, 95% CI −28.13 to −7.53, P < .01), artichoke leaf (MD −15.49, 95% CI −23.39 to −7.58, P < .01), and QuYuHuaTanTongLuo Decoction (QYHTTLD) (MD −12.65, 95% CI −24.22 to −1.08, P = .04). Additional reductions were noted for green tea, N sativa, CL16049F1, chicory, curcumin, and silymarin (P < .05 for all), whereas most remaining interventions showed no significant differences vs placebo. In contrast, R damascena was associated with a significant increase in AST (MD 33.98, 95% CI 18.05–49.91). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 83.0%), with the global Q statistic indicating variability (Q = 259.24, df = 44; P < .0001). Decomposition analyses showed heterogeneity was driven mainly by within-design variability, particularly placebo comparisons involving silymarin, N sativa, curcumin, sumac, and green tea. A smaller contribution from between-design inconsistency involved placebo vs turmeric, silymarin, and hesperidin; however, under the full design-by-treatment interaction model this component was no longer significant (P = .12), suggesting inconsistency was largely explained by design-level variation (Supplementary Table 18). Node-split analyses showed agreement between direct and indirect evidence, with localized inconsistencies confined to turmeric and turmeric + chicory comparisons (Figure 3B; Supplementary Table 19). SUCRA rankings indicated cinnamon (0.93), berberine (0.89), bergamot + CyC (0.87), artichoke leaf (0.84), and QuYuHuaTanTongLuo Decoction (0.81) had the highest probability of being most effective for reducing AST, whereas R damascena (0.19), placebo (0.14), and chicory (0.10) ranked lowest (Figure 4B; Table 2). League tables confirmed superiority of several interventions over placebo, although differences among active treatments were generally modest and frequently showed overlapping CIs (Supplementary Table 20). Funnel plot inspection and Egger’s test (P = .78) showed no evidence of small-study effects (Supplementary Figure 1B).

Gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase

For GGT, several interventions significantly reduced levels compared with placebo (Figure 2C). The greatest effects were observed with cinnamon (MD −25.80, 95% CI −39.20 to −12.40, P < .01), garlic + silymarin (MD −22.89, 95% CI −38.32 to −7.46, P < .01), berberine (MD −20.97, 95% CI −38.20 to −3.75, P = .03), silymarin (MD −19.06, 95% CI −27.28 to −10.83, P < .01), and hesperidin + flaxseed (MD −19.66, 95% CI −35.20 to −4.13, P = .04). Additional reductions were noted for CL16049F1 and curcumin (P < .05 for both), whereas other interventions showed borderline or nonsignificant effects. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate to high (I2 = 77.2%), with the global Q statistic indicating variability (Q = 70.26, df = 16; P < .0001). Decomposition analyses showed heterogeneity was driven primarily by within-design variability, particularly placebo comparisons involving silymarin and curcumin, with a smaller contribution from between-design inconsistency related to placebo vs turmeric and hesperidin (Supplementary Table 21). Under the design-by-treatment interaction model, between-design inconsistency was no longer significant, indicating improved network coherence. Node-split analyses showed no significant differences between direct and indirect estimates, supporting consistency across the network (Figure 3C; Supplementary Table 22). SUCRA rankings indicated cinnamon (0.92), garlic + silymarin (0.89), berberine (0.87), silymarin (0.84), and hesperidin + flaxseed (0.82) had the highest probabilities of being most effective for reducing GGT, whereas placebo (0.21), licorice root (0.13), and chicory (0.09) ranked lowest (Figure 4C; Table 2). League tables confirmed benefits clustered among top-ranked interventions, while most active-active comparisons showed overlapping CIs (Supplementary Table 23). Funnel plot inspection and Egger’s test (P = .56) showed no evidence of small-study effects (Supplementary Figure 1C).

Liver stiffness

For liver stiffness, few interventions were associated with significant improvements compared with placebo (Figure 2D). Curcumin (MD −0.76, 95% CI −1.44 to −0.08, P = .03) and pomegranate (MD −0.86, 95% CI −1.70 to −0.02, P = .04) demonstrated modest but significant reductions, whereas remaining interventions showed nonsignificant trends toward improvement. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 52.1%), and the global Q statistic did not indicate significant heterogeneity (Q = 8.36, df = 4; P = .0793). Decomposition analyses showed heterogeneity arose primarily within designs, particularly placebo vs curcumin comparisons, with minimal between-design inconsistency. Under the design-by-treatment interaction model, between-design inconsistency remained nonsignificant, supporting network coherence (Supplementary Table 24). Node-split analyses for flaxseed, hesperidin, and their combination showed no meaningful discrepancies between direct and indirect estimates (Figure 3D; Supplementary Table 25). SUCRA rankings indicated silymarin (0.81), hesperidin + flaxseed (0.79), pomegranate (0.77), curcumin (0.75), and Liv.52DS (0.72) had the highest probabilities of being among the most effective interventions (Figure 4D; Table 2). However, the league table confirmed that only curcumin and pomegranate were significantly superior to placebo, while most active-active comparisons showed overlapping CIs (Supplementary Table 26). Funnel plot inspection and Egger’s test (P = .86) showed no small-study effects (Supplementary Figure 1D).

Secondary Outcomes

Tumor necrosis factor alpha

The NMA for TNF-α included 19 RCTs evaluating 17 interventions. Several interventions significantly reduced TNF-α levels compared with placebo, including bergamot (MD −16.40, 95% CI −23.30 to −9.50, P < .01), bergamot + CyC (MD −23.90, 95% CI −30.74 to −17.06, P < .01), CyC alone (MD −20.40, 95% CI −27.85 to −12.95, P < .01), genistein (MD −16.96, 95% CI −29.87 to −4.05, P = .01), and hesperidin (MD −5.39, 95% CI −10.56 to −0.21, P = .04). Remaining interventions did not differ significantly from placebo (Figure 2E). Between-study heterogeneity was very high (I2 = 99.1%), with the global Q statistic indicating substantial variability (Q = 760.11, df = 7; P < .01). Decomposition analyses showed heterogeneity was driven almost entirely by within-design variability, particularly in placebo-controlled comparisons involving curcumin, whereas between-design inconsistency was small and nonsignificant (Figure 3E; Supplementary Table 27). Under the full design-by-treatment interaction model, between-design inconsistency remained nonsignificant, supporting network coherence. SUCRA rankings indicated bergamot + CyC had the highest probability of being most effective for reducing TNF-α (SUCRA 0.97), followed by CyC alone and bergamot (Figure 4E; Table 2). The league table showed significant benefits were confined to a limited number of interventions, while most active-active comparisons showed overlapping CIs (Supplementary Table 29). Funnel plot inspection and Egger’s test (P = .34) did not suggest small-study effects (Supplementary Figure 1E).

Steatosis

For steatosis, the NMA included 8 RCTs evaluating 9 interventions (Figure 2F). Compared with placebo, curcumin (MD −19.21, 95% CI −31.92 to −6.50, P < .01), fenugreek seeds (MD −23.60, 95% CI −45.67 to −1.53, P = .04), and N sativa (MD −14.52, 95% CI −27.05 to −1.99, P = .02) demonstrated significant reductions in steatosis. In contrast, Jiang Zhi Granule (MD 0.19, 95% CI 0.13–0.25, P < .01) and Qianggan (MD 0.13, 95% CI 0.04–0.22, P = .01) were associated with significant increases, while remaining interventions showed no differences. No between-study heterogeneity or inconsistency was detected (Q = 0.00; τ2 not estimable), reflecting the absence of closed loops and limited comparisons. Consequently, decomposition of heterogeneity and design-by-treatment interaction modeling were not applicable, and node-splitting analyses could not be performed because no intervention was informed by both direct and indirect evidence (Figure 3F; Supplementary Table 30). SUCRA rankings indicated fenugreek seeds had the highest probability of being most effective (SUCRA 0.89), followed by curcumin (0.81) and Na sativa (0.76), whereas Jiang Zhi Granule and Qianggan ranked lowest (Figure 4F; Table 2). A league table summarizing pairwise comparisons is provided in Supplementary Table 30. Funnel plot inspection is shown in Supplementary Figure 1F; Egger’s test was not performed due to the limited number of studies.

Fatty liver index

The NMA for FLI included 8 RCTs evaluating 10 interventions (Figure 2G). Compared with placebo, flaxseed (MD −22.83, 95% CI −41.49 to −4.17, P = .02) and hesperidin (MD −20.12, 95% CI −38.37 to −1.87, P = .03) were associated with significant reductions, whereas remaining interventions showed no significant differences. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 68.6%), and the global Q statistic indicated significant variability (Q = 6.37, df = 2; P = .04). Decomposition analyses showed heterogeneity was driven primarily by within-design variability, particularly placebo-curcumin comparisons, while between-design inconsistency was low and nonsignificant (Figure 3G; Supplementary Table 31). SUCRA rankings indicated flaxseed had the highest probability of being most effective (0.85), followed by hesperidin (Figure 4G; Table 2). League tables of pairwise comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table 33. Funnel plot inspection showed no asymmetry, and Egger’s test showed no small-study effects (P = .51; Supplementary Figure 1G).

Fibrosis-4

The NMA for the FIB-4 index included 7 RCTs evaluating 6 interventions (Figure 2H). Compared with placebo, berberine (MD −0.41, 95% CI −0.61 to −0.21, P < .01) and curcumin (MD −0.40, 95% CI −0.55 to −0.25, P < .01) were associated with significant reductions in FIB-4, while remaining interventions showed no differences. Between-study heterogeneity was low to moderate (I2 = 27.6%), and the global Q statistic did not indicate significant variability (Q = 2.76, df = 2; P = .25). Decomposition analyses showed no evidence of between-design inconsistency; due to absence of closed loops, node-splitting could not be performed (Figure 3H; Supplementary Table 34). SUCRA rankings identified berberine as most likely effective (SUCRA 0.90), followed by curcumin (Figure 4H; Table 2). Detailed league tables of pairwise comparisons are in Supplementary Table 35. Funnel plot inspection is shown in Supplementary Figure 1H; Egger’s test was not performed due to the limited number of studies.

Interleukin 6

The NMA for IL-6 included 8 studies with 7 interventions, and 6 interventions (Figure 2I). Curcumin was the only intervention associated with a statistically significant reduction in IL-6 compared with placebo (MD −5.14, 95% CI −10.07 to −0.21, P = .04); all other agents showed no significant differences. Heterogeneity was moderate to high in this network (I2 = 73.1%; Q = 7.44, df = 2, P = .02), arising entirely within designs, with no evidence of between-design inconsistency (Supplementary Table 36). No indirect and direct evidence for the same intervention is generated (Figure 3I). Curcumin ranked highest according to SUCRA (0.85; Figure 4I; Table 2). League table shows the full values detailed in Supplemental Table 37. Funnel plots for IL-6 are shown in Supplemental Figure 1I; Egger’s test was not conducted due to the limited number of studies.

Hepatic steatosis index

The NMA for HSI included 3 studies evaluating 3 interventions (Figure 2J). Curcumin was associated with a significant reduction compared with placebo (MD −1.85, 95% CI −3.60 to −0.10, P = .04). No heterogeneity or inconsistency was detected (I2 = 0%; Q = 0.03, df = 1; P = .86). Node-splitting analyses were not feasible because no comparisons were made by both direct and indirect evidence (Figure 3J). SUCRA rankings indicated curcumin had the highest probability of being most effective (0.90; Figure 4J; Table 2). Full pairwise comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table 38. Funnel plots are shown in Supplementary Figure 1J; Egger’s test was not performed due to the limited number of trials.

Subgroup Analysis

We conducted predefined subgroup analyses restricted to studies at low risk of bias with intervention durations ≥3 months. This subgroup included 47 studies for ALT, 47 for AST, 29 for GGT, 11 for liver stiffness, 3 for steatosis, 11 for TNF-α, 3 for IL-6, 6 for FLI, 5 for FIB-4, and 3 for HSI (Supplementary Figure 2A–J).

For ALT, significant reductions vs placebo persisted for multiple interventions, including caper fruit (MD −21.80, 95% CI −30.98 to −12.62; P < .01), cinnamon (MD −25.10, 95% CI −36.14 to −14.06; P < .01), N sativa (MD −23.29, 95% CI −32.60 to −13.98; P < .01), garlic + silymarin, bergamot + CyC, berberine, green tea, curcumin, among others (Supplementary Figure 2A). Heterogeneity remained moderate (I2 = 60%). For AST, significant decreases were observed with cinnamon (MD −23.50, 95% CI −37.66 to −9.34; P < .01), berberine (MD −19.62, 95% CI −30.28 to −8.96; P < .01), bergamot + CyC (MD −17.83, 95% CI −28.22 to −7.44; P < .01), N sativa, green tea, curcumin, and silymarin. R damascena produced a marked increase (MD 33.98, 95% CI 17.99–49.97; P < .01; Supplementary Figure 2B). Heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 80.6%). For GGT, significant reductions persisted for curcumin (MD −37.00, 95% CI −54.11 to −19.89; P < .01), cinnamon (MD −25.80, 95% CI −29.91 to −21.69; P < .01), berberine (MD −21.27, 95% CI −35.14 to −7.40; P < .01), hesperidin + flaxseed, bergamot + CyC, bergamot, CyC, and dihydromyricetin, among others (Supplementary Figure 2C). Residual heterogeneity was minimal (I2 = 3.7%). For liver stiffness, significant reductions were observed for curcumin (MD −2.06, 95% CI −3.37 to −0.75; P = .002), Liv.52DS (MD −0.77, 95% CI −1.45 to −0.09; P = .03), and Phyllanthus niruri (MD −0.74, 95% CI −1.25 to −0.23; P = .005), with minimal heterogeneity (I2 = 9.4%) (Supplementary Figure 2D).

Among secondary outcomes, a significant reduction in steatosis was observed only with fenugreek seeds (MD −23.60, 95% CI −45.67 to −1.53; P = .04; Supplementary Figure 2F). For TNF-α, bergamot, bergamot + CyC, CyC, and hesperidin remained significantly superior, although heterogeneity was extreme (I2 = 99.3%; Supplementary Figure 2E). For IL-6, curcumin showed a borderline effect with high heterogeneity (I2 = 86.2%; Supplementary Figure 2I). None of the interventions showed statistically significant effects on FLI or FIB-4. For HSI, curcumin remained significantly superior to placebo (Supplementary Figure 1J). SUCRA values for all subgroup analyses are provided in Supplementary Table 40, league tables of pairwise comparisons in Supplementary Tables 41-50, and funnel plots in Supplementary Figure 1A–J.

Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis Assessment

According to the CINeMA evaluation, confidence in the evidence across liver enzymes, and related outcomes were predominantly low or very low.28, 29, 30 This pattern was driven primarily by imprecision, heterogeneity, and network incoherence, with several nodes also limited by single study estimates, reducing the robustness of indirect comparisons. While most comparisons for ALT, AST, GGT, IL-6, TNF-α, FLI, steatosis grade, and liver stiffness were rated low or very low, a small proportion of ALT comparisons (artichoke leaf and berberine) achieved high confidence. Similarly, FIB-4 demonstrated pockets of high-confidence evidence, and HSI showed moderate confidence ratings. These limitations highlight the need for larger, methodologically robust randomized trials. Full CINeMA domain-level ratings are provided in Supplementary Tables 51-60.

Discussion

In this NMA, herbal interventions demonstrated outcome-specific associations with MASLD markers rather than uniform benefits. This domain-specific pattern helps interpret the substantial heterogeneity observed across interventions. While several therapies significantly improved liver enzymes, inflammatory biomarkers, and hepatic steatosis, no single intervention showed consistent benefit across all parameters, and most effects were supported by low-to-moderate certainty of evidence.

Regarding biochemical markers, multiple interventions reduced ALT, AST, and GGT, suggesting attenuation of hepatocellular and cholestatic injury. Artichoke leaf, cinnamon, and berberine ranked highest for ALT, while cinnamon, garlic plus silymarin, and berberine showed the greatest impact on GGT. Although most enzyme outcomes had low confidence, selected ALT comparisons achieved high confidence. Liver enzymes remain clinically relevant indicators of disease activity and progression risk in MASLD.161,162 Our findings are consistent with prior meta-analyses in metabolically dysregulated populations. Previous pooled analyses in patients with type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome have shown that cinnamon supplementation significantly reduces ALT, while longer-duration curcumin interventions lead to reductions in both ALT and AST.163,31 Similarly, previous meta-analyses evaluating berberine across metabolic liver disease populations reported significant improvements in ALT, AST, and GGT.161,162 Unlike earlier pairwise analyses, this NMA enables simultaneous comparison across multiple interventions. SUCRA rankings reflect relative probabilities within the network and must be interpreted alongside overall certainty. The biochemical improvements are biologically plausible and may reflect modulation of lipid metabolism, bile acid signaling, and inflammatory pathways164,165; however, low-certainty findings should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Given the central role of chronic low-grade inflammation in MASLD progression, inflammatory biomarkers represent an important therapeutic target. Bergamot plus CyC, CyC alone, bergamot, and genistein produced the largest TNF-α reductions, while curcumin consistently reduced IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α and ranked highly by SUCRA. Confidence for inflammatory outcomes was predominantly low to very low. These results are consistent with prior trials demonstrating anti-inflammatory effects of curcumin, silymarin, and berberine.3,161,162,166, 167, 168, 169, 170 Curcumin10,31,165,32, 171, 172, 173, 174, 175, 176, 177, 178 and berberine have shown cytokine-lowering and antioxidant effects, partly mediated through nuclear factor kappa B and metabolic pathway modulation.10,165,179,180 Because persistent cytokine elevation contributes to insulin resistance and fibrogenesis, these reductions may be clinically meaningful, though interpretation requires caution.169,158, 179, 180, 181, 182, 183, 184, 185

Hepatic steatosis, as the primary pathological feature of MASLD, is a key therapeutic target. Curcumin, fenugreek seeds, and N sativa demonstrated the most pronounced reductions in steatosis severity. Curcumin showed consistent efficacy across imaging- and index-based measures, including FLI and HSI, and improved selected inflammatory and fibrosis-related indices, resulting in high SUCRA rankings across multiple domains. Nonetheless, no intervention demonstrated uniform benefit across all structural and biochemical outcomes. Confidence in steatosis and liver stiffness outcomes was predominantly low, with moderate confidence for HSI and high confidence for selected FIB-4 comparisons. These findings align with SRs reporting improved ultrasound-based steatosis, particularly when herbal therapies are adjuncts to lifestyle modification.186,187 Berberine has similarly been associated with steatosis improvement through metabolic modulation.165 Improvements in steatosis and fibrosis indices may reduce the progression to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma.

For liver stiffness, curcumin and pomegranate were associated with statistically significant reductions. Subgroup analyses suggested greater effects in longer-duration, lower-risk-of-bias studies, and randomized evidence indicates potential antifibrotic benefits of silymarin requiring confirmation.10,165,32,33

Current MASLD management relies primarily on lifestyle modification, with limited approved pharmacologic options.12,188, 189, 190, 191, 192, 193, 194, 195, 196, 197 Our results indicate that selected herbal interventions, particularly curcumin, berberine, cinnamon, and artichoke leaf, were associated with improvements in liver enzymes, inflammatory markers, hepatic steatosis, and fibrosis-related outcomes. However, overall certainty ranged from low to moderate due to heterogeneity and imprecision. These findings do not establish disease-modifying efficacy or support routine clinical use but identify promising adjunctive candidates for further investigation. Safety reporting was inconsistent, and variability in formulation and dosing raises concerns regarding quality control and drug-herb interactions. Accordingly, herbal therapies should not replace established treatments and, if considered, require cautious, individualized clinical supervision. Future studies with standardized formulations, longer follow-up, comprehensive safety reporting, and validated imaging-based endpoints are needed before these interventions can be considered for broader clinical application.

Strengths and Limitations

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of herbal therapies in MASLD, based on RCTs. The NMA framework allowed simultaneous comparison and ranking of multiple single-compound herbal interventions, even in the absence of direct head-to-head evidence. Methodological rigor was strengthened through a formal grading of confidence in estimates using the CINeMA evaluation, alongside systematic assessment of heterogeneity and network coherence, with no statistically significant inconsistency detected in networks where sufficient data permitted formal evaluation. Inclusion of trials from diverse geographic regions supports external validation and broader applicability. The focus on clinically relevant hepatic enzymes, fibrosis measures, and inflammatory markers enhance clinical relevance.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Reporting of inflammatory markers was inconsistent across the studies; however, available data suggest potential anti-inflammatory effects alongside biochemical and occasional histological improvements. Variability in herbal formulation, dose, duration, and product standardization reflect variability inherent to the evidence base and may affect generalizability. Variability in herbal formulation, dose, duration, and product standardization reflect heterogeneity inherent to the evidence base and may affect reproducibility and generalizability. Importantly, a substantial proportion of included studies originated from a single country (Iran), with relatively limited representation from other geographic regions. This geographic concentration not only limits the external validity of the findings across diverse ethnic, cultural, dietary, and health-care settings, but also reflects the fact that herbal therapies for MASLD are not routinely incorporated into standard clinical management in most regions worldwide and are primarily investigated or utilized in a limited number of countries. Consequently, the applicability and clinical translation of these findings to broader international populations should be interpreted cautiously. Outcome units were harmonized using standardized conversion methods. Outcome units were harmonized using standardized conversion methods. Many interventions were evaluated in small or single trials, resulting in imprecision, limited direct-indirect overlap, and reduced confidence in several estimates. For certain outcomes, absence of closed loops constrained formal inconsistency assessment. Differences in MASLD diagnostic criteria and background lifestyle interventions may have introduced residual confounding. Additionally, safety considerations should be acknowledged, as some herbal interventions, such as green tea extract and turmeric compounds, have been associated with drug-induced liver injury, highlighting the need for cautious interpretation of efficacy findings in the context of potential hepatotoxicity and variability in formulation, composition, and quality.

Overall confidence in several comparisons remained low to moderate, underscoring the need for larger, standardized trials before clinical implementation.

Conclusion

This SR and NMA of RCTs suggest that several herbal interventions are associated with improvements in specific MASLD-related outcomes, although no intervention demonstrated consistent benefit across all endpoints, and results varied and require cautious interpretation given formulation and regulatory inconsistencies Among available agents, cinnamon, curcumin, silymarin, berberine, artichoke leaf, and N sativa demonstrated the most consistent signals of benefit. Future research should prioritize standardized formulations, dose optimization, rigorous safety assessment, and harmonized noninvasive endpoints aligned with MASLD/MASH guidelines. Robust methodological frameworks are required before determining whether these agents can safely complement evidence-based care.
