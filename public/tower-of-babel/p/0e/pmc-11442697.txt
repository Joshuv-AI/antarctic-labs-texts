A novel SOX2 frameshift pathogenic variant located in the transactivation domain in a male infant with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism
Authors: Ayano Kimura-Yoshida, Takeshi Sato, Yosuke Ichihashi, Masanori Wasa, Satoshi Narumi, Tomohiro Ishii, Tomonobu Hasegawa
Published: 2024

Highlights

● We identified a novel SOX2 frameshift variant in the transactivation domain.

● The SOX2 p.Lys115Alafs*8 variant is associated with HH.

Introduction

SOX2 is a transcription factor involved in early embryonic development and plays a critical role in the development of the eye, forebrain, and hypothalamus. SOX2 is a single-exon gene that encodes a 317-amino acid protein with two functional domains: DNA-binding and transactivation (1). Heterozygous pathogenic variants of SOX2 cause ocular manifestations, including clinical anophthalmia or microphthalmia in most patients and hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (HH) in some patients. Given the presence of cases with whole SOX2 deletions resulting in ocular manifestations, with or without HH (1, 2), haploinsufficiency is considered the major mechanism of ocular manifestations. To date, the gonadotropin-secreting capacity of patients with SOX2-associated HH has not been fully elucidated. Here, we report a novel pathogenic variant of SOX2 in a male infant presenting with HH, and discuss the gonadotropin secretion capacity of patients with HH due to SOX2 pathogenic variants.

Case Report

The proband was the second child of nonconsanguineous, healthy Japanese parents, and the pregnancy was spontaneous and uncomplicated. He was delivered at 38 wk of gestation with a birth weight of 3,160 g, length of 48.0 cm and head circumference of 33.5 cm. The patient had no relevant family history, and at birth, right anophthalmia, left blepharophimosis, and micropenis (stretched penile length: 20 mm) were noted. Hypospadias or undescended testes were not observed. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed right anophthalmia, left microphthalmia, and septum pellucidum defects, and identified the pituitary gland; however, it did not precisely assess whether the anterior pituitary gland was hypoplastic (Fig. 1A). Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with septo-optic dysplasia. Automated auditory brainstem response appeared normal. No esophageal atresia or tracheoesophageal fistulae were observed. TRH, CRH, and arginine loading tests at the age of 21 d showed no abnormal findings: basal/peak levels of TSH, ACTH, cortisol, and GH, were 1.520/10.600 µIU/mL, 131.0/131.0 pg/mL, 20.7/20.7 µg/dL, and 7.56/7.56 ng/mL, respectively; the basal levels of ACTH, cortisol, and GH were identical to the respective peak levels.

At age 4 mo, namely during mini-puberty, a GnRH loading test was performed, revealing hyposecretion of gonadotropins: basal/peak levels of LH and FSH, by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay, < 0.3/0.9 and < 0.3/2.9 mIU/mL, respectively [2.5 to 97.5 percentiles of basal levels of LH and FSH in male infants aged 3.5 to 5.0 mo by fluoroimmunoassays, 0.54–3.32 and 0.42–2.68, respectively (3)]. His testosterone level was also low for mini-puberty [0.05 ng/mL, by chemiluminescent immunoassay; 2.5 to 97.5 percentiles of basal levels in male infants aged 3.5 to 5.0 mo by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry, 0.32–9.65 (3)]. From age 6 mo, he received three monthly intramuscular injections of 25 mg testosterone enanthate. Consequently, the stretched penile length increased to 43 mm. The patient was diagnosed with HH based on the following four findings: i) micropenis, ii) low GnRH-stimulated gonadotropin secretion during mini-puberty, iii) low testosterone levels during mini-puberty, and iv) a sufficient increase in penile length by intramuscular testosterone administration.

At 2 yr of age, repeated TRH, CRH, and arginine loading tests showed no abnormal findings: basal/peak levels of TSH, ACTH, cortisol, and GH were 2.02/24.1 µIU/mL, 37.1/57.1 pg/mL, 6.1/17.1 µg/dL, and 1.19/6.42 ng/mL, respectively.

The patient was three years old at the previous visit. He has never experienced any hypoglycemic symptoms or required cortisol replacement on the day he was sick. The height velocity was maintained along the 3–10 percentile of the growth curve. His neurological development was delayed, and he was able to babble and sit with support. The patient did not experience any seizures.

Genetic analysis

After obtaining written informed consent from the patient’s legal guardians, genomic DNA was extracted from the peripheral blood of the proband. PCR-based Sanger sequencing led us to identify a heterozygous frameshift SOX2 (NM_003106.4) variant, c.343_389del, p.Lys115Alafs*8 (Fig. 1B). Notably, this variant has not been reported to be a pathogenic variant in SOX2-related diseases. This variant was not found in ToMMo 54KJPN (https://jmorp.megabank.tohoku.ac.jp/) or in the Genome Aggregation Database (https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/). Among the 91 amino acids (110–200) in the transactivation domain, 86 amino acids (115–200) appeared to have been replaced or deleted. We hypothesized that the mutant SOX2 protein would lose 94.5% of its amino acid residues in the functional transactivation domain. According to the ACMG guidelines (4), this novel variant was categorized as a pathogenic variant (PVS1, PM1, and PM2). Thus, we considered the p.Lys115Alafs*8 variant pathogenic.

Literature Review

PubMed and Google Scholar literature searches were conducted in April, 2024. We used the following search terms: “SOX2” and “hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.” Eighteen SOX2 pathogenic variants associated with HH have been reported to show a broad distribution (Fig. 1C) (1, 2, 5,6,7,8,9,10,11). We extracted data from 14 patients who underwent the GnRH loading tests and compared their clinical findings (Table 1). All patients, except for one with variant c.905delC (7), showed insufficient pubertal development and hyposecretion of gonadotropins (Table 1).

Discussion

We identified a novel frameshift variant in the transactivation domain of SOX2 in a male infant with HH. As SOX2 contains a single exon with no introns, non-sense-mediated decay is not expected (5). In this study, we expected the gene to express a truncated protein without a transactivation domain, resulting in loss of function of the protein.

The novel variant identified in our case may have additional pathogenicity. In a previous report, p.Asp123Valfs*31-and p.Tyr200*-truncated SOX2 proteins, both of which are variants located in the transactivation domain, retained their ability to bind to DNA and interfere with the function of wild-type SOX2 in vitro (i.e., a dominant-negative effect) (5). Thus, the p.Lys115Alafs*8 variant may also have a dominant-negative effect. Notably, a subset of patients with variants that cause haploinsufficiency showed the most severe ocular phenotypes, including anophthalmia and severe forms of HH (6, 10). Thus, we speculate that the severity of clinical manifestations in patients with dominant-negative variants may not differ from that in patients with variants causing haploinsufficiency.

Among the 14 patients with GnRH loading test data, the patient with the variant p.Pro302fs, aged 17 yr, showed low testosterone levels (below the lower limit of measurement), but had 4–5 mL of testes and a substantial capacity for LH secretion (basal/peak levels of LH, 1.15/14.7 mIU/mL) (7). This mutant SOX2 protein is considered to retain both the DNA-binding and transactivation domains (Fig. 1C). We speculate that in the patient with the variant p.Pro302fs, the mutant SOX2 protein has substantial residual activity and is associated with mild impairment of gonadotropin secretion and limited pubertal development. The p.Gly280fs variant also showed a certain basal level of FSH (3.5 mIU/mL), supporting our speculation (Fig. 1C) (9). However, unexpectedly, a patient with the p.Gly21fs variant, estimated to be deprived of both the DNA-binding and transactivation domains, showed gonadotropin secretion to a certain degree (basal/peak LH and FSH levels, 1–2/6.1 and 3.2–4.7/5.7 mIU/mL, respectively) (Fig. 1C) (Table 1) (11). This implies that two copies of SOX2, which encode the DNA-binding and transactivation domains, are not always essential for gonadotropin secretion.

We must recognize the complexity of the pathogenicity of SOX2 disorders; the development of clinical manifestations is not determined solely by genetic alterations. In a previous study, siblings with the same c.837delC, p.Gly280Alafs*91 variant showed different ocular phenotypes; the brother showed bilateral anophthalmia, and the sister showed left microphthalmia and retinal coloboma (9).

The mechanisms underlying LH and FSH hyposecretion in SOX2 disorders are not completely understood. Hypothalamic dysfunction is considered a mechanism of hypogonadism, as evidenced by some patients with SOX2 variants developing HH along with olfactory dysfunction (5). Adult mice exhibit Sox2 expression in the hypothalamus, further supporting hypothalamic dysfunction (5). Pituitary dysfunction has been implicated in patients with pituitary hypoplasia associated with SOX2 variants (12). Cells expressing Sox2 are found throughout the murine embryonic Rathke’s pouch and remain as stem and/or progenitor cells in the adult pituitary gland, supporting the relationship between SOX2 pathogenic variants and pituitary dysfunction in humans (13). Therefore, dysfunction of the hypothalamus and/or pituitary gland is presumed to cause HH. In clinical practice, it is sometimes challenging to accurately evaluate the gonadotropin secretion capacity and identify the cause in the hypothalamus and/or pituitary gland. In a single GnRH loading test, a subset of patients with hypothalamic HH may respond to a certain degree. Therefore, when evaluating the gonadotropin secretion capacity in patients with SOX2 variants, micropenis, cryptorchidism, olfactory function, and pituitary volume should be considered along with the GnRH loading test results.

In summary, we identified a novel pathogenic variant of SOX2 c.343_389del, p.Lys115Alafs*8, which is associated with HH.
