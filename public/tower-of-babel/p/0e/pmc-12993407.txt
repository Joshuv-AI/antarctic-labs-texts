Teduglutide use in haemodialysis-dependent renal failure from secondary hyperoxaluria in short bowel syndrome and type 3 intestinal failure – A case report
Authors: Elizabeth SL Low, Brooke Chapman, Darren Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRenal impairment is common in patients receiving long-term home parenteral nutrition (HPN) for short bowel syndrome-associated intestinal failure (SBS-IF). While glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2) analogues like teduglutide are effective in reducing parenteral support (PS) requirements, data on their use in advanced stage chronic kidney disease (CKD) or haemodialysis are scarce.Case reportWe describe a 66-year-old female with SBS-IF on HPN, complicated by hyperoxaluria and nephrocalcinosis contributing to stage 4 CKD. Initial renally-adjusted teduglutide dosing (0.025 mg/kg/day) enabled enteral autonomy, but was interrupted by intercurrent illness. As a result of progressive renal dysfunction, the patient was commenced on haemodialysis. Teduglutide was introduced post-dialysis at full dose (0.05 mg/kg), resulting in a sustained 36 % reduction in weekly PS requirements without adverse effects, despite recurrent sepsis.ConclusionThis case demonstrates a clinically significant teduglutide response in a haemodialysis-dependent SBS-IF patient, supporting post-dialysis dosing as a feasible strategy in this HPN cohort.

Introduction

Short bowel syndrome is a condition associated with less than 200 cm of remaining small bowel in continuity [1]. It is one of the most common causes of chronic intestinal failure, defined as a reduction in gut absorptive function below the minimum required such that intravenous supplementation of macronutrients and/or water and electrolytes is required to maintain health [1]. Many short bowel syndrome-associated intestinal failure (SBS-IF) patients require long-term parenteral support (PS) in the form of home parenteral nutrition (HPN) and/or fluids. While advances in intestinal rehabilitation management, including the use of glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2) analogues, have improved outcomes, long-term complications of HPN remain significant [1], [2].

Routine monitoring for metabolic complications, including hepatobiliary, renal and bone disease, are recommended in international guidelines [1]. Among these, progressive renal impairment has been increasingly recognized, with chronic kidney disease (CKD) rates increasing from 10 % at HPN initiation to 36 % after 30 months follow-up in one observational study [3]. Despite this, specific guidance on GLP-2 analogue use in patients with end-stage renal disease requiring haemodialysis is lacking.

This report outlines a case of a haemodialysis-dependent patient with SBS-IF achieving a clinically meaningful response to teduglutide administration post-dialysis, supporting its tolerability and efficacy in this complex setting.

Case report

We present the case a 66-year-old female on long-term HPN for SBS-IF. CC underwent multiple small intestinal resections 25 years earlier for penetrating Crohn’s disease, resulting in residual bowel anatomy of 80 cm of small intestine from duodenal-jejunal flexure to a jejunocolic anastomosis to the ascending colon. Her comorbidities included recurrent catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs) with a prior episode of infective endocarditis, previous infliximab-induced lupus requiring long-term prednisolone, osteoporosis, and stage 4 CKD. Previous work-up for her renal dysfunction, including autoimmune and vasculitic serologies, paraprotein levels, haemoglobin A1c, urinalysis and urine protein quantification were unrevealing for contributory aetiologies. Hyperoxaluria (urine oxalate excretion 0.8–1.8 mmol/day on serial testing, reference range <0.6 mmol/day), along with the findings of bilateral nephrocalcinosis on computed tomography abdomen and symmetrical delayed cortical clearance on MAG3 renogram, led to consensus nephrology opinion on a diagnosis of CKD presumed secondary to intestinal hyperoxalosis, with a tendency towards pre-renal acute kidney injury episodes during periods of sepsis or dehydration.

Following the availability of teduglutide in Australia, she commenced renally-adjusted 0.025 mg/kg/day subcutaneous dosing in April 2019, alongside her regular oral medications of calcitriol 0.25microg three times daily (TDS), calcium citrate 500 mg TDS for intraluminal oxalate binding, pantoprazole 40 mg daily, prednisolone 5 mg daily, loperamide 4 mg four times daily and subcutaneous denosumab 60 mg 6-monthly. At teduglutide initiation, she weighed 55 kg with a body mass index (BMI) of 21.2 kg/m2, a hand grip strength (HGS) of 23 kg (92 % expected normative value) and a serum creatinine of 186 micromol/L. However, she experienced loss of enteral autonomy 5 months after teduglutide commencement, following a period of antibiotic-related diarrhoea following antibiotic prophylaxis for a basal cell carcinoma excision. Ongoing nutritional instability, driven by recurrent episodes of sepsis and antibiotic-related diarrhoea, resulted in continued PS dependence in the years following. Teduglutide was ceased in February 2021 during an episode of self-limiting nausea, culminating in an increase in weekly PS from 3 to 5–7 nights, with higher needs during intercurrent illness. Attempts to recommence therapy were delayed by further intercurrent illness.

Teduglutide was restarted in March 2022, resulting in a clinically significant teduglutide response (50 % reduction in weekly PS requirements), despite intermittent bouts of sepsis increasing her caloric needs. However throughout this time, there was a progressive deterioration in renal function, thought multifactorial in the setting of hyperoxaluria, recurrent sepsis and fluctuations in volume state. In February 2023, she was electively commenced on thrice weekly haemodialysis. Teduglutide dosing was adjusted to 0.05 mg/kg subcutaneously post-dialysis, following consultation of pharmokinetic data and advice from Takeda Pharmaceuticals. No gastrointestinal side effects or episodes of fluid retention were experienced. To date, despite further episodes of CRBSIs, she continues to maintain a clinically significant teduglutide response after two years on dialysis without deterioration in nutritional state.

Discussion

Renal disease is common in patients on long-term HPN for chronic intestinal failure [1], [3], [4], [5], [6]. Reported prevalence rates from observational studies to date range from 28 % to 56 %, with an early retrospective review reporting a mean annual creatinine clearance decline of 3.5 % [4], [5], [6], [7]. Increasing age, infection rate, hypovolaemic state, reduced residual small intestinal length, and duration on HPN have been variably described as risk factors for reduced kidney function in this cohort [1], [3], [6], [7].

Urologic disease in particular has been recognized as a potential contributor to renal impairment in the long-term HPN population, and is a unique complication occurring in approximately 25 % with SBS and colon-in-continuity [2], [7]. In these patients, unabsorbed fatty acids preferentially bind to intraluminal colonic calcium, leaving a greater fraction of oxalate for colonic absorption [2]. This enteric hyperoxaluria may precipitate in renal tubules and form calcium oxalate stones, contributing to progressive renal dysfunction [2]. At present, whether glucagon-like peptide-2 analogues such as teduglutide can attenuate enteric-driven hyperoxaluria and its renal sequelae remain unknown.

Furthermore, while glucagon-like peptide-2 analogues are well-established in the pharmacological armamentarium for SBS-IF, evidence guiding their use in advanced CKD or dialysis is sparse [8], [9]. Teduglutide is predominantly renally cleared, with product information recommending a 50 % dose reduction for patients with a creatinine clearance < 50 ml/min and end-stage renal disease, although no specifics in the dialysis population are provided [10].

Pharmacokinetic single-dose studies suggest increased parameters with worsening severity of renal impairment [10], [11]. Reduced renal clearance leads to increased systemic exposure, with increased maximum drug plasma concentration (Cmax) of up to 2.1-fold and total drug exposure time (Area under the concentration versus time curve extrapolated to infinity, AUCinf) of 2.6-fold in end-stage renal disease patients compared to healthy subjects [10], [11]. This appears to differ from phase I studies of longer-acting GLP-2 analogues, which do not report a significant increase in drug exposure with renal impairment to warrant dose reduction [12], [13]. In this context, teduglutide administration immediately following haemodialysis may minimize premature drug removal, optimizing exposure and facilitating sustained intestinal GLP-2 receptor activation required for its intestinotrophic effects.

To date, only two published cases describe the use of a GLP-2 analogue in a SBS-IF patient on haemodialysis [14], [15]. The first case received teduglutide dosed at 0.025 mg/kg/day, although specifics regarding this decision were not provided [14]. While nutritional parameters appear to have improved in this patient, a reduction in PS was not achieved [14]. The second case reported a dose of 0.025 mg/kg but did not give further specifics on frequency or timing in relation to dialysis. No improvement in nutritional state occurred, although other factors likely affected teduglutide efficacy, with the patient experiencing progressive multi-organ dysfunction and eventually passing from septic shock [15]. In contrast, our case demonstrates durable clinical benefit with post-dialysis administration of full-dose teduglutide (0.05 mg/kg), with no deterioration overall in PS requirements. This strategy aligns with the drug’s renal clearance profile and avoids premature removal during dialysis [10].

To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating a sustained, clinically significant teduglutide response in a complex SBS-IF patient on haemodialysis, whose nutritional stability has been recurrently challenged by intercurrent illness. Despite this, teduglutide administration has enabled a sustained and significant reduction in PS. Our findings suggest that teduglutide dosed immediately after dialysis may be a pragmatic approach for other clinicians with SBS-IF patients on haemodialysis.
