Bidirectional Longitudinal Associations Between Problematic Social Media Use and Emotional Symptoms in Adolescents: Results From a Random Intercept Cross‐Lagged Panel Model
Authors: Chiara Remondi, Antonino La Tona, Laura Salerno, Rita Aiello, Valeria Donisi, Silvia Poli, Elide Francesca De Caro, Elisa Delvecchio, Agostino Brugnera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionProblematic social media use (PSMU) and emotional symptoms are consistently associated during adolescence; however, the directionality of this relationship remains debated.MethodsThe present study examined the bidirectional longitudinal associations between PSMU and emotional symptoms in a large sample of Italian adolescents using a Random Intercept Cross‐Lagged Panel Model (RI‐CLPM), which disentangles stable between‐person differences from within‐person changes over time. A total of 1562 adolescents (baseline Mage = 16.36 years, 56.7% female) participated in a three‐wave longitudinal study across 1 year and completed self‐report measures of emotional symptoms and PSMU at each wave.ResultsAt the between‐person level, adolescents who consistently reported higher levels of PSMU also reported persistently higher levels of emotional symptoms, indicating a stable dispositional association between the two constructs. At the within‐person level, higher‐than‐usual levels of PSMU were concurrently associated with higher‐than‐usual levels of emotional symptoms. Most notably, significant bidirectional spill‐over effects emerged: within‐person increases in PSMU predicted subsequent increases in emotional symptoms, and increases in emotional symptoms predicted subsequent increases in PSMU. Emotional symptoms also demonstrated significant carry‐over effects across waves.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the importance of disaggregating within‐person and between‐person effects and support the conceptualization of the association between PSMU and emotional symptoms as a dynamic feedback loop during adolescence.

Social media use has become ubiquitous among adolescents, permeating multiple domains of young people's daily lives worldwide. As digital technologies continue to evolve, screen‐based devices increasingly afford a wide range of activities, with social networking sites emerging as one of the most popular forms of engagement among young people. Indeed, platforms such as Instagram, TikTok, and Snapchat are now deeply embedded in adolescents' everyday routines (Keles et al. 2020).

Alongside this widespread use, growing concerns have been raised regarding the association between social media use and adolescents' well‐being. In fact, recent evidence highlights that among teens who report being at least somewhat concerned about the well‐being of young people today, 22% identify social media as the primary contributing factor (Pew Research Center 2025). These concerns are particularly pronounced when social media use becomes excessive or dysregulated.

Problematic social media use (PSMU) refers to a pattern of persistent and compulsive engagement with social media platforms that resembles behavioral addiction and is marked by diminished control over use (van den Eijnden et al. 2016). Empirical evidence has linked PSMU to a range of adverse psychological and behavioral outcomes, including emotional symptoms (e.g., depression and anxiety), sleep disturbances, academic difficulties, and reduced quality of life (see Pazdur et al. 2025 for a review). In particular, adolescents with PSMU consistently report higher levels of depressive and anxious symptoms, with accumulating evidence suggesting that this association may be bidirectional (Lopes et al. 2022).

From one perspective, both theoretical models and empirical evidence suggest that emotional symptoms may precede and contribute to the greater severity of PSMU. According to the Cognitive–Behavioral Model (Davis 2001) and the Compensatory Internet Use Theory (CIUT; Kardefelt‐Winther 2014), individuals experiencing psychological distress may engage excessively with digital media as a maladaptive coping strategy to regulate negative emotions and escape from real‐life problems. In this view, psychosocial maladjustment fosters maladaptive cognitions—such as the belief that Internet use can effectively alleviate one's difficulties—thereby reinforcing excessive online engagement. This perspective has been supported by a substantial body of cross‐sectional research, as well as longitudinal studies examining temporal precedence (e.g., Raudsepp and Kais 2019; Zhou et al. 2023). For instance, in a three‐wave longitudinal study of Chinese undergraduate students, Zhou et al. (2023) found that higher levels of depression and anxiety at earlier time points significantly predicted greater PSMU severity at later assessments, whereas earlier PSMU did not predict subsequent depressive or anxiety symptoms. Similarly, in a two‐wave longitudinal study among Spanish adolescents, Gámez‐Guadix (2014) showed that prior psychological distress constituted a risk factor for the development of problematic Internet use 1 year later.

In contrast, other theoretical frameworks and empirical findings suggest the opposite direction of influence, whereby PSMU may be a key precursor of emotional symptoms (Beard 2005; Kraut et al. 1998). In particular, the Displacement Hypothesis (Kraut et al. 1998) posits that excessive smartphone or Internet use displaces protective activities—such as face‐to‐face social interactions, physical activity, and adequate sleep—that are crucial buffers against emotional distress. By occupying time that would otherwise be spent engaging with family and friends, excessive Internet use may undermine social development and increase the risk of social isolation, depression, and loneliness. Supporting this perspective, Lapierre et al. (2019), in a two‐wave longitudinal study of US adolescents aged 17−20 years, found that smartphone dependency predicted increased depressive symptoms 3 months later, whereas depressive symptoms at baseline did not predict subsequent smartphone dependency. Taken together, individuals with PSMU may develop mental health problems because of their drained coping resources, compromised social functioning, and decreased self‐control.

To date, although it is accepted that the association between PSMU and emotional symptoms may be described as a dynamic feedback loop (e.g., Slater 2015), whereby depressive or anxiety symptoms guide selective exposure to symptom‐relevant content that may exacerbate emotional distress over time, only a limited number of studies have empirically tested this process using appropriate longitudinal models (e.g., Di Blasi et al. 2022; Chang et al. 2022).

To our knowledge, only a few studies have implemented the random intercept cross‐lagged panel model (RI‐CLPM; Hamaker et al. 2015), and findings from these studies have been mixed regarding the directional associations between PSMU and emotional symptoms. For instance, Chang et al. (2022), in a study examining the bidirectional associations between PSMU and psychological distress among university students, found that the association between PSMU and anxiety was reciprocal, such that adolescents with higher levels of PSMU would exhibit higher levels of anxiety and, in turn, higher levels of anxiety would predict higher levels of PSMU over time. However, while higher levels of PSMU predicted increases in depressive symptoms, depressive symptoms did not predict subsequent PSMU. Similarly, in a study examining the bidirectional relationships between PSMU and social anxiety among adolescents, Zhang et al. (2025) demonstrated the mutual predictability between social anxiety and PSMU, such that previous PSMU would heighten subsequent social anxiety, and vice versa. Conversely, in a longitudinal study among adult participants, Di Blasi et al. (2022) found that although adults with higher PSMU consistently exhibited higher levels of psychological distress, no significant bidirectional associations emerged between psychological distress and subsequent changes in PSMU over time. The inconsistent pattern of within‐person effects underscores the need for further longitudinal research in adolescent populations using models capable of disentangling stable between‐person from dynamic within‐person differences.

Therefore, the present study aims to explore the bidirectional longitudinal associations between PSMU and emotional symptoms by implementing the RI‐CLPM to answer three research questions: (1) Are stable (i.e., trait‐like) parts of adolescents' PSMU and emotional symptoms related to each other? (2) Are higher‐than‐expected levels of PSMU associated with higher‐than‐expected levels of emotional symptoms at a given time point? and (3) Are higher‐than‐expected levels of PSMU at a given time point associated with subsequent higher‐than‐expected levels of emotional symptoms, and vice versa?

First, at the between‐person level, we hypothesize that stable individual differences in PSMU will be positively associated with stable individual differences in emotional symptoms. Specifically, adolescents who consistently report higher levels of PSMU across time are expected to also report higher levels of psychological distress, and vice versa.

Second, given the mixed evidence regarding longitudinal within‐person associations between PSMU and emotional symptoms, we explore whether within‐person increases in emotional symptoms predict subsequent within‐person increases in PSMU, and whether within‐person increases in PSMU predict subsequent within‐person increases in emotional symptoms.

Most participants were born in Italy (93.7%, n = 1282). Most adolescents also reported that their parents were born in Italy (77.9% of mothers and 81.1% of fathers), indicating that the sample predominantly comprised youth without a migration background.

Regarding social media habits at baseline, TikTok emerged as the platform most frequently used by participants (49.6%, n = 678), followed by Instagram (41.3%, n = 564), other platforms (e.g., Twitch; 6.4%, n = 87), Snapchat (0.4%, n = 6), Facebook (0.4%, n = 6), and Twitter (0.4%, n = 5). Finally, 20 participants self‐reported a very limited use of social media (1.5%).

The study was conducted in accordance with ethical standards and was approved by the regional ethics committee of Umbria, Italy (Prot. N. 27844/23/AV, November 28, 2023). Written informed consent was obtained from both parents and/or legal guardians prior to adolescents' participation; participants aged 18 provided their own consent.

Measures

Emotional Symptoms

The Italian version of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ; Essau et al. 2012; Goodman 1997) is a 25‐item self‐report of strengths and difficulties in adolescents' psychological adjustment. The present study focused on the Emotional Symptoms subscale (5 items), which measures anxiety, depressive symptoms, fears, and somatic complaints (e.g., I am often unhappy, depressed, or tearful). Each item uses a 3‐point Likert‐type scale (from 0 = not true, to 2 = certainly true), with higher summed scores indicating more severe emotional symptoms. In the current study, the internal consistency of the scale was good at all time points (T0 ω = 0.75; T1 ω = 0.77; T2 ω = 0.77).

PSMU

The Italian version of the Bergen Social Media Addiction Scale (BSMAS; Monacis et al. 2017; Andreassen et al. 2016) is a 6‐item self‐report measure assessing six core components of addiction (i.e., salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict, and relapse) related to social media use during the past year (e.g., You use social media in order to forget about personal problems). Items are rated on a 5‐point Likert‐type scale (from 1 = very rarely, to 5 = very often), with higher summed scores reflecting greater PSMU. In the current study, internal consistency was good across all time points (T0 ω = 0.75; T1 ω = 0.76; T2 ω = 0.78).

Procedure

Data were collected from December 2023 to February 2025 as part of a larger longitudinal study investigating risk and protective factors of PSMU among Italian mid‐adolescents (Donisi et al. 2024). Participants completed an online survey during school hours at three time points, with the following median administration dates: T0 (December 2023), T1 (June 2024), and T2 (December 2024).

Researchers administered the online survey in classroom settings. Inclusion criteria were: (i) age between 14 and 18 years, and (ii) adequate comprehension of spoken and written Italian. Participants did not receive any compensation or incentives for taking part in the study.

Statistical Analysis

We first tested the data for univariate normality and outliers and examined descriptive statistics and zero‐order correlations among all study variables.

Bidirectional associations between PSMU and emotional symptoms were examined using a RI‐CLPM based on observed scale scores. Models were estimated using Maximum Likelihood with standard errors robust to non‐normality, with missing data handled through full information maximum likelihood (FIML). Good model fit was evaluated using the following criteria: a root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) ≤ 0.06; a comparative fit index (CFI) and a Tucker‐Lewis index (TLI) ≥ 0.95, and a standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) ≤ 0.05 (Hu and Bentler 1999).

Although the model was based on observed variables, the RI‐CLPM specifies latent random intercepts and latent within‐person components, thereby disentangling the between‐person (trait‐like) and within‐person (state‐like) associations between emotional symptoms and PSMU (Hamaker et al. 2015). At the between‐person level, the association between PSMU and emotional symptoms represents the dispositional association between the two variables (e.g., do adolescents with higher PSMU, in general, report higher emotional symptoms?). At the within‐person level, the effects refer to the relations between PSMU and emotional symptoms in terms of deviations from the person's mean score (Hamaker et al. 2015). Specifically, the within‐person effects capture three types of relations: (a) simultaneous, time‐specific associations, which reflect whether higher‐than‐usual levels of PSMU are related to higher‐than‐usual levels of emotional symptoms at the same time point; (b) carry‐over effects, which reflect whether higher‐than‐usual levels of a construct at one time point predict higher‐than‐usual levels of the same construct at the subsequent time point; and (c) spill‐over effects, which reflect whether higher‐than‐usual levels of one construct at one time point predict higher‐than‐usual levels of the other construct at the subsequent time point.

Two RI‐CLPMs were estimated: one allowing autoregressive and cross‐lagged paths to vary freely over time, and one constraining these paths to equality across time points. Following Hamaker et al. (2015), the constrained model was preferred over the unconstrained model when imposing equality constraints did not significantly worsen model fit, as it provided a more parsimonious representation of the longitudinal associations. Nested models were compared using Satorra−Bentler scaled χ 2 difference tests. At each time point, both emotional symptoms and PSMU were regressed on biological sex to control for its confounding effects.

All analyses were conducted using Mplus 8.11 (Muthén and Muthén 2024) and SPSS 29.0.1 (IBM Corp 2023). Effect sizes, including those for cross‐lagged effects, were interpreted according to guidelines (Orth et al. 2024). All tests were two‐sided, and a p < 0.05 was deemed significant.

At the between‐person level, a significant cross‐time association was found between PSMU and emotional symptoms, suggesting that adolescents who consistently reported higher levels of PSMU also tended to report persistently higher levels of emotional symptoms (r = 0.36, p < 0.001).

At the within‐person level, PSMU was significantly associated with emotional symptoms at each time point (T0, r = 0.24, p = 0.001; T1, r = 0.27, p < 0.001; and T2, r = 0.32, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that adolescents reporting higher‐than‐usual levels of PSMU at a given time point also tended to report higher‐than‐usual emotional symptoms at the same time point.

Carry‐over effects were significant only for emotional symptoms, indicating that adolescents with higher‐than‐expected levels of emotional symptoms at a given time point were also more likely to report elevated emotional symptoms at the subsequent time point, reflecting a greater persistence of emotional distress over time (T0 to T1, β = 0.24, p < 0.001; T1 to T2, β = 0.25, p < 0.001). In contrast, carry‐over effects for PSMU were not statistically significant, suggesting that fluctuations in PSMU may be more transient and less enduring than emotional symptoms (T0 to T1, β = 0.15, p = 0.056; T1 to T2, β = 0.15, p = 0.068).

Finally, significant bidirectional spill‐over effects emerged between PSMU and emotional symptoms. Greater PSMU at T0 predicted greater emotional symptoms at T1 (β = 0.16, p = 0.005), and greater PSMU at T1 predicted greater emotional symptoms at T2 (β = 0.16, p = 0.004). In turn, greater emotional symptoms at T0 predicted subsequent increases in PSMU at T1 (β = 0.18, p = 0.001), and greater emotional symptoms at T1 predicted greater PSMU at T2 (β = 0.19, p = 0.002). These effects were large in magnitude (Orth et al. 2024) and suggest the presence of a reciprocal process whereby increases in PSMU and emotional symptoms mutually reinforce one another over time.

Discussion

Given prior considerations regarding the bidirectional relations between PSMU and emotional symptoms (Slater 2015), the present study extended previous work by providing a longitudinal analysis of the between‐ and within‐person associations of PSMU and emotional symptoms in a large sample of Italian adolescents using a RI‐CLPM. Overall, our results indicated a bidirectional association between PSMU and emotional symptoms over time, suggesting that increases in adolescents' PSMU predicted increases in emotional symptoms, and, reciprocally, increases in emotional symptoms predicted increases in PSMU over time.

First, in accordance with Hypothesis 1, we found a significant positive association between PSMU and emotional symptoms at the between‐person level, indicating that adolescents who consistently report higher levels of PSMU across time also tend to report higher levels of emotional distress. Our results on the trait‐like association between PSMU and emotional symptoms are in line with prior longitudinal (Di Blasi et al. 2022) and meta‐analytic findings (Huang 2022), suggesting that individuals experiencing persistent psychological distress may be more prone to engage in excessive online behaviors as a maladaptive coping strategy. From this perspective, PSMU may represent a relatively stable behavioral expression of broader psychosocial maladjustment, whereas emotional symptoms reflect enduring affective vulnerabilities that co‐occur with, and potentially contribute to, the maintenance of dysregulated social media use. In fact, although engagement with social media may initially serve adaptive functions for adolescents, such as fostering social connection, repeated reliance on these platforms for regulating affect and emotions may, over time, undermine the use of adaptive coping strategies. As adolescents increasingly turn to social media to manage negative emotions, this pattern of use may become reinforced, thereby increasing the risk of developing problematic or compulsive engagement (Gámez‐Guadix 2014; Kim et al. 2009). Importantly, the between‐person association should not be interpreted as evidence of causal directionality at the trait level, but rather as an indication that stable emotional symptoms and stable problematic social media engagement tend to co‐occur as part of a pattern of psychosocial maladjustment.

Most notably, at the within‐person level, consistent with Hypothesis 2, the present study found evidence of bidirectional spill‐over effects between PSMU and emotional symptoms. Higher‐than‐usual levels of PSMU at one point predicted subsequent increases in emotional symptoms, and, reciprocally, higher‐than‐usual levels of emotional symptoms predicted subsequent increases in PSMU. These reciprocal effects were observed consistently across waves and suggest that fluctuations in problematic social media engagement are meaningfully linked to fluctuations in emotional functioning beyond stable predispositions. This reciprocal pattern provides empirical support for conceptualizing the association between PSMU and emotional symptoms as a dynamic feedback loop, consistent with bidirectional theoretical frameworks, such as the Reinforcing Spirals Model (Slater 2015), which proposes that depressed or anxious individuals would selectively seek content that may reinforce their symptoms, and that depressive or anxiety‐inducing content could in turn further intensify emotional symptoms. In other words, our findings suggest the presence of a bidirectional process whereby adolescents experiencing elevated emotional symptoms may turn to online environments to regulate negative affect, alleviate feelings of anxiety, or temporarily escape distressing thoughts and emotions (Nesi et al. 2018). At the same time, excessive or problematic engagement with social media may further exacerbate emotional difficulties, as social media environments can also represent a source of stress through negative social interactions, social comparison, and communication overload (Vannucci et al. 2017).

Interestingly, while PSMU did not show significant temporal stability at the within‐person level, emotional symptoms displayed significant carry‐over effects. In other words, adolescents with higher‐than‐expected levels of emotional symptoms at a given time point also tended to report higher‐than‐expected levels of emotional symptoms at the subsequent time point. A possible explanation for the observed pattern of findings may lie in the developmental characteristics of adolescence. This period is marked by heightened emotion dysregulation (Beauchaine and Hinshaw 2017) and increased negative emotionality (Sallquist et al. 2009), reflecting the profound biological, cognitive, social, and emotional transformations that occur during this stage (Lerner and Steinberg 2009; Silk et al. 2003). As regulatory capacities are still consolidating, adolescents may experience greater emotional reactivity and reduced ability to effectively modulate distress. Within this context, the significant carry‐over effects observed for emotional symptoms suggest that deviations in distress tend to persist over time, rather than dissipate quickly. In contrast, the non‐significant carry‐over effects of PSMU suggest that increases in PSMU may be less likely to persist across measurement occasions. One possible explanation is that adolescents' problematic engagement with social media may be more strongly influenced by transient contextual and situational factors, such as peer dynamics, shifts in emotional well‐being, or changing patterns of online activities (Pouwels et al. 2025). As a result, deviations in PSMU may be more episodic over time, rather than reflecting a stable pattern that consistently carries over from one assessment to the next.

Strengths, Limitations, and Future Directions

The current study contributes to the field by using a large community sample of adolescents and an RI‐CLPM to disentangle stable between‐person differences from within‐person dynamics in the association between PSMU and emotional symptoms. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, all constructs were assessed through adolescents' self‐reports, which may have inflated associations due to shared method variance. Although reliability was adequate, future studies should combine self‐report measures with other forms of assessment, such as parent or peer reports. Second, the present study did not account for other personal and contextual factors that may shape the longitudinal association between emotional symptoms and PSMU. As highlighted in recent systematic reviews (e.g., Poli et al. 2025), PSMU is embedded within a broader constellation of intrapersonal (e.g., attachment, self‐control, personality traits) and interpersonal (e.g., peer and family relationships) susceptibility factors. Future studies should therefore include these variables to better capture the complexity of the developmental processes underlying the association between emotional symptoms and PSMU. Third, emotional symptoms were assessed using a multi‐item scale encompassing heterogeneous dimensions, including anxiety, depressive symptoms, fears, and somatic complaints. While this approach captures general emotional distress, it does not allow for the examination of potentially distinct longitudinal pathways for specific symptom domains. Future studies should therefore examine specific symptom domains separately (e.g., anxiety, depressive symptoms, somatic complaints), as each may show distinct longitudinal associations with PSMU in terms of strength, directionality, or underlying mechanisms. Finally, although the longitudinal design spanned 1 year, adolescence is a prolonged developmental period characterized by substantial changes in autonomy, peer orientation, and risk‐taking behaviors. Future research should extend the application of RI‐CLPM models across broader developmental transitions, including the shift from mid‐ to late adolescence, to determine whether reciprocal dynamics between emotional symptoms and problematic digital behaviors strengthen, weaken, or change in nature over time.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide useful practical insights. Specifically, our findings suggest that interventions aimed at reducing PSMU during adolescence should not only focus on PSMU but also promote adolescents' emotional well‐being. In line with this perspective, school‐based interventions targeting adolescents' emotional self‐regulatory abilities have shown promising results in reducing problematic smartphone and social network use (Favini et al. 2024). Moreover, parents, educators, clinicians, and policymakers should consider adolescents' emotional well‐being as a key target for the prevention of PSMU.

Conclusion

Overall, the present study examined the longitudinal interplay between PSMU and emotional symptoms during adolescence using a RI‐CLPM. The findings showed that these constructs are associated at both the between‐person and within‐person levels, with reciprocal within‐person effects over time. These findings suggest that PSMU and emotional symptoms operate within a dynamic feedback process during adolescence, whereby increases in one domain contribute to subsequent increases in the other.

From a theoretical perspective, the findings indicate that the association between these constructs unfolds through both stable and dynamic processes. At the between‐person level, adolescents who consistently exhibit higher levels of PSMU also display persistently elevated emotional distress, suggesting a stable co‐occurrence of behavioral and affective vulnerability. Moreover, at the within‐person level, fluctuations in one domain prospectively predict changes in the other, supporting the existence of a reciprocal feedback process between PSMU and emotional symptoms.

From a practical point of view, our findings demonstrate that intervention programs may benefit from integrated approaches that promote adolescents' emotional well‐being alongside healthier patterns of social media engagement.
