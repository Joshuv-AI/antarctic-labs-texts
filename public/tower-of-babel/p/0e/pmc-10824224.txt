Opioid Use and Disposal Patterns of Emergency Department Patients
Authors: Valerie Hoerster, Derek Tang, Marlee Milkis, Stephanie Litzenberger, Jill Stoltzfus, Holly Stankewicz
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction:To date, there is limited literature to guide emergency providers (EPs) on the proper dosing of prescription opioids. Our study aims to assess the self-reported opioid use, storage, and disposal practices of patients presenting to the emergency department (ED) with acute pain.Methods:This prospective cohort study employed a validated, cross-sectional survey of subjects identified using electronic medical records. The survey link was e-mailed to a continuous sample of eligible participants 3–4 weeks following ED discharge. Nonrespondents were surveyed through telephone after 1 week. We used descriptive and nonparametric statistics to report survey results.Results:Of 500 eligible subjects, 97 completed the questionnaire. Only 28% of respondents reported that they took all of the prescribed pills. Of the remaining responses, 20% stated that they did not take any pills, 33% took about one-fourth, 7.2% took about half, and 12.4% took about three-fourths of the pills. Among those who did not take any pills, 42% filled the prescription. Most (71.2%) reported storing their leftover pills; among those who stored their pills, less than one-fourth (23.8%) used a locked storage location.Conclusions:Our findings suggest that less than one-third of patients who receive prescriptions in the ED for acute pain use all of their prescribed pills, suggesting that many patients are unnecessarily prescribed opioids for acute conditions. The findings of this study also suggest that many patients with unused prescription opioids do not practice safe storage or proper disposal of leftover pills. This represents a potential opportunity for EPs to improve medication safety by educating patients on proper storage and disposal practices. Limitations include low response rate and the use of self-reporting.

INTRODUCTION

Prescription opioid analgesics represent a cornerstone of acute pain management. However, patients treated with opioids are at risk for serious harm including tolerance, dependence, addiction, and overdose. Studies of postoperative patients have shown that opioids are significantly oversupplied and that this population commonly practices unsafe techniques for the storage and disposal of leftover opioid medications.[1234567] Prior studies examining opioid storage and disposal practices among emergency department (ED) patients, while limited have shown similarly poor rates of proper storage and disposal techniques.[89] Overprescribed and improperly stored opioids represent a possible source of pills that can contribute to diversion, overdose, and other forms of misuse.[1011] The public health burden imposed by opioid-associated morbidity and mortality has reached epidemic proportions and has become a prevalent topic of discussion within medicine and popular media.[121314]

Pain is a common symptom of patients seen in the ED, with most painful complaints being acute as opposed to chronic.[1516] Although the contribution of emergency providers (EPs) with respect to cumulative opioid prescriptions in the U.S. is relatively low when compared with other medical specialties, there is wide variability in prescribing practices in the ED setting.[171819] In addition, higher-intensity prescribing from providers has been linked to increased long-term opioid use, especially in individuals who had not previously filled opioid prescriptions.[2021] This variability may be in part due to a lack of evidence-based guidance on the ideal dose and duration of analgesic treatment for acutely painful conditions in a high-throughput setting. Consequently, many EPs may depend heavily on personal experience to guide prescribing decisions. Research that describes opioid use practices of ED patients could provide EPs with objective data to assist them in prescribing the minimum necessary dose of opioids.

The objective of this investigation was to elucidate self-reported patterns of opioid use after discharge from the ED with an opioid prescription for acute pain. Specifically, we sought to determine what proportion of their prescribed pills patients reported having taken, reasons for early cessation of the medication, and the adequacy of analgesia during the postdischarge period. We also examined modes of home storage and disposal of the prescribed opioids.

METHODS

Study design and setting

We conducted a survey of eligible patients discharged from either of two networks of EDs-one at an academic hospital with an emergency medicine (EM) residency and the other at a community hospital without an EM residency. The study was Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved. Potential participants were identified by periodically searching the electronic medical record (EMR) of all visits at the two EDs from June 1, 2018, to July 10, 2018. Based on response rates for other similar survey studies, we estimated that we would need to send survey requests to approximately 500 consecutive subjects to meet our goal of receiving at least 100 completed surveys for data analysis.[15] We screened for eligibility and administered surveys on a continuous basis. Data of eligible subjects were collected using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap™), a secure and HIPAA-compliant online database.[22] With the exception of two study team members and one IRB board member who were employed as physicians in one of the two study sites, EPs were not informed that the study was taking place before or during the data collection phase. Although potential subjects were identified prospectively, they were not informed of the study before the follow-up interval.

Participant selection, recruitment, and consent

Eligible participants included English-speaking patients aged 18–89 who received an opioid prescription upon discharge from the ED. Exclusion criteria included admission to the hospital; prior or current EMR diagnosis of opioid dependence, dementia, or intellectual disability; associated diagnosis of cancer, opioid withdrawal, or postoperative pain; or EMR record of a prior opioid prescription within the previous 30 days. Participants had to have either an e-mail address or phone number listed on their medical record. For those with an e-mail in their EMR, a link for the survey was sent 3–4 weeks after their discharge date. For those with only a telephone number listed, a study team member called 3–4 weeks after discharge to administer the survey over the phone. Nonrespondents were contacted again through telephone 1 week after the initial attempt. The e-mail body contained a greeting, introductory statement, and electronic survey link that opened to a statement of informed consent [Supporting Information Figures 1 and 2]. For phone surveys, verbal consent was obtained after an initial scripted greeting [Supporting Information Figure 3]. There was no financial compensation offered for study participation.

Data collection

Potential subjects were identified using an automated search of the EMR, which was performed periodically. The generated lists were further screened for eligibility by study personnel. Information was collected from the EMR onto REDCap™ for each eligible patient encounter immediately before the initial attempt to contact the subject for survey administration. Specifically, we collected demographic information (subject age, gender, and race); encounter information (campus name, primary ED diagnosis, and initial ED pain scale); and prescription information (name of opioid, the dose of opioid, number of doses prescribed, and number of refills allowed). We also collected details of any subsequent opioid prescriptions the subject had received during the data collection period.

Survey instrument

Due to limited prior research on this topic, a validated survey tool for opioid use and disposal of ED patients was not available. Researchers Bartels et al. has developed a survey tool for use in a postsurgical population.[1] Their survey was modified for use in an ED population with guidance from pain management experts. The revised survey was then administered to a cohort of medical and nonmedical individuals outside of the study team who provided additional feedback. The final survey includes questions pertaining to the proportion of prescribed opioids taken, analgesia during the postdischarge period, reasons for not taking the prescribed pills, and storage and disposal practices [Supporting Information Figure 4]. An electronic version with survey logic was created using the secure REDCap™ online system; answers were submitted directly into the REDCap™ database through the link by e-mail respondents or by study personnel over the phone.

Data analysis

To make the survey easier to complete and minimize recall errors, survey response options for the question, “How many of the pills did you take?” were simplified into quartiles [Supporting Information Figure 4]. To account for the distinct potency of different opioids, doses were converted to oral morphine equivalents using established conversion factors.[23] To evaluate for nonresponse bias, we compared the demographic characteristics of respondents and nonrespondents; for the variable “race,” data for non-Caucasian groups were combined due to limited sample sizes for non-White groups. We used SPSS version 25 to analyze our data (Armonk, NY, USA: IBM Corp.). We reported frequencies and percentages for categorical data and medians and ranges for the skewed continuous data. We compared categorical outcomes using separate Chi-square tests and compared skewed continuous outcomes using separate Mann–Whitney U-tests. For all analyses, P < 0.05 denotes statistical significance, with no adjustment for multiple testing.

Prescribing patterns

For the 500 consecutive eligible subjects, a range of 2–40 pills was prescribed, with a mean of 13.5 pills and a mode of 12 pills. The morphine milligram equivalent (MME) prescribed ranged from 15 to 450 (mean 101; mode 150). Prescribing patterns between the two study sites varied: the EPs at the academic ED prescribed significantly fewer pills and MMEs than the EPs at the community ED (P < 0.0001).[23] Oxycodone–acetaminophen was the most commonly prescribed medication. According to EMRs, a subsequent prescription for opioid analgesics had been given at the time of data collection (3–4 weeks after the initial encounter) to 48 out of 500 subjects (9.6%). There was no significant difference in prescribing patterns for respondents versus nonrespondents.

Reported opioid use

Only 28% of subjects reported that they took all of the prescribed pills [Figure 2]. Twenty percentage stated that they did not take any pills and among those who did not take any pills, 58% did not fill the prescription. Reasons for not taking any pills included: “pain was controlled without taking any pills” (68%), “concern about side effects” (16%), “concern about developing addiction or dependence” (11%), and “insurance/payment issues” (5%). Among those who took some but not all prescribed pills, about half (52%) reported that they did not use all of the pills because their pain was adequately controlled, 24% were concerned about dependence or addiction, 17% stopped because side effects were too severe, and 6% did not feel that the pills helped their pain [Figure 3]. Sixty-six percentage of respondents reported that they had used nonopioid analgesics to treat their pain. Prescribed pill counts ranged from 3 to 30 (20–300 morphine equivalents); there was no clear association between the number of pills/morphine equivalents and the proportion of pills taken [Figure 4]. Twenty-four percentage reported that they had received an additional opioid prescription within the follow-up time frame (3–5 weeks after the ED visit).

Storage and disposal

Fifty-seven subjects answered questions about storage and disposal practices [Figure 5]. Most (71%) reported storing their leftover pills and among those who stored their pills, less than one-fourth (24%) reported that the storage location was locked. Subjects who disposed of their pills most often did so in a sink or toilet (54%). Medications were returned to an authorized collection site by 31% of subjects. One subject disposed of pills in cat litter and another could not recall the disposal method. None reported disposing of pills in household garbage.

DISCUSSION

The results of our study indicate that ED patients are commonly prescribed more opioid pills than are needed. EPs with similar prescribing practices to those in our study should consider decreasing the number of pills they prescribe to treat patients’ acute pain episodes. Further objective information is still needed to guide EPs in prescribing opioids. A similar study with a larger sample size, if conducted, may be able to describe the typical minimum dose needed to achieve adequate pain control for various acutely painful conditions seen in the emergency department, such as ureteral colic or long bone fractures. Although such research is likely to be confounded by the high degree of heterogeneity within this clinical setting. Given the current body of evidence, EPs must continue to rely heavily on their professional experience in making these decisions.

Our study also indicates that opioids are often stored and disposed of incorrectly. When writing opioid prescriptions, EPs should educate their patients in safe storage and disposal practices. Prescribed medications should be stored in a locked location, out of reach of children. Unused opioids should be disposed of; the preferred method is through a community drug-disposal program. If patients do not have access to one of these programs, they should dispose of medications in the trash only after making them unusable by adding water, cat litter, bleach, or other appropriate substances.[24]

Our study had several important limitations. First, self-reported practices may not reflect actual practices. The median age of respondents was higher than of nonrespondents, so our survey population is not a representative sample of our intended study population. In addition, we had a relatively low response rate, further introducing the possibility of nonresponse bias. Second, our follow-up time frame of 3–4 weeks may have missed data for subjects who were still taking the pills or introduced recall bias for patients when remembering how many pills they took. Third, our study was not sufficiently powered to detect differences in practices among demographic groups or for specific primary diagnoses.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, more than two-thirds of subjects in this cross-sectional survey of patients discharged from two EDs in a single health network with an opioid prescription for acute pain reported unused prescription opioids. Most patients who discontinued the use of their prescribed opioids did so because their pain was adequately controlled. On the other hand, almost one-fourth of patients reported that they had received an additional opioid prescription within the follow-up time frame after their ED discharge. Furthermore, less than one-fourth of patients practice safe storage techniques. Overall, our findings are similar to those reported in the postsurgical cohort.[1234567]
