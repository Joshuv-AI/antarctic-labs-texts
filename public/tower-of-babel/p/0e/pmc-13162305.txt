Australian Rural Surgical Workforce: A Policy Analysis
Authors: Gavin J. Carmichael, Tyler Tranquille, Vanessa Di Tano, Annas Al‐Sharea, Joshua G. Kovoor, Mathew O. Jacob
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo analyse why rural health workforce initiatives have not delivered sustainable surgical services in rural and regional Australia, and to identify system‐level policy changes that could improve access.ContextNearly one‐third of Australians live in rural or remote areas, however rural hospitals face shortages of general and specialist surgeons, have dependence on locums, and frequently use inter‐hospital transfers. Despite national strategies aimed at rural workforce equity, surgical services remain highly variable by location and face difficulty in sustaining them.SourcesThis analysis draws on federal and state workforce strategies, national policy documents including the National Medical Workforce Strategy and Stronger Rural Health Strategy, accreditation and workforce governance reviews from the Australian Government, published statements from professional bodies including the Royal Australasian College of Surgeons and the Australian Medical Association, and peer‐reviewed literature on rural surgery, training pathways, and service delivery.ApproachWe applied Walt and Gilson's health policy triangle (actors, content, context and process) to map power, accountability, and implementation pathways relevant to the rural surgical workforce. Actor influence and interest were further examined using a power–interest matrix.ConclusionsResponsibility for training, accreditation, funding, and service delivery is fragmented across federal, state, and professional actors, with no mechanism to enforce or coordinate rural surgical service provision. Current reforms rely on voluntary uptake by local champions. We propose three policy shifts: reframing rural surgical access around service delivery, a national rural surgical workforce authority, and a rural surgical data and accountability framework.

Introduction

Australia's vast geography and demographic inequalities create inherent barriers to equitable surgical care. Approximately 29% of the population resides in rural and remote regions, which tend to be older, experience higher comorbidity burdens, and have lower socioeconomic status compared to metropolitan populations [1]. These communities report increased modifiable risk behaviours and face systemic access limitations, which contribute to poorer health outcomes, including reduced life expectancy [1, 2].

Access to surgical care in these areas is especially constrained. Rural hospitals are typically staffed by a small and shrinking number of generalist surgeons, many of whom operate under significant workload pressures and broader scopes of practice [3, 4]. Highly subspecialised services (e.g., neurosurgery, vascular surgery) are appropriately concentrated in higher‐capability centres in most health systems [5]. The policy challenge is therefore not that every specialty is locally available, but that rural and regional networks can reliably deliver a core scope of surgical services close to home, with safe escalation, referral, and timely transfer pathways for complex care [6]. To maintain service continuity, many hospitals rely on temporary “locum” surgeons to cover staff leave in absence of internal cover. The high cost of locums is primarily absorbed by state‐funded rural hospitals. This diverts already‐limited budgets away from infrastructure upgrades, recruitment of permanent staff, and investment in services.

State‐based perioperative and surgical capability frameworks increasingly define which procedures are appropriate at different hospital capability levels. These frameworks acknowledge that sustainable rural surgical systems depend not only on workforce supply but also on aligning service scope with infrastructure, anaesthesia, critical care capability, and transfer pathways.

Policy and institutional responses over the last couple of decades have attempted to address the disparities. Some federal initiatives have been developed such as the Stronger Rural Health Strategy (SRHS) and National Medical Workforce Strategy 2021–2031 (NMWS). These initiatives have highlighted rural workforce distribution and expansion of generalist surgical training [7, 8]. The Royal Australasian College of Surgeons (RACS) has introduced dedicated rural selection and training policies under its Rural Health Equity Strategic Action Plan [9]. Despite this, clear improvements in access for surgical staff remain limited, with policies often operating in silos and lacking interjurisdictional coordination or accountability.

This analysis examines the root causes of rural surgical workforce disparities using Walt and Gilson's health policy triangle framework [10]. It examines where and why policy stalls, across actors, content, context, and process, and proposes recommendations to strengthen coordination, accountability, and access to surgical care in rural and regional Australia.

Observations and Outcomes

Policy Actors

Australia's rural surgical workforce policy is shaped by a complex and overlapping array of stakeholders, spanning federal, state, professional, and community domains. At the federal level, the Department of Health, Disability and Ageing (DHDA) sets broad workforce policy directions and funds major initiatives, including the SRHS and the Specialist Training Program [7, 12]. State and territory governments are responsible for the direct delivery of surgical care, including hospital management, staffing, and infrastructure investment. This structural separation between policy funding and service provision creates enduring governance challenges, particularly in rural workforce planning [13].

The DHDA also houses the Office of the National Rural Health Commissioner (ONRHC). The ONRHC is a statutory body established to provide policy advice and promote rural health innovation [14]. Its role is to advocate for rural workforce needs, engage stakeholders, and offer recommendations to the Federal Minister for Health.

Professional bodies, particularly RACS, are central to shaping the surgical workforce. Within RACS, the Board of Education and Training sets national standards, while training delivery occurs through specialty‐specific training boards. These boards hold the operational regulatory power to manage the program, conduct accreditation visits, assess supervision, facilities, and case‐mix, and may withdraw accreditation if standards are not met. Consequently, rural hospitals seeking trainees must meet accreditation requirements despite limited infrastructure.

Initiatives such as the Rural Health Equity Strategy, Select for Rural, Train for Rural, and Retain for Rural [9], were designed by RACS to address the geographical maldistribution of surgeons. However, it is largely advisory; while RACS influences workforce distribution through training and credentialing, it does not control the funding or location of training posts. Currently, 29% of Australians reside in rural areas, fewer than 12% of RACS Fellows are rurally based, with some specialties reporting less than 5% rural representation [9].

At the state level, a rural workforce agency operates (e.g., NSW Rural Doctors Network, Rural Workforce Agency Victoria) which provides recruitment, and support services for rural clinicians [15]. While these agencies liaise with local communities, state and national governments, their influence is limited and depends heavily on short‐term contracts and variable state and federal cooperation.

Primary Health Networks (PHNs) are also actors with relevance to rural surgical services. PHNs are federally funded, regionally based organisations responsible for improving the effectiveness and coordination of primary care services. Their core functions are in population health planning and improving service integration. Although their focus is primarily on general practice, chronic disease management, and preventive care, PHNs can play an important indirect role in rural surgical care. This can be achieved through care coordination, system navigation, and post‐operative follow‐up in the community. The PHN Performance and Quality Framework is a framework that defines objectives such as commissioning for health equity and improving service integration. It also includes principles that, if strategically aligned, could support rural surgical systems [16]. However, PHNs' involvement in rural surgery remains inconsistently developed across jurisdictions, and there are few formal mechanisms linking PHN planning with regional surgical capacity or specialist workforce development.

Local stakeholders, including hospital boards, executive leadership, and medical staff groups, are central to the governance and operational management of rural surgical services and therefore hold substantial implementation authority. Even when policy intent and resourcing originate externally (e.g., via federal funding streams or grant programs), translation into practice is contingent on local decision‐making regarding prioritisation, feasibility, and integration into existing service models. In this sense, external funding constitutes an enabling condition rather than a guarantee of delivery.

Some other actors include professional groups like the AMA, specialty societies (e.g., General Surgeons Australia), and rural health advocacy bodies such as the NRHA and Rural Doctors Association of Australia (RDAA). They are big advocacy bodies for equitable rural service provision. For example, AMA has called for an independent national workforce planning agency and governance framework to promote rural health systems [17, 18].

Policy Content

Regarding content at the national level, the NMWS outlines a 10‐year plan across five priority areas. These are: improving generalist capability, increasing flexibility, supporting Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health, enhancing rural workforce planning, and building collaborative models [8]. The strategy lists 25 actions, including data systems [19], revised training pathways, and rural‐origin student support, representing important progress for rural workforce reform. However, its scope is broad, with few actions tailored to surgical specialties.

The SRHS is more explicitly rural‐focused. It tries to fix the maldistribution by delivering 3000 additional doctors and nurses to rural areas [2]. It is structured around a “Teach, Train, Recruit, Retain” framework and supports the expansion of rural clinical schools, bonded medical programs and reforms to Medicare to further support rural doctors. However, beyond a 2016 Joint Workshop recommendation for flexible rural general surgery training, surgical care is largely absent from its key initiatives.

The federal government has also invested in several financial and policy programs to support rural health. The Workforce Incentive Program (WIP) [20] provides incentive payments to doctors working in areas of maldistribution. Its aim is to improve access to primary health services in underserviced areas. The Rural Health Multidisciplinary Training (RHMT) [21] program funds rural clinical schools and university departments of rural health and requires that at least 25% of medical students undertake structured rural placements to strengthen future rural recruitment. The Health Workforce Data Strategy and HeaDS UPP (Health Demand and Supply Utilisation Patterns Planning) [19] tool provides national workforce mapping and modelling to inform distribution policy and training investment.

State and territory governments have implemented some workforce strategies designed to support rural health services. For instance, the Victorian Health Workforce Strategy 2022–2032 [22]. This strategy highlights a commitment to increasing rural placements by 20% by 2034 and addressing challenges such as professional isolation and limited career progression. It also recognises the role of metropolitan clinicians who engage in outreach models. There are 29 full‐time equivalent surgeons employed in rural Victoria as of 2023 [23]. Other states, including NSW, QLD and WA, operate regional training hubs, rural procedural grants, and doctor retention schemes.

Professional colleges, such as RACS, have introduced rural‐specific initiatives. The Rural Health Equity Strategy integrates rural workforce support into its training, selection, and retention [9]. “Select for Rural” offers additional selection points for rural‐background applicants [9], “Train for Rural” promotes rural rotations and curricula [9], and “Retain for Rural” focuses on clinician support [9]. The college has also developed a rural surgery curriculum framework, supported by government‐funded Specialist Training Program (STP) posts and recommended tailored online modules for rural surgical decision‐making [24].

Policy Context

Rural areas incorporate large geographic zones with diffuse populations. This presents logistical barriers to establishing consistent, high‐volume surgical services. Further, rural populations experience higher rates of chronic disease, lower life expectancy, and socioeconomic disadvantage [1, 2]. Despite multiple commissions, parliamentary inquiries, and state‐level reviews, policy continuity is inconsistent. Strategies like the National Strategic Framework for Rural and Remote Health, endorsed in 2011, have laid out shared priorities, access, models of care, and workforce distribution. However, it lacks regulatory force or authority [25]. In recent years, COVID‐19 has exacerbated existing fragilities within our rural healthcare system, with clinic closures, workforce burnout, and disrupted locum support. This has further revealed the vulnerability of already‐stretched rural surgical services [26, 27].

Federalism compounds these challenges by entrenching differing priorities within Australia's health system. The federal government's agenda centres around national workforce distribution, medical education, and training program expansion. State and territory governments prioritise service continuity, hospital efficiency, and immediate staffing needs. Although intergovernmental health agreements aim to align these objectives, discrepancies persist between these jurisdictions.

At the same time, stakeholder expectations, even despite continued advocacy, are rarely met by policy responses. Rural communities and patients seek timely, local access to safe surgical care, however they face long travel distances and delayed procedures due to workforce shortages [28]. Surgeons expect manageable hours, adequate hospital infrastructure and multidisciplinary support [29]; while colleges want sufficient accredited training posts. However, policy solutions have focused prioritised generalist models or fly‐in‐fly‐out (FIFO) arrangements, rather than permanent rural surgical capacity. This has fostered a reliance on short‐term solutions locum relief or FIFO employment instead of rural workforce retention.

Policy Process

Policy development for the rural surgical workforce has involved broad stakeholder engagement but is characterised by weak implementation mechanisms. Strategies such as the SRHS were developed following multi‐year consultations, and professional bodies like RACS have also embedded rural input into their internal strategies [5]. Similarly, the ONRHC has convened forums on rural surgical training [30] since its establishment in 2017.

However, the process is decentralised and non‐binding. Federal policies often rely on incentives (e.g., bonded places, grants), which states and universities may or may not act upon. Bonded scholarship schemes struggle with enforcement, especially across jurisdictions. While the NMWS emphasises “clear roles and accountability”, its own development documents admit that responsibility is shared across actors without effective coordination [8]. There is no national data registry to track rural surgical capacity or outcomes.

Accountability mechanisms remain diffuse. The ONRHC provides reports to Parliament but has no regulatory power. State governments are accountable to their electorates for hospital services, but rural performance is rarely singled out. The federal government funds rural training but cannot compel workforce distribution. As a result, success and failure alike are difficult to attribute, and programmatic reform is difficult to sustain.

Discussion

This analysis indicates that despite numerous initiatives, the Australian rural surgical workforce remains inadequately supported by coherent policy. Walt and Gilson's health policy triangle [10] was used to examine actors, content, context, and process. We have divided our discussion like this below.

Actors

While the federal government funds STP rural training posts, states determine whether and how those posts are embedded within local hospital systems. Professional colleges like RACS control who is trained and where but are not bound to meet workforce distribution targets. Rural hospitals depend on state health departments for staffing and resources but have a limited voice in national workforce planning. Meanwhile, communities and rural clinicians, those who are most affected by policy, often lack representation in policy decision‐making. The disconnection echoes concerns that “predominantly urban committees [are] making decisions for rural communities” [9].

Applying the framework by Shiffman et al. regarding the generation of political priority, it is critical to recognise the mismatch in power dynamics [11]. While the Federal Government possesses financial power (funding training posts via STP), the power of employment and implementation resides with State Health Departments and local hospital boards.

This uncoordinated approach to governance leads to duplicated efforts, inefficient resource allocation, and persistent service gaps. Efforts such as the National Strategic Framework for Rural and Remote Health were intended to unify actors around a shared vision, but they lack binding authority and are rarely mentioned in decision‐making [25]. Rather than power being concentrated in any single actor, it is fundamentally divided: federal funders hold financial authority, professional colleges control accreditation and training, and state governments govern employment and service delivery. This fragmentation means that no single actor can drive systemic change unilaterally, and rural health services and communities, those most affected, remain structurally isolated from decision‐making.

Content

Despite a wide range of initiatives, the content of rural health policy remains divided and generalist in focus. National and state frameworks highlight rural equity and workforce development; however, surgical care is often not specifically addressed. Policies such as the NMWS and SRHS provide specific direction for general practice and primary care but do not detail rural surgical needs. The absence of a dedicated policy framework has left surgical services reliant on short‐term programs and advocacy efforts instead of structured, coordinated planning.

While RACS and other specialists have introduced evidence‐based training reforms to support rural training and service provision, these remain largely voluntary. The capacity of individual training sites and supervisors influences uptake of rural rotations and adoption of rural curricula. Currently, no regulatory authority oversees rural surgical service targets nor are there explicit accountability mechanisms linking workforce funding to specialist distribution in underrepresented areas. As a result, progress depends on local advocates, institutional commitment, and voluntary participation.

The diffusion of responsibility across multiple levels of government and professions further weakens coherence. Individual efforts will remain isolated from one another, leading to duplication in some areas and neglect in others. For instance, bonded programs and financial incentives may support rural retention generally but are not matched by sufficient surgical infrastructure or training sites in rural hospitals.

Existing policies do not adequately address the specialist‐generalist interface. Generalist initiatives such as the National Rural Generalist Pathway or rural procedural GP models have a significant role in rural patient care, but these may not substitute for comprehensive surgical coverage. Rural general surgeons face unique training and scope‐of‐practice [35], that are not reflected in most mainstream policy documents. The current policies highlight a consensus around rural equity in general, but lack the specificity and cohesion needed to support rural communities.

The challenges facing different surgical specialties are also not uniform, and policy content rarely reflects this. General Surgery presents distinct workforce pressures from subspecialties such as ENT, urology, and vascular surgery, each of which requires different service models within a capability framework. For high‐volume, low‐acuity, short‐stay procedures, such as tonsillectomy, grommet insertion, and sinus surgery, visiting surgical services, where a subspecialist travels to a regional centre with overnight stays, may be a feasible and sustainable model that does not require a permanent local appointment. By contrast, general surgery, with its acute and unpredictable caseload, demands a different calculus. Even highly centralised subspecialties such as neurosurgery, where operative care is appropriately concentrated in high‐capability centres, can be partially decentralised through regional consultation, virtual assessment, and outreach. This can reduce the burden on patients to travel for what may be a non‐operative encounter.

A further gap in policy content is its narrow framing of surgical care as the operative event itself, rather than the full perioperative journey. The ANZCA Perioperative Care Framework defines surgical care as spanning preoperative assessment and shared decision‐making through to postoperative recovery, rehabilitation, and return to community [36]. Many components of this journey can be delivered closer to home by a broader range of health workers, specialist nurses, allied health practitioners, and GP anaesthetists, without requiring a surgeon to be locally present or residing. Task delegation models such as the Hamilton urology nurse practitioner (Table 2) demonstrate this in practice. Similar models of extended scope exist across specialties globally: optometrists extending ophthalmological reach [37, 38], audiologists supporting ENT services [39], and physiotherapists managing post‐orthopaedic rehabilitation [40]. Policy documents should therefore shift emphasis from surgeons to perioperative systems, and must account for the critical role of other practitioners in rural surgical viability.

While RACS and other specialty organisations have provided valuable leadership in advocacy and representation, their influence is almost exclusively advisory. Without authority to fund, accredit, or deploy posts, they are unable to implement policy at scale. The limits of unilateral action are visible in the ongoing debate around the federal Expedited Specialist Pathway for SIMGs: RACS successfully advocated for surgery's exclusion when the pathway launched, with an understanding that there was no plan to extend the pathway to other specialties [41]. However, state and territory health departments have since nominated General Surgery and otolaryngology as the next priority specialties for expansion, directly contesting that position [42]. This ongoing contest between college standards, federal registration architecture, and state workforce pressures exemplifies the divided governance dynamic illustrated in Figure 2, and reinforces why surgical workforce reform requires negotiated co‐design rather than unilateral action by any party.

Context

Despite rural disadvantage being well‐recognised, political commitment to long‐term solutions has been inconsistent. Rural health initiatives often develop in response to inquiries, media scrutiny, or workforce crises. However, they are rarely implemented in funding cycles or national service reform plans. In addition to this, the location of practice itself can act as a barrier. Relocation to rural areas can cause significant social and personal impacts on surgeons and their families. These include but are not limited to reduced employment opportunities for partners, limited access to education, and professional isolation [43]. Policies that fail to account for this risk overlooking a key driver of rural recruitment and retention.

A major contextual barrier is Australia's federal health system, which divides responsibility for workforce training and healthcare delivery across federal and state governments. The Federal Government (DoDH) funds rural medical schools and national training programs. The states control public hospital budgets, staffing allocations, and placements. This results in disjointed policies and misaligned priorities. For example, the Federal Government may incentivise rural training posts, but unless states fund theatre capacity and/or perioperative staff, the post remains unviable. Without binding mechanisms to coordinate this, policy implementation is variable, leading to delayed reform efforts.

Despite repeated commitments to rural health equity, the implementation of rural surgical workforce policy remains constrained by structural governance challenges. The separation between funding mechanisms and service delivery functions causes implementation delays, even when policies are thoughtfully designed. Addressing this will require strengthened state and national government alignment, clearer accountability mechanisms, and dedicated strategies that bridge the divide between policy objectives and operational delivery.

Process

The policy process surrounding rural surgical workforce reform suffers from a lack of integration, accountability, and strategic oversight. Policies are often developed collaboratively but carried out inconsistently. For example, RACS may endorse rural rotations, but these rotations typically depend on hospital readiness and funding, which is dictated through the state governments.

A central issue is the disconnect between policy formulation and execution. The lack of an overarching authority responsible for rural surgical deployment means implementation gaps exist, from medical school to registrar training to post‐fellowship placement. To bridge the implementation gap, the policy process should evolve from voluntary incentives to integrated training requirements. One high‐value shift is formalising travelling as part of training, where metropolitan specialists take trainees with them on outreach visits to smaller regional hospitals. This not only addresses immediate service gaps in regional towns like Hamilton or Portland but also embeds regional exposure as a core competency, increasing the likelihood that future specialists will support regional networks throughout their careers [44].

However, colleges do not fund or allocate posts; states have limited incentive to create roles that align with national training goals; and federal incentives are often insufficient to drive long‐term change. This division in accountability echoes findings from national accreditation reviews [45], which highlight inconsistent governance and a lack of coordination between accrediting bodies and jurisdictions.

Many rural health initiatives are pilot‐based and lack built‐in evaluation frameworks [44]. Programs are funded on short‐term cycles, with limited data collection or outcome tracking. As a result, many initiatives, such as outreach surgical services, are discontinued despite initial promise. An underutilised resource is the systematic integration of virtual care across the surgical pathway. The Victorian Surgical Reform Blueprint [46] outlines how virtual consultation can support both planned and acute care, reduce inter‐hospital transfers, and enable specialist input without requiring patient or clinician travel. Embedding virtual care into rural surgical governance, with MBS item numbers and state funding structured to support it, could extend specialist reach across the perioperative period.

Policy progress remains limited by an absence of enforcement mechanisms. High‐level strategic documents often have limited statutory authority, and accountability is diffuse across the Federal Government, state governments and professional bodies. Although national strategies, such as the SRHS or the NMWS, outline promising goals, they lack ownership of implementation. Common challenges of rural and regional doctors include on‐call fatigue, limited locum relief, and infrastructure limitations [35, 43]. The policies and strategies acknowledge these [8] but rarely address them with meaningful action. While organisations such as RACS, AMA, and the ONRHC have repeatedly identified structural solutions, these often remain unacted upon.

This piece is descriptive, drawing on policy documents, professional reports, and academic literature to analyse governance. As a qualitative policy analysis, it reflects how rural surgical workforce policy is constructed and enacted across jurisdictions, recognising that interpretations may vary by context.

Policy Recommendations

Improving rural surgical workforce equity in Australia requires a shift from generalist‐oriented policies toward a structured and measurable strategy that supports rural surgical services specifically. Below we have recommended policy changes or implementations to address and find gaps in governance, implementation, and evaluation.

Reframing Rural Surgical Access Around Service Delivery

Current policy treats surgical inequity as a workforce distribution problem, responding with financial incentives and return‐of‐service schemes to relocate surgeons. Global evidence suggests that these interventions do not produce long‐term retention [47]. A more productive reorientation focuses on how surgical care is delivered rather than where surgeons reside. This means formalising structured outreach services, expanded multidisciplinary task delegation, and virtual care embedded as a formal component of rural surgical delivery.

Rural Surgical Data Bank

There is a lack of a unified rural surgical data system to track workforce distribution, surgical volumes, procedural outcomes, and retention. A data bank would enable monitoring and improvement of rural workforce strategies. When utilising this information in policy change, national and state governments should include rural clinicians and community representatives to ensure policy alignment with service realities. The data bank should record not only surgical volumes and workforce distribution, but each specialist's contribution to rural communities, including outreach visits, virtual consultations, and follow‐up, making rural engagement visible and professionally valued. This would allow appointments, credentialing renewals, and funding allocations to be informed by demonstrated rural contribution.

National Rural Surgical Workforce Authority

Efforts should be made to establish a dedicated national coordinating to overcome disjointed governance and policy inaction. This Authority would function as a multidisciplinary body with formal representation from the federal, state and territory health departments, medical colleges (e.g., RACS), rural health advocacy organisations, and rural communities See (Tables 2 and 3). Given that perioperative policy is primarily funded and delivered at the state level, the Authority would require a joint federal‐state mandate. This would be similar to existing intergovernmental health agreements, to ensure genuine coordination rather than advisory duplication.

Conclusion

Efforts to strengthen the rural surgical workforce in Australia have been addressed with broader rural health strategies. Yet, despite multiple initiatives, progress has often been limited by disjointed governance, inconsistent funding, and a lack of long‐term sustainability. Stronger coordination is needed across community, state, and national government levels with alignment of funding with service delivery. Most importantly, engaging rural clinicians and communities in designing and delivering reforms will help ensure they reflect real‐world needs and lead to lasting improvements in surgical access and equity.
