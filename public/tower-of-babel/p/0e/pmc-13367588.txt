Knowledge and practices of school teachers toward learning disabilities: An observational study in a district of Kerala, South India
Authors: M N Sameena, N Bushra Thasneem, Resmi S. Kaimal, Indhu Rajeev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Learning disabilities (LDs) can adversely affect a child’s academic and social development if not identified and addressed early. Teachers are pivotal in recognizing LDs and implementing classroom strategies. Their knowledge and practices significantly influence inclusive education outcomes.Aim:To assess the knowledge and practices of schoolteachers regarding learning disabilities and determine associated factors.Methods:An observational study was conducted among 100 randomly selected teachers from government and private schools in Ernakulam district, Kerala. A self-structured, validated questionnaire assessed knowledge (15 items), and practices (12 items). Teachers with prior LD training were excluded. Responses were categorized as excellent (>80%), good (50%–80%), or poor (<50%). Data was analyzed using SPSS version 20.Results:Most participants were female (72%) and aged 31–40 years. The majority held B. Ed qualifications and taught both primary and secondary levels. About 72% had identified students with LDs, and 66% reported the presence of school counselors. Knowledge scores were good in 77%, excellent in 12%, and poor in 11%. Knowledge was significantly associated with sex and subject taught (P = 0.011 and P = 0.002). Practice levels were good in 76%, poor in 21%, and excellent in only 3%. While practice was not linked to demographic factors, it was significantly associated with knowledge levels (P = 0.013).Conclusion:Most teachers demonstrated good knowledge and practice, though excellence was limited. The strong association between knowledge and practice underscores the importance of training. Notably, 80% of teachers expressed interest in further LD education, indicating readiness for professional development.

Introduction

Learning disabilities (LDs) pose a significant challenge for schools and teachers. If these disabilities go unnoticed, ignored, or unaddressed, the affected children’s needs remain unmet in both regular and special education classrooms. This not only hinders the child’s academic growth, but also undermines the broader goal of universalizing elementary education and ensuring equal educational opportunities for all.[1] At the primary school level, teachers play a vital role in identifying children with LDs early on.

To support students effectively, teachers must possess the right mix of knowledge, skills, and personal qualities. A teacher who is well-trained, dedicated, disciplined, and patient—coupled with genuine love and care for students—can make a meaningful difference in the lives of children with LD. Continuous professional development, especially through special training programs on new teaching strategies and methods for addressing LDs, is essential to equip teachers for this role.

When LDs remain undiagnosed and unmanaged, they can lead to chronic academic struggles, school dropouts, and a range of emotional and behavioral issues such as depression, substance abuse, and delinquency.[23456789] Since teachers are often the first to observe a child’s academic difficulties, their awareness and understanding of LDs are crucial for early identification and intervention. While LDs are not curable, timely support can significantly improve a child’s self-esteem, academic performance, and social skills.[1]

Although earlier studies in India have assessed teachers’ knowledge of LDs, many have focused solely on factual knowledge.[101112] However, it is debatable how much knowledge is truly adequate, and knowledge alone may not necessarily lead to changes in attitude or behavior.[13] Teachers’ knowledge and opinions about LDs may, in fact, have a greater influence on how they interact with and support students facing these challenges. This study, therefore, seeks to evaluate not only the teachers’ knowledge, but also their opinions and knowledge about LDs.

In recent years, increased efforts have been made to raise awareness about LDs among both parents and educators. As family physicians and primary healthcare providers, we have a crucial role in the early detection of LDs, and in guiding families toward appropriate support services. Understanding teachers’ knowledge and practices related to LDs is thus imperative for developing effective interventions and policies that support inclusive education.

Material and Methods

The study was conducted in selected government and private schools in Ernakulam district, Kerala, and included schoolteachers as the study population. It was a questionnaire-based observational study carried out over one and a half years, from July 2020 to March 2022. The sample size was determined using the formula n > (z2 × P × q)/d2, with a prevalence rate (p) of 15%,[14] a 95% confidence interval, and a 7% margin of error, resulting in a required minimum sample size of 99, which was rounded to 100 for convenience. Participants were selected using a simple random sampling technique. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 20, with frequency and percentage used to represent categorical variables and logistic regression employed to explore relationships between knowledge/practice and study variables. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Inclusion criteria involved schoolteachers, who consented to participate, while those with prior special training on LDs were excluded. Ethical approval was obtained, and no financial or physical burden was placed on participants, as data was collected solely through a structured questionnaire. Informed consent was taken from both the school authorities and individual teachers. A self-structured questionnaire was developed based on literature review and expert input, and validated by two independent subject experts after a pilot study. The tool consisted of three parts: Part I gathered sociodemographic data (age, sex, qualification, experience, subject, level of teaching); Part II included 15 multiple-choice questions assessing knowledge about LDs, with one mark for each correct response (maximum score of 16); and Part III consisted of 12 questions on practices related to LD, where each correct or partially correct answer received one mark.

Definition of variables

Knowledge and practices are categorized based on the percentage of correct responses. For knowledge, a score greater than 80% indicates excellent knowledge, 50%–80% reflects good knowledge, and less than 50% signifies poor knowledge. Similarly, for practices, a score above 80% denotes excellent practice, 50%–80% represents good practice, and below 50% indicates poor practice.

Knowledge about learning disability

Early identification of LDs is seen as crucial by most respondents. A majority (62.79%) believed that LDs typically become noticeable when a child reaches school age, though a smaller proportion recognized signs at earlier or later stages. Common early indicators included memory disturbances and hyperactivity (23.81%), followed by anxiety and fear of going to school (21.43%).

In terms of diagnosis, many teachers recognized the importance of ruling out other contributing factors such as low IQ, and vision and hearing problems (30.95%). When it came to management, the vast majority (76.74%) emphasized the role of providing care, love, and security, although fewer acknowledged the need for educational or medical interventions. Encouragingly, 69.05% supported a collaborative approach involving teachers, parents, and psychologists.

Attitudes toward children with LDs were largely positive. Most teachers (67.5%) viewed them as children with different capabilities, and an overwhelming 97.6% expressed a willingness to teach them if provided with adequate training and counselling support. Furthermore, 85.7% advocated for inclusive education within mainstream classrooms, supplemented by counsellor services.

Overall, the findings reflect a generally high level of awareness, and a strong commitment among teachers to support students with LDs. However, they also highlight the need for more comprehensive teacher training and structured intervention strategies within educational settings.

Distribution of level of knowledge

Most teachers (77%) have a good knowledge, while 12% have excellent knowledge and 11% have a poor knowledge.

An overwhelming majority (90.7%) believe that counseling services should be available in schools, highlighting the recognized importance of mental, and emotional support for students. Additionally, 69.05% of teachers expressed interest in attending classes on LDs, demonstrating a proactive approach to professional development.

Practices among teachers

Teachers also show a strong commitment to addressing specific learning challenges. About 73.81% of respondents seek more information about referral procedures for students with LDs, and 71.43% are willing to serve as advocates, assisting these students in accessing vital support services such as financial aid and library resources.

In classroom practices, educators clearly favor supportive, and inclusive strategies over punitive ones. For students with reading difficulties, 67.44% recommend giving clear and repeated instructions, while only 2.33% support the use of punishment. Similarly, 90.48% of teachers endorse using a combination of visual, verbal, and interactive techniques to enhance memory, indicating an awareness of varied learning styles.

For students with writing difficulties, 53.49% recommend the use of checklists to guide writing tasks, and 30.23% support encouraging students to copy content for practice. When it comes to incomplete assignments, 41.86% suggest breaking down tasks into manageable sections with deadlines, and 30.23% advise listing the steps involved, reflecting a structured, and supportive approach.

Modifications to teaching methods are also widely embraced. A majority (59.09%) prefer reading tasks aloud and explaining them multiple times, while others consider using audio recordings or a combination of strategies. Notably, nearly all educators (97.62%) report that they help students find appropriate ways to express their feelings, underscoring the emphasis on emotional well-being.

Finally, in the case of a student with a learning disability, 80.95% of teachers say they would take a comprehensive approach—first understanding the child’s difficulties, then engaging with parents, and personally accompanying the student to a counsellor. This highlights a deeply empathetic and collaborative mindset among educators in addressing the needs of learners with disabilities.

Distribution of level of practice

Most teachers (76%) demonstrate good practice, while 21% have a poor practice, and only 3% have an excellent practice.

Association between knowledge and various factors

The analysis [Table 4] explored the association between knowledge levels and various demographic, and professional variables among 100 participants. Among all variables, sex, and subject taught showed statistically significant associations with knowledge levels. Female respondents had a significantly better knowledge compared to males (P = 0.011). Similarly, teachers of language subjects demonstrated a higher proportion of excellent knowledge compared to those teaching mathematics or multiple subjects (P = 0.002). Other variables such as age (P = 0.671), type of school (P = 0.937), educational qualification (P = 0.483), level of teaching (P = 0.125), teaching experience (P = 0.102), identification of children with LDs (P = 0.122), and availability of counsellor services (P = 0.770) did not show statistically significant associations with knowledge levels. This suggests that gender and subject specialization may play a more influential role in shaping teachers’ knowledge than other factors.

Discussion

In this study, we selected 100 teachers in Ernakulam district, Kerala, using a simple random sampling method to explore their knowledge and practices regarding LD. Among the 100 participants, the majority (38%) belonged to the age group 30–40 years, a distribution that mirrors previous studies conducted in India.[1516] Most of the respondents (72%) were females, consistent with prior studies[213151718] and reflecting the higher proportion of female teachers in the general population. Half of the teachers (50%) were from aided schools, which contrasts with findings from studies in Bangalore, India,[18] where the majority were from unaided schools, and another study[17] where most teachers were from government schools. This difference may be attributed to the urban setting of our study.

The educational qualifications of the teachers were predominantly graduates or postgraduates with a B. Ed. (44%), which aligns with findings from similar studies.[1819] Some earlier studies[1718] focused on the awareness of teachers with special training in LD, though our study excluded such teachers. Most of the participants (46%) taught at the primary and secondary levels, which aligns with studies that predominantly focused on primary school teachers.[1141819] In contrast, one study included secondary teacher education trainees,[20] and a few studies looked at teachers across all levels.[21] Additionally, 31% of teachers taught multiple subjects, a factor not assessed in many previous studies, which primarily concentrated on primary school teachers, making the subject taught less relevant in their research.[151821]

The study also explored the teachers’ professional experience. The majority (66%) had 11–20 years of teaching experience, whereas in most previous studies, the majority of teachers had less than 10 years of experience.[151819] Furthermore, 66% of teachers reported having counselor services available at their schools, a factor not studied by many previous researchers,[151922] except for a few.[1718] Notably, 72% of the teachers identified children with LD, which is consistent with a recent study in Nepal[15] and another from Saudi Arabia,[17] although different from a 2015 study in India.[18]

Regarding teachers’ knowledge and practices, the majority (77%) of teachers demonstrated good knowledge, while 12% showed excellent knowledge, and 11% had poor knowledge. Previous studies indicated that teachers generally had limited knowledge about LD,[14151619] although the awareness levels have improved over time,[172123] likely due to ongoing interventions. Similarly, 76% of teachers exhibited good practices, 21% had poor practices, and 3% demonstrated excellent practices. Interestingly, no studies have assessed the practice levels of teachers regarding LD.

Two significant factors associated with knowledge and practices were sex and the subject taught. Female teachers (15%) had excellent knowledge compared to male teachers (4%), with poor knowledge being higher among males (6%) compared to females (2%). Previous studies have also found a link between gender and knowledge about LD, with females generally having more knowledge.[15182021] Additionally, teachers who taught languages (24%) demonstrated better knowledge, whereas teachers teaching multiple subjects, including language and mathematics, had poorer knowledge (26%). This association with the subject taught had not been studied in previous research.

We also explored other factors such as age, type of school, educational qualifications, class taught, and experience. 19% of teachers in the age group 20–30 was found to have excellent knowledge but this was not statistically significant, which mirrors findings from earlier studies.[151621] 14% of teachers from aided schools with state syllabi demonstrated excellent knowledge, although this did not show a significant association with knowledge in our study. Other studies have reported differing results, such as a study in Kerala[22] where government teachers had better awareness, or one in Tamil Nadu[23] where private school teachers showed greater awareness.

Interestingly, 16% of teachers with postgraduation and B. Ed had excellent knowledge, but this did not have a statistically significant association with knowledge levels in our study. Recent studies[1821] indicated that educational qualification had a statistically significant impact on teachers’ knowledge of LD. Similarly, 16% of teachers at the higher secondary level showed excellent knowledge, although this did not correlate significantly with knowledge levels, unlike a previous study where such a link was found.[21] Regarding experience, 17% of teachers with over 20 years of experience exhibited excellent knowledge, but this did not have a statistically significant association with knowledge, consistent with other studies.[12172224] However, some studies did show significant associations between experience and awareness of LD.[2123]

When it comes to practices, only a small percentage of teachers in various categories, such as those over 50 years of age (5%), male teachers (7%), teachers in recognized schools with a Central Board of Secondary Education (CBSE) syllabus (7%), and others, displayed excellent practices. None of these associations, however, were statistically significant. This is particularly interesting since no studies have previously assessed the practices of teachers regarding LD, making direct comparisons difficult.

Lastly, only 15% of teachers who had identified children with LD and 14% of teachers with counselor services at their schools had excellent knowledge, with only 4%, and 3%, respectively, showing excellent practices. However, these factors did not have any statistically significant association with the teachers’ knowledge and practices, a finding that aligns with previous studies.[1718] Additionally, 82% of teachers expressed a willingness to attend classes on LD, which is consistent with findings from previous studies where most teachers showed a readiness to undergo special training for LD intervention.[13] This suggests that many teachers feel they lack sufficient knowledge and are open to improving their understanding of LD.

The association of prior identification of LD and knowledge was similarly reported in other studies.[151718] In the present study, 77% of teachers demonstrated a good knowledge of LD, while 12% had an excellent knowledge and 11% had a poor knowledge. Regarding practice, 76% of teachers showed good practice, whereas only 11% demonstrated poor practice. Among those with excellent knowledge, 91% exhibited good practice, and 78% of those with a good level of knowledge also had good practice. The level of knowledge was found to have a statistically significant association with the level of practice. One study highlighted that a higher level of knowledge was associated with a positive attitude[19]; however, no previous studies have examined the association between knowledge and practices among teachers regarding LD.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, it was conducted on a small sample size, which may not accurately represent the wider population of teachers. Additionally, the study was limited to an urban setting, restricting the applicability of its findings to rural areas, where educational challenges and resources can differ significantly. The lack of diversity in the participant pool, with no representation from different states or socio-economic backgrounds, further limits the generalizability of the results across India. The cross-sectional design of the study captures knowledge and practices at a single point in time, preventing any conclusions about changes or causal relationships. Moreover, the use of self-reported questionnaires may have introduced bias, as participants might overestimate their knowledge or practices. The study also did not include a separate analysis of teachers with specialized training in learning disabilities, which could have provided valuable insights. External factors such as school infrastructure, parental involvement, and administrative support were not considered, despite their potential influence on teachers’ practices. Lastly, the absence of a follow-up assessment means the study could not evaluate the long-term impact of any interventions or training programs on teachers’ knowledge and practices.

Conclusion

The study found that most teachers demonstrated a good level of knowledge and practice regarding LD. Notably, sex and the subject taught were significantly associated with teachers’ knowledge about LD. Furthermore, there was a statistically significant association between teachers’ knowledge and their practices related to LD. However, as the study was conducted among a small population primarily in an urban area, the findings cannot be generalized. To gain a more comprehensive understanding, larger studies involving diverse populations across both rural and urban settings are necessary. Given the wide variation in literacy rates and living standards across Indian states, conducting state-level studies would be more effective in developing tailored strategies. Future research should also include teachers who have received special training, allowing comparisons of their knowledge, and practices with those who have not. Since the study revealed that good practice levels remain low even among teachers with excellent knowledge, it is crucial to implement active interventions. These should include integrating LD management into teacher education curricula, conducting regular training sessions, and periodically assessing teachers’ knowledge and practices. Additionally, primary care physicians, often the first point of contact for families, must be trained to distinguish LD from other conditions, and provide timely guidance for early intervention.
