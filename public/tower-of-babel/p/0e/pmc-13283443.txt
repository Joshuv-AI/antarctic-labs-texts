Human dignity, technology, and solidarity in a fractured world: why Magnifica Humanitas matters for public health
Authors: Tiago Correia, Walter Ricciardi, Martin McKee
Published: 2026

Public health has never been only about health. It has always been about the societies we build, the lives we protect, and the values guiding decisions made in the name of efficiency or progress. This is why Magnifica Humanitas, the 2026 encyclical by Pope Leo XIV, deserves the attention of public health professionals [1].

The encyclical appears at a time when the moral language of public life has become dangerously thin. War, forced displacement, climate insecurity, widening inequality, and weakening multilateral institutions have made solidarity harder to sustain. At the same time, artificial intelligence, digital surveillance, and the concentration of technological power are changing how decisions are made, who is heard, and who is rendered invisible. These are not peripheral concerns for public health. They are among its central questions.

Pope Leo XIV places Magnifica Humanitas within the tradition of Catholic social teaching, tracing back to Leo XIII’s Rerum Novarum (New Things), a response to the Industrial Revolution [2]. Then, the ‘new things’ were capital, labour, industrialization, and social dislocation. Today, they are algorithms, data infrastructures, artificial intelligence, platform monopolies, and the growing capacity of a small number of actors to shape the conditions of human life at scale. The encyclical emphasizes that inequalities arise from structural factors rather than individual failings, a point that the Pope emphasized three months earlier in a speech to the Pontifical Academy for Life [3].

This is not an argument for public health to become theological. It is an argument for public health to recognize when a major ethical intervention speaks directly to its own concerns. The encyclical’s language is religious, but the problems it addresses are shared: how to govern technology, how to confront structural inequality, how to protect those treated as disposable, and how to recover the common good in societies organized around individual advantage.

Public health is often presented as a technical enterprise, concerned with evidence, modelling, interventions, and outcomes. It is all these things. But it is also normative. Decisions about vaccination, prevention, resource allocation, workforce planning, digital health, migration, or pandemic preparedness rest on assumptions about whose lives matter and what obligations people owe one another. Evidence can inform those decisions. It cannot supply their moral foundations.

Magnifica Humanitas gives renewed force to ideas that public health depends on but too often leaves implicit: dignity, solidarity, justice, the common good, and the protection of the vulnerable. Its value lies in reminding public health what is at stake when technical systems are allowed to define human worth.

Human dignity is the encyclical’s organizing principle. It insists that dignity is not earned through productivity, citizenship, health status, economic contribution, or technological usefulness. Rather, it is intrinsic to the individual. Yet, today, older people are discussed as a burden, migrants as a threat, disabled people as costs, and those living in poverty as failed economic actors. That all of these groups deserve dignity should be obvious to anyone who has read the New Testament, but, given statements by some American politicians [4], it has become necessary to reiterate it. Reaffirming inviolable dignity is therefore not pious language. It is a political and public health necessity.

This matters because health systems increasingly rely on data-driven tools to classify, predict, rank, and prioritize. These tools can improve care and support better decisions. But they also risk translating people into risk scores, cost profiles, and categories of expected benefit. The danger is not technology itself. It is the quiet normalization of a view of human beings as units to be optimized, exemplified by the head of a major bank describing his staff as ‘lower-value human capital’ [5].

The encyclical’s critique of artificial intelligence is therefore one of its most important contributions. Much discussion of AI in health still oscillates between enthusiasm and caution, with concerns about efficiency, accuracy, innovation, bias, transparency, and regulation. These debates matter, but they are incomplete unless connected to questions of power. Who controls the data? Who designs the systems? Who benefits from their deployment? Who is accountable when they fail? Whose lives become easier to ignore because they are poorly represented in the data on which systems are trained?

Public health cannot treat AI as a specialized technical issue delegated to informaticians, lawyers, or vendors. AI is already shaping disease surveillance, predictive modelling, clinical decision support, workforce planning, communication, and resource allocation [6]. It will increasingly shape entitlement and the experience of care. Public health professionals should therefore ask not only whether AI works, but for whom it works, under whose control, and at what social cost.

The same applies to data. In health, data are often described as a resource. But resources can be extracted, monopolized and used in ways that benefit some while exposing others. The encyclical’s emphasis on the universal destination of goods challenges the assumption that knowledge, technology, and data infrastructures can be governed primarily through ownership and market value [7]. In global health, this resonates with concerns about data colonialism and the extraction of information from poorer populations with limited benefit to those who generated it.

If data are to serve the public good, governance must be transparent, participatory, and oriented towards shared benefit. Consent matters, but consent alone is not enough. Public institutions need the capacity to regulate or negotiate with powerful private actors. Innovation should be judged not only by commercial success or technical sophistication, but by whether it reduces or reproduces inequality [8].

The encyclical also revives the concept of the common good. Exposure, vulnerability, access to protection, and the ability to follow public health guidance are shaped by housing, employment, income, migration status, education, and political trust. Yet preparedness is again discussed largely in terms of surveillance, stockpiles, rapid response, and technological platforms. These are necessary, but insufficient. Preparedness without solidarity is only partial.

The encyclical’s treatment of inequality is similarly important. It refuses to see disadvantage as individual failure and instead points to structures that produce vulnerability over time. Inequality is not merely inefficient or associated with worse outcomes. It is an affront to human dignity when it reflects avoidable exclusion, exploitation, or indifference. A society in which poverty shortens life, digital exclusion limits access to care, or migration status determines safety is not simply experiencing unequal outcomes. It is making choices about whose lives are easier to sacrifice.

Solidarity challenges those choices. It is often invoked as a virtue, but in public health, it is also a practical necessity. Pandemic preparedness, antimicrobial resistance, climate change, migration, and health workforce sustainability cannot be addressed through national self-interest alone. They require cooperation, reciprocity, and durable institutions. For Europe, this message is especially relevant. European public health cannot defend universalism at home while tolerating exclusion at its borders, nor can it promote ethical digital health while depending on infrastructures governed by private interests with limited democratic accountability. Solidarity must be institutional, not rhetorical.

A final strength of Magnifica Humanitas is its critique of the technocratic paradigm. Metrics, dashboards, models, targets, and digital tools are indispensable. But when they become dominant, they can narrow what counts as knowledge and obscure what cannot easily be measured: trust, care, fear, exclusion, belonging, and hope [9].

The point is not to reject technology or measurement. The point is to place them in the service of human flourishing rather than allowing them to define it. Public health needs epidemiology, economics, digital science, and implementation research. It also needs moral imagination. Without it, even effective interventions can become detached from the people they claim to serve.

Some readers will engage with the encyclical as an expression of Catholic social teaching. Others will approach it as a public moral text. That need not limit its relevance. Magnifica Humanitas belongs in the conversation on public health because it offers a coherent moral vocabulary for a fractured age.
