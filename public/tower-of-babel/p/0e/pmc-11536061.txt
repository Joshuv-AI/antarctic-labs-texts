Introduction

An empyema is a collection of pus within the pleural space, typically arising as a complication of parapneumonic or pleural effusion. Less common etiologies include infected hemothorax, ruptured lung abscess, esophageal tears, and thoracic trauma [1]. Risk factors include aspiration, poor dental hygiene, malnutrition, diabetes mellitus, intravenous drug abuse, immunosuppression, gastric acid reflux, and alcohol abuse. In the United States, empyema affects approximately 32,000 individuals annually [2]. Notably, 20–40 % of patients hospitalized with pneumonia develop parapneumonic effusion with 5–10 % progressing to empyema [3].

The overall mortality rate of empyema patients reaches 12.4 % and increases with age [4]. Streptococcus pneumoniae is the most common cause of community-acquired pneumonia, empyema, and culture-positive pleural effusion. Staphylococcus aureus is often seen in hospital-acquired cases. Gram-negative rods like Escherichia coli, Enterobacteriaceae species, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as well as atypical organisms such as Legionella, Mycoplasma, and Chlamydia are less commonly seen. Salmonella bacteria are exceedingly rare sources of pulmonary infection, although other Enterobacteriaceae, such as Klebsiella, are commonly cited causes of empyema [5]. Literature review suggests that there may be fewer than 40 reported cases of salmonella empyema (Abdelhafiz A. S., 2020).

Most cases are thought to be a result of aspiration, hematogenous spread or infection of a hepatic hydrothorax. The current case discusses a patient with end-stage cirrhosis who developed Salmonella pneumonia complicated by an empyema. The rarity of this clinical scenario demonstrates the importance of considering atypical pathogens in empyema and pulmonary infections, especially in individuals with underlying immunosuppression and comorbidities.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old male with past medical history of alcoholic cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, esophageal varices, portal vein thrombosis, diabetes mellitus type 2, hypertension and Barrett's esophagus presented with four days of nausea, vomiting and diarrhea complicated by new onset left upper extremity weakness. Physical exam revealed regular rate and rhythm, no cardiac murmurs, decreased breath sounds in the right lower lobe along with crackles in the bilateral bases, mild tenderness to palpation in the abdomen in all four quadrants, moderate abdominal distention with positive fluid wave, 0/5 strength in the left upper extremity with no other focal neurologic deficits.

Labs on admission showed electrolytes within normal limits, creatinine 1.6 mg/dL, alkaline phosphatase 222 U/L, AST 34 U/L, ALT 38 U/L, white blood cell count 10,300/ μ L, hemoglobin 9.3 g/dL, platelets 79,000/ μ L. MRI of the brain revealed a subacute cortical infarct in the right pre and postcentral gyri. He was not a candidate for thrombolytics due to prolonged last known well time. Chest X-ray demonstrated a right diaphragmatic hernia with air fluid levels. CT Chest demonstrated a right sided 14 cm loculated air-fluid collection within the pleural space (Fig. 1A).

CT abdomen pelvis demonstrated 4.9 x 4.8 × 4.2 cm heterogeneously enhancing mass, moderate volume ascites along with cirrhosis of the liver, splenomegaly, and stable non-occlusive thrombus in the portal and splenic vein. There were no signs of bowl dilation or bowel wall thickening (Fig. 2 A, B, C, and D). The patient was started on piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g/IV. Bedside ultrasound confirmed a large pocket of fluid and a 14 French pigtail catheter was placed (Fig. 1B). During placement of the pig tail catheter the patient was noted to have frank green purulent drainage from the pleural space. Analysis of pleural fluid revealed lactate dehydrogenase of 22,182 μ/L, protein 3.2 g/dL, WBC 295,503/mm^3 and RBC 34,583/mm^3. Cultures revealed non-typhoidal Salmonella (serovar type unavailable). The patient was treated with three days of intrapleural enzymes with tissue plasminogen activator and DNase. Antibiotics were de-escalated to Ceftriaxone 2 g/IV after completing 8 days of piperacillin-tazobactam. Unfortunately, the patient unintentionally removed the chest tube. After discussion with cardiothoracic surgery and pulmonology it was decided to avoid surgical intervention or repeat chest tube insertion.

Repeat outpatient imaging post antibiotic regimen revealed the right lower lobe cavitary mass measured 4.0 × 4.5 cm in comparison to prior follow up imaging revealing size of 4.7 × 5.3 cm. Imaging was also notable for adjacent pleural thickening.

Discussion

Non-typhoidal Salmonella is a major cause of gastrointestinal infections worldwide. It typically presents with fever, diarrhea, nausea and vomiting. It is not a common source of pulmonary infection. With fewer than 40 reported cases, Salmonella pneumonia can rarely lead to an empyema. In this particular case the patient developed progressively worsening leukocytosis, hemoptysis, productive cough and chest discomfort after four days of diarrhea, nausea and vomiting. Risk factors for developing a Salmonella lung infection include underlying immunosuppression, malignancy, human immunodeficiency virus, diabetes, intravenous drug use, chronic alcoholism, and cirrhosis [6].

The presence of end stage liver disease (MELD Na- 18, Child Pugh Score of 10, Class C), hepatocellular carcinoma (Li-RADS 5 mass), diabetes mellitus, ischemic stroke, and malnutrition placed this patient at a high risk of developing a Salmonella lung infection. Aspiration of infected gastric contents due to his concomitant ischemic stroke and chronic debilitation remained a working theory. Aspiration could have led to Salmonella pneumonia and direct extension into the pleural space with resultant empyema. Bacterial translocation and hematogenous spread to the lung parenchyma and pleural space and superimposed infection of hepatic hydrothorax are all likely given the patients immunosuppression [7,8].

Non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) species have several characteristics that allow them to evade or infiltrate an immunosuppressed host. The invasion of the intestinal system often occurs via fimbriae and adhesion molecules, which promote host bacterial uptake. A special mechanism facilitated by the type III secretion system allows non-typhoidal Salmonella to invade host cells [9]. This occurs through the injection of a protein into the host cell's cytoskeleton, causing disruption and aiding in host cell entry. In immunosuppressed individuals, this would likely further facilitate the hematogenous spread of infection.

Intracellular survival and evasion of intracellular killing are other unique features that further promote infection. NTS creates Salmonella-containing vacuoles that prevent fusion with lysosomes for destruction [10]. Lastly, NTS produces enzymes such as catalase and superoxide dismutase, which allow them to evade destruction [10].

An additional factor to explore in this case is the use of chronic proton pump inhibitors therapy. Proton pump inhibitors have major implications and increases the severity of gastrointestinal infections in individuals on chronic therapy. H.H Wu et al., 2014 states that in addition to suppressing gastric acid, proton pump inhibitors decrease the integrity of the gastrointestinal barrier [11]. Individuals may develop dysbiosis, which refers to the reduction in gut microbial diversity [11]. Lastly, gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) is associated with the regulation of the gastrointestinal immune system. Proton pump inhibitors may decrease the effectiveness of GALT [11]. This patient was on chronic proton pump inhibitor therapy with pantoprazole. The chronic use of pantoprazole is most likely a contributing factor in the hematogenous spread of infection.

Diagnostic modalities for diagnosis and treatment of empyema's include computerized tomography, magnetic resonance imaging, and ultrasonography [12]. In addition to imaging an analysis of the pleural fluid should be performed. Expected pleural serology findings in an empyema include a pH of less than 7.2, glucose level of less than 40, lactate dehydrogenase level of more than 1,000, gram stain and or culture is positive, and white blood cell counts may be greater than 50,000 [13].

Treating an empyema often requires a multidisciplinary team approach with involvement of Pulmonology, Infectious Disease and Cardiothoracic Surgery. Patients should be initially treated with empiric antibiotics to include gram positives, gram negatives and anaerobes along with source control via drainage of the pleural fluid [14]. Large or loculated collections require an indwelling chest tube for adequate drainage and often intrapleural enzymatic therapy [15]. Repeat imaging to evaluate for resolution of empyema and intrapleural loculations is often helpful. If there is no clinical improvement with conservative measures, surgical intervention with decortication and large bore chest tubes are often necessary [16].

Conclusion

Pulmonary and intrapleural infections with Salmonella are a rare occurrence. Risk factors include aspiration, poor dental hygiene, malnutrition, diabetes mellitus, intravenous drug abuse, immunosuppression, gastric acid reflux, and alcohol abuse. Patients usually present after acute Salmonella infection as a result of direct extension in setting of aspiration or hematogenous spread. Rapid diagnosis and initiation of antibiotics are important aspects of treatment. Source control via chest tube or surgical intervention are necessary. Management by a multi-disciplinary team leads is beneficial for successful treatment of empyema.
