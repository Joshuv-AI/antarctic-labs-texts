Calpeptin improves the cognitive function in Alzheimer's disease‐like complications of diabetes mellitus rats by regulating TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome
Authors: Luyao Qiao, Shouqin Yi, Tianpei Li, Xin Pan, Gege Wang, Xu Liu, Min Li, Jun Min, Huahui Le, Zhenyu Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsDiabetes mellitus (DM) is closely associated with Alzheimer's disease (AD), and is considered an accelerator of AD. Our previous study has confirmed that the Calpain inhibitor Calpeptin may alleviate AD‐like complications of diabetes mellitus. This work further investigated its underlying mechanism.Materials and MethodsDiabetes mellitus rat model was constructed by a high‐fat and high‐sugar diet combined with streptozotocin, followed by the administration of Calpeptin. Moreover, rats were micro‐injected with LV‐TXNIP‐OE/vector into the CA1 region of the hippocampus one day before streptozotocin injection. The Morris water maze test assessed the spatial learning and memory ability of rats. Immunohistochemistry and western blotting detected the expression of the pericyte marker PDGFRβ, tight junction proteins occludin and ZO‐1, calpain‐1, calpain‐2, APP, Aβ, Aβ‐related, and TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome‐related proteins. Immunofluorescence staining examined the blood vessel density and neurons in the hippocampus. Evans blue extravasation and fluorescence detected the permeability of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) in rats. Additionally, the oxidative stress markers and inflammatory‐related factors were assessed by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay.ResultsCalpeptin effectively reduced the expression of Calpain‐2 and TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome‐related proteins, improved the decreased pericyte marker (PDGFR‐β) and cognitive impairment in hippocampus of DM rats. The neuronal loss, microvessel density, permeability of BBB, Aβ accumulation, inflammation, and oxidative stress injury in the hippocampus of DM rats were also partly rescued by calpeptin treatment. The influence conferred by calpeptin treatment was reversed by TXNIP overexpression.ConclusionsThese data demonstrated that calpeptin treatment alleviated AD‐like symptoms in DM rats through regulating TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome. Thus, calpeptin may be a potential drug to treat AD‐like complications of diabetes mellitus.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder with a global epidemic trend. It mainly comprises glucose and lipid metabolism disorders, as well as various chronic complications 1 . The main reason for diabetes mellitus is insufficient, or relatively insufficient, insulin secretion. With the development of the global economy and the improvement of people's living standards, the prevalence of diabetes mellitus is increasing year by year, especially type 2 diabetes mellitus, which is a universal trend worldwide 2 . It is estimated that by 2030, the total number of patients with diabetes mellitus in the world will exceed 300 million 3 . Persistent high blood glucose and long‐term metabolic disorders can lead to a series of chronic complications involving the cardiovascular system, nervous system, retina, kidney, and other organs and tissues 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 . As a complication of diabetes mellitus, Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a degenerative disease of the central nervous system, which is even considered as type 3 diabetes mellitus 8 . The clinical manifestations of AD include cognitive dysfunction, memory loss, thinking ability, and behavioral changes, and the major pathological hallmarks of the disease are the deposition of amyloid‐β (Aβ) and the formation of neurofibrillary tangles (NFT) in the brain regions 9 .

An increasing number of studies indicate that diabetes mellitus is a high‐risk factor for cognitive impairment, which can increase the risk of dementia and lead to the occurrence of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease 10 . Actually, multiple pathophysiological characteristics exist between diabetes mellitus and Alzheimer's disease, including impaired insulin sensitivity, Aβ accumulation, inflammation, oxidative stress, and brain vasculopathy 11 . The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is a cell complex composed of brain microvascular endothelial cells and their tight junctions, capillary basement membrane, pericytes, and astrocytes, which can prevent damaging substances from entering the central nervous system, regulate the transportation of essential substances, maintain the homeostasis of the brain's internal environment, and exert immune regulation functions 12 . Previous study has shown that elevation of blood glucose levels in patients with diabetes mellitus leads to impairment of the BBB function, which induces a series of central nervous system complications 13 . Moreover, the BBB is a way to transport Aβ out of the brain. Damage to the BBB may accelerate the production, convergence, and deposition of Aβ, reduce the clearing of Aβ, thereby promoting the pathological process of AD, and aggravating the impairment of cognitive function 14 . Thus, the BBB plays a crucial role in AD‐like complications of diabetes mellitus.

Calpain is a type of cysteine protease activated by Ca2+, which is widely present in various tissues and organs. Calpain is composed of a 30 kDa regulatory subunit and an 80 kDa catalytic subunit. Calpain‐1 and Calpain‐2 are the most common calpains. Calpain plays an important role in the plasticity of synapses and in the development of neurons, thereby affecting learning and memory ability 15 . A previous study has shown that Calpastatin/Calpain is decreased in the brain tissue of AD mice, Calpastatin is relatively low and the expression of Calpain is increased, and then elevates the intracellular calcium ion concentration and activates calpain 16 . The hyperactivated Calpain causes the division and hydrolysis of related proteins in neurons, and ultimately leads to neuronal degeneration and loss in the brain 17 . In addition, studies have shown that Calpain is activated in Alzheimer's disease, which in turn promotes Aβ production, and upregulation of Calpain plays an important role in the pathogenesis of many neurodegenerative diseases among Alzheimer's disease 17 . Therefore, Calpain inhibitors are gradually being developed for the treatment of Alzheimer's disease, and exploring their specific mechanisms of action has become particularly important.

Our previous study has confirmed that the Calpain inhibitor Calpeptin reduces Aβ accumulation, pericyte loss, NLRP3‐mediated inflammation, and oxidative stress injury in DM rats, thereby alleviating DM‐induced AD‐like cognitive impairments 18 . Furthermore, studies have shown that activation of the NF‐κB/NLRP3 inflammasome axis in patients with diabetes mellitus may be a pathogenic pathway for the development of Parkinson's disease (PD), which is the second most common neurodegenerative disease after Alzheimer's disease 19 . These results indicate that studying the NLRP3 inflammasome may also be closely related to DM‐induced AD‐like cognitive impairments. Ruan et al. 20 have confirmed that inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasomes contributes to alleviate memory deficits of AD mice. Leucine‐rich repeats in thioredoxin‐interacting protein (TXNIP) can bind to the NLRP3 inflammasome in various diseases 21 . Therefore, it is necessary to explore the changes in TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasomes for the treatment of DM‐induced AD‐like cognitive impairments.

In this work, we constructed a rat model of diabetes mellitus, and used Calpeptin to inhibit the activity of Calpain in DM rats. Then, we explored whether Calpain can alleviate BBB damage and AD‐like symptoms in DM rats by regulating TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Sprague Dawley (SD) male rats, 8 weeks old (weighing 160 ± 10 g; Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China) were kept in a SPF condition at 23 ± 2°C and a relative humidity of 60 ± 5%. All protocols were authorized by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital.

Experimental design

The DM rat model was constructed by a high‐fat and high‐sugar (HFHS) diet combined with streptozotocin (STZ). The SD rats were 8 weeks old and fed with HFHS diet (sucrose 20%, fat 10%, cholesterol 2%, sodium cholate 1%) for 4 weeks. After 4 weeks of HFHS diet, the rats were intraperitoneally injected with 30 mg/kg·d streptozotocin (Sigma‐Aldrich; St Louis, MO, USA) for 5 days following 20 h of fasting. After fasting for 8 h, the levels of blood glucose were measured using a portable glucometer (Injex Sens II). When the blood glucose levels were ≥16.7 mmol/L, the rat was considered to be a DM rat. After 4 weeks of the HFHS diet, rats were intraperitoneally injected with 1.75 mg/kg·day Calpeptin (Calpain inhibitor; MedChem Express, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) for 1 week. Rats were fed with normal diet and intraperitoneally injected with phosphate buffer saline (PBS) as a control.

Intracerebroventricular microinjections

For stereotactic injection of lentivirus (LV), the rats were anesthetized with 40 mg/kg 1% pentobarbital sodium and fixed on a stereotactic apparatus (Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL, USA) using a clamp. We sterilized the surgical area of the head with lodophor or 75% alcohol, and then used a surgical knife to fully expose the skull. After that, we searched for Bregma points on the skull and marked them. Rats were micro‐injected with LV‐TXNIP‐OE or LV‐vector into the CA1 region of the hippocampus 1 day before streptozotocin injection. We gently rotated the nut in the three‐dimensional direction for stereoscopic positioning, the coordinates of micro‐injection were listed as follows: −3.8 mm posterior to bregma; ±2.2 mm lateral; 2.7 mm dorsal‐ventral. The injection speed was set at 0.2 μL/min. When injecting, the needle should be kept in the mouse's body for 8–10 min to allow the virus to spread rapidly. Afterwards, we quickly sutured the wound and disinfected, and later injected antibiotics to prevent infection. The mice were allowed to recover for 7 days. LV medicated TXNIP overexpression vector (TXNIP‐OE) and its empty vector (LV‐Vector) were obtained from Genepharm (Shanghai, China).

Morris water maze

The Morris water maze test was carried out to measure the spatial learning and memory ability of rats as described previously 22 . The Morris water maze test was performed in a circular water tank (1 m in diameter) filled with white opaque water. The test contained training and testing phases. During the training phase, the rats were allowed to swim within 60 s or until they reached the platform. The rats that did not reach the platform after 60 s were gently guided to the platform. All rats were allowed to stay on the platform for 10 s. The trials were performed four times a day for 3 days. During the testing phase, the latency period for rats to find the platform from the new starting position was recorded 24 h after the last training session. ANY‐maze video tracking software was used to monitor the trajectory of the rats.

Isolation and culture of hippocampal neuronal cells

Newborn SD rats were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane inhalation and euthanized through cervical dislocation. Under an aseptic environment, bilateral hippocampal tissues were obtained from the suckling mice. The hippocampal tissues were cleaned with PBS, and then digested with 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA (4 mL) at 37°C for 15 min. The digested cells were filtered through a 200 mesh sieve to prepare the single cell suspension of hippocampal neurons. After the cell counting was completed, the resuspended cells were inoculated in a 24‐well plate at a density of 500 μL/well, and the culture plate was placed in a cell culture incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Hippocampal neuronal cells cultured in good condition on day 10 were incubated in a cell culture incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 24 h for the next experiment. Hippocampal neuronal cells were treated with high glucose (HG, 25 mM) or HG + calpeptin for 24 h.

Cell transfection

For TXNIP overexpression and Calpain‐1 or Calpain‐2 knockdown, pcDNA‐3.1 expressing TXNIP (TXNIP‐OE) and shRNA specially targeting Calpain‐1 or Calpain‐2 (sh‐Calpain‐1 or sh‐Calpain‐2) were constructed by VectorBuilder. Empty pcDNA‐3.1 vector (Vector) and scrambled shRNA (sh‐NC) served as a control. Hippocampal neuronal cells were transfected with 2.5 μg of TXNIP‐OE, Vector, sh‐NC, sh‐Calpain‐1 or sh‐Calpain‐2 utilizing 4 μL lipo8000™ reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Immunohistochemistry analysis

The hippocampus of rats was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. After dewaxing and hydration, the hippocampus sections were treated with citrate antigen retrieval solution (Beyotime), and incubated with 3% bovine serum albumin. The sections were incubated with the primary antibody, PDGFRβ (1:100 dilution; Cat#PA5‐96085; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), ZO‐1 (1:100 dilution; Cat#ab276131; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), occludin (1:200 dilution; Cat#ab216327; Abcam), Aβ (1:100 dilution; Cat#PA5‐19922; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4°C overnight. Then, the sections were incubated with the HRP‐IgG antibody (1:10,000 dilution; Cat#ab6721; Abcam) at room temperature for 2 h, and then stained with diaminobenzidine (DAB; Solarbio, Beijing, China) in darkness for 5 min. Five random fields were selected for immunohistochemistry analysis, and the levels of positive cells in the hippocampus were analyzed using Image J software.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Rats were perfused with PBS until the liver turned white, and then the rats were euthanized with an intraperitoneal injection of 150 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium. The hippocampus was removed from the rats and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The hippocampus sections at the injury site were cut into consecutive slices (15 μm). Slices were blocked with 5% goat serum and then stained with tetramethylrhodamine (TRITC)‐labeled lectin (1:100; Bioss, Beijing, China) at 37°C for 30 min. Cell nuclei were stained with 4′6‐diamadino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI; Boster, Wuhan, China).

To observe the neurons in the hippocampus, the hippocampus sections were stained with anti‐NeuN antibody (1:100 dilution; Cat#ab177487; Abcam) at 4°C overnight, followed by 1 h of incubation with donkey anti‐rabbit IgG‐Alexa Fluor® 647 (1:500 dilution; Cat#ab150075; Abcam). Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI. The fluorescence intensity of lectins and NeuN in the hippocampus was observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The vascular density was analyzed using Image J software, and the quantitative calculation method was the measured fluorescence area/total area of hippocampal tissue × 100.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from the hippocampus using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein samples (25 μg) were separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE gel electrophoresis, and transferred onto the PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, including PDGFRβ (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#PA5‐96085; Thermo Fisher Scientific), ZO‐1 (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#ab276131; Abcam), occludin (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#ab216327; Abcam), Calpain‐1 (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#ab108400; Abcam), Calpain‐2 (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#ab126600; Abcam), TXNIP (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#ab188865; Abcam), NLRP3 (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#ab263899; Abcam), caspase‐1 (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#GTX111630; GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA), IL‐1β (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#ab254360; Abcam), amyloid precursor protein (APP; 1:1,000 dilution; Cat#PA1‐84165; Thermo Fisher Scientific), Aβ (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#51‐2700; Thermo Fisher Scientific), α‐secretase (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#ab124695; Abcam), β‐secretase (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#ab183612; Abcam), γ‐secretase (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#ab76083; Abcam), LRP1 (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#ab92544; Abcam), and ABCB1 (1:1,000 dilution; Cat#ab170904; Abcam). Then, the membranes were stained with the goat anti‐rabbit HRP‐IgG antibody (1:10,000 dilution; Cat#ab6721; Abcam) at room temperature for 2 h. Normalization of the data was conducted for the expression of GAPDH (1:10,000 dilution; Cat#ab181602; Abcam). The gray levels of blots were analyzed by Image J software.

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was carried out to detect the levels of inflammatory factors and oxidative stress‐related markers in the hippocampus of rats utilizing ELISA kits. The malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit (Cat#A003‐4‐1), superoxide dismutase (SOD) assay kit (Cat#A001‐3‐2) and nitric oxide (NO) assay kit (Cat#A013‐2‐1) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). The rat IL‐1β ELISA kit (Cat#ER20275S) and rat TNF‐α ELISA kit (Cat#ER20497S) were purchased from Weiaobio (Shanghai, China). Rat NF‐κB (pS536 + Total) ELISA kit (Cat#ab176663) was obtained from Abcam. The microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to detect the absorbance of samples.

Evans blue extravasation and fluorescence

Evans blue extravasation and fluorescence was carried out to detect the permeability of the BBB in rats as a previous study described 23 . The rats were injected with Evans blue dye (2%, 5 mL/kg; Sigma‐Aldrich, St Louis, MO) into the left femoral vein and circulated for 60 min. Then, the rats were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of 40 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium, and then intracardially perfused with PBS. The brain tissues were obtained and weighed. Following homogenization and centrifugation, the supernatant of tissue homogenate was incubated with trichloroacetic acid at 4°C overnight. The absorbance value of the supernatant at 615 nm was quantified by a spectrophotometer.

For Evans blue fluorescence, PBS was replaced by 4% paraformaldehyde after PBS intracardial perfusion. The hippocampus was obtained from rats and sectioned as for the immunofluorescence staining. The red autofluorescence of Evans Blue was observed under a fluorescent microscope (Olympus).

Statistical analysis

Each assay was carried out three times. All values are presented as mean ± SD. The SPSS 22.0 statistical software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Because only two groups were compared in the study, two‐tailed Student's t‐test was used to analyze the statistical difference. A value of P < 0.05 was considered as a significant difference.

RESULTS

Calpeptin treatment elevated BBB integrity in DM rats

In order to determine whether Calpeptin can affect AD‐like symptoms in DM rats, we established a DM rat model by administration of STZ combined with the HFHS diet. In addition, in order to evaluate the effect of Calpeptin in DM rats, we selected the DB rats injected with the DMSO as the control, and carried out a Morris water maze test to analyze the spatial learning and memory ability of DM rats. The escape latency of DM rats was reduced by Calpeptin treatment. All rats exhibited similar swimming speed (Figure S1A,B). Moreover, compared with DM rats injected with the DMSO, the number of times that the DM rats crossed the platform and the time that DM rats spent in the target quadrant were significantly increased in the presence of Calpeptin (Figure S1C,D). These results indicated Calpeptin treatment improved the spatial learning and memory ability, and mitigated the cognitive dysfunction in DM rats.

The influence of Calpeptin on the expression of pericyte marker PDGFRβ in the hippocampus of DM rats was analyzed by immunohistochemistry. As shown in Figure 1a, the levels of PDGFRβ‐positive pericytes were significantly decreased in the hippocampus of DM rats. Calpeptin treatment caused an increase of PDGFRβ‐positive pericytes in the hippocampus of DM rats. Western blotting also verified this phenomenon. Calpeptin treatment reversed the down‐regulation of PDGFRβ in the hippocampus of DM rats (Figure 1b). Then, we examined the microvessel density in rats by immunofluorescence analysis. The DM rats exhibited a decrease of microvessel density in the hippocampus. Calpeptin treatment enhanced the microvessel density in the hippocampus of DM rats (Figure 1c). Evans blue is regarded as an indicator of BBB function. We applied Evans blue extravasation and fluorescence to evaluate the BBB integrity. The extravasation and fluorescence intensity of Evans blue was notably increased in the hippocampus of DM rats, which was reduced by Calpeptin treatment (Figure 1d,e). Moreover, the expression of tight junction proteins in the hippocampus was examined by immunohistochemistry and western blotting. Down‐regulation of ZO‐1 and occludin was observed in the hippocampus of DM rats. The expression of ZO‐1 and occludin was increased in DM rats following calpeptin treatment (Figure 1f,g). All these data indicated that Calpeptin treatment elevated BBB integrity in DM rats.

Calpeptin treatment reduced calpain‐2 expression and TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome in DM rats

To test if Calpeptin treatment could affect Calpains in DM rats, western blotting was conducted to show the expression of Calpains in DM rats. As shown in Figure 2a–c, Calpain‐1 showed no change, while the expression of Calpain‐2 was notably increased in the hippocampus of DM rats. Calpeptin treatment notably inhibited the expression of Calpain‐1 and Calpain‐2 in DM rats. Then, the expression of TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome‐related proteins in the hippocampus of DM rats was evaluated. Increased expression of TXNIP, NLRP3, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β was observed in the hippocampus of DM rats. Calpeptin treatment led to a down‐regulation of TXNIP, NLRP3, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β in hippocampus of DM rats, as determined by western blotting (Figure 2d–h). Taken together, Calpeptin treatment reduced Calpain‐2 expression and TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome in DM rats.

Calpeptin treatment inhibited Calpain‐2 expression and TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome in HG‐treated rat hippocampal neuronal cells

To determine the influence of Calpeptin treatment on Calpain‐2 and TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome of HG‐treated rat hippocampal neuronal cells, we isolated hippocampal neuronal cells from suckling mice. Hippocampal neuronal cells were treated with high glucose (HG, 25 mM) or HG + calpeptin for 24 h to mimic diabetes mellitus conditions in vitro. In HG‐treated hippocampus neuronal cells, Calpain‐1 showed no change, while the expression of Calpain‐2 was notably elevated. Calpeptin treatment notably inhibited the expression of Calpain‐1 and Calpain‐2 (Figure S2A–C). Moreover, the expression of TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome‐related proteins was evaluated in HG‐treated hippocampus neuronal cells. Calpeptin treatment led to a down‐regulation of TXNIP, NLRP3, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β in the HG‐treated hippocampus neuronal cells (Figure S2D–H).

Thereafter, to clarify which subtype of Calpain truly mediated the function of calpeptin in regulating TNXP/NLRP3 inflammasomes, we constructed sh‐Calpain‐1 and sh‐Calpain‐2 to knock down Calpain‐1 and Calpain‐2 in hippocampus neuronal cells. The western blotting results showed that the expression level of Calpain‐1 and Calpain‐2 was notably reduced (Figure S3A,B). Transfection of sh‐Calpain‐1 or sh‐Calpain‐2 led to a down‐regulation of TXNIP, NLRP3, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β in HG‐treated hippocampus neuronal cells, while the down‐regulation was particularly significant after knocking down Calpain‐2 (Figure S3C). Taken together, Calpeptin treatment reduced Calpain‐2 expression and TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome in HG‐treated hippocampus neuronal cells, Calpain‐2 truly mediated the function of calpeptin in regulating TNXP/NLRP3 inflammasomes.

Calpeptin treatment improved the spatial learning and memory ability in DM rats through regulating TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome

To determine whether Calpeptin treatment alleviated AD‐like cognitive impairments and pathologies in DM rats, the spatial learning and memory ability of DM rats was analyzed by the Morris water maze test. The route taken by rats to reach the platform is shown in Figure 3a. The escape latency of DM rats was reduced by Calpeptin treatment. TXNIP overexpression caused a longer escape latency in DM rats (Figure 3b). Meanwhile, we have demonstrated the successful overexpression of TXNIP through qRT‐PCR and WB detection (Figure S4A,B). All rats exhibited a similar swimming speed (Figure 3c). Compared with DM rats, the escape number and the time spent in the target quadrant were increased in DM rats in the presence of Calpeptin. TXNIP overexpression reversed the phenomenon following Calpeptin treatment (Figure 3d,e). Taken together, Calpeptin treatment improved the spatial learning and memory ability.

Furthermore, it has been confirmed that Calpeptin treatment can significantly improve AD like pathological changes in DM model rats. The results of immunofluorescence staining showed that Calpeptin treatment could improve the neuronal loss in the hippocampus of DM rats (Figure 3f). The expression of APP and Aβ was reduced by Calpeptin treatment. TXNIP overexpression caused a higher expression in DM rats (Figure 3g). The main pathological feature of AD is Aβ deposition in the brain. Our immunohistochemical test results indicated that Calpeptin treatment could effectively eliminate the deposited Aβ in the hippocampal cells of DM rats, while the effect was weakened by TXNIP overexpression (Figure 3h). Then, the expression of Aβ‐related proteins in the hippocampus of DM rats was evaluated. Western blotting results revealed that the protein levels of the Aβ clearance‐related factors LRP1 and ABCB1 were upregulated, Aβ deposition‐related factors α‐secretase was elevated, while β‐secretase and γ‐secretase were decreased by Calpeptin treatment. However, these expression levels were reversed following TXNIP overexpression (Figure 3i). These findings demonstrated that Calpeptin treatment improved the spatial learning and memory ability in DM rats through regulating TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome.

Calpeptin reduced oxidative stress and inflammation in DM rats through regulating TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome

Then, markers of oxidative stress were detected in DM rats. Calpeptin treatment reduced the levels of MDA and elevated the levels of SOD and NO in DM rats. The influence of Calpeptin on oxidative stress markers was abrogated by TXNIP overexpression (Figure 4a–c). The levels of inflammatory factors IL‐1β, TNF‐α, and NF‐κB (pS536) were decreased in DM rats in the presence of Calpeptin, which was abolished by TXNIP overexpression (Figure 4d–f). Additionally, the expression of inflammasome proteins was evaluated by western blotting. Calpeptin treatment led to a down‐regulation of TXNIP, NLRP3, caspase‐1, and IL‐1β in DM rats. These proteins were up‐regulated in DM rats following Calpeptin combined with TXNIP overexpression (Figure 4g–k). These findings demonstrated that Calpeptin reduced oxidative stress and inflammation in DM rats through regulating TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome.

Calpeptin treatment repressed permeability of BBB in DM rats through regulating TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome

Finally, we examined the effect of Calpeptin treatment on permeability of BBB in DM rats. The expression of pericyte marker PDGFRβ in DM rats was assessed. Immunohistochemistry analysis and western blotting results revealed that Calpeptin treatment elevated the expression of PDGFRβ in DM rats. TXNIP overexpression inhibited the expression of PDGFRβ in Calpeptin‐treated DM rats (Figure 5a,b). Then, we examined the microvessel density in rats by immunofluorescence analysis. The Calpeptin treatment‐mediated increase of microvessel density in DM rats was repressed by TXNIP overexpression (Figure 5c). Interestingly, the extravasation and fluorescence intensity of Evans blue was notably reduced in DM rats in the presence of Calpeptin treatment. TXNIP up‐regulation elevated the extravasation and fluorescence intensity of Evans blue in Calpeptin‐treated DM rats (Figure 5d,e). Furthermore, the expression of tight junction proteins in DM rats was examined by immunohistochemistry and western blotting. Compared with DM rats, Calpeptin treatment caused an up‐regulation of ZO‐1 and occludin in DM rats. These proteins were decreased in DM rats following Calpeptin treatment and TXNIP‐OE (Figure 5f,g). Thus, Calpeptin treatment repressed the permeability of BBB in DM rats through regulating TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome.

DISCUSSION

Numerous studies have demonstrated a significant association between type 2 diabetes mellitus and Alzheimer's disease, and type 2 diabetes mellitus is considered to be an accelerator of AD 11 , 24 . In the present work, we elucidated the mechanism of action of Calpeptin in AD‐like complication of DM. The Calpain inhibitor Calpeptin effectively reduced the expression of PDGFRβ and the permeability of BBB, while it enhanced the microvessel density in DM rats. The expression of Calpain‐2 and TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome in DM rats was inhibited by Calpeptin treatment. Additionally, Calpeptin treatment also led to a decrease in neuronal loss and Aβ accumulation, as well as an improvement in spatial learning and memory ability in DM rats. The influence conferred by Calpeptin treatment was reversed by TXNIP overexpression. Thus, Calpeptin treatment alleviated AD‐like symptoms in DM rats through regulating TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome.

Calpeptin, as a Calpain inhibitor, participates in the progression of many diseases by inhibiting the activity of Calpain 25 . For instance, Calpeptin treatment repressed the expression of Calpain‐2 in pancreatic cancer cells and pancreatic stellate cells, and disrupted the cancer–stromal interaction, thereby inhibiting the migration of pancreatic cancer 26 . Calpeptin mitigated acrylamide‐induced neuropathy in rats by inhibiting the activity of Calpain and neurofilament degradation 27 . Calpeptin exerted neuroprotective effects in early cerebral injury after subarachnoid hemorrhage through inhibiting neuronal apoptosis 28 . Our previous research has confirmed that Calpeptin treatment can effectively improve the cognitive impairments of DM rats. This work further confirmed that Calpeptin treatment can reduce neuronal loss, oxidative stress damage, and Aβ accumulation in DM rats. Calpeptin treatment alleviated AD‐like symptoms in DM rats, which may be attributed to the regulation of TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome.

The BBB is an important component of the neurovascular network, whose damage can lead to neurological deficits 29 . Pericytes, a kind of cell with multidirectional potential, are located outside the vascular endothelium and share the basement membrane with the vascular endothelium, which are an important component and regulatory factor of the BBB 30 , 31 . Pericytes play a significant role in various central nervous system injuries, including AD and cerebral ischemia 32 , 33 . In this work, we found that Calpeptin enhanced the PDGFRβ‐positive pericytes and elevated the microvasular density in the hippocampus of DM rats. Moreover, Calpeptin treatment reduced the permeability of BBB in DM rats. Thus, these data suggested that Calpeptin may alleviate the AD‐like symptoms in DM rats by regulating the permeability of the BBB.

The main cause of AD is widespread oxidative stress and inflammation in the central nervous system 34 . Additionally, the levels of oxidative stress are significantly increased in DM patients 35 , 36 . Diabetes mellitus enhances the levels of inflammatory factors such as TNF‐α and IL‐6, triggers the degradation of tight junction protein in the brain, increases inflammation and white blood cell infiltration, promotes the destruction of BBB, and ultimately leads to cognitive dysfunction 37 . Inflammation is an important mechanism that mediates BBB damage 38 . Vitamin D and E, as well as their combination, can reduce the oxidative stress levels and inhibit neuronal loss, which contributes to improving memory and learning deficit in AD rats 39 . Inhibition of oxidative stress and inflammatory response can improve cognitive functions in AD animal model 40 , 41 . In this work, we found that oxidative stress and TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome were significantly increased in DM rats, which was effectively eliminated by Calpeptin treatment. The expression of TXNIP, NLRP3, caspase‐1 and IL‐1β was elevated in DM rats, which was inhibited by calpeptin treatment. It suggested that Calpeptin reduced TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome in DM rats. A previous study has reported that TXNIP is highly expressed in hippocampus of AD rats, knocking down TXNIP is beneficial for AD treatment 42 . TXNIP contributes to inflammation to aggravate AD development 43 . TXNIP overexpression reversed the influence of calpeptin treatment on AD‐like symptoms in DM rats. Thus, Calpeptin treatment may improve cognitive functions in DM rats by repressing oxidative stress and TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome.

In conclusion, this work demonstrates that Calpeptin reduces the permeability of the BBB and improves the cognitive function in AD‐like complications of DM rats by regulating TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome. Therefore, Calpeptin may be a potential drug for the treatment of AD‐like complications of diabetes mellitus.
