Effect of aerobic and Pilates exercise on balance, gait, functional capacity, lower extremity strength, fear of falling, sleep quality, and quality of life in older adults
Authors: Abdullah, Witaya Mathiyakom, Tsuyoshi Asai, Nasrullah Khan, Irfan Ullah, Anong Tantisuwat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe primary objective was to compare the effects of aerobic and Pilates exercise programs on balance, gait performance, functional capacity, lower extremity strength, fear of falling, sleep quality, and quality of life in older adults. A secondary objective was to determine which exercise modality yields greater improvements in these outcomes, thereby providing evidence to guide the selection of effective exercise interventions for promoting health and well-being among the older adults.MethodsForty-four older adults, aged 60–69 years, were randomly assigned into either the aerobic (n = 22) or Pilates exercise group (n = 22). The aerobic group trained three sessions per week (30 min of active exercise per session; 90 min/week), while the Pilates group (PG) trained two sessions per week (45 min of active exercise per session comprising mat, standing, and TheraBand components; 90 min/week), each for eight weeks, yielding equivalent total active exercise volumes (720 min per group). The outcomes of Mini-Balance Evaluation System Test (MBEST), Timed Up and Go test (TUGT), 2-Minute Walk Test (2MWT), 10-Meter Walk Test (10MWT), Five-Time Sit-to-Stand test (5xSTST) and self-reported questionnaires of the Fall Efficacy Scale International (FES-I), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and WHOQOL-Brief were collected at baseline and post-treatment.ResultsGroups were equivalent at baseline across all measures. Significant Group × Time interaction effects were observed for gait velocity (10MWT) and dynamic balance (MBEST), with the aerobic group demonstrating significantly greater improvements in both (10MWT: p = 0.016; MBEST: p < 0.001). ANCOVA adjusting for baseline score, age, and BMI confirmed these between-group differences (10MWT: p = 0.012; MBEST: p < 0.001). Significant time main effects (p < 0.05) were observed for all remaining outcome measures (TUGT, 5×STST, 2MWT, FES-I, PSQI, all WHOQOL-Brief domains), indicating meaningful within-group improvements shared across both interventions.ConclusionBoth aerobic and Pilates exercise programs produced clinically meaningful improvements in gait velocity, functional capacity, and balance in older adults, with the aerobic group demonstrating significantly greater improvements in dynamic balance. Both interventions additionally reduced fall-risk classifications based on clinical threshold analyses. The findings of this study suggest that aerobic exercise may be the preferred modality for optimizing functional mobility and balance in community-dwelling older adults, though both modalities offer valuable and clinically meaningful health benefits. Future trials equalizing both volume and frequency are warranted to confirm modality-specific effects with full design equivalence.Trial RegistrationThis Clinical Trial is registered with Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR) approved by WHO. Registration number TCTR20240403002. Date of registration 13 March 2024. URL https//www.thaiclinicaltrials.org/#.

Introduction

The population of older individuals is rapidly increasing in both number and proportion worldwide [1]. The global population aged ≥ 60 years was ~ 1 billion in 2019, projected to reach 2.1 billion by 2050 [2]. By 2050, two-thirds of the global population aged 60 and above will reside in underdeveloped and developing countries [3]. Pakistan, the world’s fifth most populous country, had 6% of its population aged 65 or older in 2019, a figure projected to triple by 2050 [4, 5]. The aging process is anticipated to have a significant impact on the economies and health systems of nations [6].

Maintaining functional independence and quality of life are primary priorities for older adults [7]. Falls and associated injuries in older adults have been recognized as a public health concern [8]. Falls affect 30–50% of community-dwelling older adults annually, with ~ 10% resulting in serious injuries such as hip fractures or traumatic brain injury, and over 80% of fall-related deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries [9, 10]. Falls and their consequences significantly affects the QOL along with individual, social, and healthcare expenses [11].

Exercise is a key intervention for enhancing balance, reducing falls, improving sleep, and increasing life quality in older adults [12, 13]. Various exercises, including Pilates, aerobics, resistance, Yoga, Tai Chi, and multicomponent training improves balance, reduces falls, enhances sleep, and increases quality of life in older adults [14–16]. A recently published trial concluded that aerobic training improves sleep quality and overall quality of life [17]. Additionally, aerobic exercise has been shown to reduce the fear of falling [18]. Pilates based exercise primarily emphasizes core strength, flexibility, and body control [19], and demonstrated enhanced balance, mobility, and postural stability, reducing the risk of falls in older adults [20]. This is further supported by a systematic review which indicated that Pilates may significantly improve sleep quality [21].

According to the published literature, there is a scarcity of clinical trials comparing aerobic and Pilates exercises regarding their effects on balance, gait, and functional capacity. Further research in this area helps in identification of most effective exercise interventions for improving the health and well-being of older individuals. Therefore, this study aimed to compare Pilates and aerobic exercise programs on balance, gait performance, functional capacity, lower extremity strength, fear of falling, sleep quality, and QOL in older adults. The results of this study may provide insight into which exercise is more effective in improving these outcomes in this geriatric population.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a single-blind parallel randomized controlled trial in which only the outcome assessor (a trained physiotherapist independent of the exercise delivery team, responsible for administering all pre and post-treatment outcome measurements) was kept blind to group allocation. The trial was conducted at the Physiotherapy Lab 2, Abasyn University, and a public park nearby. According to the Helsinki Declaration, the Institutional Ethics Committee of Khyber Medical University reviewed and approved the study protocol (No. KMU/IPHSS/Ethics/2024/DH/0196). Prior to testing and training, written informed consent was obtained from each participant; all were informed about the objectives, procedures, and potential risks of the study. The protocol was registered in the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (no. TCTR20240403002).

Recruitment and eligibility criteria

Participants were enrolled from community-based populations in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa Province, Pakistan, by inviting them through advertisements (social media, Google Forms, and online forums or groups) and announcements in community centers, local events, and mosques to participate. Participants were recruited between 1st to 13th April, 2024. After recruitment, the intervention was conducted from 14 April to 30 June 2024.

Sample selection

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria involved (i) Participants aged 60–69 years, capable of following verbal instructions and walking independently without assistance were included in the study; (ii) subjects with no current musculoskeletal pain or injury requiring treatment or limiting mobility in the past 3 months (iii) subjects with no history of cardiovascular disease (as screened by the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q), hospitalization within the past three months, and able to understand English or Urdu (iv) who have self-reported engagement in less than 30 min of moderate physical activity, fewer than three times (< 600 MET-minute/ week), according to International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-SF) and no involvement in structured exercise programs in the past six months (v) patients with no untreated sleep disorder; screened by Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) and additional clinical history and are non-smokers; (vi) can provide written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were (i) Individuals with visual or auditory impairments (unable to pass the Snellen eye chart or Whisper test), severe chronic, cognitive, or neurological diseases (such as Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, Huntington’s, multiple sclerosis, or epilepsy) (ii) significant health issues like the presence of a pacemaker, uncontrolled hypertension (systolic BP > 180mmHg and diastolic BP > 110mmHg), obesity with a BMI of 25 or higher based on the World Health Organization (WHO) Asian classification (iii) who cannot maintain at least 85% compliance with the exercise session.

Sample size

Using the G-Power calculator version 3.1.9.4, predicated on statistical assumptions derived from a prior study by Sousa et al. (2017). The effect size of 1.00 for 5xSTST was taken [18]. The alpha error probability (α) was set as 0.05, and the power analysis was set as 0.95. A total sample size of 16 was calculated for each group. Considering a drop-out rate of approximately 40% after allocation, we recruited 22 participants for each group.

Randomization and blinding

Recruited participants were randomly allocated to either the Aerobic exercise arm or the Pilates arm (each group n = 22) at a ratio of 1:1. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated randomization sequence using OpenEpi online software. Before starting the intervention, the instructors who supervised the training programs opened sealed opaque envelopes containing the name of the corresponding group. Assessors were blinded to group allocation during the trial period to minimize performance and detection bias. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Intervention

The aerobic and Pilates instructors were responsible for their respective groups, ensuring correct and consistent administration of interventions for 8 consecutive weeks, monitoring participant safety and compliance, and keeping detailed logs. All subjects were notified 3 days in advance about the venue, date, and time of data collection. All participants were asked to maintain their usual daily-life activities during the intervention period.

Aerobic exercise program

Before starting aerobic exercises, participants followed a 2-week conditioning program consisting of low-intensity walking (three sessions per week, 20 min per session) at approximately 50–60% of maximum safe heart rate, designed to gradually acclimate participants to exercise and reduce injury risk. Afterward, the main exercise program commenced. Each session started with a 10-minute warm-up and ended with a 10-minute cool-down. The aerobic exercise sessions consisted of a 30-minute moderate-intensity walk, targeting 64% to 76% of the maximum safe heart rate [22]. Maximum age-related heart rate was estimated by subtracting age from 220. There were 5 rounds of moderate-intensity walking, with a 1-minute rest after each round. Exercise intensity was monitored every five minutes using a pulse oximeter. Participants followed this routine 3 days per week for 8 consecutive weeks.

Pilates program

Before starting the Pilates program, participants followed a 2-week conditioning program consisting of introductory Pilates movements (two sessions per week, 30 min per session) at low intensity, designed to familiarize participants with core activation principles and basic mat postures. After that, the main exercise program commenced. The Pilates program, based on key principles and tailored for older adults, was demonstrated and explained by a certified instructor. Participants (n = 19) were divided into two groups of 10 and 9 in each group. The exercises included mat exercises, standing chair-assisted exercises, and exercises with a medium-resistance TheraBand to allow for optimal participation. The program was offered twice weekly for 8 consecutive weeks, with each session lasting 60 min under the supervision of a certified Pilates instructor.

The Pilates program was divided into 3 stages: the first week for beginners, followed by 3 weeks of intermediate, and the final 4 weeks of advanced. If a participant completed the current level, they were promoted to the next. The exercise session began with a 10-minute warm-up of light stretching, followed by 25 min of active and resisted exercises on the mat, concentrating on strengthening the extremities and core and improving the flexibility of spinal segments. A 10-minute standing exercise to enhance lower extremity stability and strength followed by TheraBand exercises in sitting and standing positions to strengthen upper, lower, and core muscles. The session ended with a 5-minute cool-down. Table 1 outlines the key principles of Pilates [20].

Safety

Pilates and aerobics instructors inquire participants about any adverse events during the aerobic and Pilates interventions. Adverse events were defined as any undesired or unintended signs, symptoms, or conditions occurring during or after the intervention without clear causality. Participants were monitored for musculoskeletal pain, excessive fatigue, or discomfort during and after sessions. Any reported issues were documented, and the seriousness of adverse events and their potential link to the exercise interventions were determined by investigator.

Outcomes

The Timed Up and Go test (TUGT) was used to determine a dynamic standing balance and gait. The participants performed three test trials, and the average time (recorded with a digital stopwatch) of the three trials was used for data analysis. The Five-Time Sit-to-Stand test (5xSTST) assessed lower extremity strength and functional mobility in older adults [23]. The 5xSTST has excellent intra-rater reliability (ICC: 0.914–0.933) and test-retest reliability (ICC range: 0.988–0.995) in healthy older adults [24]. For 5xSTST, a stopwatch was used to determine the time to complete five sit-to-stand-to-sit cycles. An average of 2 trials of 5xSTST, with a 3-minute rest period between trials, was recorded.

The 2-Minute Walk test (2MWT) measured self-paced walking ability and functional capacity [25]. Participants were briefed to walk as fast as possible without an assistive device for 2 min, and the distance covered in meters was recorded to assess their performance [26]. Two practice walks were conducted before performing a single recorded 2MWT to ensure familiarity with the test procedure [27]. Functional mobility and gait were determined with 10-meter Walk tests (10MWT) [28]. Participants walked 20 m without an assistive device, with time measured for the intermediate 10 m, allowing 5 m for acceleration and deceleration. The mean time for two trials was noted to the nearest hundredth of a second, with rest allowed between trials if needed [29].

The Mini-Balance Evaluation System Test (MBEST) was used for comprehensive balance assessment [30]. The MBEST consists of 14 tasks covering various balance subsystems, such as responses to external perturbations, anticipatory postural adjustments, gait stability, and sensory orientation, with a total possible score of 28 points [31], with higher scores indicating greater ability to control balance. The Fall Efficacy Scale-International (FES-I), a self-reported questionnaire, assessed the fear of falling. It has excellent sensitivity (73-87.1%) and moderate specificity (50–69.0%) in identifying poor physical function [32]. A high score on the scale means a greater fear of falling and an increased risk of falling [33, 34]. Sleep quality was assessed by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), which comprises of seven sleep components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction [35]. A global PSQI score of ≤ 5 indicates good sleep, and a score of 6 or above indicates poor sleep [36]. The PSQI and FES-I questionnaires were given either through in-person interviews or self-administration, based on the participants’ abilities and preferences.

The World Health Organization Quality of Life Brief Version (WHOQOL-Brief), a self-administered questionnaire, was used to assess the QOL with excellent reliability and validity [37, 38]. It consists of four domains: physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and environmental health. The score of each item was stipulated as a five-point ordinal scale and then transformed linearly to a 0–100 scale [39]. Both the transformed score and scores of the four domains were used in the statistical analyses. A high score indicates a good QOL.

Statistical analysis

The intention-to-treat analysis, including all subjects, was used for data analysis, with the last observation carried forward method to handle missing data. The data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Descriptive statistics including mean and standard deviation (SD) were reported for quantitative variables; frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Baseline group equivalence was confirmed using independent-samples t-tests for continuous outcomes and chi-square or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables that were not normally distributed. Mixed ANOVA was used to assess the effects of time (baseline and post-treatment), group (aerobic vs. PG), and the Group × Time interaction on each outcome variable; partial eta-squared (ηp²) was reported as the effect size estimate. Post-hoc Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons were conducted when interaction effects were statistically significant. Effect sizes for within-group changes were quantified using Cohen’s d (paired); d values of 0.2–0.49, 0.5–0.79, and ≥ 0.80 were interpreted as small, medium, and large, respectively. 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for between-group differences in change scores were calculated. For outcomes with non-normal distributions (MBEST and FES-I), non-parametric sensitivity analyses were conducted: Wilcoxon Signed-Rank tests for within-group comparisons and Mann-Whitney U tests for between-group change score comparisons; rank-biserial correlation (r) was reported as the non-parametric effect size. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed on post-treatment scores for primary outcomes (MBEST, 10MWT, TUGT, 2MWT), with baseline score, age, and BMI as covariates, to assess between-group differences independent of potential confounders. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed with MBEST change score as the dependent variable to identify independent predictors of balance improvement; predictors included group allocation, age, BMI, baseline MBEST, change in FES-I, and change in PSQI. Pearson correlation analyses were additionally conducted between change scores to explore associations among changes in fear of falling (FES-I), sleep quality (PSQI), and quality of life (WHOQOL-Brief domains). A Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) was conducted on all eight change scores simultaneously to assess the overall multivariate group effect. Clinical significance was assessed by comparing mean change scores and individual responder rates against published Minimum Clinically Important Difference (MCID) values and Reliable Change Indices (RCI) for each outcome. Clinical fall-risk reclassification was evaluated using published threshold values: TUGT > 12 s, MBEST < 21 points, and 10MWT < 0.8 m/sec. Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS version 29.0.1.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Python (version 3.11; packages: scipy, pingouin, statsmodels). A statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

A total of 108 individuals were screened for eligibility. Of these, 47 did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 17 did not consent to participate. In total, 44 individuals met all the eligibility criteria and were randomly assigned into two intervention groups: the aerobic group (n = 22) and the PG (n = 22). During the intervention phase, two participants from the aerobic and three participants from the PG dropped out (Fig. 1). All exercise sessions were monitored by trained professionals to ensure participant safety, and no adverse events or injuries were reported during the intervention phase.

The mean (SD) age of the participants was 64.39 (2.46) years, with no significant between-group differences in any demographic characteristic (all p > 0.05). These results indicated comparable baseline socio-demographic characteristics for evaluating the effects of the interventions on the outcomes.

Table 3 presents the mixed ANOVA results of all outcome measures. Significant Group × Time interaction effects were only observed for 10MWT (F (1, 42) = 8.03, p = 0.007, ηp² = 0.160) and MBEST (F (1, 42) = 8.47, p = 0.006, ηp² = 0.168). Both represent medium-to-large effect sizes. The results also demonstrated significant time main effects (p < 0.05) for all outcome measures, indicating that both groups improved over time across all variables. No significant group main effect was observed (all p > 0.05). A supplementary MANOVA conducted on all eight change scores simultaneously yielded a non-significant multivariate group effect (Wilks’ λ = 0.674, F (8, 35) = 2.115, p = 0.061), consistent with the pattern of intervention-specific rather than global differential effects between the two groups.

In the aerobic, gait velocity measured using the 10MWT was significantly faster (p < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 1.979, very large effect) at post-treatment (0.97 ± 0.04 m/sec) than at baseline (0.87 ± 0.03 m/sec), representing a mean improvement of 0.104 m/sec. Likewise, for the PG, gait velocity was also significantly faster (p < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 1.087, large effect) at post-treatment (0.94 ± 0.03 m/sec) than at baseline (0.88 ± 0.04 m/sec), representing a mean improvement of 0.059 m/sec. At post-treatment, the aerobic demonstrated significantly faster gait velocity than the PG (p = 0.016; mean between-group difference in change score: 0.045 m/sec, 95% CI: 0.013–0.078). The aerobic mean improvement of 0.104 m/sec exceeded the published Minimum Clinically Important Difference (MCID) for gait speed in older adults, confirming clinical as well as statistical significance. ANCOVA, controlling for baseline gait speed, age, and BMI, confirmed a statistically significant between-group difference favoring the aerobic (adjusted group coefficient: −0.027 m/sec, 95% CI: −0.047 to − 0.006; p = 0.012, R² = 0.249). These results indicate that both groups significantly improved gait performance and functional mobility from baseline to post-treatment, with the aerobic exercise group demonstrating a significantly and clinically meaningfully greater effect than the Pilates exercise group. Furthermore, clinical fall-risk threshold analysis showed that no participants in either group fell below the 0.8 m/sec gait-speed fall-risk threshold at either time point, indicating that this sample maintained adequate baseline gait speed; both groups further improved above this threshold by post-treatment.

Both groups demonstrated significant improvement in dynamic balance as indicated by the MBEST scores. In aerobics, the MBEST score was significantly higher (p < 0.001; Cohen’s d = 1.713, very large effect) at post-treatment (26.09 ± 1.15) than at baseline (22.64 ± 1.65), representing a mean improvement of 3.45 points. In the PG, the MBEST score was also significantly higher (p = 0.021; Cohen’s d = 0.665, medium effect) at post-treatment (24.64 ± 1.14) than at baseline (23.09 ± 2.00), representing a mean improvement of 1.55 points. Wilcoxon Signed-Rank non-parametric tests confirmed both within-group improvements (aerobic: W = 0.0, p < 0.001; PG: W = 23.0, p = 0.006). At post-treatment, the MBEST score was significantly greater in the aerobic than the PG (p < 0.001; mean between-group difference in change score: 1.91 points, 95% CI: 0.58–3.23). Mann-Whitney U sensitivity analysis confirmed this between-group difference (U = 364.0, p = 0.004, effect r = 0.504, large non-parametric effect). ANCOVA, controlling for baseline MBEST, age, and BMI, confirmed the aerobic advantage after covariate adjustment (adjusted coefficient: −1.43 points, 95% CI: −2.15 to − 0.70; p < 0.001, R² = 0.310). Multiple regression analysis identified group allocation (β = −1.488, p < 0.001) and baseline MBEST (β = −1.033, p < 0.001) as the only significant predictors of balance improvement, with the full model explaining 78.2% of variance in MBEST change scores (Adj. R² = 0.746). Clinical fall-risk threshold analysis showed that all participants with impaired dynamic balance (MBEST < 21) at baseline four in the aerobic and three in the PG improved beyond the clinical threshold by post-treatment, achieving a 0% impairment rate in both groups. Taken together, the aerobic demonstrated a significant, clinically meaningfully, and robustly greater improvement in dynamic balance and postural control than the PG, confirmed across parametric, non-parametric, ANCOVA-adjusted, and regression-based analyses.

For other physical performance measures (TUGT, 5xSTST, and 2MWT) and self-reported outcomes (FES-I, PSQI, and WHOQOL-Brief), significant time main effects were observed (Table 3), reflecting meaningful within-group improvements shared across both interventions, without significant Group × Time interaction effects (all p > 0.05). Nonetheless, within-group effect sizes were clinically noteworthy. For TUGT, both the aerobic (d = − 0.834, large effect; mean improvement: −1.63 s) and PG (d = − 0.676, medium effect; mean improvement: −0.86 s) demonstrated meaningful reductions in completion time; the aerobic mean improvement exceeded the published MCID of − 1.4 s. Clinical fall-risk threshold analysis for TUGT (> 12 s) demonstrated that the proportion of aerobic participants classified as at elevated fall risk decreased from 95% at baseline to 55% at post-treatment (a 40-percentage-point reduction), while the PG decreased from 100% to 64% (a 36-percentage-point reduction). For 5xSTST, both aerobic (d = − 0.274, small effect) and PG (d = − 0.520, medium effect) improved, with 45% of aerobic and 27% of PG participants individually exceeding the MCID of − 2.0 s. For 2MWT, both aerobic (d = + 0.991, large effect; mean improvement: +10.50 m) and PG (d = + 0.690, medium effect; mean improvement: +6.73 m) significantly improved endurance; the aerobic mean improvement of + 10.50 m exceeded the published MCID of + 9.0 m, with 55% of aerobic participants achieving individually clinically meaningful improvement. For FES-I, both aerobic (d = − 0.753, medium effect) and PG (d = − 0.707, medium effect) significantly reduced fear of falling; 73% of PG participants individually exceeded the MCID of − 3.5 points, indicating broad individual-level clinical benefit for fall efficacy in the PG. For PSQI, both aerobic (d = − 0.469, small effect) and PG (d = − 0.218, small effect) improved sleep quality, with 64% of aerobic and 68% of PG participants individually exceeding the MCID of − 1.5 points. For WHOQOL-Brief Physical Domain, both aerobic (d = + 0.801, large effect) and PG (d = + 0.335, small effect) demonstrated improvements in quality of life. Correlation analysis among change scores revealed that improvements in FES-I and PSQI were moderately correlated (r = 0.413), and both were significantly associated with improvements in WHOQOL-D1 (r = − 0.464 and − 0.458, respectively), suggesting that reducing fear of falling and improving sleep quality are synergistic pathways to enhanced quality of life in older adults. Examination of the mean data within each group further confirmed that both intervention groups demonstrated positive improvement in all outcome measures from baseline to post-treatment.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to compare the effects of aerobic and Pilates exercise on various factors contributing to maintaining functional independence in older adults, including balance, gait velocity, functional mobility, lower extremity strength, fear of falling, sleep quality, and quality of life. Our findings demonstrate that both interventions produced significant and clinically meaningful within-group improvements across all outcome measures. However, significant Group × Time interaction effects were observed only for gait velocity (10MWT) and dynamic balance (MBEST), with the aerobic exercise group exhibiting significantly greater improvements in these two outcomes than the PG. These between-group differences were confirmed as robust across multiple analytical approaches: parametric mixed ANOVA, non-parametric sensitivity tests, ANCOVA after controlling for age and BMI, and multiple regression modelling Furthermore, within-group effect sizes were very large for the aerobic group (), compared to large and medium effects respectively for the PG, highlighting not only statistical but substantial practical significance. Clinical fall-risk threshold analyses demonstrated that both interventions produced meaningful real-world reclassification: the proportion of participants classified as at elevated fall risk by TUGT (> 12 s) decreased from 95% to 55% in the aerobic group and from 100% to 64% in the PG; and both groups entirely eliminated impaired-balance classifications (MBEST < 21) by post-treatment. The supplementary MANOVA confirmed that the aerobic advantage was domain-specific concentrated in balance and gait rather than representing a broad global superiority. It should be noted that total active exercise volume was equivalent across groups (aerobic: 3 sessions/week × 30 min = 90 min/week; Pilates: 2 sessions/week × 45 min active = 90 min/week; 720 min total each). Both groups received the same exercise dose, which was appropriate for a previously sedentary population. The remaining between-group design difference-session frequency (3×/week aerobic vs. 2×/week Pilates) is acknowledged as a minor limitation, as more frequent sessions may independently promote neuromuscular adaptation; this does not, however, overturn the modality-specific interpretation, which was further supported by ANCOVA controlling for baseline scores, age, and BMI, and by the absence of a comparable literature precedent for head-to-head comparison of these modalities on MBEST and 10MWT outcomes.

Gait velocity, an important indicator of functional mobility in older adults, is improved by both aerobic and Pilates exercise. Gait velocity has been established as a clinically meaningful fall risk screening tool in older adults, with slower gait speed associated with higher fall incidence and poorer functional outcomes [40]. Our results showed that, in both groups, the gait speed at post-treatment was significantly faster than at baseline. These results are consistent with previous studies, where both aerobic [41] and Pilates exercise [42] were shown to improve gait velocity. Although both interventions significantly improved gait velocity, our results showed a significantly greater improvement in the aerobic group than in the PG, as reflected in a significantly faster gait velocity at post-treatment. The distinct characteristics of these exercises may explain these differences. Aerobic exercises, which involve cyclic movements and continuous stepping patterns, have been associated with enhanced gait mechanics, stride length, and walking stability, contributing to more efficient walking [41]. In this study, the dynamic, repetitive, and endurance-based nature of aerobic exercise may have contributed to improvements in neuromuscular coordination, cardiovascular efficiency, and lower limb power, all of which are important for gait speed improvements [43], Additionally, aerobic training involves continuous, rhythmic, weight-bearing movements that may support cardiovascular endurance, neuromuscular coordination, and proprioceptive feedback, potentially contributing to enhanced gait speed and functional mobility [44]. In contrast, Pilates primarily targets muscle endurance and core stability but lacks the high-repetition, weight-bearing nature of aerobic exercises that directly influence efficiency [45]. While Pilates focuses on isolated muscle activation, core control, and joint stability [43], these benefits may not translate as effectively to improvements in dynamic gait parameters [46]. These differences in exercise characteristics likely explain why aerobic achieved greater improvements in gait speed compared to PG.

Similarly, both aerobic exercise and Pilates exercise used in this study also significantly improve dynamic balance and postural adjustment in older adults as indicated by significant increases in the MBEST scores from baseline to post-treatment. These results are consistent with those previously reported [47, 48]. Aerobic exercise also demonstrated a significantly greater improvement in dynamic balance and postural control in older adults than Pilates exercise. The weight-bearing, high-repetition, and multi-directional nature of aerobic exercise challenges postural stability under varying conditions and may promote proprioceptive adaptation and neuromuscular control, although these mechanisms were not directly measured in the present study [49]. Additionally, prior research suggests that aerobic training may enhance cerebral perfusion and sensory integration, potentially improving reaction times, vestibular function, and postural adjustments, which are important for maintaining balance [50]. Furthermore, the higher metabolic and cardiovascular demands of aerobic exercise may contribute to improved oxygen delivery, fatigue resistance, and movement efficiency, which could support better balance performance [41]. In contrast, Pilates exercise focuses on core stability and controlled postural responses, which are essential for maintaining static balance and improving muscle endurance, making it highly beneficial for older adults at risk of strength-related functional decline [51]. While these benefits contribute to postural control, they may not provide the level of dynamic and reactive balance adaptations similar to those of aerobic training. These findings highlight the distinct advantages of aerobic exercise in enhancing both gait speed and dynamic balance, reinforcing its effectiveness in improving overall functional mobility in older adults.

Other physical performance outcomes, including TUGT, 5xSTS, and 2MWT, showed only significant time main effects without significant interaction effects. These results suggest positive changes in these outcomes. However, the effects of these two interventions cannot be differentiated. Despite the inability to highlight the effects of these two interventions, these results underscore the value of structured physical activity in enhancing functional mobility, lower extremity strength, and gait endurance, reinforcing its importance in maintaining functional independence in older adults. The results were consistent with previous studies where physical activity was shown to improve the overall functional capacity [52]. Although the sample size was based on the results of a previous published study, the effect size of a single outcome measure cannot be generalize to all outcome measures used in this study. As a result, non-significant intervention-specific results over time were observed in these outcomes. The intensity and duration of the interventions may not have been sufficient to elicit substantial changes in these outcomes, rendering non-significant statistical results. While both interventions were structured, a more personalized program could have enhanced the effectiveness of each intervention [53].

Similarly, self-reported outcome measures of FES-I, PSQI, and WHOQOL-Brief domains significantly improved over time, regardless of the group, highlighting the broad effect of structured physical activity on fear of falling, sleep quality, and QOL in older adults. It was notable that despite significant MBEST improvements in both groups, the FES-I did not show a significant Group × Time interaction, indicating that both modalities were equally effective in reducing fear of falling. Clinically, 73% of Pilate’s group participants individually exceeded the MCID of − 3.5 points for FES-I, demonstrating that the Pilates intervention delivered broad individual-level clinical benefit for fall efficacy even without a statistically significant interaction effect. Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis of change scores revealed that improvements in FES-I and PSQI were moderately correlated (r = 0.413, p < 0.05), and that improvements in both FES-I (r = − 0.464) and PSQI (r = − 0.458) were significantly associated with improvements in WHOQOL-D1, suggesting that reducing fear of falling and improving sleep quality are synergistic and interdependent pathways to enhanced quality of life in older adults. Despite the non-significant group-specific interaction effects for self-reported outcomes, detailed examination of the mean FES-I values demonstrated that both groups improved from having a high concern for falling at baseline to a moderate concern about falling at post-treatment, representing clinically meaningful change at the group level [54]. Moreover, some outcomes, such as sleep quality and quality of life, relied on self-reported measures, which are inherently subjective and prone to bias [55]. These factors may have contributed to the non-significant Group × Time interaction effects observed for FES-I, PSQI, and WHOQOL outcomes, although both intervention groups showed significant improvements over time.

Limitations

The study included the following limitations, the absence of a passive control group (i.e., a sedentary or usual-care comparator) limits the ability to attribute observed improvements entirely to the exercise interventions, as natural recovery, regression to the mean, or Hawthorne effects cannot be fully excluded. Future studies should include a waitlist or inactive control arm to strengthen causal inference. Second, a clarification of the exercise dose is warranted. When calculated correctly, total active exercise volume was equivalent across groups: both the aerobic group (3 sessions/week × 30 min = 90 min/week × 8 weeks = 720 min) and the PG (2 sessions/week × 45 min = 90 min/week × 8 weeks = 720 min) accumulated identical active exercise doses, a dose well-established to produce functional improvements in previously sedentary older adults. The one remaining methodological difference is session frequency (aerobic: 3×/week vs. Pilates: 2×/week). More frequent aerobic sessions may independently promote neuromuscular adaptation through greater stimulus repetition per week, independent of total volume, and this cannot be fully excluded as a partial contributor to the observed between-group differences in 10MWT and MBEST. A difference in training frequency remained between the intervention groups and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Future studies should equalize both training frequency and total exercise volume to further isolate modality specific effects. Adherence was monitored and all participants met the ≥ 85% compliance threshold; future trials should equalize both total volume and session frequency to fully isolate modality-specific effects. Third, the relatively short duration of the intervention may not have captured the full benefits or sustained effects of Pilates and aerobic exercise in the older adult population. Additionally, the intervention effects observed in this study may not necessarily persist over time, and extended follow-up periods could help assess whether the improvements are maintained. The non-intervention-specific effect results also suggest that future research could explore the combined effects of these interventions to assess whether a hybrid approach might yield greater benefits for addressing multiple aspects of health in older adults. Fourth, the study’s sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly for diverse populations of older adults with varying health statuses and physical abilities. A larger and more heterogeneous sample would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the efficacy of these interventions across different subgroups. Fifth, the study did not account for potential confounding variables, such as dietary habits, psychological stress, or pre-existing medical conditions, which may have influenced the outcomes. These limitations emphasize the need for further studies with larger, more diverse populations, longer follow-up periods, and objective measurement tools to better evaluate the effectiveness of exercise interventions for older adults.

Conclusion

The study highlights the health benefits of both aerobic and Pilates exercises for older adults, particularly in improving balance, functional capacity, and gait. While both interventions demonstrated positive effects, the aerobic exercise group demonstrated greater improvements in dynamic balance and gait velocity at post-treatment. Although total active exercise volume was equivalent across groups, session frequency differed, which is acknowledged as methodological limitation that cannot be fully excluded as a partial contributor to the between-group differences. Nevertheless, these between-group findings were robust across ANCOVA-adjusted and regression-based analyses, and both modalities offer meaningful benefits for promoting physical well-being in older adults. Future trials equalizing both total volume and session frequency are warranted to confirm modality-specific effects with full design equivalence. Further longitudinal research is also needed to explore the extended effects of these interventions and the potential for combining aerobic and Pilates exercises to optimize health outcomes for older adults.
