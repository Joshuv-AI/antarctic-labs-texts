Two-Step Clinical Pathways to Cardiovascular Mortality in Chronic Kidney Disease and Dialysis -- A Narrative Review
Authors: Tetsuo Shoji, Christoph Wanner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) face a markedly elevated risk of death from cardiovascular disease (CVD); in particular, those requiring hemodialysis have a 10- to 30-fold higher risk than the general population. This extremely increased risk of CVD death reflects the coexistence of multiple traditional and nontraditional risk factors. The present narrative review considers two distinct steps: first, the occurrence of a CVD event, and second, death resulting from an inability to recover from the CVD event. Patients undergoing hemodialysis are at an increased risk for both of these steps, accounting for the dramatically higher risk of CVD death in this population. High risk for the second step—death following a CVD event—may be driven by conditions called decreased physical resilience and increased frailty. Studies of patients on hemodialysis show that predictors for death at this stage include key components of malnutrition-inflammation-atherosclerosis syndrome—also called the malnutrition-inflammation-complex-syndrome or protein-energy wasting—such as lower body mass index, lower serum albumin, and higher C-reactive protein. Other important contributors include higher age, longer dialysis duration, diabetic kidney disease, phosphate, calcium, serum calcification propensity (T50), and insulin-like growth factor 1 levels. Notably, some of these factors also predict death following infection, suggesting that the risk predictors for the second step are shared between CVD and infection. Recognizing these steps may facilitate prevention and greater preparedness for CVD, infection, and other stressful events among patients with CKD.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Case fatality, Hemodialysis, Chronic kidney disease

Introduction

Atherosclerosis is the key pathology underlying most cardiovascular disease (CVD) and death from CVD in the general population. Thus, it is reasonable to attempt to prevent CVD death by mitigating atherosclerosis risk. Many epidemiological studies in the general population, such as the Framingham Heart Study 1) and the Hisayama Study 2) , have identified traditional risk factors for coronary artery disease and atherosclerotic CVD.

Patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) 3) , particularly those undergoing hemodialysis 4) , carry elevated risks of CVD and death from CVD. A previous nationwide study in the United States 4) revealed that the risk of CVD death was 10–30 times higher in the dialysis population, compared to the general population. To explain this epidemiologic fact, multiple nontraditional risk factors have been proposed 5) , including uremic toxins, renal anemia, CKD-associated mineral bone disorder (CKD-MBD), electrolyte abnormalities, oxidative stress, inflammation, and malnutrition. Among nontraditional risk factors, low serum albumin, low body mass index (BMI), and elevated inflammation markers, e.g., C-reactive protein (CRP), exhibit strong associations with all-cause mortality and CVD mortality. Moreover, the important role of malnutrition, or wasting, has been expressed using several different terminologies, including malnutrition-inflammation-atherosclerosis (MIA) syndrome 6) , malnutrition inflammation complex syndrome (MICS) 7) , and protein-energy wasting (PEW) 8 , 9) .

The presently available knowledge raises three questions. First, do the added effects of the proposed risk factors fully explain the extremely elevated risk of CVD death in the dialysis population? Second, over-nutrition is an important mechanism for atherosclerosis and atherosclerotic CVD in the general population; does malnutrition (poor nutritional status) also promote atherosclerosis among patients with CKD? And third, if a higher BMI is associated with higher all-cause and CVD mortality in the general population 10) , why is a higher BMI associated with lower risk for all-cause and CVD mortality among dialysis patients 11) ? Can the additional effects of the risk factors explain this “obesity paradox” or “reverse epidemiology” 12) ?

To address these questions, the present narrative review proposes two distinct steps leading to CVD death, and two groups of risk factors corresponding to the two steps 13 - 15) . Death from CVD involves two steps: first, the experience of a CVD event, and second, dying rather than recovering after this CVD event. In general, traditional risk factors are the main contributors to the first step, while malnutrition/wasting contributes to the second step. Some risk factors contribute to both steps. Patients in more advanced stages of CKD exhibit higher risks for both two steps, and multiplication of the elevated risk for each step can explain the extremely elevated risk for CVD mortality among dialysis patients. Although the risks for both steps are higher in patients with more advanced stages of CKD, the relative contributions change, such that the risk for the second step predominates in later stages of CKD, causing the paradoxical epidemiology.

This review expands the two-step framework of CVD outcomes to infection-related outcomes. The predictors of death at the second step are shared between CVD and infection. Patients having an elevated risk of death at the second step are considered to have decreased physical resilience, in other words, increased frailty.

Risk for CVD Event Occurrence in Dialysis Patients

Iseki et al. conducted epidemiologic studies focused on myocardial infarction among dialysis patients, compared to the general population, in Okinawa, Japan 16) . Their results showed a relative risk of 2.5 for men, and 4.6 for women. Similarly, in their investigation of stroke, they found that all-cause stroke occurred at a rate of 17.2 per 1000 patient-years in their hemodialysis cohort 17) , whereas it was 6.3 per 1000 patient-years in the third wave of the Hisayama study. Thus, the relative risk of stroke can be calculated as 2.7 in the hemodialysis population, compared to the general population, in Japan. These studies indicate that the risk of CVD events was several times higher in the dialysis population than in the general population, although the relative risks were much lower than the relative risk of 10–30 reported for cardiovascular death.

Risk of Death Following CVD Events in Dialysis Patients

In a 2000 study by Iseki et al. 16) , among patients with acute myocardial infarction, nearly half died within one month in the hemodialysis population, compared to 25% in the general population of Okinawa. Notably, it took 13.6 years to reach 50% mortality in the general population. Thus, the survival rate after myocardial infarction was much lower among hemodialysis patients, compared to the general population in Japan. Similarly, patients undergoing hemodialysis also exhibit poor survival after stroke events 17) ; at one month after stroke, the death rate was 47% in the hemodialysis cohort, compared to only 12.3% in the Okinawa general population. Together, these studies revealed that the risk of death following CVD events is several times higher in the dialysis population, compared to the general population.

CKD Stages and Risk of CVD Events

Go et al. 3) clearly demonstrated that the risk of CVD events is higher among individuals with lower kidney function; groups with eGFR ≥ 60, 45–59, 30–44, 15–29, and ＜15 mL/min/1.73 m2 showed the following age-adjusted rates of cardiovascular events: 2.11, 3.65, 11.3, 21.8, and 36.6 per 100 person-years, respectively. After full adjustment, the relative risk was 3.4 for individuals with eGFR ＜15 mL/min/1.73 m2 (CKD stage G5), compared to those with eGFR of ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Notably, this relative risk is similar to the above-mentioned relative risk of myocardial infarction in patients on dialysis. Additionally, an epidemiological study in Japan confirms that the risk of CVD events is higher in more advanced stages of CKD 18) . These findings are in good agreement with the described arterial wall changes associated with kidney function decline.

CKD Stages and Risk of Death Following CVD Events

Smith et al. constructed survival curves after acute myocardial infarction for deciles of kidney function (based on creatinine clearance, blood urea nitrogen, or eGFR), which demonstrated a worse survival rate in cases with lower kidney function 19) . The hazard ratio was 2.52 for the lowest decile of creatinine clearance (＜27 mL/min) compared to the highest decile (≥ 86 mL/min). The increased risk in dialysis patients 16) can be considered to lie along this continuum. The available evidence indicates that kidney function is one determinant of the survival rate after CVD events, and that the risk of death following CVD events is several times higher in hemodialysis patients than in the general population.

Two Distinct Steps Leading to CVD Death

Fig.1 illustrates the two distinct steps involved in death following a CVD event: first, the occurrence of a CVD event, and second, death rather than recovery following this event 13 - 15) . The risk of each step is several times higher for hemodialysis patients, compared with the general population. When these risks are multiplied, the result approximates the relative risk of CVD death among hemodialysis patients (10–30) compared to in the general population. The risk of patients with dialysis-independent CKD lies between these two groups.

Compared to individuals with normal kidney function, patients with kidney failure who are undergoing hemodialysis (CKD stage G5D) exhibit a several-fold higher risk for the occurrence of CVD events (the first step), along with a several-fold higher risk for death following CVD events (the second step), explaining the 10- to 30-times higher risk of CVD death in this population. The risks for both steps to CVD death are higher in more advanced stages of CKD. Abbreviations: CVD, cardiovascular disease; CKD, chronic kidney disease.

Contributions of Risk Factors to the First and Second Steps to CVD Death

The Framingham Heart Study, and other cohort studies in the general population, have identified the traditional risk factors for CVD. Importantly, in most studies, the end-point has been the occurrence of CVD events (e.g., ischemic heart disease); therefore, the traditional risk factors can be considered risk factors for the first step to CVD death in the general population. Among individuals with dialysis-independent CKD, kidney function is a risk predictor for both the first step 3) and second step 19) to CVD death. In cohort studies among patients undergoing hemodialysis, the outcome of interest has most commonly been all-cause mortality 20) , death from CVD 21) , and other cause-specific mortality; however, several studies have separately examined the risk factors for the occurrence of new CVD events, and for death following CVD events, as described below and summarized in Table 1 and Fig.2 .

Asymptomatic Atherosclerosis and Vascular Calcification

As expected, presence of asymptomatic atherosclerosis is a strong predictor of occurrence of new CVD events in hemodialysis patients. According to the study by Joki et al. 22) , more than half of asymptomatic patients at the initiation of hemodialysis had coronary disease (CAD) by coronary angiography. During follow-up of the asymptomatic patients at dialysis initiation, the strongest predictor of cardiac event was the presence of CAD with an adjusted hazard ratio of 611 [14, 26549] followed by diabetes and increased CRP 23) . Patients with kidney failure have increased carotid artery intima-media thickness (IMT) 24) and aortic pulse wave velocity (PWV) 25) , and both IMT 26) and PWV 27) are the independent predictors of all-cause and CVD mortality, although further studies are needed to determine whether these vascular parameters are associated with the first and second steps to CVD mortality in this population.

Many studies showed that the presence of vascular calcification predicted all-cause and CVD mortality in hemodialysis patients 28) . Also, some studies showed that the presence and degree of vascular calcification predicted occurrence of new CVD events in patients with CKD 29) . So far, however, information is needed whether vascular calcification is associated with death following a CVD event or not.

Dyslipidemia

In Japan, a nationwide cohort study was performed among hemodialysis patients, to examine whether high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and non-HDL-C were associated with first-time incident myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and hemorrhagic stroke 30) . The results indicated that higher non-HDL-C and lower HDL-C were both predictive of new onset of myocardial infarction and ischemic stroke, but not hemorrhagic stroke. Moreover, higher low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) was associated with the CVD outcomes in a similar manner to non-HDL-C. These results among hemodialysis patients in Japan are similar to findings in the general population. Neither non-HDL-C nor HDL-C was an independent predictor of death following CVD events. Thus, in this study, dyslipidemia is an independent predictor of the first step, but not of the second step leading to CVD death.

Blood Pressure Abnormalities

Although hypertension is an established Framingham risk factor for CVD, the association between blood pressure and CVD-related outcomes are complicated in patients with kidney failure. Hypertension is a significant predictor of occurrence of new CVD events also in patients undergoing hemodialysis 31) . However, according to Kalantar-Zadeh 32) , there is a reverse epidemiology between hypertension and mortality in kidney failure, and a U-shaped association was reported between systolic blood pressure and all-cause mortality 33) . In addition, intradialytic hypotension predicts a higher risk of mortality 34) . Although direct demonstration is lacking, low blood pressure, not high blood pressure, may be predictive of death following CVD events. Otherwise, coexistence of heart failure in patients with low blood pressure may play an important role.

Calcium and Phosphate

CKD-MBD is considered the CKD-associated systemic condition that promotes vascular calcification and left ventricular hypertrophy, and increases the risks of CVD and all-cause mortality 35) . The main components of CKD-MBD are serum phosphate, calcium, and parathyroid hormone, and recent studies have proposed the addition of fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23), serum magnesium, calciprotein particles (CPPs), T50 calciprotein crystallization time (serum calcification propensity), and other factors 36 , 37) . How do these components of CKD-MBD affect the first and/or second step to CVD death? High phosphate levels have been repeatedly identified as an independent predictor of all-cause mortality 20 , 21 , 38) and of death from CVD 21) . Since vascular calcification predicts occurrence of CVD events as discussed above, one may expect that hyperphosphatemia is a predictor of the first step. However, a nationwide cohort study in Japan demonstrated that high phosphate was not associated with the incidence of composite CVD, but was significantly associated with death following CVD events 39) . The same study reported that high corrected serum calcium was not associated with CVD incidence, but that lower corrected serum calcium was associated with lower risk of death after CVD events. These results indicate that serum phosphate and calcium levels are more closely associated with the second step than the first step leading to CVD death. Also, associations of serum phosphate with individual CVD events may vary. In fact, a higher serum phosphate predicted a higher risk of hemorrhagic stroke but a lower risk of ischemic stroke in a large cohort of hemodialysis patients 40) .

Fetuin-A and T50

Liver-derived glycoprotein fetuin-A stabilizes calcium-phosphate clusters, and forms primary CPP (CPP-1), preventing its spontaneous transformation into secondary CPP (CPP-2) that can induce inflammation, oxidative stress, and vascular calcification 41) . Using an in vitro assay system that enabled measurement of the transformation time (T50) from CPP-1 to CPP-2 in serum, a study revealed that a lower T50 value indicates lower protective ability of the serum against calcification stress 42) . Accordingly, a lower T50 has been associated with a higher risk of all-cause mortality in cohort studies of patients with dialysis-independent CKD 43) and patients receiving dialysis treatment 44) . On one hand, fetuin-A suppresses vascular calcification; however, it also induces insulin resistance that could result in atherosclerosis 45 , 46) , raising questions about how T50 and fetuin-A are associated with the first and second steps in hemodialysis patients. In a recent cohort study, Mori et al. examined associations of serum fetuin-A and T50 with the occurrence of new CVD events, and death following CVD events, in a cohort of hemodialysis patients 47) . Lower fetuin-A level was more closely associated with the occurrence of CVD events than with death following CVD events. On the other hand, shorter T50 was preferentially associated with death following CVD events, compared to with the occurrence of CVD events. Thus, like serum calcium and phosphate, T50 appears to be preferentially related to the second step to death from CVD.

The precise mechanisms are unknown by which serum calcium, phosphate, and T50 are more closely related to the second step rather than the first step although these are factors related to vascular calcification. There are possible explanations. First, abnormal mineral metabolism promotes vascular calcification, increases arterial stiffness, resulting in decreased downstream blood flow and impaired recovery from tissue survival following a CVD event. Second, hyperphosphatemia and hypercalcemia could cause increased cell death. Experimental studies showed that high phosphate medium induced apoptosis of vascular smooth muscle cells and other cell types, which was promoted in high calcium concentration 48 , 49) . In damaged tissue such as infarct myocardium, increased inflammation and oxidative stress could promote transformation to CPP-II which results further tissue damage and vicious cycle. Thus, abnormalities of calcium, phosphate, and T50 could be related to the second step following a CVD event. It is possible that CKD-MBD is a disorder which affects both the first and second steps, although the contributions of CKD-MBD components may vary.

IGF-1

Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) is secreted by the liver, through the action of growth hormone from the anterior lobe of the pituitary. IGF-1 acts on cells throughout the body, mediating the systemic effects of growth hormone. In an investigation of a cohort of hemodialysis patients 50) , lower serum IGF-1 level was associated with higher risk of all-cause mortality, and was predictive of both CVD death and non-CVD death. However, IGF-1 level was not independently associated with the occurrence of CVD events, or hospitalization for infection. Instead, a lower IGF-1 level was independently associated with death following CVD events, and with death following hospitalization for infection. This indicates that IGF-1 is preferentially associated with the second steps to death from CVD, and to death from infection, compared to the first steps.

Age, Sex, and Dialysis Vintage

Of the four above-mentioned studies, two 30 , 39) reported the associations of clinical outcomes with not only the exposure variables of interest, but also with covariates, such that they described the associations of these covariates with the first and second step to CVD death. These covariates include age, sex, dialysis vintage (duration of dialysis), presence of diabetes (diabetic kidney disease as the underlying kidney disease), serum albumin, body mass index, and C-reactive protein.

Age was strongly associated with both the first and second steps 30 , 39) . Male patients reportedly have higher risks for the occurrence of myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, and cerebral hemorrhage 30) . In contrast, sex was not significantly associated with death following the composite of these CVD event, indicating that male sex is preferentially associated with the first step over the second step to CVD death. Dialysis vintage was not significantly associated with the incidence of composite CVDs 26) or with the incidence of cerebral infarction or cerebral hemorrhage 30) , but it was associated with the incidence of myocardial infarction 30) . Although dialysis vintage was not significantly associated with death following composite CVD events, it was positively associated with death following cerebral infarction 30) . Thus, although various types of CVDs may show different associations, it appears that dialysis vintage shows only neutral or modest associations with the first and second steps to CVD death.

Diabetes

One study 30) demonstrated that diabetes (diabetic kidney disease versus other etiologies for kidney failure) was an independent factor associated with the incidence of new-onset myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, and cerebral bleeding. Another study revealed that diabetes was an independent predictor of the incidence of composite CVDs 39) . These studies both show that diabetes is a strong and independent factor associated with the first step to CVD death. With regards to the second step, one study demonstrated that diabetes tended to be associated with a higher risk of death following an individual CVD event, although this association was not statistically significant after adjustment. Another study 39) reported that diabetes was an independent predictor of death following composite CVD events. Thus, the present evidence indicates that diabetes can be regarded as a risk factor for both the first and second steps to CVD death.

C-Reactive Protein (CRP)

In one study of patients undergoing hemodialysis 26) , a higher CRP level was independently associated with the occurrence of composite CVD events, and with death following CVD events. Another study 30) demonstrated that higher CRP was an independent predictor for the occurrence of myocardial infarction and cerebral infarction, but not cerebral hemorrhage. The same study reported that higher CRP was an independent predictor of death following composite CVD events, although the association between CRP and death following myocardial infarction was not statistically significant. These results show that CRP is an independent predictor of both the first and second steps to CVD death, when considering composite CVD, and that the association of CRP may vary depending on the type of CVD.

Body Mass Index (BMI)

BMI is considered a marker of nutrition, or the reserve of protein and energy. In the general population, higher BMI or obesity is an established predictor of higher risks of incident CVD and mortality. Paradoxically, a higher BMI predicts better survival among patients receiving dialysis, and those with advanced stages of dialysis-independent CKD 11) . Limited information is available regarding the relationship between BMI and CVD event occurrence in CKD. In a cohort of 471 patients with dialysis-independent CKD, Russo et al. found that BMI was not an independent predictor of CVD events 51) . However, in a study of 37,690 prevalent patients receiving hemodialysis in Japan 39) , lower BMI was an independent predictor of higher risk for the occurrence of composite CVD events (myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, and cerebral hemorrhage), although the odds ratio of 0.98 (95% confidence interval: 0.96, 0.99]) per 1 kg/m2 higher BMI was less impressive, compared to that for all-cause mortality (0.89 [0.88, 0.91]). The same study demonstrated that lower BMI was an independent predictor of death following composite CVD events, with an odds ratio of 0.93 [0.89, 0.96]. In another study of 45,390 prevalent patients receiving hemodialysis, BMI was inversely associated with the occurrence of cerebral infarction but was not significantly associated with myocardial infarction or cerebral bleeding 30) . The same study demonstrated that lower BMI was an independent predictor of death following composite CVD events, with an odds ratio of 0.93 [0.89, 0.97] per 1 kg/m2, whereas BMI was not significantly associated with death following myocardial infarction. Overall, these studies indicate that lower BMI in hemodialysis patients is a predictor of the risk of both steps to CVD death—particularly the second step—and that the association between BMI and CVD-related outcomes may vary depending on the type of CVD. Notably, the obesity paradox may be caused by the higher post-event mortality rate (case fatality rate), which is closely associated with BMI.

Body Fat and Lean Mass

There is some variability in the reported associations of body fat and lean mass (mostly skeletal muscle) with CVD-related outcomes. In a study of 808 patients receiving hemodialysis, analysis of body composition by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry 52) confirmed the known inverse association between BMI and all-cause mortality. This study also revealed that higher fat mass index was associated with lower risk of death from non-CVD causes, whereas higher lean mass index was associated with lower risk of death from CVD. Although this study did not examine the association between BMI and the occurrence of CVD events, it appears that the role of body composition in the clinical outcomes of hemodialysis patients may vary depending on the type of events.

Regarding muscle mass measurement, recent studies have used estimates of muscle mass based on serum creatinine, such as a modified creatinine index 53) , in hemodialysis patients whose kidneys excrete negligible amount of creatinine. Arase et al. reported that a modified creatinine index was associated with all-cause and CVD-related death 54) , and with infection-related mortality 55) , in a cohort of over 3000 hemodialysis patients. They reported that a lower modified creatinine index was an independent predictor of CVD-related death and infection-related death, as well as all-cause mortality. Although they did not determine whether the modified creatinine index predicted the occurrence of CVD or infection events, or whether the index was associated with post-event mortality, it appears that lower muscle mass was associated with mortality from any cause in this population.

Serum Albumin

Serum albumin is considered a serum marker of nutrition (protein reserve), and a marker of inflammation, because it is one of the inverse acute phase reactants. One study among patients on hemodialysis 26) demonstrated that a lower serum albumin level was an independent predictor of composite CVD events, and an independent predictor of death following composite CVD events. In another study 30) , a lower serum albumin level was an independent predictor of the occurrence of myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, and cerebral hemorrhage. The same study revealed that lower serum albumin was an independent predictor of death following myocardial infarction, and of death following cerebral infarction, but not of death following cerebral hemorrhage. These results indicate that serum albumin is an independent predictor of both the first and second steps to CVD death, when considering composite CVD events, and that the association may vary depending on the type of CVD. The finding that serum albumin and BMI exhibit different associations with clinical outcomes may suggest that serum albumin reflects not only nutrition but also inflammation.

Other Potential Factors Influencing CVD-Related Outcomes

All-cause and CVD-related mortality in CKD has also been associated with many other factors—including vascular calcification 56) ; left ventricular hypertrophy 57) and its related biomarkers 58) ; electrocardiogram abnormalities, including QT interval elongation 59) ; oxidative stress 60) ; renal anemia and anemia-related parameters 61 - 63) ; low serum magnesium 64 , 65) ; low serum zinc 66) ; low selenium 67) ; low T3 syndrome 68) , and others. Limited information is available regarding which of the two steps is involved in these associations. Inaba et al. showed a positive association between serum creatinine and free triiodothyronine (T3) to free thyroxine (T4) ratio in patients on maintenance hemodialysis 69) . Since serum creatinine is a surrogate of skeletal muscle mass if urinary excretion is negligible, and since skeletal muscles convert T4 into its active form T3, the result suggests the role of sarcopenia in low T3 syndrome.

Two Steps to Death from Infection

The risk of infection-related hospitalization is higher among patients with lower kidney function 70) , and in patients with lupus nephritis compared to other etiologies 71) . Among patients undergoing hemodialysis, the type of vascular access (catheter versus arteriovenous fistula) is a key predictor of infection risk 72) , and infection is one of the leading causes of death in patients receiving dialysis 73) . Table 1 presents predictors of the risks of hospitalization and death due to infection. Dalrymple et al. reported that predictors of the risk for infection-related hospitalization included older age, female sex, diabetes, heart failure, pulmonary disease, and low serum albumin 74) . Few studies have examined predictors of the risk for death following infection-related hospitalization. Among patients receiving hemodialysis, death following hospitalization for infection is independently predicted by nutritional status, as indicated by a lower serum albumin concentration 75) , a higher Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) 76) , a higher Nutritional Risk Index for Japanese Hemodialysis patients (NRI-JH) 77) , and a lower serum IGF-1 level 50) . These results are in agreement that poor nutritional status is a predictor of the risk of death following hospitalization for infection, suggesting shared risk factors between the second steps to death following infection and to death following CVD events.

Physical Resilience, Frailty, and the Second Step

When a person falls ill or suffers an injury (stressor), their physical capacity initially declines, and then the body attempts to recover. An individual with sufficient resilience can regain their original strength, while a weaker person will only recover to a lower level, or might even die ( Fig.3 ) . Because a CVD event is a strong stressor, physical resilience plays a key role in the clinical course following a CVD event. The same is also true after infections and other diseases. Patients who face an elevated risk of death at the second step are considered to have decreased physical resilience, i.e., to be frail, with frailty defined as “a biologic syndrome of decreased reserve and resistance to stressors” “causing vulnerability to adverse outcomes” 78) . Thus, a person’s risk level at the second step is higher with decreased physical resilience and increased frailty.

When physical reserve is decreased by a stressor, an individual can follow one of three clinical courses. (A) A person with high physical resilience will exhibit full recovery to the original level. (B) A person with low physical resilience may exhibit partial recovery to a level somewhat lower than the original. (C) A person with very low physical resilience may not recover, leading to death from the event. Frailty is defined by decreased physical reserve against stressors. Repeated stressful episodes would further lower physical reserve and increase frailty. When considering CVD as a stressor, these clinical courses are determined by the severity of the CVD event, and the person’s physical reserve before the event; namely how frail the person is. Stressors include not only CVD but infection, injury, surgery, and other stressful events as well.

Reality is a Little More Complicated

In this review, the risk of CVD-related death is approximated as the product of the risk for the occurrence of CVD events multiplied by the risk for death following CVD events. Then, an overview is presented of the risk predictors for the two steps, particularly those for the second step. However, the reality is a little more complex.

First, the risk of death at the second step is determined not only by the patient’s intrinsic factors but also by the severity of the event and the accessibility of medical care ( Fig.2 ) . Most studies have not reported or considered the severity of CVD events. Additionally, treatment has advanced over recent years. Among all deaths of patients receiving dialysis in Japan, the proportion of death from myocardial infarction was 7.4% in 2001 79) , and was decreased to 2.1% in 2023 80) , presumably reflecting the evolution of the treatment of coronary artery disease.

Second, cause of death is sometimes difficult to determine. For example, if a patient experienced a stroke and developed dysphagia and fatal aspiration pneumonia, how should the cause of death be classified? This death may be adjudicated as death from infection; however, stroke is a key contributor. Additionally, the risk of CVD events is elevated after infection, particularly within the first 30 days after an infection 81) . Thus, causes of death can compete, and a patient’s cause of death may vary between studies, depending on the rules of adjudication ( Fig.4 ) .

Third, associations evaluated in observational studies cannot directly be translated into treatment. While observational results can be used to generate hypotheses for treatment, these hypotheses must first be examined in clinical trials. For example, higher cholesterol was shown to predict incident CVD events in a large cohort of hemodialysis patients; however, in randomized controlled trials among hemodialysis patients, statin treatment did not significantly decrease the composite CVD end-point 82 , 83) , although the statin group did show a reduction of patients with coronary angioplasty 82) . As another example, many cohort studies among dialysis patients show that treatment with vitamin D receptor activators is associated with lower risks of all-cause mortality and death from CVD 84 , 85) . A literature review reveals both observational and experimental studies supporting these observational results 86) , supporting the hypothesis that vitamin D receptor activators may be a panacea for patients on dialysis. However, in a randomized clinical trial among patients undergoing hemodialysis, the group treated with alfacalcidol had a 25% higher (not lower) risk for the occurrence of new CVD events, compared to the control group, although this difference was not statistically significant 87) . Thus, it must be noted that the associations shown in the present review do not necessarily indicate causality.

Recent Advances in Medical Treatment to Improve CVD and Kidney Outcomes

In the modern management of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), recommended treatments include angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI), beta blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA), and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. These medications are called “The Fantastic Four”, because they reduce mortality and symptom burden in these patients 88) . For the management of patients with diabetes, clinical practice guidelines recommend treatment with SGLT2 inhibitors and/or glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor antagonists (GLP-1RA), which have been shown to have major benefit across the spectrum of CVD, heart failure, and kidney outcomes in people with type 2 diabetes 89) .

In the EMPA-REG Outcome trial 90) , the primary composite outcome was death from cardiovascular causes, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke. Compared to placebo, empagliflozin treatment decreased the primary outcome with a hazard ratio of 0.86 (95% confidence interval: [0.74, 0.99]), and also reduced death from CVD. Importantly, the rate of reduction of CVD-related death (35%) was greater than the reduction of risk of the composite CVD events (14%). Although this was not directly documented, it is likely that empagliflozin treatment also reduced the risk of death following CVD events, namely the second step to CVD death.

The SUSTAIN-6 trial investigated once-weekly semaglutide versus placebo in patients with type 2 diabetes 91) , and the primary composite outcome was the first occurrence of cardiovascular death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or nonfatal stroke. Semaglutide treatment decreased the primary outcome with a hazard ratio of 0.74 [0.58, 0.95], whereas the rates of death from cardiovascular causes were similar between the two groups. It is possible that body weight reduction with GLP-1RA decreases the risk of the first step, but it may increases the risk of the second step, thereby resulting in a similar rate of death due to CVD events; however, the risk of death at the second step was not directly demonstrated. The benefit of treatment with GLP-1RA was greater in the patients with higher BMI among patients with type 2 diabetes 92) . Thus, there remains a need for more information regarding the benefit of GLP-1RA when used in patients with type 2 diabetes and kidney failure undergoing hemodialysis, since such patients exhibit an “obesity paradox”.

The PISCES trial examined the effect of daily supplementation of 4 g of fish-oil (n−3 fatty acids) versus corn oil placebo on CVD events in patients receiving hemodialysis 93) . The primary end point was a composite of all serious cardiovascular events including sudden and nonsudden cardiac death, fatal and nonfatal myocardial infarction, peripheral vascular disease leading to amputation, and fatal and nonfatal stroke. The rate of the primary endpoint was lower in the fish-oil group with a hazard ratio of 0.57 [0.47, 0.70]. Death from any cause, one of the secondary endpoints, was also lower with a hazard ratio of 0.89 [0.73, 1.01] at a marginal significance. Safety profile was similar between the two groups. The result of this trial was consistent with previous observational studies in which serum levels of n−3 fatty acids were the exposures 94) . With regards to the two steps, it is difficult to distinguish the effects of fish-oil supplementation on the first and second steps, because the endpoints of this trial include both fatal and nonfatal events. However, fish oil may offer meaningful benefits for patients on dialysis presumably by preventing CVD events.

Prevention and Preparedness

The occurrence of CVD can result in death among patients with decreased resilience or increased frailty. Within the two-step framework for death following CVD, there are preventative measures that can be taken to reduce the risk of the first step (CVD occurrence). Prevention is particularly important in CKD because post-event mortality rate is remarkably higher in patients with lower kidney function particularly those on dialysis 16 , 17 , 19) . However, no preventative steps can completely eliminate the possibility of CVD; thus, we must also take measures to improve patients’ preparedness for post-event mortality (the second step) following CVD events. The two-step framework can be extended to non-CVD death. Notably, nephrologists have been calling for the importance of nutritional management in patients with CKD using terminologies such as MIA, MICS, and PEW, without recognizing that this is a protective measure against the second step ( Fig.4 ) . Identifying the risk factors that influence each step can help us make sure that we are adequately addressing both steps.

From Rehabilitation to Prehabilitation

Patients who are planning to undergo major surgery will be exposed to stressful conditions, will experience a decreased physical reserve after surgery, and will receive rehabilitation to facilitate recovery. To improve post-surgery outcomes, surgeons have recently started recommending prehabilitation before surgery, rather than rehabilitation after surgery 95) . This is a measure to support preparedness for surgery. Similarly, it is important to maintain a regular exercise habit, nutritional support, and other non-pharmacological care, not only as a preventative measure, but also to improve preparedness for CVD, infection, and other stressful events.

Conclusion

This review considered two distinct steps leading to CVD death. Patients with CKD carry elevated risks for both steps, and multiplication of these two risks can explain the extremely high risk of CVD-related death in patients with CKD, particularly those undergoing dialysis treatment. The high-risk condition for the second step can be characterized by reduced physical resilience or increased frailty, with specific risk predictors including factors related to malnutrition and inflammation, which are the key components of MIA syndrome, MICS, and PEW. An obesity paradox is apparent in populations with increased risk of the second step. Higher age, diabetes, and decreased kidney function are factors associated with both steps, whereas other factors appear to be preferentially associated with either the first or the second step to CVD death. Notably, it is likely that factors associated with the second step are common for deaths from CVD, infection, and other causes. There is a clear need for further interventional studies to examine whether the findings from observational studies are useful for increasing preparedness for CVD, infection, and other stressful events among patients with CKD.
