Recent advances in understanding molecular and clinical heterogeneity in osteoarthritis: one disease or several?
Authors: Tonia L Vincent, Thomas A Perry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe failure to develop disease modifying drugs in osteoarthritis (OA) has been a frustration for academia and industry as well as a disappointment for patients. Many reasons have contributed to this failure, most notably an incomplete understanding of its pathogenesis and a lack of clarity on whether OA is one disease or a collection of several different diseases. The challenges in OA are compounded by the heterogeneous clinical presentation of disease and its slow insidious course in most individuals. Patient reported pain is variable, only partly correlates with structural disease, and is complicated by the development of central sensitisation leading to pain amplification in many, especially women. Happily, in recent years, a number of substantial advances have helped to define the core tenets of disease; revealing the breadth of the biology and its variance across patients with different clinical phenotypes. These advances have included large-scale agnostic approaches to unravel the biology through multi-omic analyses, improved clinical phenotyping, and phenotype-endotype comparisons. What emerges is evidence of one core biological pathway, suggesting a shared common disease process, with evidence of biology that changes as disease progresses and with clinical phenotypes such as biological sex, age and obesity. Together, these findings support the need for both shared therapeutic strategies and more personalised approaches in OA, which are likely to enhance future treatment success.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Endotypes, Phenotypes, Multiomic analysis, Biomarkers

Early molecular studies in OA

Whilst the epidemiology of OA and pathological changes in the joint have been known for many decades, the biological study of OA is relatively recent - around 30 years. This occurred with the exponential growth in molecular tools for analysis of human and mouse tissues, development of genetically modified mice, and the ability to perform human molecular studies at scale (through affordable high-throughput analysis). Tool development was fuelled in the 1980s by the purification and cloning of inflammatory molecules (cytokines) and proteases, including the metalloproteinases, that were capable of degrading extracellular matrix-rich tissues such as cartilage [[1], [2], [3]]. Inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1 (IL1) and tumour necrosis factor (TNF) were able to induce proteolytic degradation of articular cartilage in vitro [4,5], thus identifying MMPs and inflammatory cytokines as putative pathogenic drivers in OA [[6], [7], [8]]. Towards the end of the 1990s, anti-cytokine monoclonal antibodies were shown to have therapeutic efficacy in inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis [9]; the hunt was on to find such a biological therapy for OA. Sadly, the results of randomised controlled trials in which disease modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) were tested in OA have been disappointing – showing little or no significant difference over placebo [10]. In retrospect, whilst modest increases in cytokine levels are measurable in the OA joint, few studies supported a mechanistic, driving role for these cytokines. Moreover, the epidemiology, which largely described a mechanical aetiology in OA, and joint pathology which demonstrated a modest inflammatory infiltrate and bone gain rather than bone loss, did not fit with an “inflammatory” joint disease. Osteoarthritis was evidently quite distinct from the immune-mediated arthritides.

Agnostic large scale omic studies in OA

The danger of investigating candidate proteins such as inflammatory cytokines in OA is that one is unable to assess their relative importance in disease biology as a whole. The advent of more agnostic approaches to molecular discovery was therefore very welcome. Early microarray studies of human OA cartilage revealed the breadth of active biology in disease [[11], [12], [13]] and this was soon replicated in preclinical rodent models of OA, including those in which OA was induced by surgical destabilisation of the joint [[14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Molecules regulated in disease included the inflammatory cytokines, chemokines and metalloproteinases, but also included molecules relating to tissue remodelling and repair involving growth factors and matrix proteins - molecules also involved in musculoskeleletal development [20]. Importantly, regulation of all these molecules was completely abrogated in mouse joints when the destabilised joint was immobilised (by prolonged anaesthesia or neurectomy), demonstrating a link between mechanical load and pathological gene regulation in OA [21].

One major recent breakthrough in elucidating OA biology has been through large-scale genome wide association studies. In nearly 2 million individuals, over 900 genetic risk variants have been identified and many of these have been linked to putative effector genes [22]. For the first time, it has been possible to align putative effector genes on key biological pathways. These pathways are consistent with those associated with the injury response of articular cartilage, including key developmental and repair pathways involving transforming growth factor beta (TGFb), bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), Wnt and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) pathways [20,[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Genetic variants associated with the retinoic acid pathway are also strongly represented, although interestingly, this appears to be almost exclusive to hand OA. Retinoic acid is also highly mechanosensitive and is able to suppress inflammatory signalling in response to cartilage injury [27,28]. No clear evidence of an immune-mediated disease is evident from genetic data. Separate mechanistic studies suggest that the growth factor mediated biology reflects a chronic injury/repair response in the joint, driven by chronic mechanical stress [29].

OA phenotypes

For a long while, clinicians have ascribed phenotypes to OA patients in clinic. These are defined by shared clinical features that appear to separate subgroups of patients. Perhaps the best described of these is “inflammatory OA” [30,31]; first coined for those patients with erosive (on X-ray) hand OA in whom palpable synovitis was also especially evident. Large joint OA may also be described as inflammatory if the joint displays cardinal features of inflammation such as heat, redness and swelling (joint effusion/synovial hypertrophy/synovitis). This is a subjective assessment and most patients will have some of these features that fluctuate over time. Many patients with OA (affecting hand and large joints) will have “inflammatory flares” of disease and some of these events may have an associated crystal arthritis which can exacerbate established OA [[32], [33], [34]]. Clinical phenotyping has developed beyond descriptors relating to the joint itself and may be by associated feature/co-pathology e.g. ageing, obesity, diabetes mellitus, history of joint trauma [[35], [36], [37]]. Much effort has gone into trying to find consistent features to define patients in this manner and to link phenotype to different types of drug treatment that might enhance success in clinical trials. Phenotype-specific drug testing has been examined in the context of ‘inflammatory OA’, where clinical trials have been enriched for individuals exhibiting erosive hand OA or with evidence of synovitis by power doppler/MRI [[38], [39], [40]]. Few studies have performed a head-to-head comparison of drug effect in individuals with ‘non-inflammatory’ OA. There are a few exceptions to this including a nested sub-study in the HERO [41] (Hydroxychloroquine Effectiveness in Reducing symptoms in hand OA) study in which hydroxychloroquine was examined in an active hand OA population in which some individuals had evidence of synovitis by power doppler. Neither the whole group, nor the enriched inflammatory group, showed an improvement of symptoms with hydroxychloroquine. Small therapeutic effects have been seen with the anti-inflammatory treatment, methotrexate. Some of the studies enriched for a patient population with evidence of more inflammatory disease. These study results are inconsistent and where an effect has been demonstrated it is small and not deemed to be clinically meaningful [38,39,42].

OA endotype discovery in OA

Endotypes are defined as molecular signatures or markers (usually a cluster of molecules or whole molecular pathways) that are quantitative measures of biology (protein, protein fragments, mRNA, miRNAs, genetic markers) from patient tissue. Such molecular signatures could align with phenotypes and help confirm the potential value of subdividing patients by clinical features, especially if the molecular endotype could predict a biological pathway that might be a drug target. Molecular endotypes could be independent of clinical phenotype. Both candidate (e.g. measures of inflammatory cytokines, collagen fragments) and agnostic (e.g. unbiased mRNA sequencing) approaches have been used to identify molecular markers that might associate with different clinical phenotypes and outcomes. These studies are both cross-sectional and longitudinal and have the potential to discover candidate molecular targets, identify those individuals who may respond better to a given drug, and predict those individuals whose OA is more likely to progress over a typical trial period (1-3 years). Published studies have used a mixture of tissues for analysis including histopathological and transcriptomic analyses of solid joint tissues. In one such study, genome-wide expression analysis using unsupervised clustering of intact knee OA cartilage identified two molecular endotypes, one (Group A) characterised by chondrogenic matrix remodelling with innate immune and chemokine pathway activation, and another (Group B) defined by non-chondrogenic matrix changes with altered Wnt and transforming growth factor beta signalling, complement activation, and enhanced mechanotransduction [43]. No attempt was made in this study to look at how these endotypes related to patient phenotype. Molecular heterogeneity of OA cartilage has also been examined by single-cell RNA sequencing, identifying distinct chondrocyte populations that correlated with clinical disease progression [44]. Histopathological assessment has been performed in OA synovium. For instance, in nearly 500 synovial biopsies from a mixture of non-OA (mainly post-mortem) and arthroplasty cases, where chondropathy and bone features were also documented, 3 OA subgroups could be defined based on synovitis score. These were associated with overlapping cartilage and bone pathologies [45]. The same group also examined transcriptomic profiles of snap frozen synovium from symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals with OA or non-OA controls (split across discovery and replication sets). 96 genes associated with pain and inflammation, were profiled by RT-PCR, of which 8 replicated and were distinct between symptomatic and asymptomatic OA individuals. Whilst this study matched patient samples by age, BMI and biological sex, solid tissue analyses of this sort are rarely able to adjust for potential confounding factors [46].

Plasma/serum/urine offer several advantages for endotyping studies as they are accessible, can be repeatedly sampled, and are amenable to high throughput analysis. In the IMI-APPROACH cohort, 297 patients, pre-selected for disease progression, were used to identify three data-driven endotypes based on combined predefined biochemical (16 serum or urine markers) and clinical features; C1 (“low tissue turnover”: low repair and low tissue turnover), C2 (“structural-damage”: high bone formation/resorption and cartilage degradation), and C3 (“systemic-inflammation”: elevated inflammatory and MMP-driven markers with relatively low bone/cartilage markers) [47]. These clusters were replicated in an external FNIH/OAI cohort and were linked to different longitudinal outcomes (C1 enriched for non-progressors, C2 linked to structural progression, C3 to sustained or worsening pain) [47]. Using the same cohort, Hannini et al. found only modest longitudinal stability of endotypes with an average of 55% concordance (95% CI 0.46-0.60) in endotype assignment at up to 24 months follow-up [48]. More recent analyses of 15 serum cytokines in IMI-APPROACH found that, compared with tissue-turnover biomarkers, cytokines fluctuated more over time (18-months follow-up) and were less able to discriminate the three previously identified endotypes [49]. In other proteomic studies of serum/plasma of knee OA subjects, Luo et al. showed in two separate cohorts (n=106, n=147) that a low cartilage formation endotype defined by reduced circulating PRO-C2 (a marker of type II collagen synthesis) was associated with significantly greater radiographic joint space narrowing over 24 months and a 3-4-fold higher risk of any radiographic structural progression [50]. Werdyani et al. applied targeted plasma metabolomics to 615 primary OA patient samples and identified three clusters (clusters A-C) driven respectively by elevated butyrylcarnitine (C4), low arginine, and low lysophosphatidylcholine (with palmitic acid) - interpreted as “muscle-weakness”, “arginine-deficient” and “low-inflammatory” endotypes - with strong discriminatory performance for key metabolites (AUCs up to 0.99 for arginine) [51]. These metabolomic endotypes showed distinct clinical correlation with other co-morbidities such as diabetes but their relationship to OA clinical outcome was not explored.

The synovial fluid (SF) offers an attractive fluid in which to do molecular endotype analyses. Like plasma/serum/urine, it can be sampled from individuals at all stages of disease. Unlike systemic fluids, SF provides a window into the biology of a given affected joint, is not confounded by the number of joints affected and is not diluted by the plasma volume, although will reflect the plasma proteome as it is partly formed by an ultrafiltrate of plasma. Ali et al., used mass spectrometry to perform an agnostic analysis of the SF in 31 individuals: split across healthy controls, early-stage (degenerative meniscal tear) and late-stage (arthroplasty) knee OA. Of the 406 eligible proteins that were measured in all three groups, principal component analysis was able to separate the three clinical groups. The authors reported elevation of proteins in early OA versus controls and an overall reduction in protein abundance in late-stage OA [52].

A larger scale analysis of OA SF is STEpUP OA (Synovial fluid to detect molecular endotypes by unbiased proteomics in OA) [53]. In this international consortium, 1361 OA synovial fluid samples were analysed by SomaScan (semi-quantitative protein assay covering over 7000 proteins). Synovial fluid proteomic data were linked to cross-sectional clinical (patient reported knee pain, radiographic disease severity) and demographic (age, sex, BMI) data. In the primary analysis a k-means cluster analysis was used to identify molecularly driven clusters. Two clusters were identified although these were continuous (overlapping biology) rather than distinct (separated from one another by nature of having different biological pathways) and reflected changes in the levels of specific proteins, rather than different molecules/pathways. Cluster structure in STEpUP OA was preserved after correcting for confounding factors, and after stratification by sex and radiographic disease stage [54]. These results suggested that OA represents a biological continuum rather than a collection of distinct clusters each defined by its own distinct biology. This might explain the lack of cluster stability seen in IMI-APPROACH. The core pathway associating with radiographic disease severity in STEpUP OA was ‘epithelial mesenchymal transition’, characterised by matrix remodelling enzymes, growth factors and matrix proteins. This was evident in all patients irrespective of whether they had been stratified by clinical phenotypes including biological sex, obesity status or age [55]. Other pathways, including “complement” and “coagulation” were robustly regulated in the whole dataset, but lost statistical significance in certain groups after stratification. For instance, obesity (BMI>30) was associated with significant complement and coagulation pathway activation, which was not significant in non-obese individuals. Complement and coagulation are part of the innate immune response to tissue injury, and have been recognised previously [56] to have therapeutic targeting potential [57,58]. C-reactive protein (CRP), a clinical marker of systemic inflammation, and often used as a biomarker of inflammatory OA, was also higher in the obese group, as others have described previously [59], and independently associated with complement and coagulation pathways [55]. A biological continuum has also been recognised by others using serum proteomics in 200 women from the Chingford cohort, in which a panel of proteins previously shown to predict progression of established OA were also identified as predicting incident radiographic knee OA [60].

Conclusion

Recent studies have advanced the field substantially and addressed key questions that had limited clinical translation to date. Substantial progress has been made in understanding molecular pathways that underpin OA particularly using agnostic approaches (Fig. 1). The change in approach has led to a shift in how we consider the term ‘endotype’; from early studies using small panels of putative biomarkers of disease such as collagen fragments, to more recent molecular screening which has identified whole pathways associated with disease processes, some of which correlate more strongly with particular clinical phenotypes e.g. obesity status. This has changed the narrative to OA as a biological continuum with patients expressing different levels of a given pathway at any one time. The biological continuum suggests that early molecular changes persist and evolve in OA and supports the view that incident and progressive OA reflect different stages of a common underlying molecular process. How historical biomarkers of matrix degradation, say, relate to biological pathways is unknown at present, but it is clearly the case that both ‘biomarkers’ and broader molecular ‘endotypes’ will now need to prove their worth by showing that they can be used to stratify groups to improve patient outcomes e.g. with a given treatment, or help predict prognosis. If successful, such approaches have the potential to make a major impact on the validation of new targets in OA. Their impact would be greatest where molecular signatures align with recognisable clinical phenotypes so that selected individuals can be more easily identified in the clinic.
