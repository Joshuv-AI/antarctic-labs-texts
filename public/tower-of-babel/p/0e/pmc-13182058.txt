An implementation mapping approach to equitable program delivery: building readiness in community organizations
Authors: Thea Franke, Lindsay Nettlefold, Joanie Sims Gould, Karim M Khan, Heather A McKay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommunity-based, not-for-profit organizations have potential to advance health equity through delivery of health-promoting interventions to underserved populations. However, structural barriers (e.g., limited resources) and contextual nuances often inhibit organizations’ ability to adopt, implement, and sustain evidence-based interventions (EBIs). Implementation support systems (‘support systems’) can address these challenges by providing tailored support to enhance organizational readiness — an essential precursor for high-quality equitable implementation. In this paper, we used a case study approach to identify implementation strategies (‘strategies’) that a support system uses to build readiness in community organizations to adopt, implement, and sustain a health-promoting EBI for diverse groups of older adults (Choose to Move; CTM).MethodsWe used a novel application of implementation mapping to name, define, and operationalize strategies used by the support system. We operationalized each strategy across eight dimensions: actor, action, action target, dose, theoretical justification, mechanism of action, temporality, and implementation outcomes addressed. We aligned strategies to a readiness framework (R=MC2; motivation, innovation-specific capacity, general capacity) and devised an equitable implementation roadmap to build organizational readiness.ResultsWe identified 17 strategies enacted by the support system to enhance organizational readiness, particularly motivation and innovation-specific capacity, across a five-stage implementation roadmap: 1. engagement, 2. readiness assessment, 3. feedback and prioritization, 4. preparation, and 5. support during delivery. The 17 strategies aimed to improve adoption, reach, fidelity, and sustainment of CTM.ConclusionsOur results highlight the critical role of support systems to achieve equitable implementation of EBIs in community organizations. When support systems use tailored strategies to build readiness, they contribute to equitable access to health-promoting EBIs which in turn can prevent widening health inequities. The equitable implementation roadmap offers a practical guide for others working in community health promotion. Future studies that evaluate the selection and effectiveness of strategies that build readiness to achieve equitable implementation and improve health outcomes in diverse groups are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00918-x.Keywords: Implementation mapping, Organizational readiness, Implementation support, Community-based research, Practical implementation science, Health equity, Older adults

Background

“Regardless of how valuable interventions may be, they scarcely implement themselves” [1]

Implementation science “is the scientific study of methods to promote the systematic uptake of research findings and other evidence-based practices into routine practice, and to improve the quality and effectiveness of health services” [2]. Historically, implementation science most often focused on healthcare or clinical settings [3]. Research in clinical settings contributed to the development of frameworks, theories, models, and taxonomies of implementation strategies (‘strategies’; “methods and techniques used to enhance the adoption, implementation, and sustainability of a clinical program or practice”) [4, 5]. Despite these many valuable contributions, few implementation studies evaluated programs or practices in non-clinical settings [6, 7]. Implementation science has also underutilized its potential to advance health equity [8, 9]. Health equity is defined as “opportunities for all to achieve optimal health” [9, 10]. Community-based, not-for-profit and non-government organizations [11] (sometimes referred to as the ‘third sector’), play an increasingly critical role in public health [12]. Community organizations are often tasked with delivering health-promoting evidence-based interventions (EBIs), such as those designed to foster peoples’ physical activity, social connectedness, and mental health [13]. Many organizations seek to reach underserved populations, such as individuals or groups who are older adults, of low-income, or racialized. Thus, the third sector has potential to be a key player in societal efforts to advance health equity. However, these organizations often lack the necessary staff, financial resources, or specialized skillsets to support implementation of EBIs [6, 7, 14–17].

The misalignment between expectations for third sector organizations to deliver health-promoting EBIs and an organization’s readiness (‘readiness’) to do so, creates challenges. Readiness is a multi-dimensional construct that refers to the "tangible and immediate indicators of organizational commitment to implement an intervention" [18]. Readiness is more formally defined as ‘motivation × innovation-specific capacity × general capacity’ and abbreviated as R=MC2 [19]—readiness is essential for high-quality implementation [20, 21]. Motivation comprises “incentives and disincentives that contribute to the desirability to use an innovation” (e.g., support that contributes to its use). General capacity refers to attributes of an organization that influence whether or not an organization can successfully implement an innovation (e.g., resources, staffing). Innovation-specific capacity is more complex as it comprises the knowledge, skills and abilities required to implement an innovation and depends on the varying nature of the innovations nature and scope). Although general capacity (e.g., leadership, organizational culture) and innovation specific capacity (e.g., staff skills) are critical components of readiness, they are not enough to effect change on their own. Readiness also depends on an organization’s motivation (e.g., relative advantage and priority of the EBI compared to other organizational initiatives) to implement an EBI [22]. Community organizations with low readiness may struggle to deliver EBIs as designed [23], resulting in diminished effectiveness. This creates a paradox as community organizations that are ‘less ready’ often serve more marginalized groups that experience collective barriers to health based on ethnicity, age, socioeconomic status (and other factors) [9]. If implementation researchers prioritize community organizations that are more ‘ready’ to deliver, this may perpetuate existing health inequities. Thus, offering tailored support to enhance the readiness of all organizations to implement health-promoting EBIs is essential to tackle health inequities [24]. Equitable implementation support (“when strong equity components —including explicit attention to the culture, history, values, assets, and needs of the community—are integrated into the principles, strategies, frameworks, and tools of implementation science” [25]) enables diverse community organizations to serve their constituents effectively and diminishes the likelihood of unintentionally reinforcing disparities.

Implementation support systems (‘support systems’), also known as implementation support practitioners, bridge the gap between research knowledge and real-world practice [16, 26, 27]. Support systems often operate external to community organizations that commit to delivering an EBI [16]. Their role is to identify when motivation and/or capacities are low within an organization, and work with them to identify appropriate strategies (e.g., training, tools, technical assistance) to build readiness [19] to adopt, implement, and sustain the EBI [28]. High quality, tailored, readiness assessment instruments [29] designed for community-based health promotion settings can help prevent health inequities, by addressing readiness-building efforts where they are needed [20]. However, readiness is rarely measured in community settings. The role of support systems to enhance readiness, and the methods (e.g., strategies) they use to do so, are rarely operationalized and measured [30–33]. Thus, both the measurement of readiness and the role of support systems remains under developed and understudied [26, 34].

Herein, we address the need for a repository of field-tested strategies that support systems can use to equitably build readiness in community organizations [6, 35–37]. We used a case study approach to devise a readiness-building roadmap that addresses the following two objectives: 1. to name and define readiness-building strategies that support community organizations to adopt, implement, and sustain a health-promoting EBI for older adults (called Choose to Move; CTM), and 2. to operationalize the strategies and describe the support system’s role in offering equitable implementation support.

Methods

Design and setting

Choose to Move: context and frameworks that guide implementation

CTM is a flexible, community-based health-promoting EBI for older adults that was and continues to be implemented and scaled up using a phased approach (2015-2024+). Between phases we systematically adapted CTM to enhance fit and support broad scale-up [38], for virtual delivery during COVID [39], and to reduce resource use (cost) [40]. Elsewhere we describe the co-design, development, and implementation of CTM at scale [41].

Briefly, the current CTM program [40] is choice-based, coach- and peer-supported, and designed for low active (< 150 min/week moderate to- vigorous physical activity) community-dwelling older adults. CTM comprises two components: a 30 min one-on-one consultation with an activity coach and 8 group meetings (online/in-person) with other participants facilitated by the activity coach. Within each component, program activities (e.g., group discussion, worksheets) are used to support core functions. Core functions (e.g., goal setting, action planning) are defined as essential elements (behaviour change techniques; BCTs) that yield positive health outcomes [42, 43]. CTM has engaged > 7000 (as of December 2024) older adults and 30 organizations across British Columbia (BC), Canada (https://www.choosetomove.ca) and effectively enhanced older adult physical activity and mobility, while reducing their social isolation and loneliness [40].

Implementation of CTM is guided by Durlak and Dupre’s Framework for Effective Implementation [44] which embeds elements of the Interactive Systems Framework for Dissemination and Implementation (ISF) [16] within an ecological model. The ISF consists of three systems (Prevention Delivery, Prevention Support and Prevention Synthesis and Translation systems). Within the Framework for Effective Implementation, the Prevention Delivery and Prevention Support systems are nested within, and influenced by, the community context (e.g., funding), provider characteristics (e.g., self-efficacy) and innovation characteristics (e.g., adaptability). Within the ISF, readiness is touted as a critical determinant of successful implementation [16].

For CTM, the Prevention Delivery System consists of a broad range of large and small not-for-profit community organizations that implement CTM. Initially, we partnered with two large community organizations (i.e., YMCA and British Columbia Recreation and Parks Association), as they had broad geographic reach through recreation centres to implement CTM [45, 46]. These organizations were well-equipped (e.g., infrastructure, staffing, knowledge, skillsets, and resources) to implement CTM broadly. In 2018, we began working with small partner organizations (e.g., neighbourhood houses, seniors’ centres, community services) with reach to individuals that experience many intersecting structural barriers to health (e.g., populations underserved due to race, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, age, and/or geography). To date we have partnered with 28 small partner organizations (Additional file 1. Summary of Choose to Move delivery partner organizations). Of these 28 organizations 43% had reach to older adults in rural/remote locations (geography), 25% had reach to low-income older adults, and 32% had reach to ethnoculturally diverse older adults (e.g., Chinese, Arabic, Punjabi, Indigenous). In our experience, these organizations required more support to implement CTM in their organization compared with our large partners. Without support, many organizations would be unable to overcome challenges related to limited implementation capacity. Organizations would struggle to adopt, adapt and implement programs, potentially limiting program reach to ethnoculturally diverse older adults. Therefore, to ensure equitable implementation of CTM, we provided organizations with tailored support to ‘fit’ their local contexts and resource needs [34].

The CTM Prevention Support System comprises the Active Aging Society (www.activeagingsociety.org) —a not-for-profit organization with a mission to equitably promote older adults’ physical and social health. In 2017, scaling up CTM became the signature initiative of the Active Aging Society. A Central Support Unit (CSU) is embedded within the Active Aging Society; CSU staff have 4–12 years of implementation and scale-up experience. The CSU works directly with all organizations, using strategies (e.g., recruitment support, hire and train an activity coach) tailored to enhance the organizations’ readiness to adopt, implement and sustain CTM.

The Prevention Synthesis and Translation System includes all members of the Active Aging Research Team. We (the Active Aging Research Team) acquired external competitive grant funding to evaluate adaptation, implementation, effectiveness, and cost of CTM [34, 45–47].

Implementation mapping

Implementation mapping is a systematic approach that links implementation determinants to strategies using theoretical or conceptual reasoning [48, 49]. In collaboration with the CSU, we used implementation mapping to retrospectively [50] name, define, and operationalize strategies that the CSU used for small partner organizations. Here we focus on small (rather than large) partner organizations as these organizations typically required greater support; strategies needed to be tailored to address the specific implementation barriers each organization faced. The CSU aligned strategies with the culture, history, values, assets, and needs of the community.

Three implementation mapping steps (described below) guided us to select, tailor, and apply strategies [20, 51, 52] to build readiness and ensure equitable implementation across small partner organizations.

Step 1. Name it (identify and compile strategies used for CTM)

We reviewed the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) taxonomy of strategies [53] to identify strategies used by the CSU to support implementation and scale-up of CTM in small partner organizations.

Step 2. Define it (standardize terminology for community settings)

We used definitions provided by Nathan et al. [54] to include the focus on sustainment, while revising definitions as per Balis et al. [6], to ensure terminology reflected the community setting.

Step 3. Specify it (operationalize each strategy)

We operationalized each strategy across eight dimensions (actor, action, action target, dose, theoretical justification, mechanism of action, temporality, and implementation outcomes addressed), as per strategy reporting guidelines [5].

An equitable implementation roadmap used to enhance readiness to adopt, implement, and sustain evidence-based interventions

Objective 2: operationalize the implementation strategies and describe the role of the support system in achieving equitable implementation

Below we operationalize the eight dimensions of each CTM implementation strategy (actor, action, action target, dose, theoretical justification, mechanism of action, and implementation outcome) across each stage of the equitable implementation roadmap. At each stage, the CSU tailored the use (e.g., dose) of specific strategies to address implementation barriers and enhance each organization's readiness to adopt, implement, and sustain CTM (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5).

Stage 1 - engage organizations

In the initial stage the CSU engaged and built new community partnerships with small organizations reaching underserved populations. The CSU enacted three strategies (Table 1). First, they contacted the organization and distributed a two-page handout about CTM and the support the organization would receive (develop and distribute an implementation glossary); second, they met with the organization to begin initial conversations (conduct educational meetings); and third, they gauged with the organization whether or not delivering CTM would be a good fit for the organization (conduct a local needs assessment). The amount of contact between the CSU and the organizations varied based on how motivated each organization was to proceed (perceived), and the length of support required by the organization in the initial stage. To illustrate, small organizations with limited human resources (fewer staff) had less time to speak with CSU staff. Some organizations had less motivation to implement a new program, as new programs were often not considered a priority. The CSU made sure to tailor conversations to the organization’s specific context (e.g., needs and resources). These three strategies aimed to enhance the organization’s understanding of CTM’s benefits, increased the organization’s motivation to adopt the program, and guided them to become a CTM delivery partner organization (sign a contract with the Active Aging Society; end of Stage 3) if they chose to do so (Table 1).

Stage 2 – assess readiness of the organization to adopt, implement, and sustain Choose to Move

To better understand an organization’s level of readiness to adopt, implement, and sustain CTM, the CSU conducted a readiness (identify barriers and facilitators) assessment (assess for readiness) (Table 2). In the initial phases of CTM, when the CSU began working with small partner organizations, the CSU met by phone with the organization as a follow up to the initial meetings in stage one, and prior to contract signing and/or further involvement or support from the CSU. The CSU spoke with the organization to identify barriers and facilitators to organizational readiness to adopt, implement, and sustain CTM. Since 2023, to optimize this process and provide a more structured and detailed approach, the CSU administered a survey (electronically) that measures organizational readiness on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 7 = Strongly Agree). We adapted the previously used survey [19] to ensure it applied to community contexts and CTM. This strategy provided the CSU an understanding of how ‘ready’ an organization was to implement CTM, specific areas where the CSU could best support organizations to get ready, and how best the CSU could support the organization during implementation of CTM (Table 2).

Stage 3 – provide feedback and prioritize areas to improve

In stage three the CSU enacted two strategies (Table 3). First, they booked a follow up meeting with the organization to review and discuss the readiness survey results. They also discussed with the organization whether CTM was a ‘fit’ with their organization’s priorities, and the needs of the organization’s target population (conduct local consensus discussions). Second, the CSU worked with the organization to identify how CTM could be adapted to suit the needs of the organization (involve executive boards) and their target populations. If the organization chose to move forward, the Active Aging Society drafted a delivery agreement (strategies enacted by AAS: obtain formal commitments, fund and contract for the clinical innovation) that described timelines, deliverables (e.g., number of programs), budget, and support the organization would receive from the CSU to implement CTM (e.g., contact hours). If an organization chose not to move forward, the CSU asked if they would be willing to use and promote CTM website resources, and the CTM newsletter and Facebook page. The organization was invited to have their constituents sign up for the CTM newsletter to receive updates about CTM that were being offered elsewhere (Table 3).

Stage 4 - prepare organizations to deliver Choose to Move

In stage four, the CSU enacted seven strategies to prepare the organization to deliver CTM (Table 4). First, the CSU scheduled a meeting with the organization’s staff to discuss hiring an activity coach. If the activity coach was external to their organization, the CSU provided the organization with an activity coach ‘key characteristics’ document and a job description template (identify and prepare champions) that could be tailored to the organization’s needs (Additional file 8. activity coach ‘key characteristics’ and job description). Second, the CSU supported recruitment of participants into CTM. Each organization used its own recruitment methods to promote CTM; the CSU provided a variety of materials that could be tailored to the organization’s context and target population and supported recruitment and promotion if the organization did not have their own materials (develop resource sharing agreements). For example, some organizations’ target populations included Chinese speaking older adults. Therefore, the CSU provided recruitment materials with images relevant to the target population. Third, the CSU checked in with the organization to address challenges prior to delivery of CTM (facilitation). Fourth, the CSU facilitated tailoring of CTM delivery required by the organization (tailor strategies). Fifth, the CSU provided an online training platform for the activity coach (self-directed and interactive training modules). The self-directed training component provides an overview of CTM, information sessions, group meetings, one-on-one consultations, optional program components, organization information, and materials. The interactive training component provides the activity coach small group case studies that describe common challenges and situations activity coaches might encounter during CTM delivery (make training dynamic). Sixth, the CSU scheduled a training debrief call with the activity coach to answer any questions or queries about training, and to ensure that the activity coach felt comfortable and ready to deliver CTM (conduct ongoing training). Seventh, the activity coach gained access to CTM resources and ‘administrative materials’ and ‘participant facing materials’ after they completed training (distribute educational materials); materials were reviewed with the CSU during the training debrief call. One-month prior to each program’s launch, the CSU contacted the activity coach to determine anticipated start date, planned delivery format, delivery location, and to monitor any further adaptations (facilitation continued) (Table 4).

Stage 5 - support organizations while they deliver the Choose to Move program

In stage 5, the CSU enacted four strategies (Table 5). The CSU regularly scheduled contact with activity coaches throughout program delivery to discuss progress (provide ongoing consultation). The CSU was available to counter any challenges (provide local technical assistance) on an ‘as needed’ basis. The CSU hosted ‘Community of Practice’ meetings that brought together activity coaches from across organizations. Activity coaches shared delivery tips and success stories and discussed solutions to challenges they experienced during program delivery (create a learning collaborative). The CSU established and built working relationships internally within partner organizations and externally, outside the partner organization to promote information sharing and collaborative problem-solving with other organizations (promote network weaving) (Table 5).

Discussion

Few studies in the field of implementation science and public health research describe how best to integrate equity into implementation processes and outcomes [24]. In this paper, we respond to a call to action [62, 63] to fill the evidence gap in equitable implementation [24, 62]. We used a novel application of implementation mapping to offer a 5-stage implementation roadmap that supports readiness-building in diverse community organizations and enables them to adopt, implement, and sustain health-promoting EBIs (like CTM). Although our roadmap focuses on an EBI for older adults, it provides a transferable template for health promotion across sectors to engage and prepare "less ready" organizations to deliver health-promoting EBIs effectively [9]. To operationalize strategies within the roadmap, they must be tailored to alternative contexts and specific interventions.

The roadmap

We translated a comprehensive organizational readiness framework (R=MC2) [19] into a systematic process that builds readiness in diverse community settings. The CTM readiness-building process provides an equitable implementation roadmap that is adaptable, reproduceable, measurable, and actionable. The process guides users to identify specific strategies or bundles of strategies that promote equitable implementation. The process aligns with calls to develop ‘guiding principles and methods’ [64] or ‘logic models’ that guide interactions between the delivery system and the support system [65]. Support systems often provide reactive, problem-solving support rather than a comprehensive implementation plan [66]. The roadmap enables the CSU (support system) to proactively tailor strategies to the needs and resources of the delivery system.

Our roadmap closely aligns with the updated ISF 2.0 [27] that explicitly describes the need to integrate the culture, values, assets, and needs of communities into implementation science methodologies and practice [63]. Organizations that serve marginalized populations must overcome structural barriers (e.g., limited resources) and contextual nuances that may inhibit the adoption and implementation of EBIs in under-resourced settings [24, 27, 62]. Without tailored, and often more intensive, support these organizations may fail to adopt and implement health-promoting EBIs. This failure perpetuates health inequities [9]. There may be greater financial costs (e.g., support system time) to ready these community organizations to deliver health-promoting EBIs [27]. However, the potential societal, social, and health benefits to marginalized groups far outweigh these costs [27, 67].

Support systems

A cornerstone of our roadmap is the CSU (support system). While support systems are generally understudied [16, 26], it is increasingly recognized that their role is critical to bridge the gap between research knowledge and real-world practice [68]. This is particularly true in resource-constrained community contexts. For CTM, the CSU plays a key role in building readiness and supports equitable implementation throughout the lifecycle of the program. Support systems must redress power imbalances and work in harmony with community organizations to integrate their voices throughout the process (collaborative decision making) [25], from initial planning to program delivery (e.g., engage, assess, prioritize, prepare and support) [69]. For CTM, the CSU aims to foster equitable partnerships between researchers and community organizations. To enhance trust, relevance, and ownership, the CSU engaged community organizations to co-design the implementation process, and tailored strategies to their needs [70]. The roadmap and the strategies have supported organizations in clarifying roles and processes, streamline implementation planning, and strengthen internal capacity to deliver the program. These factors may contribute to more efficient use of resources and expand reach to ethnoculturally diverse older adults over time. The CSU continues to make targeted efforts to work with community organizations to ensure academic or research priorities do not overshadow community needs. This begs the question of how ready the support system is/needs to be to be able to provide support — (e.g., support system competencies) [36, 71]. Future work should consider describing readiness of the support system as well as the delivery system [64].

In CTM, the CSU is embedded within the Active Aging Society and is comprised of implementation support staff who have delivered and evaluated health-promoting EBIs in community contexts for more than a decade. The CSU works with both the organizations (delivery system) and academic partners (AART – synthesis and translation system). However, the structure of a support system may vary over time. For example, our research team initially served in the role of support system. In later stages of scale-up, CTM was shepherded by the Active Aging Society [54]. Evidence-based and evidence-informed support is an essential part of dissemination and implementation of any intervention. Investment in support systems by funders is necessary across the lifecycle of any program, and leads to improved outcomes by delivery systems [27].

Implementation mapping

Matching strategies to implementation determinants and indicators of organizational readiness is essential to improve adoption and implementation of EBIs– yet it is often overlooked [72]. Currently, there is no consensus on how to match strategies to address determinants of implementation, particularly in community settings [37, 52]. Some determinant strategy mapping used the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)-ERIC match tool [73]. However, we noted a few challenges for those newer to public health and health promotion who might otherwise benefit from using this tool. First, the match tool does not distinguish between strategies relevant for community settings vs. health care settings [37]. Second, the tool’s output does not present one strategy per CFIR construct; instead, it provides a list of potential strategies with percentages of agreement identified based on expert opinion. There are no recommendations for how many and which strategies address specific barriers and when these strategies should be used. An evidence-based standard implementation mapping approach [48] can be used to formally identify, operationalize, track, and evaluate the role of support systems as they develop and enact strategies that build readiness in community organizations [28, 59, 74, 75]. Along with what strategies the support system can use, there needs to be more focus on how strategies can be delivered [27].

Our implementation mapping process extends the work of Domlyn et al. [60] by more formally considering the strategies required to engage organizations and enhance their readiness (motivation) to adopt CTM in the initial stages. In our context of community-based health promotion there is often a lot of time invested with organizations before they adopt (i.e., sign a funding contract). Within our roadmap, the strategies in stages one (‘Engage’) and two (‘Assess readiness’) did not directly target a specific implementation outcome [19]. These strategies were used to increase organizational motivation to adopt CTM; however, adoption as an implementation outcome did not occur until stage three when organizations signed a funding contract. Ideally, strategies that target motivation-related subcomponents are targeted early in the implementation process to enhance organizational readiness to adopt the program [76]. Organizational readiness to implement the program (e.g., innovation-specific capacity and related subcomponents) may be more relevant later in the implementation process (e.g., stage 4 ‘preparing to deliver’) and target implementation outcomes more directly (e.g., fidelity). Our roadmap acknowledges that readiness is dynamic and fluctuates over time and across stages of implementation [60]. However, the optimal order of targeting specific readiness components and subcomponents to achieve specific implementation outcomes is not yet clear [77].

Strengths and limitations

We highlight three key strengths of our work. First, our findings are built upon the vast experience of the CSU, that skillfully linked support, delivery, and research systems across almost a decade of CTM scale-up. The implementation mapping process was grounded in the ‘practical’ real world experiences of the CSU. CSU staff bridged the knowledge-to-practice gap by documenting strategies that build readiness, and impact implementation outcomes in diverse community settings. Second, we describe, in detail, the actors, actions, timelines, and doses of support required to equitably implement a health-promoting EBI. Other researchers and implementation practitioners can use this information as a model to track implementation, calculate costs, guide future implementation approaches, and weigh program investments. Third, we envision that our findings will catalyze much-needed discussion regarding the costs and accountability of funders associated with equitable approaches to implementation of health promoting EBIs. The question of whether there is a minimum (and affordable) level of support that should be available to effectively build readiness in “less ready” organizations - remains unanswered.

The key limitation of our study is that implementation mapping is commonly used prospectively in behavioral health and clinical settings [52]. Mapping is rarely applied to identify strategies retrospectively—although we adopted this approach. Ideally, the actors, dose and actions involved in each strategy would be defined in advance and measured prospectively to enable comparison with what was done during the implementation process (e.g., evaluation). However, in community settings, implementation tends to be driven by practical expertise. Strategies reflect participatory action with community partners and the unique needs of each organization. Thus, strategies are often selected ad hoc [50]. We demonstrated that mapping principles could be applied retrospectively to "reverse engineer" implementation of CTM and provide a roadmap moving forward. Thus, although conceptually a limitation, this could also be perceived practically as a strength.

Future directions and conclusions

There is a dire need to prioritize equitable implementation of EBIs. This can be achieved by building the capacity and motivation of less ready community organizations to support implementation and advance health equity. It is time for implementation practitioners and scientists to: (1) find ways to measure readiness within community organizations; (2) use implementation approaches and tailored strategies to enhance organizational readiness; (3) specify and test mechanisms of change for strategies; (4) conduct effectiveness research on discrete, multi-faceted strategies that target readiness at specific stages; and (5) systematically track and report strategies that build readiness across organizations that serve diverse groups.

We advocate that in community settings, researchers identify and describe the strategies used to ensure equitable implementation so that a standardized implementation barrier-strategy matching tool within an equity-focused framework can be developed. To expand the knowledge base and help refine the mapping process, we encourage more researchers to document contextual factors that influence successful and equitable implementation and match them with strategies. This new knowledge can be used to develop customized tools that serve diverse community settings [37]. Ultimately, these tools can be used to promote equitable implementation of health-promoting EBIs in a variety of communities—including those that serve marginalized and underserved populations.

Important next steps are to i. more comprehensively measure readiness with high quality, context specific instruments, ii. evaluate implementation and effectiveness of strategies and levels of support provided to enhance organizational readiness, and iii. evaluate ways that costs can be optimized without compromising effectiveness. Studies are needed that describe when and how equitable strategies improve readiness, implementation effectiveness, and health outcomes in diverse groups [3, 24, 78]. We escalate the call to action for researchers and practitioners to int17egrate equity into implementation science and public health research [24, 62], and call upon funding partners to support them to do so.
