Do policy context and structural disadvantage shape SNF VBP performance? Evidence from national facility-year data
Authors: Juhi B Shahani, Kathryn D Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSkilled nursing facility (SNF) performance under the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) Skilled Nursing Facility Value-Based Purchasing (SNF VBP) program is intended to reflect quality of care but may also be shaped by broader policy and structural contexts. This study evaluates whether Medicaid expansion status and structural disadvantage, measured using the Structural Racism Effects Index (SREI), are associated with high SNF performance.MethodsWe conducted a pooled repeated cross-sectional analysis of facility-level data from fiscal years 2019-2021 and 2024, including 57 816 facility-year observations. High performance was defined as a total SNF VBP score ≥50 (upper quartile). Multivariable logistic regression estimated associations between Medicaid expansion, SREI, and high performance, adjusting for ownership, facility size, resident census, nurse staffing, prior penalties, and year.ResultsFacilities in Medicaid expansion states had higher odds of high performance (aOR = 1.19; 95% CI: 1.13-1.24), while greater structural disadvantage was associated with lower odds (OR = 0.87; 95% CI: 0.85-0.89). Higher registered nurse staffing was associated with better performance, while ownership, LPN staffing, and penalties were not significant.ConclusionSNF performance reflects both policy and structural conditions. Without accounting for these factors, value-based payment models may reinforce existing inequities.

Introduction

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services'(CMS) Skilled Nursing Facility Value-Based Purchasing (SNF VBP) program links Medicare payment incentives to skilled nursing facility performance to promote higher-quality care.1 Enacted under the Protecting Access to Medicare Act of 2014 and implemented beginning in October 2018,1 the program withholds 2% of Medicare Part A payments and redistributes a portion of this withhold based on each facility's performance score.1 This composite score, which ranges from 0 to 100 and reflects both achievement and improvement in 30-day, all-cause, unplanned hospital readmission rates, is used to assign an incentive payment multiplier that determines whether facilities lose or gain revenue under the SNF VBP program.1 SNFs with lower performance scores that receive an incentive payment multiplier of less than 1.0 are penalized, which results in the return of only part or none of their withheld funding. In contrast, SNFs with higher performance scores are assigned IPMs greater than 1.0, enabling them to recoup their full 2% withhold and receive up to an additional 2% in bonus payments.

Prior work has shown that SNF performance under value-based purchasing is shaped by structural, policy, and facility-level contexts rather than by clinical care alone.2 As noted by Wheeler et al., SNFs may provide high-quality care during a post-acute stay, yet residents return to communities affected by racism, poverty, and limited access to primary and community-based care.2 These post-discharge conditions can contribute to readmissions, limiting the extent to which readmissions can be attributed to facility performance alone. Because the risk-adjustment models used to evaluate readmission rates in SNF VBP do not fully account2 for these structural and social factors, performance metrics may conflate facility quality with structural disadvantage. At the same time, racially and economically marginalized populations are disproportionately concentrated in under-resourced facilities, which complicates efforts to distinguish systemic disadvantage from true differences in quality.2

In this study, we used Center for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) data from fiscal years 2019 to 2021 and 2024 to examine how facility performance under the SNF VBP program relates to broader policy, structural, and staffing contexts. First, we focused on the intersection of the contextual factors: Medicaid expansion status and area-level structural racism, as defined by the SREI, to capture racialized and socioeconomic disadvantage in the communities SNF facilities serve. Then, we evaluated how facility-level characteristics, including ownership type, facility size, resident census, staffing intensity, and prior enforcement exposure, were associated with the likelihood of being classified as a high-performing SNF. This study also considers how these associations change across fiscal years, particularly before and after the COVID-19-related program adjustments. We examined whether SNF performance varied by state Medicaid expansion status, area-level structural disadvantage, and facility staffing capacity.

Methods

We conducted a pooled, repeated cross-sectional analysis of skilled nursing facility-level data from fiscal years 2019, 2020, 2021, and 2024 to examine the relationship between SNF performance and policy, structural, and operational factors under the CMS SNF VBP program; focusing on between-facility differences rather than within-facility changes over time. The analytic period represents the most recent years with complete performance scoring, excluding 2022 and 2023 because CMS suspended SNF VBP scoring during the COVID-19 public health emergency.

Data sources and analytic sample

We used publicly available data from CMS, including the SNF VBP performance files, Provider Information files, and Care Compare data (formerly Nursing Home Compare).3 Supplemental facility-level characteristics were obtained from CMS's Ownership files and combined with contextual data, including Medicaid expansion status by state and area-level measures of structural inequity.

Structural context was measured using the Structural Racism Effect Index (SREI), a composite index capturing racialized and socioeconomic disadvantage across domains including the built environment, criminal justice, education, employment, housing, income and poverty, social cohesion, transportation, and wealth.4 Unlike traditional deprivation indices such as the Area Deprivation Index (ADI), which primarily reflect material disadvantage, SREI is designed to capture structural inequities shaped by historical and contemporary policy and institutional processes.4 We selected SREI to operationalize contextual disadvantage in a manner aligned with our conceptual framework, which emphasizes the role of structural and policy environments in shaping facility performance under value-based purchasing. SREI values were linked to each facility based on the facility's ZIP Code Tabulation Area using a ZCTA-to-county crosswalk.

Facilities were included if they had nonmissing SNF VBP performance scores and complete information on state Medicaid expansion, ownership, staffing, certified beds, resident census, penalties, and SREI values. The final analytic sample consisted of 57 816 facility-year observations. Facilities contributed observations in any year for which complete data were available; facilities were not required to have data in all four study years. All datasets were publicly available and deidentified, and therefore this study was exempt from institutional review board (IRB) review.

Variables

The primary outcome was high performance under SNF VBP, defined as achieving a total performance score of 50 or higher. Examination of the distribution of performance scores across study years indicated that this threshold approximately corresponds to the upper quartile of facilities, providing an empirically grounded and policy-relevant classification of higher performance. Policy context was operationalized using Medicaid expansion status as a binary indicator (Y/N), distinguishing states that adopted Medicaid expansion under the Affordable Care Act from those that did not. Structural context was measured using both the continuous SREI and a four-level categorical measure of SREI quartiles (Q0-lowest disadvantage, Q1, Q2, Q3- highest disadvantage), where higher quartiles reflect greater area-level disadvantage. Facility-level covariates included ownership type (for-profit, nonprofit, or government-owned), number of certified beds, total number of residents, registered nurse hours per resident-day, licensed practical nurse hours per resident-day, and the total number of penalties recorded in CMS enforcement data for the relevant fiscal year.

Analytic approach

Descriptive statistics summarized facility characteristics and outcomes by fiscal year, with bivariate comparisons conducted using chi-square tests for categorical variables and two-sample t-tests for continuous variables. We then estimated multivariable logistic regression models to examine the association between policy context (Medicaid expansion status) and structural context (SREI) with the likelihood of high SNF VBP performance. Unadjusted models included Medicaid expansion status and SREI only. Adjusted models additionally controlled for facility ownership, number of certified beds, resident census, registered nurse (RN) and licensed practical/vocational nurse (LPN) hours per resident-day, penalties, and fiscal year indicators. Standard errors were clustered at the facility level to account for repeated observations of the same facilities across years. In sensitivity analyses, structural racism was modeled using SREI quartiles to assess potential nonlinear associations with the likelihood of high performance.

We additionally conducted a sensitivity analysis using an alternative outcome definition based on penalty status (penalty vs no penalty), reflecting programmatic consequences under the SNF VBP framework. This approach has been used in prior evaluations of value-based purchasing programs and allows assessment of robustness to outcome specification. Analyses were conducted in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

The analytic sample included 57 816 skilled nursing facility-year observations from fiscal years 2019, 2020, 2021, and 2024. Roughly two-thirds were located in Medicaid expansion states, and about 70% were for-profit. Mean certified bed count and total resident census were relatively stable across years, with facilities averaging about 107 certified beds and 81 residents. Registered nurse hours per resident-day ranged from 0 to 10.43, with a mean of approximately 0.72 hours. Licensed practical nurse hours per resident-day averaged roughly 0.89 hours. The number of penalties recorded during a given fiscal year varied but generally remained low, with mean values between 0.95 and 1.00. Descriptive comparisons indicated that high-performing facilities tended to have slightly higher RN staffing and slightly more residents, while nonhigh performers tended to be located in areas with greater structural disadvantage [Table 1].

Relationship between Medicaid expansion, SREI and facility performance

Unadjusted associations

In unadjusted logistic regression models that included Medicaid expansion, the continuous SREI, and year, facilities located in Medicaid expansion states had higher odds of being high performers compared with those in nonexpansion states (OR = 1.22; 95% CI: 1.17-1.27; P < 0.001). Higher levels of structural disadvantage were associated with lower odds of high performance. Each one-unit increase in the index corresponded to an estimated 14% decrease in the odds of being a high performer (OR = 0.86; 95% CI: 0.84-0.88; P < 0.001). Fiscal year indicators showed higher odds of high performance in FY 2021 relative to FY 2019 (OR = 1.13; 95% CI, 1.07-1.20), while odds in FY 2020 and FY 2024 did not differ significantly from FY 2019.

Associations adjusting for facility-level characteristics

In adjusted logistic regression models that controlled for facility ownership, number of certified beds, resident census, staffing levels, prior penalties, Medicaid expansion status, and fiscal year, both policy and structural context remained associated with high SNF VBP performance. Higher values of the continuous SREI were associated with lower odds of high performance (OR = 0.87; 95% CI, 0.85-0.89; P < 0.001). Facilities located in Medicaid expansion states had higher odds of being high performers compared with those in nonexpansion states (OR = 1.19; 95% CI: 1.13-1.24; P < 0.001). Among facility-level characteristics, registered nurse hours per resident-day were positively associated with high performance (OR = 1.17; 95% CI: 1.12-1.21; P < 0.001), while ownership type, licensed practical/vocational nurse hours, and total penalties were not significantly associated with performance. Resident census was modestly associated with higher odds of high performance (OR = 1.002; 95% CI: 1.001-1.003; P < 0.001). Fiscal year indicators showed higher odds of high performance in FY 2021 relative to FY 2019 and slightly lower odds in FY 2024, consistent with temporal variation observed in unadjusted models.

In sensitivity analyses, structural racism was modeled using SREI quartiles to assess potential nonlinear and dose–response associations with high SNF VBP performance. Results from both unadjusted and adjusted models were directionally consistent with analyses using continuous SREI, demonstrating progressively lower odds of high performance with increasing levels of structural disadvantage. Associations with Medicaid expansion status and other covariates were substantively unchanged.

In additional sensitivity analyses using penalty status as the outcome, results were consistent in direction and magnitude with the primary analysis, with Medicaid expansion associated with a lower likelihood of penalties and higher structural disadvantage associated with an increased likelihood of penalties. Discussion

This study identified four central patterns in SNF performance across FY2019 to FY2021 and FY2024 under the SNF Value-Based Purchasing program. First, facilities located in Medicaid expansion states had higher odds of high performance across study years. Second, higher levels of area-level structural disadvantage, measured using the Structural Racism Effect Index (SREI), were consistently associated with lower odds of high performance. Third, staffing capacity, particularly registered nurse (RN) hours per resident-day, was positively associated with performance, while ownership type was not independently associated with high performance after accounting for policy context, structural disadvantage, and staffing. Together, these findings suggest that SNF VBP performance reflects not only facility-level characteristics but also the broader policy and structural contexts in which facilities operate.

The association between Medicaid expansion status and SNF performance highlights the role of state policy environments in shaping operational conditions for post-acute care providers. Medicaid expansion may influence payer mix,5 financial stability,6 and access to resources that support staffing and care delivery,7 through broader health system financing and workforce effects that can spill over to post-acute providers, although this analysis does not establish causality. Rather than reflecting a direct policy effect, the observed pattern suggests that facilities operating in expansion states may be better positioned to sustain the infrastructure needed to perform well under value-based payment programs. Expansion states may experience lower uncompensated care burdens8 in the broader health system, more stable Medicaid enrollment,9 and improved care continuity across acute10 and post-acute settings,11 including more consistent outpatient access after discharge and better transitions across settings, all of which may indirectly support SNF performance on readmission-based measures. More broadly, variation in Medicaid policy context has been shown to shape provider participation,12 resource availability,13 and performance under Medicare and Medicaid value-based purchasing arrangements.14 Consideration of state-level policy variation may therefore be important for equitable evaluation of facility performance, particularly in programs that impose financial penalties based on outcomes sensitive to system-level capacity.

Structural disadvantage, as captured by the SREI, reflects a combination of historically rooted and present-day conditions, including economic deprivation and limited access to primary and community-based services, that shape the environments to which SNF residents are discharged. Although SREI is conceptualized as a measure of structural inequity, the absence of resident-level demographic composition limits our ability to directly assess differential impacts across populations. Accordingly, SREI in this analysis should be interpreted as a proxy for area-level structural disadvantage rather than a direct measure of differential exposure to structural racism at the individual level. Structural racism has been increasingly recognized as a fundamental cause of health inequities,15,16 operating through residential segregation,17 disinvestment in historically excluded communities,17 and differential access to care.18 Because the SNF VBP program relies on 30-day all-cause hospital readmission rates, performance scores are sensitive to post-discharge conditions that extend beyond facility control. Consistent with this mechanism, we observed a graded association across SREI quartiles, with progressively lower odds of high performance as structural disadvantage increased. Prior research has demonstrated that neighborhood disadvantage19 and racialized geography20 are associated with higher readmission risk and poorer post-acute outcomes. Facilities serving residents from structurally disadvantaged areas may therefore face elevated readmission risk even when the quality of in-facility care is comparable.2 The persistent association between higher SREI and lower performance observed in this study suggests that current risk-adjustment approaches in this program's readmission-based measure may not fully account for these contextual factors,21 allowing performance metrics to reflect community environment22 alongside facility quality.

Staffing capacity, particularly RN hours per resident-day, was consistently associated with higher odds of high performance. This finding aligns with a substantial body of evidence linking RN staffing levels to quality outcomes in skilled nursing facilities.23-25 The specificity of the association to RN hours, rather than LPN hours, is consistent with a skill-mix mechanism relevant to clinical surveillance and care transitions. However, workforce capacity in long-term care settings is shaped by broader labor market conditions,26 Medicaid reimbursement levels,27 and regulatory requirements,28,29 rather than only by facility-level managerial decisions. In this context, staffing capacity may operate as one pathway through which Medicaid expansion and structural disadvantage jointly influence performance under value-based purchasing, reinforcing the interconnectedness of policy environment, place-based disadvantage, and facility resources.

These findings also underscore a broader limitation of the SNF VBP program's reliance on a single outcome measure. While readmissions capture an important dimension of post-acute care quality, they do not reflect the full range of outcomes relevant to skilled nursing facility performance. Facilities may perform well in domains such as functional recovery, resident experience, or safety yet still receive penalties if readmissions are driven by post-discharge conditions. When the primary outcome is readmissions alone, and readmissions are partly shaped by community conditions, facilities in higher-disadvantage areas may face systematic scoring disadvantages even when in-facility care is comparable. Prior work has shown that value-based payment programs may disproportionately penalize safety-net providers30 and facilities serving socially and economically marginalized populations.31 As a result, payment models that rely heavily on readmission-based metrics risk reinforcing existing inequities rather than incentivizing meaningful quality improvement.

Recent CMS program updates and posted measure set changes for fiscal years 202632 and 202733 signal a shift toward more comprehensive performance measurement within the SNF VBP program. These updates include the addition of new quality measures beyond readmissions, expansion of the performance measure set, and the introduction of health equity adjustments that modify scoring based on the characteristics of populations served. Specifically, CMS has proposed incorporating social risk factors and peer grouping approaches to compare facilities serving similar patient populations.

If implemented as intended, these changes may improve equity by reducing systematic disadvantages faced by facilities serving higher-risk populations, particularly those in areas with greater structural disadvantage or in nonexpansion Medicaid states. By benchmarking facilities against more comparable peers and accounting for differences in patient complexity and social risk, these adjustments may allow performance scores to more accurately reflect facility-level care rather than underlying community conditions.

However, the findings of this study suggest that structural and policy-level factors such as area-level disadvantage and Medicaid expansion status may not be fully captured by patient-level or facility-level adjustments alone. Because readmission-based measures remain sensitive to post-discharge environments, equity adjustments that do not incorporate broader contextual factors may only partially address disparities in performance scoring.

Additional approaches, including the incorporation of area-level measures, stratified benchmarking based on structural risk, or explicit adjustment for policy context, may be necessary to ensure that value-based purchasing programs do not systematically disadvantage facilities operating in high-risk environments.

This study has several limitations. First, the pooled repeated cross-sectional design does not permit causality, and residual confounding related to resident case mix or unmeasured clinical complexity may remain. Because we rely on publicly available facility-level files, we were not able to adjust for resident-level clinical severity, dual eligibility, or other composition measures that may correlate with place-based disadvantage. Second, the analysis relies on administrative data, which may be subject to reporting error or measurement limitations. Third, structural disadvantage was measured at the ZIP Code Tabulation Area level, which may not fully capture within-area variation or facility-specific social context. Because SNFs often serve patients from broader geographic regions, this may introduce exposure misclassification and attenuate estimated associations. Finally, although FY 2024 reflects a return to standard SNF VBP scoring, some effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on staffing and care delivery may persist.

Conclusion

In this national analysis of skilled nursing facilities participating in the SNF Value-Based Purchasing program, facility performance was associated with state Medicaid expansion status, area-level structural disadvantage, and staffing capacity. These findings suggest that value-based purchasing outcomes reflect the policy and structural environments in which facilities operate, in addition to facility-level characteristics. As CMS continues to refine the SNF VBP program, incorporating equity-sensitive adjustments and multidimensional performance measures may help ensure that performance assessments more accurately reflect quality of care rather than contextual disadvantage. Addressing structural constraints alongside financial incentives may be important for advancing equity across communities in post-acute care delivery.
