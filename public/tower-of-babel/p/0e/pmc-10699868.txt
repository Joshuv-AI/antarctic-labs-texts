A volatilized pyrethroid insecticide from a mosquito repelling device does not impact honey bee foraging and recruitment
Authors: Margaret J Couvillon, Bradley D Ohlinger, Connor Bizon, Lindsay E Johnson, Laura C McHenry, Benjamin E McMillan, Roger Schürch, Elizabeth Walsh
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBecause nontarget, beneficials, like insect pollinators, may be exposed unintentionally to insecticides, it is important to evaluate the impact of chemical controls on the behaviors performed by insect pollinators in field trials. Here we examine the impact of a portable mosquito repeller, which emits prallethrin, a pyrethroid insecticide, on honey bee foraging and recruitment using a blinded, randomized, paired, parallel group trial. We found no significant effect of the volatilized insecticide on foraging frequency (our primary outcome), waggle dance propensity, waggle dance frequency, and feeder persistency (our secondary outcomes), even though an additional deposition study confirmed that the treatment device was performing appropriately. These results may be useful to consumers that are interested in repelling mosquitos, but also concerned about potential consequences to beneficial insects, such as honey bees.Keywords: pyrethroid, prallethrin, foraging, waggle dances, communication

Introduction

In both agricultural and residential settings, the use of chemical control to manage insect pests should be balanced against any potential adverse effects to nontarget organisms, such as insect pollinators, who provide valuable, ecological services (Heinrich 1975, McGregor 1976, Motten 1986, Delaplane and Mayer 2000, Kremen et al. 2002, Klein et al. 2007, Allsopp et al. 2008, Potts et al. 2010). The impact and severity of these effects will vary depending on which insecticide is used, what insects are involved, the environment in which it is used, and the method by which it is applied (Sponsler et al. 2019, Knapp et al. 2022, 2023, Rundlöf et al. 2022, Benjamin and Nandini 2023, Hellström et al. 2023). For example, some of the methods to apply insecticides are through foliar sprays, bathing, trunk injections, stem or soil application, and seed treatments (Krupke et al. 2012, Kumar et al. 2012, Pisa et al. 2015, Van der Sluijs et al. 2015, Dhananjayan et al. 2020).

It is also possible to volatilize an insecticide (Lichtenstein and Schulz 1970, Holoman and Seymour 1983, Achee et al. 2012, Revay et al. 2013), which creates a chemical plume that then is used in the control of some flying insect pests. Devices that utilize this volatilization of insecticides are popular in the repelling of mosquitos, for example on residential patios. However, a chemical plume might also impact other flying insects, including beneficial pollinators that may be foraging near the patio, and little is known about the potential impact of these devices on them. Prallethrin, which is a pyrethroid, is one such insecticide that may be volatilized. Pyrethroids, the synthetic analogs of pyrethrins (from pyrethrum flowers), are a widely recognized, major class of synthetic pesticides that targets the insect central nervous system via disruption of neuronal voltage-gated sodium channels (Soderlund and Bloomquist 1989, Casida and Durkin 2013).

Honey bees are a widely used, tractable organism commonly deployed to investigate the impact of pest control measures on bee health (Wahl and Ulm 1983, Kremen et al. 2002, Desneux et al. 2007, Cresswell 2011, Eiri and Nieh 2012, Henry et al. 2012, Krupke et al. 2012, Goulson et al. 2015, Kessler et al. 2015, Sánchez-Bayo and Wyckhuys 2019, Ohlinger et al. 2022a). Conveniently, honey bees can be trained to collect food at an artificial feeder (Couvillon et al. 2015, Ohlinger et al. 2022a), which allows investigators first to monitor individual foraging and recruitment behaviors and then to determine if these behaviors are affected by a treatment.

Here, we investigate in a blinded, randomized controlled study the effect of a MR300 Portable Mosquito Repeller (Thermacell Repellents Inc., 32 Crosby Dr., Suite #100, Bedford, MA 01730) charged with mats that emit prallethrin on the behaviors of honey bees. To reflect common consumer habits, we investigated the effects on (1) honey bee foraging and recruitment during the late afternoon and early evening while the device is on and (2) honey bee persistency the next morning while the device is off.

Materials and Methods

Study Organism

We studied honey bees of mixed subspecies, predominantly Apis mellifera ligustica, housed in glass-walled observation hives at the Prices Fork Research Center in Blacksburg, Virginia, USA. We built the observation hives using three American Standard Deep frames and connected them to the outside via a 3 × 30 cm plastic tube. We worked with one colony per trial and performed three trials between July 28 and August 4, 2022, on days with good foraging weather, with subsequent video analysis occurring through the fall and winter 2022–2023. We chose to do our experimental portion during summer because it is easier to train bees to feeders when there is a relative forage dearth in the landscape (Ohlinger et al. 2022b). Colonies were managed throughout the project to prevent overcrowding and to standardize the number of empty cells because it is important for there to be space available for additional sucrose solution storage (Seeley 1989, 1995, Couvillon et al. 2015). The colonies were not given supplemental food for the duration of the field experiments.

Training of Honey Bee Foragers to the Feeders

Foraging honey bees from the observation hives were trained using our step-wise method (Ohlinger et al. 2022a). Briefly, on the day prior to the experiment (Day -1), we placed a feeder containing 2 M scented (10 µL/L) sucrose solution on a wooden platform (approx. 30 cm × 30 cm) that was fitted to the top of a camera tripod, which we positioned approximately 1 m away from the hive entrance tube. We used linalool for trial 1, lavender for trial 2, and linalool for trial 3 (the observation colony used in trial 1 was removed before we started trial 3). Once several bees had accumulated on the feeder to drink, we stepped the feeder + tripod approximately 10–12 times, with c. 10–15 m between steps, across an adjacent parking lot. We marked the bees that arrived at the feeder after the first step with individually numbered plastic discs (Opalithplättchen, Christian Graze KG, Weinstadt-Endersbach, Germany), so that we could confirm whether the marked bee returned to our focal colony.

We continued to mark newly arriving, unmarked recruits for the duration of our training phase (Day −1 and Day 0). With each successive step, we allowed the trained bees to find and to drink from the feeder at least three times. It usually took a few hours to reach our goal for that day, which was a location in front of and center to the eventual position of the two experimental feeders for the next day (‘Feeder 1’ and ‘Feeder 2’, see Fig. 1).

Schematic of feeder training set-up. We worked sequentially with three colonies, one per trial, housed in observation hives. For each trial, we trained two cohorts of bees to forage from either Feeder 1 or Feeder 2, which were identical and equidistant from the colony, but randomly allocated either to treatment or control for each trial.

On the next morning (Day 0, the experimental day), we returned the tripod and feeder to the same penultimate position and ‘reactivated’ the colony, which involved squirting inside the hive using one of the ventilation holes the same, scented, 2M solution as was used on Day −1. The familiar scent has been shown to induce the experienced foragers to return to the previous day’s feeder (Reinhard et al. 2004). We added a second, identical feeder on top of the tripod platform and allowed the trained bees to self-sort across the two feeders. Then we simultaneously stepped the two feeders, now on two tripods, in different directions until they were in final position, equidistant from the hive (Fig. 1). Once there, we began to record the identity and time of a bee visit to each feeder, which we entered on an Android tablet running the app ODK Collect. The report forms from ODK Collect were uploaded into a Google sheet and stored on the project’s Google Team Drive.

To avoid recruiting honey bees from hives from a nearby apiary, we removed the marked foragers that returned to the feeder but were not observed inside the observation hive. Both feeders were left at the final location until a minimum of ten confirmed foragers were reliably visiting each feeder, which usually took until mid or late afternoon on Day 0, right before the start of the experimental phase.

Mosquito Repelling Device

We used a Thermacell MR-300 Portable Mosquito Repeller with a butane cartridge to heat the mats, which causes the repellent to volatilize, during the experimental phase on Day 0. Thermacell provided us with two sets of mats, labelled either ALPHA or OMEGA, and researchers remained blind to which mats were treatment versus control until after the completion of the statistical analyses. The treatment mats contained the active ingredient prallethrin, a pyrethroid. Control mats contained no active ingredient but were otherwise indistinguishable from treatment mats.

Experimental Trial—Primary Data Collection (Foraging Frequency)

The experimental phase ran from 16:00 to 20:00, which represents a time when a consumer is most likely to use a mosquito repelling device that could also overlap with diurnal insect pollinator activity. At the start of the experiment, we removed the two identical, training feeders containing 2 M scented syrup and replaced them with two new feeders containing 1M unscented solution. We chose 1 M because that concentration would generate good (Schürch et al. 2019, Ohlinger et al. 2022a), but not maximum, foraging and recruitment (Seeley 1995) for our study location at that time of year, which is important if there is to be an observable effect of treatment. We placed the two Thermacell devices possessing either treatment or control mats on two additional tripods at 90o, each at 2.28 m from the tripods with the feeders.

During the four-hour experimental phase, we counted how many visits an individual bee made to her 1M feeder, either Feeder 1 or Feeder 2, which were allocated as control and treatment for each trial. For a visit to be recorded, we used a consistent set of criteria. First, the marked bee must land on the feeder, extend her tongue, and drink the solution. Second, visits by a bee were only counted if three minutes had passed from the previous visit (Ohlinger et al. 2022a). We term this discrete, positive count the foraging frequency during the experimental phase, and this was our primary outcome.

Experimental Trial—Secondary Data Collection (Waggle Dance Propensity, Waggle Dance Frequency, Feeder Persistency)

We also video-recorded waggle dances made by the marked bees within the observation hive during the four-hour experimental phase. The waggle dance is a unique behavior in which a returning bee who is foraging at a profitable food source communicates the distance and direction from the hive to the feeding location (von Frisch 1967, Couvillon 2012). We used two Canon Vixia HF R82 video cameras, each positioned on either side of the glass-walled hive, and we recorded to a SanDisk Extreme SD card and then later uploaded the video files to a Google Team Drive for analysis.

During subsequent video analysis, we monitored for two dance metrics. First, we determined the proportion of successful foragers that made any dances at all during the experimental phase. We term this binomial (yes/no) outcome the waggle dance propensity during the experimental phase. Once a bee had made a dance, we monitored how often she then repeats a dance (i.e., how many unique dances that bee will perform). We term this discrete, positive count the waggle dance frequency during the experimental phase.

The next morning (Day 1), we returned the tripods with platforms to the two positions (Fig. 1) and placed two, empty, unscented feeders for the persistency phase from 9:00 AM until 12:00 PM. The Thermacell devices + tripods were also returned to their position from the previous evening, but the devices were turned off, as we were collecting next-day effect data. We counted visitations of marked bees to the empty feeders during the three-hour persistency phase. We term this discrete, positive count feeder persistency during the persistency phase. We collected these data as previous research has shown that bees are more persistent (i.e., returning on Day 1 to check if the empty feeder has become re-rewarding) if the location had previously been highly rewarding (Al Toufailia et al. 2013), making persistency another honey bee behavior that correlates with the resource quality. These last three response variables (waggle dance propensity, waggle dance frequency, and feeder persistency) were our secondary outcomes.

Although sample sizes per treatment cannot be controlled completely, as we are reliant on bees’ recruitment, we achieved fairly even treatment allocation across our three trials (Treatment = 67 bees, Control = 74 bees). Correlation among bees within hives is very low for many behaviors and comparable to between-hive correlations (Schürch et al. 2016, 2019). Therefore, we consider each bee as an independent replicate. To balance treated/untreated devices in respect to feeder position, we performed the three trials and randomly assigned the treatment to a feeder for each trial (see published code for randomization code).

In summary, we used a blind, randomized, paired, parallel group trial with an approximate 1:1 allocation ratio between treated (exposed to active compound) and control (not exposed to active compound) bees. We collected and analyzed the impact of treatment on our primary outcome (foraging frequency) and on our secondary outcomes (waggle dance propensity, waggle dance frequency, and feeder visit persistency).

Deposition Study to Validate the Presence of Prallethrin

During one of our experimental trials, Thermacell’s Science and Research Division (C.B and B.E.M) concurrently performed a deposition study to confirm that the devices and repellent mats were functioning appropriately (see Supplemental Information online for full report). Briefly, this additionally involved setting up six data collection stations around each Thermacell device, both treatment and control. Each data collection station was equipped with three pieces of filter paper, secured by a ring stand, at 0 m (ground), 1 m, and 2 m, to act as a passive dosemeters. The three inner-most test stations featured an air sampling pump with appropriate media (see supplementary online for details). The six data collection stations around the control feeder were far enough away to avoid any potential cross contamination of the systems.

A typical Thermacell multi-insect repellent mat contains approximately 10.86% prallethrin. To represent the worst possible scenario, the treatment mats used in our experiment were slightly overdosed to create a situation where the honey bees were exposed to as much of the compound as possible. Because insect neurons are sensitive to even low concentrations of pyrethroids (Soderlund and Bloomquist 1989), the aim was to create a situation where the foraging honey bees would receive the highest possible exposure from our experimental set-up. As usual, a control mat containing no repellent was loaded into the other repeller device.

The deposition study ran for the entire four-hour experimental phase on Day 0. Afterwards, the concentration of the active ingredient, prallethrin, was determined via GC–MS for the filter papers and air sampling media. The Thermacell MR-300 Portable Mosquito Repeller utilizing the prallethrin treatment emitted the insecticide at a level consistent with previous experiments and shows that the device and mat functioned properly in the study: the filter paper recovery in the field (mean prallethrin Spike Recovery (%) = 96% at 10 minutes) was similar to the previously determined lab mean Spike Recovery Rate for the same time (97.4%, Table 6 of Supplementary Online Information). After four hours, the mean recovery was 90%. In contrast, the control mat demonstrated no detectable concentrations of prallethrin in any of the material tested (<LOD). In summary, the devices and mats and therefore our treatment and control functioned as expected. Full details of the deposition study report are available in the Supplementary Online.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were done in R 4.2.3 (R Core Team, 2020). We present raw data in graphical form and summarized data by treatment and trial phase (training, experimental, and persistency). We present both means (and 95%-CI) and medians (and lower and upper quartiles) in these summaries. Differences in treatments were derived from mixed-models. We present point estimates for the treatment differences along with two-sided 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) and corresponding P-values. In addition, we analyzed the bees according to the intention-to-treat principle (Gupta 2011). That is, we determined to which feeder a bee was trained, either treatment or control. In our study, it was rare for a bee to swap between feeders once they were trained, which allowed us to omit a per-protocol analysis (Ohlinger et al. 2022a). Finally, we calculated the number of visits to a feeder during the training phase and added it to our models as a fixed effect to control for the individual commitment of a bee, regardless of treatment. This is important because the bees that had longer to experience a feeder will form a tighter commitment (Seeley 1995, Couvillon et al. 2015), which may impact the primary and secondary outcomes.

We analyzed count data for foraging frequency and dance frequency during the experimental phase and feeder persistency during the persistency phase using Poisson generalized linear mixed-effect models allowing for trial specific and individual specific intercepts. These latter observation specific random intercepts were necessary because in model checks we detected over-dispersion. The data were modeled using the glmer function from the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015). The binomial outcome of dance propensity was modeled with a Binomial generalized linear mixed-effect model (function glmer, package lme4) (Bates et al. 2015). We used the emmeans package to calculate treatment group contrasts and extract 95% CI (Lenth, 2021).

Finally, we discovered during our video analysis that a small proportion of our bee number tag combinations of font/background color made the font illegible, although of course the color, which should designate whether the bee was treatment or control (see above), was still visible. We dealt with this by assuming these numbers to be missing at random in our data sheet and ignored unidentified dancers for the dance outcomes analyses, which were two of our secondary outcomes (note: our primary analysis was not impacted by this). As an additional sensitivity analyses, where we could only identify the color, not the number, of the tags, for our waggle dance frequency analysis, we assigned the dances of the unidentified bees to the identified dancers in proportion to how many dances these positively identified bees performed. We then performed the same analysis as on the original data set where we assumed IDs to be missing at random. All data and analyses code are available at https://doi.org/10.7294/24147375 (a permanent DOI will be provided after final acceptance of paper).

Results

We Observed No Qualitative Effect of Treatment in Any Phases of the Trial

We began by graphing the raw data and saw that there was no apparent difference in the total number of feeder visits during our three phases of the trial (training, experimental, and persistency) between our control versus our treated bees (Fig. 2). In particular, crude geometric means (95% CI) indicate that bees visited feeders during the training phase 2.12 times (1.25–3.61), during the experimental phase 7.32 times (4.41–12.15), and during the persistency phase 0.33 times (0.21–0.52). The median number of visitations for bees during the experimental phase, which is analyzed below as our primary outcome, was 33 for control and 33 for treatment, respectively.

We observed no qualitative difference between our control and treatment bees in the raw number of visits to the feeders (circles) during either the training phase, experimental phase, and persistency phase for all trials combined. Feeders provided sugar solution during the training and experimental phases, and bees were only exposed to the treatments during the experimental phase. Boxes show the interquartile range and bold line in each box indicates the median. The median number of visitations for bees during the experimental phase (middle panel) was 33 for both treatment and control.

There was no Significant Effect of Treatment on Foraging Frequency During the Experimental Phase

The mean foraging rate predicted by our mixed-model was higher in treatment compared to control during the experimental phase, but nonsignificantly so (mean (95% CI); control: 12.58 (7.09 to 22.31); treatment: 18.42 (11.07 to 30.66); ratio control/treatment: 0.68 (0.44 to 1.07); P = 0.094; Fig. 3). In other words, the number of visitations for treatment on average were 1.46 times higher than visitations for control, but the overlap in 95% CIs indicates that our data also support the possibility that control visitations could be higher. Sensitivity analysis with an offset for observation time during the experimental phase also suggests no effect of treatment (P = 0.151). Adding the potential confounder of number of visits during the training phase (see above) still indicated that treatment was a non-significant predictor of visits during the experimental phase (P = 0.315). This nonsignificance in foraging frequency during experimental phase was our primary outcome.

There was no significant difference in foraging frequency, our primary outcome, between our control and treatment bees. Graph displays the estimated number of bee visits to the feeders during the four-hour experimental phase while the repellent devices were running.

Because Fig. 2 indicates that the Poisson model we pre-specified in the protocol may not be fully appropriate to analyze the primary outcome of feeder visits during the experimental phase, we additionally performed a Wilcoxon rank sum test as a sensitivity analysis, ignoring the potential correlation within hives. This test also indicated that there is no difference in foraging between control and treatment (W = 1820, P = 0.176).

There was no Significant Effect of Treatment on Waggle Dance Propensity and Waggle Dance Frequency During the Experimental Phase

There was no significant difference in the propensity to dance, or whether or not a bee performed at least one waggle dance during the four-hour experimental phase, between our control and treatment bees. For control, 28 bees did not dance and 35 bees danced versus for treatment, where 25 bees did not dance and 42 bees danced (χ2 = 0.4; df = 1; P = 0.517).

Analyzing the data in a generalized mixed-model framework, we found that the propensity to perform waggle dances heavily depended on how long the bees were in the experiment (see above, P < 0.001; result not shown), irrespective of treatment. This effect was so stark that the model did not fit without this added term. Adjusting for this training effect, we found again that there were no differences in waggle dance propensity between treatments (probability to dance (95%-CI): control: 0.73 (0.55 to 0.86); treatment: 0.72 (0.54 to 0.85); OR (95% CI): 1.06 (0.43 to 2.62); P = 0.9). This nonsignificance in waggle dance propensity during experimental phase was one of our secondary outcomes.

Once a bee did perform a waggle dance, the number of dances performed during the experimental phase was not significantly different between control and treatment (Fig. 4; mean number of dances (95%-CI): control: 2.20 (0.89 to 5.44); treatment: 1.86 (0.78 to 4.44); OR (95% CI): 1.18 (0.59 to 2.36); P = 0.638). This effect did not change if we assigned missing IDs to the positively identified dancers in our sensitivity analysis. In fact, the relationship between treatment and number of dances performed became weaker (mean number of dances (95%-CI): control: 2.37 (0.90 to 6.25); treatment: 2.02 (0.80 to 5.11); ratio control/treatment (95% CI): 1.18 (0.57 to 2.42); P = 0.657). This non-significance in waggle dance frequency during the experimental phase was one of our secondary outcomes.

There was no significant difference in dance frequency, one of our secondary outcomes, between our control and treatment bees. Graph displays the estimated number of bee dances during the four-hour experimental phase while the repellent devices were running.

There was no Significant Effect of Treatment on Feeder Persistency During the Persistency Phase

The mean foraging rate predicted by our mixed-model approach was not significantly different between treatment and control during the persistency phase (mean (95% CI); control: 1.13 (0.82 to 1.57); treatment: 1.37 (1.02 to 1.83); ratio control/treatment: 0.83 (0.56 to 1.23); P = 0.351; see Fig. 5). This non-significance in feeder visits during the persistency phase was one of our secondary outcomes.

Discussion

Here, we report that we found no significant effect of a MR300 Portable Mosquito Repeller, which emits prallethrin, a pyrethroid insecticide, on the foraging and recruitment behaviors of honey bees. In particular, we found no significant difference in foraging frequency, waggle dance propensity, waggle dance frequency, and feeder persistency, even though a deposition study confirmed that the devices and mats were performing appropriately. These results may be useful to consumers that are interested in repelling mosquitos but also concerned about potential consequences to beneficial insects, such as honey bees.

Honey bees are exceptionally sensitive to reward, particularly molarity, and will modulate their foraging and recruitment behaviors accordingly to reflect how profitable a bee has assessed the resource quality to be (von Frisch 1967, Seeley 1994, 1995, Couvillon 2012, Couvillon et al. 2014, 2015). Of course, the two feeders during the experiment phase possessed identical profitability (1M) and identical cost (equidistant from the hive), so any differences in the measured behaviors between the treatment and control would indicate an effect of the emitted prallethrin from the treatment Thermacell device. Likewise, honey bees are more likely to dance and will dance more often if the forager is visiting a highly profitable resource (Seeley 1995, Couvillon 2012), and she will persistently return to check a resource location, even after it has become unprofitable, for more profitable resources (Al Toufailia et al. 2013). The fact that we saw no significant difference in any of our outcomes suggests that the honey bees that were trained to forage near a pyrethroid-emitting device, intended to repel mosquitos, were either unaffected or not affected in the responses we measured.

The mosquito repelling devices used in our study volatized prallethrin, which is a pyrethroid, one of the classes of synthetic pesticides with neurotoxic effects (Soderlund and Bloomquist 1989, Casida and Durkin 2013, Barrios-Arpi et al. 2022). Pyrethroids were introduced in the 1970s and are currently available worldwide, both in liquid and heated delivery methods, effective against a range of insect pets, including mosquitos (Lee 2007, Achee et al. 2012, Revay et al. 2013). Prallethrin in particular has been shown to be effective against mosquitoes in laboratory (Adanan et al. 2005, Abdel-Mohdy et al. 2008, Chen et al. 2018) and semi-field (Katsuda et al. 2009) settings. Although the exact molecular basis for pyrethrum repellency in mosquitos and honey bees is unknown, it may involve the activation of odorant receptors, which was demonstrated in fruit flies (Wang et al. 2021). It would be interesting to determine how repellents interact with the complex odorant environment of both beneficial and pest insects like mosquitos.

Previous studies have demonstrated that honey bees do in fact experience lethal and sublethal effects of insecticides in general and pyrethroids in particular (Cresswell 2011, Henry et al. 2012, Schneider et al. 2012, Zhu et al. 2014, Ingram et al. 2015, Johnson 2015, Alkassab and Kirchner, 2017,Ohlinger et al. 2022a). Why, then, did we see no effect of treatment on our honey bees? It may be that behaviors we monitored (foraging frequency, waggle dance propensity, waggle dance frequency, and feeder persistency) are not affected by treatment; however, we know from previous studies that this paradigm is ideal for testing the effects of treatments (Couvillon et al. 2015, Ohlinger et al. 2022a) because the behaviors are so sensitive to reward. We also considered if the effects might be so subtle as we could not measure them, although that would lead one to question if the effect has biological relevancy. One possible explanation could be that the time-course for the effects might be longer than our experimental period, even though the treatments are very quickly efficacious against other insects, like mosquitos (Bibbs and Kaufman 2017, Chen et al. 2018). Finally, it may be that the physiological differences between mosquitos and honey bees render mosquitos more susceptible to the treatment insecticide compared to honey bees. Some previous work suggests this may be the case: in a laboratory assay, all tested pyrethroid active ingredients were found to be more toxic to the mosquito Aedes albopictus than to the honey bee, where honey bees were found to be 3.04x more tolerant to prallethrin compared to Ae albopictus (Sanchez-Arroyo 2019, 2021).

We placed the repellant station at 2.28 m, or 7.48 ft, which is right on the edge of the 15 ft zone of protection specified for this repelling device (Thermacell Repellents Inc.). However, although on the edge, that distance is still sufficient to repel mosquitos and thus should still represent an exposure to the honey bees. We chose that distance to reflect common consumer practices, where a repeller might be set up on a patio near to a garden.

The analysis we prespecified in our protocol for our primary outcome, written before we performed the experiment, was a Poisson mixed-model. In our data analysis here, it appears that we reached an upper limit of visitations during the experimental phase, which was confined to four hours, and the data therefore do not appear to be Poisson distributed. One likely explanation is that our feeders were too rewarding for that particular summer, which was exceptionally hot and dry, even though the molarities we used had been appropriate in previous summers. Such weather dries out natural nectar resources, exacerbating the foraging dearth. Future studies might consider using a lower molarity of sucrose solution during the experimental phase, or to titrate the concentrations based on training visits. However, despite this limitation, a non-parametric sensitivity analysis suggests that our inference of a noneffect of treatment is valid.

We performed our experiment from 16:00 to 20:00, which reflects a common time when a consumer would reasonably run a mosquito repeller. We initially expected that there would be very low foraging traffic across that interval, and although we did find that the number of visits per bee per hour (approx. 3.75, Fig. 3) was about half to what was previously reported in experiments that ran in the early afternoon (Couvillon et al. 2015, Ohlinger et al. 2022a), foraging frequency was still high enough that we reached the upper limit specified in our models. We even experienced waves of recruitment around 16:30. Foraging continued regularly until about 19:45, at which point it abruptly tapered off. However, there is no doubt that foraging is less in the early evening, and this plus the foraging cessation after 19:45 would result in total lower bee exposure.

In summary, despite a deposition study to confirm that devices and vaporization of the treatment were working properly, we found no significant effect of the volatilized pyrethroid on any of the behaviors that we measured (foraging frequency, waggle dance propensity, waggle dance frequency, and feeder persistency). These data suggest that this type of insecticide delivered by this method may be a safe option for use in areas where honey bees are present, such as residential patios. Our study also opens up other interesting questions. For example, would the results be different if another active compound is used? What would be the effects of this device and compound on non-Apis bees? Lastly, are there any lasting effects of prallethrin exposure, for example on the developing larvae? Such investigations would help in our understanding of the risk to pollinating insects foraging in environments that must also utilize chemical methods of pest control.
