Building Bridges: A Story of How Institutional Investment in Discipline‐Based Education Research Enables Evidence‐Based Teaching in Biochemistry Education
Authors: Megna Tiwari, Erin L Dolan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTScientific discoveries often advance more rapidly than they are integrated into undergraduate instruction, leaving emerging fields like glycoscience largely absent from classroom curricula. At the University of Georgia, institutional investment in discipline‐based education research (DBER) faculty has set the stage for collaboration between disciplinary scientists and DBER researchers to co‐develop evidence‐based teaching materials that align with how students learn. The NSF‐funded BioFoundry: Glycoscience Research, Education, and Training (BioF:GREAT) serves as one example of this approach. Through curricular materials and public‐facing resources, the education arm of BioF:GREAT aims to broaden access to glycoscience education. This brief communication highlights how initiatives like BioF:GREAT leverage collaboration with DBER scholars to integrate emerging scientific fields into biochemistry education in ways that are both scientifically current and pedagogically effective.Keywords: biochemistry, biochemistry education, biology education research, curriculum development, Discipline‐Based Education Research (DBER), education, glycobiology, glycoscience, life Science, teaching and learning, undergraduate

Brief Communication

Science education is increasingly embracing evidence‐based teaching, but implementation is often fragmented and especially lags in emerging fields like glycoscience. One contributor to this disconnect is the fact that science knowledge advances faster than science curricula [1, 2]. Instructors may not yet have the expertise or materials needed to teach using evidence‐based strategies in emerging fields. As a result, students may not encounter these fields until long after the fields have become central to research and practice (e.g., medicine, industry). At the University of Georgia (UGA), strategic hiring of tenure‐track discipline‐based education research (DBER) faculty has created an ecosystem for collaboration in undergraduate science education. By embedding education researchers within various departments, UGA has created opportunities for disciplinary scientists and DBER faculty to co‐develop evidence‐based teaching materials that keep up with scientific advances.

The NSF funded BioFoundry: Glycoscience, Research, Education and Training (BioF:GREAT) at UGA serves as an example of how this collaborative approach can be implemented in practice. BioF:GREAT is designed to democratize glycoscience through four interconnected efforts: advancing glycoscience research and innovation across the tree of life, developing AI and machine learning‐enabled tools and technologies to accelerate discovery in glycoscience, offering glycoscience training opportunities for practicing scientists in other fields, and building capacity for students and the public to learn about the field (Figure 1). All four efforts are integrated and inform one another, with the research and innovation being guided by a team of three faculty and the AI/ML, training, and education components each being guided by a single faculty member. Together, these efforts aim to expand who can participate in glycoscience and how they engage with it. Education is a substantial component of BioF:GREAT as discoveries alone cannot advance glycoscience as a field if students never have opportunities to learn about the field and its significance. In creating educational opportunities alongside research and training, BioF:GREAT aims to build awareness of and interest in glycoscience among the next generation of life scientists.

BioF:GREAT seeks to democratize glycoscience through four interconnected efforts: Research and innovation, AI and machine learning‐enabled tool development, training, and education. Together, these efforts broaden participation in glycoscience and create multiple pathways for engagement with the field.

Within BioF:GREAT, the education effort integrates educational research with educational practice. Research on teaching and learning informs the design of instructional materials, which are then implemented, evaluated, and disseminated to students, instructors, and the broader public. Through this collaboration, the biology education research team is developing a suite of curricular materials that instructors can use to introduce their students to glycoscience. These materials are designed to support learning at multiple instructional levels from introductory biology through advanced graduate education. Introductory modules engage students in learning foundational knowledge and skills in glycoscience. Whereas instructional case studies engage upper‐division students in learning about the importance and relevance of glycosylation via real‐world topics such as blood doping, women's health, cancer immunotherapy, and host–pathogen interactions. Across these materials, evidence‐based teaching practices are woven into the curriculum design. Introductory modules are structured to move students from guided to independent thinking using instructional modeling [3]. In this progression, students first observe the instructor demonstrating application of concepts, then practice with guidance, and ultimately work independently through the material. These modules also incorporate retrieval practice [4] and spaced repetition of core ideas [5] to help students build foundational glycoscience literacy. Instructional case studies support students in developing glycoscience knowledge while strengthening their abilities in analyzing and interpreting glycoscience data through scaffolded engagement [6]. For example, students may begin by interpreting symptoms or clinical observations, then evaluate protein structure, and finally analyze various biochemical data to explain how glycosylation alters biological function [7]. All of the curricular materials being created by BioF:GREAT are designed to include active‐learning strategies that promote collaboration, reflection, and revision [8]. These include Claim–Evidence–Reasoning prompts [9], Process‐Oriented Guided Inquiry Learning‐style group work [10], and formative assessments like think‐pair‐share [11]. Beyond the classroom, public‐facing resources such as “Sweet Spot Videos” and “Scientist Spotlights” introduce viewers to glycoscience by explaining glycoscience concepts in everyday language and sharing the stories of various people doing glycoscience research [12]. Together, these materials create multiple entry points into the field.

BioF:GREAT illustrates how emerging disciplines can be integrated into classroom instruction in a way that is both timely and pedagogically effective. The potential of this approach is driven by several structural and pedagogical components. First, DBER faculty bring expertise in how students learn, how to design curriculum and instruction to align with how people learn, and how to evaluate whether new materials are effective. When hired in tenure‐track roles with research labs, they provide both legitimacy and continuity, ensuring that evidence‐based teaching is not treated as an add‐on, but as a sustained component of departmental culture [13]. Second, this structure enables genuine collaborative co‐design, where disciplinary scientists and education researchers work together to develop curricular materials, rather than operating in parallel or in isolation [14]. Disciplinary scientists contribute expertise in the emerging science discipline, while the education researchers lead the curriculum design. Drawing on research in teaching and learning, education researchers can guide the establishment of learning goals, lead the development of curriculum and assessments, and work with disciplinary scientists to ensure scientific accuracy. Together, they iteratively test and refine materials so that they are effective for learners. Curricular materials developed through initiatives like BioF:GREAT are designed for adaptation and dissemination, extending their impact beyond a single institution and supporting broader adoption of evidence‐based practices. This broader reach is possible because education researchers are more likely than disciplinary scientists to be connected with networks of instructors interested in adopting and implementing new materials.

In this context, the hiring of DBER scholars should be viewed not as a supplemental investment, but as essential infrastructure for advancing science education. Developing approaches like BioF:GREAT will require institutional commitment to hiring and supporting DBER research faculty, as well as fostering meaningful partnerships with disciplinary scientists. As the sciences continue to evolve, so too must the ways in which they are taught. Bridging what we know with how we effectively teach it is essential for preparing the next generation of the STEM workforce and STEM‐literate citizens.
