Septic embolus resulting in M1 occlusion with separate, multiple distal mycotic aneurysms, complicated by surrounding concurrent cerebral abscess: illustrative case
Authors: Nicholas K. Dietz, Jasmine Omair, Andrea Becerril-Gaitan, Joel M. Sequeiros, Redi Rahmani, Dale Ding, Isaac J. Abecassis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDIntracranial mycotic aneurysms are rare, representing only 0.7%–5.4% of all intracranial aneurysms, and typically arise from septic emboli secondary to infective endocarditis. Large-vessel occlusion due to a septic embolus is exceedingly uncommon and carries high morbidity.OBSERVATIONSA 29-year-old man with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus endocarditis and intravenous drug use presented with intracerebral and subarachnoid hemorrhage. CT angiography revealed a left parieto-occipital hematoma with midline shift. An emergency craniotomy for hematoma evacuation exposed a ruptured distal middle cerebral artery (MCA) mycotic aneurysm, which was clipped, and pathological analysis confirmed abscess formation within the aneurysm wall. Despite antibiotic therapy, a new distal MCA aneurysm developed within an abscess cavity. Cerebral angiography later demonstrated M1 occlusion requiring mechanical thrombectomy, achieving partial reperfusion consistent with Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction grade 2a. The persistent abscess and aneurysm required Onyx embolization and abscess drainage in a hybrid operating room.LESSONSSequential mycotic aneurysms with concurrent large-vessel occlusion and abscess formation represent a rare and aggressive manifestation of septic emboli. Successful management depends on coordinated microsurgical clipping, endovascular embolization, thrombectomy, and abscess evacuation. Mycotic aneurysms are rare cerebrovascular lesions caused by infection of a typically distal segment arterial wall, accounting for approximately 0.7%–5.4% of all intracranial aneurysms.1 Circulation of infective emboli (bacterial or fungal) into small distal cerebral arteries triggers intense inflammation in the media and adventitia, leading to vessel wall destruction and subsequent aneurysm formation.2 Reported rupture rates of mycotic aneurysms vary, reaching up to approximately 60%.3 Mortality is 80% after rupture, 30% in those that remain unruptured, with no reliable predictors of rupture identified to date.4,5

Rarely, infective emboli may cause large-vessel occlusion requiring mechanical thrombectomy.6 While thrombectomy is feasible, outcomes are generally less favorable than noninfective thromboembolic infarction, with lower rates of successful recanalization, symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) in 15% of cases, and mortality approaching 34%.7 In addition, septic emboli may not only trigger aneurysm formation but also evolve into overt parenchymal infection, with brain abscess reported in 1%–7% of infective endocarditis patients and higher prevalence observed in imaging-based series.8

Infectious aneurysms are often insidious and clinically silent, first diagnosed in 5%–10% of autopsies.2 While angiography remains the gold standard for diagnosis, CT angiography (CTA) is often used as a first-line imaging modality. Nonetheless, small mycotic aneurysms may be difficulty to visualize on CTA, thereby requiring conventional diagnostic angiography to confirm their presence. Microbiological confirmation may be obtained through positive blood cultures or the infected aneurysm wall itself.9

We present a complex case of a ruptured mycotic aneurysm treated with craniotomy and clipping, and subsequent septic embolus resulting in occlusion and ischemic stroke in the M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery (MCA) that underwent thrombectomy, and eventual embolization of a more distal mycotic aneurysm, which had a concurrent cerebral abscess requiring surgical evacuation.

Illustrative Case

A 29-year-old male with a history of intravenous polysubstance use, cigarette smoking, syphilis, and prior infective endocarditis was found unresponsive, with last known normal 20 hours prior to arrival to an outside hospital. Per the outside hospital’s records, his Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 14 and initial evaluation revealed leukocytosis (22 × 103/μL), hyponatremia (125 mmol/L), and markedly elevated troponin I (1442 ng/L), consistent with a type 2 non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction. Urine toxicology was positive for amphetamines. Noncontrast CT of the head demonstrated an acute subarachnoid hemorrhage with a small ICH. Empiric antibiotic therapy with vancomycin and cefepime was initiated for suspected infective endocarditis, and the patient was transferred to our institution for further management.

On arrival, repeat CT of the head demonstrated a left parietal ICH measuring 4.4 × 3.5 cm (ICH score of 1), brain compression, and convexity subarachnoid hemorrhage. Chest radiography showed multiple cavitary lung lesions. The patient’s GCS score on initial assessment was 12 (E3V4M5). Baseline functional assessments included an FUNC score of 11, Barthel index of 100, and modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 2. Subsequent CTA revealed a larger hematoma (4.8 × 3.2 × 3.9 cm) with a 9-mm midline shift, early left uncal herniation, and evidence of a mycotic aneurysm versus active extravasation (Fig. 1A–C). Postadmission, the patient became aphasic, minimally responsive, localizing briskly on the right and withdrawing to pain on the left. Because of the patient’s progressive neurological decline, he was intubated and underwent emergency left parieto-occipital craniotomy with minimally invasive hematoma evacuation using a stereotactic tubular retractor system (21 mm × 7 cm) and clipping of the ruptured mycotic aneurysm parent vessel (Fig. 1D–F). Samples of the aneurysm and surrounding abnormal parenchyma were sent for pathological analysis, with the final report showing brain tissue with mild gliosis and negative Congo red as well as fibrovascular tissue with abscess formation in the aneurysm specimen. Blood cultures were positive for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), for which the patient continued antibiotic therapy with cefepime and vancomycin, as well as penicillin G for concomitant syphilis infection. Postoperative transesophageal echocardiography identified large mobile vegetations involving the mitral and tricuspid valves.

Postclipping angiography demonstrated a separate left distal MCA mycotic aneurysm. Serial imaging showed progressive aneurysmal enlargement and development of abscess surrounding the aneurysm (Fig. 2). Endovascular embolization of the new aneurysm was attempted; however, the patient developed an acute left M1 occlusion that was appreciated before catheterization of left-sided vessels. This was apparent via robust posterior cerebral artery (PCA)–MCA pial-pial collaterals on the first angiogram and was thus presumed to be likely secondary to a septic embolus from the endocarditis during induction (Fig. 3A), requiring mechanical thrombectomy (Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction [TICI] grade 2a after five passes) and intra-arterial administration of verapamil to the left internal carotid artery (Fig. 3B). A postthrombectomy brain MRI demonstrated acute to early subacute infarcts involving left basal ganglia and frontal and temporal lobes (Fig. 3C). The plan was to observe the patient’s neurological examination and perform a repeat diagnostic angiogram with possible embolization of the unruptured aneurysm in 2 weeks after the infarction if there was any progression of the aneurysm. After the stroke, the patient’s neurological examination worsened with a GCS score of 8 (E3VTM5 on the left and M4 on the right side). The abscess and mycotic aneurysm were persistent and, unfortunately, the abscess grew despite broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy; therefore, drainage of the abscess was pursued 2 weeks after the mechanical thrombectomy. Given the presence of a mycotic aneurysm, we felt that endovascular embolization was critical to the safety of stereotactic abscess drainage. The patient was treated in a hybrid operating room and underwent preoperative embolization using Onyx of the left MCA mycotic aneurysm present within the abscess to reduce risk of intraoperative bleeding. This was immediately followed by a Medtronic Stealth navigation–guided left parietal craniotomy for drainage of the supratentorial abscess in a hybrid operating room suite (Fig. 4). Pathological examination of abscess contents revealed fibrin and inflammatory exudate consistent with an infected mycotic aneurysm. Stains for acid-fast bacilli and fungi were negative, as well as immunohistochemical staining for treponemal organisms. The patient was discharged to subacute rehabilitation on hospital day 56, with a calculated mRS score of 4 and GCS score of 10. He was briefly readmitted 1 month later after falling out of his bed at the facility, with a slightly improved examination (GCS score 12). Discussion

Observations

This case illustrates a rare and aggressive course of a ruptured intracranial mycotic aneurysm and a separate, unruptured distal mycotic aneurysm with associated cerebral abscess, complicated by large-vessel occlusion. Management included emergency craniotomy with clipping of the ruptured aneurysm, mechanical thrombectomy for large-vessel occlusion, targeted antibiotics, and later liquid embolization of a second, unruptured aneurysm in a hybrid operating room prior to surgical abscess drainage.

In patients presenting with ICH in the setting of infective endocarditis, early and structured vascular imaging and echocardiography are critical as septic emboli, ischemic infarcts, infectious intracranial aneurysms, and hemorrhage may coexist. Recent evidence shows that up to 60% of endocarditis patients harbor neurological complications detectable only through systematic CTA/MRI screening rather than clinical examination alone.10 Baseline CTA provides effective first-line evaluation for ischemic emboli and small mycotic aneurysms, which may appear subtle but still pose risk for rapid enlargement or rupture.11 Our patient’s evolving vascular pathology, including sequential aneurysm development, abscess formation, and an acute M1 occlusion, closely mirrors this structured imaging approach and illustrates why serial CTA, digital subtraction angiography, and MRI were necessary to capture progression of septic emboli.

Up to 70% of endocarditis-related mycotic aneurysms are located in the MCA or its distal branches, reflecting the pattern of septic emboli lodging in M4 cortical vessels.12 Proximal vessel involvement, such as M1 occlusion, is exceedingly uncommon, but septic embolic occlusion in this territory has been associated with rapid aneurysm formation and catastrophic rupture.13 The present case is unusual in that two separate distal MCA aneurysms developed in sequence, with the first presenting with rupture and the second enlarging within a progressively enlarging cerebral abscess, despite prolonged antibiotic therapy. While data directly quantifying cerebral abscess in mycotic aneurysms remain scarce, one radiological series found that up to 77% of such cases exhibit some form of central nervous system infection, such as cerebral abscess, meningitis, encephalitis, or ventriculitis.14 This highlights the potential for aggressive, multifocal disease progression.

Risk Factors for Bacterial Mycotic Aneurysm

Septic emboli typically originate from infective endocarditis, lung abscess, oral abscess, or local soft tissue infections, all of which can seed bacteria into the bloodstream.15,16 These infections serve as sources for embolic fragments that dislodge and seed distal cerebral vessels, particularly involving virulent pathogens.7 The patient’s history of intravenous drug use, MRSA bacteremia, and septic endocarditis encompassed several well-established risk factors for mycotic aneurysm formation. Bacterial infective endocarditis accounts for approximately 65% of cases, followed by intravenous drug use (6.3%), bacterial meningitis (5.2%), poor dental hygiene (4.2%), and cavernous sinus thrombosis (2.8%).15 Pathogenesis may involve bacteremia, direct vessel wall injury, local extension of infection, or embolization of infected material.16 Although mycotic aneurysms can be caused by fungal or viral organisms, the vast majority result from bacterial infection—most commonly S. aureus and Streptococcus species.15 Less frequently reported pathogens include gram-negative bacteria such as Salmonella and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as well as fungi (Aspergillus and Candida species), Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Cardiobacterium hominis.12,17

While both mycotic aneurysms and cerebral abscesses may arise from septic emboli, their pathophysiological mechanisms diverge at the site of bacterial penetration. In mycotic aneurysms, infected emboli lodge within distal cerebral arteries, triggering transmural infection and destruction of the arterial wall, typically involving the media and adventitia, leading to aneurysm formation and risk of rupture.1,2,15 In contrast, cerebral abscesses result from septic emboli that penetrate the blood-brain barrier or from local spread of infection, leading to cerebritis, liquefactive necrosis, and encapsulated pus formation within brain parenchyma.8,16 Both entities may coexist or evolve sequentially, as seen in our case, highlighting the aggressive and multifocal nature of infective endocarditis–related neurological complications.

Lessons

Treatment strategies of mycotic aneurysm vary by case and include medical management, surgery, and endovascular therapy.16,18 Presentation dictates appropriate treatment as unruptured mycotic aneurysms may be asymptomatic and resolve with antibiotics alone, while ruptured aneurysms require embolization or open surgery involving clipping, trapping, anastomosis, and proximal ligation.16,19 Additional considerations when treating mycotic aneurysms include the presence of active infection with an associated higher propensity for aneurysmal rupture.16 While peripheral location of the distal vessels may be more accessible, mycotic aneurysm clipping may be technically challenging due to friable walls or aneurysm neck deformity. Additionally, CTA navigation and MRI-guided protocols may assist in these cases. Endovascular management is a reasonable treatment strategy in patients with high surgical risk, additional comorbidities, or surgically inaccessible/multiple aneurysms.20 Liquid embolic agents, such an Onyx, are advantageous for distal aneurysms arising from the PCA or MCA, as shown in the present case. Onyx generally allows for more precise control and a longer precipitation time than other liquid embolic agents, achieving controlled embolization.20 Similar cases of intracranial mycotic aneurysms most frequently describe single lesions, often located in distal MCA branches, associated with infective endocarditis (Table 1).21–24 However, multifocal aneurysms, particularly with sequential presentation and persistent progression, are rare. The present case expands on the current literature, demonstrating aggressive disease dynamics with rupture, abscess formation, and continued aneurysm growth despite appropriate antibiotic therapy. This contrasts with most previously published cases, in which a single distal aneurysm was identified and successfully managed either surgically, endovascularly, or with medical therapy alone. Sequelae of mycotic aneurysms, including rupture, abscess, and large-vessel occlusion, are associated with significant morbidity and mortality. We present a case encompassing all three associations in a single patient who was managed effectively with 1) surgical decompression, 2) aneurysmal clipping, 3) mechanical thrombectomy, and 4) abscess evacuation. Neurosurgeons should be aware of the nuanced pathophysiology of this disease entity and the complications associated with mycotic aneurysm development as well as combined treatment options.
