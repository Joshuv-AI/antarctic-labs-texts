Epidemiological and Clinical Microbiological Characteristics of Staphylococcus argenteus Isolated from a Tertiary Care Hospital in Korea in 2021–2024
Authors: Jihoon Yoo, Kwangjin Ahn, Young Uh
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Staphylococcus aureus causes a broad spectrum of infections, including skin and soft tissue infections, bacteremia, pneumonia, and infective endocarditis [1]. Advances in molecular identification have allowed the reclassification of some isolates previously identified as S. aureus into the S. aureus complex, which includes S. aureus subsp. anaerobius, S. argenteus, and S. schweitzeri [2]. Among these, S. argenteus lacks the yellow pigment (staphyloxanthin) and typically forms silver-white colonies [3].

Number of reports describing S. argenteus isolates is increasing, particularly in Asia and Oceania [4]. However, as S. argenteus yields positive results in nuclease A gene–based PCR assays used to identify S. aureus, conventional microbiological methods cannot distinguish between both species. Misidentification of S. argenteus as S. aureus is common without whole-genome sequencing or colony morphology analysis [5]. S. argenteus and S. aureus cause similar clinical syndromes, including food poisoning, skin and soft tissue infections, respiratory tract infections, and infective endocarditis, and delayed or inappropriate empirical therapy may result in severe outcomes [6]. However, in Korea, data on the isolation frequency and clinical significance of S. argenteus remain limited [7].

In Wonju Severance Christian Hospital, Wonju, Korea, S. argenteus was isolated from 3 of 67,504 patients who underwent culture testing between January 1, 2021 and December 31, 2024. We retrospectively analyzed all three isolates to evaluate their epidemiological and clinical microbiological characteristics. The institutional review board of Wonju Severance Christian Hospital approved the study (approval number CR325346; December 2, 2025) and waived the informed consent requirement.

Specimens were processed using standard microbiological procedures and identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; Bruker Biotyper instrument; Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co., Bremen, Germany). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed using the Phoenix M50 system (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Milford, MA, USA). Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were interpreted according to the CLSI criteria [8].

The first patient was a 24-yr-old woman with anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor autoimmune encephalitis who developed catheter-related bacteremia caused by S. argenteus during immunosuppressive treatment. The second patient was a 76-yr-old woman with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease who presented with acute respiratory failure during an acute exacerbation of IPF. S. argenteus was isolated from her sputum. The third patient was a 51-yr-old man with advanced gastric cancer and peritoneal seeding who developed catheter-related bacterial peritonitis, with S. argenteus isolated from the ascitic fluid and catheter cultures (Table 1).

The AST profiles of the S. argenteus isolates are summarized in Table 2. All isolates were susceptible to oxacillin (MIC, <0.25 μg/mL), vancomycin (MIC, 1 μg/mL), and linezolid (MIC, 2 μg/mL) but resistant to penicillin. The isolate from the third patient exhibited resistance to gentamicin (MIC, 16 μg/mL), erythromycin (MIC, 8 μg/mL), clindamycin (MIC, 8 μg/mL), and trimethoprim (MIC, 16 μg/mL) and intermediate resistance to doxycycline (MIC, 8 μg/mL). All patients improved after treatment, and S. argenteus was no longer detected in follow-up cultures.

All patients with S. argenteus infection were associated with advanced age, immunosuppression, or had undergone an invasive medical procedure. S. argenteus is more commonly associated with nosocomial or polymicrobial infections, with various isolation sites and clinical manifestations, suggesting that host factors largely determine its clinical significance. The antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of S. argenteus and S. aureus were similar, although some differences were observed in the pattern of S. argenteus isolated from the third patient [9]. Empirical treatment should follow those for S. aureus (β-lactam/β-lacta­mase inhibitors, first-generation cephalosporins, or vancomycin), with adjustments based on AST results.

S. argenteus is not highly virulent or multidrug resistant but can cause severe infections in immunocompromised patients [10]. Because clinical characteristics alone cannot distinguish S. argenteus from S. aureus, accurate laboratory identification remains essential. The increasing frequency of isolation and potential emergence of distinct resistance patterns highlight the need for species-specific diagnostic and therapeutic guidelines.

This study has certain limitations. This retrospective study did not involve confirmatory methods (such as whole-genome sequencing) beyond Gram staining and MALDI-TOF MS; thus, some isolates identified as S. aureus may have been S. argenteus and vice versa. For the second patient, the quality of the sputum specimen might have been inadequate [10–25 white blood cells/low-power field (LPF) and >25 epithelial cells/LPF]. However, elevated C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate suggest the presence of infection. Notably, no organisms were isolated from the other specimens. Gram-positive cocci were observed on microscopic examination; culture testing revealed S. argenteus as the predominant organism. Further evidence is required to establish a clear association between S. argenteus and acute IPF exacerbation.
