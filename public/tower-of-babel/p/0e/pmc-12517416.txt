Exploring Fungal Diversity in Marine Plastic (PET) Wastes and Seafoam in Udo Island, South Korea, with Reports of Two New Species (Leptospora conidiifera and Neodevriesia oceanoplastica)
Authors: Wonjun Lee, Sumin Jo, Soo Hyun Maeng, Ji Seon Kim, Myung Kyum Kim, Young Woon Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarine fungi play a crucial role in maintaining the ocean ecosystem functions by participating in organic matter degradation, carbon and nitrogen biogeochemical cycles, and interactions with other marine organisms. Nevertheless, many marine habitats remain poorly explored for fungal diversity, as fungi have historically been overlooked in marine research. In this study, we investigated marine fungi in two underexplored coastal habitats (PET plastic waste and seafoam) collected from the intertidal zone of Udo Island, South Korea. A total of 88 fungal strains were isolated and identified as 45 taxa (22 taxa from PET waste and 24 from seafoam) based on multigene phylogenetic analysis and morphological characteristics. The distinct fungal communities recovered from PET plastic waste and seafoam highlight the ecological value of anthropogenic and ephemeral marine habitats. Among these, we report two novel species – Leptospora conidiifera sp. nov. and Neodevriesia oceanoplastica sp. nov. – along with five previously unrecorded marine fungi species in Korea. These findings suggest that the two habitats can serve as reservoirs of unique fungal biodiversity and marine fungi may play unrecognized roles in marine nutrient cycling and microbial interactions.Keywords: Marine plastic waste, PET, seafoam, unexplored species, marine fungi

Introduction

Marine microorganisms play a crucial role in maintaining ocean ecosystem functions [1–3]. While extensive research has primarily emphasized phytoplankton, bacteria, and archaea, marine fungi remain relatively underexplored. However, recent studies have begun to highlight the ecological significance of marine fungi, which participate in organic matter degradation, carbon and nitrogen biogeochemical cycles, and interactions with other marine organisms [4–8]. Although fungal diversity and ecological roles vary greatly across marine environments [8,9], many marine habitats remain insufficiently studied, largely because fungi have historically been neglected in marine research [10,11]. Investigating fungal communities in these previously overlooked habitats is essential for advancing our understanding of their functional roles in the ocean.

Plastic pollution is a major environmental concern, with plastics persisting in marine ecosystems for extended periods due to their resistance to degradation [12,13]. This has led to the formation of the plastisphere, a unique microbial ecosystem on plastic surfaces in the ocean [14]. The plastisphere harbors a distinct microbial community, including fungi, whose ecological functions may differ from those of free-living marine microbes [15–17]. Several fungal strains from marine plastic have been reported to possess potential plastic-degrading capabilities [18].

Seafoam is another dynamic marine habitat that remains largely unstudied regarding fungal diversity. Naturally or anthropogenically formed, seafoam occurs in areas with high concentrations of organic and inorganic materials [19–21]. It has been shown to accumulate various substances, including toxins, chemicals, microalgae, and fungal spores, potentially creating ephemeral microbial hotspots [21–25]. Microorganisms within seafoam can utilize these materials as energy and nutrient sources, yet the fungal diversity within these environments remains entirely unexplored.

Since the establishment of the Marine Fungal Resources Bank in South Korea in 2014, we have actively explored marine fungi across various environments to better understand their ecological roles and assess their potential for industrial applications. In this study, we investigated fungi isolated from PET plastic submerged in seawater and from naturally occurring seafoam collected along the shores of Udo Island, South Korea. Initial identification was conducted using the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region, followed by the use of additional genetic markers for accurate identification. Despite the limited number of samples, we recovered diverse fungi from the two habitats, each showing distinct compositions. These habitats provide unique ecological conditions that may harbor specialized fungal assemblages. Among the recovered fungi, we describe two new species and report five species previously unrecorded in South Korea, supported by detailed morphological and phylogenetic analysis.

Materials and methods

Sampling and fungal isolation

PET plastic and seafoam were sampled in an intertidal zone from Udo Island in South Korea in 2024. The PET samples were collected from submerged intertidal zones, where they were positioned between rocks and covered with macroalgae (Figure 1). After being washed three times using sterile seawater (SSW), sampled PETs were cut into 1 × 1 cm to place them in 2 mL cryotube containing 1.5 mL SSW and 2.8 mm ceramic beads (OMNI International, Kennesaw, GA). To isolate firmly attached fungal cells, The PET pieces in the cryotube were homogenized using a vortexer at the maximum speed to dislodge fungi attached to the plastic surface. For each samples, a 150 μL aliquot of homogenized suspension was spread onto four media: potato dextrose agar (PDA; BD Difco, Sparks, MD), marine agar 2216 (MA; BD Difco, Sparks, MD), MA with 1% (w/w) low density polyethylene (LMA), and minimal salt agar (NaNO32.0 g, K2HPO4 0.3 g, KH2PO4 0.7, KCl 0.5 g, MgSO47H2O 0.5 g, FeSO47H2O 0.1 g, and Bacto agar 30 g/L) with 1% (w/w) low density polyethylene (LMS). Of these, PDA and LMS were supplemented with SSW, whereas MA and LMA were supplemented with distilled water (DW) as MA already mimics marine conditions. The remaining PET pieces were also placed directly onto the same media. The culture media used in this study were selected with consideration of the plastic-associated environment.

Samples collected from the intertidal zone of Udo Island. Plastic (PET) wastes are shown in (a). Seafoam sampled from the sea surface is shown in (B).

Seafoam on high tide zone was collected using a 50 mL conical tube while minimizing seawater intake and stored at 4 °C to minimize changes in the fungal community until arrival at the laboratory (Figure 1). Sampling was conducted at a single site, and three replicates were collected. Plating was performed within 3 d of collection. The seafoam samples settled and transitioned into a liquid state. Each 100 μL samples were spread on the media. To isolate a diverse range of fungi from seafoam, the following culture media were employed: MA, Dichloran Rose Bengal Chloramphenicol (DRBC; BD Difco, Sparks, MD) agar, and yeast peptone dextrose agar (YPDA; BD Difco, Sparks, MD). DRBC and YPDA were supplemented with SSW, considering saline environments.

All isolation plates were incubated at room temperature for one to three weeks. Each fungal colony was subcultured on pure PDA supplemented SSW. All the pure isolates were stored in 20% glycerol with SSW at −80 °C in the metabolically inactive state and deposited in the Seoul National University Fungus Collection (SFC) (Table S1).

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing

Hyphal mats of each isolate were cut into approximately 0.5 × 0.5 cm2 pieces and ground using 2.8 mm ceramic beads (OMNI International) through a Bead Ruptor Elite Homogenizer (OMNI International, Kennesaw, GA). Subsequently, genomic DNA was extracted using AccuPrep Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Bioneer Co., Daejeon, Korea). To identify the isolated strains at the genus level, the ITS region was primarily amplified through PCR using an AccuPower PCR premix (Bioneer Co., Korea) on a C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) or a Mastercycler Nexus Gradient (Eppendorf). ITS1F [26] and ITS4 [27] primer sets were used to amplify the ITS region (Table S2). PCR products were validated through gel electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel and purified using ExoSAP-IT Express PCR Product Cleanup (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The purified products were sequenced using the ABI 3730xl System by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea). The generated sequences were manually edited and trimmed on Geneious Prime 2025.1.2 (Biomatters Ltd., San Diego, CA, https://www.geneious.com) [28]. Primary identification of the isolates using ITS was performed by the BLASTn search method at NCBI [29]. After primary identification, other genetic markers required for taxon-specific identification were amplified for accurate species-level identification. The genetic markers utilized in this study include large subunit of the nuclear ribosomal RNA (LSU), actin (ACT), calmodulin (CaM), chitin synthase 1 (CHS-1), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), mini-chromosome maintenance complex component 7 (MCM7), RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (RPB2), translation elongation factor (TEF1), and partial β-tubulin (TUB2), and the appropriate primers and PCR conditions for their amplification are presented in Table S2.

Phylogenetic analysis

Generated sequences were assembled from forward and reverse reads using the “De Novo assemble” function in Geneious Prime 2025.1.2 (Biomatters Ltd., San Diego, CA, https://www.geneious.com) [28]. To identify isolates, reference sequences were retrieved from GenBank using GenMine [30]. Phylogenetic analysis were primarily carried out using the FunVIP pipeline [31], which includes sequence alignment with MAFFT v7.525 [32], trimming with TrimAl version 1.4.rev15 [33], concatenation of genetic markers, model selection with PartitionFinder version 2.3.4 [34], and maximum likelihood tree construction using RAxML version 8.2.12 with 1000 bootstrap replicates [35]. For taxa not processed by FunVIP, including Metarhizium and Neodevriesia, phylogenetic trees were manually conducted in Geneious Prime 2025.1.2 (Biomatters Ltd., San Diego, CA, https://www.geneious.com) [28]. In these cases, sequences were aligned using MAFFT version 7.490 [32], and maximum likelihood trees were generated using RAxML, following model selection based on MEGA12 [36]. Final tree layouts were edited using Affinity Publisher version 2.6.3.

Morphological observation and physiological test

To observe detailed morphological characteristics, new species and unrecorded species were subcultured onto pure PDA media supplemented with DW. Fresh colonies were subsequently transferred to various media, selected based on the characteristics of each fungal genus: PDA, malt extract agar (MEA; Oxoid, Hants, UK), oatmeal agar (OA; BD Difco, Sparks, MD), potato carrot agar (PCA; HiMedia, India), and synthetic nutrient-poor agar (SNA; KH2PO4 1 g, KNO3 1 g, MgSO47H2O 5 g, KCl 0.5 g, glucose 0.2 g, saccharose 0.2 g, and Bacto agar 20 g/L) [37]. To observe the conidial structures of Neodevriesia, the cultures were incubated under 395 nm UV light. Additionally, to simulate marine conditions, PDA medium supplemented with 3.5% NaCl (3.5% NaCl PDA) was prepared and used for all strains. Morphologies on 3.5% NaCl PDA were compared with those on standard PDA to evaluate phenotypic variations.

After incubating the yeast strains on YPDA for 5 d, cell morphology was observed using a LEICA DM500 microscope. To support morphological identification of yeast species, biochemical properties were evaluated with the API 20 C AUX system (bioMérieux) following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Results

A total of 88 fungal strains were isolated from PET wastes (42 strains) and seafoam (46 strains) in Udo Island. They were identified as 45 taxa across 35 genera (Figures 2–3 and S1–S12). Among them, 36 species belonged to Ascomycota, 8 to Basidiomycota, and 1 to Mucoromycota. Of the total, 22 taxa were isolated from PET waste and 24 from seafoam. Parengyodontium torokii is the only species recovered from both habitats (Table 1).

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that three taxa formed distinct clades with 100 bootstrap values (Geotrichum sp.: SFC20250301-M034–037) and separate lineages (Leptospora sp.: SFC20250301-M042; Neodevriesia sp.: SFC20250301-M047), supporting their status as separate species (Figures 2 and S5). Several taxa, including Curvularia muehlenbeckiae/platzii/pisi (Figure S2), Emericellopsis sp. (Figure S4), Neopestalotiopsis sp. (Figure S8), Neoascochyta soli/paspali (Figure S7), Nothophoma spiraeae (Figure S8), Nothophoma sp. (Figure S8), Purpureocillium lilacinum/roseum (Figure S9), and Sphaerulina hydrangeicola/idesiae (Figure S11) could not be definitively identified at the species level due to either inconsistent across different genetic markers, taxonomic complexity or close phylogenetic proximity to related species. The marine strain SFC20250301-M059 showed identical sequences to both Phomatospora biseriata and P. uniseriata (Figure 3). As P. biseriata was described earlier, the strain was identified as P. biseriata [38,39]. Similarly, the marine strain SFC20250301-M070 was identical to both Remotididymella capsici and R. anemophila (Figure S10). Since R. capsici [40] was published prior to R. anemophila [41], the strain was assigned to R. capsici.

Five species (Anthracocystis heteropogonicola, Cladosporium pruni-salicina, Jattaea algeriensis, Parengyodontium torokii, and Phomatospora biseriate) are the first reported fungi in South Korea (Figure 3). Among the five species, the first four were isolated from seafoam, whereas only Phomatospora biseriata was obtained from PET waste. Phomatospora biseriata is monophyletic with P. uniseriata, exhibiting minimal sequence divergence. Similarly, Remotididymella capsici forms a clade with R. anemophila, with their sequences being nearly indistinguishable.

On MEA. Mycelia septate, branched, smooth. Conidiophores solitary, septate, swollen, bearing 1–2 conidia. Newly developed conidia hyaline, smooth. Conidiogenous cells, 0–1-septate, smooth, 5.5–9 × 3–7 μm (mean ± SD = 7.8 ± 1.3 × 4.8 ± 1.3 μm). Conidia chained or solitary, muriformly septate, 0–2 longitudinal septa, 1–3 transverse septa, smooth, 12.5–24.5 × 9–19 μm (mean ± SD = 16.8 ± 3.0 × 13.4 ± 2.1 μm). Chlamydospores observed.

Notes: Most species in the genus Leptospora have been described based on their sexual morphs [39,42–45]. In contrast, Leptospora conidiifera is characterized by a distinct asexual morph not previously reported in this genus, making direct morphological comparisons with other species difficult. However, its growth rate provides a useful distinguishing feature. L. conidiifera grew rapidly, reaching 55–72 mm on PDA and 47–54 mm on MEA within two weeks. In comparison, L. hydei and L. rubella reached only about 35 and 40 mm on PDA after four weeks [42,43], while L. macarangae reached 30–40 mm in three weeks [45]. On MEA, L. clematidis reached only 30 mm after 4 weeks [39]. These results suggest that L. conidiifera grows significantly faster than other known species in the genus.

Discussion

Fungi inhabit a wide range of marine environments [9], but many ecological niches, even in coastal regions, remain poorly characterized. In this study, we investigated two previously underexplored coastal microhabitats – plastic waste (PET) and seafoam – collected from Udo Island, South Korea. From a limited number of samples, we isolated 88 fungal strains representing 45 species, including two novel species and five previously unrecorded species in Korea. This high taxonomic diversity underscores the value of even anthropogenic or ephemeral habitats as distinct reservoirs of marine fungi.

Phylogenetic analyses revealed that the isolates spanned multiple phyla – Ascomycota, Basidiomycota, and Mucoromycota—with many strains falling into well-supported clades. The use of genus-specific markers alongside ITS enabled species-level resolution in most cases. Notably, several isolates occupied distinct phylogenetic positions, with Leptospora conidiifera sp. nov. and Neodevriesia oceanoplastica sp. nov. proposed as new species based on combined morphological and molecular data. The asexual morph of Leptospora conidiifera represents the first such report within this genus. On the other hand, Neodevriesia oceanoplastica sp. nov. was unusual in that it did not produce conidial structures under any tested conditions, including dark, normal light, and near-UV light conditions. A similar lack of conidial production has also been reported in Neodevriesia grateloupiae [51], suggesting that certain species in the genus may have restricted sporulation requirements or may not readily form conidia under laboratory conditions.

Additionally, unresolved phylogenetic boundaries, as observed in Phomatospora and Remotididymella, reflect broader taxonomic challenges in fungal taxonomy, including near-simultaneous species descriptions and potential ecological oversplitting. Phomatospora biseriata and P. uniseriata exhibit no discernible genetic differences and are morphologically indistinguishable [38,39]. Therefore, based on the principle of nomenclatural priority, P. uniseriata should be regarded as a later synonym of P. biseriata, which was published earlier [38,39]. Similarly, Remotididymella capsici and R. anemophila form a strongly supported clade with high sequence similarity, providing insufficient evidence to justify their separation at the species level. Because R. capsici [40] was published earlier than R. anemophila [41], R. anemophila should be synonymized under R. capsici. Based on this reasoning, the marine isolates SFC20250301-M059 and SFC20250301-M070 were identified as P. biseriata and R. capsici, respectively. These cases highlight the need for future taxonomic revision based on holotype comparisons and integrative approaches. Lastly, we provide additional morphological descriptions of five fungal species – Anthracocystis heteropogonicola, Cladosporium pruni-salicina, Jattaea algeriensis, Parengyodontium torokii, and Phomatospora biseriate – that are reported for the first time in South Korea.

The recovery of distinct fungal communities from PET and seafoam suggests that each habitat presents unique ecological conditions that may shape fungal assemblages in different ways. Several fungi isolated from plastic waste were previously reported in plastic- or algae-associated environments and are known for their degradative capabilities. For instance, Cladosporium spp. have been shown to degrade polyethylene and other polymers [52,53]. Rhodotorula, particularly R. mucilaginosa, has demonstrated the ability to metabolize plastic-derived compounds [54,55]. Other genera, such as Fusarium, are also known to degrade plastics [56]. In many cases, PET samples were entangled with macroalgae, which likely facilitated colonization by algae-associated fungi, including Cladosporium [6,57,58], Emericellopsis [59], and Neoascochyta [60,61]. Algae are primary producers in marine systems and frequently interact with fungi in both symbiotic and degradative contexts [6,62]. Fungi isolated from macroalgae are known to produce enzymes capable of breaking down complex algal polymers [62–64], and it is plausible that some of these fungi have opportunistically expanded their niches to colonize plastic substrates, particularly under resource-limited conditions. This potential ecological shift suggests a form of niche plasticity, in which marine fungi adapt from degrading natural substrates like macroalgae to more persistent anthropogenic materials like plastic. We did not include environmental controls from the surrounding habitats, and therefore further evaluation of the plastic-degrading abilities of the isolated fungi will be necessary to clarify whether they truly reside on and degrade plastic.

Seafoam, although ephemeral and physically unstable, yielded fungal diversity comparable to that of PET samples. Several taxa were unique to seafoam, suggesting it harbors a distinct fungal community possibly shaped by dynamic environmental changes. According to the Korean Meteorological Administration, the average sea surface temperature around Korea, including Jeju Island (a large island located adjacent to Udo Island, our sampling site), was 1.1 °C higher than the recent 10-year mean in the summer of 2024 [65]. The unusually high temperatures of the sea surface during the summer of 2024 may have enhanced phytoplankton blooms and exudate release, facilitating seafoam formation and enriching it with organic matter [66]. Such conditions likely support microbial colonization, as seen in previous studies on seafoam-associated bacteria [25]. In our study, several fungal genera – including Aspergillus, Cladosporium, Purpureocillium, and Rhodotorula – were dominantly isolated from seafoam. While these genera are commonly reported from diverse marine environments [58,67–69], the mechanisms underlying their dominance in seafoam remain unclear. In addition, although the samples were stored at 4 °C for 3 d after collection to minimize potential changes in the fungal community, we cannot exclude the possibility that shifts in community dominance occurred during storage. Given the limited understanding of fungal ecology in this niche, further studies employing both culture-based and culture-independent approaches (e.g., amplicon sequencing, metagenomics) are needed to uncover the diversity and ecological functions of seafoam-associated fungi.

The fungal genera Cladosporium, Purpureocillium, and Rhodotorula were isolated from both types of environmental samples in this study. Cladosporium was recently shown to enhance the thermal tolerance of corals, such as Stylophora pistillata, under elevated seawater temperatures, and its inoculation led to a more stable bacterial community within the coral holobiont [70]. Although the ecological roles of Cladosporium in marine ecosystems remain largely unclear, such findings suggest a potential buffering function that may help host organisms adapt to environmental stress, a hypothesis further supported by its frequent isolation from a variety of other marine organisms [58,71–73]. Purpureocillium is known to tolerate high hydrostatic pressure in deep-sea environments and exhibits nematicidal activity [74]. This may point to potential trophic interactions with marine nematodes, which are abundant in coastal ecosystems [75]. Rhodotorula has also been shown to adapt to marine environments at both genomic and metabolic levels [76,77]. Although these ecological roles remain putative and require functional validation, these observations suggest that marine fungi are not merely passive inhabitants accumulating in the marine environment, but rather, actively adapt and perform diverse ecological functions. Further studies integrating genomic, metabolomic, and environmental approaches – especially those addressing interactions with other marine organisms – may reveal critical insights into how marine fungi contribute to ecosystem resilience in the face of environmental change.

In conclusion, this study provides new insights into the diversity of marine fungi by investigating two previously unexplored coastal habitats – plastic waste and seafoam – on Udo Island, South Korea. Despite the limited number of samples, distinct fungal communities were recovered, including novel and previously unrecorded species in South Korea. The markedly different community compositions observed between the two habitats suggest that marine fungi may play habitat-specific ecological roles. These findings emphasize the importance of continued exploration of such niche environments to uncover overlooked components of marine fungal ecology.
