Associations between wildland fire smoke PM2.5 and maternal blood pressure
Authors: Patrick S. Reuther, Sam D. Faulstich, William J. Metcalf, Calvin S. Riss, Howard H. Chang, Lyndsey A. Darrow, Barrett M. Welch, Heather A. Holmes, Matthew J. Strickland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWildland fires are a significant source of pollution that can cause sudden changes in ground-level ambient fine particulate matter (PM2.5), an exposure that has been associated with gestational blood pressure increases. However, estimating the health risks specific to wildland fire smoke PM2.5 is often encumbered by limitations to smoke plume modeling.MethodsWe utilized a novel approach to estimate and characterize wildland fire smoke PM2.5 in Reno, Nevada, USA, an area prone to seasonal smoke from wildland fires. Daily, 24-h averaged, plume-specific wildland fire smoke PM2.5 concentrations were estimated at two air quality monitoring stations, and each plume was categorized by both the intensity of the source fire (high vs. low intensity) and the primary fuel type (forest, shrub/scrub, grassland, and other). Using electronic medical records (2012–2019), we identified a set of healthcare encounters of pregnant individuals, assigned these encounters to the air quality monitor closest to the maternal residential address at the time of the encounter and estimated the acute effects (0 to 3 day lag periods) of wildland fire smoke PM2.5 on maternal systolic and diastolic blood pressure measures across gestational periods using a multi-level linear regression model.ResultsWe evaluated 132,570 clinical encounters with a blood pressure measurement over 22,399 pregnancies (median 4.0 measures per pregnancy). Overall, we found null associations between wildland fire smoke PM2.5 and maternal blood pressure, regardless of the lag period. However, there was evidence that suggested associations could differ between gestational exposure periods, as positive associations not meeting statistical significance criteria were observed for almost all first trimester exposures. Associations did not differ by fire intensity or fuel type.DiscussionWe did not find evidence for short-term associations between wildland fire smoke PM2.5 and maternal blood pressure. Similar to other studies in the field, challenges to modeling smoke plumes and subjects altering their behavior during smoke events may be impacting the analyses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12940-026-01323-6.

Background

During pregnancy, elevated blood pressure is associated with increased risk of poor pregnancy outcomes, including stillbirth [1], low birth weight [2], maternal morbidity [3], and increased risk of cardiovascular disease after pregnancy [4]. Increasing ambient air PM2.5 (particulate matter < 2.5 microns in diameter) concentrations have been positively associated with maternal gestational blood pressure [5–9],however, some studies have reported not finding this association [10, 11]. Ambient PM2.5 is hypothesized to affect maternal gestational blood pressure and lead to gestational hypertension through irritation and edema of the circulatory vessels after crossing the alveolar membrane [12]. Maternal blood pressure may be especially vulnerable to PM2.5 during the 1st trimester of pregnancy, as rapid changes to the fetal circulatory system during early pregnancy require a regulated balance of proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory mediators that are responsive to inflammatory stress, which differs from how PM2.5 may impact the maternal cardiovascular system in later stages of pregnancy [13].

Wildland fires have been increasing in frequency and intensity both worldwide [14] and in the western United States [15]. Wildland fires can release large quantities of PM2.5 into the atmosphere that can be carried across vast geographic distances and create hazardous levels of ground-level ambient PM2.5 [16]. Similar to studies of total PM2.5 [17], researchers have reported cardiovascular effects of smoke-specific PM2.5 among pregnant individuals. Previous research has found an exposure increase of 1 µg/m3 of wildland fire smoke PM2.5 was associated with increased risk of gestational hypertension [18], exposure to wood smoke-sourced PM2.5 was associated with increased risk of preeclampsia [19], and third trimester exposure to wood burning smoke was associated with increased odds of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy [20].

Research on the health effects of wildland fire smoke PM2.5, however, is often limited by exposure data that inadequately characterizes ambient smoke-specific ground-level PM2.5 during a wildland fire event [21]. Although advances have been achieved over the last decade in the measurement and modeling of wildland fire smoke plume rise, dispersion, and transport utilizing satellite, aircraft, drone, and ground based sensors [22], smoke plumes can exhibit abrupt geographic and temporal changes that existing models may smooth over, and the geographic sparsity of the ground monitoring air quality network alone may be insufficient to capture plume dynamics for studies on the health effects of wildland fire smoke PM2.5. Additionally, monitoring stations do not differentiate PM2.5 from smoke and non-smoke sources, so land managers and researchers lack a “ground truth” for model training and validation of smoke-specific PM2.5. Additional measurement limitations of existing data sources include: gridded datasets can be computationally intensive to generate with adequate spatial and temporal resolution, monitoring station measurements may have limited relevance for areas outside the immediate vicinity of the monitor, and satellite datasets are often encumbered by error defining the edges of the smoke plumes and missing retrievals due to cloud cover [23].

For this study, our goal is to estimate associations between modeled estimates of ground-level PM2.5 specific to wildland fire smoke plumes and gestational blood pressure for various wildland fire types and exposure periods. We hope to overcome the limitations of previous work by utilizing novel smoke modeling methodology that combines data from air quality monitors, fire emissions inventories, an atmospheric dispersion model, and a Bayesian time series model (used to estimate the non-smoke PM2.5 concentration on days impacted by wildland fire smoke) to differentiate wildfire smoke PM2.5 from background PM2.5 [24] and create accurate estimates of the association between wildland fire smoke PM2.5 and gestational blood pressure.

Methods

Setting

The Reno Metropolitan Area of western Nevada encompasses the neighboring cities of Reno and Sparks, with a 2020 census population of 490,596 [25]. The region is notable for its high elevation, 1,373 m above sea level [26], mountainous terrain that bounds the area on all sides to form a singular air basin [27], and frequent impacts from wildland fire smoke plumes originating from the nearby Sierra Nevada Mountain Range and other surrounding areas [28]. The largest healthcare system in the region is Renown Health, which includes 5 inpatient hospitals, 17 primary care facilities, and 10 urgent care facilities [29]. Renown Health’s electronic health record (EHR) data is managed using Epic Systems Corporation software, and historic anonymized data from Renown Health’s EHR were utilized in these analyses. An 8-year study period of January 2012-December 2019 and the location were utilized to match dates when smoke-specific PM2.5 estimates were available.

Participants

We employed two strategies to identify clinical encounters where blood pressure measurements were recorded for pregnant individuals. First, State of Nevada Vital Statistic birth records were utilized to identify mothers of all babies delivered at Renown Health facilities, matching on the medical record number. Clinical encounters in the EHR occurring between the mother’s last menstrual period (LMP) date and the date of delivery were included. When an LMP date could not be identified (n = 5 pregnancies, 0.02%), a gestational period of 40 weeks was assumed for the purpose of determining gestational age. Second, to find clinical encounters during pregnancy for births that may have occurred outside the State of Nevada, we examined the EHR ICD-10 codes for encounters that would indicate pregnancy (“excess vomiting while pregnant”, “supervision of other normal pregnancy”, etc.). We developed a list of 10,249 ICD-10 codes that indicated the patient was likely pregnant at the time of the encounter (Supplemental Table 1 – List of ICD codes identifying likely pregnancy), and for the identified individuals, we evaluated the recent encounters for a recorded LMP date within the prior 45 weeks to determine the gestational age of the fetus at the time of the clinical encounter, linking encounters longitudinally on the health system’s patient identifier. We did not include clinical encounters identified using the second strategy that did not have an identified LMP date.

Exclusion criteria

Clinical encounters where systolic blood pressure was recorded as 50 mmHg or less were excluded as presumed data entry errors; no exclusions were made for diastolic blood pressure values. This exclusion was made a priori; we deemed a systolic blood pressure below 50 mmHg to be incompatible with life. Encounters within 7 days of the baby’s delivery date were excluded to remove the possible effects from labor and delivery. Encounters where the pregnant person was < 18 years old or > 45 years old at the time of delivery were excluded to remove groups at high risk of pregnancy complications [30, 31]. If a patient had multiple blood pressure measurements on a given day, only the first measure was used in the analysis to reduce the impact of medical interventions that may have occurred after the initial encounter. Encounters were also excluded if the most-recent residential address of the patient at the time of the encounter [32] was > 20 km from the closest air quality monitoring site with modeled wildland fire smoke PM2.5 estimates. Exclusion criteria and the final sample size are shown in Fig. 1. The decision to define the study area with a 20 km radius captures the vast majority of the Reno metropolitan area’s air basin and population without extending to neighboring communities, which may have smoke exposure patterns differing from what was modeled at the air quality monitoring sites.Fig. 1Exclusions flow chart

Exclusions flow chart

Environmental variables

Daily, 24-h averaged PM2.5 and ozone concentrations over the study period were gathered from the EPA’s AirData [33] for the air quality monitors at site IDs 320310016 (‘Reno3’) and 320311005 (‘Sparks’). Historical daily average temperatures at the Reno-Tahoe International Airport were obtained from the National Weather Service’s NOWData [34]. The daily average temperature was included as a covariate in all models, and the daily ozone concentration was included only in models utilized for secondary analyses.

Daily ground-level wildland fire smoke PM2.5 estimates were modeled at the two air quality monitor sites through a novel approach combining data from air quality monitors, the HYSPLIT atmospheric dispersion model, combined fire emission inventories, meteorology, and a Bayesian structural time series model to simulate where smoke travels and at what concentration. This novel method allows for spatial variation in the wildland fire smoke PM2.5 transport modeling within a plume, categorizes smoke plumes by fire characteristics, and is described in detail by Faulstich, et al. [24]. The daily difference between the EPA’s PM2.5 measurement and the modeled wildland fire smoke PM2.5 estimate was characterized as non-wildland fire smoke PM2.5 and included as a fixed effect in all models. In addition, the smoke PM2.5 model categorized each smoke plume by the intensity (defined as the ratio of heat produced to area burned) of the fire (high intensity (> 3,000 kBTU/km2) vs. low intensity) and the primary landcover of the burned area (forest, shrub/scrub, grassland, other). We created models to estimate associations with (1) all wildland fire smoke PM2.5, (2) high intensity fire smoke vs. low intensity fire smoke, and (3) forest fire vs. shrub/scrub fire vs. grassland fire smoke.

Each clinical encounter was assigned environmental variables by identifying the closest air quality monitor (Reno3 vs. Sparks) to the current residential address recorded in the EHR on the date of the encounter.

Clinical variables

The primary outcomes were the systolic and diastolic blood pressure measures (mmHg) as recorded in the EHR. Self-reported race of the pregnant person (Asian, Black, Latino, Native American, Pacific Islander, White) was controlled as a potential confounder. Cubic B-splines were included for age at delivery (knots at ages 25 & 35 years), pre-pregnancy BMI (knots at 18.5, 25, 30, & 35), and long-term trends (annual knots). Gestational week (0–40) at the time of the blood pressure measurement was modeled as a cubic polynomial to capture the within-pregnancy trend.

Statistical methods

We performed multi-level linear regression to estimate the effects of wildland fire smoke PM2.5 on systolic and diastolic blood pressure. In our study, a pregnant person may have multiple clinical encounters over the course of a single pregnancy, so individual pregnancies are treated as a 2nd level variable in the model. Additionally, a person may have multiple pregnancies, each with one or more clinical encounters over the course of the study period, so maternal identification is treated as a 3rd level variable in the model. Linear regression coefficients were estimated using the Mixed procedure in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). In the primary analysis, systolic and diastolic blood pressure associations were separately modeled for a variety of exposure periods (full pregnancy, 1st, 2nd, and 3rd trimester), wildland fire smoke classifications (high vs. low intensity, primary landcover), and exposure lag periods ({0, 1, 2, 3, 0–1, 0–2, 0–3, 1–2, and 1–3} days). Encounters occurring from LMP date through week 12 day 6 were categorized as 1st trimester, week 13 day 1 through week 27 day 6 as 2nd trimester, and week 28 day 1 to delivery as 3rd trimester. Exposure lag periods were categorized as either a single day lag period {0, 1, 2, 3} or by taking the average smoke specific PM2.5 over multiple days {0–1, 0–2, 0–3, 1–2, and 1–3} with separate models created for each lag period.

Secondary analyses

Secondary analyses included adding a fixed effect for daily, 24-h averaged ozone concentrations and removing control for non-wildland fire smoke PM2.5. Additionally, the approach to modeling ground-level wildland fire smoke PM2.5 produces estimates that may be ‘false negatives’ (days where no wildland fire smoke PM2.5 is estimated at one site but is being estimated at the neighboring site, 5.5 km away). In a separate secondary analysis, we used multiple imputation with 10 imputations to estimate ground-level wildland fire smoke PM2.5 at the potentially false negative site, with the imputation constrained so that it could not exceed the estimate at the neighboring site.

As an additional sensitivity analysis, we performed the primary analysis after excluding all pregnancies that only had one BP measure, to evaluate whether these pregnancies were unduly influencing the associations generated by the multi-level model.

Institutional review board

This study was deemed to be Institutional Review Board exempt by the University of Nevada, Reno Institutional Review Board (#1106618–25) as part of a larger body of work “Interoperability, operational efficiency and quality of care improvements through health data analysis”.

As depicted in Fig. 5, the relationship between the two measurements at the two monitors is linear, as expected; however, there are days where the smoke PM2.5 model is unexpectedly estimating significant wildland fire smoke PM2.5 at one monitor, but none at the other monitor. There were 323 days where one of the 24-h average smoke PM2.5 estimates was zero and the neighboring monitor estimate was non-zero. On 146 of those days (45%), the non-zero monitor estimates were less than 1 µg/m3, but for 10 days, the non-zero monitor estimates were greater than 10 µg/m3. These days of high discrepancy between monitor estimates motivated the sensitivity analysis where we impute smoke PM2.5 values at the monitor estimating no smoke.

Observed associations

We used multi-level linear regression to estimate associations between a 1 µg/m3 increase in the 24-h averaged wildland fire smoke PM2.5 concentration and changes in blood pressure during pregnancy over a variety of exposure periods, smoke categories, and lag periods. We found null associations with narrow confidence intervals between wildland fire smoke PM2.5 and blood pressure across most combinations of exposure periods, wildland fire fuel classifications, and lag periods after controlling for non-wildland fire smoke PM2.5. The most consistent associations on both systolic and diastolic blood pressure were noted for exposures during the 1st trimester of the pregnancy, and 1st trimester associations were primarily positive across all lag periods (apart from lag of 3 days and certain fuel types). Most of the reported associations for 2nd trimester exposure were negative. The 1st trimester associations when looking at a 3 day lag often differs in direction from lag periods of 0, 1, or 2 days, which may indicate that after 3 days, the body is able to recover from the insult caused by the wildland fire smoke PM2.5 exposure. Even so, the magnitude of these associations was consistently small, as all associations were lower than 0.1 mmHg per 1 µg/m3 increase in smoke PM2.5. Across all lag periods, 1st trimester associations are less precise than associations later in the pregnancy due to fewer measures taken during the 1st trimester (shown in Table 1 and Fig. 3), and we note the associations reported in Fig. 6 are not all independent from one another.

The secondary analyses showed no appreciable changes to the regression coefficient estimates when we added ozone to the primary analysis model nor when we removed control for non-smoke PM2.5. When we used multiple imputation to model potential ‘false negative’ days in the smoke models, the confidence intervals for the regression coefficient on the effect of smoke PM2.5 on blood pressure became narrower, though the estimates did not change appreciably. When we limited the analysis to exclude pregnancies with only one blood pressure measure as a sensitivity analysis, the only notable change was wider confidence intervals (Supplemental Table 3 – Secondary analysis regression coefficients).

Discussion

In this study of 132,570 blood pressure measurements taken over the course of 22,399 pregnancies we found highly precise but mostly null associations between maternal blood pressure and wildland fire smoke PM2.5. Although the associations were mostly null, it is still both valuable and novel to categorize wildland fire smoke plumes based on fire intensity and overland fuel type and to evaluate the extent to which the health effects of wildland fire smoke PM2.5 might differ according to these factors. The novelty of utilizing a model that separates and categorizes PM2.5 specific to wildland fire smoke from background PM2.5 is a major strength of this study.

Among the associations we observed, the most notable pattern was for exposure to smoke from a high intensity fire lagged 1 day during the 1st trimester, as the associations for both systolic and diastolic blood pressure were statistically significant and positive. This finding is consistent with Decrue et al.’s description of exposure to proinflammatory agents, such as inhalation of PM2.5, causing inflammation during early gestation periods [13]. Overall, the associations between wildland fire smoke PM2.5 and blood pressure were generally strongest for exposure during the 1st trimester of pregnancy. Although exposure modeling practices and health outcomes differ among epidemiologic studies, greater cardiovascular health impacts for increased PM2.5 exposures during the 1st trimester of pregnancy, as we observed, is both biologically plausible and consistent with other works [18]. Even so, the magnitude of the observed associations was small; all estimates were below 0.1 mmHg increase in blood pressure per 1 µg/m3 increase in smoke PM2.5. More studies are needed to determine whether these modest associations between blood pressure and smoke PM2.5 during the first trimester might be present in other populations.

The exposure model used is notable for its ability to study the impacts of specific fires on air quality in the study area, to separate PM2.5 specific to wildland fires from the background (non-smoke) PM2.5, and to categorize the wildland fire PM2.5 based on overland fuel type and fire intensity. This model is an important strength of our study. To our knowledge, only two other studies have attempted to use a similarly complex wildland fire smoke exposure models to examine the health impacts specific to categorized wildland fire smoke PM2.5 [38, 39], which both found both fuel type and fire intensity impact associations between wildland smoke PM2.5 and health outcomes.

The 432 reported associations, we note, are not independent, as lag periods and the categorizations for fuel type/fire intensity overlap and the systolic/diastolic blood pressures are not independent measures within an individual. Of the 32 statistically significant associations, 19 (59%) are positive associations, and of the 13 statistically significant associations looking at diastolic blood pressure, 11 (84.6%) were for 1st trimester exposures. Statistically significant associations were never found at lag 3 days, and only one statistically significant association (p = 0.046) was found at lag 2 days, suggesting that associations between wildland fire smoke on maternal blood pressure likely manifest within 48 h of exposure.

Both secondary analyses (including the 24-h average ozone concentrations, and imputing exposures on days when there was discrepancy between the two monitors) showed highly precise, primarily null results consistent with the primary analysis. The patterns of positive association point estimates and wider confidence intervals in the 1st trimester were consistent across both secondary analyses, indicating that neither the inclusion of ozone nor the monitor discrepancies were driving the results from our main analysis.

Although it is possible that there is no association between wildfire smoke PM2.5 and blood pressure during pregnancy, there are factors that may be systematically biasing our observations toward a null association, masking a true effect, which include an imperfect smoke model and behavior patterns of the subjects. The 24-h averaging of the smoke model may be masking short-term, high-concentration periods of ambient smoke exposure. By their nature, wildland fire smoke plumes can move quickly across large geographical areas, which makes them difficult to model. Our model utilized 24-h averaged time periods, which may be too broad to capture short-term spikes in PM2.5, and if the frequency or intensity of those PM2.5 spikes are the driver of changes in maternal blood pressure, this averaging would introduce bias toward the null. A second factor which may be mitigating the impacts of smoke on blood pressure is the behavior of the study participants. On days with high smoke concentrations, participants may be electing to remain indoors, with fewer participants breathing unfiltered ambient air, a behavior pattern described by Heft-Neal et al. [40] which would be expected to bias our observed results toward no association. Additionally, because of the deidentification process that was implemented by the data provider to ensure confidentiality of health records, we could not investigate the role of socioeconomic status and other related variables in this analysis. Fortunately, such factors are unlikely to confound the associations estimated in this study, though unmeasured effect modification may be present. Our analyses are based on within-person contrasts in wildland fire smoke PM2.5 concentrations during pregnancy, and socioeconomic characteristics such as education, income, etc. do not vary in ways that are associated with short-term changes in smoke PM2.5 concentrations. There is also likely variation and inconsistency in how blood pressures were measured and entered into the EHR across the 8-year study period and different departments within the health system. The inconsistent measurement techniques may introduce random error into blood pressure recordings unrelated to the presence of ambient wildland fire smoke PM2.5 and so would introduce Berkson-like error and likely decrease the precision of the estimate [41].

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study did not provide strong evidence that PM2.5 specific to wildland fire smoke was impacting maternal blood pressure. There were indications that associations might be present during the first trimester for short lag times (lag 0 and/or lag 1 exposure), but the magnitude of these findings was small, and more studies are needed to evaluate whether these findings replicate. Further, there are concerns, albeit not unique to this study, that the associations reported in this study could be biased towards no association. Continued improvements in wildfire smoke exposure modeling should help to reduce errors in exposure estimation, which will in turn benefit epidemiologic studies by reducing bias in association estimates. Our findings highlight the need to continue to refine exposure models to carefully categorize the heterogeneous mixture of wildland fire smoke until the health effects are more clearly understood.
