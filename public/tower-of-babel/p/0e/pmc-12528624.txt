Exploring ambient-stable lead-free (CH₃NH₃)₃Bi₂I₉ perovskites for solar cell applications
Authors: Abhinav Thirantevda, P Sujith, C S Suchand Sangeeth, Saidi Reddy Parne, Goutam Kumar Chandra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHybrid organic-inorganic perovskites, with power conversion efficiencies comparable to those of polycrystalline silicon-based counterparts, as well as tunable band gaps and low-temperature solution processability, represent a promising class of optoelectronic semiconductors suitable for next-generation electronics. In this work, we synthesised high-quality single-crystal and thin films of methylammonium bismuth iodide (MBI) using an antisolvent assisted method. The X-ray diffraction analysis showed a hexagonal crystal structure for the synthesised MBI crystal with space group P63/mmc. The bandgap of the MBI film estimated from the absorption spectra is 2.19 eV. The thermogravimetric analysis indicates that the thermal stability of MBI exceeds that of its constituents, thereby offering improved thermal stability. Furthermore, a trap state density comparable to that of vacuum processed condition is achieved by antisolvent treatment. The impedance and dielectric characteristics of MBI are recorded for a frequency range of 100 Hz to 1 MHz and the complex impedance is analysed by fitting the Nyquist plot. A mobility of 2.29 × 10− 6 cm² V− 1 s− 1 is deduced by fitting the space charge limited current (SCLC) region, and a photovoltaic analysis was performed using MBI as an absorber.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-025-04365-w.

Introduction

The introduction of organic–inorganic lead (Pb) halide perovskite in solar cells began in 2009 with a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of approximately 3%, Over the next decade, these materials revolutionized the field, achieving a certified efficiency exceeding 24% by 2019, and are anticipated to rival the efficiency of modern silicon-based solar cells [1, 2]. Tunable band gap, large absorption coefficient, high carrier diffusion length, better charge carrier mobility, ease of defect-free state formation, and excellent ion migration along with low-temperature solution processability make this class of material a viable candidate for solar cells and other areas such as light-emitting diodes, transistors, photodetectors, sensors and memories [3–8]. Along with the flexibility, this class of materials opens applications not only in the large-scale solar sector but also in small-scale building-integrated photovoltaics [9], which in turn can reduce the societal dependence on high-carbon energy sources. Despite the theoretical prediction, suggesting a PCE exceeding 31% [10] for this class of materials, their practical advances in commercialization have been overshadowed by inherent stability and toxicity concerns related to the element Pb [11, 12]. A viable method to modify the tolerance factor and, consequently, the inherent stability of the perovskite structure would be to tune the cation/anion composition of the perovskite [13, 14]. Moreover, B-site doping is employed to mitigate the toxicity of lead ions and enhance the charge carrier lifetime, thereby improving solar energy conversion efficiency and light absorption [15, 16]. The toxic nature of these materials can be mitigated by replacing Pb with environmentally acceptable elements like Ge, Sn, Sb, Bi, Cu, Ti, etc., but with a significant reduction in efficiency [17–23]. Even though Sn-based halides have reported a 9% efficiency, the rapid oxidation of Sn in the environment renders their further advancement [24].

The element Bi is a potential substitute for Pb as not only is the ionic radius of Bi3+ (1.03 Å) comparable to that of Pb2+ (1.19 Å), but also has an isoelectronic valence shell with Pb2+ [25]. Tunable electronic and optical properties can be achieved by combining different anion groups with bismuth iodide octahedra as they result in different geometries [26]. A promising Bi-based perovskite structure, A3B2X9 (A = Cs, methylammonium, K, Rb; B = Bi; X = Cl, Br, I), features a crystalline lattice composed of hexagonally or cubically packed A and X ions. In contrast to the 3D framework constituted by corner-sharing PbX6 octahedra found in APbX3 perovskites, A3Bi2X9 and similar Bi-based alternatives are characterised by the arrangement of trigonal BX3 layers. This unique structural configuration provides fresh insights into the properties and potential applications of Bi-based perovskites. MBI ((CH3NH3)3Bi2I9) perovskite adopts a zero-dimensional (0D) structure by the framework of isolated bioctahedral B2X93− anions. Compared to the degradation of methylammonium lead iodide at 85 °C, even in inert conditions and under ambient conditions for more than 10 min [27, 28], MBI offers better thermal stability. Lyu et al. demonstrated for the first time a solution processed MBI solar cell with an open circuit voltage of 0.354 V and PCE of 0.19% [29]. A hysteresis-free PCE over 3% along with long-term stability is reported by Jain et al. in the very next year [30]. Simulations have reported that MBI perovskite solar cells can achieve an efficiency of 14% [31]. Therefore, comprehending its material properties is imperative for advancing further development.

Here we report the synthesis of high-quality single crystal and film of MBI perovskite using the antisolvent vapour-assisted crystallisation (AVC) method. Our results demonstrate that the anti-solvent-assisted method effectively reduces trap states in MBI films, which is crucial for improving device efficiency. The structural, optoelectronic and dielectric properties of low-temperature solution-processed MBI were investigated, followed by using MBI as a solar absorber.

Experimental and method

Materials

Methylamine hydroiodide (MAI, purity > 99.0%) and Bismuth(III) iodide (BI, purity > 98.0%,), were purchased from TCI chemicals. N,N-Dimethylformamide (DMF, purity ≥ 99.9%), Diethyl ether (anhydrous, purity ≥ 99.0%), Chloroform (purity ≥ 99.8%), Poly[2-methoxy-5-(2-ethylhexyloxy)-1,4-phenylenevinylene] (MEHPPV), Zinc acetate dihydrate (purity ≥ 98%), ethanolamine (purity ≥ 99.5%), 2-methoxyethanol (purity ≥ 99.8%) and Toluene (anhydrous, purity ≥ 99.8%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals Private Limited. The aforementioned chemicals were used as received.

The MBI precursor solution was prepared by dissolving BI and MAI (2:3 mol ratio) in DMF. The solution was then stirred on a hot plate at 60 °C for 12 h and filtered (0.22 μm, PTFE) to obtain a dark red, clear solution.

Growth of MBI single crystal

The AVC technique takes advantage of the different solubility levels of perovskite materials in various solutions. To promote the formation of high-quality single crystals, an appropriate anti-solvent is slowly allowed to diffuse into the perovskite precursor solution. For that, a 200 μL portion of the MBI precursor solution is placed into a 3 mL vial with a narrow opening, which is then kept inside a 20 mL vial containing 6 mL of chloroform (acting as the antisolvent) at room temperature. The antisolvent assisted technique eliminates the need for precise temperature regulation and circumvents temperature-dependent phase transitions. After 18 h, crystals of noticeable size begin to form at the bottom of the vial. The system is then left undisturbed for several days to promote the growth of larger, millimetre-sized crystals. As depicted in Fig. 1a, as MBI crystals begin to form, the solution's colour shifts from a deep red to a pale red.

Electrical characterization of MBI thin film

Prepatterned indium tin oxide (ITO) substrates (20 Ω/sq, Ossila) were treated with ultrasonication successively in soap water, deionised water, acetone and isopropanol each for 10 min. Subsequently drying, the substrate was subjected to UV-Ozone treatment for another 20 min. 60 μL of the filtered MBI precursor solution is dropped on the substrate and spin-coated at a rate of 2000 rpm for 20 s. 100 μL of antisolvent (toluene) is applied slowly for the last 10 s, which increases the fast nucleation. The coated substrate was then annealed at 80 °C for 30 min to remove any residual solvents (Fig. 1b). Finally, the top copper electrode (since the work function is close to that of ITO) was thermally deposited by the physical vapour deposition method at a base pressure of 2 × 10–5 mbar through a shadow mask to a thickness of 100 nm.

Solar cell fabrication

ZnO solution (0.5 M) is prepared by following the reported procedure [32]. An amount of 60 μL of this solution was spin-coated onto a UV-ozone-treated, pre-cleaned ITO substrate at 4000 rpm for 40 s. The coated substrate was then annealed at 180 °C for 15 min. After annealing, the ZnO film underwent an additional 20 min UV-ozone treatment. Subsequently, 40 μL of the filtered MBI precursor solution is dropped on the substrate and spin-coated at a rate of 1500 rpm for 10 s followed by 4000 rpm for 30 s. During the final 10 s of the spin-coating process, 100 μL of toluene was added dropwise. The substrate was then annealed at 80 °C for 30 min. After annealing, MEHPPV solution was spin-coated at 1500 rpm for 10 s followed by 2500 rpm for 30 s. The substrate was then annealed at 80 °C for 10 min. Finally, 7 nm MoO3 and 100 nm silver top electrodes are thermally evaporated at 2 × 10–5 mbar pressure with an active area of 4 mm2 to complete the cell.

Testing and characterization

The absorption characteristics of the samples were recorded using UV–Visible (UV–Vis) spectrophotometer (AvaSpec-UL2048-USB). Powder x-ray diffraction (PXRD) spectra for the grinded MBI crystal were collected using a PANalytical X’pert3 powder x-ray diffractometer (Kα Cu, λ = 1.5406 Å). Grazing incidence x-ray diffraction (GIXRD) studies of the thin film were conducted using a Bruker D8 (Kα Cu, λ = 1.5406 Å) advance twin twin x-ray diffractometer. The Bruker D8 Venture (Kα Mo λ = 0.71073 Å) single crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) is used for the examination of crystal structure and unit cell parameters of MBI single crystal. Graphical representation of the crystal is obtained using the VESTA [33]. Surface morphology measurements were studied using an atomic force microscopy (AFM, Park XE-100) system and scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS Gemini-300), for thermal stability, thermogravimetric (TG) measurements were conducted using a HITACHI STA7200 series analyser at 10 °C/min flow rate in an inert atmosphere. The photoluminescence (PL) spectra of the sample at room temperature were collected using a Horiba FluoroMax® 4 spectrometer with a 388 nm excitation source coupled with a photon counting detector (R928P photomultiplier tube). Dielectric and impedance properties of MBI were measured from the impedance analyser (Wayne Kerr, 6500B), and current–voltage measurement of ITO/MBI/Cu device was measured by a Keithley 2612A source meter. Current − voltage characteristics of solar cells were measured using a precision source measure unit (Agilent B2902A) under AM 1.5G illumination (Photo emission tech solar simulator#SS50AAA).

Results and discussions

Crystal structure, morphology, optoelectronic and thermal properties

The MBI compound is described by the general formula A3B2X9. The MBI crystal is formed by MA+ cations neighbouring face-sharing bioctahedra (Bi2I9)3− clusters with hexagonal symmetry [34]. The slow diffusion of antisolvent in the precursor solution initiates the nucleation and results in millimetre-sized dark red crystals. The crystal structure and grown millimetre-size crystals are shown in Fig. 2a and b, respectively. The single crystals of MBI obtained by the AVC method were washed in diethyl ether to remove any residual impurity. The crystal diffraction spot obtained from the SCXRD measurements at room temperature is presented in Fig. 2c. The unit-cell parameters a = b = 8.571(1) ± 0.008 Å and c = 21.690(4) ± 0.03 Å with mutual angles α = β = 90°, and γ = 120° resulting in a cell volume of 1382 ± 3 Å3 derived from SCXRD data are in accordance with the literature value Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre (CCDC) 1,433,118 [35]. The MBI single crystal is classified within the hexagonal crystal system, and they possess the P63/mmc space group. The powder x-ray diffractogram of MBI powder and grazing incident x-ray diffractogram of MBI film on glass is presented in Fig. 2d. The obtained diffraction peaks of PXRD are in good agreement with hexagonal symmetry (P63/mmc) with unit cell parameters a = b = 8.568 Å and c = 21.757 Å (ICSD:242,180) [36]. The MBI film shows diffraction peaks at about 16.23°, 24.51°, 41.36°, and 50.28°, which are attributed to (004), (006), (0010), (0012) diffraction planes, respectively. In the XRD peak analysis, no peaks corresponding to MAI and BI are observed, indicating the presence of only MBI phase is present, which demonstrates the successful synthesis of a highly crystalline MBI phase.

The surface morphology of the MBI thin film deposited on a glass substrate was inspected using SEM and AFM measurements. Figure 3a and b show the SEM images of MBI with and without anti solvent treatment, respectively. It is observed that anti solvent treatment promotes the rapid formation of MBI crystals, leading to a reduction in crystal tile size. This, in turn, minimizes pinhole formation, resulting in a more uniform and better-quality film. Figure 3c depicts the MBI microcrystals formed in the absence of antisolvent treatment have a grain size of approximately 5 μm, and there are no grain boundaries within the hexagonal tile. The growth of MBI microcrystals in a hexagonal shape can be attributed to the hexagonal crystal lattice structure of the MBI phase. The presence of pores/voids within the microcrystal is assigned to the synthesis protocol [37]. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis of MBI powder and thin film is carried out to verify the expected elemental composition, and the I:Bi ratio for the powder is 4.18:1 and for thin film is 3.81:1 (Figure S1-S2 of the supplementary information). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted to verify the elemental composition and determine the valence states of Bi and I in the MBI powder. High resolution XPS spectra of C 1 s, N 1 s, I 3d, Bi 4f are shown in figure S3 of the supplementary information. The peaks observed at Bi 4f7/2 (159.70 eV) and 4f5/2 (164.95 eV), along with I 3d5/2 (619.10 eV) and 3d3/2 (630.6 eV), correspond to the characteristic signals of Bi3⁺ and I⁻ species, respectively. AFM analysis was used to characterize the surface roughness of the fabricated MBI thin film, with a scan area of 2.5 × 2.5 μm2. The two-dimensional (2D) AFM image of the MBI thin film reveals a root mean square roughness (Rq) of 52.87 nm and an average roughness (Ra) of 40.77 nm (Fig. 3d). These relatively low values of Ra and Rq indicate the smooth surface of the MBI thin film, which could be advantageous for its intended application [38, 39].

Because of its remarkable absorption capabilities, the MBI perovskite structure has been proposed as an alternative for Pb-based perovskites in photovoltaic applications [35, 40, 41]. The optical properties were analysed using UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy over a wavelength range of 300–800 nm, and the prominent absorption peak at 500 nm is attributed to the intrinsic exciton absorption resulting from the transition of the Bi3+ cation from the 1S0 to the 3P1 state [42]. Absorption spectra of MBI film also depend on the orientation of the crystals [43], UV–Vis absorption spectrum of MBI film is shown in Fig. 4a. From the Tauc plot, a direct band gap of 2.19 eV was estimated for the MBI film, which is more than that of BiI3 [44] and is in accordance with the previous reports [40]. The band gap observed for MBI is slightly larger than the optimal value typically recommended for solar cell materials (1.1 eV < Eg < 1.6 eV) making them suited for the top cell of a tandem cell [45]. The MBI single crystal has a well-defined absorption edge around 660 nm, indicating remarkable purity and reducing the possibility of unwanted electronic transitions caused by low-density shallow energy level defects [46]. The absorption spectra of MBI film indicate an onset (~ 590 nm) and an excitonic peak (~ 500 nm). Figure 4b shows the steady-state PL spectrum of MBI crystal and solution under an excitation wavelength of 388 nm. The MBI crystal exhibits a peak at 634.6 nm (1.95 eV) and at room temperature, which is attributed to the indirect band gap transition by Hoye et al. [47]. Narrow PL emission spectra of MBI crystals are linked to high crystallinity, where atoms or molecules arrange into a well-ordered lattice with fewer defects and dislocations [48]. These are crucial factors deciding the performance of the optoelectronic devices.

(a) UV–Vis absorption spectrum of MBI, Inset: direct band gap from Tauc plot method. (b) PL spectrum of MBI

The thermal stability of the synthesised MBI crystal is investigated through thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) over a temperature range of 30 – 600 °C in an inert atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. A single stage decomposition of MBI with no weight loss was observed up to 296 °C, and it is found to be more stable than the corresponding components (Fig. 5). Single stage decomposition of MBI over the two stage decomposition of methylammonium lead iodide [49] suggest the possibility of thermal coating of this material, but the organic component MAI thermally degrades at relatively low temperatures by primarily releasing volatile fragments instead of subliming as an intact molecule [50], Co-evaporation of binary precursors were performed to synthesis high quality MBI film [51]. Compared to the degradation of methylammonium lead iodide at 85 °C under inert conditions, MBI offers better thermal stability under ambient conditions.

There are several potential reasons for the relatively low efficiencies observed in the early stages of research on these new solar cell materials. This recombination can obstruct the performance of solar cells.

Conclusion

We successfully synthesised high-quality, eco-friendly MBI single crystals and film by AVC method. The MBI compound demonstrated exceptional stability under ambient conditions, which represents a significant advantage over conventional Pb and Sn halide perovskites. The crystalline structure was determined through single-crystal and powder X-ray diffraction analysis. The SEM analysis showed that the antisolvent treatment on film preparation improves the surface coverage, which is crucial for device applications. The carrier mobility, trap state density, and conductivity of these materials are explored using J-V characteristics, and it is found that their carrier mobility is comparable to that of their inorganic counterparts. Thus fabricated film exhibits a low trap density comparable to that of vacuum processed conditions. Photovoltaic devices were fabricated, demonstrating a strong photo response in the UV–Vis range. The presence of trap states in this material is detrimental to the operational efficiency of the devices. Hence, the low PCE observed can be improved with suitable dopants.
