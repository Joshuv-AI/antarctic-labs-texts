The effects of leukocyte’s RNA-binding proteins on the immune responses of dogs infected with canine parvovirus
Authors: Khosravi Mohammad, Bakhshandeh Maedeh, Mosallanejad Bahman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCanine parvovirus (CPV) is a highly contagious virus that can lead to severe clinical complications in dogs. RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) play a crucial role in regulating translation and stabilizing RNA. Due to the failure of the immune system to respond effectively in dogs infected with CPV, this study aimed to investigate various immunological markers in CPV-infected dogs and to evaluate the impact of RBPs from these infected dogs on immune responses. Blood samples were collected from both healthy and parvovirus-infected dogs. Parvovirus infection was confirmed using immunochromatographic kits. Monocytes were isolated, and exosomes were extracted from these cells and other leukocytes. RBPs were isolated from the leukocytes of parvovirus-infected dogs using Fe-nanoparticles conjugated to RNA. The delivery of RBPs into monocytes and leukocytes was achieved through exosomes. Subsequently, the activities of anti-protease, lysozyme, myeloperoxidase, survival rates, and the expression some of the inflammatory response genes were assessed in the recipient cells.ResultsDogs with acute CPV exhibited significantly lower specific antibody titers and comparable total immunoglobulin levels, along with reduced lysozyme and myeloperoxidase activities in their leukocytes compared to asymptomatic dogs. A significant effect of exosomes and RBPs were observed on lysozyme activity, myeloperoxidase levels, and leukocyte survival. The expression of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) (16.4-fold) and NOX2 (7.61-fold) was significantly elevated in monocytes treated with RBPs compared to untreated cells. Additionally, no viral proteins were found in RBPs extracted from infected animals.ConclusionsThe RBPs from infected dogs demonstrated a compensatory role in regulating immune responses in infected animals, rather than mediating immune regulation by CPV through RBPs. These findings enhance our understanding of the immune response alterations in dogs affected by parvovirus infection, the role of RBPs in this process, and the potential development of novel therapeutic approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05105-z.

Introduction

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) are essential regulators of immune responses. They modulate gene expression by influencing splicing, polyadenylation, localization, translation, and degradation of target mRNAs. Current evidence indicates that RBPs play significant roles in the development, activation, and function of lymphocyte populations within the immune system. One study highlighted the role of RBPs in regulating post-transcriptional gene expression during lymphocyte maturation [1]. Gene expression regulation can occur at any stage in the transfer of genetic information from DNA to protein. The regulation of transcription levels is controlled by transcription factors and epigenetic mechanisms, such as DNA methylation, as well as by RNA degradation. Effective translation of mRNA into protein requires proper localization and access to the transcript. Additionally, qualitative modifications of cellular RNA—such as polyadenylation, methylation, and RNA editing—significantly influence RNA fate and proteome composition. These processes are dynamically regulated by RBPs, which are recognized as key regulators of post-transcriptional gene expression.

It is estimated that the mammalian genome encodes over 1,500 RBPs [2, 3]. RBPs perform their functions by binding to RNA sequences or secondary structures through various RNA motifs. Some RBPs bind to short, single-stranded sequences in mRNA, including AU-rich elements, GU-rich elements, or polypyrimidine tracts [4]. The interaction between RNAs and RBPs regulates RNA biology within dynamic ribonucleoprotein complexes [5]. RBP proteins respond to numerous biological and environmental stimuli. Consequently, it is not surprising that defects in RBP expression and function are associated with various diseases, including neuropathies, muscular atrophy, metabolic disorders, and cancers, all linked to altered RBP expression [6].

Research on specific RBPs in pigs has demonstrated that these proteins regulate the expression of the non-structural proteins NS1 and NS2 mRNA by binding to porcine parvovirus DNA, thereby controlling viral replication [7]. Similar findings have been reported in studies on human parvovirus, where RNA motif-binding protein 38, significantly influenced the replication of the non-structural 11 kDa protein [8]. Notably, non-structural proteins play crucial roles in viral replication, chromatin remodelling, and the DNA damage response during parvovirus replication [9]. The nuclear positioning and dynamic interactions of viral proteins with the nuclear infrastructure are essential during infections with DNA viruses. Observing the nuclear interactions and replication dynamics of parvovirus proteins in live cells provides new insights into the specific interactions of these proteins within the cellular structure.

Canine parvovirus (CPV) is a common DNA virus affecting members of the Canidae and Felidae families. It is a reportable infectious disease primarily characterized by clinical signs of gastroenteritis in young dogs. In addition to gastrointestinal symptoms, dogs infected with CPV often exhibit leukopenia, lymphopenia, and neutropenia, which are associated with thymic atrophy [10]. The continuous presence of a pathogen in a specific area causes the disease to become endemic. Most dogs affected by this disease are under six months of age. Additionally, susceptibility between ten and twelve weeks of age is higher due to the reduced protective effect of maternally derived antibodies or unsuccessful vaccination [11].

The viral proteins—particularly those associated with DNA viruses—play crucial roles during viral infections by entering the nucleus and interacting with nuclear components. Labeling the NS1 protein of parvovirus with a fluorescent marker has enabled visualization of its movement and accumulation within the cell nucleus, as well as its interactions with nuclear components [12]. More recently, the interaction between canine parvovirus NS1 and tumor cell DNA, along with the subsequent induction of cell destruction, has been identified [13]. Additionally, another study demonstrated the role of NS2 in modulating chromatin accessibility during canine parvovirus replication [9].

Previous studies have highlighted the impact of canine parvovirus on lymphocyte destruction and the resulting suppression caused by decreased leukocyte counts in the pathogenesis of this disease [14]. Building on these findings, alterations in immune responses in dogs infected with parvovirus may arise from the regulation of splicing, editing, and RNA expression, which are influenced by changes in the expression and function of both host and viral RNA-binding proteins. Exosomes utilize pathways and mechanisms similar to those of viruses to influence endosomes. For this reason, they play a key role in the spread of viral diseases from cell to cell by evading the host immune response and delivering biological contents to nearby or distant target cells. Exosomes can affect various cellular processes, including inflammation, coagulation, and immune modulation during infection [15]. According to the current hypothesis, the transfer of leukocyte RBPs from CPV-infected dogs may exert similar modulatory effects on the leukocytes of healthy dogs, assuming these proteins have a regulatory function in infected animals. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the regulatory role of RBPs on leukocyte function by isolating RBPs from dogs infected with CPV and evaluating their effects on the immune responses of leukocytes, particularly monocytes by using exosome as vehicle of these proteins.

Materials and methods

Animals

The current study involved unvaccinated mixed-breed stray dogs, both healthy and infected with parvovirus, sourced from a local kennel in Ahvaz, Iran. All animals were female and aged between 2 and 8 months. Animals of the same age were included in both the healthy and infected groups. Healthy and infected dogs were identified based on clinical symptoms and the results of an antigen detection test using immunochromatography kits (Hangzhou Evegen Biotech), which demonstrate high sensitivity (95.4%) and moderate specificity (71.4%) [16]. Clinical symptoms observed in infected dogs included lethargy, bloody diarrhea, vomiting, anorexia, and dehydration. Healthy dogs were diagnosed based on a negative antigen test and the absence of clinical signs. Blood samples were collected in accordance with animal health and welfare guidelines, with ethical approval obtained from the Ethics Committee of Shahid Chamran University of Ahvaz, Iran, in 2023 (verification code IR.SCU.REC.1402.016). Blood collection was performed under sterile conditions using heparin as the anticoagulant. A volume of 5 mL was drawn from the cephalic vein without sedation or anesthesia. A total of 7 samples were obtained from infected dogs and 11 from healthy animals; additionally, blood samples were collected from 6 healthy dogs for leukocyte cell culture, resulting in a total of 24 samples. The hematological analysis revealed severe leukopenia in the infected animals (Supplementary Table 1). The RBP samples were extracted from the monocytes and other white blood cells (WBCs) of CPV-infected dogs. After being loaded into WBC-derived exosomes, these samples were taken up by monocytes or other leukocytes from healthy dogs. Isolation of leukocytes

The blood samples of healthy and infected dogs were collected in Falcon tubes and transported to the laboratory. Blood samples were centrifuged for 12 min at 1200 g. The plasma was separated and frozen at −20 °C. The buffy coat was isolated and transferred to a microtube, to which 1.5 mL of red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer was added. Since some RBCs may have been transferred with the buffy coat, a lysis buffer consisting of 1.6 g of ammonium chloride dissolved in 200 mL of distilled water was used to eliminate excess red blood cells. The buffer was added in a 3:1 ratio to the buffy coat in the microtube. After mixing, the solution was incubated for 3 min. It was then centrifuged at 800 g for 4 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the previous steps were repeated until removing the RBCs. Finally, the isolated WBC were used for the separation of monocytes or the purification of RBPs.

Isolation of monocyte and other leukocytes

Canine monocytes were isolated by optimizing the incubation time, type and volume of culture media, following standard protocols based on monocyte adherence to the plastic surface of the flasks, as previously described [17, 18]. The isolated WBCs were suspended in RPMI cell culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. The flask containing the leukocyte culture was incubated at 37 °C for 3 h to allow monocytes to adhere to the culture flasks. Subsequently, the flasks were washed three times to remove any non-adherent leukocytes. These non-adherent cells were classified as leukocytes other than monocytes and labeled as WBCs. An appropriate volume of 0.05% trypsin-EDTA solution (250 µL for a T25 flask) was added to the cell culture flasks and incubated for 3–4 min to detach the adherent cells. The trypsin was neutralized by adding 1 mL of FBS to the detached cells. The cell suspension was carefully collected using a pipette and transferred to a sterile microtube. The separated adherent and non-adherent cells were centrifuged separately at 800 × g for 4 min; after observing the cell pellet, the supernatant was discarded. Fresh culture medium was added to the pellet and mixed thoroughly to resuspend the cells, which were then counted using Trypan Blue staining. The cells were evenly distributed into flasks or 96-well plates. To isolate exosomes from the cultured cells, the regular culture medium was replaced with FBS-free medium for 24 h. The conditioned medium was collected in a sterile Falcon tube, centrifuged at 2600 × g for 15 min, filtered through a 0.2 μm filter, and used for exosome extraction.

Exosome extraction

Exosomes were extracted from monocytes and WBC of the healthy dogs using the EXOCIB extraction kit. Briefly, precipitant reagent A was brought to 37 °C, briefly vortexed, and then added to the sample at a 1:5 ratio (1 volume of reagent to 5 volumes of sample). The samples were vortexed for 5 min to ensure thorough mixing. They were then incubated at 4 °C for approximately 12 h, with gentle mixing every hour to enhance extraction efficiency. The samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 30 min. The supernatant was completely removed, and the pellet was resuspended in 50–200 µL of reagent B. Following extraction, protein quantification was performed using Bradford reagent, and electrophoresis was conducted using SDS-PAGE.

Total immunoglobulin assay

The total immunoglobulin assay was conducted using the zinc sulfate precipitation method to assess serum immunoglobulin levels. First, a 0.7 M zinc sulfate buffer was prepared, and its pH was adjusted to 5.8. Then, 850 µL of the zinc sulfate solution was mixed with 12.5 µL of the serum sample. The mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature for 2 h. The turbidity resulting from the precipitation was measured using a spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 600 nanometers [19].

Measurement of total serum IgG

The measurement of IgG titer was performed using an in-house Single Radial Immunodiffusion (SRID) method. The specific anti-dog IgG was obtained from rabbits hyperimmunized with purified dog IgG. For this purpose, IgG was isolated from dog serum via ion exchange chromatography, following the method described by Hay and Westwood (2012) [20]. The hyperimmunization of rabbits with dog IgG and the purification of their total IgG were conducted according to Khosravi et al. (2021) [21]. The protein concentration, purity, and reactivity of the purified IgG were assessed using the Bradford assay, SDS-PAGE, and ELISA, respectively. A 1% agarose gel in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was prepared, and once the gel temperature had decreased to around 40–45 °C, rabbit IgG was added at a concentration of 2 mg/mL. The gel solution was then poured into 8 mL plates. After gelation, wells were punched into the gel, ensuring at least 2 cm of space between each well. The test samples were diluted 1:10, and 10 µL were added to each well. Purified dog IgG was used as the standard. The plates were incubated at room temperature in a humidified chamber for 24 h. The diameter of the resulting circular precipitin rings was carefully measured using a ruler.

Measurement of specific antibody titter for parvovirus

In the coating step, 100 µL of a monovalent parvovirus vaccine (BioVeta brand), diluted 1:10 in carbonate-bicarbonate sodium buffer (pH 9.6), was added to the designated wells of an ELISA plate (Jet Biofil, China). The plate was then covered and stored overnight at 4 °C. During the blocking step, 250 µL of a 4% skim milk solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Then, plasma samples were diluted 1:50 in PBS containing 0.1 M beta-mercaptoethanol. After one hour of incubation at room temperature, these dilutions were added to the wells. Previously prepared hyperimmunized dog serum against parvovirus and its purified IgG were used as positive controls. Additionally, 100 µL of PBS-T was added to some wells containing only the vaccine as negative controls. The plate was then covered and incubated at room temperature for 45 min. After optimizing the test steps, the experiment was repeated three times. The rabbit anti-dog antibody was used as secondary antibody at a dilution of 1:100 (20 µg/mL) in volume of 100 µL. The plate was incubated at room temperature for 45 min. Subsequently, a conjugated anti-rabbit antibody (Immuno Chemistry Technologies Company, USA) was prepared at a dilution of 1:20,000 and added to each well at a volume of 100 µL. The TMB substrate was added to each well at a volume of 60 µL and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Finally, 60 µL of stop solution was added, and the optical density of the wells was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Preparing Fe-RNA conjugate

RNA extraction

Four leukocyte samples were randomly selected from healthy dogs and pooled in equal volumes. RNA extraction was performed using the Sinaclone kit (Batch No: EX6031) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 500 µL of lysis buffer was added to the mixed cells, followed by 300 µL of precipitation solution. The mixture was inverted ten times and processed according to the protocol. The extracted RNA was evaluated using agarose gel electrophoresis, and the optical density ratio at 260/280 nm was measured.

RNA conjugation to Fe-nanoparticles

250 µL aliquot of washed Fe-nanoparticles, prepared according to Khosravi et al. (2021) [21] and exhibiting an optical absorption of 0.312 at 600 nm, was mixed with 1 mL of MES buffer. Subsequently, 100 µL of EDC (4 mg/mL) was added, and the mixture was incubated on a shaker for 30 min. Following this, 100 µL of NHS (6 mg/mL) was introduced, and the mixture was incubated for an additional 30 min. The extracted RNA was then added to the EDC-NHS-activated Fe-nanoparticles, followed by an incubation period of one hour. The Fe-nanoparticles were then blocked using 0.1 M Tris buffer at the final concentration (pH 8). After two hours, the nanoparticles were collected again and resuspended in 2 mL of sterile PBS containing 1% BSA, then stored at 4 °C [22]. To confirm RNA conjugation to the Fe-nanoparticles, nanoparticle analysis was performed using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and imaging via field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM).

FTIR analysis of Fe-RNA conjugate

The Fe nanoparticles, EDC-NHS-activated Fe nanoparticles, and Fe-RNA conjugates were incubated at 45 °C for 72 h. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) using a Perkin-Elmer 2400 instrument was employed to characterize the molecular groups of the nanoparticles. For FTIR analysis, the samples were mixed with potassium bromide (KBr) and compressed into pellets. The KBr pellet method was utilized, producing absorption spectra in the range of 4000 to 400 cm⁻¹.

Imaging of Fe-nanoparticles and exosomes

Samples included monocyte exosomes, leukocyte (white blood cells other than monocytes) exosomes, Fe nanoparticles, and Fe-RNA conjugates, which were spread on aluminum sheets measuring 2 cm by 2 cm. A volume of 50 µL from each exosome sample was applied to these sheets and evenly distributed across the surface. The sheets were then allowed to dry at room temperature for one hour. For fixation, a series of diluted acetone solutions (50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%) were prepared from 100% acetone and distilled water. The sheets were sequentially immersed in these solutions, with a 10-minute interval between each step. The samples were subsequently fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde. Finally, the sheets were placed in a 37 °C incubator for 24 h to ensure complete drying. Imaging was conducted using an FESEM (MIRA2).

RBP extraction

Due to the low quantity of RBPs that could be extracted from a 5 mL blood sample, pooled buffy coat samples were used for RBP extraction to obtain a higher yield. From each sample of canine parvovirus-positive buffy coat, which had been frozen and thawed at least 20 times, 100 µL was taken and placed into a microtube. Next, 0.5 mL of Fe-RNA conjugates and 0.5 mL of PBS were added. The mixture was shaken for one hour. Neodymium N42 magnets measuring 40 × 40 × 20 mm were then used to attract the nanoparticles, and the supernatant was carefully removed. The nanoparticles were washed with sterile PBS until the protein concentration in the supernatant reached zero. Subsequently, 250 µL of 0.1 M Tris buffer (pH 8) was added, and the mixture was incubated in a water bath at 60 °C for 5 min to release RNA-binding proteins. The Fe-RNA conjugates were collected using a magnet, and the supernatant was retained as RBP.

Fluorescent labelling of RBP

Initially, 1 mL of 0.1 M MES buffer was mixed with 1 µL of FITC (5 mg/mL). Next, 100 µL of EDC (4 mg/mL) was added to the tube, and the mixture was shaken for 30 min. Subsequently, 100 µL of NHS (6 mg/mL) was introduced, and incubation continued for an additional 30 min. Following this, 100 µL of previously isolated RBP (adjusted to 100 µg/mL) was added to the mixture and incubated for one hour. Afterward, 100 µL of 1.5 M Tris buffer (resulting in a final concentration of 100 mM) was added for blocking, and the mixture was shaken for 60 min. The prepared samples were dialyzed in PBS for 48 h. The conjugated samples were analysed using fluorimetry, with control samples—including unconjugated FITC and RBP—serving as negative controls. The excitation wavelength was set at 520 nm for FITC.

Optimization of exosome uptake into cells

0.5 mL of sterile, filtered bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6) was mixed with 250 µL of exosomes (100 µg/mL), 1 µL of FITC (5 mg/mL) or RBP-FITC conjugate, or 50 µg/mL of monovalent parvovirus vaccine antigen. The mixture was incubated on a shaker for 30 min. Subsequently, an exosome precipitation protocol was performed. The control samples consisted of exosomes without RBP, FITC without RBP, or exosomes alone.

Isolated monocytes and other leukocytes were treated with prepared samples in 24-well plates for 24 h. The evaluated samples included: (1) leukocytes treated with leukocytes exosomes, (2) leukocytes treated with monocyte exosomes, (3) monocytes treated with leukocytes exosomes, (4) monocytes treated with monocyte exosomes, (5) PBS, (6) monocyte cells without exosomes, and (7) leukocytes cells without exosomes. For the control group, 100 µL of free FITC was added to three wells without exosome treatment. The wells containing monocyte cultures were washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, 200 µL of trypsin was added to each well and incubated for 5 min at 37 °C, followed by neutralization with 0.5 mL of FBS. For the wells containing other leukocytes, the medium was removed, and after centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded. The pelleted cells were washed three times with PBS. The treated leukocyte and monocyte samples were fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde for 10 min, followed by subsequent analysis using fluorometric assays, fluorescence microscopy, and Western blotting. The visualization of exosome uptake was performed using fluorescence microscopy analysis on target leukocytes treated with 0.5 mL of FITC-labelled exosomes (100 µg/mL) in a 24-well culture plate. Cells treated with exosomes containing parvovirus antigens were analysed using ELISA and western blotting to confirm exosome uptake. Initially, the treated cell samples underwent multiple freeze-thaw cycles. For the coating step, the samples were diluted in bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6) to a concentration of 10 µg/mL, and 100 µL was added to the wells of a polystyrene ELISA plate. Subsequent ELISA steps were performed as described in previously established protocol.

SDS-PAGE and Western blotting analysis

Western blotting was performed on extracted RBPs, with Canine parvovirus used as a positive control, at a total protein concentration of 25 µg/mL. Briefly, electrophoresis was carried out using a 10% separating gel and a 4% stacking gel. Samples were prepared in a sample buffer containing 0.125 M Tris (pH 6.8), 20% glycerol, 0.01% bromophenol blue, 4% SDS, and 5% 2-mercaptoethanol. The sample solution was boiled for 10 min, and then 30 µL was loaded into each well along with a protein ladder (Sinaclone; CAT No. SL7001). The pre-stained protein molecular weight marker includes proteins ranging from 11 to 245 kDa. The gel was run at a constant voltage (90 V for the stacking gel and 100 V for the separating gel) for 90 min, using a running buffer at pH 8.3 (25 mM Tris base, 192 mM glycine, 0.1% SDS). The polyacrylamide gel was stained with Coomassie blue for 20 min, followed by overnight destaining. In parallel, after electrophoresis, the protein bands were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane for Western blot analysis. The transfer was performed for 2 h at 20 V in a Mini Trans-Blot electrophoretic transfer cell (Bio-Rad) using a transfer buffer composed of 13 mM glycine, 95 mM Tris, and 20% methanol. Following the transfer, the membrane was blocked with 0.2% Tween-20 for 2 h, followed by three washes with PBS-T. The membrane was then incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with a 1:20 dilution of serum from either parvovirus-infected or healthy dogs. After washing as before, the membrane was incubated with rabbit anti-dog IgG (1 mg/mL diluted 1:1,000) for 60 min at room temperature. Following washing, HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Immuno Chemistry Technologies, USA; Catalog Number: 6293; 1 mg/mL diluted 1:7,500) was added to the membrane. Subsequently, the substrate diaminobenzidine (Sigma) was applied for color development. After 10 min, the membrane was washed three times with sterile water.

MTT assay

The viability of monocyte and leukocyte cells from six healthy dogs was assessed in 96-well microplates after a 24-hour treatment with exosomes derived from monocytes or leukocytes, as well as exosomes containing RBP from dogs infected with parvovirus, at a concentration of 40 µg/mL. To perform the assay, 5 mg of MTT powder was weighed and dissolved in 10 mL of RPMI culture medium to achieve a 0.5% solution. For the monocyte cells, after removing the treatment medium from the wells, the wells were washed twice with sterile PBS. Subsequently, 100 µL of the MTT solution was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After incubation, the MTT solution was carefully removed, and 100 µL of DMSO was added to each well. The samples were then incubated for an additional 30 min at 37 °C. Optical density measurements were taken at a wavelength of 600 nm. Cell viability for each treatment was calculated relative to the control wells [23]. For the evaluation of leukocyte viability, which was conducted in suspension culture, the cells from each well were transferred to microtubes and washed by centrifugation at 800 g for 5 min. Finally, to assess optical density, the samples were transferred to a sterile 96-well microplate. All experiments were performed in four wells for each condition and repeated three times.

Evaluation of innate immune responses

Lysozyme activity

This test evaluates the ability of lysozyme to lyse Micrococcus lysodeikticus. A total of 40 µL of PBS was mixed with 20 µL of serum from either parvovirus-negative or parvovirus-positive dogs, or with leukocytes from both groups. Additionally, samples of treated monocytes and leukocytes from healthy dogs were included, as described in the MTT assay. Three wells served as controls, containing no serum or cell samples. Subsequently, 100 µL of a suspension of Micrococcus lysodeikticus bacteria in sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.36) was added to the wells [24]. The optical density of the samples was measured at time zero and again 15 min later at a wavelength of 600 nm. The lysozyme activity in one mL of serum per minute was calculated using the following formula: (Initial Optical Density − Final Optical Density) × 1000/minutes.

Myeloperoxidase activity

In this assay, 10 µL of the dog’s serum sample was added to 100 µL of HBSS buffer. The optical density was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm. Subsequently, 20 µL of TMB was added to each well, and after 5 min, 50 µL of H₂SO₄ was added. The optical density was then measured again at 450 nm [25]. Myeloperoxidase activity was calculated using the following formula: (Final Optical Density − Initial Optical Density).

Anti-trypsin activity

This test involved mixing 10 µL of samples (positive and negative parvovirus serum, positive and negative leukocytes, and cell culture mixtures) with 100 µL of a 0.1 M Tris-HCl solution (pH 8.2) containing 20 µL of 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA [26]. Four control samples were prepared, each containing 20 µL of serum-free Tris-HCl with trypsin. The samples were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, 20 µL of the substrate BAPNA (Na-benzoyl-DL-arginine-p-nitroanilide HCl) at a concentration of 2 mM in Tris-HCl with 20 mM calcium chloride was added to all samples. After 15 min, 50 µL of 30% acetic acid was added to terminate the reaction. Optical density was measured at 405 nm. Anti-trypsin activity was calculated by determining the difference in absorbance between the serum-free positive control and each sample, divided by the absorbance of the serum-free positive control.

Evaluation of cytokine gene expression

RNA extraction was performed on treated cells, including leukocytes and monocytes, which were exposed to monocyte- or WBC-derived exosomes containing RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), as well as on monocyte or leukocyte exosomes lacking RBPs and untreated monocytes and WBCs as controls. The extraction was carried out using the Sinaclon kit (Cat. No: EX6031) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. To remove contaminating DNA, the following steps were carried out as per the Sinaclon kit instructions: In a sterile microtube, 3 µL of extracted RNA was combined with 10 µL of DEPC-treated water, 2 µL of 10X reaction buffer containing MgCl2, and 1 µL of RNase-free DNase. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Then, 1 µL of EDTA was added, and the samples were further incubated for 10 min at 60 °C. Subsequently, cDNA synthesis was initiated.

To synthesize cDNA, 9 µL of the RNA solution was combined with 2 µL of random hexamer primers and incubated in a water bath at 70 °C for 5 min. The mixture was then placed on ice, and the components required for cDNA synthesis were added, including 4 µL of 5X reaction buffer, 2 µL of 10 mM deoxynucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs), 0.5 µL of RNase inhibitor, and 2 µL of distilled water, all pre-mixed. The solution was subsequently incubated at 25 °C for 5 min. After adding 1 µL of MuLV reverse transcriptase, the sample was incubated at room temperature for 10 min, followed by 90 min at 42 °C. To terminate the reaction, the mixture was heated at 70 °C for 10 min.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed by combining 1 µL of forward primer, 1 µL of reverse primer, 5 µL of distilled water, 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix, and 3 µL of cDNA sample, resulting in a total volume of 20 µL. The primer sequences are provided in Table 1. Gene amplification was carried out over 35 thermal cycles, beginning with an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min (1 cycle), followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 40 s. Gene expression data were evaluated using StepOne software with the ΔΔCT comparative method. The results of real-time PCR were calculated based on the general formula of ΔΔCT as [ΔΔCT = (Ct target – Ct GAPDH) sample – (Ct target – Ct GAPDH) control], and fold change expression was reported as 2−ΔΔCT. All experiments were repeated three times.

Data analysis

The documented results were analyzed using SPSS software (version 23) and one-way ANOVA statistical tests. Graphs were plotted using GraphPad Prism 8 software. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data are presented as means with standard deviations (SD).

Results

Measurement of Fe-nanoparticle size

The size of Fe-nanoparticles conjugated to dog RNA, with an average diameter of 49.7 nm, was significantly larger (p = 0.010) than that of Fe-nanoparticles alone, which had an average diameter of 29.35 nm. The uniformity of the nanoparticles and the likelihood of RNA attachment to both the linker and Fe-nanoparticles were on suspected size and morphology (Fig. 1A and B and supplementary Fig. 1).

Shape and size of exosomes

Measurement of the diameters of exosomes extracted from canine monocytes and WBC cells, using electron microscopy images, revealed no significant difference in the average size of these particles. However, exosomes derived from WBCs had a slightly larger average diameter of 136.23 nm compared to those from monocytes, which averaged 112.27 nm (Fig. 1C and D and supplementary Fig. 1); this difference was not statistically significant. The mean concentrations of the extracted monocyte and WBC exosomes were 192 µg/mL and 406 µg/mL, respectively.

FTIR results

The FTIR results indicated the successful conjugation of Fe nanoparticles to the linker and RNA (Fig. 1E). Changes in the chemical groups of the Fe nanoparticles were observed in the region between 1663 and 1138 cm⁻¹ following activation mediated by EDC-NHS. Amide bonds were formed between the Fe nanoparticles and RNA at 793, 1596, and 1666 cm⁻¹ through the EDC-NHS linker. Additionally, the carboxyl groups in the RNA at 1435 and 1044 cm⁻¹ formed amide bonds with the iron nanoparticles.

SDS-PAGE and western blotting assay

The protein band patterns of RBP extracted from nanoparticles conjugated with EDC-NHS are shown in Fig. 1F. Similarities were observed in the monocyte-derived and WBC-derived exosomes at bands higher than 135 kDa, specifically at 180 and 245 kDa. Notably, a 25 kDa band is present in the monocyte-derived exosome but absent in the WBC-derived exosome. Conversely, the 17 kDa and 11 kDa bands, as well as bands below 11 kDa, are present in the WBC-derived exosome but not observed in the monocyte-derived exosome. Additionally, the 63 kDa and 100 kDa bands in the WBC-derived exosome are more intense (Fig. 1F). The Western blot assay confirmed the uptake of exosome-contained parvovirus antigens by treated monocytes and WBC; as shown in Fig. 3G, the major bands corresponding to parvovirus antigens were detected in the treated cell samples. Additionally, no reaction was observed between parvovirus-infected or healthy dog serum and RBPs extracted from the leukocytes of infected dogs, indicating the absence of viral antigens in leukocyte RBPs.

Total immunoglobulin and total IgG levels

The total antibody levels in the serum of dogs infected with parvovirus showed no significant difference (p = 0.22, t = 1.249, df = 21, F = 1.19) compared to those in healthy dogs (Fig. 2A). However, the Mancini test revealed that total IgG levels in dogs infected with parvovirus were significantly decreased (p = 0.0004, t = 4.7, df = 13, F = 2.44) compared to healthy dogs (Fig. 2B).

Parvovirus-specific antibody titer

For the coating process, a monovalent parvovirus vaccine at a concentration of 10 µg/mL was used. During the loading phase, both negative and positive parvovirus samples were included, as well as negative and positive parvovirus samples treated with 2-mercaptoethanol at a 1:40 dilution. The results showed that the overall titer of parvovirus-specific antibodies in dogs infected with parvovirus was significantly lower than that in healthy dogs (p = 0.0037, F(4, 40) = 27.67). However, despite this reduction, the specific IgG levels in infected dogs did not differ significantly from those in healthy dogs (Fig. 2C).

Exosome loading and cellular uptake

Fluorometric analysis was performed on exosomes isolated from white blood cells to evaluate their uptake by monocytes and leukocytes. Monocytes and WBCs without exosomes or treated with PBS served as controls. The results demonstrated that the uptake of leukocyte-derived exosomes by monocytes was significantly higher (p < 0.0001) compared to other conditions. Additionally, the uptake of both monocyte- and leukocyte-derived exosomes by leukocytes occurred at comparable levels (Fig. 3A). ELISA results confirmed the successful delivery of the parvovirus vaccine antigen into monocytes and leukocytes via exosomes (Fig. 3B). In addition, fluorescent microscopy analysis showed that qualitatively, more than half of the treated cells uptake the WBC-fluorescent-labeled exosomes (Fig. 3C and D and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Viability results

In this assay, we evaluated the viability of WBCs loaded with RBP-containing exosomes, untreated WBCs, WBCs treated with RBP alone, and exosomes without RBP. The experiment was conducted on two groups: monocytes and WBCs other than monocytes. The results indicated that treating leukocytes with exosomes (WBC Exo: p = 0.006, M Exo: p = 0.0151) and RBP (p = 0.0355) significantly reduced leukocyte activity. Furthermore, treatment with monocyte-derived exosomes (p < 0.0001) and leukocyte-derived exosomes (p < 0.0001) from dogs infected with parvovirus significantly decreased the activity of monocytes in healthy dogs. Additionally, treatment with RBP alone resulted in a significant reduction (p = 0.0007) of monocyte activity (Fig. 4).

Lysozyme activity

The test samples included both positive and negative parvovirus sera, as well as white blood cells (WBCs) from dogs that were either positive or negative for parvovirus. The results indicated that leukocytes from parvovirus-infected dogs exhibited significantly lower lysozyme activity (p < 0.0001) compared to those from healthy dogs. Furthermore, leukocytes treated with exosomes containing RBP from parvovirus-infected dogs showed significantly different lysozyme activity (p = 0.0004) compared to leukocytes from healthy dogs (Fig. 5A, B, and C).

Myeloperoxidase activity

Myeloperoxidase activity in dogs infected with parvovirus did not differ significantly from that in healthy dogs. However, leukocytes from parvovirus-infected dogs exhibited a significant increase (p < 0.001) in myeloperoxidase activity compared to the serum of the same infected dogs. Treatment with leukocyte-derived exosomes significantly enhanced (p = 0.042) myeloperoxidase activity in monocytes (Fig. 5D, E, and F).

Anti-trypsin activity

No significant difference was observed in the anti-trypsin activity of serum and leukocytes between parvovirus-infected dogs and healthy dogs. However, exosomes derived from monocytes significantly reduced anti-trypsin activity in monocytes (p = 0.0266) (Fig. 5G, H, and I).

Gene expression analysis

Monocytes and other leukocytes, collectively referred to as WBCs, from healthy dogs were evaluated for the expression of cytokine gene markers indicative of activation or differentiation. The evaluated samples included WBCs treated with WBC-derived or monocyte-derived exosomes containing RBP, as well as monocytes treated with WBC-derived or monocyte-derived exosomes containing RBPs. Control groups consisted of untreated WBCs and monocytes, as well as monocytes and WBCs treated with WBC-derived or monocyte-derived exosomes lacking RBPs. The expression levels of IFN-γ (p = 0.0341) and NOX2 (p = 0.007) were significantly elevated in monocytes and WBCs treated with monocyte-derived exosomes containing RBPs in comparison to their vehicle exosome; however, these effects were not observed following treatment with WBC-derived exosomes containing RBPs. WBC-derived exosomes had a significant enhancing effect on NOX2 gene expression in treated monocytes (14.12-fold increase), contrasting with the inhibitory effects (0.13- fold decrease) observed when these exosomes were loaded with RBPs from parvovirus-infected dogs.

IL-4 expression analysis revealed a significant increase (p = 0.006) in monocytes treated with monocyte-derived exosomes containing RBPs, showing a 2.35-fold increase compared to untreated monocytes. Treated white blood cells (WBCs) exhibited a 6.72-fold increase in NOX-2 gene expression when exposed to monocyte-derived exosomes containing RBPs, whereas other treatments significantly reduced NOX-2 gene expression in WBCs. IL-4 expression was reduced to 0.1-fold in WBCs treated with WBC-derived exosomes (Fig. 6).

Discussion

In the present study, the titer of parvovirus-specific antibodies was measured in normal and infected dogs to canine parvovirus; results indicating the endemic presence of the virus in the studied area. Furthermore, the lower in antibody titer in infected animals was attributed to the neutralization of specific antibodies upon interaction with the virus or immunosuppression in the acute phase of the disease. This result is consistent with previous statements regarding the immunosuppressive effects of CPV in infected animals, such as lymphopenia, neutropenia, and monocytopenia [28]. However, it contradicts the findings of Phillips and Schults (1987) [29], who reported that CPV does not cause immunosuppression. To date, no research has investigated the effects of exosomes or RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) on the activity of dog immune factors and the survival of leukocytes, particularly in dogs infected with canine parvovirus (CPV).

A study demonstrated significantly elevated levels of citrulline, TNF-α, and SOD in CPV-positive animals compared to their healthy counterparts, while levels of IL-6 and SAA were decreased. These changes in inflammatory and oxidative markers, may indicate that the animals, despite exhibiting clinical symptoms, were in the recovery phase of the CPV [30]. The current study was conducted on leukocytes from animals in the acute phase of the disease. The results indicated immunosuppression and an inflammatory state, as evidenced by changes in white blood cell activity and serum markers.

Previously, exosome of the infected-feline coronavirus (FCoV) cell line of CRFK have different average sizes at 48 and 72 h [31]. Also, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from beagle dogs; exosomes derived from the NK cells was between 40 and 200 nm in diameter [32]. In the current study, the sizes of exosomes extracted from monocytes and other leukocytes showed no significant differences. Maslov et al. (2024) reported no significant difference in the mean size (< 200 nm) and concentration of serum extracellular vesicle between healthy dogs and those infected with Babesia canis. Most identified proteins in exosomes were associated with membrane and cytosolic functions, as well as components of the innate and adaptive immune systems. The majority of EV proteins originated from immune system cells, including dendritic cells, neutrophils, B cells, monocytes, and platelets [33]. In the current study, treatments with monocyte-derived and other leukocyte-derived exosomes exhibited varying cell-specific effects on immune activity of the treated cells. These findings are consistent with previous reports regarding the regulatory role of exosomes in modulating immune responses.

In a recent study, various cell lines were infected with canine coronavirus (CCoV) to investigate the sensitivity, biology, and pharmacological activity of exosomes. The sizes of EVs ranged from 100 to 200 nm at 48- and 72-hours post-infection. EVs derived from infected MDCK cells exhibited larger particle sizes and higher concentrations, whereas the particle sizes of CRFK-derived EVs decreased at 48 h post-infection. These findings indicate that coronavirus infection affects cell viability, modulates EV biogenesis, and alters protein composition and trafficking, potentially influencing the progression of viral diseases [15]. Additionally, the study observed monocyte sensitivity to exosomes, suggesting their involvement in disease pathogenesis and exosome-mediated signaling pathways. According to the cited studies and current findings, exosomes derived from different cell types and extracted from cells in various states exhibit distinct regulatory effects on immune responses. The present research revealed differing regulatory effects of exosomes derived from monocytes and other leukocytes on immune responses, and the same effects on leukocyte survival. A significant decrease in the viability of monocytes and other leukocytes was observed after treatment with exosomes derived from the leukocytes of healthy dogs, which is attributed to immune system modulation mediated by exosomes. Additionally, these treatments resulted in a decrease and an increase in anti-protease and myeloperoxidase activity of monocytes, respectively, following treatment with monocyte-derived and WBC-derived exosomes.

A study demonstrated the potential of exosomes for delivering tumor antigens to affected dogs [34]. Consistently, another finding of current study was the favorable strong affinity of these exosomes to leukocytes. Also, in agreement with previous research [35], the current study suggests that exosomes have significantly influence cell viability. This study also evaluated the effects of monocyte and other leukocyte exosomes from healthy dogs on the immune activity and viability of leukocytes, highlighting the different roles these exosomes play in regulating immune responses. Therefore, the source of the exosome could have a significant impact when used as a vehicle for delivering antigens or medications.

Exosomes derived from WBCs containing RBPs showed a significant reduction in lysozyme activity in both monocytes and other leukocytes. Additionally, exosomes derived from monocytes containing RBPs exhibited significant effects on cytokine gene expression. However, Western blot analysis revealed no viral proteins among the isolated RBPs. Furthermore, there were both similarities and differences between the immune responses of infected dogs and experimentally treated cells with RBPs. Therefore, it is suspected that canine parvovirus (CPV) infection has direct impacts on immune responses and also mediates effects on the expression of RBPs, which could regulate immune responses in infected animals. In some cases, these effects may represent a mechanism by which immune cells mitigate the side effects of the initial inflammatory response during the acute phase of the disease or mediate activation of immune responses. In this context, Zhou et al. (2025) identified 140 proteins that interacted with VP2 in the F81 cell line using immunoprecipitation. Their analysis indicated that these proteins are involved in signaling pathways regulating cellular metabolism [36]. Additionally, Ihalainen et al. (2012) reported that the TATA-binding protein, transcription factor IIB, and poly(A) binding protein nuclear 1 were differentially recovered from virus-infected and non-infected cells [37]. Together with the current study, these findings suggest that protein-protein interactions regulate host immune responses rather than direct interactions of the CPV with host RNAs.

Another study characterized the procoagulant activity of extracellular vesicles [38]. In the current study, the significant effects of exosomes and RBP-containing exosomes were observed on the immune responses and viability of monocytes. These findings were attributed to the efficient uptake of leukocyte-derived exosomes by monocytes and their heightened sensitivity to these biological molecules.

Exosomes derived can transfer viral and host transcripts, proteins, and components of the innate immune system. Viruses may also utilize microvesicles to expand their tropism and evade host immune responses [39]. Similar mechanisms could be involved in the observed effects of exosomes and exosome contained RBPs obtained from healthy dogs. CPV-induced apoptosis in two human cancer cell lines has demonstrated the virus’s potential to control cancer cell growth [40]. The present study confirmed the effects of exosomes and RBPs on immune responses of the WBC and their viability. These finding supporting strategies aimed at neutralizing these molecules to manage immune responses of the infected animals. In this respect, the efficacy of human dialyzable leukocyte extract as an immunomodulator was demonstrated for the treatment of CPV [41]. Based on the regulatory role of the RBPs, these proteins could be candidates for modulating immune responses; however, this process requires complete identification of the extracted RBPs and elucidation of their roles in maintaining host health.

Consistent with the current study, another investigation compared methods for measuring viral infections in parvoviruses by utilizing the MTT assay to evaluate the cytopathic effects of various parvoviruses, including porcine parvovirus (PPV), canine parvovirus (CPV), and murine parvovirus (MVM). The results demonstrated that the effectiveness of the MTT assay correlated well with other methods, such as TCID50, and provided valuable screening for viruses like Sindbis. The MTT assay was effectively adapted to viral systems that produced measurable cytopathic effects [42]. Additionally, a study identified CPV as the primary cause of canine neutrophil myeloperoxidase deficiency [48]. Consistent with this, the current results showed a significant decrease in myeloperoxidase and lysozyme activity in the WBCs of infected animals. However, unlike lysozyme, the decreased activity of myeloperoxidase was not related to the RBPs.

Reyes-Cruz et al. (2021) elucidated the suppression of type I interferon (IFN) by CPV in experimentally infected MDCK cells [44]. The current study confirmed the role of RBPs in infected leukocytes in modulating IFN-γ gene expression. Additionally, Zhou et al. (2025) reported that decreased expression of interferon-stimulated genes resulted from reduced expression of the VP2-interacting protein FHL2 gene, leading to overexpression of CPV [36]. Therefore, activation of NOX2 and IFN-γ gene expression by RBP treatment may represent a compensatory mechanism for controlling CPV infection. IL-4 expression in monocytes is identified as a marker for monocyte differentiation into the M2 phenotype [45, 46]. The increase in IL-4 levels on monocytes after treatment with RBPs was lower than the elevation observed for IFN-γ and NOX2. These factors promote differentiation of more monocytes into the M1 phenotype, initiating immune responses during viral infection.

Furthermore, Wang X. et al. (2025) demonstrated that NS1 mediates a reduction in the phosphorylation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), thereby causing a shutdown of host translation [47]. Wang S. et al. (2025) suggested that combining IL-12 with VP2 vaccination results in enhanced protective immunity in vaccinated puppies [48]. Collectively, these studies highlight the importance of T cell and phagocytic cell differentiation via IFN-γ in inducing effective immune responses against CPV. The current study indicates that monocyte-derived exosome-contained RBPs have activating effects on monocytes and other leukocytes. Additionally, the RBPs exhibit varying effects depending on the source of the vehicle exosomes, which may influence the interactions of the RBPs.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that CPV-infected dogs exhibited lower specific antibody titers, comparable total immunoglobulin levels, and reduced leukocyte lysozyme and myeloperoxidase activities compared to asymptomatic dogs. Gene expression analysis revealed increased expression of IFN-γ and NOX2 in leukocytes treated with exosomes containing RBP. Among the study’s key findings were significant effects of exosomes on lysozyme activity and leukocyte viability. Ultimately, identifying RBPs in healthy and infected animals, a limitation of the current study, and investigating their individual effects on the expression of genes associated with immune responses could pave the way for developing novel and effective therapies to combat viral infections.
