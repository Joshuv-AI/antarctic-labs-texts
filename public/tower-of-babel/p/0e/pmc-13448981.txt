Glucose is a potential source of glutamate for glutamine-deprived pancreatic cancer cells with KRAS mutation
Authors: Zhiwen Xiao, Yiying Li, Nuoyan Zhang, Yang Shi, Yilin Song, Yi Liu, Xinyue Wang, Quanxing Ni, Guopei Luo
Published: 2026

Pancreatic cancer is a highly lethal malignancy, with a five-year survival of only 13% for pancreatic cancer overall and just 8% for the most common form, pancreatic adenocarcinoma [1]. Its aggressive nature is driven by key molecular events. Specifically, KRAS mutations, which occur in over 90% of cases, act as pivotal oncogenic driver [2]. Concurrently, profound metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of pancreatic cancer. Research indicates that a glycolytic switch is activated very early, even in precancerous lesions. This switch is driven by factors such as the transcription factor SP1, which promotes tumorigenesis by activating glycolytic enzymes. This rewired glucose metabolism not only fuels tumor growth but also helps cancer cells evade novel cell death mechanisms such as copper-dependent cuproptosis [ 2– 4] .

Glutamate and glutamine play interconnected and important roles in pancreatic cancer metabolism, with their dysregulation driving tumor progression. Glutamine fuels carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) biosynthesis via the hexosamine pathway, and its blockade suppresses tumor growth and metastasis, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy [5]. Building on the seminal 2013 study by Son et al. [6], which investigated the unique metabolic dependencies of pancreatic cancer, this research focused on glutamine’s role. The study identified a critical, non-canonical glutamine metabolism pathway essential for pancreatic cancer tumor growth, regulated by the oncogenic KRAS mutation—a hallmark of this cancer. A major finding was that pancreatic cancer cells bypass the typical enzyme glutamate dehydrogenase (GLUD1) to channel glutamine into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Instead, they rely on a pathway where glutamine-derived aspartate is processed in the cytoplasm by aspartate transaminase (GOT1). Extracellular glutamine is the primary source for these cells. However, pancreatic tumors are often nutrient deficient. Under glutamine deprivation, the cells may rewire glucose metabolism to generate glutamate, utilizing pathways involving α-ketoglutarate and non-canonical TCA cycle routes [7].

The relationship between glucose metabolism, diabetes, and pancreatic cancer is complex and bidirectional, involving both shared risk factors and direct metabolic consequences [8]. Long-standing type 2 diabetes is a well-established risk factor for pancreatic cancer, with diabetic patients having approximately twice the risk of developing pancreatic cancer compared to non-diabetic individuals. The high glucose environment of diabetes also upregulates the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway, increasing protein O-GlcNAcylation, which is linked to both insulin resistance and KRAS mutations in pancreatic cancer [9]. Conversely, pancreatic cancer itself can induce a unique form of diabetes known as pancreatic cancer-related diabetes mellitus (PDAC-DM) or type 3c diabetes [10]. This distinct entity arises when the malignant tumor disrupts the pancreas’s endocrine function, leading to insulin deficiency and glucose intolerance. In the current study, patients diagnosed with pancreatic adenocarcinoma were selected from a prospectively (forward-lookingly) updated database within a single academic medical center (BioBank of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center) from 2021 to 2025. The criteria for case inclusion were as follows: (1) pathological confirmation of pancreatic adenocarcinoma from surgical specimen or biopsy; (2) adequate clinical information and follow-up data; and (3) information on baseline fasting blood glucose (FBG). Data recorded at the time of diagnosis, encompassing patient demographic information, previous diabetes history, FBG levels, and tumor biomarkers, were extracted. Baseline FBG measurements were taken under conditions where patients had abstained from calorie intake for a minimum of 8 h. Informed consent was obtained from the participants. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (2008222-16). It is noteworthy that only 19.6% of patients had normal fasting glucose (684 cases; diabetes, 50.7%; impaired fasting glucose, 30.7%; Figure 1A). In addition, high fasting blood glucose (≥ 126 mg/dL) was confirmed to be an adverse prognostic factor for patients ( P = 0.028; Figure 1B). In the multivariate Cox regression model, elevated fasting blood glucose remained an adverse prognostic factor (hazard ratio, 1.41, 95% confidence interval, 1.07‒1.86, P = 0.015) when adjusted for age, sex, tumor location, TNM stage, and pre-existing diabetes mellitus. These findings suggest that aberrant blood glucose plays a key role in pancreatic cancer progression. The analysis utilized the TCGA Firehose Legacy cohort ( http://www.cbioportal.org/study/summary?id=paad_tcga, which comprised 170 samples. KRAS mRNA expression levels were stratified using the median value as the cutoff. All continuous variables were analyzed using Student’s t test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. The analysis of glutamate metabolism-related gene expression in pancreatic cancer patients stratified by KRAS expression levels revealed distinct patterns between low and high KRAS expression subgroups. As shown in the heatmap ( Figure 1C), mRNA expression levels of key genes involved in glutamate metabolism—including GPT, GPT2, CIC, SLC7A11, GOT1, GOT2, ACLY, GLS, GLUL, GLUD1, and GLUD2—were visualized, highlighting variability across samples. The violin plot further demonstrated significant expression differences between the two subgroups. Specifically, ACLY, GLUD1, GLUD2, and SLC7A11 were markedly upregulated in the high- KRAS expression group compared to the low- KRAS group ( Figure 1D). These findings suggest that enhanced glutamate metabolism activity, particularly through these genes, may be associated with elevated KRAS expression in pancreatic cancer, potentially influencing tumor progression and metabolic reprogramming.

To trace the metabolic flux of glucose in KRAS-mutated pancreatic cancer cells using isotope tracing untargeted metabolomics, two pancreatic cancer cell lines with the KRAS G12V mutation [CFPAC1, CAPAN, from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, USA)] were cultured in medium deprived of glutamine, as previously described [5]. Cells were cultured for 24 h or 48 h in D-Glucose- 13C6 (4.5 g/L, 110187-42-3; Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, USA). Cells cultured in unlabeled glucose (50-99-7; Sigma-Aldrich) were used as controls. A total of 1 × 10 7 cells were sent for analysis. Samples were prepared as previously described [5]. LC-MS/MS analyses were performed using a UHPLC system (1290; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) with a UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm; Agilent Technologies) coupled to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). An in-house MS2 database (BiotreeDB, Shanghai, China) was applied for metabolite annotation ( Supplementary Table S1). As shown in Figure 2A,B, glucose not only participates in established cellular processes such as the TCA cycle, cell division, and ROS response but also serves as a precursor for the synthesis of UDP-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc) and UDP-N-acetyl-D-galactosamine (UDP-GalNAc), which are essential glycosylation substrates. Additionally, isotopic labeling was observed in proline and 5-oxo-D-proline, compounds structurally linked to collagen and extracellular matrix formation. All these metabolites are closely related to glutamate. Together, glucose is an important source of glutamate for glutamine-deprived pancreatic cancer cells with KRAS mutation, highlighting the dynamic role of glucose in sustaining critical cellular functions in KRAS-driven pancreatic cancer under glutamine limitation. In summary, this study investigates the metabolic adaptations of pancreatic cancer cells with KRAS mutations under glutamine deprivation, revealing glucose as an alternative source of glutamate. Pancreatic cancer, driven by KRAS mutations in > 90% of cases, exhibits profound metabolic reprogramming, including early activation of glycolysis and reliance on non-canonical glutamine metabolism pathways. While extracellular glutamine is the primary source, nutrient-deprived tumor cells rewire glucose metabolism to generate glutamate via α-ketoglutarate and alternative TCA cycle routes, bypassing the typical GLUD1-dependent pathway. Clinically, aberrant glucose metabolism is linked to poor outcomes: only 19.6% of patients had normal fasting glucose, and elevated fasting blood glucose (≥ 126 mg/dL) was independently associated with adverse prognosis. Using isotope tracing (D-glucose- 13C6) in KRAS-mutated cell lines (CFPAC1 and CAPAN), this study demonstrated that glucose-derived carbon flows into glutamate and related metabolites ( e. g., proline, UDP-GalNAc) under glutamine deprivation, supporting tumor growth and metastasis. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting glucose-glutamine metabolic crosstalk in pancreatic cancer.
