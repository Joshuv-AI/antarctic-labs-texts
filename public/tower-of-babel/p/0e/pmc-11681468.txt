Diagnostic Potential of Combined Photoplethysmography and Ankle‐Brachial Index in Peripheral Arterial Disease: A Duplex Ultrasonography‐Based Comparative Study
Authors: Han‐Luen Huang, Chia‐Hsiu Chang, Men‐Tzung Lo, Chen Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough ankle‐brachial index (ABI) and photoplethysmography (PPG) have also shown adequate sensitivity in detecting peripheral arterial disease, their diagnostic performance is less reliable in asymptomatic cases or those with high atherosclerotic cardiovascular risks.Methods and ResultsWe evaluated 130 participants using ABI, PPG, and duplex ultrasonography, diagnosing 65 with peripheral arterial disease. From the PPG, we derived 2 parameters: PPG amplitude ratio of the lower‐to‐upper extremities (PPGratio) and the PPG amplitude of the lower extremity (PPGamp). Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and the area under receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve were calculated for PPG parameters and ABI, and their combination of both methods. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression assessed the prognostic potential of these parameters. ROC analysis revealed optimal cutoff values in diagnosing peripheral arterial disease were 0.417 for PPGratio and “58” for PPGamp. Both PPGratio and PPGamp demonstrated significantly higher sensitivities, 78.4% and 75.7%, respectively, compared with 55.9% for ABI <0.9 (P<0.05). The areas under the ROC curves of combination models, including model 1 (ABI <0.9 and PPGratio), model 2 (ABI <0.9 and PPGamp), and model 3 (ABI <0.9, PPGratio, and PPGamp), exhibited improved performance with areas under the ROC curves of 0.922, 0.922, and 0.931 (all P<0.01) compared with ABI alone (area under the ROC curve, 0.822). Additionally, the PPG parameters, both alone and combined with ABI, were associated with major adverse cardiac events and all‐cause mortality after adjusting for other relevant factors.ConclusionsOn the basis of duplex ultrasonography, combining ABI and PPG markedly improves peripheral arterial disease diagnosis in high‐risk individuals compared with either method alone and provides crucial insights into major adverse cardiac events and all‐cause mortality risks.Keywords: ankle‐brachial index, duplex ultrasonography, peripheral arterial disease, photoplethysmography, toe‐brachial indexSubject Categories: Diagnostic Testing, Prognosis, Ultrasound

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) stands as a significant cause of both morbidity and mortality within the spectrum of atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases. This condition, which encompasses both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients, has garnered considerable attention because of its widespread prevalence. Epidemiologic studies have consistently highlighted key risk factors associated with PAD, including age, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and dyslipidemia. 1 , 2 Although PAD can manifest in symptoms such as intermittent claudication to even tissue loss, it often remains underdiagnosed, primarily because of the presence of asymptomatic cases and the unreliability of physical examination findings. 3 , 4

Early identification of PAD is crucial for prompt treatment and effective risk factor management. For early detection of these patients at high risk of atherosclerosis, the measurement of ankle‐brachial index (ABI) has been widely recommended as a first‐line screening tool. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 A lower ABI, indicating a decreased pressure ratio between ankle and brachial arteries, correlates with higher cardiovascular mortality. 9 , 10 , 11 However, in a meta‐analysis, Xu et al reported in patients with diabetes or advanced age, ABI has a high specificity but lower sensitivity (at best 80%) than that reported in earlier studies because of arterial calcification. 12 In such cases, further assessment using toe‐brachial index (TBI) in asymptomatic patients when ABI>1.3 or a treadmill exercise test in symptomatic patients with normal ABI is advised. 13

Photoplethysmography (PPG) emerges as a promising technique for assessing arterial pulsations in the lower extremities, and detecting perfusion abnormalities attributable to arterial stenosis or obstruction, such as reduced pulse amplitude and delayed or dampened waveforms. Previous studies have demonstrated that PPG exhibits high sensitivity and specificity in detecting PAD when compared with traditional ABI measurements. 14 , 15 ABI and PPG use different mechanisms for PAD detection, yet they share similarities in measurement preparation. We hypothesize that PPG's pulse amplitude direct evaluation of distal perfusion may compensate for ABI's lower diagnostic sensitivity. To date, no study has systematically compared ABI, parameters derived from PPG, and combinations in diagnosing PAD in subjects with high cardiovascular risk, using duplex ultrasonography (DUS) as the reference standard. 16 This study aims to comprehensively assess the diagnostic value of ABI and PPG‐derived parameters in individuals with high cardiovascular risk, based on results obtained from DUS.

METHODS

The PPG data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Participants

This prospective observational study was conducted at the Cardiovascular Center of Hsinchu Cathay General Hospital. Between October 24, 2019, and October 12, 2021, we prospectively enrolled a total of 130 participants, from both the outpatient department and ward, with high atherosclerotic cardiovascular risks or showed suspicion of PAD and had been recommended for ABI evaluation. Patients with uremia or those who cannot undergo PPG examination were excluded. Informed consent was obtained for all participants involved in the study. The examination sequence involved an initial ABI assessment, followed by PPG examination and DUS. Demographic data, including age, sex, height, weight, body mass index, and a comprehensive review of cardiovascular risk factors (such as diabetes with varying durations, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and smoking habits), as well as cardiovascular comorbidities, were extracted from electronic medical records. All the collected data were approved by the institutional review board of the Cathay General Hospital (CGH‐P108042). All the subjects received a 24‐month follow‐up period. Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), including nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke (cerebral infarction, hemorrhagic stroke), coronary and peripheral intervention, coronary or peripheral arterial bypass surgery, amputation surgery, and all‐cause mortality, were recorded from medical records or telephone reviews.

Experimental Design and Setup

Participants were instructed to lie flat on the examination table in a room maintained at a temperature of at least 23 °C for a 5‐minute resting period before undergoing both the ABI and PPG assessments. A single technician conducted both tests using a peripheral vascular diagnosis system (FALCON/PRO, VIASONIX), equipped with an 8‐MHz continuous‐wave Doppler probe and a PPG system. Subsequently, participants underwent DUS using an ultrasound system and a 3‐ to 11‐MHz continuous‐wave Doppler probe (IE33, Phillips, USA), performed by a single cardiologist on the same examination table at room temperature. An infrared heater had been temporarily used to keep the room temperature above 23 °C if the initial room temperature fell below this threshold. The average temperature was 24.4±0.7 °C, with an average humidity of 55.1±4.1%rh (relative humidity) at the end of the PPG measurement.

Ankle‐Brachial Pressure Index Measurement and Photoplethysmography‐Derived Parameters

Automatic measurements of ABI were taken for both brachial arteries, posterior tibial arteries (PTAs), and dorsalis pedis arteries (DPAs). The numerator represented the higher systolic pressure between PTAs and DPAs for each lower extremity, whereas the denominator was determined by the higher of the 2 brachial artery systolic pressures. An abnormal ABI was defined as <0.9.

The PPG system used a transmittance‐type PPG clip to measure light absorption through amplitude differences between an emitter and a photodetector, sampled at 32 Hz. Each clip featured 2 light‐emitting diodes emitting light at wavelengths of 700 and 840 nm. Four PPG clips were attached to the distal extremities of the participants, specifically on both middle fingers and second toes (Figure 1). PPG waveforms from all 4 extremities were simultaneously recorded for a minimum of 5 minutes. Two primary parameters were derived from the PPG waveforms.

Schematic representation of photoplethysmography (PPG) system implementation for PPG signal acquisition and analysis.

Four PPG sensors were affixed to the distal extremities of the study participants, specifically positioned on both middle fingers and second toes. Continuous PPG waveforms were simultaneously recorded from all 4 extremities for a minimum duration of 5 minutes. Subsequently, 2 key parameters were extracted from the averaged beat waveforms by aligning the PPG peaks in each location. PPG amplitude is the amplitude of each lower extremity (eg, ARL) and PPG amplitude ratio is calculated by the ratio of the amplitude of each lower extremity to the greater amplitude of both upper extremities (eg, ARL/ARH).

PPG Amplitude Ratio (PPGratio )

This parameter was calculated as the ratio of the averaged 5‐minute beat‐by‐beat pulse amplitude of each lower extremity to the greater averaged 5‐minute beat‐by‐beat pulse amplitude of both upper extremities.

PPG Amplitude (PPGamp )

This parameter was determined as the averaged 5‐minute beat‐by‐beat pulse amplitude of each lower extremity.

Peripheral Arterial Disease Diagnosis via Duplex Ultrasonography

For vascular assessment, B‐mode morphology and continuous‐wave Doppler measurements were conducted bilaterally on the following arteries: bilateral radial artery, ulnar artery, external iliac artery, common femoral artery, superficial femoral artery, deep femoral artery, popliteal artery, anterior tibial artery (ATA), DPA, peroneal artery, PTA, and medial artery, and lateral plantar artery. Duplex sonography findings were interpreted independently by 2 cardiologists, both of whom adhered to the University of Washington Duplex criteria. An agreement was reached between the 2 cardiologists. Only lesions classified as 50% to 99% stenotic or total occlusion were considered indicative of PAD.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean±SD for continuous variables and as proportions for categorical variables. The performance of diagnostic tests for PAD was assessed in terms of accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. 17 To facilitate the comparison of various diagnostic tests, the 95% CIs for sensitivity and specificity were computed using the Wilson score method without continuity correction. Sensitivity analyses of the ABI and PPG‐derived parameters were performed, with subgrouping based on the significant risk factors. Optimal diagnostic cut points were determined using the Youden Index from ROC curves. To evaluate the discriminative ability of these models, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was computed. ROC curves for various parameters and multivariable models were compared using the DeLong method. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate the potential of the PPG‐derived parameters as prognostic predictors for MACEs and all‐cause mortality. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 26; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and R software (Version 3.5.0).

RESULTS

Participant Demographics

Between October 24, 2019, and October 12, 2021, we enrolled 130 participants, aged 40 to 85 years, in the study. The cohort comprised 92 men and 38 women, representing 259 lower limbs; 1 case involving a prosthetic implant was excluded. Table 1 summarizes the demographics of the study population. The mean±SD age of participants was 68.1±9.9 years. Participants exhibited a range of risk factors and comorbidities: 76% had diabetes, 75% had hypertension, 80% had dyslipidemia, 25% were smokers, 32% had coronary artery disease, and 5% had experienced a cerebral vascular accident. Additionally, 36% of participants had been living with diabetes for >10 years, and 15% were at the advanced stages (stages 3b–4) of chronic kidney disease (CKD). During the follow‐up period, MACEs and all‐cause mortality were recorded in 38 subjects. Of these, 3 experienced a myocardial infarction, 2 experienced a cerebral infarction, and 1 had a hemorrhagic stroke. Additionally, 26 patients had previously undergone coronary or peripheral interventions. Four patients received an amputation. Furthermore, 1 patient died because of a liver abscess, and another succumbed to complications related to lung cancer.

Assessing the Diagnostic Performance of PPG‐Derived Parameters Compared With ABI

Of the 130 subjects in the study, 65 were diagnosed with PAD. Among the 259 lower extremities examined, 111 were diagnosed with PAD by duplex sonography. Specifically, 11 cases (10%) involved above‐knee arteries (4 external iliac arteries and/or common femoral arteries; 7 superficial femoral arteries and/or popliteal arteries), 79 cases (71%) involved below‐knee arteries (37 ATAs, 10 PTA, and 32 both ATAs and PTA), and 21 cases (19%) involved both above‐knee and below‐knee arteries (Figure 2). Among the 47 single stenotic/occluded arteries (37 ATAs and 10 PTA), 34 were fully occluded with collateral flow, whereas the remaining 13 exhibited 50% to 99% stenotic.

Both PPGratio and PPGamp with the optimal thresholds (PPGratio<0.417 and PPGamp<58) demonstrated higher sensitivity levels, 78.4% (95% CI, 70.3%–85.6%; P<0.05) and 75.7% (95% CI, 67.6%–82.9%; P<0.05), in comparison to ABI <0.9, which had a sensitivity of 55.9% (95% CI, 46.6%–65.8%), whereas the specificity of PPGamp (92.6% [95% CI, 87.8%–96.6%]) was not different from ABI <0.9 (98.7% [95% CI, 96.6%–100%]). When examining below‐knee levels with single‐artery stenosis/occlusion, ABI demonstrated notably low sensitivity (19.1%) compared with PPGratio (68.1%) and PPGamp (55.3%). Using Combined Models of ABI and PPG‐Derived Parameters for Diagnosing and Prognosticating PAD

The combination models, including model 1 (ABI <0.9 and PPGratio), model 2 (ABI <0.9 and PPGamp), and model 3 (ABI <0.9, PPGratio, and PPGamp), exhibited improved performance compared with using ABI alone (AUC, 0.82), achieving AUC values of 0.922, 0.922, and 0.931, respectively, compared with an AUC of 0.82 for ABI alone (DeLong test P<0.01) (Figure 3B). The sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy after the combination were 81.1% (95% CI, 73.9%–88.3%), 92.6% (95% CI, 88.6%–96.7%), and 87.6% (95% CI, 83.8%–91.5%) for model 1, 82.0% (95% CI, 74.8%–89.2%), 89.2% (95% CI, 84.5%–93.9%), and 86.1% (95% CI, 81.9%–90.0%) for model 2, and 82.9% (95% CI, 75.7%–89.2%), 95.3% (95% CI, 91.9%–98.7%), and 90.0% (95% CI, 86.1%–93.4%) for model 3 (Figure 4B). The sensitivity and accuracy of model 3 were significantly higher compared with ABI <0.9 alone (P<0.05). To perform logistic analysis on a subject basis, ABI and PPG‐derived parameters were selected from the limb with the lower ABI in subjects diagnosed with PAD in both lower limbs. The univariate logistic analyses revealed that an abnormal ABI (ABI <0.9), a PPGratio <0.417, and a PPGamp <58 were associated with an increased risk of adverse events. Given that only 38 patients experienced adverse events, we conducted 2 multivariable logistic analyses: model A adjusted for diabetes and smoking history, and model B adjusted for age and hypertension, as shown in Table 3. The associations remained significant in both multivariable models. Additionally, the parameters combining ABI and PPG‐derived metrics were also significantly associated with MACEs and all‐cause mortality.

DISCUSSION

This study systematically examined the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of the ABI, parameters derived from PPG, and their combinations in diagnosing PAD based on the results of DUS. First, we found that ABI's sensitivity was notably lower at the below‐knee level, especially in cases of single stenotic or occluded ATAs or PTA. Second, PPG demonstrated higher sensitivity compared with ABI, particularly in individuals with a high atherosclerotic risk profile. Third, combining ABI and PPG parameters proved to be a simple yet effective method for enhancing sensitivity and accuracy in diagnosing PAD in both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients, also showing potential related to prognosis.

The Influence of PAD Locations on ABI Sensitivity

The ABI demonstrated variability depending on the level of stenosis or occlusion. Specifically, its sensitivity was notably lower at below‐knee sites compared with above‐knee sites in our study. When cases at these sites were excluded from the analysis, the overall sensitivity of ABI in the remaining 64 stenotic or occluded arteries increased substantially, from 55.9% to 83.1%, as confirmed by DUS. This variability is linked to the traditional method of ABI calculation, which uses the higher systolic pressures from either the DPA or PTA and the brachial arteries as the numerator and denominator, respectively. Although this method is highly specific for diagnosing PAD, Schröder et al suggested using the lower systolic pressures from the lower extremities as the numerator to counteract the overestimation of lower limb pressure caused by medial calcinosis in the PTA or DPA. This adjustment increased the sensitivity of ABI detection from 68% to 89%, although specificity slightly decreased from 99% to 93%. 13 , 18 In our study, 34 cases of fully occluded single ATA or PTA exhibited collateral flow and reduced pressures. We used the traditional ABI method, which selects the ‘higher systolic pressure’ from the PTA or DPA as the numerator. This approach may preferentially represent the healthier vessel and contribute to underdiagnosing occluded vessels with significantly lower pressures, thereby reducing the sensitivity of ABI measurements. PPG‐derived parameters, such as PPGratio and PPGamp, could serve as supplementary tools. Their use may enhance diagnostic sensitivity at below‐knee occlusion sites or in cases involving single PTA or ATA by more accurately capturing variations in blood flow and pressure that are less affected by arterial calcification, thus providing a more precise evaluation of vascular health in these complex scenarios.

Improvement of Diagnostic Sensitivity With PPG in Patients at High Atherosclerotic Risk

The prevalence of disease or associated risk factors can significantly influence the sensitivity of diagnostic tools. In specific cases, such as individuals with diabetes, advanced CKD, advanced age, or those on long‐term immunosuppressive therapy, the sensitivity of the ABI is compromised primarily because of medial sclerosis. In a comprehensive review by Xu et al on the sensitivity and specificity of ABI, they reported high specificity (83.3%–99.0%) and accuracy (72.1%–89.2%) when ABI was <0.9 in PAD. However, they noted relatively low sensitivity (15%–79%) in patients of advanced age or those with diabetes. 12 For patients with symptoms or high atherosclerotic risk, measuring great toe pressure and calculating the TBI is recommended, particularly when the ABI exceeds 1.3 or when ankle arteries are incompressible. 12 , 13 However, the threshold value of 0.7 for TBI is not well supported scientifically. 19 Additionally, TBI mainly assesses perfusion from the PTA and medial plantar artery and does not directly evaluate circulation in the lateral forefoot, which involves the DPA and ATA. In our study, the subjects had an average age of 68.1±9.9 years, with 78% experiencing diabetes. Notably, both the PPGraio and PPGamp demonstrated superior overall sensitivity and accuracy compared with ABI under these conditions. Furthermore, PPG parameters exhibited enhanced sensitivity in subjects with other atherosclerotic risk factors, such as being >65 years old, having hypertension, having advanced CKD, and smoking. However, it is important to emphasize that ABI still maintained high overall specificity.

Potential Implications of PPG‐Derived Parameters as a Complementary Tool to ABI

In a study by Allen et al, they reported diagnostic parameters for PPG using the shape index method, with an 88.9% sensitivity, 90.6% specificity, and 90.2% accuracy in diagnosing PAD. Notably, their study referenced the established ABI measurement results. 14 , 15 In contrast, our PPG study was conducted with reference to DUS results, enabling us to directly compare the diagnostic performance of PPG with that of ABI. The results from our study suggest that PPG can significantly enhance the sensitivity of ABI, particularly in cases involving below‐knee lesions and individuals with atherosclerotic risk factors. The treadmill exercise test combined with ABI is also recommended for symptomatic patients with normal ABI. However, arranging and performing this test is time‐consuming, and certain contraindications restrict its use. 20 , 21 The advantage of PPG lies in its ability to be performed nearly simultaneously with ABI assessment using PPG sensors, as a simple, cost‐effective, and noninvasive optical tool. In addition to traditional methods such as TBI or the treadmill exercise test, the combination of ABI and PPG offers a viable alternative for the early detection of PAD, especially in patients at high risk of atherosclerosis. When combining either PPGratio or PPGamp with ABI, our study demonstrated improved sensitivity and accuracy compared with relying solely on PPG or ABI. Furthermore, an abnormal ABI (ABI <0.9) has been proposed as a predictor of MACEs and all‐cause mortality in patients with coronary artery disease or diabetes. 22 , 23 The parameters derived from PPG have demonstrated their potential to serve as independent prognostic predictors. The odds ratio for predicting adverse outcomes increases when combining both ABI and PPG parameters.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, it excluded the subjects with CKD stage V in uremic status who were known to have Mönckeberg medial sclerosis. 24 Second, although the study confirmed that the combination of ABI and PPG can increase the sensitivity and accuracy in the diagnosis of PAD and has potential prognostic value for MACEs and all‐cause mortality, the sample size was relatively small, comprising 130 subjects with 259 lower extremities. Further comprehensive studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to evaluate the reproducibility of the ABI and PPG combination and its association with MACEs or all‐cause mortality. Additionally, the instrument used for measuring ABI in this study recorded only the highest systolic pressure between PTA and DPA as the numerator. This method prevented us from assessing modified ABI as per the approach outlined by Schröder et al. Further investigation is needed to explore the potential synergy between PPG and modified ABI.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrated that ABI has a lower sensitivity compared with PPG in detecting PAD in participants with a high risk of atherosclerosis or below‐knee single stenotic/occluded artery. On the basis of the results of DUS, the combination of ABI and PPG could significantly enhance both the sensitivity and the accuracy of diagnosing PAD over either method alone. For patients with high atherosclerotic risks, such as those aged >65 years, smokers, and those with hypertension, diabetes, and advanced CKD, the combination of ABI and PPG could offer a simple, alternative screening method. It also holds potential diagnostic value for MACEs and all‐cause mortality, warranting further investigation.
