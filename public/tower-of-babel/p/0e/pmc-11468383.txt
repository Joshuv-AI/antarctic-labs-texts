Lemierre’s syndrome associated-diabetic ketoacidosis in an elderly female: a case report
Authors: Yueguo Wang, Yuanzhi Bie, Xiaoyuan Wang, Shuang Li, Yu Cha, Lei Shen, Chunyan Wang, Erchao Ji, Rumeng Zhang, Xiancong Wang, Yu Huang, Shusheng Zhou, Kui Jin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe co-occurrence of Lemierre’s syndrome, primarily triggered by Fusobacterium necrophorum following oropharyngeal infection, with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) in diabetes mellitus (DM) patients, underscores a rare but life-threatening clinical scenario. Lemierre’s syndrome induced DKA is extremely rare, with only one case report in adult and no case yet reported in elderly.Case PresentationWe reported a case of a 69-year-old female who presented with DKA triggered by deep neck space infection (DNSI), leading to rapid clinical deterioration within 6 h that necessitated high flow nasal cannula (HFNC) and antibiotic administration. Laboratory findings included leukocytosis, elevated serum C-reactive protein, hyperglycemia, ketonemia, and severe metabolic acidosis. Culture of the fluid from a neck mass puncture drainage and blood were positive for Klebsiella pneumoniae. The patient was further complicated by thrombosis of the left internal jugular vein with extension to the sigmoid and a neck abscess surrounding the carotid artery sheath, consistent with Lemierre’s syndrome. This condition was managed aggressively with fluid resuscitation, insulin therapy, surgical drainage, antibiotics, and anticoagulation led to a significant improvement in her condition. Following a 13-day hospitalization, there was significant clinical improvement, culminating in the patient’s discharge.ConclusionsThe case highlights the need for greater awareness and understanding of the interrelated and mutually promoting conditions of DKA and Lemierre’s syndrome among clinicians. Early recognition and treatment are crucial to prevent mortality in such complex cases.

Background

Lemierre’s syndrome is a rare yet severe infection typically originating from the oropharynx and characterized by thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein and metastatic infections, predominantly caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum [1]. It was systematically described by the French microbiologist André Lemierre in The Lancet in 1936 [2]. Primarily affecting young and otherwise healthy individuals [3], it can lead to various systemic complications if not promptly diagnosed and treated [4]. Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) represents the most feared acute presentation among patients with DM, irrespective of type [5, 6]. Conventionally, common precipitating factors such as infection and nonadherence to insulin therapy significantly elevate the risk of DKA [7]. However, the co-occurrence of Lemierre’s syndrome with DKA presents a unique clinical scenario, emphasizing the complexity of managing patients with severe infections in the context of DM.

Deep neck space infection refers to the infection of potential spaces in the neck, particularly around the fascial planes, presenting as either abscess or cellulitis [8]. It commonly stems from infections of the mandibular teeth, salivary glands, tonsils, and upper respiratory tract [9]. Left untreated, DNSI can result in serious complications such as airway obstruction and disseminated infection [10]. To date, only one case of Lemierre’s syndrome involving DNSI caused by Streptococcus anginosus has been reported in the literature [8]. Herein, we present a patient with concurrent Lemierre’s syndrome and DKA, featuring an atypical finding of an isolated deep neck abscess.

Case presentation

A 69-year-old female was admitted to the emergency department on March 22, 2024, presenting with sore throat, left-sided neck swelling, pain, and fever persisting for 4 days, accompanied by nausea and tachypnea over the past half day. Initially, she experienced onset of neck swelling and pain on the left side, treated externally with a blood-activating analgesic patch. Despite treatment, her condition deteriorated, marked by a high fever peaking at 38.8 °C, deep and labored breathing, and nausea, without signs of dizziness, headache, cough, abdominal pain or other discomforts. At a local clinic, her treatment excluded blood routine, glucose, and serum electrolyte measurements. Following approximately 5 h of fluid replacement, antimicrobial, and gastroprotection therapy without symptom improvement, she was transferred to the emergency department of our institution. She was hypoxic to 85% on room air. A rapid blood glucose test indicated a level of 30.8 mmol/L, shortness of breath (37 breaths per minute), and severe metabolic acidosis leading to her admission into the emergency intensive care unit (EICU) for critical care. Since symptom onset, the patient has experienced reduced mental clarity, appetite, and urine output. Further medical history revealed a six-year diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), managed with metformin hydrochloride sustained-release tablets (500 mg, daily) and glipizide tablets (5 mg, twice daily), though blood sugar was not monitored regularly. Consultations with nephrology and ophthalmology specialists were conducted to exclude diabetic kidney disease (DKD) and diabetic retinopathy. She also has been prescribed medication for hypertension (amlodipine besylate, 5 mg, daily) for last ten years. Additionally, the patient denied using tobacco, alcohol, or recreational drugs.

Physical examination on admission

The patient exhibited mild fever with a body temperature of 37.6 °C, an elevated pulse rate of 124 beats per minute (bpm), a respiratory rate of 25 breaths per minute, and a blood pressure of 132/74 mmHg. She received HFNC administration (flow rate 40 L/min and oxygen concentration 40%) with a normal oxygen saturation of 97% upon admission to the EICU. And clinical observations included deep and significant respirations and dry skin. The patient was drowsiness, with no jaundice observed in the skin or mucous membranes. Both pupils were equal in size and shape, each with a diameter of 3.0 mm, and responded sensitively to light. A firm, warm-to-the-touch mass approximately 10 cm×6 cm in size was palpable from beneath the left mastoid process, tender upon palpation, and with clear demarcation from surrounding tissues (Fig. 4A). The patient had sinus tachycardia at 124 bpm, with robust heart sounds and no audible murmurs. Auscultation revealed coarse breath sounds in both lungs without rales. There were no abnormal findings in abdomen auscultation, no enlargement of the liver or spleen, and no neurological deficits were detected. The Ear-Nose-Throat (ENT) examination diagnosed a deep neck space infection.

Approach to Diagnosis: The patient, with a history of T2DM, presented with sore throat, left-sided neck swelling, pain, and a persistent fever lasting four days, following three days of excessive sunflower seed consumption. These symptoms raised the suspicion of a possible oropharyngeal infection. To evaluate her condition, we conducted a thorough assessment, including blood routine tests, infection index, biochemistry, coagulation, and arterial blood gas. Physical examination revealed a palpable neck mass, which warranted further imaging with ultrasound and MRI of the head and neck. Given the persistent fever and the need to identify the infectious etiology, we performed aspiration of the neck mass and obtained blood cultures. These diagnostic measures facilitated a comprehensive understanding of the disease’s complexity, enabling us to establish an accurate diagnosis and implement an effective treatment plan.

Laboratory Examinations: Blood tests showed marked inflammation with a white blood cell count of 24.85 × 10^9/L, predominated by neutrophils at 94.2%, with an exceptionally high C-reactive protein level of 216 mg/L, procalcitonin was elevated at 51.063 ng/ml, and interleukin-6 at 1231.90 pg/ml. Renal function tests revealed acute renal injury with urea nitrogen at 11.60 mmol/L, creatinine at 159.40 µmol/L, and uric acid at 550.10 µmol/L. The patient’s rapid blood glucose level was significantly elevated at 31.94 mmol/L. Glycated hemoglobin was 11.6%, indicating poorly controlled diabetes. An arterial blood gas analysis showed severe metabolic acidosis (AG Elevated Metabolic Acidosis Respiratory Compensation) with a pH of 6.987, carbon dioxide of partial pressure (PaCO2) at 9.6 mmHg, and oxygen partial pressure (PaO2) at 160.0 mmHg, base excess (BE) was − 27.3 mmol/L, bicarbonate (HCO3-) at 2.3 mmol/L.The anion gap (AG) was 30.0 mmol/L, and the serum lactate level was 1.6 mmol/L. Blood ketone body tested positive and urine analysis revealed a dark yellow cloudy appearance with significant ketonuria (3 + ketone bodies) and glycosuria (2 + levels of glucose). Electrolytes were noted as follows: potassium 4.0 mmol/L, sodium 131.0 mmol/L, and chloride 99.0 mmol/L. In addition, cardiac function profiles showed N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and myohemoglobin levels were elevated at 2960 pg/ml and 341.00 µg/L, respectively. Whereas cardiac troponin I, liver, and thyroid function tests were within normal ranges. In addition, despite all three sputum specimen cultures collected grew no organisms, cultures from neck mass puncture drainage under bedside ultrasound guidance and blood were positive for Klebsiella pneumoniae. Imaging Findings: The computed tomography (CT) scan of the neck showed left neck swelling with emphysema and enlargement of the left submandibular lymph nodes (Fig. 1A). Ultrasound scan of neck showed thickness of the left sternocleidomastoid muscle (Fig. 1C) and segmental partial thrombosis of the left internal jugular vein (Fig. 1D, E). The head-neck magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan with contrast showed that thrombus of left sigmoid sinus and internal jugular vein (Fig. 2A, B), with pharyngomaxillary space soft tissue infection extending to the deep neck space on the left side of the neck, with extensive phlegmon and abscess (Fig. 2C-F). And the CT scan of the thorax revealed pulmonary nodular lesions (Fig. 3A), multiple patchy infiltrations in both lungs (Fig. 3B, C) and bilateral small pleural effusion (Fig. 3D). Besides, abdominal ultrasound showed no abnormalities, and the bedside transthoracic echocardiogram showed no images suggestive of vegetation.

Treatment Process and Prognosis: Upon admission, the patient’s dehydration severity was promptly assessed, along with vital signs, blood glucose, consciousness level, pupil reaction, and urine output. A fasting regimen was instituted, alongside fluid resuscitation. To manage blood glucose, small doses of insulin (0.05–0.1 U/kg/h) were administered via intravenous drip, with hourly monitoring to ensure a glucose reduction rate of 3.9–6.1 mmol/L per hour, adjusting the drip rate based on glucose levels and urine output.

In treating Lemierre’s syndrome, after blood and neck mass puncture drainage samples were drawn for microbiological analysis, the patient received empirical intravenous antimicrobial therapy with cefuroxime sodium (3.0 g/day) and metronidazole (1.5 g/day). Additionally, enoxaparin, a low-molecular-weight heparin, was used for anticoagulation, complemented by nutritional support and efforts to maintain organ function for comprehensive care. By the second day, the patient’s consciousness clarity improved, acidosis was corrected, and inflammatory markers (white blood cell count, C-reactive protein, and procalcitonin) were reduced, along with blood and urine ketones, normalized.

On day 3, she experienced a high fever (39.5 °C) in the morning. Considering the ineffectiveness of prior antibacterial regimens, antibiotic therapy was escalated to include penicillin sodium (1.6 million units/day) and imipenem-cilastatin sodium (6.0 g/day) intravenously. Simultaneously, surgical intervention for incision and drainage of the left neck parapharyngeal space was performed under general anesthesia. Intraoperative found a large amount of purulent secretes and necrotic tissue on the deep surface of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, ranging from the anterior to the posterior hyoid bone to the cervical spine, up to the parapharyngeal space, and down to the middle of the sternocleidomastoid muscle (Fig. 4B, C). By the fourth day, the patient’s oxygen demand decreased and temperature normalized, she was subsequently transferred to the ENT ward. Subsequently, A neck CT scan re-examination on day 8 indicated significant improvement.

The patient, diagnosed with Lemierre’s syndrome, severe diabetic ketoacidosis, type 2 diabetes, necrotizing fasciitis, and systemic inflammatory response syndrome, was discharged on day 13. She was prescribed cefdinir (0.1 g, three times daily) and levofloxacin (0.5 g, daily) for 14 additional days and rivaroxaban (20 mg, daily) for 30 days. Home therapy included ongoing oral antidiabetic and antihypertensive medications, with regular follow-up appointments scheduled.

Discussion and conclusions

The concurrent presentation of DKA and Lemierre’s syndrome in this patient delineates a rare yet critically significant medical condition necessitating immediate and comprehensive intervention strategies. The uniqueness of this case is heightened by the patient’s advanced age and the induction of DKA by Lemierre’s syndrome, an extraordinarily uncommon event that significantly contributes to the medical knowledge base on managing intricate comorbid conditions. Taken together, this case highlights the urgent need for heightened clinical vigilance for Lemierre’s syndrome in scenarios presenting severe infection and thrombosis, irrespective of patient age or concurrent DKA.

The most common etiological agent in Lemierre’s syndrome is Fusobacterium necrophorum [11], an anaerobic commensal gram-negative bacteria of the oral flora, with Klebsiella pneumoniae being a relatively rare pathogen [12], often found in patients with poor glycemic control [13]. In this case, Klebsiella pneumoniae was identified in blood and neck mass puncture drainage samples taken immediately after admission, although multiple sputum cultures yielded negative results. As the patient received high-flow oxygen therapy and did not undergo mechanical ventilation, her inability to tolerate a bronchoscopy due to a sore throat prevented the acquisition of qualified sputum specimens.

Lemierre’s syndrome is a rare disease entity caused by bacterial oropharyngeal infection, often leading to thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein and pulmonary septic emboli as the commonest presentation of disseminated infection [14–16]. In a study by Riordan et al.. showed pulmonary involvement in 92% of cases [17], suggesting that septic thrombosis of the internal jugular vein can lead to complications in remote organs via blood circulation. In our patient, a chest CT scan upon admission revealed multiple inflammatory lesions in both lungs, although multiple sputum cultures were negative for Klebsiella pneumoniae, possibly due to prior administration of broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage and the patient’s inability to tolerate bronchoscopy, which hindered the acquisition of etiological evidence.

Additionally. despite early identification of the etiology and appropriate antibiotic use being fundamental to treatment, surgical drainage of abscesses or debridement of necrotic tissue remains the preferred approach for these patients [3, 18]. Following diagnosis, it is crucial to evaluate whether surgical intervention is necessary through active consultation with other specialists. This underscores the indispensable role of an interdisciplinary approach in managing complex medical cases, integrating medical, surgical, and adjunctive therapies to ensure positive patient outcomes.

In conclusion, this study presents the first documented case of concurrent DKA and Lemierre’s syndrome complicated by DNSI caused by Klebsiella pneumoniae. The comprehensive summary of the diagnosis and treatment of this patient has the potential to enhance clinicians’ understanding of this rare co-occurrence. This case underscores the critical necessity for increased awareness and comprehension among clinicians regarding the interconnected and mutually reinforcing nature of diabetic ketoacidosis and Lemierre’s syndrome, aimed at minimizing instances of misdiagnosis and treatment delays. Besides, the effective management and subsequent recovery of this patient serve as a notable demonstration of the paramount importance of early detection, proactive intervention, and a thorough understanding of the intricate interplay between infectious diseases and metabolic disturbances.
