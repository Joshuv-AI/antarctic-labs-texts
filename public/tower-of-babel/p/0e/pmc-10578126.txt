In silico protein dynamics in the human cytoplasm: Partial folding, misfolding, fold switching, and non‐native interactions
Authors: Premila P. Samuel Russell, Meredith M. Rickard, Mayank Boob, Martin Gruebele, Taras V. Pogorelov
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe examine the influence of cellular interactions in all‐atom models of a section of the Homo sapiens cytoplasm on the early folding events of the three‐helix bundle protein B (PB). While genetically engineered PB is known to fold in dilute water box simulations in three microseconds, the three initially unfolded PB copies in our two cytoplasm models using a similar force field did not reach the native state during 30‐microsecond simulations. We did however capture the formation of all three helices in a compact native‐like topology. Folding in vivo is delayed because intramolecular contact formation within PB is in direct competition with intermolecular contacts between PB and surrounding macromolecules. In extreme cases, intermolecular beta‐sheets are formed. Interactions with other macromolecules are also observed to promote structure formation, for example when a PB helix in our simulations is shielded from solvent by macromolecular crowding. Sticking and crowding in our models initiate sampling of helix/sheet structural plasticity of PB. Relatedly, in past in vitro experiments, similar GA domains were shown to switch between two different folds. Finally, we also observed that stickiness between PB and the cellular environment can be modulated in our simulations through the reduction in protein hydrophobicity when we reversed PB back to the wild‐type sequence. This study demonstrates that even fast‐folding proteins can get stuck in non‐native states in the cell, making them useful models for protein–chaperone interactions and early stages of aggregate formation relevant to cellular disease.

INTRODUCTION

Experimental, theoretical, and computational studies done over the years have established that the information necessary for spontaneous protein (re)folding in vitro under dilute water conditions is generally stored in the primary sequence of proteins, although folding efficiency rapidly decreases due to a rougher energy landscape as protein size grows (Chen et al., 2020; Dill et al., 2008). Interactions of an unfolded polypeptide with its surrounding cellular environment however can open up new pathways that involve multiple factors: misfolding, chaperones, co‐translational folding, redistribution of population among accessible states, and fold switching (Ellis, 2007; Ellis & Hartl, 1999; Hingorani & Gierasch, 2014; Hong & Gierasch, 2010; Kim & Porter, 2021; Rickard et al., 2020; Samuel et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2016). In our previous multi‐microsecond E. coli cytoplasm simulation study of the monomeric β‐sheet WW domain of human origin, we observed its trapping in misfolded states, partially folded intermediates, and transient heterogenous intermolecular interactions, driven by E. coli cytoplasm protein–protein sticking that was exacerbated by force field effects, especially with the CHARMM22* force field (Rickard et al., 2020).

In another extensive molecular dynamics (MD) simulation in vitro study under dilute water conditions (Lindorff‐Larsen et al., 2011), Shaw and co‐workers used the CHARMM22* force field and TIP3P water model to study folding dynamics of the fast‐folding (Lindorff‐Larsen et al., 2011; Takada, 2001; Wang et al., 2004) small three‐helix bundle protein B (PB) (Cramer et al., 2007; Johansson et al., 1995; Johansson et al., 1997; Lejon et al., 2004), which was seen to fold to its native state rapidly (3.9 ± 0.9 μs) with 19 reversible folding and unfolding events across previously unreachable 104 μs trajectory at 340 K in constant‐volume, constant‐temperature (NVT) ensemble. In agreement, in vitro experimental studies showed that this PB variant has a maximum folding rate constant of ~1 μs−1, while the experimental melting temperature (Wang et al., 2004) of 372 K was higher than the predicted melting temperature of 315 K extrapolated from the simulations (Lindorff‐Larsen et al., 2011). The latter can be used a guidance for selecting temperature of simulations. The PB variant examined in both studies contained buried Lys11Ile/Lys45Val mutations that was introduced to increase folding speed through increased protein hydrophobicity.

In this study, we assess for comparison how a simulated Homo sapiens cellular environment influences the folding of a PB variant, containing the Lys11Ile/Lys45Val mutations. The 47‐residue monomeric PB protein construct (sequence in Methods) originates from the Finegoldia magna (previously Peptostreptococcus magnus) bacterial sequence of an albumin‐binding specific GA domain of the bacterial surface PAB protein (UNIPROT ID Q51911) (Johansson et al., 1995; Johansson et al., 1997). The virulence of the pathogenic F. magna has been traced to its high‐binding affinity via PAB protein to the acellular human serum albumin protein (de Château et al., 1996). We placed three unfolded copies of PB within each of two all‐atom cytoplasm models of the human U‐2 OS cell line (Figure 1a), named “Cell1” and “Cell2” (Methods). During our extensive MD simulations (Samuel Russell et al., 2022) (~31.2 μs for Cell1 and ~ 32.6 μs for Cell2) using the special‐purpose supercomputer (Shaw et al., 2014) Anton 2, and the proteins parameterized with CHARMM36m force field and a modified TIP3P water model, we captured a range of folding‐related events for PB in the cytoplasm model, including reaching a native‐like compact state (Figures 1b and 2c,f; Figures S1, and S2F). Interestingly, both folding and misfolding were induced by PB interactions with surrounding macromolecules, present at concentrations of 260 mg/mL (Figures 1b and 3, 4, 5, 6). We observed that unfolded PB visits alternative folds of β‐strands (Figures 1b, 2, and 6; Figures S2 and S3) compared to past water box simulations (Boob et al., 2023; Lindorff‐Larsen et al., 2011), due to the in‐cell environment increasing access to different structural states such as through seeding of non‐native β‐strands at intermolecular contact formation sites (Figure 6; Figure S5). Both the bacterial PB and human WW domain were simulated within non‐native human and bacterial model cytoplasm environments (Rickard et al., 2020), respectively. These perhaps could lead to non‐native cellular interactions that disrupt the normal folding of these proteins—in accordance with what Oliveberg, Danielsson, and colleagues had shown using in‐cell NMR—as human proteins are unnaturally stickier when placed in bacterial cells (Mu et al., 2017). We also examined wild type PB (wtPB, without the Lys11Ile/Lys45Val mutations) folding in our cell model and observed that protein stickiness, despite being somewhat overestimated by force field effects (Rickard et al., 2020), can be modulated in our cytoplasm model by a decrease in protein mean hydrophobicity though a full folding event was still not observed (Figure 7; Figures S6 and S7).

Furthermore, in cytoplasm, specialized protein folding machineries called molecular chaperones are present to bind and isolate unfolded/partially unfolded proteins thus aiding protein folding, preventing protein misfolding, and assisting in protein quality control (Baumketner et al., 2003; Ellis, 2007; Fink, 1999; Wentink et al., 2019). In both, the present human cytoplasm models and past bacterial cytoplasm models, we had not placed such folding chaperones in the models, mainly due to size limitations. In the absence of chaperones, in‐cell folding landscape of the WW domain and PB is rougher due to crowding‐driven attractive forces acting on the unfolded protein's exposed sticky hydrophobic patches, normally buried away in the folded protein. The absence of folding chaperones in our models might have further contributed to the unfolded proteins not reaching their native state within the sampling time of our simulations.

In a word on force field selection, for atomistic simulations of both unfolded and intrinsically disordered proteins in crowded heterogenous environments, either selecting or modifying one of the current available modern force fields is a somewhat tricky problem that lies at the heart of ongoing force field optimization efforts—finding a fine balance across these three major aspects relative to experiments—maintaining protein stability, not over‐compactifying/overestimating foldability of disordered protein states, and reducing non‐specific protein–protein interactions (Yoo & Aksimentiev, 2018). In recent force field developments, dampened protein stickiness with macromolecules was obtained either at the cost of increased protein instability (Matsubara et al., 2022; Yoo & Aksimentiev, 2018) or over‐compactification of disordered states (Piana et al., 2020; Yoo & Aksimentiev, 2018). Increased accuracy in structural characterizations of disordered protein conformers meanwhile were seen at the cost of underestimation of protein–protein/protein‐RNA interactions (Piana et al., 2020; Robustelli et al., 2018; Yoo & Aksimentiev, 2018). As a result, for present simulations we selected, a protein force field that has been optimized for more accurate structural characterization of disordered proteins while maintaining stability of folded conformers (Huang et al., 2017)—CHARMM36m, together with a modified TIP3P water model. We selected CHARMM36m due to both its transferability and especially for the following reasons. In our past bacterial cytoplasm simulations, we observed the CHARMM36m force field to result in the least protein–protein stickiness, even though stickiness was still somewhat over‐estimated (Rickard et al., 2020). In our past bacterial models, we had also successfully benchmarked CHARMM36m together with the following complementary force fields—Beglov and Roux's parameters (Beglov & Roux, 1994) for inorganic ions, CHARMM36 additives for nucleic acids and lipids, and CGENFF (Vanommeslaeghe et al., 2010) for metabolites. With the presence of high concentrations of different metabolites (175 mM) and inorganic ions (190 mM) in our U‐2 OS cytoplasm as well as a tRNA macromolecule, we had to take into account how direct and indirect interactions between proteins and non‐protein components can significantly influence dynamics, effective charges, and stabilities of each other (Boob et al., 2023; Rickard et al., 2019; Rickard et al., 2022).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Folding of protein B in the model cytoplasm

Within each of our two U‐2 OS cytoplasm models, we simulated (see Methods for simulation setup details) three copies of a PB variant with the Lys11Ile/Lys45Val mutations, which were also present in the variant used by Shaw and coworkers (Lindorff‐Larsen et al., 2011). In addition, our sequence includes a tryptophan residue which could be used as a marker for future fluorescence‐based experiments (see Methods for full sequence). Our two U‐2 OS cytoplasm models (see Methods) were simulated at 318 K, consistent with the upper range of U‐2 OS cell viability. We further intentionally chose a higher temperature to increase sampling of protein dynamics and to decrease protein–protein non‐specific interactions. In our previous study of PB (Boob et al., 2023) (also with Lys11Ile/Lys45Val mutations), where we addressed influence of solvent, PB remained stably folded in water for the duration of 16 μs at 340 K, well above the temperature used here.

While all three unfolded PB copies in the present study sample a wide range of dynamic conformations over a total of ~63.8 μs (Figures 1, 2, 3, and 6; Figures S1–S3), PB does not undergo a complete folding event. However, at ~7 μs into the simulation of Cell2, large segments of all three PB helices (Helix1, Helix2, and Helix3) formed simultaneously in PB3 (Figure 2c), and at 7.92 μs, PB3 packed into the topology of a compact helix bundle (Figures 1b and 2c,f; Figures S1 and S2F), with a radius of gyration (R g ) of 1.21 nm, close to the folded state's R g of 1.03 nm (Figure S1B), and with a root‐mean‐square‐deviation (RMSD) of α‐carbon atoms of 1.04 nm (Figure S1C). This compact state formed Q = 40% of native contacts (Figure S1A). PB3 briefly visited this near‐folded ensemble and then reverted to small Q values.

Helix propensity in the model cytoplasm

Folding kinetics and thermodynamics of a globular protein are primarily driven by the hydrophobic effect when the non‐polar side chains are buried away from the polar solvent to form a hydrophobic core (Bellissent‐Funel et al., 2016; Dyson et al., 2006; Kauzmann, 1959). An isolated protein, in dilute environment, can only form contacts between its own hydrophobic residues. In cell, hydrophobic residues of the unfolded protein, as well as hydrophilic or charged surface residues, can also be in contact with other macromolecules. Such residues are then shielded from the solvent.

We were able to characterize here a special case of how solvent shielding by the surrounding macromolecules within the crowded environment is promoting formation and stabilization of Helix2 in PB (Figure 3), the helix with the highest mean hydrophobicity across all three PB helices (Table 1). Helix propensity for the three helical sequences of PB shows that Helix1 of PB has the highest helix formation tendency in isolation, based on different in vitro experimental scales developed by Pace and Scholtz (Pace & Scholtz, 1998) and O'Neil and DeGrado (O'Neil & DeGrado, 1990) (Table 1). However, when we calculated both the secondary structure formation (Figure 2, Figures S2 and S3) across the trajectories and the average fraction of helix formation (Table 1; Table S1) for all PB copies across our Cell1 and Cell2 simulations, Helix2 had a longer occurrence than both Helix1 and Helix3. Long‐lived complete formation of Helix2 was further seen to occur in two distinct cases, for both PB3 in Cell1 (Figure S3C) and PB2 in Cell2 (Figure 2b). This helix, the shortest among the three helices, has a net charge closer to zero (−0.3 at pH 7.4, Table 1) than other helices.

We propose that the formation and stabilization of this Helix2 can be driven by both crowding and solvent shielding effects in our cytoplasm model (Figures 3 and 4a) where the effects are acting concurrently across the Cell1 simulation for PB3. We further characterize (Figure 3) a spontaneous event where the solvent shielding is being facilitated by PB3 confinement in a restricted space between two other macromolecules. In agreement, an analogous observation has been reported previously by Pande and co‐workers in a simulation study mimicking in vitro environments—their villin confinement within a restricted space that excluded water was seen to promote protein folding (Lucent et al., 2007). In another relevant study, Shea and co‐workers (Friedel et al., 2003) demonstrated that confinement and crowding effects not only facilitate protein folding but further modify both the folding mechanism and intermediates, such as being seen here. Our results (Figures 3 and 4) therefore can help in our understanding of how cells can present different microenvironments that facilitate protein folding in non‐specific confinements that perhaps could occur in known highly‐evolved settings such as within the hydrophilic cage of the E. coli GROE/EL chaperone system (Baumketner et al., 2003) and folding directed within an interacting nano‐chamber formed by the human TRiC/CCT chaperone (Gestaut et al., 2022).

We calculated the in‐cell SASA (Lee & Richards, 1971; Ooi et al., 1987) of each residue of PB3 during both Cell1 (Figure 4a, bottom) and Cell2 (Figure 4b, bottom) simulations. To describe the in‐cell dynamics in more detail, we employ SASA (Lee & Richards, 1971; Ooi et al., 1987) because this characteristic does not make assumptions about the folded structure and differs significantly between in vivo macromolecular crowding conditions and in vitro dilute water conditions due to solvent shielding by other macromolecules. We coin the term “in‐cell SASA” (Figures 3, 4, 5, 6), defined as the accessibility of a protein surface to a probe solvent molecule after excluding the surface shielded by contact with other macromolecules. This contrasts with general “SASA,” which is the usual full surface area of a protein accessible to a probe solvent molecule (Ali et al., 2014; Ooi et al., 1987), irrespective of surrounding macromolecules. In Cell1, the segment of PB3 corresponding to Helix2 has a lower average in‐cell SASA (Figure 4a, bottom) than neighboring residues across the simulation. Initially, PB is in contact with many water molecules (Figure 3a), then it becomes wedged between phosphoglycerate mutase (PGM) subunit A and calmodulin, resulting in a confined and nearly water‐free environment for promoting Helix2 formation (Figures 3b and 4a). At 3.288 μs (Figure 3c), the formation of Helix2 is seen within the confined space. Helix2 native fold persists (Figure 3d), and its region of PB is in contact with far fewer water molecules than at the start (Figure 3a). In the Cell2 simulation, PB3 segments that correspond to Helix1 (from ca. 5 to 15 μs) and Helix2 again (from ca. 25 to 30 μs) experience similar drying (Figure 4b, bottom). Across the Cell1 simulation, residues of PGM subunit A (PGMA) that interacted with the highest frequency (within a cutoff of 0.45 nm) with Helix2 region were a loop stretch of Glu207‐Leu208‐Asn209‐Leu210‐Pro211, a bend/β‐strand region of Lys228‐Pro229‐Met230‐Gln231‐Phe232, and a helix region of Arg240‐Lys241 (Figure 4a; Tables S2). For calmodulin and PB3 Helix2 interactions (Figure 4a; Table S3), the most frequent interacting calmodulin residues were centered around a calmodulin helical segment (Ala15, Ser17, Leu18, Phe19, and Asp20) and another calmodulin helical segment (Thr34, Val35, Arg37, Ser38, and Gly40).

The competition between intramolecular and intermolecular interactions quantified by in‐cell hydrophilic and hydrophobic SASA

PB monomers have been shown to stick strongly to human proteins (Cramer et al., 2007), so one would expect the “in‐cell SASA” generally to be smaller than SASA. These two variants of SASA help us distinguish how the surrounding macromolecular interactions with PB compensate for reduced solvent interaction. SASA calculations can be further refined into hydrophilic and hydrophobic SASAs (Figure 5) to capture the solvent accessibility of respective residues on the protein surface (Prigozhin et al., 2019; Sukenik et al., 2016). Protein hydrophobic SASA can be an effective measure to quantify the hydrophobic effect in protein folding. On the other hand, the hydrophilic SASA can also be used to measure the hydration potential of the hydrophilic protein residues, a potential that can also guide protein folding by mediating interactions between polar or charged residues (Levy & Onuchic, 2004).

Based on our SASA calculations, and keeping in mind potential force field artifacts, we see that the rugged protein folding landscape that PB explores in our cytoplasm models likely results from macromolecular interactions that are competitive with hydrophobic burial driving the folding. We compare (Figure 5) in‐cell SASA (which excludes PB surface area in contact with macromolecules) and SASA (which includes the area occupied by macromolecules) with the reference to folded PB in a dilute water box simulation (Methods). The hydrophobic SASA for all PB copies across Cell1 and Cell2 was at least 5 nm (Chen et al., 2020) larger relative to the folded PB reference (Figure 5a). However, when the in‐cell hydrophobic SASAs were measured, the distribution shifted toward the reference folded hydrophobic SASA (Figure 5b). When hydrophilic SASAs were measured in a similar manner (Figure 5c,d), the in‐cell hydrophilic SASAs of the PBs were distributed within a wide range of ~10 nm (Chen et al., 2020) above and below the reference folded PB hydrophilic SASA, with the distribution shifting toward below the reference line with time. This contrasts with the cell‐free hydrophilic SASA distributions for PB in the cytoplasm models that were primarily shifted above the reference SASA value. Thus, the cellular matrix can also accommodate hydrophilic residues at the PB surface that are normally involved in hydrogen bonding to water.

We also observe in Cell2 that sticky interactions between the sequence‐identical monomers PB1 and PB3 (Figure 4a; Tables S4 and S5) primarily involve hydrophobic residues, whereas the interactions between cyclophilin G (CycG) and PB3 involve a nearly equal mixture of hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues. In Figure S4, we summarize the measurements of all protein–protein sticking interactions involving PB in our models. Both the in‐cell hydrophobic and hydrophilic SASA for PB3 when it reached the native‐like conformer at 7.92 μs (asterisks in Figure 5) did not shift relative to the corresponding hydrophobic and hydrophilic SASA values, indicating minimal external sticky interactions when the most native‐like structure was reached. Immediately after this near‐folding event, sticky interactions between PB3 and CycG are seen to disrupt the folding transition (Figure 1b). Thus, our simulations illustrate that folding of PB can occur when protein–cytoplasm interaction is at a minimum, and that the cellular matrix disrupts folding via both hydrophobic and hydrophilic interactions with PB, even though individual helices (e.g. Helix2) can form and persist for long times in the confined environment between cytoplasmic macromolecules.

Fold change triggered by protein–protein interactions

While PB has been characterized as an α‐helical protein during in vitro experiments and simulations under dilute water conditions (Cramer et al., 2007; Johansson et al., 1995; Johansson et al., 1997; Lejon et al., 2004), we observed the formation of long‐lived β‐strands persisting 1–7 μs during our simulation (Figure 2; Figures S2 and S3). This observation agrees well with a study of the structural plasticity potential of GA domains wherein Bryan, Orban, and colleagues had demonstrated reversible GA domain 3α fold switching to immunoglobulin‐binding GB folds of 4β + α can be induced by a single critical mutation, with the GA and GB sequences originating from the bacterial Streptococcus protein G (Alexander et al., 2009). When the different β‐strand stretches (Figure 2; Figures S2 and S3 and Table S1) formed in the PBs were compared to a GB fold engineering by Bryan, Orban, and colleagues to have higher sequence similarity with GA folds (Alexander et al., 2009; He et al., 2008), we did observe overlap of β‐strands occurring along PB sequences within residues 12–18, 40–46, and residues 50–53 to correspond with the β‐strand elements of the engineered GB fold (Figure S5). However, frequent β‐strand stretches were seen across the simulated PBs outside of the overlapping regions as well.

In the Helix3 region of our in‐cell PB simulations, long stretches of α‐helix formation are rare and short‐lived relative to the other two helical segments (Figure 2; Figures S2 and S3 and Table S1). When we calculated the average secondary structure per residue across all PB copies and across both our cell model trajectories, the following two short stretches of amino acids emerged to form extended β‐sheet elements at the highest frequency (at least 5% average across all the trajectories of all PB copies)—(a) Ala35‐Lys36‐Thr37‐Val38, centered around the loop connecting Helix2 and Helix3; and (b) Glu47‐Ile48‐Leu49 at the C‐terminal of the Helix3 segment (Table S1). This calculation was done using CPPTRAJ on individual PB trajectories within the cytoplasm boxes, without accounting for interfacing oligomers, and so it underestimates transient formation of β‐sheet/strand elements across oligomeric pairs such as visualized in Figure 6, following calculation using the STRIDE algorithm (Frishman & Argos, 1995) with VMD software (Humphrey et al., 1996).

Two types of cellular interactions were seen to be triggering and stabilizing the β‐strand elements in the PB copies across our present simulations: (1) hetero‐oligomeric sticky interactions between PB and either β‐strands or unfolded regions of other cellular proteins and (2) homo‐oligomeric transient interactions between PB copies. In contrast, the binding of folded PB and human serum albumin (HSA), an α‐helical protein, has been shown not to involve any fold changes in PB (Lejon et al., 2004). Fold change with oligomerization has also been observed experimentally in different proteins as in the case of the β/α monomeric lymphotactin in equilibrium with its dimer of a novel all β‐sheet fold (Bryan & Orban, 2010; Tuinstra et al., 2008).

Seeding of β‐strands in PB copies in the Cell2 were seen occurring when PB1 and PB3 interacted (Figure 6a). We observed an anti‐parallel β‐sheet comprised of Val41‐Ala43 on PB1 interfacing with Gly22‐Thr24 on PB3, which is forming an interface with Asn30‐Asn33 on another PB3 strand. As the simulation continued, anti‐parallel β‐strands were seen forming between Ala51‐His52 of PB1 and Asn42‐Ala43 of PB3 as well, indicating multiple nucleation sites for intermolecular β‐sheet formation.

PB1 and PB3 were also seen forming an interface with each other through β‐strands while interacting with CycG (Figure 6b). In a fold‐switch change only 96 ns after its native‐like folding event at 7.92 μs (Figure 1b), PB3 forms a pair of anti‐parallel β‐strands, consisting of Phe27‐Tyr28 and Leu44‐Val45 that were previously forming Helix2 and Helix3 α‐helical structures. This fold‐switch occurred again in PB while undergoing sticking interactions with CycG protein (Figure 1b). Stabilizing interactions between the β‐barrel of CycG and the β‐strand elements in PBs were likely occurring across the simulations via both long‐range attractive and cross‐strand side–chain interactions (Figures 2b and 5a) (Smith & Regan, n.d.; Culka & Rulíšek, 2019).

These observations of transient PB oligomers agree with a past study involving dynamic light scattering that showed PB to exist in a mixture of homo‐oligomeric states in solution, with the dominant species being a homo‐tetramer (Cramer et al., 2007). Given our present observation of the intermolecular β‐strands forming at the transiently interacting interfaces between PB copies, we propose that these simulation events could perhaps shed light on the early events leading to the formation of a protein aggregation nucleus, rich with intermolecular β‐strand elements. The formation of a nucleus has been described to be the critical first step and slow phase in protein aggregation and amyloid formation, followed by rapid aggregate growth (Auer et al., 2007; Hall et al., 2015; Schreck et al., 2020).

Folding of bacterial wild‐type protein B in the model cytoplasm

To capture the role of mutations, we next reversed PB3 in Cell2 back to wild‐type (wt) sequences (Lys11, Leu18, and Lys45) by carefully choosing a PB3 configuration at 7.872 μs simulation timepoint that is right before the near native‐like configuration event (Figure 2c,f). Following further preparation (see Methods), Cell2 now containing wtPB underwent simulation for ~4.5 μs.

The in‐cell SASA (Figure 7a) revealed that these wild‐type mutations that had decreased the mean hydrophobicity of PB likely led the simulated wtPB—when compared to PB3 in previous Cell1 and Cell2 simulations—to not only have an increased in‐cell SASA at the wild type charged residues (Lys11 and Lys45) but also at the surrounding residues (Figure 4a,b bottom). The in‐cell SASA calculations, further supported by the protein–protein interactions contact map (Figure S7), indicate that the reduction in PB hydrophobicity led to decreased stickiness of wtPb with surrounding macromolecules. Interestingly, relative to the PB3 in Cell2, wtPB did not show any significant fold‐switching behavior involving the formation of extended beta sheets (Figures 2c and 7b; Figures S2F, and S6B). This perhaps could be traced to the reduction of sticking interactions between wtPB, relative to PB, and neighboring macromolecules though even longer sampling is needed to make a conclusive statement. Meanwhile, in a past study, Mirceta et al. (2013) had observed that increased concentration of the functional monomeric myoglobin protein in muscles of deep diving mammals, in comparison to terrestrial mammals, is directly correlated with a higher positive surface charge on the myoglobin. The higher surface charge was critical to reduce off‐pathway myoglobin aggregation during protein assembly (Samuel et al., 2015).

However, the reduction in stickiness was still not sufficient to allow for wtPb to undergo a complete folding event during the extensive sampled simulation period (Figure 7b; Figure S7). This is likely due to insufficient sampling time given that despite the wtPB being a fast‐folding protein, its minimum folding time in vitro was determined experimentally (Wang et al., 2004) to be still ~6× slower than that of Lys11Ile/Lys45V PB variant, which took 3.9 ± 0.9 μs to fold in water box (Lindorff‐Larsen et al., 2011). In the present simulation, none of the three helices in wtPB completely formed simultaneously (Figure 7b) nor did the structure reach its native Rg (Figure S6A), determined to be 0.99 nm from the wtPB crystal structure (PDB ID 1PRB), with the minimum Rg reached being 1.27 nm at 480 ns.

CONCLUSIONS

Our focus on extended dynamics in realistic all‐atom cellular models allows for the evaluation of in‐cell helix propensities, trapped intermediates along protein folding pathways due to hydrophobic interactions with other proteins replacing intramolecular interactions, and intermolecular non‐native β‐strand formations.

Our present and past simulation (Samuel Russell et al., 2022) studies of protein folding dynamics in cytoplasm models, as well as different experimental studies (Guo et al., 2014; Smith et al., 2016; Song et al., 2019), demonstrate that protein folding in vivo and in vitro can differ significantly. These studies show attractive interactions between the protein and the cellular environment can lead to protein folding in‐cell being slowed down or disrupted relative to in vitro environments. Transient interactions between proteins and surrounding macromolecules in the cell lead to a continuum in protein stability changes from destabilizing to without change to stabilizing, as observed in our cell simulations (Samuel Russell et al., 2022; Rickard et al., 2019; Rickard et al., 2020) and other experimental studies (Scalvini et al., n.d.; Dhar et al., 2010; Dhar et al., 2011; Smith et al., 2016). In fact, these interactions sometimes have been seen to have a functional character such as in our recent work on modeling quinary interactions between glycolytic enzymes (Samuel Russell et al., 2022). However, our present simulations, in tandem with different experimental studies (Guo et al., 2014; Smith et al., 2016), are showing that the exposed sticky hydrophobic patches on the unfolded proteins, which are normally buried in folded proteins, can especially give rise to disruptive attractive interactions with the cellular environment that compete with formation of normal intracellular native contacts. The difference in stickiness between wtPB and PB with the cellular environment in our simulations further suggests that protein mutations that can promote protein folding efficiency in vitro might not act in a similar manner in vivo. The hydrophobic Ile11/Val45 mutations are seen to increase PB stickiness with the cellular environment—interactions that are disruptive to a functional protein folding trajectory. Our models also highlight the need for molecular chaperones to be present in facilitating protein folding in‐cell and further suggest the potential negative impacts of chaperonopathies in‐cell resulting from defective chaperone expressing genes (Macario et al., 2005).

In this study of the early folding events of a fast‐folding α‐helical protein, we show that future helix propensity scales for in vivo folding may have to be refined from the currently accepted values (Blaber et al., 1993; Engel & DeGrado, 2004; Fujiwara et al., 2012; Lacroix et al., 1998; Myers et al., 1998; O'Neil & DeGrado, 1990; Pace & Scholtz, 1998; Park et al., 1993; Yang et al., 1997) that are based on in vitro studies. As revealed here in PB's case, while such in vitro environment favors stabilizing solvent‐exposed helical segments, in‐cell solvent shielding by macromolecular crowders could favor formation of helical segments that might be stabilized by more hydrophobic environments. In future development of helix propensity scales for in vivo environments, both the solvent shielding effect by macromolecules and the charge screening effect by the small inorganic ions and metabolites in solutions should be considered.

We also propose the transient interactions observed between PB copies modeled with inter‐molecular β‐strand have the potential to evolve under the influence of macromolecular crowding into non‐reversible interactions forming a protein aggregation nucleus when the interactions occur with the right orientation and critical number of PB copies. Such an evolution potential could also perhaps be partly interpreted in terms of a generalized diffusion‐collision‐coalescence model (Modler et al., 2003). In our previous work on WW domain folding in a bacterial cytoplasm model, we had also observed transient β‐strand formations at the sticking interfaces between WW and two nearby macromolecules with the CHARMM22* force field but not with the CHARMM36m force field (Rickard et al., 2020). Hence perhaps this cannot be regarded as a rare and unique phenomenon, even if it is perhaps enhanced by some current MD force fields. Importantly, our cell models can serve as platforms for future studies of initiation of in‐cell protein aggregation diseases, and of the role the naturally occurring in vivo chaperones play in preventing amyloid formation (Ellis, 2007; Wentink et al., 2019).

A recent study by Minami et al. (2023). had predicted over 10,000 novel protein αβ‐folds that have not detected in nature, yet. PB protein has the GA domain topology, known to undergo fold switching between 3α to 4β + α when mutated (Alexander et al., 2009). A theoretical study hypothesized that a fold switch can be triggered just by fatty acid binding around the albumin binding site in the GA domain (Polticelli et al., 2012). Some of the PB residues that interface with human serum albumin—including Ser25, Phe27, Tyr28, Ala31, and Leu44 (Lejon et al., 2004)—have been observed to be among the top residues involved in sticking with CycG, PGM, calmodulin, or between PB copies (Tables S2–S5). The sticking interactions between cellular macromolecules and PB copies are driving a wide range of secondary structural elements in our in‐cell models (Figures 3, 4, and 6). Such structural plasticity perhaps can also facilitate fold switching in vivo, especially of partially unfolded GA domains and should be investigated further.

Materials and methods

We constructed two atomic models of a section of a human cell cytoplasm and randomly placed three unfolded copies of PB (named here PB1, PB2, and PB3) in each model (Figure 1). The beginning folded PB structure was obtained from the native structure (PDB ID 1PRB) in Protein Data Bank (PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/). This PB structure was then truncated with the removal of the first six amino acids at the N‐terminus and further mutated as Lys11Ile/Leu18Trp/Lys45Val. The two models (Cell1 and Cell2)—consisting of macromolecules, metabolites, ions, and water—were built to closely mimic the cellular content of the human bone osteosarcoma cell line known as U‐2 OS (Beck et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2016; Diener et al., 2016; Theillet et al., 2014). Cell1 size is 203,843 atoms, while Cell2 size is 203,903 atoms. The two models are similar with the main difference being Cell2 having a yeast glycolytic enzyme in place of its human homolog in Cell1 (see Figure S1 for model proteome details). In addition to the proteome listed in Figure S1, the other macromolecule present in both our cytoplasm models was a tRNA. Each model was simulated with all‐atom classical molecular dynamics (MD) methods for about 30 μs each with 2 fs time‐step, in the constant‐pressure, constant‐temperature (NPT) ensemble at 1 atm pressure and 318 K, with the MTK barostat and the Noose‐Hover thermostat. Simulation temperatures were selected within the range of the melting temperature for PB of 315 ± 5 K as previously calculated from MD simulation studies conducted using the Noose‐Hover thermostat, relative to experimentally measured melting temperature of 372 K (Lindorff‐Larsen et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2004). Production runs of the simulation were done on the supercomputer Anton 2 at the Pittsburgh Supercomputer Center. Proteins in the cytoplasmic models, including PB, were parameterized with the CHARMM36m (Huang et al., 2017) force field while the water model used was TIP3P (Jorgensen et al., 1983). Snapshots of the simulation trajectories were saved every 24 ns for analysis with the initial 216 ns of simulation time not considered in the analysis described below. Detailed descriptions of model content as well as for methods for model construction, equilibration, and simulation are described in our previous work (Samuel Russell et al., 2022).

The structure of PB used is of the GA domain of P. magnus. The 47 residues KNAIEDAIAEWKKAGITSDFYFNAINKAKTVEEVNALVNEILKAHA of PB sequence used in our present work, ranging from Leu7 to Ala53, originate from the GA domain sequence of Thr1 to Ala53 that corresponds to residues 213–266 in the F. magna albumin‐binding protein (UNIPROT ID Q51911). This PB structure consists of three helices: Helix1 (Lys8 to Ala21), Helix2 (Asp26 to Lys34), and Helix3 (Val38 to Lys50). The P. magnus truncated GA domain sequence, beginning at Leu7, containing mutations Lys11Ile in Helix1 and Lys45Val mutation in Helix3, was previously identified as a fast‐folding three‐helix bundle in vitro and under dilute water box simulations (Lindorff‐Larsen et al., 2011; Takada, 2001; Wang et al., 2004). The PB protein in the present study further contains a Leu18Trp mutation in Helix1, which could be useful in future experimental tests of our modeling.

The unfolded wtPB was obtained by mutating PB3 conformation from Cell2 at 7.872 μs using VMD's Mutator plugin (Humphrey et al., 1996). This modified Cell2 with wtPB was charged equilibrated by removing a potassium ion. The modified Cell2 system was then minimized for 5000 steps prior to production run.

We characterized PB folding events with respect to its folded state conformation in terms of secondary structure formation, Rg, distances between the regions that form helices, native contacts, root‐mean‐square‐deviation (RMSD) of Ca atoms, and SASA. A reference folded state conformation was obtained from 20 ns water box MD simulation, under dilute water conditions, of the folded PB. The short NPT simulation was done with the CHARMM36m (Huang et al., 2017) force field and TIP3P (Jorgensen et al., 1983) water model at 298 K. Interactions between PB and the cytoplasm model environment were measured both in terms inter‐protein residue specific contact formation, with a distance cutoff of 0.45 nm between two inter‐protein atoms, and a reduction of SASA at the site of macromolecular interactions. Secondary structure formation across the trajectories and the average structure for each residue was calculated via the CPPTRAJ (Roe & Cheatham 3rd., 2018) software using the DSSP algorithm (Kabsch & Sander, 1983) (Figure 2; Figures S2 and S3, Table 1; Table S1). Secondary structure calculations for PB monomers done using CPPTRAJ in this work were on trajectories of individual PB copies, and so there will be underestimation of transient extended β‐sheets or isolated β‐bridges when they occur across oligomeric pairs. Visualization of proteins within the cytoplasm models was done with VMD software using the STRIDE algorithm (Frishman & Argos, 1995) (Figures 1, 3, and 6), where the transient β‐sheets or isolated β‐bridges occurring across oligomers could be fully captured. Rg and SASA measurements were done using VMD software (Humphrey et al., 1996). For SASA calculations, atomic radii were set according to CHARMM36m (for proteins) and CHARMM36 nucleic acid additive force field. Distances between nascent helices, and residue‐specific inter‐protein distances were calculated in VMD (Humphrey et al., 1996). Ala14 on Helix1, Ile32 on Helix2, and Val41 on Helix3 were chosen as probes to calculate distances between the nascent helices. These probes were specifically chosen as they are hydrophobic residues facing each other within the protein core and are good indicators of the helix bundle collapse (Prigozhin et al., 2015). Distances were measured between centers‐of‐mass of the probe side chains. Ca‐RMSD was calculated with CPPTRAJ, and the fraction of native contacts (Q) was calculated with the approach of Best, Hummer, and Eaton (Best et al., 2013) with the MDTraj software (McGibbon et al., 2015). Cytoplasm model contents were wrapped back into the simulation box prior to data analysis of cellular relevant SASA and macromolecular interactions with PB using VMD software's (Humphrey et al., 1996) PBCTools to account for periodic boundary conditions. Protein sequence alignments were done with CLUSTAL software (Higgins & Sharp, 1988).
