Right ventricular-protective ventilation in acute respiratory distress syndrome: phenotypes, monitoring and ventilatory management
Authors: Chengchao Peng, Le Yang, Yongran Wu, You Shang, Xiaojing Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite the widespread implementation of lung-protective ventilation, mortality in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) remains substantial, underscoring the importance of extrapulmonary determinants of outcome. Right ventricular (RV) dysfunction is recognised as a frequent and clinically meaningful complication of ARDS, affecting a sizeable proportion of patients and consistently associated with adverse outcomes. ARDS-related pulmonary vascular injury, including hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, hypercapnia and acidosis, microangiopathy, and an imbalance in vasoconstrictor/vasodilator signalling, reduces the functional pulmonary vascular bed and increases pulmonary vascular resistance, thereby imposing an acute afterload challenge on the thin-walled right ventricle. Mechanical ventilation may further exacerbate RV stress through alveolar overdistension, increased intrathoracic pressure and decreased venous return, promoting RV dilation, RV-pulmonary arterial uncoupling and circulatory collapse in severe cases. In this review, we summarise the pathophysiological basis of RV injury in ARDS and propose a physiology-informed clinical framework integrating RV phenotyping with multimodal monitoring. This includes echocardiography as the bedside cornerstone, selected invasive haemodynamic assessments and emerging noninvasive techniques such as electrical impedance tomography, as well as advanced cardiac imaging modalities and serological biomarkers for complementary assessment. We further discuss key components of RV-protective ventilation, including limitation of tidal volume and airway pressures, haemodynamics and recruitability-guided positive end-expiratory pressure titration, cautious use of permissive hypercapnia, avoidance of excessive mean airway pressure, prone positioning, appropriate application of spontaneous breathing and extracorporeal support to facilitate ultra-protective lung ventilation. Finally, we highlight unresolved clinical questions and emphasise the need for prospective studies incorporating standardised RV phenotypes and RV-centred end-points.

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a life-threatening condition characterised by noncardiogenic pulmonary oedema and severe hypoxaemia, often requiring mechanical ventilation as a fundamental supportive intervention [1]. Over the past two decades, lung-protective ventilation, characterised by low tidal volume (LTV) ventilation, limitation of plateau pressure and avoidance of excessive driving pressure, has become the cornerstone of ARDS management [2]. Despite the widespread implementation of lung-protective ventilation and adjunctive therapies, overall mortality in ARDS has shown little improvement [3, 4]. This apparent dissociation suggests that factors beyond alveolar injury and impaired gas exchange may play a critical role in determining clinical outcomes.

Among the extrapulmonary complications of ARDS, right ventricular (RV) dysfunction has emerged as a frequent and clinically significant condition. Approximately 20–50% of patients with ARDS develop RV dysfunction, with a higher prevalence in severe cases requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support [5–8]. The presence of RV dysfunction is associated with a substantial increase in both short-term and overall mortality, with reported estimates approaching a 50% relative increase [5, 8]. In addition, patients with RV dysfunction are more likely to receive prone positioning, vasoactive therapy and inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) as rescue therapies [9]. Therefore, the recognition and management of RV dysfunction have emerged as a critical determinant of outcomes in ARDS.

Multiple ARDS-related factors contribute to an increased RV afterload. Mechanical ventilation itself may further impose a significant haemodynamic burden on the RV by increasing pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), altering transpulmonary pressure and reducing venous return. Prior studies have reported associations between RV dysfunction and tidal volume, plateau pressure, driving pressure, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) and mean airway pressure [10–13]. Adjunctive interventions such as prone positioning have also demonstrated favourable effects on RV loading conditions by improving ventilation–perfusion (V̇/Q̇) matching, reducing hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction and decreasing PVR [14, 15]. Nevertheless, these observations are largely derived from observational studies or secondary analyses [16], and the optimal ventilatory approach for preventing or mitigating RV dysfunction remains uncertain.

This review aims to provide an integrated overview of RV dysfunction in ARDS, focusing on how mechanical ventilation contributes to RV overload. By reframing ventilatory management through the lens of cardiopulmonary interactions, this review seeks to identify unresolved clinical questions and inform future research aimed at optimising ventilatory strategies in ARDS patients complicated by RV dysfunction.

Pathophysiological basis of RV dysfunction and ventilatory strategies in ARDS

ARDS and RV dysfunction: pathophysiological connections

ARDS and RV dysfunction exhibit a complex bidirectional relationship that has a substantial impact on patient outcomes. This interdependence arises from multiple pathophysiological mechanisms, including distinct cardiopulmonary interactions, alterations in PVR, the effects of mechanical ventilation and the systemic consequences of RV failure (RVF). With limited contractile reserve, the right ventricle is particularly vulnerable to acute increases in afterload [17]. When RV dysfunction develops during the course of ARDS, it typically indicates a more advanced and unstable stage of the syndrome, characterised by a higher risk of circulatory compromise and impaired systemic oxygen delivery.

Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction

Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, also known as the Euler–Liljestrand mechanism, normally redistributes blood flow toward better-ventilated lung regions to optimise V̇/Q̇ matching [20]. Although hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction is a beneficial regional adaptive mechanism, it becomes maladaptive in ARDS. Atelectasis and alveolar flooding with protein-rich fluid reduce alveolar oxygen tension and markedly impair gas exchange. In ARDS, persistent and widespread hypoxaemia leads to continuous activation of diffuse hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction and excessive afterload on the RV [21]. When compounded by inflammatory injury and pulmonary vascular remodelling, this process may progress toward fixed pulmonary hypertension, RV dysfunction and adverse haemodynamic effects, thereby exacerbating systemic hypoxaemia and organ hypoxia.

Hypercapnia and acidosis

Severe endothelial injury causes thrombotic microangiopathy, microthrombosis and neoangiogenesis of pulmonary capillaries, which leads to an increase in dead space and refractory hypercapnia in ARDS patients [22, 23]. Clinical evidence from transoesophageal echocardiography indicates that hypercapnic acidosis induced by LTV ventilation may adversely affect RV function, independent of PEEP effects [24]. Elevated arterial CO2 caused by hypoventilation leads to respiratory acidosis, which reduces myocardial contractility and increases cardiac stress, thereby predisposing patients to haemodynamic compromise [16, 25]. Hypercapnia potentiates hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, thereby increasing PVR and mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP). This effect may aggravate RV afterload and contribute to the development of acute cor pulmonale (ACP) [26].

In situ thrombosis and microangiopathy

In situ thrombosis and microangiopathy represent hallmark vascular abnormalities in ARDS. These structural obstructions reduce the functional cross-sectional area of the pulmonary vascular bed, thereby elevating PVR and imposing additional afterload on the RV. Early angiographic and haemodynamic studies in severe ARDS have directly documented pulmonary vascular obstruction associated with increased mPAP and PVR [27]. The resulting maldistribution of pulmonary blood flow not only contributes to higher pulmonary pressures but also impairs V̇/Q̇ matching and systemic oxygen delivery [28]. Beyond mechanical obstruction, the associated endothelial dysfunction amplifies vasomotor dysregulation. Loss of endothelial nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability together with heightened endothelin and thromboxane signalling promotes vasoconstriction and further increases PVR [29]. These abnormalities synergise with hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction and with mechanical ventilation-induced compression of intra-alveolar vessels, producing a cumulative rise in RV afterload.

Imbalance of pulmonary vasoconstrictors and vasodilators

In ARDS, dysregulation of vasoactive mediators further exacerbates pulmonary hypertension. Endothelin-1 (ET-1), a potent pulmonary vasoconstrictor, is often elevated in ARDS and correlates with increased PVR and worse outcomes [30]. Conversely, vasodilators such as NO and prostacyclin are reduced due to endothelial dysfunction. The imbalance between vasoconstrictors (ET-1, thromboxane A2, serotonin) and vasodilators (NO, prostacyclin) shifts pulmonary vascular tone toward sustained vasoconstriction [31]. Over time, this not only increases RV load but also promotes vascular remodelling.

Preload and venous return during positive-pressure ventilation

Beyond increased afterload, RV dysfunction in ARDS may also be aggravated by preload disturbances during positive-pressure ventilation. Increases in intrathoracic pressure may reduce the pressure gradient for venous return, thereby diminishing effective RV preload [32–34]. In parallel, increased pleural and pericardial pressures may cause intracavitary filling pressures to overestimate true transmural RV filling, such that apparently “adequate” or even elevated central venous pressure (CVP) does not necessarily indicate effective preload or predict fluid responsiveness [35]. If RV loading worsens further, RV dilatation, septal shift and pericardial constraint may impair biventricular diastolic filling through ventricular interdependence, such that reduced forward output may coexist with elevated right-sided pressures and systemic venous congestion [36, 37]. Clinically, management of mechanically ventilated patients with ARDS and RV dysfunction therefore requires careful balancing of intravascular underfilling against the risk that fluid loading may exacerbate pre-existing or ventilation-related RV dilatation, tricuspid regurgitation (TR), septal shift and end-organ venous congestion [13, 19, 38].

RV phenotypes in ARDS

Rationale for phenotyping

RV dysfunction in ARDS represents a highly heterogeneous clinical spectrum. The conventional dichotomous definition (“presence” versus “absence” of RV dysfunction) inadequately reflects the pathophysiological and prognostic heterogeneity observed in ARDS. Recent data-driven latent class analyses (LCA) and integrative reviews have identified reproducible cardiovascular subphenotypes among ARDS patients, characterised by distinct haemodynamic profiles and RV adaptations [39–41].

Major RV phenotypes

Building on echocardiographic studies by Zochios et al. [42] and subsequent LCA findings, three major phenotypes of RV involvement are increasingly recognised [39, 41].

1) Normal RV function

Patients within this phenotype exhibit near-normal RV morphology and function, characterised by normal RV size (RV end-diastolic area/left ventricular (LV) end-diastolic area ratio <0.6), preserved systolic indices (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) ≥17 mm, RV fractional area change (FAC) ≥35%) and an absence of significant TR, and maintain a stable cardiac index without echocardiographic evidence of RV dilation. This phenotype reflects preserved RV–pulmonary arterial (PA) coupling and generally tolerates conventional lung-protective ventilation without haemodynamic compromise. In a transthoracic echocardiography (TTE)-based LCA, the mostly normal LV/RV function class had the lowest 90-day mortality (19%) [39].

2) RV dilation with preserved systolic function

This intermediate phenotype is characterised by RV dilatation (RV/LV end-diastolic area ratio >0.6) with preserved systolic function (TAPSE ≥17 mm, FAC ≥35%). This pattern typically develops in response to increased pulmonary vascular load resulting from elevated airway pressures, hypoxaemia, acidosis or microvascular obstruction. It represents an adaptive response to increased pulmonary vascular resistance through preload recruitment and Frank–Starling-mediated augmentation of stroke volume [43]. While cardiac output (CO) is generally maintained, systemic venous congestion (enlarged inferior vena cava, abnormal hepatic or renal venous Doppler patterns) may emerge, potentially leading to end-organ compromise. Despite its high prevalence, this adaptive phenotype is not consistently associated with excess mortality after adjustment for confounders. In the same TTE-based LCA, the dilated RV with preserved function class had a 90-day mortality of 40% [39].

3) Decompensated RVF (RV dilation with impaired systolic function)

The decompensated RVF phenotype represents a high-risk state characterised by marked RV dilation (often with an RV/LV end-diastolic area ratio >1.0) and severe systolic impairment (TAPSE <17 mm, FAC <35%, frequently associated with reduced cardiac index). This condition reflects a maladaptive pattern of RV–PA uncoupling, often exacerbated by hypercapnia, acidosis or high mechanical load. It is typically associated with significant TR, elevated central venous pressure, systemic hypotension and congestion. These haemodynamic disturbances may contribute to impaired RV filling and multiorgan dysfunction, evidenced by a higher Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and an increased need for renal replacement therapy. Consequently, this phenotype is consistently linked to higher mortality and, in several cohorts, persists as an independent predictor after adjustment (adjusted odds ratio (OR) for 90-day mortality 6.9, 95% CI 4.0–11.8). In the same TTE-based LCA, the dilated RV with impaired systolic function class had a 90-day mortality of 78% [39].

However, RV performance is highly dynamic and may shift over time according to changes in transpulmonary pressure, arterial carbon dioxide tension (PaCO2), acid–base status, vasopressor exposure, pulmonary vascular tone, and intravascular filling. This framework provides an important physiological basis for individualised, RV-protective ventilation strategies in ARDS, while acknowledging that these phenotypes represent dynamic and context-dependent haemodynamic states rather than fixed stages.

Because RV performance is determined by preload, contractility and afterload, noninvasive surrogates reflecting RV–PA coupling, such as the TAPSE/pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) ratio, have gained clinical interest. However, these indices require cautious interpretation in mechanically ventilated patients. TAPSE is load- and angle-dependent and reflects longitudinal annular motion rather than global RV function. The pressure component depends on adequate TR Doppler signal quality [45–47]. Severe TR, poor acoustic windows, abrupt changes in preload and afterload, and disease-specific differences in the source and nature of RV afterload may all influence this ratio. As a result, the same TAPSE/PASP value may reflect distinct underlying RV–PA interactions among patients, which limits direct interpatient comparability [48].

Beyond conventional two-dimensional (2D) echocardiography, three-dimensional echocardiography is particularly relevant for RV evaluation because the complex geometry of the right ventricle limits the accuracy of 2D single-plane imaging and measurements [49, 50]. By allowing more direct quantification of RV volumes and ejection fraction, it may provide a more comprehensive assessment of global RV function and has shown better agreement with cardiac magnetic resonance than conventional 2D approaches, although some bias in RV volume estimation still exists [50, 51]. However, its application in critically ill mechanically ventilated patients remains constrained by image quality, operator expertise and reproducibility. Venous congestion ultrasound approaches such as the Venous Excess UltraSound (VExUS) score may complement cardiac imaging by integrating inferior vena cava size with hepatic, portal and intrarenal venous Doppler patterns to identify systemic venous congestion [52]. This may be clinically relevant when RV dysfunction, elevated intrathoracic pressures and fluid overload coexist, although supporting evidence remains stronger in cardiac and cardiorenal settings than in unselected general intensive care unit (ICU) populations [52, 53].

However, the utility of echocardiography and related ultrasound techniques remains limited by operator dependency, image quality and the need for specialised training, which may restrict their widespread adoption in routine clinical practice.

Pulmonary artery catheter

The pulmonary artery catheter (PAC), also known as the Swan–Ganz catheter, has long been regarded as the gold standard for advanced haemodynamic monitoring, as it enables direct measurements of PAP, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, CO and mixed venous oxygen saturation [54]. In ARDS, these variables may help characterise RV loading conditions and clarify the haemodynamic effects of ventilator settings and fluid administration. In selected complex cases of ARDS, particularly when severe RV dysfunction, refractory hypoxaemia, shock or diagnostic uncertainty is present, the PAC may provide valuable haemodynamic information [55]. Pressure-based, PAC-compatible approaches for estimating RV–PA coupling are also emerging, although their clinical applicability and prognostic value in ARDS require further validation.

However, PAC should not be used routinely in all patients with ARDS. Its invasive nature, potential procedural complications and the absence of proven clinical benefit with unselected use support a selective, rather than indiscriminate, application. In addition, PAC-derived parameters require careful interpretation in relation to ventilator settings, intrathoracic pressure, volume status and the overall haemodynamic profile [56].

Electrical impedance tomography

Electrical impedance tomography (EIT) has gained increasing attention as a noninvasive technique for monitoring lung ventilation and perfusion in critically ill patients. Although it has traditionally been used to assess ventilation status, recent studies have explored its application to the evaluation of pulmonary haemodynamics, including changes in PVR. This capability is particularly relevant in conditions such as ARDS, pulmonary hypertension and heart failure. Multiple clinical studies have demonstrated that hypertonic-saline contrast-enhanced EIT can be effectively applied to the assessment of PA hypertension [57], pulmonary embolism [58] and ARDS [59]. In patients with PA hypertension, Hovnanian et al. [60] demonstrated that EIT-derived perfusion amplitude (ΔZQ) was significantly reduced compared with normotensive controls and strongly correlated with invasive haemodynamic parameters, particularly stroke volume (r=0.76). Similarly, EIT has demonstrated the ability to detect pulmonary vascular abnormalities in idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension (IPAH). In a study of IPAH patients undergoing vasoreactivity testing, ΔZQ showed a meaningful correlation with changes in PVR and mPAP in the single vasodilator responder, indicating that EIT can reliably capture intravascular pulmonary blood volume variations [61]. Collectively, these findings indicate that EIT-derived measures, including pulmonary volume pulse and ΔZQ, provide a novel, noninvasive means of assessing PVR and overall haemodynamic status. Such measures offer physiologically relevant information that may reflect disease severity in pulmonary vascular disorders.

Advanced cardiac imaging

Unlike bedside echocardiography, advanced imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) are not routinely used for real-time ventilator titration in unstable patients with ARDS. In patients with ARDS, chest CT is frequently performed to assess the extent of lung parenchymal involvement or exclude competing pulmonary diagnoses and can concurrently provide complementary signs suggestive of RV pressure overload or dysfunction. These include increased RV size, septal flattening or shift and RV/LV disproportionality, findings that may be associated with ACP and adverse outcomes in selected studies [62, 63]. However, the need for patient transport, radiation exposure and limited repeatability restrict its routine use for serial bedside guidance in critically ill mechanically ventilated patients.

CMR remains the reference standard for noninvasive quantification of RV volumes, mass and ejection fraction due to its superior spatial resolution and ability to assess myocardial tissue characteristics [64]. In addition, CMR-derived functional parameters, including RV ejection fraction and myocardial deformation indices, may further characterise RV systolic impairment [65]. Nonetheless, logistical complexity and limited feasibility in the acute ICU setting, particularly in mechanically ventilated patients, mean that CMR is better regarded as a tool for selected diagnostic questions, research applications or post-recovery evaluation rather than immediate ventilator adjustment in ARDS.

Serological biomarkers

Circulating biomarkers reflecting myocardial wall stress or injury may complement imaging and haemodynamic assessment, but should be viewed as supportive rather than standalone monitoring tools for bedside ventilator titration. Elevated natriuretic peptides, particularly N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide, are frequently observed in ARDS and have been associated with worse outcomes; however, their interpretation is limited by biological nonspecificity in critical illness, as elevated levels may also reflect concomitant LV dysfunction, renal dysfunction, fluid loading or overall illness severity [66]. Similarly, elevations of high-sensitivity cardiac troponin I or T indicate myocardial injury secondary to RV strain or systemic inflammation and have been associated with poor prognosis in severe ARDS [67]. Emerging biomarkers such as growth differentiation factor-15 and soluble ST2 may provide additional information on myocardial stress, inflammation or remodelling, but their role in ARDS-related RV dysfunction remains investigational [68]. Mechanical ventilation strategies for RV-protective management

Mechanical ventilation exerts profound effects on RV loading conditions in ARDS, and inappropriate ventilatory settings may aggravate PVR, increase transpulmonary pressure and ultimately precipitate ACP [69]. The interaction between ventilatory parameters and RV function is governed by the balance between alveolar recruitment and overdistension, the distribution of regional lung stress and strain, and changes in intrathoracic pressure that influence venous return. A clear understanding of these mechanisms is essential for optimising mechanical ventilation in a manner that mitigates RV afterload while preserving lung protection. Specifically, several key ventilatory components, including tidal volume, plateau pressure, PEEP and mean airway pressure (Pmean), may independently or synergistically increase pulmonary vascular resistance, thereby exacerbating RV dysfunction. Tidal volume and plateau pressure

Individualised optimisation of tidal volume and plateau pressure is fundamental to lung-protective ventilation in ARDS. While LTV (6 mL·kg−1 predicted body weight) remains standard care, further individualisation based on lung volume reserve and respiratory mechanics may improve outcomes [75]. Excessive tidal volume in injured lung regions may promote alveolar overdistension, compressing alveolar capillaries and augmenting hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, thereby increasing RV afterload. Osman et al. [70] reported that tidal volume was higher in patients who developed RVF, whereas total PEEP and plateau pressure did not differ between those with and without RVF. Similarly, Slobod et al. [10] demonstrated that RV afterload increased proportionally with rising tidal volume in cardiac surgery patients receiving controlled mechanical ventilation postoperatively. Therefore, minimising tidal volume and avoiding unnecessary inspiratory overdistension are key components of RV-protective ventilation.

Plateau pressure should be maintained below safety thresholds (typically <30 cmH2O) to prevent ventilator-induced lung injury, but the optimal plateau pressure may vary depending on chest wall compliance and abdominal pressure. High plateau pressure (>27 cmH2O) has been associated with a high incidence of RV dysfunction (up to 60%) and high mortality rates (up to 42%) in ARDS [11]. Jardin et al. [11] found that the incidence of cor pulmonale was 56% when plateau pressure exceeded 35 cmH2O, 32% when it ranged between 27 and 35 cmH2O, and 13% when it was below 27 cmH2O in a cohort of 352 patients with ARDS. Elevated plateau pressure may indicate widespread alveolar overdistension and increased transpulmonary stress. First, increased alveolar pressure compresses alveolar capillaries, leading to elevated pulmonary vascular resistance. Second, when alveolar pressure exceeds left atrial pressure, West zone 2 conditions (a state in which pulmonary venous pressure is insufficient to maintain vessel patency, causing intermittent blood flow) can develop, impeding venous return and increasing RV afterload [10].

Permissive hypercapnia

Permissive hypercapnia warrants consideration within an RV-protective ventilation framework because it is often the physiological cost of strict lung-protective ventilation. Although a moderate rise in PaCO2 may be accepted to avoid injurious tidal volumes and airway pressures, hypercapnia and the resulting respiratory acidosis are not necessarily benign from an RV perspective [76]. Elevated PaCO2 can increase pulmonary vascular resistance, raise PAP and aggravate RV afterload, particularly when hypercapnia is severe or develops rapidly [13, 77]. In ARDS cohorts managed with protective ventilation, PaCO2 ≥48 mmHg has been identified as an independent risk factor for ACP, and higher PaCO2 levels have repeatedly been linked to worsening RV loading conditions [12]. Therefore, permissive hypercapnia should be individualised rather than routinely adopted across all patients with ARDS. In patients with established RV dysfunction or a high risk of ACP, marked hypercapnia should prompt reassessment of the ventilatory strategy and consideration of alternative CO2-management approaches, including optimisation of lung-protective settings, prone positioning and extracorporeal support in selected refractory cases [78].

PEEP titration

PEEP, while a cornerstone of ARDS management, exerts complex effects on RV function. A hallmark of ARDS is the heterogeneous spatial distribution of pulmonary pathology, in which densely consolidated or atelectatic regions coexist with normally aerated lung tissue [79]. The relationship between PVR and lung volume is classically U-shaped, with the lowest resistance near functional residual capacity [80]. In mechanically ventilated patients, this relationship is better interpreted according to end-expiratory lung volume, which is largely determined by PEEP. At low end-expiratory lung volumes, derecruitment and hypoxic vasoconstriction may increase PVR, whereas at excessive lung volumes, alveolar overdistension may compress intra-alveolar vessels and likewise increase PVR [81, 82]. Beyond its effects on PVR, excessively high PEEP may also increase intrathoracic pressure and reduce systemic venous return, thereby further compromising RV performance [5, 82].

Recent mechanistic evidence further supports consideration of RV physiology during PEEP titration. In an invasive biventricular pressure-volume loop study in a porcine model, Wood et al. [83] showed that stepwise increases in PEEP caused stepwise reductions in biventricular stroke work; however, whereas LV afterload and coupling were largely preserved, the RV demonstrated increased afterload, RV–PA uncoupling and a reduction in RV ejection fraction. Consistent with this concept, clinical data in severe ARDS have shown that increasing PEEP at constant plateau pressure may improve oxygenation and alveolar recruitment yet still be associated with RV dilatation, leftward septal shift/LV deformation, and reduced cardiac index [24].

Various methods exist for PEEP individualisation, including compliance-based titration, EIT-guided titration, transpulmonary pressure-guided titration and imaging-based techniques (CT, lung ultrasound, bedside pressure–volume curve analysis). EIT allows real-time assessment of regional ventilation distribution and identification of PEEP levels that may minimise alveolar collapse while limiting overdistension [84]. Transpulmonary pressure-guided PEEP titration may help avoid end-expiratory collapse in patients with high pleural pressure [85, 86]. In comparative studies, transpulmonary pressure-guided PEEP titration has been associated with improved oxygenation, better pulmonary mechanics and less right heart strain than some alternative titration strategies, although this evidence remains limited [87]. However, elevated PEEP levels may transiently impair CO in haemodynamically vulnerable patients.

Driving pressure and transpulmonary pressure

Driving pressure and transpulmonary pressure monitoring have emerged as valuable tools for personalising mechanical ventilation settings. Driving pressure, calculated as plateau pressure minus PEEP, reflects the stress applied to the respiratory system with each breath and has shown a strong correlation with mortality in ARDS patients [88]. While maintaining a driving pressure below 15 cmH2O is generally recommended [3, 89], further individualisation may be necessary based on patient-specific factors. In a large prospective study involving 752 patients with moderate to severe ARDS, Mekontso Dessap et al. [12] identified a driving pressure ≥18 cmH2O as an independent risk factor for the development of ACP.

Transpulmonary pressure, measured as airway pressure minus pleural pressure (estimated via oesophageal manometry), provides a more direct assessment of lung stress by accounting for chest wall mechanics. This is particularly important in patients with abnormal chest wall compliance, such as those with obesity, ascites or abdominal compartment syndrome. Monitoring transpulmonary pressure allows clinicians to individualise PEEP settings to achieve positive end-expiratory transpulmonary pressure, preventing alveolar collapse while avoiding excessive lung distension. In a prospective two-centre study including both adults and children with ARDS, increased lung stress was significantly associated with RV impairment (RVI), with children showing a higher susceptibility to RVI [90]. Moreover, a recent PEEP-titration study found that a transpulmonary pressure-oriented approach yielded superior RV functional indices (TAPSE, RV FAC) compared with conventional or driving pressure-guided methods [87].

Mean airway pressure

Mean airway pressure represents the average airway pressure across the respiratory cycle and reflects the combined effects of ventilator settings, including tidal volume, PEEP, inspiratory time and flow pattern. From an RV perspective, its relevance lies in the fact that it may summarise the cumulative haemodynamic burden imposed by mechanical ventilation rather than serve as an independent therapeutic target. In ARDS, Riddell et al. [73] demonstrated in a post hoc analysis that Pmean was independently associated with mPAP. This observation supports the concept that higher cycle-averaged airway pressure may accompany increased RV afterload and reduced pulmonary vascular reserve. Accordingly, when RV dysfunction or ACP is suspected, an elevated Pmean should prompt reassessment of modifiable determinants such as tidal volume, PEEP, respiratory rate, inspiratory time and the risk of overdistension, while balancing recruitment and gas exchange.

Prone position ventilation

Prone positioning is an evidence-based strategy shown to improve oxygenation and survival in patients with moderate to severe ARDS. By redistributing transpulmonary pressure and improving V̇/Q̇ matching, it promotes more homogeneous ventilation and reduces alveolar overdistension [91, 92]. Beyond its pulmonary benefits, prone positioning may reverse some of the mechanisms contributing to RV dysfunction during ARDS. First, by promoting a more uniform distribution of pleural pressure, it reduces dorsal lung compression and alleviates the collapse of gravity-dependent regions. This redistribution of transpulmonary pressure facilitates alveolar recruitment without excessive PEEP, thereby improving pulmonary vascular patency and decreasing PVR. Second, prone positioning enhances venous return and central blood volume, which may relieve the compression of pulmonary microvessels and ultimately reduce RV afterload [93]. Third, the enhanced arterial oxygenation mitigates hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, further reducing PVR. In a study of 42 patients with severe ARDS, prone positioning significantly reduced RV dilatation, as evidenced by a marked decrease in the end-diastolic RV/LV area ratio after 18 h, and proved to be an effective method for controlling RV pressure overload [94]. Early and prolonged prone sessions (at least 16 h·day–1) are recommended in suitable patients, provided that haemodynamic stability and airway safety can be maintained [74, 95]. Integration of prone positioning into individualised ventilation strategies may further enhance lung recruitment while protecting the RV from excessive afterload.

Controlled versus spontaneous breathing

The choice between controlled mechanical ventilation and assisted ventilation with preserved spontaneous breathing warrants careful consideration, as it influences transpulmonary pressure swings, venous return and cardiopulmonary interactions [26, 96]. During spontaneous inspiration, more negative intrathoracic pressure enhances venous return and increases RV preload by elevating the pressure gradient between the right atrium and extrathoracic veins. However, in ARDS patients with excessive respiratory drive and reduced lung compliance, vigorous inspiratory effort may markedly increase transpulmonary pressure and PVR, further increasing RV afterload. The combined elevation of preload and afterload induced by vigorous spontaneous breathing may contribute to RV dilatation, interventricular septal shift, impaired LV filling and subsequent haemodynamic instability [96, 97].

Controlled ventilation may be advantageous in early or severe ARDS with excessive respiratory drive, as it enables stricter regulation of tidal volume and inspiratory pressure, and helps limit high inspiratory effort and potentially injurious transpulmonary pressure swings [96, 97]. However, controlled ventilation is not intrinsically RV-protective, since excessive airway pressure, lung overdistension, or inappropriately elevated PEEP may also increase PVR and impair RV ejection. In contrast, when respiratory effort is moderate and well controlled, spontaneous breathing may preserve diaphragm activity, improve ventilation distribution and patient–ventilator interaction, and reduce sedation exposure [98, 99]. Accordingly, current evidence supports an individualised approach based on ARDS severity, respiratory drive, patient–ventilator synchrony, lung recruitability and the presence or risk of RV dysfunction [99, 100].

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation

ECMO may provide favourable conditions for RV unloading in selected patients with severe ARDS when conventional ventilatory approaches are insufficient, particularly in the presence of refractory hypoxaemia, persistent hypercapnia or progressive RV dysfunction [101]. Veno-venous (VV) ECMO enables extracorporeal gas exchange and facilitates ultra-protective ventilation, with reductions in tidal volume, driving pressure, plateau pressure and mean airway pressure. This reduction in ventilatory intensity mitigates alveolar overdistension and cyclic lung stress while improving oxygenation and correcting hypercapnic acidosis, thereby attenuating hypoxic and CO2-mediated pulmonary vasoconstriction and lowering PVR [102].

Physiological and observational studies have reported short-term improvements in haemodynamic or echocardiographic variables after VV-ECMO initiation, suggesting that RV loading conditions may improve in some patients after extracorporeal support is initiated [101]. However, these findings should be interpreted as short-term haemodynamic changes rather than definitive evidence of sustained therapeutic benefit for RV function. A systematic review and meta-analysis showed that RVI during VV-ECMO remained associated with increased mortality, indicating that extracorporeal support does not uniformly eliminate RV risk [103].

RV assessment during VV-ECMO is challenging and both the definition and interpretation of RVI require caution [104, 105]. No universally accepted definition of RVI exists in VV-ECMO and expert statements conceptualise it as a clinical syndrome encompassing a spectrum of abnormal RV biomechanics, including RV dilatation, RV dysfunction, RV failure or a combination thereof [104]. Echocardiographic interpretation is further complicated by RV geometry, dynamic loading conditions, ventilator settings, vasoactive support and ECMO configuration [105]. These factors, together with heterogeneity in RVI criteria, assessment timing and support conditions, may explain inconsistent findings across studies [103, 106].

While VV-ECMO remains the preferred configuration for severe ARDS, veno-arterial (VA)-ECMO or veno-arterial-venous (VAV)-ECMO may be considered in carefully selected patients with ARDS complicated by cardiogenic shock, such as those with severe RV failure and circulatory collapse [107]. In contrast, VA-ECMO is generally not indicated in cases of pure distributive shock with preserved or high CO, in which vasoplegia rather than pump failure dominates.

Direct clinical evidence confirming that ECMO consistently improves RV function remains limited; accordingly, larger, well-designed clinical studies are still warranted. Whether these physiological advantages translate into reproducible patient-centered benefit specifically through RV protection remains uncertain.

Future research directions and unresolved clinical issues

Although advances have been made in understanding cardiopulmonary interactions in ARDS, RV protection has not yet been systematically incorporated into ventilatory decision-making, and evidence supporting RV-targeted individualisation remains limited. Consequently, several critical knowledge gaps persist.

First, research on individualised ventilation in ARDS has thus far focused on a limited number of domains, including oxygenation, CT-based lung morphology, lung recruitability and inflammatory subphenotypes [108–111]. However, none of these approaches has directly evaluated RV responses or assessed whether tailoring ventilation to these phenotypes mitigates RV dysfunction. Incorporating RV-centred end-points into such phenotype-based strategies may therefore uncover novel therapeutic opportunities.

Second, a key barrier to RV-protective ventilation is the lack of a scalable, reproducible bedside tool for RV assessment in mechanically ventilated patients. Although echocardiography is the clinical cornerstone, acoustic windows, patient positioning and inter-operator variability can limit feasibility and reproducibility, particularly in severe ARDS [112]. Future work should develop and validate multimodal RV monitoring frameworks that combine simplified imaging metrics with complementary surrogates of RV loading, venous congestion and systemic perfusion. Promisingly, artificial intelligence-enabled integration of multimodal bedside data, including focused echocardiography, EIT-derived regional ventilation–perfusion signals and circulating biomarkers of myocardial stress or injury, may facilitate dynamic RV phenotyping and risk stratification within a structured decision protocol.

Third, the determinants of RV dysfunction are unlikely to be uniform across ARDS patients. Variability in inflammatory burden, endothelial injury, microthrombosis and hypoxic vasoconstriction may alter how ventilatory pressures and gas-exchange targets translate into pulmonary vascular load and RV–PA uncoupling. Future studies should therefore combine multimodal monitoring with biological profiling to identify patient-specific drivers of increased PVR and RV afterload.

To close these knowledge gaps, large-scale, multicentre studies incorporating standardised RV phenotyping and multimodal physiological monitoring are urgently needed. Establishing validated RV-specific end-points will be essential for designing future trials of RV-protective or individualised ventilation strategies.

Conclusion

RV dysfunction is a frequent and prognostically significant complication of ARDS, driven by the interplay between pulmonary vascular injury and adverse mechanical ventilation-related effects. Although lung-protective ventilation remains the cornerstone of ARDS management, optimal strategies tailored to mitigate RV overload and stress remain poorly defined. Integrating RV phenotyping with multimodal respiratory and haemodynamic monitoring may help individualise ventilatory settings and guide stratified therapeutic escalation in high-risk patients. Further prospective studies are urgently required to validate the clinical benefits of RV-protective ventilation and confirm its prognostic impact in patients with ARDS.
