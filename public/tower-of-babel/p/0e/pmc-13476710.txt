Rethinking intraluminal thrombus in abdominal aortic aneurysm
Authors: Salvatore T Scali
Published: 2026

Invited commentary

In their article, “Growth rate, diameter at rupture, and associated patient characteristics of abdominal aortic aneurysms without an intraluminal thrombus,” Liljeqvist et al1 addressed an uncommon but provocative abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) phenotype: the aneurysm without significant intraluminal thrombus (ILT). Their study is valuable because it moves beyond treating ILT as a binary radiographic signature and asks whether its absence identifies patients with different growth or rupture behavior. Across elective, ruptured, and surveillance cohorts, absent or minimal ILT was uncommon, associated with smaller aneurysm diameter, and, despite rupture occurring at a smaller median diameter, was not independently associated with rupture after adjustment for patient characteristics.

The investigators should be commended for examining a rare phenotype across three complementary cohorts, using longitudinal diameter measurements and multiple sensitivity analyses. The observation that ILT-free aneurysms may have less atherosclerotic burden is also hypothesis generating and supports the broader concept that AAA is not a single disease but rather a spectrum of biologically distinct remodeling phenotypes.

However, there are important limitations that temper the clinical interpretation. The primary cohort was surgically selected, the surveillance cohort was supportive rather than confirmatory, and only 18 elective and 12 ruptured aneurysms had absent or minimal ILT. Consequently, the study cannot exclude clinically meaningful effects or establish that ILT absence is innocuous. Moreover, ILT was characterized predominantly by maximal thickness at one cross-sectional level. This pragmatic metric does not capture total thrombus burden, spatial distribution, age, organization, permeability, hemorrhage, or regional relationships among thrombus, wall stress, and wall degeneration.

These distinctions matter biologically. ILT is neither inert nor uniformly protective. It may reduce mural stress while simultaneously promoting hypoxia, inflammatory cell accumulation, protease activity, extracellular matrix degradation, and smooth muscle cell loss in the adjacent wall.2, 3, 4, 5 The foundational work by Pyo et al6 demonstrating the importance of inflammatory cell-derived matrix metalloproteinase-9 in experimental aneurysm formation reinforces why an anatomic “present-versus-absent” classification cannot fully represent the proteolytic biology of AAA.6, 7 The absence of ILT may therefore indicate an earlier stage of disease, a different hemodynamic environment, or a truly distinct wall-remodeling phenotype.

The next step is not simply a larger retrospective comparison. Prospective multicenter studies should combine serial three-dimensional ILT segmentation with patient-specific hemodynamics and wall-stress modeling, molecular imaging, circulating biomarkers, and spatially matched tissue profiling. Comparing a thrombus-free wall with a wall underlying a thrombus using single-cell, proteomic, and extracellular matrix analyses could determine whether these aneurysms follow convergent or divergent biological pathways.

For now, these data do not justify changing repair thresholds solely because ILT is absent. Their greater contribution is conceptual: personalized AAA management will require integration of diameter with geometry, biomechanics, thrombus phenotype, and wall biology. This study brings that objective one step closer.
