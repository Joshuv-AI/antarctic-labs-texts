“AND Gate” Logic Fluorescence Sensing Platform for Highly Specific Detection of Dual microRNA Biomarkers
Authors: Guoshi Lin, Shichang Rao, Xiaocheng Li, Zhipeng Liang, Aishun Guo, Ruisheng Lin, Zongqing Zheng, Weiping Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early diagnosis of glioma, which is the most common and highly malignant primary tumor of the central nervous system, is highly important for improving patient survival rates. MicroRNAs (miRNAs), such as miR-21 and miR-155, are aberrantly expressed in gliomas and serve as potential early diagnostic biomarkers. In this study, an “AND gate” logic fluorescence sensing platform based on upconversion nanoparticle (UCNP) and mesoporous silica (mSiO2) was used for the highly sensitive and specific measurement of the dual targets miR-21 and miR-155. Under optimized conditions, the limits of detection of the method for miR-21 and miR-155 were 0.181 nM and 0.185 nM, respectively, with linear ranges of 0.5∼10 nM. The platform can trigger significant fluorescence signals only in the presence of dual targets, and there is no response from single or interfering miRNAs. In actual serum samples, the spiked recoveries of miR-21 and miR-155 were 92%∼100.69% and 96.78%∼100.66%, respectively, with relative standard deviations of less than 7%. In summary, this sensing platform has the merits of easy operation, no need for enzyme amplification, and a low background signal. It demonstrates good accuracy and stability in intricate biological matrices, offering a promising technological approach for early identification, progression assessment, and precise diagnosis of gliomas.

Introduction

Glioma is the most common primary tumor in the central nervous system and is highly malignant. , Their incidence rates and mortality are high. Early diagnosis is the key for improving patient survival. However, the early symptoms of glioma are insidious and lack specific signs, and most patients are diagnosed in the middle to late stages; thus, the best treatment opportunity is missed. , Therefore, developing efficient and accurate early diagnostic methods has important clinical significance. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are endogenous noncoding RNAs that regulate target gene expression through complementary base pairing and are widely involved in key life processes, such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and metastasis. − Numerous studies have shown that the abnormal expression of miRNAs (such as miR-21 and miR-155) is closely related to the occurrence, development, invasion, metastasis, and prognosis of various malignant tumors, such as gliomas. , They are biomarkers for early diagnosis, molecular typing, and prognostic evaluation of tumors. , Therefore, developing highly sensitive and specific determination methods based on miRNAs is highly important for the early identification and precise treatment of gliomas.

Currently, conventional detection approaches for miRNA include real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR, Northern blot hybridization, in situ hybridization, and next-generation sequencing. Although these methods are widely used in clinical and scientific research, they generally have problems such as cumbersome operation, dependence on precision instruments, and insufficient sensitivity in detecting low-abundance targets. , In contrast, the sensing strategy based on nucleic acid DNA probes has the advantages of flexible design, simple synthesis, high stability, easy signal amplification, and multifunctional integration, which can effectively overcome the limitations of traditional detection methods. − Therefore, it has broad prospects in miRNA detection. However, traditional fluorescence sensors based on hairpin probes (such as molecular beacons) are usually in a “normally on” state, which is susceptible to nonspecific interference in complex biological sample matrices, leading to the opening of nontargeted loops, resulting in high background signals and false-positive results, limiting their detection accuracy and practical application reliability. − Therefore, developing a novel fluorescence sensing system with controllable activation characteristics to reduce background interference and improve detection specificity has become a key challenge that urgently needs to be addressed in this field.

Upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs) can convert near-infrared (NIR) light (such as 980 or 808 nm) into short-wavelength visible or ultraviolet light, making them an ideal core for NIR light-controlled sensing platforms because of their unique optical properties. , However, UCNPs prepared by classical methods, such as high-temperature solvothermal methods and thermal decomposition methods, usually have hydrophobic organic ligands, such as oleic acid, coated on their surfaces, resulting in the strong hydrophobicity of the particles and the inability to stably disperse them in aqueous systems, greatly limiting their application in the field of biosensing. , Therefore, surface functionalization of UCNPs is a key step in achieving their transfer from the oil phase to the water phase and expanding their practical applications. At present, functional modification methods for UCNPs include ligand exchange, silica coating, and metal-organic framework material coating. Among these, the use of mesoporous silica (mSiO2) is recognized as one of the most promising strategies because of its large specific surface area, adjustable pore size, and easily functionalized surface. The UCNP@mSiO2 core–shell structure can integrate photoconversion, probe loading, fluorescence activation, and stimulus-responsive probe exposure within a single nanoplatform. Under 808 nm irradiation, the UCNP core can generate ultraviolet emission at approximately 365 nm, which provides an internal light source for activating photoresponsive sensing modules without the need for direct external UV irradiation. Meanwhile, because bare UCNPs are generally hydrophobic and exhibit poor dispersibility in aqueous systems, the mSiO2 shell can improve their water dispersibility and colloidal stability while providing abundant surface sites and mesoporous channels for nucleic acid probe loading. More importantly, when the silica shell is appropriately engineered with cleavable linkages, it can respond to biological stimuli such as high concentrations of glutathione (GSH), thereby promoting probe exposure or release and improving the accessibility of target molecules. In addition, the core–shell architecture can spatially confine the recognition probes around the UCNP core, which facilitates local photoactivation and helps maintain a low background signal before target recognition. Therefore, UCNP@mSiO2 serves not only as a nanocarrier but also as a stimulus-responsive and NIR-controlled nanophotonic platform for low-background biosensing.

DNA logic-gate-based sensing systems have recently attracted increasing attention in miRNA analysis because they can convert specific combinations of molecular inputs into programmable signal outputs. Among these, AND-gate logic requires the simultaneous presence of two or more inputs to generate an output signal, which is particularly useful for reducing nonspecific responses and improving diagnostic specificity. Representative DNA logic systems based on cascade strand displacement reactions and DNA-walker amplification have been reported for miRNA diagnostics, demonstrating the potential of DNA logic circuits in multibiomarker detection. − Although UCNP@mSiO2-based fluorescent sensing platforms have been extensively explored, most reported systems focus mainly on material-assisted signal conversion, controlled release, or single-biomarker detection. For disease diagnosis, single-miRNA detection may be insufficient because miRNA expression is often heterogeneous, and the same miRNA may also be aberrantly expressed under different pathological conditions. Therefore, developing dual-miRNA cooperative recognition strategies is important for improving diagnostic specificity and reducing false-positive results. The integration of UCNP@mSiO2-mediated optical activation with DNA “AND gate” recognition may provide a promising route for constructing low-background and highly specific miRNA sensing platforms.

Herein, a miRNA-detection platform based on “AND gate” operation was developed, achieving highly sensitive and specific detection of the dual biomarkers miR-21 and miR-155 through a GSH response and light control strategy (Scheme ). This platform uses UCNP as its core and is coated with an mSiO2 shell for loading three types of DNA chains: long chains containing the quencher BHQ2 (S2), short chains featuring photocleavable sites (PC linker) (S1), and short chains labeled with Cy5 (S3). In the presence of GSH, the mSiO2 shell is specifically cleaved, releasing DNA probes and UCNP. When excited by 808 nm NIR light, UCNP subsequently emits approximately 365 nm ultraviolet light, breaking the PC linker on S1 and revealing the anchor chain. The analytes miR-21 and miR-155 simultaneously displace S1 and S3 through a foothold-mediated chain displacement reaction, leading to the separation of Cy5 from the BHQ2 quencher and the restoration of the fluorescence signal, thereby allowing quantitative analysis. This detection system integrates the “AND gate” logic of “GSH trigger + light trigger” with the “dual-strand cooperative identification” principle, efficiently minimizing the risk of background noise and false positives. It offers the benefits of a user-friendly process and spatiotemporal controllability. This platform enables highly sensitive and specific determination of two miRNAs in complex biological specimens, offering a novel approach for early identification, progression assessment, and precise diagnosis of gliomas.

Preparation of UCNP

In this study, a NaGdF4:Yb,Tm core structure upconversion nanoparticle (core UCNP) was synthesized by a solvothermal method. Furthermore, a core–shell-structured upconversion nanoparticle (core–shell UCNP) was constructed by introducing a NaGdF4:Yb,Nd shell layer to increase the excitation efficiency of 808 nm near-infrared light and reduce the surface quenching effect. Subsequently, via a ligand exchange strategy, the hydrophobic oleic acid (OA) ligands on the particle surface were displaced by the cationic surfactant cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) to fabricate CTAB-modified UCNP (denoted as CTAB-UCNP). The CTAB decorated on the nanoparticle surface can serve as a structural template and provide reactive sites for the subsequent coating of the mesoporous silica (mSiO2) shell.

Synthesis of Core UCNP

We accurately weighed 0.1115 g of GdCl3·6H2O (0.3 mmol), 0.0038 g of TmCl3·6H2O (0.01 mmol), and 0.2674 g of YbCl3·6H2O (0.69 mmol) and added them to a 100 mL three-neck round-bottom flask containing 6 mL of oleic acid and 15 mL of 1-octadecene. After the experimental apparatus was set up, we opened the gas valve to introduce air and then heated it to 85 °C. Once the temperature stabilized, we continued heating it to 110 °C and preserved it for 30 min to remove moisture from the system. After confirming complete dissolution, we allowed the solution to cool naturally to 40 °C. We weighed 0.1 g of NaOH (2.5 mmol) and 0.148 g of NH4F (4 mmol) powder and added them to 10 mL of methanol. We then ultrasonicated the sample to ensure complete dissolution of the powder. We gradually added the NaOH-NH4F methanol solution to the cooled reaction mixture to complete nucleation. Afterward, the reaction solution was heated to 80 °C and left to stand for 20 min to completely remove the methanol from the system. It was further heated to 110 °C and left to stand for 20 min to remove the remaining moisture. Afterward, the reaction solution was heated to 300 °C and left to stand for 60 min for the crystallization reaction (when the temperature exceeded 150 °C), a condenser was added). First, 10 mL of ethanol was added to the cooled reaction solution for dispersion, which was then placed in a centrifuge at a speed of 10,000 r/min for 5 min; the supernatant was discarded, and 15 mL of cyclohexane was added for dissolution (if the dissolution was incomplete, ultrasonic treatment was performed for 2 min). Then, 15 mL of deionized water was added, and the mixture was gently shaken 10 times before it was allowed to stand overnight. The upper cyclohexane phase was removed, 10 times the volume of the same solvent was added, and the mixture was shaken evenly before it was allowed to stand for 3 min to allow the UCNP nuclei to precipitate. It was subsequently placed in a centrifuge at a speed of 10,000 r/min for 5 min to collect the precipitate, after which it was dispersed in 4 mL of cyclohexane for storage.

Synthesis of Core–Shell UCNP

We accurately weighed 0.1784 g of GdCl3·6H2O (0.48 mmol), 0.0310 g of YbCl3·6H2O (0.08 mmol), and 0.0861 g of NdCl3·6H2O (0.24 mmol) and added them to a 100 mL three-neck round-bottom flask containing 6 mL of oleic acid and 15 mL of 1-octadecene. At room temperature, argon gas was continuously introduced, and the mixture was stirred for 15 min. All subsequent experimental steps were carried out under argon protection and continuous stirring. The system was heated to 85 °C. If the temperature rose rapidly, it was promptly adjusted to 110 °C and kept at this temperature for 30 min to remove the moisture. The system was further heated to 160 °C. After complete dissolution was confirmed, the solution was naturally cooled to 80 °C. Four milliliters of a cyclohexane dispersion solution of the prepared 4 mL NaGdF4:Yb,Tm UCNP core was added dropwise to the cooled reaction solution and maintained at 80 °C for 30 min to ensure full immersion of the core surface. Afterward, the system was warmed to 110 °C and maintained at that temperature for 20 min to remove moisture, after which the solution was cooled to 40 °C. First, 0.1 g of NaOH (2.5 mmol) and 0.148 g of NH4F (4 mmol) powder were weighed and added to 10 mL of methanol. Ultrasonication was performed until the powder was completely dissolved. The NaOH-NH4F methanol solution was gradually added to the reaction mixture to complete the nucleation of the shell layer. Afterward, the temperature was raised to 110 °C and maintained at that temperature for 40 min to completely remove moisture from the system. The reaction mixture was heated to 300 °C and maintained at that temperature for 60 min for the shell layer crystallization reaction (a condenser was added when the temperature exceeded 150 °C). Ten milliliters of ethanol were added to the cooled reaction mixture and centrifuged at 10,000 r/min for 5 min; the supernatant was discarded; 15 mL of cyclohexane was added to dissolve it by shaking. Then, 15 mL of deionized water was added and it was gently inverted 10 times before being left to stand overnight. The upper cyclohexane phase was added to an equal volume of ethanol, mixed evenly, and allowed to stand for 3 min to allow the UCNP core–shell structure to precipitate and separate. The sample was subsequently centrifuged again at 10,000 r/min for 5 min to collect the precipitate, which was then dispersed in 4 mL of cyclohexane for storage.

Modification of UCNP

We weighed 0.1 g of CTAB and dissolved it in 20 mL of distilled water. After ultrasonic magnetic stirring, a clear, transparent solution was formed. Afterward, we dispersed the UCNP in cyclohexane as mentioned above. This OA-UCNP was transparent and clear. It was added to the CTAB solution, and an open flask was placed on a magnetic stirrer at room temperature for 24 h. The cyclohexane evaporated, resulting in the formation of CTAB-stabilized UCNP, which is called CTAB-UCNP.

We mixed 20 mL of distilled water and 3 mL of ethanol under magnetic stirring. We mixed 10 mL of the CTAB-UCNP solution mentioned above and slowly added it to a mixed solution of ethanol and water under magnetic stirring. The pH of the system was adjusted to 11 with NaOH solution, and the temperature was increased to 70 °C. It was kept at 70 °C, after which 200 μL of TEOS and 100 μL of BTES were slowly added (60 μL of TEOS was added once every 2 min, and the addition of 60 μL was completed). After waiting for 0.5 h, the remaining 240 μL of TEOS and BTES was added. The solution was slowly added to the bottle at 70 °C and vigorously stirred for 2 h. The electric heating bath was removed, and stirring was continued until the sample gradually cooled to room temperature. After balancing, centrifugation was performed, and the sample was subsequently washed three times with a mixture of ethanol and water. After drying, UCNP@mSiO2 could be obtained.

Construction of a Fluorescent Biosensor

To obtain the DNA AND (DA) logic gate for the detection of two miRNAs (miR-21 and miR-155), S1 (10 μM), S2 (10 μM), and S3 (10 μM) were mixed together and annealed at 95 °C for 5 min to form DA. Twenty μL of DNA double strands (10 μM) and 50 μL of the as-prepared UCNPs@mSiO2 were mixed with 100 μL of HEPES buffer (125 mM HEPES, 685 mM NaCl, pH 7.4), followed by incubation at room temperature for 1 h. After centrifugation, UCNP@mSiO2@DNA was acquired.

Analysis of MiR-21 and MiR-155

First, 30 μL of the as-prepared UCNPs@mSiO2@DNA was collected, and 15 μL of 10 mM glutathione (GSH) solution was added. Afterward, PBS solutions containing different concentrations of target miRNAs were introduced into the mixture. To determine the miR-21 levels, gradient concentrations of miR-21 were added to a mixture of UCNP@mSiO2@DNA upon the addition of excess miR-155 in PBS. For the quantification of miR-155, various concentrations of miR-155 were spiked into a PBS solution containing UCNP@mSiO2@DNA with excessive miR-21. The mixed solution was reacted at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by irradiation with 808 nm infrared light to trigger the strand displacement reaction. Finally, the solution was further incubated at 37 °C for several hours. The fluorescence spectra of the solution were recorded in the wavelength range of 655–700 nm under excitation at 643 nm.

Detection of miRNAs in Actual Serum Samples

To evaluate the detection performance of the as-constructed sensor in actual complex samples, human serum was selected as the matrix for carrying out the spiked recovery experiment. First, to reduce matrix interference, the raw serum sample was diluted 100-fold with PBS. Subsequently, the standard addition method was employed for detection: a series of miR-21 and miR-155 standards with known concentrations were precisely spiked into the diluted serum samples, followed by experimental operation and fluorescence measurement according to the procedures described in Section . The recovery rates of the target miRNAs were calculated to verify the accuracy and feasibility of this approach for the analysis of actual samples.

Results and Discussion

Preparation and Characterization of the Nanomaterials

Morphology and Structure

To confirm the morphology and structure of the prepared nanomaterials, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) were used to analyze the core UCNP, core–shell UCNP, and UCNP@mSiO2. As shown in Figure A, the core UCNP exhibits a uniform spherical morphology with a narrow particle size distribution and good dispersion. After coating with an inert NaGdF4:Yb,Nd shell, the particle size of the core–shell UCNP significantly increased, while good dispersibility was maintained, and no obvious agglomeration phenomenon was observed (Figure B). After further coating with mesoporous silica, a typical core–shell structure was clearly observed, and the UCNP was enveloped by a uniformly thick layer of silica with a mesoporous structure (Figure C), confirming the successful preparation of UCNP@mSiO2.

(A) TEM image of the core UCNP; (B) TEM image of the core–shell UCNP; (C) TEM image of UCNP@mSiO2; (D) XRD diffractograms of the nanomaterials; (E) EDS spectrum of UCNP@mSiO2.

The XRD characterization results are presented in Figure D. The core UCNP and core–shell UCNP exhibit sharp and clear diffraction peaks at 2θ values of approximately 17°, 27°, 30°, 31°, 35°, 40°, 44°, 52°, 54°, and 64°, indicating that the prepared UCNP has high crystallinity and good phase purity, and that no impurities were detected. After the mesoporous silica layer was coated, UCNP@mSiO2 retained all the characteristic diffraction peaks of UCNP, which confirms that the crystal structure of UCNP was maintained during the coating process. The decrease in the diffraction intensity of UCNP@mSiO2 is attributed to the signal attenuation effect of the amorphous silica shell, which is consistent with the previously reported silica-coated UCNP system.

Further analysis of the elemental composition of UCNP@mSiO2 was conducted using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), and the results are illustrated in Figure (E). In the EDS spectrum, rare-earth elements such as Yb, Tm, Gd, and Na were clearly observed, while Si and O clearly appeared, confirming the successful encapsulation of the mesoporous silica shell. The results of the EDS analysis are consistent with those of the TEM and XRD characterizations, verifying the gradual and successful construction of the core–shell structure and silica-coated nanocomposites.

Performance Analysis

To further verify the surface modification and gradual preparation process of the nanomaterials, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and zeta potential analysis were employed to characterize the surface chemical properties of the nanomaterials. As shown in Figure A, the wide absorption band of the original UCNP at 3413 cm–1 is attributed to the stretching vibration of O–H bonds between surface-adsorbed water molecules and terminal hydroxyl groups. CTAB-UCNP exhibited two distinct characteristic peaks at 2927 cm–1 and 2856 cm–1, corresponding to the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations of the C–H bond in the CTAB alkyl chain, confirming the successful coating of CTAB on the surface of UCNP. A series of characteristic absorption bands of the silica framework clearly appear in the spectrum of UCNP@mSiO2: 1078 cm–1 (Si–O–Si asymmetric stretching), 798 cm–1 (symmetric stretching), 458 cm–1 (bending vibration), and 962 cm–1 (Si–OH stretching). Moreover, the characteristic CTAB peak located at 730 cm–1 in the CTAB-UCNP spectrum completely disappeared in the UCNP@mSiO2 spectrum, indicating that the CTAB template was effectively removed and that the mesoporous silica shell was successfully formed. Further validation of the success of UCNP stepwise surface functionalization was carried out using zeta potential analysis, as shown in Figure B. The original UCNP has a negative zeta potential of approximately −12 ± 5 mV, and after CTAB modification, the zeta potential of the CTAB-UCNP was positive at 67 ± 5 mV, confirming the successful coating of the positively charged CTAB bilayer on the surface of the UCNP. After mesoporous silica was coated, the zeta potential of UCNP@mSiO2 decreased to −30 ± 4 mV because of the abundance of deprotonated silanol groups on the surface of mSiO2 in a neutral aqueous solution. After the DNA recognition probe was covalently attached to the UCNP@mSiO2 surface, the zeta potential of the obtained UCNP@mSiO2-DNA further decreased to −35 ± 3 mV, which was attributed to the negatively charged phosphate backbone in the DNA strand. The above results clearly indicate that the gradual preparation process of the material and the successful construction of nucleic acid biosensing can be used for subsequent miRNA detection.

Following the confirmation of successful material preparation, the optical response properties of the nanomaterials were assessed. To evaluate the enhancing effect of the core–shell structure on the upconversion luminescence performance, the luminescence spectra of the core UCNP and core–shell UCNP were measured under 808 nm NIR irradiation. As shown in Figure C, both nanoparticles exhibit typical Tm3+ characteristic emission peaks, with the emission band located at approximately 365 nm D2 → H4), which corresponds perfectly to the characteristic electronic transitions of Tm3+. Notably, the luminescence intensity of the core–shell UCNP is substantially greater than that of the core UCNP in all the emission bands (Figure D). This enhancement is attributed to the effective surface passivation effect of the inert NaGdF4 shell grown by epitaxy: this inert shell can effectively isolate the core UCNP from surface quenchers in the surrounding environment, suppress non-radiative relaxation caused by surface defects, and significantly improve the luminescence efficiency. These results are consistent with those of previous reports, confirming that constructing core–shell structures is an effective strategy for enhancing the luminescence performance of UCNP. In contrast, after the mesoporous silica was coated, the luminescence intensity of UCNP@mSiO2 significantly decreased, mainly because of the light-scattering effect of the silica shell and the nonradiative energy transfer of its surface hydroxyl groups. This phenomenon is mutually confirmed by the TEM and XRD results, which together confirm the successful encapsulation of the mesoporous silica shell and lay a favorable basis for the subsequent loading of nucleic acid DNA probes. Importantly, this luminescence quenching effect can be effectively restored under the triggering of GSH, thereby endowing the material with excellent stimulus-response performance.

On the basis of the optical response properties, the GSH-responsive performance of UCNP@mSiO2 was further investigated. Therefore, the luminescence response behavior of the nanoprobe was investigated at different incubation times and concentrations of GSH. As shown in Figure E, after UCNP@mSiO2 was incubated with GSH for 2–14 h, the luminescence intensity at 360 nm continued to increase with increasing incubation time and plateaued after 12 h. This time-dependent luminescence recovery is attributed to GSH-triggered mSiO2 shell-specific cleavage, which alleviated the luminescence quenching effect caused by silica. Further investigation of the changes in luminescence intensity after treatment with gradient concentrations of GSH (10–40 nM) was conducted, as shown in Figure F. The luminescence intensity of all the treatment groups increased in a time-dependent manner, and the degree of increase was positively correlated with the concentration of GSH. This phenomenon is attributed to the higher concentration of GSH providing more reactive thiol groups and accelerating the efficiency of mSiO2 shell cleavage. The above results indicate that the UCNP@mSiO2 nanoprobes have GSH response performance, making them suitable for controlled release and biosensing applications in complex biological environments.

Detection Principle and Verification of Feasibility

Herein, a miRNA detection approach based on “AND gate” logic was developed, which achieved highly sensitive and specific determination of miR-21 and miR-155 through GSH-responsive and light-controlled strategies (Figure A). The assay is designed around the UCNP and wrapped with an mSiO2 shell for loading three types of DNA strands: S2 containing BHQ2, S1 containing a PC linker, and S3 containing Cy5. In the presence of GSH, the mSiO2 shell is broken, releasing DNA probes and UCNP. Upon excitation at 808 nm, UCNP emits 365 nm ultraviolet light, breaking the PC linker of S1 and exposing the toehold chain. The target miR-21 and miR-155 undergo a toehold-mediated chain displacement reaction, simultaneously replacing S1 and S3, causing Cy5 to move away from BHQ2 and restore fluorescence, enabling quantitative analysis.

(A) Schematic diagram of the sensor determination principle; (B) definition of the activation conditions, molecular inputs, fluorescence output, threshold criterion, and truth table for the dual-miRNA AND-gate sensing system; (C) verification of strand exchange by PAGE electrophoresis; (D) comparison of fluorescence response intensity under different target combinations.

The unique advantage of this sensing mechanism lies in its dual-miRNA cooperative recognition rather than in the UCNP@mSiO2 structure alone. In this system, miR-21 and miR-155 act as two independent molecular inputs. A single-target miRNA can only partially interact with the DNA assembly and is insufficient to generate an efficient fluorescence output. In contrast, when miR-21 and miR-155 coexist, they cooperatively drive the toehold-mediated strand-displacement process, resulting in the effective separation of Cy5 from BHQ2 and significant fluorescence recovery. Therefore, the system converts the simultaneous presence of two glioma-associated miRNA biomarkers into a single fluorescence output, which improves the recognition specificity and minimizes false-positive responses caused by single-marker fluctuation or interfering miRNAs.

To further clarify the AND-gate mechanism of the sensing system, the inputs, output, and threshold criterion were defined, as shown in Figure B. The system consists of an upstream activation AND gate and a downstream dual-miRNA recognition AND gate.

In the activation stage, GSH and 808 nm NIR irradiation serve as two inputs, where the input states “0” and “1” represent absence and presence, respectively. Only when both inputs are present can the UCNP@mSiO2@DNA platform be activated. In this process, GSH promotes the exposure or release of DNA probes, while 808 nm irradiation triggers the UCNP-mediated photoresponsive process. After activation, miR-21 and miR-155 serve as the inputs of the second AND gate. Only when both miRNAs are simultaneously present can a strand-displacement reaction occur, resulting in the separation of Cy5 from BHQ2 and the generation of a fluorescence signal. The Cy5 fluorescence intensity at 665 nm was defined as the output signal. The threshold was set as the maximum value of “mean fluorescence intensity + three times the standard deviation” among all negative control groups in which any one of the required inputs was absent. Fluorescence signals below the threshold were defined as logic output “0”, whereas signals equal to or above the threshold were defined as logic output “1”. Therefore, only when GSH, 808 nm irradiation, miR-21, and miR-155 were simultaneously present did the system generate an output of “1”, confirming its cascaded AND-gate behavior.

Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) was used to verify the construction of the DNA detection assay on the basis of “AND gate” logic and the feasibility of the miRNA-triggered chain replacement reaction. The formation process of the system was characterized by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) (Figure C). Lanes 1 and 2 correspond to the individual target analytes, miR-155 and miR-21, respectively. Lanes 3 and 4 represent the duplex systems with the addition of one of the two targets, respectively, without near-infrared (NIR) light irradiation. Lanes 5 and 6 correspond to the same systems as Lanes 3 and 4, respectively, but subjected to 808 nm NIR light irradiation. A distinct band is clearly observed in Lane 8, where both targets were added after 808 nm NIR light irradiation, which is significantly different from that in Lane 7, where both targets were added without NIR light irradiation. This electrophoresis experiment clearly validates the feasibility of the proposed AND logic gate.

To verify the specific optical response of the constructed sensor to the synergistic combination of miR-21 and miR-155, the fluorescence intensity was compared under different target combination conditions. As presented in Figure D, the sample group containing both miR-21 and miR-155 and irradiated at 808 nm exhibited a fluorescence intensity of approximately 1.48 × 106 a.u. at the characteristic emission peak at 665 nm. In contrast, the irradiation group containing only a single target miRNA (miR-21 or miR-155) and the nonirradiated dual-miRNA control group presented significantly weaker fluorescence intensity. The above results confirm that the presence of two target miRNAs is the molecular recognition basis for triggering signal amplification and that 808 nm near-infrared irradiation is necessary for achieving high signal-to-noise ratio fluorescence output. Therefore, this system can be employed for the joint specific measurement of miR-21 and miR-155.

Optimization of Experimental Parameters

To achieve the best detection sensitivity, the key experimental parameters in the developed sensing system were systematically optimized. In the detection system of miR-21 and miR-155, 808 nm light is used to activate the upconversion luminescence of UCNP, causing it to emit 365 nm ultraviolet light, thereby cleaving the PC bonds in the S1 chain and exposing the foothold sequence. Therefore, the illumination time (0∼28 min) was first optimized. As shown in Figure A, D, in the initial state (0 min), the background fluorescence emission of the sensing system can be ignored, which is attributed to the efficient fluorescence quenching effect between Cy5 and BHQ2 in the original nanocomposite material. With increasing illumination time, the Cy5 fluorescence intensity of the system at 665 nm continued to significantly increase and reached its maximum value at 24 min. Therefore, 24 min was determined as the optimal illumination time for the sensor. Subsequently, 808 nm light irradiation systems with different powers (0.25∼2.25 W) were used to investigate the effect of light irradiation power on the reaction system. As shown in Figure B, E, when the power exceeded 1.5 W/cm2, the increase in fluorescence intensity significantly slowed and plateaued. Therefore, 1.5 W/cm2 was chosen as the optimal illumination power for subsequent experiments. Finally, the effects of different incubation times (10∼60 min) on the fluorescence signal at an emission wavelength of 665 nm were investigated. As shown in Figure C, F, as the incubation time increased, the fluorescence intensity gradually increased and reached its maximum value at 40 min. Thus, 40 min was determined as the optimal incubation time for subsequent miRNA quantitative detection experiments.

Detection Performance of the Developed Biosensor

UCNP@mSiO2-DNA biosensors equipped with S1 (including a PC linker), S2 (including BHQ2), and S3 (including Cy5) were used. In the presence of GSH, the mSiO2 shell cleavage releases UCNP and DNA strands. After excitation with 808 nm light, UCNP emits 365 nm ultraviolet light to cleave the PC linker of S1, exposing the foothold sequence. The target miR-21 and miR-155 simultaneously replace S1 and S3 through a chain displacement reaction, leading to the separation of Cy5 from BHQ2, restoring fluorescence, and achieving quantitative detection. Under optimized conditions, different concentrations of miR-21 and miR-155 were added to the detection system, and the fluorescence spectra at 665 nm were recorded. As depicted in Figure A, as the concentration of miR-21 increased, the fluorescence signal at 665 nm gradually increased, suggesting that the system was concentration-dependent. In the range of 0.5∼10 nM, there was a good linear relationship between the fluorescence intensity and the miR-21 concentration (Figure B), and the linear regression equation was Y = 20726X + 38638, with a correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.9925. According to the 3σ/k method, the limit of detection (LOD) of miR-21 was calculated to be 0.181 nM. Similarly, as shown in Figure C, with increasing miR-155 concentration, the fluorescence intensity at 665 nm significantly increased. In the concentration range of 0.5∼10 nM, there was a good linear relationship between the fluorescence intensity and the miR-155 concentration (Figure D), and the linear regression equation was Y = 20228X + 38172, with a correlation coefficient of R2 = 0.9995. According to the 3σ/k method, the LOD of miR-155 was calculated to be 0.185 nM. This work was compared with reported miRNA detection methods (Table ). Although the detection limit of this method is slightly lower than or comparable to that of some methods, its outstanding advantage is that it does not involve enzymes or additional signal amplification steps and can simultaneously measure two miRNAs. These findings indicate that the designed nanoprobes are suitable for simple and sensitive detection of multiple miRNAs.

Comparison of Different Methods for Detecting MicroRNAs

In addition, the ability of the developed sensor to recognize target miRNAs is crucial for its practical applications in complex biological samples. Therefore, the sequence selectivity of the sensor was further evaluated by comparing the fluorescence responses toward fully matched target miRNAs, single-base mismatched miRNAs, and nontarget miRNAs. As shown in Figure E, the Cy5 fluorescence signal at 665 nm was significantly restored in the presence of both fully matched miR-21 and miR-155, indicating efficient dual-target recognition and fluorescence recovery. In contrast, compared with the fully matched target group, the single-base mismatched group, DNA + miR-21–1M + miR-155–1M + NIR, exhibited a markedly reduced fluorescence intensity. Moreover, other interfering miRNA groups, including the DNA + miR-21 + miR-222 + NIR, DNA + miR-122 + miR-222 + NIR, and DNA + miR-21–1M + miR-122 + NIR groups, produced only weak fluorescence responses. These results indicate that the constructed sensor can effectively distinguish fully matched target miRNAs from single-base mismatched and nontarget miRNAs with similar sequences, demonstrating its good sequence selectivity and potential applicability in complex biological samples. Finally, to verify the detection stability of the sensor, its performance over 8 days was evaluated (Figure F). The results showed that, during the testing period, the sensor maintained good consistency in its detection response to miR-21 and miR-155, demonstrating excellent detection stability. In summary, the prepared biosensor has good specificity and high stability and can achieve stable and specific recognition and detection of miR-21 and miR-155 in complex physiological environments.

Testing in Actual Samples

To assess the quantification capability of the developed approach in actual complex samples, serum samples were selected as actual detection objects for spiked recovery experiments. To reduce matrix interference, the original serum sample was diluted 100 times with PBS. Using the standard addition method, different concentrations (1.0, 5.0, and 10.0 nM) of miR-21 and miR-155 standards were accurately added to the diluted serum samples to construct spiked serum samples. Under optimized conditions, the spiked serum sample was introduced into the constructed sensing system, and the fluorescence signal was recorded at 665 nm. By comparison of the theoretical spiked concentration with the actual measured concentration, the recovery rate and relative standard deviation (RSD) of the sample were calculated to evaluate the accuracy and repeatability of the detection method. As shown in Table , under different spiked concentration conditions, the measured miRNA concentrations were close to the theoretical spiked concentrations. The spiked recovery rate of miR-21 ranged from 92% to 100.69%, with a relative standard deviation (RSD) below 7.0%, specifically within the range of 0.8% to 6.15%. For miR-155, the recovery rate ranged from 96.78% to 100.66%, with an RSD ranging from 1.29% to 5.3%. The above results indicate that the sensing system has good accuracy and repeatability for the detection of two-analyte miRNAs in biological matrices.

Conclusion

In this study, an “AND gate” logic fluorescence sensing platform based on UCNP was constructed for the highly sensitive and specific determination of miR-21 and miR-155. This approach features a core–shell structure UCNP as its core, which is encapsulated with an mSiO2 shell responsive to GSH, achieving efficient loading and controlled release of three functional DNA strands. Through the dual “AND gate” logic of “GSH trigger + light trigger” and the “dual-strand cooperative identification” principle, the activation threshold of detection is significantly improved, effectively enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio while minimizing false positives. The results revealed that the sensing platform exhibited good linear relationships in the range of 0.5∼10 nM for miR-21 and 0.5∼10 nM for miR-155, with LODs of 0.181 nM and 0.185 nM, respectively, and demonstrated excellent specificity, stability, and anti-interference ability. A spiked recovery experiment in actual serum samples further validated its accuracy and reliability in complex biological samples (a recovery rate of 92.0%∼100.69%, RSD < 7%). In summary, this sensing platform is easy to operate and does not require additional signal amplification steps. It can achieve precise detection of dual-target miRNAs in complex biological samples, providing a new strategy with good application prospects for early screening, progression monitoring, and accurate diagnosis of gliomas. However, this study was mainly validated using spiked diluted serum samples, which cannot fully represent real clinical or intracellular environments. In future work, we will further evaluate this platform using clinical serum samples from glioma patients and more complex biological matrices to better assess its diagnostic potential.
