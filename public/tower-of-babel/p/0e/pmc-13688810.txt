Ceftazidime-avibactam as a polymyxin-sparing option for multidrug-resistant Gram-negative infections in children: a real-world comparative cohort study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative infections are challenging to treat in children, and polymyxin-based regimens are limited by toxicity. Children aged < 18 years who received definitive therapy for microbiologically confirmed MDR Gram-negative infections between January 2022 and May 2024 were included. The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality. Background

The global rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), particularly among Gram-negative pathogens, represents a major public health challenge and is associated with substantial mortality and healthcare burden worldwide [1, 2]. Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) and other multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative organisms have emerged as important causes of severe infections in children, contributing to prolonged hospitalization, limited therapeutic options and increased mortality [3–5].

Historically, severe MDR Gram-negative infections in children have been treated with older agents such as colistin, often in combination with carbapenems. Although these regimens remain necessary when effective alternatives are unavailable, their use is limited by nephrotoxicity, dosing complexity and uncertain efficacy in critically ill paediatric patients [4–6]. Accordingly, contemporary international guidelines recommend newer β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations for selected resistant Gram-negative infections whenever susceptibility data support their use, underscoring the importance of polymyxin-sparing treatment strategies [7, 8].

Ceftazidime-avibactam (CZA) is a combination of a third-generation cephalosporin and a non-β-lactam β-lactamase inhibitor. It is active against organisms producing Ambler class A, class C and selected class D β-lactamases, including many KPC-producing CRE and OXA-48-like-producing Enterobacterales. However, it lacks reliable activity against metallo-β-lactamase-producing organisms unless combined with an appropriate companion agent, such as aztreonam [7, 8]. Clinical trials have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of CZA in children with complicated urinary tract and intra-abdominal infections, while more recent pharmacokinetic and safety studies have expanded clinical experience to neonates and young infants [9–11].

Despite these advances, comparative real-world paediatric data remain scarce. Published evidence consists mainly of case series, paediatric intensive care cohorts and small observational studies, whereas recent systematic reviews suggest that CZA is a promising treatment option when alternatives are limited [12–20]. In Türkiye, CZA was approved in 2019; however, access during the study period was influenced by national reimbursement policies that restricted its use to selected hospitalized patients, particularly those requiring intensive care. A subsequent reimbursement revision in 2026 broadened inpatient access [21–23]. These policy-related restrictions may have influenced treatment selection and the clinical characteristics of children receiving CZA in routine practice. We therefore evaluated the clinical outcomes and safety of CZA-based therapy compared with non-CZA regimens in children with MDR Gram-negative infections treated at a tertiary referral center. This study is framed as a descriptive comparative cohort designed to generate real-world evidence on feasibility, safety and polymyxin-sparing use, rather than to establish causal comparative effectiveness.

Methods

Study design and setting

This single-center retrospective comparative cohort study was conducted at a tertiary university-affiliated hospital in Istanbul, Türkiye. The study followed the principles of the STROBE reporting framework for observational studies where applicable [24].

The aim was to describe treatment patterns, clinical outcomes and safety among children receiving CZA-based definitive therapy compared with non-CZA definitive regimens for confirmed MDR Gram-negative infections. Because treatment assignment was determined by routine clinical care rather than randomisation, all between-group comparisons were interpreted as observational and vulnerable to confounding by indication.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Marmara University Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee (09.2024.616) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design and use of existing clinical records.

Participants

Children < 18 years of age admitted between January 2022 and May 2024 were eligible if they received definitive antimicrobial therapy for a microbiologically confirmed MDR Gram-negative bacterial infection. Patients were included once the index culture and antimicrobial susceptibility results identified an MDR Gram-negative pathogen considered the primary therapeutic target. Patients with incomplete records or polymicrobial infections in which the Gram-negative organism was not the primary therapeutic target were excluded.

Patients could contribute more than one infectious episode during the study period if each episode represented a distinct microbiologically confirmed MDR Gram-negative infection requiring a separate course of definitive antimicrobial therapy. Episodes representing continuation of the same infection were not considered separate study episodes. Each eligible infectious episode was analysed independently.

Definitions

MDR was defined according to the international interim standard definition as non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories relevant to Gram-negative bacteria [3]. Infection syndromes, including pneumonia, bacteraemia, urinary tract infection, meningitis and skin/soft tissue infection, were classified according to the treating team’s diagnosis supported by compatible clinical, radiographic, laboratory and microbiological evidence documented in the medical record, with reference to CDC/NHSN site-specific infection criteria where applicable [25].

Rectal colonization was defined as the asymptomatic gastrointestinal carriage of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacilli. According to the institutional infection control protocol, a universal hospital-wide screening strategy was not implemented; instead, targeted active surveillance was performed for all eligible patients. This targeted approach specifically focused on high-risk pediatric populations, including patients admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU), neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), hematology-oncology services, and bone marrow transplant (BMT) units. Additionally, individuals with a history of prolonged prior exposure to broad-spectrum antibiotics and patients requiring mechanical ventilation were included in the screening protocol.

Screening was performed upon admission and repeated weekly for all patients within the designated target risk group. Patients with negative surveillance cultures continued weekly screening under standard precautions. Conversely, patients with positive results (colonized individuals) were promptly managed under strict contact isolation, and antimicrobial treatment was withheld for isolated colonization. For colonized patients who subsequently developed clinical infections, the empirical antimicrobial therapy was appropriately adjusted based on the specific resistance profile and susceptibility patterns identified during rectal surveillance.

Clinical response was defined as improvement in syndrome-specific signs and symptoms, defervescence when applicable, improvement or non-progression of inflammatory markers, and no documented need for escalation because of clinical failure. Documented microbiological clearance was defined as a negative follow-up culture for the index pathogen from the initially positive site. Follow-up cultures were obtained according to clinical indication rather than a predefined protocol. Consequently, repeat cultures were not performed in some patients after successful source control, when repeat invasive sampling was not clinically justified, or when patients died before microbiological clearance could be documented. Therefore, the primary comparative microbiological endpoint should be interpreted as documentation-dependent clearance rather than true pathogen eradication. Absence of a qualifying follow-up culture was conservatively treated as absence of documented clearance. Time to culture negativity was calculated only among patients with documented negative follow-up cultures.

The primary outcome was 28 day all-cause mortality after the index infectious episode. One-year all-cause mortality was also recorded. Secondary outcomes included clinical response, documented microbiological clearance, acute kidney injury (AKI), drug-induced liver injury (DILI).

Treatment classification and comparator regimen

Treatment selection reflected routine clinical practice and physician discretion, considering susceptibility results, previous antimicrobial exposure, infection syndrome, perceived severity, and drug availability. The CZA group included patients who received CZA alone or in combination as definitive therapy. Patients initially treated with a non-CZA regimen who were subsequently escalated to CZA because of inadequate clinical and/or microbiological response, or after antimicrobial susceptibility results supported CZA use, were classified in the CZA group, as treatment groups were defined according to the definitive antimicrobial regimen. Clinical and microbiological outcomes were attributed to the definitive treatment regimen. No patients receiving definitive CZA therapy were subsequently switched to a non-CZA regimen. The non-CZA group received conventional non-CZA regimens, most commonly meropenem plus colistin or meropenem plus an aminoglycoside. Because the comparator arm was clinically heterogeneous, definitive treatment regimens were tabulated in detail in Additional file 1, and a predefined subgroup analysis compared CZA with the most common conventional regimen, meropenem plus colistin.

Ceftazidime-avibactam was administered according to the FDA-approved pediatric dosing regimen (62,5 mg/kg every 8 h), with dose adjustments based on body weight and renal function where applicable. Meropenem was administered as intermittent infusions rather than prolonged or continuous infusions, generally at total daily doses of 60–120 mg/kg/day according to the infection type and clinical indication. Comparator regimens otherwise followed standard institutional pediatric dosing practices.

Microbiology

Pathogen identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed as part of routine clinical microbiology practice. Carbapenemase production was available as a binary variable in the dataset. Carbapenemase enzyme types such as KPC, NDM, VIM, IMP or OXA-48-like enzymes, minimum inhibitory concentration values and full antimicrobial susceptibility profiles were not consistently retrievable and were therefore not available for analysis.

Safety outcomes

AKI was assessed using KDIGO criteria [26]. DILI was assessed using prespecified laboratory thresholds with upper-limit-of-normal and baseline adjustment, informed by consensus DILI phenotype definitions [27]. Safety outcomes were evaluated from available laboratory and clinical records. In addition to AKI and DILI, medical records were reviewed for documented hypersensitivity reactions, including skin rash. Other potential adverse events, such as hematologic or neurologic toxicity, could not be systematically assessed because of the retrospective study design and were therefore not included in the predefined safety analysis. Need for renal replacement therapy, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) or plasma exchange specifically attributable to drug-induced toxicity was recorded.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SPSS version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were summarised as n (%) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were mostly non-normally distributed and are reported as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test.

No formal power calculation was performed because the study included all eligible patients during the prespecified period. Given the small cohort and 18 deaths, multivariable logistic regression and propensity-score methods were not pursued in the revised analysis to avoid overfitting and unstable effect estimates. Underlying comorbidities and baseline severity proxies are presented descriptively, and all outcome comparisons should be interpreted as exploratory. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Definitive regimens, microbiology and underlying diseases

Definitive antimicrobial regimens are detailed in Additional file 1. In the CZA group, 12 patients received CZA alone, two received CZA plus amikacin and one received CZA plus trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. In the non-CZA group, meropenem plus colistin was the most common regimen (14/30, 46.7%), followed by meropenem plus amikacin (8/30, 26.7%) and other meropenem-based multidrug combinations (5/30, 16.7%).

Pseudomonas aeruginosa was the most common pathogen (23/45, 51.1%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia (9/45 each, 20.0%). Pneumonia was the most common infection syndrome (23/45, 51.1%), followed by bacteraemia (12/45, 26.7%). Underlying disease categories were heterogeneous; malignancy and primary immunodeficiency were the most frequent categories overall. Detailed distributions of pathogens, infection syndromes and underlying diseases are provided in Additional file 1.

Documented microbiological clearance occurred in 46.7% and 63.3% of patients in the CZA and non-CZA groups, respectively (P = 0.286). Among patients with documented negative follow-up cultures, time to culture negativity did not differ between groups. Absence of documented microbiological clearance should not be interpreted as microbiological treatment failure, as follow-up cultures were obtained according to clinical indication rather than a predefined protocol. Several patients died before microbiological clearance could be documented, whereas others did not undergo repeat sampling because source control had been achieved, further invasive sampling was not clinically indicated, or clinical recovery occurred without microbiological reassessment. In addition, persistently positive respiratory cultures despite clinical improvement were observed in some patients with pneumonia. Accordingly, this outcome should be interpreted as documented microbiological clearance rather than true microbiological eradication.

AKI and DILI were infrequent and did not differ significantly between the groups. Hemodialysis within 30 days before or around the index episode was recorded in two patients in the non-CZA group and in none of the CZA-treated patients (Table 1). No documented hypersensitivity reactions, including skin rash, were identified during chart review. Other adverse events, including hematologic and neurologic toxicities, could not be systematically evaluated because of the retrospective study design. CRRT or plasma exchange specifically initiated for drug-induced toxicity also could not be reliably adjudicated from the available records.

Discussion

In this retrospective single-center paediatric cohort, ceftazidime-avibactam (CZA)-based therapy demonstrated comparable observed clinical response, 28-day mortality and safety outcomes to conventional non-CZA regimens in children with multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative infections. Importantly, these outcomes were achieved despite the CZA group including patients with more frequent prior carbapenem exposure and recent corticosteroid use, factors generally associated with greater clinical complexity and antimicrobial resistance burden. Our findings therefore support the use of CZA as a feasible polymyxin-sparing therapeutic option in selected paediatric patients with difficult-to-treat MDR Gram-negative infections.

The clinical relevance of these findings is substantial. Therapeutic options for MDR Gram-negative infections in children remain limited, and conventional regimens frequently rely on polymyxin-based combinations associated with substantial nephrotoxicity and treatment burden. In recent years, increasing emphasis has been placed on minimising polymyxin exposure whenever active β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor alternatives are available [7, 8]. Within this context, the absence of an apparent excess mortality or toxicity signal in the CZA group is noteworthy and aligns with the growing international shift toward newer β-lactam-based strategies for appropriately selected resistant pathogens.

An important observation in the present study is that CZA appeared to be preferentially reserved for clinically more complex or treatment-experienced children. Patients receiving CZA had more frequent prior carbapenem exposure, higher rates of recent steroid use and numerically greater carbapenemase positivity. This likely reflects real-world prescribing behaviour in which access to newer agents is frequently restricted to patients perceived to have higher resistance risk or more severe illness. Consequently, the comparable outcomes observed between groups should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence; rather, they may suggest that CZA achieved similar outcomes despite being used in a population with potentially less favourable baseline characteristics. This interpretation is particularly relevant in healthcare settings where reimbursement limitations or antimicrobial stewardship policies influence access to novel agents. In addition, in our setting, treatment options for carbapenem-resistant infections are constrained by limited availability of novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor agents, which are reserved for selected indications. Therefore, carbapenem-resistant infections in non-eligible cases were often managed with combination regimens including meropenem plus colistin and/or amikacin based on susceptibility results and clinical judgment. The predefined comparison with the most frequently used conventional regimen (meropenem plus colistin) partially reduced the impact of comparator heterogeneity; however, residual heterogeneity remained because the overall non-CZA group included several different treatment combinations.

Our findings are broadly concordant with previous paediatric observational studies reporting favourable clinical outcomes with CZA in severe MDR Gram-negative infections, including infections caused by carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales and difficult-to-treat Pseudomonas aeruginosa [12–16]. However, the generalisability of both our findings and the existing literature should be interpreted with caution because the epidemiology of MDR Gram-negative pathogens, carbapenemase mechanisms, antimicrobial susceptibility patterns and access to newer antimicrobial agents vary substantially across geographic regions. Much of the published paediatric experience with CZA has originated from Mediterranean countries, including Türkiye, Greece, Italy and Spain, where OXA-48-like carbapenemases are relatively prevalent. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted within the context of local epidemiology, resistance mechanisms, antimicrobial availability and stewardship practices.

Existing paediatric evidence, although still limited, has generally demonstrated acceptable tolerability and promising effectiveness of CZA across heterogeneous clinical settings, including paediatric intensive care units, oncology patients and medically complex children [12–20]. Our study adds comparative real-world data from a tertiary referral center and extends the available evidence by directly comparing CZA-based therapy with conventional non-CZA regimens. Although no significant differences in effectiveness or safety outcomes were observed, our findings support the feasibility of CZA as a polymyxin-sparing option in appropriately selected paediatric patients.

Interpretation of the microbiological outcome requires caution because follow-up cultures were obtained according to clinical indications rather than a standardized protocol. Patients without documented microbiological clearance included those who died before clearance could be documented and those in whom repeat cultures were not clinically indicated after source control or clinical recovery. In addition, persistently positive respiratory cultures may not reflect microbiological treatment failure despite clinical improvement. Therefore, this endpoint should be interpreted as documented microbiological clearance rather than true microbiological eradication.

Acute kidney injury and drug-induced liver injury were infrequent, and no clear increase in adverse events was identified in the CZA group despite extensive prior antimicrobial exposure in many patients. Numerically, AKI occurred less frequently in the CZA group than in the comparator group (6.7% vs. 17.2%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance. We do not interpret this finding as evidence of comparable nephrotoxicity between CZA- and polymyxin-containing regimens. Rather, given the small sample size and the low number of AKI events, our study was likely underpowered to detect clinically meaningful differences in nephrotoxicity. Nevertheless, the observed trend toward fewer AKI events is consistent with the rationale for polymyxin-sparing strategies and warrants further evaluation in larger prospective studies.

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design introduces the potential for residual confounding and selection bias and precludes causal inference. Second, the relatively small sample size limited statistical power and precluded robust multivariable or propensity score-based analyses. In addition, some patients contributed more than one infectious episode during the study period, and analyses were therefore performed at the episode level without adjustment for within-patient correlation. Given the small sample size, statistical methods accounting for correlated observations were not applied. Third, the comparator arm was heterogeneous and included several different definitive treatment regimens, although the predefined comparison with meropenem plus colistin partially addressed this limitation. Fourth, standardized severity-of-illness scores (e.g., PRISM, PELOD and PIM), detailed organ dysfunction parameters, vasoactive support requirements and lactate measurements were not consistently available, limiting adjustment for baseline disease severity. Fifth, carbapenemase subtype data, minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values and comprehensive antimicrobial susceptibility profiles were not uniformly available, limiting detailed microbiological interpretation. Finally, mortality assessment was limited to all-cause mortality because infection-attributable mortality could not be reliably adjudicated retrospectively, and microbiological follow-up was not standardized across infection syndromes, limiting interpretation of documented microbiological clearance as a measure of treatment success.

Despite these limitations, the study provides clinically relevant real-world paediatric data in an area where evidence remains scarce. The inclusion of a direct comparator cohort, detailed regimen reporting and concurrent evaluation of both effectiveness and safety outcomes strengthen the clinical applicability of the findings. Importantly, the study reflects routine practice in a tertiary-care setting where treatment decisions are shaped not only by microbiological data but also by drug availability, reimbursement restrictions and antimicrobial stewardship considerations.

Conclusions

CZA-based therapy was associated with comparable outcomes with conventional non-CZA regimens without a clear excess toxicity signal in children with MDR Gram-negative infections, despite preferential use in patients with potentially greater resistance burden and clinical complexity. These findings support the role of CZA as a feasible polymyxin-sparing option in selected paediatric patients when guided by susceptibility testing, infection source and local epidemiology [7, 8]. Larger prospective multicenter studies incorporating standardised severity assessment, detailed microbiological characterisation and predefined comparative analyses are needed to better define the optimal place of CZA in paediatric MDR Gram-negative infection management.
