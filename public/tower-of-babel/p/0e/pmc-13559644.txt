A nomogram integrating multidimensional lymph node indicators with clinicopathological factors predicts overall survival in non-metastatic gastric cancer patients after radical gastrectomy
Authors: Longjiang Fu, Wenjie Zhu, Jipeng Liu, Jun Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-metastatic gastric cancer patients undergoing radical gastrectomy still show substantial prognostic heterogeneity. The conventional American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system, particularly N stage, may be affected by variations in examined lymph node (ELN), resulting in stage migration. We aimed to evaluate the efficacy of a multifactor model incorporating the log odds of positive lymph nodes (LODDS), ELN, and N stage in survival prediction for patients with non-metastatic gastric cancer undergoing radical gastrectomy, and to construct an accurate prognostic prediction model.MethodsClinicopathological data of patients with non-metastatic gastric cancer who underwent radical gastrectomy between 2010 and 2019 were extracted from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Independent risk factors for overall survival (OS) were determined using univariate Cox regression, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, and multivariate Cox analysis. A nomogram was subsequently established according to these factors. The prognostic performance and clinical utility of the model were evaluated in the training, validation, and overall cohorts using the concordance index (C-index), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, Akaike information criterion (AIC), calibration curves, net reclassification improvement (NRI), integrated discrimination improvement (IDI), and decision curve analysis (DCA). Patients were stratified into different risk groups based on the total nomogram scores, and the discriminatory ability was validated using Kaplan-Meier survival curves.ResultsMultivariate Cox analysis revealed that age, race, tumor size, T stage, N stage, ELN, LODDS, and chemotherapy status were independent prognostic factors. The areas under the curves (AUCs) of the nomogram for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in the training, validation, and overall cohorts all exceeded 0.76, outperforming the AJCC TNM staging system (all AUCs <0.72). Calibration curves demonstrated close agreement between predicted and observed survival outcomes. DCA, NRI, and IDI analyses further confirmed that the nomogram provided superior clinical net benefit and improved discrimination compared with the TNM staging system. The risk stratification (low-, middle-, and high-risk groups) based on the model scores significantly distinguished different survival outcomes (P<0.001).ConclusionsThe nomogram integrating multidimensional factors including LODDS, ELN, and traditional TNM staging is a reliable prognostic tool for patients with non-metastatic gastric cancer after radical gastrectomy. Compared with the conventional TNM staging system, this model exhibits superior predictive accuracy and clinical applicability, assisting clinicians in individualized prognostic assessment and adjuvant treatment decision making.

Highlight box

Key findings

• The nomogram integrating eight independent prognostic factors [age, race, tumor size, T stage, N stage, examined lymph node (ELN), log odds of positive lymph nodes (LODDS), and chemotherapy] achieved superior predictive accuracy for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival (OS) in non-metastatic gastric cancer patients after radical gastrectomy, with all areas under the curves (AUCs) exceeding 0.76 across training, validation, and overall cohorts, outperforming the traditional American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system (all AUCs <0.72).

• Risk stratification based on total nomogram scores (low-risk: ≤162; middle-risk: 163–270; high-risk: ≥271) significantly distinguished survival outcomes among the three groups (P<0.001)

What is known and what is new?

• The AJCC TNM staging system, particularly N stage, is the standard for gastric cancer prognosis prediction but is limited by stage migration due to variations in ELNs. LODDS has shown prognostic value in multiple cancers.

• This study innovatively incorporated three complementary lymph node indicators (N stage, ELN, and LODDS) together with clinicopathological factors into a single nomogram. The model demonstrated superior discrimination, calibration, and clinical net benefit compared to the AJCC system, effectively mitigating stage migration bias.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This nomogram provides clinicians with a more accurate and individualized tool for postoperative prognostic assessment, enabling identification of high-risk patients who may require more aggressive adjuvant therapy and personalized follow-up strategies.

• The model can serve as an effective complement to existing staging systems for individualized postoperative survival prediction. Future studies will further conduct external validation to assess its generalizability.

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains among the most prevalent and lethal cancers globally, with its occurrence ranking fifth and mortality ranking fourth across all malignancies (1). The number of gastric cancer patients globally is gradually increasing, placing a heavy burden on public health (2,3). Gastric cancer is highly invasive and has a poor prognosis, with 30–40% of patients already presenting with distant metastasis at the time of initial diagnosis. These patients typically lose the opportunity for curative surgical resection (4). For gastric cancer patients without distant metastasis at initial diagnosis, radical surgical resection is the primary means of achieving a cure (5). Compared to patients with distant metastasis, these patients are generally considered to have a better prognosis. However, even after undergoing radical surgery, significant individual differences in survival outcomes persist, with a considerable number of patients experiencing recurrence or metastasis shortly after surgery, ultimately leading to death (6). This prognostic heterogeneity suggests that relying solely on the traditional tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system is insufficient to fully capture individual patient risk.

The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system currently serves as the foundation for directing gastric cancer therapy and predicting prognosis (7). However, it relies primarily on anatomical information, such as the depth of tumor invasion, the number of metastatic lymph nodes, and the presence of distant metastasis, and fails to fully incorporate individual specific factors, such as age and race, as well as treatment related factors that play a critical role in prognosis (8). Furthermore, the N stage within the AJCC TNM system, as a key prognostic indicator for gastric cancer, ranks among the most significant prognostic determinants during the 5-year period following radical gastrectomy (9). Nevertheless, the reliability of the N stage as it has been used to date has been controversial. Previous studies have shown that obtaining a sufficient number and appropriate extent of nodal dissection can precisely forecast and enhance patient outcomes (10,11). Nevertheless, the N stage is affected by the count of examined lymph nodes (ELNs) during surgery, a factor that may contribute to stage migration (12). For example, in advanced gastric cancer, at least 16 lymph nodes are required for accurate staging of patients with N3a (7–15 metastatic lymph nodes) and N3b (≥16 metastatic lymph nodes) following radical surgery. If fewer than 16 ELNs are examined intraoperatively, N3b patients may not be detected (13). Such patients may be unable to receive a more personalized postoperative adjuvant treatment plan, thereby affecting their prognosis. Therefore, establishing a precise predictive model that incorporates various clinicopathological variables is essential for identifying high risk populations and implementing individualized treatment and follow up strategies.

The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, initiated by the National Cancer Institute in the United States, covers a maximum geographic area that represents roughly 36.7% of the American population, providing valuable resources for cancer epidemiological research (14). This study aims to retrospectively analyze the clinicopathological data of patients with non-metastatic gastric cancer who underwent radical surgical resection, using data from the SEER database. Independent risk factors affecting postoperative overall survival (OS) are identified through univariate Cox regression, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Based on these factors, a prognostic nomogram model with high accuracy and clinical utility is constructed and validated. The development of this model is anticipated to offer clinicians a more accurate tool for personalized prognostic evaluation and treatment planning within this patient group. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1165/rc).

Methods

Data sources and patient selection

Using SEER*Stat version 9.0.42.0, data of patients without distant metastasis who underwent radical gastrectomy for gastric cancer were obtained from the SEER database. The selected database for this study was “SEER Research Data, 17 Registries, Nov 2024 Sub (2000–2022)”. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Patients meeting the following criteria were included: (I) diagnosed with gastric cancer between 2010 and 2019 [primary site = C16.0–C16.9; International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3), Hist/behav = 8140/3, 8141/3, 8142/3, 8143/3, 8144/3, 8145/3, 8211/3, 8255/3, 8262/3, 8323/3, 8480/3, 8490/3]; (II) underwent primary tumor resection (RX summ-Surg Prim Site = 30–80); (III) aged ≥18 years; (IV) had complete records of survival time and survival status. Additionally, patients meeting the following exclusion criteria were excluded: (I) unknown survival time or survival status, or survival duration below one month; (II) missing other clinical information (such as age, sex, T stage, N stage, surgical status, etc.). Ultimately, a total of 9,098 patients were included in this study.

Using the caret package in R, the 9,098 patients were divided by stratified sampling based on survival status into a training cohort and an internal validation cohort at a ratio of 7:3. To ensure the reproducibility of the results, a random seed (set.seed[123]) was set during the allocation to allow for experimental reproducibility. Ultimately, three datasets were generated in this study: a training cohort consisting of 6,369 patients for variable screening and model construction, a validation cohort of 2,729 patients, and the overall cohort of 9,098 patients for rigorous internal validation of the constructed model (Figure 1).

Study variables

In this study, we collected data on demographic characteristics, such as age, sex, race, and marital status; cancer features, including tumor site, tumor size, histological type, T stage, N stage, grade, stage, ELN, log odds of positive lymph nodes (LODDS), radiation, and chemotherapy; treatment information, such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy; and survival information, including survival time and survival status. LODDS was derived as log{[positive lymph node (PLN) + 0.5]/(ELN − PLN + 0.5)}. For the four continuous variables, age, size, ELN, and LODDS, cutoff values were determined using X-tile software, and each was divided into three groups: age (≤65, 66–77, ≥78 years), size (≤25, 26–50, ≥51), ELN (≤10, 11–26, ≥27), and LODDS (≤−1.01, −1.00 to −0.12, ≥−0.11). X-tile can identify optimal prognostic cut-off values based on survival outcomes and convert continuous variables into categorical variables. In the construction of prognostic models, this approach facilitates the development of clinically interpretable nomograms, enabling clinicians to rapidly identify patients with different levels of risk. Race classifications were categorized as White, Black, and other. Marital status groupings were classified as single, married, and other. Tumor site was defined as cardia/fundus, gastric body, antrum/pylorus, lesser curvature/greater curvature, and overlapping/not otherwise specified. Histological type categories were defined as adenocarcinoma, signet ring cell carcinoma, and other. T stage categories were defined as T1, T2, T3, and T4. N stage categories were defined as N0, N1, N2, and N3. Grade categories were defined as I, II, III, and IV. Stage categories were defined as I, II, and III. Radical surgical treatment was classified as yes or no. Chemotherapy was classified as yes or no. Radiotherapy was classified as yes or no. OS time, measured in months, was defined as the duration from the diagnosis of gastric cancer to death from any cause. Survival status was classified as deceased or alive.

Constructing risk and prognosis related models and validation

The training cohort served to build the model, and variable selection was performed through three steps. First, univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to preliminarily identify risk factors associated with gastric cancer. Variables that were statistically significant (P<0.05) in this step were subsequently included in further analyses. Second, LASSO Cox regression analysis was performed using the glmnet package in R. By applying an L1 penalty, this method forces the regression coefficients of non-contributory variables to become zero, thereby simplifying the model and minimizing multicollinearity among variables. The optimal penalty parameter lambda (λ) was determined using ten-fold cross validation. Ultimately, the “lambda.1se” criterion was used to select λ, and nine variables, Age, Race, Size, Stage, T, N, ELN, LODDS, and Chemotherapy, were identified. These variables were then entered into a multivariate Cox regression model. Variables that retained statistical significance (P<0.05) at this stage were finally identified as independent prognostic predictors, including Age, Race, Size, T, N, ELN, LODDS, and Chemotherapy. These were incorporated into a prognostic nomogram to predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probabilities of patients without distant metastasis after radical gastrectomy.

To evaluate the performance of our model, we used the concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) to assess its discriminative ability. Calibration accuracy was verified by plotting predicted survival probabilities against observed outcomes based on 500 bootstrap resamples. Clinical net benefit was quantified through decision curve analysis (DCA) across a range of threshold probabilities. Finally, Akaike information criterion (AIC) was used to compare the model performance among different combinations of lymph node indicators, and the net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were calculated to quantify the incremental predictive value of our proposed model over the N stage model. Based on the above metrics, the superiority of this model over the AJCC staging system in predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS accuracy was validated. Finally, to convert the continuous risk scores obtained from the Cox proportional hazards model into clinically relevant risk categories, the risk score for each patient was calculated based on the nomogram in the training cohort. X-tile was used to classify patients in the training cohort into low-, middle-, and high-risk groups. The grouping rules established in the training cohort were then utilized in the validation cohort and the overall cohort, classifying patients in these cohorts into low-, middle-, and high-risk groups as well. Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test were used to assess survival differences among the risk groups. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3). The chi-square test was used to compare the distribution of clinical variables between the training cohort and the validation cohort. LASSO regression reduced collinearity among variables. Before running multivariate Cox regression analysis, it was verified that all variables met the conventional collinearity thresholds [tolerance >0.1 and variance inflation factor (VIF) <10], indicating that the dataset was free from serious multicollinearity.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between 2010 and 2019, a total of 9,098 patients without distant metastasis who underwent primary tumor resection in the SEER database satisfied the enrollment criteria for this analysis. These patients were randomly assigned to a training cohort (n=6,369) and a validation cohort (n=2,729) (Table 1). In the overall population, patients aged 65 years or younger accounted for the majority (46.5%). Males predominated (63.6%). The predominant race was White (64.0%). Married individuals accounted for 64.3% of the overall population. Regarding tumor site, cardia/fundus and antrum/pylorus were the most frequent, each representing roughly 30% of the overall cohort. Tumor size was predominantly 26–50 cm (38.7%). The main histological type was adenocarcinoma (73.7%). Stage I (39.0%) and grade III (62.7%) were the most common. T3 (40.8%) and N0 (43.8%) were the most frequent. In the overall population, 54.6% of patients had 11–26 ELNs. The majority of patients (54.0%) had LODDS values in the range of ≤−1.01. Regarding treatment, 34.7% of patients received radiotherapy, and 58.3% received chemotherapy. In the comparison of baseline characteristics between the training cohort and the validation cohort, all variables showed no differences except for LODDS (P>0.05). For LODDS, the P value was <0.05, possibly due to the influence of sample size. However, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was 0.067 (<0.1), indicating that the two cohorts were very balanced on this variable with only a minor difference. Moreover, the SMD for all other variables was <0.1. In summary, the training cohort and validation cohort in this study demonstrated favorable consistency and representativeness.

Analysis of prognostic risk factors for gastric cancer patients

To develop a prognostic prediction model for patients without distant metastasis who underwent primary tumor resection for gastric cancer, we first conducted univariate Cox regression analysis across all variables incorporated in this study. The preliminary analysis identified all variables as risk factors (P<0.05), demonstrating that the variables included in this study are closely related to clinical significance. Variable selection was performed using regression coefficient path curves (Figure 2A). The optimal penalty parameter lambda (λ) was determined to be 0.04500106 through ten-fold cross-validation, yielding a relatively concise and well-performing model (Figure 2B). The selected variables included Age, Race, Size, Stage, T, N, ELN, LODDS, and Chemotherapy. We evaluated potential collinearity between variables by applying the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF). The results showed that, except for N (GVIF =4.807584) and LODDS (GVIF =3.892809), the GVIF values for all other variables were below 2. The correlation between N and LODDS with lymph node status is reasonably clinically expected. Furthermore, the adjusted GVIF [GVIF1/(2*Df)] for all variables was less than 2, indicating no serious multicollinearity issues. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that age, race, size, T, N, ELN, LODDS, and Chemotherapy were independent risk factors in this study (P<0.05) (Table 2).

Development and validation of a prognostic nomogram for gastric cancer patients

According to univariate and LASSO Cox regression analyses, this work constructed a new nomogram for predicting survival outcomes without distant metastasis who underwent primary tumor resection for gastric cancer (Figure 2C) (https://1nomogram.shinyapps.io/DynNomapp/). The nomogram incorporated three lymph node indicators. Although these indicators are mathematically interrelated, they capture different prognostic dimensions. N stage provides the absolute number of metastatic lymph nodes in a clinically familiar form, ELN reflects the quality of surgery and the adequacy of pathological examination, and LODDS provides an estimate of lymph node involvement that is relatively resistant to stage migration. Moreover, the model including all three lymph node variables (N stage, ELN, and LODDS) had the lowest AIC value (57,386.93) (Table 3), indicating that the addition of ELN and LODDS significantly improved the predictive performance of the model and that this improvement was not simply attributable to overfitting caused by an increased number of parameters. The nomogram also showed favorable discriminative ability, with C-index values of 0.698 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.689–0.707], 0.704 (95% CI: 0.691–0.718), and 0.700 (95% CI: 0.692–0.707) in the training, validation, and overall cohorts, respectively, outperforming the AJCC staging system. In addition, after ELN and LODDS were added to the model containing only N stage, both NRI and IDI showed significant positive improvements for the prediction of 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS in gastric cancer patients across the training, validation, and overall cohorts, further confirming a significant improvement in risk reclassification (Table 4). In the training cohort, the AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were 0.766, 0.773, and 0.767, respectively, all higher than those of the AJCC staging system (0.655, 0.698, and 0.699) (Figure 3A-3C). In the validation cohort, the AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were 0.771, 0.775, and 0.775, respectively, all superior to those of the AJCC staging system (0.661, 0.699, and 0.714) (Figure 3D-3F). In the overall cohort, the AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were 0.768, 0.773, and 0.769, respectively, all outperforming the AJCC staging system (0.657, 0.698, and 0.703) (Figure 3G-3I). These results suggest that the proposed nomogram exhibits strong prognostic capability for OS in individuals without distant metastasis following primary tumor resection for gastric cancer. Calibration curves of the nomogram showed strong agreement between predicted and observed OS in the training cohort (Figure 4A-4C), validation cohort (Figure 4D-4F), and overall cohort (Figure 4G-4I). DCA further confirmed that the nomogram demonstrated superior clinical utility to the traditional AJCC staging system for predicting OS in the training cohort (Figure 5A-5C), validation cohort (Figure 5D-5F), and overall cohort (Figure 5G-5I).

Discussion

In recent years, nomograms have become important predictive tools in personalized cancer care, transforming complex prognostic models into individualized risk assessments for clinical decision making (15). Although previous studies have integrated prognostic models for patients after radical gastrectomy (16,17), few have developed a more comprehensive prognostic model that combines lymph node involvement with other clinicopathological features. Utilizing SEER database, we developed a clinical prediction model incorporating eight variables: age, race, size, T, N, ELN, LODDS, and chemotherapy. Notably, in this model, lymph node involvement is predicted by three variables: N, ELN, and LODDS. This is because lymph node metastasis is the primary route of gastric cancer spread; therefore, lymph node related indicators serve a vital function in accurately assessing the stage and outcomes of gastric cancer patients (18). Consequently, identifying the most accurate lymph node staging system and combining it with other clinicopathological features to predict postoperative prognosis in gastric cancer patients remains a research hotspot.

The N stage of the traditional AJCC system is widely used in clinical practice due to its ease of acquisition, but its limitations are evident. The N stage strongly correlates with the count of PLNs detected during surgery; if too few PLNs are identified, it may lead to stage migration and consequently underestimate the severity of the disease (19). To accurately assess N stage, avoid bias caused by stage migration, and ensure reliable prognostic judgment, a minimum of 16 lymph nodes is required by the AJCC TNM staging system for examination (20). However, during surgery, the number of lymph nodes obtained is influenced by many variables, including the extent of lymph node dissection, tumor size and location, the surgeon’s experience and skill, the condition of the specimen itself, and individual patient differences (21). These factors greatly diminish the prognostic efficacy of using only the AJCC N stage to evaluate the effect of lymph nodes status on postoperative prognosis of individuals with gastric cancer. Furthermore, in many cancers, ELN is also closely associated with patient prognosis. Unlike the AJCC N stage, ELN includes both PLNs and negative lymph nodes (NLNs); a higher number of ELNs generally indicates a better prognosis. Zhang et al. (22) found that an insufficient number of ELN (≤21) is associated with worse survival outcomes in gastric cancer patients and can lead to stage migration in patients with N3 disease. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) gastric cancer guidelines also suggest that resecting more than 30 lymph nodes provides more accurate prognostic assessment. However, some studies have shown that gastric cancer patients with more than 30 ELNs do not have a better prognosis than those with 25–29 ELNs (23). Additionally, Wong et al. (24) indicated that ELN is not significantly correlated with survival in gastric cancer patients. Recent studies have also shown that the LODDS, derived from PLN and ELN, exhibits excellent predictive performance in cancer. LODDS, which comprehensively considers the full spectrum of positive as well as NLNs via a logarithmic transformation, reduces bias arising from variations in surgery or pathological examination through a more consistent and precise estimation of nodal status, demonstrating superior prognostic value in multiple cancer types, including gastric cancer (25-27). This model appropriately and reasonably incorporates the AJCC N stage, as well as the recently studied lymph node indicators such as ELN and LODDS, and is expected to achieve superior predictive performance for lymph node involvement in gastric cancer patients.

Furthermore, in this study, age was divided into three groups: ≤65, 66–77, and ≥78 years. This is because gastric cancer is more common in elderly patients, although its incidence has been increasing in younger populations in recent years (28). This is consistent with our data, as patients aged ≥66 years accounted for 53.5% of the cases. With increasing age, elderly gastric cancer patients tend to have a poorer prognosis even after undergoing radical surgery. Notably, some studies have shown comparable prognoses between young and elderly patients, or even worse outcomes in young gastric cancer patients (29,30). This could be explained by young gastric cancer patients often presenting with different clinicopathological characteristics than elderly patients, such as being diagnosed at an advanced stage or having a more aggressive histological type (31).

For patients undergoing primary tumor resection, the T stage, representing the extent of local tumor progression, is a critical determinant of postoperative recurrence and survival. As a core component of the TNM staging system, T stage has long been recognized as a strong prognostic indicator (32). Tumor size also mirrors the proliferative status of the malignancy and is strongly associated with patient outcomes (33). Our findings showed that increased tumor size and more advanced T stage were associated with worse patient prognosis, which is consistent with previous studies. Histological type has often been considered a key prognostic factor (34). Notably, however, the multivariate analysis in this study revealed that histological type did not demonstrate independent predictive value after incorporating the aforementioned variables. This finding aligns with clinical reality: after surgical resection, patient prognosis is primarily determined by whether a curative (R0) resection has been achieved and the severity of lymph node metastasis, rather than by the histological subtype alone. Adjuvant chemotherapy is the cornerstone of systemic therapy for gastric cancer, and our study also confirmed that patients receiving postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy experienced markedly improved outcomes versus non-recipients. Notably, chemotherapy was associated with poorer survival in the univariate analysis but emerged as a protective factor in the multivariate analysis. This phenomenon may be attributable to confounding by indication, as patients receiving chemotherapy often present with more advanced disease or higher-risk clinicopathological characteristics and therefore have a poorer baseline prognosis. Consequently, a higher mortality risk is more likely to be observed in unadjusted analyses. After adjusting for potential confounding factors, including age, tumor stage, and lymph node-related variables, the potential survival benefit of chemotherapy became evident. Furthermore, our nomogram indicated that White patients had a higher risk than Black patients and patients of other races, which is consistent with recent studies (17,35). We speculate that factors such as socioeconomic status, tumor biological behavior, and treatment aggressiveness may influence the prognosis of Black patients; however, interpretation of this finding requires further extensive research in the future.

Although this study constructed and validated a prognostic nomogram model with excellent predictive performance based on the SEER database, the following limitations should be acknowledged. First, the data in this study were derived from the SEER database. Although this database has the advantages of a large sample size and broad geographic coverage, it is inherently a retrospective data collection, and selection bias and information bias cannot be avoided. In addition, this study used OS as the primary endpoint and did not further evaluate cancer-specific survival (CSS). Although OS is the most widely used endpoint in clinical research and provides a comprehensive assessment of patients’ overall prognosis, non-cancer-related deaths may have a considerable impact on survival outcomes, particularly among elderly patients. Future studies should incorporate CSS and competing-risk models to provide a more precise evaluation of gastric cancer-specific mortality. Second, the SEER database lacks certain key clinicopathological information, which may affect the accuracy of our model to varying degrees. For example, this study focused on patients who underwent radical gastrectomy for gastric cancer, but the SEER database does not provide pathological information on surgical margin status (R0/R1/R2). Surgical margin status is one of the key predictors of local recurrence and long-term survival after gastric cancer surgery, and its absence may lead to overestimation of survival predictions in patients with positive margins (36). The SEER database only records binary chemotherapy variables (yes/no) and lacks specific regimen details, whereas the efficacy of different chemotherapy regimens varies significantly. Furthermore, neoadjuvant chemotherapy can lead to discrepancies between the pathological stage (ypTNM) and the initial clinical stage (37). Moreover, the SEER database lacks molecular biomarkers closely associated with the prognosis of gastric cancer, such as human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), microsatellite instability (MSI) status, and tumor mutational burden (TMB). With the rapid development of precision medicine in gastric cancer, these molecular characteristics have become important bases for guiding targeted therapy and immunotherapy. The absence of this information limits the clinical applicability of our model in the current context of precision medicine (38). Third, this study innovatively incorporated three indicators reflecting lymph node involvement, N stage, ELN, and LODDS, but could not obtain information on the specific locations of PLNs. Previous studies have shown that lymph node metastases at specific sites have differential impacts on prognosis. Furthermore, the lack of information on the extent of lymph node dissection (D1/D2) may affect the accuracy of staging. D2 dissection can detect more lymph nodes than D1 dissection, potentially leading to a higher N stage (39,40). However, our model already includes ELN, which to some extent partially corrects for the differences caused by the extent of dissection. Finally, from a clinical perspective, the web-based dynamic nomogram developed in this study provides a convenient tool for individualized postoperative survival prediction. Compared with the conventional AJCC TNM staging system, the proposed model incorporates lymph node-related indicators, including ELN and LODDS, enabling more accurate identification of high-risk patients with poor prognoses and providing additional guidance for postoperative surveillance and individualized management. It should be emphasized that this model is intended to complement rather than replace the current AJCC staging system and guideline-recommended treatment strategies. At present, this model was only internally validated using the SEER database, without independent external validation. Therefore, its applicability across different geographic regions, medical centers, and racial populations remains to be further validated. In the future, we will strive to collect data from our own center and from multiple centers within China to form an independent external validation cohort, or explore other public databases for cross-database validation, thereby further advancing the development of personalized prognostic assessment tools for gastric cancer.

Conclusions

The current work constructed and verified a new nomogram incorporating eight variables (age, race, tumor size, T stage, N stage, ELN, LODDS, and chemotherapy) to predict OS in non-metastatic gastric cancer patients after radical gastrectomy using the SEER database. The model demonstrated robust discrimination (AUCs >0.76) and excellent calibration across training and validation cohorts, with superior clinical net benefit compared to the traditional AJCC TNM system. Further evaluation using the C-index, AIC, NRI, and IDI consistently demonstrated the superior prognostic discriminative ability of the proposed model. Moreover, the risk stratification system based on the total nomogram score effectively distinguished patients with different prognostic risks, while the web-based dynamic nomogram further enhanced the model’s clinical accessibility and practical applicability. Overall, this model may serve as a valuable complement to the AJCC TNM staging system and provide useful guidance for individualized prognostic assessment, risk stratification, and postoperative follow-up management in patients with non-metastatic gastric cancer.
