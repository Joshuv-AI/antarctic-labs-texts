The Cyber Paranoia and Fear Scale-Updated (CPFS-U): development and implications for digital health engagement
Authors: Frances T Greenway, Mark Weal, Emma Palmer-Cooper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo update and revalidate the Cyber Paranoia and Fear Scale to reflect current technological contexts and examine its relevance to digital health readiness and engagement.MethodsUsing an online community sample (n=433), exploratory factor analysis was conducted to examine the factor structure of the revised item pool. Items were refined through consultation with Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement groups to ensure contemporary relevance and clarity.ResultsAnalysis supported a four-factor structure representing artificial intelligence (AI) and digital dependence, technological risk awareness, perceived data vulnerability and surveillance-related mistrust. The updated Cyber Paranoia and Fear Scale-Updated (CPFS-U) demonstrated good internal consistency and supported construct validity. Cyber-paranoia and fear were conceptually and empirically distinct from general paranoia and anxiety, highlighting the specific cognitive and emotional responses elicited by digital technologies.ConclusionsThe CPFS-U offers a psychometrically robust, modernised measure for understanding individuals’ responses to digital and AI-based technologies. Its application in digital health research and practice can inform risk communication, user engagement strategies and the design of trustworthy digital interventions. By identifying individuals who may disengage due to online mistrust, the CPFS-U has the potential to inform more inclusive and psychologically informed digital health systems.Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, eHealth, Digital Technology

Introduction

The Cyber Paranoia and Fear Scale (CPFS), developed by Mason et al,1 was a pioneering measure of digital-technology mistrust. Mason et al1 aimed to assess individuals’ attitudes and distrust related to online activities and cyber threats, terming the extremes of these attitudes as ‘cyber paranoia’. Cyber paranoia was conceptualised as being related to, but distinct from, general trait paranoia.1

Paranoia describes the belief that one is being deliberately targeted or threatened by others, even when there is little or no evidence to support this.2 3 Paranoid thinking is not limited to clinical populations; it is also present on a continuum within the general population.4 Cognitive models emphasise how interpretations of ambiguous or anomalous experiences, shaped by prior life events, attentional and attributional biases and emotional states contribute to feelings of paranoia5

In the context of cyber paranoia, a lack of familiarity and knowledge about technology has previously been associated with delusional ideation or paranoid thoughts about digital environments. This suggests that digital unfamiliarity may cause mistrust of digital systems.1 Mason et al1 argued that other predictors of paranoia may not apply in the same way to cyber paranoia, highlighting that cyber paranoia and fear appear to be distinct from general paranoia. These findings are consistent with the broader understanding that psychotic symptoms, including paranoid thoughts, exist on a continuum, with milder psychotic-like experiences occurring in the general public.6 7

Distrust is a core feature of paranoia, conceptualised as arising from negative expectations and beliefs about the lack of trustworthiness regarded for other people.8 Within digital contexts, distrust manifests as scepticism toward privacy, data security and algorithmic fairness.8 9 As digital technology has advanced the boundaries between rational and irrational fears have become increasingly blurred, particularly with developments in artificial intelligence (AI), algorithmic surveillance and deepfake generation. Contemporary digital fears may stem less from a lack of technological familiarity, as previously reported by Mason et al,1 and more from heightened awareness of realistic threats enabled by sophisticated technologies. Since 2014, technological progress has transformed the digital landscape, offering unprecedented opportunities, alongside new and significant risks.10

The proliferation of autonomous AI systems, algorithmic decision-making and personalised digital environments has introduced new dimensions of uncertainty and fear.11 Concerns have evolved towards the manipulation of identity through the expansion of social media, targeted misinformation through algorithms and ads and the erosion of trust in digital content, which has been exemplified by the rise of deepfakes and AI-generated media. Everyday technologies have also become more embedded and ambient, including wearable devices, voice assistants (eg, Alexa, Google Home) and location-tracking applications, resulting in increasingly intimate data collection and sharing. With technologies such as AI increasingly being applied to tasks traditionally performed by humans, and with their growing integration into daily life, the field of cyberpsychology has emerged as a unique discipline, defined as the study of psychological processes related to and underlying technologically interconnected human behaviour.12 Therefore, understanding how people perceive AI and other emerging technologies is crucial for evaluating reactions to modern digital environments.

While AI is being incorporated into most areas of life, one area which has gained significant development is healthcare. AI has currently been utilised in diagnosing diseases in patients,13 helping surgeons perform surgery14 15 and as a treatment tool in mental health services.16,18 From AI-enabled chatbots offering therapeutic support to predictive models used in triage and risk assessment, AI is transforming clinical care. However, their adoption also raises concerns around transparency, autonomy and algorithmic bias particularly among individuals who already experience heightened levels of digital mistrust.19 20 As AI becomes more embedded in healthcare, understanding public perceptions of these technologies is crucial to ensuring ethical implementation.

One aspect of trust in digital systems involves users feeling that they understand them. This understanding can occur at two levels: a functional understanding of the system’s purpose (eg, delivering therapeutic content in healthcare) and a mechanistic understanding of how it operates (eg, underlying algorithms or data use). When explanations are limited or overly technical, individuals may interpret the system as intrusive, especially if they already hold concerns about surveillance or digital manipulation.20 Perceptions of digital threats are rarely formed in a vacuum; rather, individuals rely on their existing beliefs, experiences and levels of digital literacy (the ability to confidently and effectively use digital technologies) to interpret system behaviour.21

Despite growing interest in digital mistrust, relatively few validated measures capture these concerns in a comprehensive and contemporary way. Existing tools tend to assess narrower or earlier conceptualisations of technology-related anxiety. For example, the Computer Anxiety Rating Scale22 assesses anxiety associated with computer use and technological competence, while more recently, the Cybersecurity Anxiety Scale23 has been developed to capture concerns around cybersecurity anxiety while navigating digital threats. While both valuable, these measures do not account for broader psychological constructs of mistrust, paranoia and perceived surveillance associated with emerging technologies.

The original Cyber Paranoia and Fear Scale1 addressed this gap by conceptualising mistrust as a distinct psychological response to digital environments. The original scale has been referenced in studies exploring online behaviour, digital trust and emerging concerns around technological risk, highlighting its conceptual relevance within cyberpsychology. However, its use in applied or clinical contexts remains limited, and relatively few studies have extended or adapted the measure in line with rapid technological developments.

In response to these limitations, there is a need for an updated, psychometrically robust measure to reflect the evolving dynamics and complexities of technology. This study aims to create an updated version of the Cyber Paranoia and Fear Scale originally developed by Mason et al1 refining the measure to better capture contemporary digital concerns while enhancing its transparency, interpretability and user-centred design with the use of public and patient involvement and engagement (PPIE). By retaining the core elements of the original scale, this study aims to enhance the relevance and applicability in capturing emerging concerns related to contemporary technologies, resulting in the updated measure: Cyber Paranoia and Fear Scale-Updated (CPFS-U). Higher scores on the CPFS-U indicate greater levels of cyber paranoia, suggesting that individuals with elevated scores are more likely to experience mistrust and anxiety related to digital interactions.

Methods

This study evaluated the factor structure and validity of the CPFS-U. PPIE was used to inform the redevelopment of the scale. This process led to the addition of eight new items and the rewording of several existing items to improve clarity and ensure relevance to contemporary digital technologies.

Patient and Public Involvement

An iterative process of refining the CPFS was used to ensure the acceptability, relevance and clarity of the CPFS in a rapidly changing digital context. PPIE played a central role in this redevelopment, as digital paranoia and fear is a lived and evolving experience, influenced by the technologies people use each day. The revised scale focuses on broad technological categories (eg, AI, biometric systems, digital surveillance) to maintain relevance as technologies advance. Two structured discussions (n=5 undergraduate contributors, 60 min each) were held with students familiar with contemporary digital environments. Participants received the original CPFS beforehand and discussed item clarity, relevance and emerging concerns. To support comprehension of technological concepts (eg, AI, algorithmic monitoring and digital data use), item wording was refined through PPIE activities. These discussions ensured that terminology reflected language commonly understood by lay users rather than technical definitions. Items were therefore phrased in accessible, everyday language (eg, referring to ‘voice assistants’ or ‘apps collecting data’) rather than relying on technical terminology. Two researchers facilitated sessions, synthesised feedback and proposed revisions, which were independently reviewed and finalised following participant validation. PPIE feedback highlighted several evolving concerns related to digital technology and privacy, which informed the development of the CPFS-U. Key themes identified can be found in online supplemental table 1.

Study sample

A total of 433 UK adults (207 via Prolific; 226 students) participated. Eligibility required age 18+and UK residency. Participants received either £6 /hour or six course credits for participation.

Procedure

Participants were recruited via Prolific (Prolific, n.d.), an anonymous online platform that enables individuals to take part in research studies in exchange for monetary compensation. Interested participants accessed the study hosted on Qualtrics (2005) and were shown the information sheet with an option to provide informed consent via a tick box. Participants were then asked to provide basic demographic information, information about use of technology and digital systems, followed by three validated questionnaires (The Paranoia Scale24; Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD) 725; Online Trust Measure26) and the CPFS-U. The procedure took on average 15 min to complete.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected using Qualtrics (https://www.qualtrics.com) and analysed using IBM SPSS 29 Mac (V.29.0.1.0; IBM, 2020). Following data cleaning, descriptive statistics were calculated for all key variables. An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using principal axis factoring with varimax rotation was conducted on the full sample to examine the underlying structure of the CPFS-U. Varimax (orthogonal) rotation was applied to aid interpretability by producing a simpler factor structure with minimal cross-loadings. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin value of 0.79 indicated adequate sampling, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ²(171) = 866.17, p<0.001), confirming suitability for factor analysis. Initial eigenvalues suggested a four-factor or five-factor solution; comparison showed that the four-factor model provided the clearest and most interpretable structure. Items loading ≥0.41 on a single factor and <0.30 on others were retained (see pattern matrix in online supplemental table 2).

To examine the relationships between CPFS-U scores and psychological and digital variables, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated across the combined sample.

Exploratory Factor Analysis

The final 19-item scale comprised four factors: AI and Digital Dependence Concerns (six items, 19.15% variance), Technological Risk Awareness and Distrust (five items, 12.94%), Perceived Data Vulnerability (four items, 8.87%), and Surveillance and Monitoring Paranoia (three items, 5.97%). One behavioural item (‘I avoid sharing my personal data on the internet’) was removed due to poor loading. Factor loadings ranged from 0.42 to 0.65 for Factor 1, 0.39 to 0.73 for Factor 2, 0.34 to 0.49 for Factor 3 and 0.33 to 0.67 for Factor 4, indicating acceptable to strong item-factor relationships. The full pattern matrix is presented in online supplemental table 2. Concurrent validity analyses showed that CPFS-U scores were positively associated with trait paranoia, general anxiety and higher importance placed on online trust, while trusting beliefs were negatively associated. Digital confidence and activity were largely unrelated to overall scores. (See online supplemental table 3 for full Correlation Matrix of CPFS-U, Paranoia, Anxiety and Digital Trust Variables.)

Internal consistency of the CPFS-U and its subscales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The overall scale demonstrated good reliability (α=0.74). Subscale reliability was good for AI and Digital Dependence Concerns (α=0.72) and Technological Risk Awareness and Distrust (α=0.73). Reliability for Perceived Data Vulnerability (α=0.55) and Surveillance and Monitoring Paranoia (α=0.59) was lower but within an acceptable range for shorter subscales.27 These findings suggest that, overall, the CPFS-U demonstrates satisfactory internal consistency across its dimensions.

Discussion

This study redeveloped and investigated the underlying factor structure of the CPFS and explored its relationship with general trait paranoia, anxiety and digital literacy. The updated 19-item CPFS-U demonstrated a clear four-factor structure, reflecting modern digital concerns shaped by technological change and grounded in PPIE. See online supplemental table 4 for the CPFS-U scale. While the original CPFS has primarily been used in research settings to assess levels of digital mistrust and to examine how these concerns relate to psychological variables and patterns of technology use,1 the CPFS-U extends the scope of measurement to include contemporary concerns such as AI, algorithmic monitoring and passive data collection. In practice, the CPFS-U may be used to identify individuals who are hesitant to engage with digital health interventions, inform the design of more transparent and trustworthy systems and guide communication strategies that address specific fears. For instance, individuals with elevated surveillance-related concerns may benefit from clearer explanations of data use and privacy protections, while those with higher AI-related concerns may require greater transparency around how automated decisions are made.

Descriptive statistics revealed the sample scored highly on the CPFS-U, particularly on the factor AI and Digital Dependence Concerns, suggesting that apprehension about the growing influence of AI and digital integration is widespread. The four extracted factors illustrate distinct facets of cyber paranoia and fear. ‘AI and Digital Dependence Concerns’ reflects apprehension about AI expansion, automation and potential loss of human control. ‘Technological Risk Awareness and Distrust’ captures beliefs that the public lacks knowledge about AI and data security and broader scepticism toward institutional transparency.28 Both reflect the growing public and academic concern surrounding AI ethics, which now accounts for over 40% of AI-related publications.29 ‘Perceived Data Vulnerability’ encompasses fears about social media privacy, data misuse and identity distortion. ‘Surveillance and Monitoring Paranoia’ captured concerns related to smartphone tracking, CCTV surveillance and perceived health risks from electromagnetic waves. The emergence of this factor highlights the psychological salience of being observed in increasingly ambient and passive ways, consistent with recent shifts in how surveillance technologies are embedded in everyday life.

One behavioural item, ‘I avoid sharing my personal data on the internet’, did not load onto any of the four extracted factors and was excluded from the final questionnaire. While appearing conceptually related to cyber paranoia and digital fear, its non-loading may reflect a behavioural distinction rather than a cognitive or affective fear response. Avoiding data sharing may be interpreted by some participants as normative or security-conscious rather than fear-driven. This may also suggest that digital caution and cyber paranoia are not always aligned, and that some individuals may engage in cautious behaviours without experiencing elevated levels of mistrust or fear.

Overall CPFS-U scores were found to decrease with age; largely reflecting lower scores on Perceived Data Vulnerability among older adults. Older adults reported fewer concerns regarding identity misrepresentation or social media breaches, which may reflect lower engagement with these platforms, while younger adults’ heavier reliance on digital platforms for occupational, social and personal tasks may increase their exposure to privacy threats and technological uncertainties. However, Surveillance and Monitoring Paranoia, which encompasses concerns about smartphone tracking, CCTV surveillance and potential health effects from electromagnetic waves, increased with age, suggesting greater sensitivity to these more tangible, real-world forms of monitoring in older adults. Digital Dependence Concerns and Technological Risk Awareness and Distrust showed minimal negative correlations with age. This divergence suggests that age-related differences in cyber paranoia may be domain-specific, with younger adults more concerned about digitally mediated identity risks and older adults more attuned to traditional surveillance threats.

Associations with psychological variables support construct validity. Cyber Paranoia showed small (r=0.23) but significant relationships with trait paranoia, suggesting conceptual relatedness while indicating that digital fears extend beyond general mistrust (Mason et al1). The moderate association with general anxiety (r=0.37), particularly for Perceived Data Vulnerability (r=0.41), highlights the role of emotional processes in sharing perceptions of digital threats. The CPFS-U’s multidimensional structure reinforces this distinction by capturing digital-specific fears not explained by general paranoia. However, Surveillance and Monitoring Paranoia correlated negatively with anxiety (r=–0.28), suggesting that surveillance concerns may reflect differences in how predictable threats are interpreted, with some forms of monitoring perceived as routine rather than ambiguous or threatening.

CPFS-U scores were not significantly correlated with digital activity or digital confidence, although lower digital confidence was modestly linked to higher Perceived Data Vulnerability (r=–0.15), suggesting that specific skills may influence feelings of exposure, but that digital literacy alone does not protect against cyber-related fears. The Importance of Online Trust did not show a significant relationship with the CPFS-U or individual factors. However, Trusting Beliefs were negatively correlated with overall CPFS-U scores (r=–0.12), indicating that individuals who tend to view online systems as trustworthy and acting in users’ best interests are less likely to report cyber-paranoia and fear. Conversely, the Importance of Online Trust was positively correlated with CPFS-U scores (r=0.303), suggesting that individuals who place a high value on trust in online interactions may be more acutely aware of perceived breaches or absences of trust, leading to heightened sensitivity and concern about digital risks.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study was the integration of public involvement, particularly the contribution of student PPIE contributors during item refinement. As a highly digitally engaged group, their insights ensured that the updated scale was grounded in the lived experiences and perceptions of those navigating a digital engagement more frequently. Feedback from these contributors informed not only the language used in the updated items but the inclusion of themes such as algorithmic surveillance, data misuse and AI. This collaborative approach helped enhance the face validity and relevance of the measure.

Several limitations should be noted. The use of a community-based volunteer sample introduces the potential for both self-selection and non-response bias. Individuals who are more willing or able to participate in online research. Nearly half (47.8%) of the sample were recruited via Prolific, which may overrepresent digitally confident individuals and limit generalisability to populations with lower digital access or literacy. Conversely, individuals with higher levels of paranoia or digital mistrust may be less likely to participate due to concerns about how their data may be used. As a result, the sample may underrepresent those with more severe or clinically significant forms of cyber paranoia, potentially limiting generalisability. This is particularly relevant given that mistrust itself may act as a barrier to both research participation and digital engagement. Future research should aim to recruit more diverse samples, including individuals with lower digital access or higher mistrust, and consider alternative methodologies to better capture perspectives that may be systematically excluded from online research.

Future directions

The present study incorporates contemporary digital concerns to redevelop the pre-existing CPFS. While the observed associations with general paranoia and anxiety support concurrent validity, further consideration of discriminant validity is warranted. The modest correlations between CPFS-U scores and trait paranoia suggest that, although related, cyber paranoia represents a distinct construct rather than a direct extension of general paranoid thinking. Similarly, the association with general anxiety, particularly for perceived data vulnerability, may reflect a broader sensitivity to uncertainty and threat, rather than concerns that are specific to digital environments. However, the multidimensional structure of the CPFS-U, alongside its focus on contemporary digital contexts, suggests that it captures a form of mistrust specific to digital environments. Future research should more explicitly examine discriminant validity, for example through confirmatory factor analysis to further establish the CPFS-U as a distinct digital-specific construct, particularly in clinical populations where paranoia may present differently.

Although input PPIE was integrated into item development, these discussions were not formally analysed. An in-depth qualitative study could offer richer insights into how cyber paranoia and fears manifest across populations. Future research may consider validating the CPFS in clinical populations, such as individuals with obsessive-compulsive disorder, GAD or psychosis disorders, given that these populations often report heightened mistrust, intolerance of uncertainty and threat-focused cognition. Understanding how cyber-related paranoia and fears manifest in clinical populations could help tailor digital interventions and improve therapeutic engagement. Additionally, the CPFS-U may be a useful tool for evaluating readiness and suitability for digital intervention. Future research should examine whether CPFS scores predict engagement, dropout or satisfaction with digital interventions, providing a more nuanced framework for understanding and supporting technology use in mental healthcare. Beyond clinical and individual applications, understanding cyber paranoia also has implications for digital policy and governance. As governments and institutions increasingly deliver services through online platforms, public trust in digital systems becomes central to effective policy implementation. Recognising and addressing psychological barriers such as cyber-related mistrust can inform the development of equitable digital inclusion strategies, strengthen online safety regulation and enhance public confidence in digital government initiatives.

Conclusion

This study highlights the contemporary concerns individuals have in relation to an ever-changing digital landscape. Findings demonstrated that cyber paranoia and fear are significantly related to generalised anxiety and trait paranoia. Notably, emotional processes such as anxiety were most strongly linked to data vulnerability concerns, while broader distrust in online systems was related to lower trusting beliefs. In contrast, digital literacy indicators such as confidence and activity levels showed limited relevance, suggesting that cyber paranoia may be less about technical skill and use and more about how individuals interpret and emotionally respond to digital risk. Overall, this study presents a psychometrically grounded updated version of the Cyber Paranoia and Fear Scale, supported by EFA, and enriched through PPIE. The CPFS-U scale captures the multidimensional nature of cyber paranoia and fear in the context of technological developments, distinguishing between general paranoia and anxiety, as previously understood by Mason et al.1
