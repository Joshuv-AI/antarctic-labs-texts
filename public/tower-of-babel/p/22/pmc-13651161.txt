Decoding the shared genetic architecture of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease: a genomic structural equation modeling analysis
Authors: Danfeng Zhong, Yaping Ye, Tingting Ye, Shishi Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) represents a broad spectrum of phenotypes with shared pathology. However, the joint genetic architecture across different vascular beds remains incompletely characterized.MethodsWe applied genomic structural equation modeling (Genomic SEM) to integrate GWAS summary statistics from six ASCVD-related traits: coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, peripheral artery disease, carotid intima-media thickness, and intracranial aneurysm. Downstream analyses included multivariate GWAS, fine-mapping, TWAS with FOCUS, MAGMA, pathway enrichment, cell-type and functional annotation, gsMAP and polygenic risk score.ResultsA single latent common factor model demonstrated an excellent fit to the genetic covariance matrix (CFI = 0.9837, SRMR = 0.1448). The multivariate GWAS identified 839 genome-wide significant SNPs, delineating 85 independent lead loci, of which 67 were novel. Integration of TWAS and FOCUS prioritized 6 high-confidence putative effector genes, including CDKN2B, MIA3, and NBEAL1. Functional enrichment highlighted lipid remodeling and apolipoprotein binding as predominant pathways. Notably, heritability was significantly localized in endothelial cell lineages across multiple tissues. Spatial mapping further identified the lung and kidney as key developmental tissue contexts for ASCVD genetic risk.ConclusionOur study provides a unified genetic framework for ASCVD, demonstrating that its shared liability is anchored in lipid homeostasis and systemic endothelial dysfunction. These findings offer a foundational atlas for early genomic-based risk stratification and therapeutic targeting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06297-4.Keywords: Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, Genomic structural equation modeling, Genetic pleiotropy, TWAS, Fine-mapping

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is commonly regarded as a spectrum of clinical conditions that includes coronary artery disease (CAD), myocardial infarction (MI), ischemic stroke (IS) and other events arising from underlying atherosclerosis, and it represents the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Within this overall framework, CAD, MI, IS, peripheral artery disease (PAD) and arterial phenotypes such as increased carotid intima media thickness (cIMT) and intracranial aneurysm (IA), although arising in different vascular beds and showing distinct clinical presentations and risk profiles, broadly share a common pathological basis in which atherosclerotic plaques gradually accumulate within the walls of medium and large arteries, narrow the lumen and promote thrombotic events [2]. At the genetic level, large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of CAD and related ASCVD phenotypes have demonstrated a substantial inherited component, identifying dozens of common risk variants that reach genome-wide significance and more than one hundred independent association signals that together explain a considerable proportion of disease heritability, thereby providing a solid basis for polygenic risk scores (PRS) that improve risk prediction beyond conventional factors [3]. Nevertheless, although extensive single-trait GWAS have established a substantial inherited component for individual ASCVD phenotypes and have mapped many susceptibility loci, our understanding of how these traits share and differ in their genetic architecture is still incomplete, and the overall pattern of shared genetic etiology across cardiovascular traits remains to be clarified [4]. Existing PRS or genetic risk scores derived for single cardiovascular end points are consistently associated with the incidence of CAD and broader cardiovascular events and can provide some improvement in discrimination and reclassification beyond traditional clinical risk models, but these gains are often modest and substantial heterogeneity in how such scores are constructed limits their current clinical usefulness and makes it difficult to capture the joint genetic architecture shared across multiple key ASCVD phenotypes [5]. Against this backdrop, integrating genetic information across multiple ASCVD phenotypes, including CAD, MI, IS, PAD, cIMT and IA, and constructing a quantitative model of their joint genetic architecture may enable earlier and more refined identification of high-risk individuals, improve our understanding of functional pathways and support more targeted strategies for prevention and intervention beyond existing clinical risk scores [6].

In the current genetic research paradigm, large-scale GWAS of CAD and MI have combined data from more than one million participants and have systematically identified and characterized over 250 risk loci and hundreds of candidate genes for CAD, outlining a polygenic framework in which many common variants of small effect jointly shape susceptibility to this prototypical ASCVD phenotype [7]. Earlier generations of large-scale CAD and MI GWAS, including population-specific analyses in Japanese cohorts and subsequent trans-ancestry meta-analyses with European studies, have repeatedly shown that CAD susceptibility arises from many common variants of small effect, with successive discovery of additional risk loci and refinement of PRS, so that a polygenic architecture for CAD is now well established at the single-trait level [8]. Likewise, for cerebrovascular phenotypes, cross-ancestry GWAS of IA have included more than ten thousand cases and over three hundred thousand controls, identified more than a dozen independent susceptibility loci and demonstrated a clearly polygenic genetic architecture that explains over half of the disease heritability, with substantial genetic correlations between IA and other cerebrovascular traits, indicating that these brain vascular phenotypes are also driven by many common variants of modest effect [9]. For peripheral and subclinical measures of atherosclerosis, large-scale GWAS and meta-analyses of cIMT and carotid plaque in tens of thousands of individuals have identified multiple susceptibility loci and, through genetic correlation and colocalization analyses, have shown that these structural traits are significantly genetically correlated with CAD, overall stroke and IS, indicating that peripheral and carotid atherosclerotic phenotypes also exhibit a polygenic basis and, at the single-trait level, already point to a shared genetic background with major ASCVD outcomes [10]. Taken together, although CAD, IS, PAD, cIMT and IA each have relatively well defined polygenic association maps from single-trait GWAS and scattered follow-up analyses have revealed genetic correlations and pleiotropic signals across these traits, the prevailing variant-by-variant, single-phenotype GWAS paradigm still has clear limitations in detecting multi-variant effects and in systematically characterizing the shared genetic architecture across related phenotypes, making it difficult to capture the joint structure of these ASCVD-related traits within a unified genetic framework [11].

In contrast, multivariate genetic analysis frameworks in complex traits such as brain imaging and psychiatric disorders have shown that jointly modelling multiple correlated phenotypes can better exploit pervasive genetic pleiotropy, substantially increasing the number of discovered risk loci and candidate genes, clarifying the shared genetic mechanisms across traits and, in some settings, improving genetic prediction of disease outcomes [12]. Within this multivariate framework, genomic structural equation modeling (Genomic SEM) goes beyond standard multivariate GWAS (mvGWAS) by using the empirical genetic covariance matrix estimated from GWAS summary statistics via linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) to extract latent factors that capture the shared genetic covariance across traits and then fitting mvGWAS models for these latent dimensions, thereby collapsing multiple related phenotypes into a smaller number of genetically informed factors and providing a more structured view of the joint genetic architecture of complex traits [13]. In practice, Genomic SEM has already been applied to spectra of immune-mediated disorders, where multivariate analyses of GWAS summary statistics revealed a continuum structure with four latent genetic factors spanning classical autoimmune to autoinflammatory diseases and demonstrated extensive genome-wide overlap consistent with a polygenic continuum, illustrating that modelling latent factors across related disorders can uncover biologically meaningful genetic clusters and suggest axes along which disease classification and therapeutic strategies may be refined [14]. In the cardiovascular field, multitrait genetic analyses have begun to be applied to atherosclerotic phenotypes, for example by jointly analysing CAD, PAD and multiple traditional risk factors to identify novel pleiotropic loci and unified biological pathways, but these efforts have largely focused on combinations such as CAD and PAD with risk factors or heart failure with lipid, blood pressure and glucose traits, and there is still no multivariate framework that systematically models CAD, IS, PAD, cIMT and IA together within a single ASCVD genetic architecture [15].

Building on these considerations, we will integrate summary statistics from multiple large-scale GWAS and use a Genomic SEM framework to construct and quantify an ASCVD multitraits genetic structure model that jointly includes CAD, MI, IS, PAD, cIMT and IA, derive latent genetic factors that capture shared and distinct components across vascular beds and then perform mvGWAS and downstream functional annotation analyses on these factors to provide a more systematic view of the joint genetic architecture and molecular pathways underlying these phenotypes. The first key innovation of our study is to move away from a single index disease and instead focus on a set of core ASCVD phenotypes, constructing latent genetic factors within a unified Genomic SEM framework to systematically delineate the joint genetic structure of the ASCVD. Building on the ASCVD latent factors constructed in our Genomic SEM framework, a second key innovation is that we will perform mvGWAS on these shared genetic dimensions rather than re-running only single-trait GWAS, with the goal of aggregating information across correlated phenotypes to increase statistical power and uncover susceptibility variants that do not reach genome-wide significance in any individual trait. Furthermore, a third innovation of our study is to carry out systematic gene-level and pathway-level annotation for loci associated with both shared ASCVD latent factors, including gene-set enrichment, tissue or cell-type enrichment and genetic correlation with related traits, in order to map these statistical genetic axes onto concrete biological processes. Taken together, by constructing latent ASCVD genetic factors within a unified Genomic SEM framework, performing mvGWAS on these factors to integrate association signals from multiple clinical phenotypes and systematically annotating the implicated variants at the gene and pathway level, our study aims to address the current lack of joint modeling for ASCVD multitraits at both the genetic architecture and biological mechanism levels and to provide more coherent evidence on shared and distinct genetic underpinnings, novel risk loci and candidate pathways that may inform future prevention and intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Acquisition of GWAS Summary Statistics

We compiled large-scale GWAS summary data for six phenotypes that clinically define ASCVD to construct the genetic foundation for Genomic SEM. These traits included CAD, MI, IS, PAD, cIMT, and IA. Ethical approval and informed consent protocols were confirmed in the primary publications for each dataset. Table S1 provides comprehensive details regarding the data sources, sample sizes, ancestry profiles and study designs for the included GWAS.

Pre-analysis quality control

Before commencing Genomic SEM, we subjected all summary statistics to a rigorous and uniform quality control protocol. The analysis was restricted to autosomal SNPs present in the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 European reference panel. We filtered out variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) below 0.01 or those with strand ambiguity. To mitigate bias from complex linkage disequilibrium structures, variants within the major histocompatibility complex region on chromosome 6 (25–35 Mb) were removed.

Modeling the latent genetic architecture

We utilized the GenomicSEM R package to investigate the shared genetic liability underlying the six ASCVD traits. This process followed a two-stage approach [16]. First, we applied multivariate LDSC to the processed summary statistics to generate a genetic covariance matrix and its corresponding sampling covariance matrix for CAD, MI, IS, PAD, cIMT, and IA. Table S2 lists the SNP-based heritability estimates derived from this step. Second, we specified a common factor model positing that a single latent factor drives the shared genetic susceptibility across these traits. The model was fitted to minimize the difference between the observed and model-implied covariance structures. We assessed model fit using the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), chi-square statistic (χ²), akaike information criterion (AIC), and comparative fit index (CFI), as detailed in Tables S3 and S4. This framework allowed us to incorporate association signals from individual autosomal SNPs into the covariance matrices, facilitating a genome-wide evaluation of the latent ASCVD factor. We also performed SNP-wise heterogeneity tests and removed variants with a Cochran’s Q statistic p-value below 0.05.

Model validation and diagnostics

Beyond standard fit indices, we employed LDSC-based diagnostics to verify the stability of the Genomic SEM results. We analyzed the mean χ², genomic control lambda (λGC), maximum χ², SNP-based heritability, and LDSC intercept. We also calculated the attenuation ratio, defined as (LDSC intercept − 1)/(mean χ² − 1), to detect residual confounding from population stratification or other systemic biases. The quality control process retained SNPs regardless of missing values or INFO scores below 0.9 and MAF below 0.01, provided they had valid p-values and unambiguous strand alignment.

Genomic loci identification and novelty assessment

We employed the FUMA platform to map genomic regions linked to the latent ASCVD factor. Genome-wide significant SNPs were defined by a p-value threshold of 5 × 10⁻⁸, with lead variants identified through linkage disequilibrium clumping (r² < 0.1) [17]. To distinguish novel findings from established associations, we implemented a GWAS-minus-locus strategy. This involved comparing the latent factor results against the single-trait GWAS for CAD, MI, IS, PAD, cIMT, and IA. A locus was classified as novel if the lead SNP resided more than 1 Mb from any previously reported genome-wide significant variant for the constituent traits. We also cross-referenced the GWAS Catalog to check for prior associations. MAGMA was used for gene-based and gene-set enrichment analyses, with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.05 for significance.

Statistical fine-mapping

We prioritized potential functional variants within the identified loci using the echolocatoR R package (v2.0.3), which implements both SuSiE and FINEMAP algorithms [18]. The analysis focused on a 250 kb window centered on each lead SNP from the Genomic SEM output. We calculated posterior probabilities (PP) for all variants in these regions. SNPs that fell within the 95% credible sets and achieved a mean PP exceeding 0.95 across methods were selected for further functional characterization.

Transcriptome-wide association analysis

We conducted a transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) to explore whether genetic risks for ASCVD are mediated by gene expression changes. Unlike SNP-level fine-mapping described in Sect. 2.6, TWAS operates at the gene level by testing the association between genetically predicted gene expression and the trait of interest. Using the sparse canonical correlation analysis TWAS (SCCA-TWAS) framework, we integrated the latent factor summary statistics with tissue-specific expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) weights from GTEx version 8 [19]. Genes showing significant associations (FDR < 0.05) underwent gene-level fine-mapping via FOCUS to estimate the posterior inclusion probability (PIP) for each gene. This Bayesian method accounts for local linkage disequilibrium and expression correlations among genes to evaluate colocalization between GWAS and eQTL signals. Genes with a PIP exceeding 0.8 were identified as high-confidence putative effector mediators.

Pathway enrichment analysis

We investigated the biological mechanisms driving the ASCVD latent factor through gene-set and pathway enrichment analyses. Using the Molecular Signatures Database and gene set enrichment analysis, we focused on genes prioritized by MAGMA. The background set comprised all protein-coding genes. Significance was determined using an FDR threshold of 0.05.

Cell-type specificity analysis

We applied CELLECT to determine if the heritability of the ASCVD latent factor is enriched in specific cell lineages. This analysis integrated the GWAS results with single-cell RNA-sequencing data from the Tabula Muris mouse atlas [20, 21]. CELLECT utilizes expression profiles processed by CELLEX and employs stratified LDSC to assess heritability enrichment in genes specific to certain cell types [22]. Additionally, we used stratified LDSC with the baseline-LD model to partition heritability across functional annotations, such as regulatory elements and conserved regions [23].

Spatial mapping of trait-associated cells

We used gsMap to spatially resolve the tissue context of cells associated with the ASCVD latent factor. This involved integrating the Genomic SEM summary statistics with mouse embryonic spatial transcriptomics data (E16.5 E1S1.MOSTA). We utilized a mouse-human homolog mapping file for cross-species alignment, restricting the analysis to one-to-one orthologous genes.

Polygenic risk score estimation

We evaluated PRS at the chromosomal level using PRS-CS. This method combines the ASCVD summary statistics with linkage disequilibrium data from an ancestry-matched reference panel to calculate posterior SNP effect sizes. These estimates were aggregated for each chromosome to quantify their respective contributions to the total genetic liability of ASCVD.

Results

Structural equation model fitting

Estimations from LDSC regression demonstrated that all six ASCVD-linked phenotypes, comprising CAD, MI, IS, PAD, cIMT, and IA, exhibited non-zero estimates for SNP-based heritability (Table S2). The most substantial SNP heritability was observed for CAD (h² = 0.1061, SE = 0.0056, Z = 18.9), with MI (h² = 0.0164, SE = 0.0015, Z = 11.1), IS (h² = 0.0136, SE = 0.0013, Z = 10.3), and PAD (h² = 0.0118, SE = 0.0012, Z = 10.1) following in order, while IA and cIMT showed more modest heritability estimates. Analysis of genetic covariances highlighted a unified structural pattern of shared genetic architecture across these atherosclerotic and vascular manifestations (Table S3 and Fig. 1). Notably, CAD displayed powerful positive genetic covariances with MI (cov = 0.9376, SE = 0.0587) and PAD (cov = 0.6123, SE = 0.0462), as well as significant covariance with IS (cov = 0.4727, SE = 0.0426). MI also showed considerable genetic covariance with PAD (cov = 0.6015, SE = 0.0632). Subsequently, we employed Genomic SEM to fit a single common factor model that represents the latent genetic liability shared among these six traits. This one-factor framework demonstrated an excellent fit to the observed genetic covariance matrix, as evidenced by CFI = 0.9837 and SRMR = 0.1448 (Table S4). These indices suggest that the cross-trait genetic correlations are parsimoniously accounted for by a single latent factor. Evaluation of standardized factor loadings indicated that MI (λ = 0.9426, SE = 0.0487), CAD (λ = 0.9396, SE = 0.0440), and PAD (λ = 0.6772, SE = 0.0457) possessed the most significant loadings on the ASCVD common factor, followed by IS (λ = 0.5206, SE = 0.0483) (Table S5). Using this finalized model, we performed a Genomic SEM-based GWAS for the ASCVD common factor. This analysis incorporated summary statistics for 5,362,003 SNPs to map the collective genetic architecture onto the latent factor.

Stability assessment of the genomic SEM via LDSC

To examine the stability of the ASCVD common factor GWAS and detect potential biases such as population stratification, we performed LDSC regression analysis. After the integration of Genomic SEM summary statistics with the European LD-score reference panel, a total of 1,044,494 SNPs were utilized for the regression. The ASCVD common factor yielded a mean χ² of 1.1789 and a λGC of 1.1364, which reflects a minor inflation in test statistics. We identified an LDSC intercept of 0.9804 (SE = 0.0079), and the calculated attenuation ratio was less than zero. This suggests that the slight inflation primarily stems from genuine polygenic signals rather than residual confounding factors. These findings confirm the stability of the Genomic SEM model and indicate that artifacts at the genome-wide level are unlikely to bias subsequent discovery or functional investigations.

Risk genetic loci

The GWAS of the ASCVD common factor identified 839 genome-wide significant variants (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) (Table S6, Fig. 2). Subsequent clumping of these signals resulted in 85 independent lead SNPs at an LD threshold of r² < 0.1 (Table S7), which signifies that the shared liability for ASCVD is influenced by numerous distinct genomic regions. According to FUMA functional annotations, a high proportion of these variants were located in intronic (45.2%) and intergenic (35.2%) regions. Novelty analysis further revealed 67 SNPs that did not achieve genome-wide significance in any individual input GWAS, demonstrating that the ASCVD common factor identifies shared genetic signals that remain obscured in single-trait assessments (Table S8). Investigation through the GWAS Catalog showed that several lead SNPs are located in loci with established links to CAD, MI, and lipid metabolism (Table S9). Specifically, rs10738607 at the 9p21 locus has been previously associated with IS, CAD, and various lipid traits. Similarly, rs56289821 at 19p13 is strongly connected to LDL cholesterol levels and CAD. These patterns of pleiotropy confirm the biological importance of the identified loci and point toward a convergence on pathways related to atherosclerosis and lipid regulation (Table S9).

Fine-mapping

We conducted SNP-level statistical fine-mapping of the ASCVD common factor GWAS to narrow down potential functional variants within the associated loci. This analysis identified 63 SNPs with high PP among the genome-wide significant signals (Table S10, Fig. 3). Strong evidence for specific likely functional SNPs was found in several loci. Notably, rs11043482 at the RP11-606D9.1 locus, rs6744602 at the AC019129.1 locus, and rs12627269 at the PRDM15 locus all demonstrated mean PP of approximately 1.0. These results indicate that these lead SNPs may represent the functional variants driving the association signal at their respective loci. These findings refine the initial GWAS associations into a specific collection of candidate variants for further experimental exploration.

Gene-level identification of susceptibility

Complementing the SNP-level fine-mapping described above, we utilized MAGMA and TWAS to investigate gene-level susceptibility. These approaches aggregate SNP-level signals to the gene level, testing whether the cumulative genetic association within a gene region or the genetically predicted expression of a gene is associated with the ASCVD common factor. Through sCCA-TWAS, we identified 26 genes that exceeded the FDR threshold (FDR < 0.05), consisting of 7 genes with negative and 19 with positive Z-scores (Fig. 4). Gene-level fine-mapping using FOCUS highlighted 15 genes with a PIP exceeding 0.8, marking them as putative effector genes for the ASCVD common factor. The intersection of TWAS and FOCUS results produced 6 high-confidence putative effector genes (Table 1). For instance, CDKN2B showed a strong negative association (TWAS Z = − 29.9, FDR = 1.47 × 10⁻¹⁹⁶, PIP = 1.00), while MIA3 and NBEAL1 exhibited positive associations and high PIP values (Table 1). Furthermore, gene-based analysis via MAGMA detected 73 genes associated with ASCVD at an FDR-controlled level (Table S11). Among these, PHACTR1 (Z = 7.86, P = 1.88 × 10⁻¹⁵, FDR = 3.47 × 10⁻¹¹) and AIDA (Z = 7.11, P = 5.71 × 10⁻¹³) showed the most robust gene-level associations (Fig. 5).

Pathway enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis of the associated genes underscored lipid and lipoprotein metabolism as the predominant biological themes (Table S12). Within the framework of canonical pathways, significant enrichment signals (FDR < 0.05) were concentrated in mechanisms governing plasma lipoprotein remodeling, assembly and clearance, and cholesterol metabolism. These findings were consistently echoed across multiple annotation sources, particularly highlighting the metabolic regulation of LDL, HDL, and triglyceride-rich particles. Gene Ontology evaluations provided further structural and functional support, revealing marked enrichment in protein-lipid complexes and apolipoprotein binding activities. Additionally, GWAS Catalog enrichment confirmed that the identified loci are highly representative of clinical traits such as CAD, MI, and lipoprotein(a) levels. Collectively, these convergent patterns indicate that the shared genetic liability of ASCVD is deeply integrated into the fundamental pathways of systemic lipid homeostasis and atherogenesis.

Cell type annotation and enrichment analysis

We performed heritability enrichment analysis using cell type-specific expression signatures to identify relevant cellular contexts for ASCVD genetic risk (Table S13). Notably, endothelial cells from various tissues emerged as primary sites of genetic susceptibility, with the strongest signals observed in fat endothelial cells (P = 0.007) and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (P = 0.008). Significant associations were also found in heart endothelial cells (P = 0.010), pancreas endothelial cells (P = 0.015), and trachea endothelial cells (P = 0.022). Beyond the vasculature, pancreatic stellate cells (P = 0.010) and megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors in the bone marrow (P = 0.018) also exhibited marked enrichment. Additional significant findings included endothelial populations in the mammary gland (P = 0.033), limb muscle (P = 0.037), and kidney (P = 0.043). These results collectively highlight that the shared genetic architecture of ASCVD is predominantly localized in endothelial lineages across multiple systems, alongside key metabolic and hematological supporting cells.

Spatially mapping in embryonic tissues

We further examined the spatial distribution of the ASCVD common factor signal across embryonic tissues. Spatial mapping identified several tissues with significant associations (Table S14). Tissues such as the lung (P = 2.51 × 10⁻⁵) and kidney (P = 5.06 × 10⁻⁵) showed the most robust evidence of enrichment, followed by the submandibular gland, connective tissue, and gastrointestinal tract (P < 0.05). These results indicate that the shared genetic liability for ASCVD is primarily localized in developing cardiometabolic and structural tissues rather than neural structures.

Heritability enrichment across genomic functional and regulatory regions

To clarify the genomic context of ASCVD heritability, we performed stratified LDSC analysis across diverse functional and regulatory annotations (Table S15). The heritability of the ASCVD common factor was significantly concentrated in categories associated with conserved regions and active regulatory elements. Specifically, extended conserved regions (Enrichment = 2.02, FDR = 0.002) and enhancer marks like H3K27ac (Enrichment = 2.23, FDR = 4.99 × 10⁻⁵) and H3K9ac tended to show high enrichment estimates. Annotations for super-enhancers also demonstrated positive trends. Conversely, repressed chromatin and certain transcribed regions did not exhibit strong enrichment. These results show that ASCVD heritability is preferentially located in evolutionarily conserved and actively regulated genomic regions, particularly those associated with enhancers.

Polygenic risk score construction from summary data

Finally, we calculated a PRS for the ASCVD common factor and assessed the distribution of the polygenic load across chromosomes (Table S16). The highest PRS score sums were found on chromosome 2 (0.447) and chromosome 1 (0.441), with chromosomes 3 through 6 showing intermediate contributions. Smaller chromosomes, such as 19 through 22, provided relatively smaller fractions of the total PRS sum. These findings indicate that while the polygenic architecture of ASCVD is spread across the genome, the distribution is heterogeneous, with larger chromosomes accounting for a greater proportion of the genetic risk.

Discussion

This study applied Genomic SEM to delineate the shared genetic architecture across six primary ASCVD phenotypes. By modelling a single latent common factor with excellent fit, we localized 85 independent genomic regions significantly associated with the ASCVD spectrum, of which 67 represent novel associations not detected in any constituent univariate GWAS. Complementary post-GWAS analyses—including fine-mapping, TWAS with FOCUS, pathway enrichment, cell-type analysis, and spatial transcriptomic mapping—collectively established a comprehensive, multi-dimensional evidence base for the biological functions, cellular contexts, and systemic distribution of ASCVD genetic risk. It is important to contextualize the present work within the broader landscape of multivariate genetic analysis. Genomic SEM was originally introduced as a flexible framework for analysing the joint genetic architecture of complex traits and has since been widely applied to psychiatric phenotypes—where a joint analysis of five disorders identified 27 novel loci beyond univariate GWAS [16]. Within the cardiovascular domain specifically, Genomic SEM has recently been applied to characterize the shared genetic architecture of cardiac conduction disorders—including sick sinus syndrome, atrioventricular block, and bundle branch blocks—identifying novel risk loci and candidate genes for arrhythmogenic phenotypes [24]. However, this application focused on the cardiac electrical conduction system rather than the ASCVD pathology that underlies coronary, cerebrovascular, and peripheral arterial disease. For atherosclerotic phenotypes themselves, several prior efforts have examined shared genetic architecture, yet with important methodological and phenotypic limitations. Recent large-scale multi-trait GWAS efforts combined summary statistics from CAD, coronary artery calcification, cIMT, and traditional cardiometabolic risk factors through 15 separate pairwise or multi-way analyses, identifying 442 shared risk loci but without constructing a unified latent factor model [25]. Similarly, earlier GWAS meta-analyses of cIMT and carotid plaque established genetic correlations with CAD and ischaemic stroke using LDSC but focused on subclinical measures alone and did not integrate multiple clinical endpoints [26]. Multi-trait analyses combining CAD with PAD and conventional risk factors have identified pleiotropic loci and unified pathways [15], but these efforts have generally not incorporated cerebrovascular phenotypes such as ischaemic stroke or intracranial aneurysm. Against this background, the present study is, to our knowledge, the first to apply Genomic SEM to systematically model a unified genetic architecture spanning the full spectrum of ASCVD manifestations—CAD, MI, IS, IA, PAD, and cIMT—within a single latent factor framework. Biologically, this common factor may be interpreted as capturing the shared genetic propensity toward systemic endothelial dysfunction and dysregulated lipid homeostasis—two interrelated processes that underpin atherosclerotic plaque formation across arterial beds. This interpretation aligns with our cell-type enrichment findings, which consistently implicated endothelial lineages, and our pathway analyses, which converged on lipoprotein remodelling and cholesterol metabolism. Reassuringly, our analysis recapitulated several well-established features of ASCVD genetics that serve as important internal validation of the modelling framework. These include robust signals at canonical lipid-handling loci such as LPA, LPL, LDLR, and APOE, pathway enrichment concentrated in lipoprotein remodelling and cholesterol metabolism, and heritability localisation in endothelial cell lineages across multiple tissues. The convergence of these expected findings with the existing body of literature confirms that the Genomic SEM common factor captures the core genetic substrate of ASCVD. Beyond this validation layer, however, the principal contribution of the present study lies in uncovering genetic signals that extend the biological understanding of ASCVD beyond the classical lipid-centric paradigm. Among the 67 novel loci not reaching genome-wide significance in any input univariate GWAS, fine-mapping and gene-level analyses prioritised high-confidence candidates implicated in vascular cell cycle regulation and senescence, extracellular matrix remodelling and monocyte–endothelial adhesion, trained innate immunity following myocardial injury, and organic cation handling in cardiomyocytes. Furthermore, spatial transcriptomic mapping localised ASCVD genetic risk not only to the expected vascular and adipose compartments but also to metabolic and homeostatic organs such as the lung and kidney, suggesting that systemic regulation of vascular integrity may be governed by developmental programmes in tissues beyond the arterial wall itself.

Our SNP-level fine-mapping analysis highlighted a set of high-confidence variants within loci that harbour genes with well-established roles in ASCVD pathogenesis. Among the fine-mapped SNPs, those within the LPA gene region are concordant with its established role of this locus in encoding lipoprotein (a), which accounts for most of the inter-individual variation in lipoprotein (a) concentrations and underlies the established link between elevated lipoprotein (a) and ASCVD [27]. In line with the established role of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins as a established risk factor for CAD, high-confidence SNPs identified within the LPL gene region support the concept that genetically determined variation in lipoprotein lipase activity contributes to the triglyceride-related component of residual ASCVD risk [28]. Beyond pathways involved in circulating lipids, the high-confidence SNPs within the ITGA1 gene region indicate a vascular-wall component of risk and aligns with experimental data showing that the COL4A1–ITGA1 pathway modulates monocyte–endothelial adhesion and extracellular matrix organisation in atherosclerotic arteries [29]. Consistent with evidence that MI induces a trained inflammatory state in monocytes characterised by increased SYK expression and accelerated atherosclerosis progression, the high-confidence SNPs within the SYK gene region suggest that genetically mediated variation in this kinase may modulate immune and thrombotic processes that are relevant to ASCVD [30]. The high-confidence SNPs within the SLC22A3 gene region in our fine-mapping analysis is in keeping with previous data showing that variants in this organic cation transporter are associated with CAD risk, at least partly by modulating SLC22A3 expression and attenuating lipopolysaccharide-induced monocytic inflammatory responses [31]. The high-confidence SNPs we detected within the WDR12_CARF region is consistent with previous GWAS that identified the 2q33 region in WDR12 as a susceptibility locus for early-onset MI, suggesting that genetic variation in this nucleolar complex component may influence cardiovascular structure and function in addition to traditional risk pathways [32]. In addition to SNPs within protein-coding gene regionss, the high-confidence SNPs we observed at regions annotated to hsa-mir-7162 and LINC00536 indicate that noncoding RNA–mediated regulation may also contribute to ASCVD susceptibility, in line with evidence that microRNAs and long noncoding RNAs fine-tune key inflammatory and lipid pathways involved in plaque progression and regression [33]. Taken together, the high-confidence SNPs at lipid-related gene regions such as LPA and LPL, vascular and immune regulators including ITGA1, SYK, SLC22A3 and WDR12_CARF, alongside noncoding RNA regions, delineate a multilayered genetic architecture in which lipid handling, vascular wall remodelling and inflammatory control jointly shape individual susceptibility to ASCVD.

Moving from SNP-level fine-mapping to gene-level interpretation, we next focused on genes prioritised by the integration of TWAS with FOCUS and MAGMA. These gene-level approaches complement SNP-level analyses by aggregating genetic signals across gene regions or by testing whether genetically predicted gene expression is associated with the trait, thereby providing a more direct link to potential effector genes. We first concentrated on genes that participate in lipoprotein and triglyceride-rich remnant metabolism, which are recognised as central pathways in the established connection between dyslipidaemia and ASCVD [34]. Within this lipid-related component, our gene-level analyses placed classical loci such as LDLR, APOE and LPL in prominent positions, in line with large-scale CAD genetics showing that GWAS-identified targets in lipoprotein metabolism provide some of the most robust and actionable signals for ASCVD risk [35]. The prominence of LDLR, APOE and LPL in our results is biologically plausible, because lipoprotein lipase generates cholesterol-enriched remnants from triglyceride-rich lipoproteins, apolipoprotein E promotes the hepatic uptake of these remnants and low-density lipoprotein receptor participates in the clearance of apoB- and apoE-containing particles that drive atherogenesis [36]. Consistent with this lipid-related pattern, our MAGMA analysis also highlighted the LPA locus, which determines plasma lipoprotein (a) levels, and this finding aligns with large epidemiologic and genetic studies showing that elevated lipoprotein (a) is a prevalent, genetically driven risk factor for ASCVD even when low-density lipoprotein cholesterol is well controlled [37]. In the same chromosomal region, PLG, encoding plasminogen, also emerged in our MAGMA results, which fits with the view that the plasmin system not only drives vascular fibrinolysis but, when dysregulated, can influence inflammatory mechanisms involved in atherosclerosis and atherothrombotic events [38]. Taken together, the convergence of our gene-level findings on lipoprotein metabolism and fibrinolysis-related loci, including genes such as LDLR, APOE, LPL, LPA and PLG, is in line with contemporary concepts that ASCVD arises from the interplay between disordered lipid accumulation, vascular inflammation and a thrombosis-prone milieu, rather than from cholesterol overload alone [39].

Beyond the lipid- and thrombosis-related loci, our integrated TWAS with FOCUS and MAGMA results also converged on several genes linked to arterial wall biology and inflammatory signalling, which is consistent with the current view that atherosclerosis represents a form of vascular chronic inflammation initiated by interactions between classical risk factors and arterial wall cells [40]. In this context, our TWAS and MAGMA signals at the 9p21.3 region, pointing to CDKN2B, recapitulate one of the most robust non-coding CAD loci identified by GWAS, where long-range enhancers and non-coding RNAs modulate CDKN2A/CDKN2B expression to influence vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) proliferation and ageing [41]. Functionally, CDKN2B together with CDKN2A encodes cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors that restrain the cell cycle in VSMCs, and 9p21 risk alleles have been shown to reduce p15/p16 expression, enhance smooth muscle cell proliferation and increase smooth muscle content within atherosclerotic plaques, providing a plausible route by which this locus contributes to CAD susceptibility [42]. At the 2q locus, our TWAS and MAGMA results converged on NBEAL1, and this is supported by an independent TWAS in coronary atherosclerosis in which NBEAL1 was the only gene detected by all four TWAS methods, showed a protective relationship in summary-data-based Mendelian randomization analysis and exhibited differential expression in macrophages, plasma cells and endothelial cells within coronary plaques [43]. Given that NBEAL1 belongs to the beige and Chediak–Higashi (BEACH)-domain protein family, which is implicated in vesicle trafficking and lysosome-related organelles, and that NBEAL1 itself carries a vacuolar-targeting motif suggestive of lysosomal localization, its protective association with coronary atherosclerosis and differential expression in macrophages and endothelial cells may reflect a role in fine-tuning vesicular pathways that shape inflammatory and lipid-handling responses within the arterial wall [44]. In addition to CDKN2B and NBEAL1, our gene-based analyses also identified a cluster of organic cation transporters at 6q26–q27, with SLC22A3 showing strong signals, which is consistent with its role as a broadly expressed polyspecific organic cation transporter that handles endogenous amines and many cationic drugs in vivo [45]. Together with previous work showing that SLC22A3 mediates norepinephrine entry into cardiomyocytes and is required for activation of intracellular β1-adrenergic receptor–PKA signalling at the sarcoplasmic reticulum that phosphorylates phospholamban and augments cardiac contractility, our signals at the 6q26–q27 transporter cluster raise the possibility that genetically determined variation in SLC22A3 may influence ASCVD risk in part by altering how the heart and vascular wall handle catecholamine exposure [46]. Overall, the clustering of non-lipid susceptibility genes such as CDKN2B, NBEAL1 and SLC22A3 around pathways that regulate VSMC behaviour, inflammatory and vesicular processes in plaque leukocytes and endothelial cells, and local handling of catecholamines by the heart and vessel wall is consistent with the notion that ASCVD arises from an imbalanced lipid metabolism coupled to a maladaptive immune response and chronic arterial inflammation [47].

Taken together, the pathway and cell type enrichment profiles in this study point to a genetic background that is closely aligned with lipid metabolism and vascular pathways central to ASCVD. In this context, the enrichment of pathways related to lipoprotein remodeling and cholesterol handling is consistent with evidence that triglycerides and the cholesterol carried in triglyceride rich lipoproteins or remnant particles play an important role role in ASCVD [48]. Beyond these pathway level links, our findings are in line with extensive clinical and genetic evidence that LDL cholesterol acts as a major driver of ASCVD [49]. In addition, the prominence of apolipoprotein B (apoB) containing particles and lipoprotein (a) in our enrichment patterns is biologically plausible, because large epidemiologic and genetic studies have shown that higher lipoprotein (a) concentrations are continuously related to ASCVD outcomes across different populations [37]. Against this background, the enrichment signals we observe for lipid related traits in the GWAS Catalog, including LDL cholesterol, apoB, lipoprotein (a) and triglyceride levels, suggest that our genetic findings are anchored in lipid phenotypes that have already been implicated as strongly implicated in ASCVD. Consistent with this lipid centered pattern, the enrichment of multiple end point traits in the GWAS Catalog, such as CAD, MI and PAD, fits with the view that ASCVD represents a spectrum of vascular conditions in which coronary and lower extremity arterial involvement often coexist [50]. In particular, the presence of enrichment signals for coronary artery calcification (CAC) related traits is noteworthy, because CAC scoring has emerged as a reproducible and widely available method for assessing risk of major cardiovascular outcomes and is regarded as a robust marker of subclinical atherosclerotic burden [51]. Taken together, the convergence of pathway level lipid remodeling signals, enrichment for genetically supported lipid traits such as LDL cholesterol, apoB and lipoprotein (a), and overrepresentation of clinical ASCVD end points including CAD, MI, PAD and CAC, indicates that our genetic findings map onto multiple stages of the atherosclerotic disease process rather than reflecting isolated associations. In line with this, the enrichment of platelet related pathways in our analysis is also plausible, because erosion or rupture of lipid rich vulnerable plaques exposes plaque components to the circulation and triggers tissue factor activation, platelet recruitment and aggregation, ultimately leading to superimposed thrombus formation on atherosclerotic lesions and acute ischaemic syndromes [52]. Extending this vessel wall perspective, the enrichment we observe in several endothelial cell populations is consistent with the central role of endothelial dysfunction in ASCVD, as endothelial dysfunction is regarded as a hallmark of panvascular disorders including atherosclerosis, hypertension and diabetes [53]. In this context, the enrichment of endothelial cells from adipose and cardiac tissues in our cell type analysis is compatible with the notion that dysfunctional adipose depots and perivascular fat, through chronic inflammation and altered adipokine release, disturb vascular homeostasis and promote endothelial dysfunction, thereby linking obesity related adipose remodeling to vascular disease risk [54]. Overall, the coherent enrichment pattern spanning lipid remodeling pathways, genetically supported lipid traits, clinical ASCVD end points and markers of plaque burden, together with platelet related pathways and endothelial cell populations, supports the interpretation that the genetic background captured in this study is tightly embedded within the multifaceted pathobiology of ASCVD rather than reflecting nonspecific systemic signals.

To precisely localize the spatial distribution of genetic signals underlying the ASCVD common factor, we implemented the gsMap framework. Our results revealed genetic enrichment across multiple non-vascular systems, particularly within the lung and kidney tissues. While the biological interpretation of these findings remains speculative, the predominance of signals in these organs may point to a potential cardiopulmonary-renal axis in ASCVD pathology, possibly involving shared developmental regulators such as platelet-derived growth factors [55]. Furthermore, the concurrent enrichment in smooth muscle and adipose tissues may reflect genetic overlap between vascular remodeling and systemic metabolic regulation, although the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated. One possible interpretation is that adipokine-mediated signaling could contribute to plaque stability by modulating smooth muscle cell dynamics [56]. The identification of genetic signals in the gastrointestinal tract and connective tissues raises the possibilitythat gut-derived metabolites might influence vascular integrity via extracellular matrix remodeling, though this hypothesis requires further investigation [57]. The observed enrichment in the submandibular gland and oral tissues is consistent with a previously suggested link between oral health and cardiovascular risk, potentially through shared pleiotropic loci such as ANRIL [58]. Metabolic hubs, including the liver and skeletal muscle, exhibited enrichment patterns consistent with known roles of hepatic lipid homeostasis and peripheral insulin sensitivity in driving ASCVD progression [59, 60]. Collectively, these spatial mapping results provide preliminary evidence for a multidimensional genetic architecture of ASCVD across tissues, although functional validation will be necessary to establish the biological relevance of these tissue-specific enrichments.

While our study provides a unified genetic framework for ASCVD and identifies novel biological insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the six ASCVD phenotypes differ substantially in clinical presentation and vascular bed involvement, and a single common factor model may not fully capture this heterogeneity. Nevertheless, the model demonstrated acceptable fit, with factor loading gradients reflecting the degree of shared genetic variance across phenotypes. Future studies may explore multi-factor structures to further dissect coronary, cerebrovascular, and peripheral vascular components of ASCVD liability. Second, the input GWAS varied considerably in sample size and statistical power, with CAD and MI having substantially larger samples than cIMT and IA. While this imbalance may reduce precision in detecting associations for less-powered phenotypes, prior methodological work has shown that factor loadings and model fit in Genomic SEM remain robust to sample size variation [61]. Nonetheless, estimates for smaller GWAS should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Future studies leveraging larger GWAS for cerebrovascular and subclinical phenotypes will help refine these findings. Third, all contributing GWAS were conducted in populations of European ancestry, and the LD reference panel used for Genomic SEM was derived from the 1000 Genomes European subset. Consequently, the generalizability of our findings to non-European populations remains uncertain, and future multi-ancestry analyses are warranted. Fourth, while TWAS and statistical fine-mapping prioritise candidate genes and variants with high posterior probabilities, these approaches do not establish causality. The genes highlighted in this study, such as CDKN2B and NBEAL1, require experimental validation to confirm their functional roles in ASCVD pathogenesis. Fifth, the spatial mapping of genetic signals to embryonic tissues such as lung and kidney provides correlative evidence of tissue-specific enrichment but does not establish functional relevance. The developmental interpretations proposed here are speculative and require experimental validation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study systematically delineates the joint genetic architecture of the ASCVD spectrum. By moving beyond single-trait analyses to a unified Genomic SEM framework, we have identified a robust shared genetic liability localized in endothelial cell lineages and lipid-handling pathways. The discovery of 67 novel loci and the identification of high-confidence genes like CDKN2B and NBEAL1 emphasize that ASCVD risk is driven by a complex interplay of systemic metabolic regulation and vascular wall biology. From a translational perspective, the shared genetic architecture identified here may facilitate the development of pan-vascular PRS that capture liability across multiple arterial beds, potentially improving early identification of individuals at high risk for diverse ASCVD manifestations. Furthermore, the prioritised genes and pathways represent candidate targets for therapeutic strategies aimed at the common biological substrate of atherosclerosis. These insights provide a more coherent biological basis for understanding cardiovascular morbidity and may inform future precision strategies for multi-trait risk assessment and intervention.
